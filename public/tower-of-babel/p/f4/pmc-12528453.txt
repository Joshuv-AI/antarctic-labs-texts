An EEG-based Imagined Speech Database for comparing Paradigm Designs
Authors: Edgar Aguilera-Rodríguez, Alma Cuevas-Romero, Santiago Mendoza-Franco, Jonathan Wornovitzky-Green, Eduardo Rivera-Cerros, David Villanueva-Cazares, Luis Alberto Muñoz-Ubando, David Ibarra-Zárate, Luz María Alonso-Valerdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) attempt to establish a connection between the human mind and a computer system. While recent computational advances continue to improve these interfaces, human factors have been overlooked. Factors such as fatigue and attention play a key role in brain signal modulation. This arises the need for paradigms designed and implemented in terms of human factors. Therefore, it is proposed to improve the level of engagement to diminish fatigue and increase attention by a video game-based paradigm for an imagined speech BCI. For this purpose, a sample of 15 volunteers (females = 7) was recruited to study the quality of their imagined speech when it is evoked under an abstract scenario (traditional paradigm) and a video-game paradigm. This dataset helps to study the differences in imagined speech signals when using two different paradigms: (1) one that does not consider human factors, and (2) one that does. Additional applications may include designing imagined speech decoding models for BCI and studying the relationship between users’ profile and their imagined speech signals.

Background & Summary

Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) aim to interpret human commands and turn them into real-world actions. This can be accomplished within an iterative cycle where both the user and the computer learn to adapt to each other1. First, brain signals are measured while the user modules them by internally (mental tasks) or externally (stimulus-oriented tasks) controlled actions. Then, the signals are pre-processed, and task-related features are extracted and fed into a prediction model. Finally, users receive the computer feedback in accordance with the model prediction and evaluate if the system action is in accordance with their intentions. Several iterations of this cycle allow the user to adapt to the model and vice versa. In this context, Electroencephalography (EEG) is the most common method to measure these brain signals since it is non-invasive and has a high temporal resolution. On the other hand, imagined speech provides an intuitive mental task to control BCIs and promises positive results in comparison with other tasks such as visual imagery2 and motor imagery3.

To achieve real-time interaction, an initial prediction model needs to be trained. For this purpose, users firstly engage in an offline acquisition paradigm to record their brain signals while performing the mental task of interest. Afterwards, a model is trained to this brain information, and it is used for real-time interaction. However, the model (regardless of its computational architecture) frequently shows unexpected pitfalls, and a significant number of users achieve poor performances. This problem, known as BCI Illiteracy, had been considered as a user disability, categorizing between 15% and 30% of users as BCI illiterate. Some studies in this matter have found that context could be the authentic problem4.

Factors that may cause poor BCI performance are categorized in transient, social and experimental design. Transient factors refer to attention level, fatigue, or frustration. Social ones have been associated with emotional responses, interactions and social cues. Experimental design issues have been mainly related to non-intuitive and boring training protocols. Some of them even lead to participants falling asleep5.

To minimize those factors, approaches such as user-centered designs have been proposed. This approach designs and implements systems in line with the user necessities and interests, and it has shown a significant performance improvement6. Hence, the present database comprises two paradigm protocols: (1) a traditional paradigm, based on an abstract scheme; and (2) an experimental paradigm, based on user-centered design approach. This database promotes the study of human factors in BCI development and helps address the limited availability of publicly accessible imagined speech databases.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Medical School at Tecnologico de Monterrey, under protocol number CONBIOETICA-19-CEI-011-20161017. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, which included consent to take part in the study and to have their anonymized data used for research and publication purposes. Participant confidentiality was maintained by assigning random alphanumeric codes to each dataset, and all personally identifiable information was removed before storage and analysis.

Sample

Fifteen participants, eight males and seven females, with an age range of 18 to 27 years old, participated in this study. All of them reported no sensory (auditory or visual) and/or neurological (attention deficit) conditions. All participants were native Spanish speakers and reported no speech or language impairments. Each participant undertook two sessions (around one hour each) under laboratory-controlled conditions between June and October 2023.

Materials and equipment

Four previous validated questionnaires were applied for this study. First, the Internal Representations Questionnaire was used to identify individual modes of internal representation7. This questionnaire provides values between 1 and 5 that measure individual propensities to perform visual imagery, internal verbalization, and orthographic imagery. Additionally, it provides a self-rating on manipulating mental representations. The results of this questionnaire can help us understand differences in brain signals between participants with different propensities to certain internal representations.

Second, the Short Form of the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory was applied to confirm each participant’s handedness8. In the applied version, four situations were presented. Participants selected which hand they used in each situation and how frequently they used it. The possible answers were the following five: (1) right hand, (2) usually right hand, (3) no preference, (4) usually left hand, and (5) always left hand. The results of this questionnaire are useful to study the relationship between handedness and the dominant hemisphere in speech production.

Third, the Self-Assessment Manikin is a non-verbal pictorial assessment tool designed to gauge a person’s affective response to a diverse range of stimuli by measuring valence, arousal, and dominance9. Each of the three affective dimensions is represented by five images, and participants are prompted to rate them on a 9-point scale. For valence, the scale spans from negative (depicted by a frowning figure) to neutral and then to positive (illustrated by a smiling figure). Arousal is visually conveyed, ranging from low (eyes closed) to high (eyes wide open). Additionally, the arousal scale includes imagery over the abdomen area to depict the intensity of arousal, varying from low (symbolized by a small pin prick) to high (represented by imagery resembling an explosive-like burst). Dominance or control is the last dimension measured, extending from a sense of being controlled or submissive (depicted by a very small figure) to a feeling of being in control or dominance (illustrated by a very large figure). The results of this test address the effects of transient and social factors on the measured brain signals.

Finally, the User Experience Questionnaire allows participants to provide feedback about the paradigms’ qualities and weaknesses. It comprises 22 pairs of contrasting attributes, each pair with seven circles between them representing gradations. This questionnaire provides levels of attractiveness, perspicuity, efficiency, dependability, stimulation, and novelty for each paradigm10.

To record EEG activity was employed the mBrainTrain Smarting system (Belgrade, Serbia). This device has a 500 Hz sampling rate and a 24-channel recording cap. The EEG channels are Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T7, T8, P7, P8, Fz, Cz, Pz, Afz, CPz, POz, M1, and M2. The latter two channels (M1 and M2) are used for re-referencing purposes. Additionally, the FCz electrode was used as a reference, and the Fpz electrode was the ground for the bioamplifier.

Laboratory setting

All experimental sessions were conducted in NeuroTechs Research Laboratory at Tecnologico de Monterrey. The laboratory is a temperature-controlled room maintained at 20 °C. The room had a consistent ambient noise level of approximately 40 dB, ensuring minimal acoustic interference. The dimensions of the laboratory are 2.98 m in length, 2.25 m in width, and 2.59 m in height, providing a controlled and standardized environment for data collection.

Comparison of paradigm designs for imagined speech based on EEG approaches.

The paradigm of interest in this study is the naturalistic design proposed by11. This one, to the best of our knowledge, was the first attempt to move toward a user-centered approach in imagined speech research. Given that the objective of this work was to enhance the naturalistic aspects of paradigm design, the proposal presented in11 was adopted as a reference and considered the traditional baseline (hereunder referred to as traditional paradigm). In this paradigm, participants perform imagined speech tasks following a rhythm guided by auditory beeps. The experimental paradigm is a gamification approach based on achieving the goal of leading a character out of a maze, following a similar logistic as the traditional paradigm.

The same prompts are used in both paradigms to allow comparisons between them. These prompts are “avanzar,” “retroceder,” “derecha,” and “izquierda” (which correspond to “advance”, “backwards”, “right” and “left”, respectively). We chose these words because they differ in semantics and phonetics, as well as being useful for controlling a video game character. The resulting datasets allow us to compare the effectiveness of both paradigms to acquire distinguishable imagined speech signals, specifically focusing on their applicability in BCI systems. For both paradigms, participants seated comfortably in front of a monitor. Each participant engaged in one session per paradigm, each consisting of 30 repetitions per prompt. In total, 120 trials per participant were obtained (30 repetitions x 4 prompts) for each paradigm. The traditional paradigm was designed and implemented using OpenViBE 3.5.0. The experimental paradigm was designed and implemented in Python 3.11.4 using Pygame 2.5.2 along with Smarting software for EEG recording. The connection between Python and Smarting was established using Lab Streaming Layer.

Traditional paradigm

Participants were provided with headphones and instructed to adjust the volume to a comfortable level. To familiarize them with the paradigm, they first completed a set of practice trials using vocalized speech instead of imagined speech, ensuring they fully understood the task instructions. Once proper task execution was confirmed, participants proceeded with the imagined speech trials. The experiment consisted of 40 pseudo-randomized trials, the order of which was unknown to the participants.

Each trial began with a black screen, followed by the presentation of a visual cue (a written word displayed on a monitor) and an auditory cue (a beep sound delivered through the headphones). The visual cue remained on screen for seven-time intervals, while the auditory cue was repeated four additional times at a fixed rhythm (period T = 1.4 s), establishing the pacing of the task. Participants were instructed to imagine pronouncing the displayed word each time they heard a beep. After the final beep, they continued the task for two additional imagined repetitions at the same rhythm, but without auditory guidance. Only these last three instances of each trial were recorded. Each trial concluded with a 2-second rest period, during which no cues were presented, and no specific mental activity was required. Experimental paradigm

This paradigm emulates a video game, but without the user controlling the character. The two-dimensional video game is a redesigned version of the Pac-man game available in GitHub repository. The game consisted of a character that could move in four directions: forward, backward, left, and right. The goal was to escape a maze by visiting all checkpoints in numerical order. The main character of this video game was an animated dog with an identifiable face and tail, which allowed indicating where it was facing. Figure 2 shows every view of the character. Character movement was based on the way it is faced. Namely, the same command would not move the character in the same direction for different views. For example, if the character was facing left, the command “avanzar” would move the character to the screen left, but if the character was facing right, “avanzar” would move the character to the screen right. The selection of a command is based on the character view, the next checkpoint, and the available path. The command “avanzar” (advance) moves the character in the direction it is facing.Fig. 2The four different views of the character. The commands “retroceder” (backwards), “derecha” (right), and “izquierda”(left) turn the character and move it into the new direction it is facing. Specifically, “retroceder” (backwards) turned the character around 180 degrees, while commands “derecha” (right) and “izquierda” (left) turned the character 90 degrees into the indicated direction. Cues on this paradigm were given by color changes in the maze borders, indicating user action. White borders indicated no specific action, green borders indicated imagined speech, and blue borders indicated vocalized speech. Before the experiment, a tutorial was provided to the participants, which introduced the character, its views, the proper words to use in each case, and a few trials to understand how the game works.

The experiment started with a maze appearing on the screen, composed of white borders, progress dots, and checkpoints represented by cookies. The trial was divided into four steps: (1) movement decision, (2) imagined speech, (3) vocalized speech, and (4) character movement. The first three steps were performed each in period T = 1.4 s. The trial starts with white borders, the character standing still, and an auxiliary arrow pointing in the next correct direction. Based on the way the character was faced, the user must decide which word is adequate to achieve the goal (movement decision). Then, the maze borders turn green, indicating the participant must imagine pronouncing the previously decided word (imagined speech). The maze borders then turn blue, indicating the participant must verbally pronounce the imagined word (vocalized speech). Finally, the borders turn white, the arrow disappeared, and the character turns and moves towards the correct direction at three progress dots (character movement). Note that there is no real-time control, so the character always moves in the correct direction even if the participant makes an error. To simplify movement decision, only one path was available to reach the following checkpoint. A total of three mazes were completed per participant, consisting of 44 trials on the first maze and 38 trials on the other two mazes. Traditional versus experimental approaches

The main difference between both paradigms is the gamification component. Participants were tasked with collecting cookies in numerical order and escaping a maze, which provided them with a general goal to achieve. In video games, this type of goal gives participants a sense of general purpose. Also, the character is shown moving in the correct direction after the user vocalized the word, providing immediate feedback. In video games, this feedback gives meaning to the users’ actions. Note that if the correct word is thought, participants receive positive feedback by seeing the character moving in the corresponding direction. If the imagined word is incorrect, participants receive negative feedback by seeing the character moving in another direction, pushing participants to correct their errors. Either way, this feedback motivated participants to perform better. Additionally, video game elements such as progress dots and levels were provided to reinstate that progress is being made. Overall, gamified elements enhanced participant motivation and helped prevent fatigue during the task.

Using a gamified paradigm could lead to certain disadvantages in terms of mental dynamics. The participants were required to comprehend the game environment, assimilate the player position, and decide the correct response all before performing imagined speech. Colors, movement, arrows, and other game objects could overtax the visual feedback. However, with a gamified paradigm, we can divide this process into sections and retrieve only the imagined speech segment of each trial. This way, the video game features promote attention and imagined speech is performed at a specific segment of the video game.

The major issue of the EEG technique is that different brain signals superpose each other when they reach the electrodes. To improve signal spatiality, participants must focus on performing only the mental task. In the traditional paradigm, two main signals may superpose with imagined speech: sound processing and reading. The first extracted instance of each trial included the auditory cue. See Fig. 1. Auditory cues are particularly difficult to remove from imagined speech signals, given that they are processed in nearby brain regions. This paradigm also included a written word during imagined speech instances. This may lead to participants reading the word constantly, even if it was involuntary. Reading and saying a word are different tasks and thus produce different signals. The experimental paradigm eliminated both the auditory cue and the written cue. Instead, it provided visual cues that generated stimuli in regions distinct from speech areas and prevented the participant from reading the word. Nevertheless, participants may visually imagine the character moving in the desired direction while performing imagined speech, creating a superposition of signals.

Simultaneous tasks present a pitfall for EEG signal quality, as user focus is divided. The traditional paradigm relies on participants following a rhythm. If this additional task is performed incorrectly, it can lead to imagined speech instances containing just a fraction of the word, more than one repetition, or even lacking the presence of a word. These errors can increase the within-class variability. Additionally, the participant focus is divided between following the rhythm and performing imagined speech. This makes the imagined speech signal weaker and, thus, harder to identify. The experimental paradigm works towards avoiding this problem by giving each task a dedicated period T.

EEG signals must reflect a genuine brain process, especially those used for training BCIs. Speech is a natural process that has a clear purpose: communicating with others. This is done in a series of steps, starting with elaborating an abstract idea and assigning a word to it12. To reproduce these steps, the experimental paradigm includes a task at each trial: deciding the next move based on character direction and checkpoint location. This leads the participants through the natural language process of not only saying a word but expressing an idea with it. Furthermore, in this paradigm, participants perform imagined speech only once per instance, with the aim of mitigating habituation effects.

EEG experiments can present a significant challenge in identifying trial errors, given that there is no certainty that the correct task was performed. To identify errors, participants in the experimental paradigm were instructed to verbally articulate the imagined word. This allows researchers to easily identify error trials and thus remove them before training a classification model. The traditional paradigm presents no way of identifying errors; hence, all trials of this paradigm are assumed to be correct, which may lead to poor performance when training classification models.

Finally, the paradigm was intentionally structured to enable real-time character control with minor code adjustments, laying the groundwork for innovative future research and interactive applications.

Traditional paradigm

Participants were thereafter introduced to each paradigm accordingly and were encouraged to communicate any uncertainties about the experiment. For the traditional paradigm, a random set of trials were taken as a tutorial. Researchers made sure every prompt was said at least once in this tutorial. Participants were first asked to vocalize the written word each time they heard a beep, and to continue with the established rhythm two more times after the beep stopped. Having understood the paradigm functionality, we instructed them to perform imagined speech instead of vocalized speech. After this familiarization, participants proceeded to complete the traditional paradigm.

Experimental paradigm

A tutorial was displayed to instruct participants. This tutorial first introduced participants to the character, the goal of the game, and the prompts that can be used. Then, it introduced participants to the visual cues (color changes) that indicated the actions to be performed. Two different examples were shown for each command. Finally, participants completed four training mazes, following the previous instructions. In this training, participants learned to use the game by understanding their errors and getting feedback from the researchers. Then, they proceeded to complete the experimental paradigm. For every instance, the vocalized word was documented in a spreadsheet, as well as the corresponding error, if any.

After paradigm

After completing each paradigm, participants answered the Self-Assessment Manikin Test to study how the paradigm modified their emotional state. Data Records

The database comprises EEG recordings from 15 participants who completed two imagined-speech paradigms: a traditional paradigm and an experimental gamified version. For each participant, both raw and pre-processed data files are available. Raw data is provided in two formats: EDF-file for traditional paradigm and XDF-file for experimental one. For the traditional paradigm, EEG signals were acquired by using the OpenViBE platform and EDF-file is the standard format to store data. For the experimental paradigm, EEG signals were acquired by using Python through Lab Streaming Layer, where the available format to store data is XDF-format.

Pre-processed data is provided in SET-files, which is the standard format in EEGLAB where was carried out the pre-processing analysis. Such analysis included removal of gyroscope signals, visual inspection to exclude noisy segments, re-referencing to M1/M2, band-pass (1–100 Hz) and notch (60 Hz) filtering, and ICA-based artifact correction. EEG recordings include 24 channels (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T7, T8, P7, P8, Fz, Cz, Pz, Afz, CPz, POz, M1, M2), using FCz as reference and Fpz as ground, and sampled at 500 Hz.

Supplementary materials include questionnaires assessing internal representations (visual, verbal, orthographic), emotional states (Self-Assessment Manikin, before and after each paradigm), and user experience measures (measurement dimensions such as attractiveness, efficiency, novelty). This information is found in the spreadsheet file labeled “Questionnaire Answers.xlsx”. Additionally, vocalized speech responses were captured during the gamified paradigm and logged in spreadsheets, with erroneous trials flagged to avoid using them in training classifiers. This information is found in the spreadsheet file labeled “Error Quantification.xlsx”.

The database can be consulted in13 and is organized in the following four folders: (1) raw data from the traditional paradigm, (2) preprocessed data from the traditional paradigm, (3) raw data from the experimental paradigm and (4) preprocessed data from the experimental paradigm. Each folder has 15 EEG recordings (S1/, S2/, … S15/). Technical Validation

Questionnaires

The Self-Assessment Manikin Test provided us with knowledge of the emotional state of the participants before and after each paradigm. Considering the results of this questionnaire, it seems that the experimental paradigm elicits heightened levels of valence and arousal among the participants. In contrast, the traditional paradigm either sustains or diminishes these emotional dimensions. These findings suggest that the traditional paradigm might have a detrimental impact on participant fatigue.

The User Experience Questionnaire allows users to evaluate the paradigm. By analyzing Fig. 6, the experimental paradigm was better evaluated than the traditional one in all evaluation metrics: attractiveness, perspicuity, efficiency, dependability, stimulation and novelty.

EEG-based on ERPs

Event Related Potentials (ERPs) are EEG evoked responses that are time-locked to sensory, motor, or cognitive events. They represent the summed activity of many neurons firing in synchrony while processing information14. Thus, it is expected that imagined speech elicits a cognitive ERP in both paradigms. A grand average of the 22 EEG channels and the 15 participants are presented in Fig. 7 for the traditional paradigm and in Fig. 8 for the experimental one. The four imagined speeches used as control commands are displayed in each paradigm. According to previous ERP-based studies15,16, three components have been detected during imagined speech: N400, P300 and N200. N400 is a negative-going ERP component peaking around 300 and 500 ms, typically maximal over centro-parietal electrodes, and is classically associated with semantic processing and lexical access in overt language tasks, and internal generation of words or mental representations. N200 is characterized by a negative deflection around 150 and 350 ms, often seen in tasks involving early phonological. P300 is a positive ERP, peaking around 250 and 500 ms, typically over parietal or centro-parietal scalp regions. This component reflects stimulus evaluation, attention, or categorization processes.

As can be seen from Fig. 7, imagined speeches during the traditional paradigm reflected the N200 component. A slight N400 for the right command was elicited as well. That is, early phonological and internal generation of words were the primary cognitive processes involved in the traditional paradigm. In contrast, imagined speeches during the experimental paradigm reflected the P300 component. Namely, attention and evaluation of the imagined speeches were the primary cognitive processes in the experimental paradigm. In both paradigms, ERPs validate the existence of cognitive processes related to imagined speech.

Intra-participant classification

A simple classification model was trained to demonstrate the data viability to be classified. A dataset was created for each participant by extracting temporal features of each instance for every channel. The temporal features used were mean, standard deviation, kurtosis, skewness, and third-order moment. The data were split into 70% training data and 30% testing data. A random forest classifier was implemented. A grid search was performed to compare possible hyperparameters. The tested parameters were: 30 and 100 estimators, and a maximum depth of 2 and no maximum depth. The average classification of the traditional and experimental paradigms was 32.48% and 35.65%, respectively. Table 6 shows the classification accuracy for every participant for both paradigms. Limitations

Acquiring EEG signals presents challenges that are often beyond researcher control. One key issue is the inability to verify whether the word verbally expressed by the participant truly matches the word they initially imagined. In the experimental paradigm, it is assumed without direct confirmation, that the vocalized word reflects the one previously imagined. In contrast, the traditional paradigm lacks any mechanism to detect such mismatches, making these errors undetectable. These facts provoke different EEG pattens every trial, what could be diminishing the classification accuracy (Table 6).

Another significant limitation of EEG acquisition is the presence of noise that exceeds experimental control. This noise may result from involuntary movements, natural physiological processes (such as heartbeat or eye blinks), or external interference like line noise. Each paradigm presents its own limitations. The traditional paradigm requires participants to follow a rhythmic sequence, a task that many find difficult. The experimental paradigm, on the other hand, imposes a higher cognitive load. Within a single trial, the participant must monitor changes in the maze border color, decide on the appropriate word, perform imagined speech, and then vocalize the selected word. Although efforts were made to temporally separate these stages, the imagined speech signal may overlap with other concurrent cognitive processes. Furthermore, both paradigms can activate unrelated brain functions for instance, reading in the traditional paradigm or imagining character movement in the experimental paradigm.
