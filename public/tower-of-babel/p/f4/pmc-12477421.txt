Paediatric Epstein-Barr virus-associated rhabdomyolysis complicated by acute compartment syndrome treated with decompressive fasciotomy
Authors: Masamichi Koike, Masatoshi Oba, Koji Tanoue, Satomi Mori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEpstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a common virus that causes paediatric rhabdomyolysis. Acute compartment syndrome is a rare complication of rhabdomyolysis that can lead to severe consequences. We report the case of a previously healthy boy in early childhood with EBV-associated rhabdomyolysis complicated by acute compartment syndrome that required decompressive fasciotomy. The patient recovered without loss of strength, sensation or range of motion. This case report aims to focus attention on serious complications such as acute compartment syndrome that can occur with common viruses.Keywords: Paediatrics, Orthopaedics, Infectious diseases

Background

Rhabdomyolysis is a relatively common disease encountered by paediatricians that is most commonly caused by a viral infection, followed by other causes such as trauma, exercise and other inherited diseases.1,6 Viral infection accounts for more than one-third of paediatric rhabdomyolysis cases.2 Influenza, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and cytomegalovirus have been frequently reported as causes of rhabdomyolysis.2 Although compartment syndrome is a known cause of rhabdomyolysis, to the best of our knowledge, there are only seven case reports of acute compartment syndrome presenting as a complication of virus-induced rhabdomyolysis.7,13 This is the first case report of paediatric compartment syndrome with EBV that required decompressive fasciotomy.

Acute compartment syndrome is a severe complication that, if untreated, can lead to irreversible nerve and muscle injury with permanent loss of function and limb contracture or death. There is no definitive diagnostic test for acute compartment syndrome, and a standard definition of when compartment syndrome begins has not yet been established. This case report emphasises the value of prompt recognition and intervention of acute compartment syndrome.

Case presentation

A boy in early childhood with a 3-day history of fever was admitted to a secondary medical hospital for bilateral lower limb pain. Three days prior to admission, the patient experienced a fever as high as 40°C, difficulty walking and red urine. A review of systems revealed no history of drug ingestion, no exposure to similarly ill individuals, no ingestion of contaminated food, no travel abroad, no trauma and no exercise. Furthermore, the patient did not have a family history of muscle disease.

The patient appeared ill when he arrived at our hospital; however, he was not disoriented. He had a temperature of 37.5°C, blood pressure of 110/67 mm Hg, pulse rate of 126/min and respiratory rate of 24/min. The skin below the hip joint was taut and exhibited lustre and pallor. Passive extension of the lower extremities produced pain that was disproportionate to the clinical presentation. A thorough skin examination did not reveal any bites or stings.

Blood tests revealed the following results: creatine kinase (CK), 270 000 U/L; CK-myocardial band (MB), 498 U/L; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level, 4077 U/L; alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level, 940 U/L; and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, 4109 U/L. His electrolytes and kidney function were within the normal range. His leucocyte count was 9000/µL, with 33.5% segmented neutrophils, 2.5% band neutrophils, 60% lymphocytes, 2.0% atypical lymphocytes and 2.0% monocytes. The urinalysis revealed orange-yellow urine, 3+ occult blood in urine and a myoglobin level of 262 000 ng/mL. A microscopic urinalysis revealed fewer than four erythrocytes per high-power field.

Investigations

Because of the tension and colour change of the skin of the lower extremities (figure 1), our orthopaedist measured the compartment pressure using an A-line monitor at the bedside. The pressure of the anterior compartment of both lower legs was more than 60 mm Hg, and that of the posterior compartment was 30 mm Hg to 40 mm Hg. These findings indicated a diagnosis of acute compartment syndrome.

Significant swelling and skin discolouration of the lower legs prior to fasciotomy.

Treatment

Intravenous fluids of acetated Ringer’s solution containing 1% glucose were administered with the rate of 70 mL/hour and the total amount of 3000 mL/m2. The compartment pressure measurements observed in the operating room before fasciotomy were the same as those observed at the bedside (figure 2). Therefore, we performed fasciotomy of the four compartments of the lower leg using one medial incision and one lateral incision. When the lateral incision was made, the fascial tension of the anterior tibial muscle and peroneus muscle appeared to be relieved immediately, and the compartment pressure decreased to less than 20 mm Hg. Although the medial incision did not release the fascia tension of the gastrocnemius−soleus muscle complex, the deep posterior compartment pressure decreased to less than 20 mm Hg. The results observed in both legs were similar. Clear fluid was observed in the oedematous fascial planes. Necrotic areas were not observed. Intravenous cefazolin was administered on the day of fasciotomy and on postoperative day 1.

Outcome and follow-up

The EBV capsid immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G levels were positive. Additionally, the EBV DNA was positive, with 628 copies/μg. The multiple nasopharyngeal swabs were used to determine the rapid viral diagnosis, and the FilmArray test was negative for other respiratory viruses. The blood culture results were negative. A comprehensive metabolic panel and amino acid analysis did not yield any useful findings. An evaluation based on the clinical history and examination yielded findings that indicated a diagnosis of EBV-associated rhabdomyolysis complicated by acute compartment syndrome.

After evaluation and decompression, the areas were irrigated sequentially with normal saline two times per week for 1 month. Treatment comprising elevation of both legs and empirical antibiotic therapy was administered for 1 week. Within 1 week, the CK-MB, AST and ALT levels normalised. The serum CK and LDH levels normalised within 4 weeks.

On postoperative day 9, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) was applied to both legs (figure 3). After 36 days of NPWT, the wounds were completely covered with healthy granulation tissue, and NPWT was discontinued (figure 4). Two weeks later, complete epithelialisation of the wound was achieved. On postoperative day 50, the wounds were completely healed. Lower limb rehabilitation was performed for 2 weeks, and the patient was able to stand and gradually walk. The patient was discharged from the hospital 57 days after admission. Recurrence of rhabdomyolysis did not occur during the 1-year follow-up period.

Discussion

This is the first reported case of compartment syndrome associated with EBV in a child. A previously healthy boy in early childhood developed rhabdomyolysis complicated by acute compartment syndrome following a viral infection. However, due to prompt recognition and timely fasciotomy, he recovered without permanent sequelae.

Although a viral infection is the most common cause of paediatric rhabdomyolysis and several cases of EBV-induced rhabdomyolysis have been described in the literature,14,19 only seven cases of acute compartment syndrome presenting as a complication have been reported in the English literature (table 1).7,13 Skellett et al reported a previously healthy 8-year-old boy who died following rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome caused by influenza A. Cases of acute compartment syndrome secondary to a common viral infection are rare, but unrecognised acute compartment syndrome could leave patients with non-viable limbs requiring amputation or be life-threatening. We believe that the best determinants of good outcomes are early diagnosis and fasciotomy. In all reported cases, fasciotomy was performed on the first day of admission, and all patients except one made a complete recovery; one patient reported by Skellett et al had cardiac arrest while waiting to be attended in the emergency department and was already in multiorgan failure when he was transferred to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit. Therefore, if a child with a common viral infection complains of severe muscle pain or has difficulty walking, clinicians should be aware of the potentially life-threatening nature of compartment syndrome, and orthopaedic referrals and fasciotomy should be considered.

Most cases of virus-induced rhabdomyolysis complicated by compartment syndrome have involved worsening limb pain and swelling, and the patient was admitted to the hospital 3–7 days after symptom onset.7,13 Paletta et al reported the case of a patient admitted to the hospital 3 weeks after the onset of fever and sore throat.12 In that case, rhabdomyolysis and lower extremity compartment syndrome were caused by the influenza B virus.12 Because influenza virus symptoms usually last approximately 1 week, the patient may have had another infection before the onset of the influenza B infection. The period between the peak of common viral infection symptoms and recovery from that infection is typically 3–7 days. Therefore, it is important to recognise severe complications, such as compartment syndrome, experienced by patients with virus-induced rhabdomyolysis during this period.

The only effective treatment of acute compartment syndrome is immediate fasciotomy.20 If left untreated, irreversible nerve and muscle injury with permanent loss of function and limb contracture could be the result. However, acute compartment syndrome does not have a definitive diagnostic test, and the standard definition of when compartment begins has not been established. The normal pressure in a muscle compartment is less than 10 mm Hg, but there are no consensus recommendations on what pressure thresholds are best for fasciotomy.21 In contrast, the standard clinical signs and symptoms of acute compartment syndrome are pain and swelling, which are just as common in patients without acute compartment syndrome. Ulmer et al assessed whether published studies support basing the diagnosis of compartment syndrome of the lower leg on clinical findings of pain, paraesthesia, pain with passive stretch and paresis.22 He concluded that the probability of compartment syndrome increases markedly if three or more clinical findings are present simultaneously.22 Ramos et al suggested that directly measured compartment pressure values of children are useful data; however, fasciotomy should not be delayed for patients with obvious physical deterioration.23 In the case reported here, compartment pressure was elevated. However, if symptoms such as paraesthesia, severe swelling or pain—particularly severe pain with passive muscle stretching—are present, physicians should actively discuss the indication for fasciotomy with the surgical team, regardless of compartment pressure measurement.

Patient’s perspective

Patient’s mother: I still remember that day my son became ill so clearly. He, who had been doing fine until that day started complaining of unusual pain of his lower body. There was nothing I was able to do. I was perplexed at the rapid symptom change, and even bad thoughts crossed my mind. The time from examination upon admission to emergency surgery went by so fast that I felt like walking through darkness for a few days after the surgery until the day I saw my son’s smile. Even at his very young age, he did his best to receive the treatment and overcame the long hospitalisation on his bed. I was not able to imagine the day would come, when he would completely recover to his normal life. I’m deeply grateful to the doctors and other medical staff who were involved in my son’s recovery. I hope that my son’s experience will help to treat other patients in a similar situation.
