An analytical framework for nanohydroxyapatite evaluation by ICP-OES and quantification of hydroxyapatite-equivalent Ca and P in toothpaste formulations through multivariate optimization
Authors: Prabhakaran Mathiyalagan, Stephen Alocius, Deepapriya Velumani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) is widely incorporated into toothpaste as a biomimetic agent for preventive oral care, yet robust quantitative methods for determining its content in finished formulations remain limited. In this study, nHA was first characterized structurally and morphologically to establish a reference material. X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed high crystallinity consistent with pure hydroxyapatite. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) revealed rod-shaped nanoparticles with an average size of 22.36 nm and an aspect ratio of 2.25. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) further verified characteristic phosphate vibrational bands at 1023.25, 562.26, and 600.84 cm⁻¹. Following characterization, nHA was incorporated into a toothpaste formulation, and an analytical strategy was developed to quantify hydroxyapatite-equivalent content based on stoichiometric determination of calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). A 2³ factorial design was employed to optimize key instrumental parameters—nebulization gas flow rate, applied power, and plasma flow rate. Exploratory statistical tools, including ANOVA, Pareto charts and residual analyses, identified nebulization gas flow rate as the most influential factor. The optimized method was validated for linearity, sensitivity, accuracy, precision, robustness and matrix interference. Calibration curves for both standard and matrix-matched solutions showed excellent linearity (R² > 0.999). Limits of quantification were 25.16 µg L⁻¹ for Ca and 12.24 µg L⁻¹ for P. Spike recoveries ranged from 95% to 110%, confirming method accuracy. Because ICP-OES measures total elemental Ca and P after digestion, the method quantifies hydroxyapatite-equivalent content and does not distinguish nHA from other Ca/P-containing ingredients. Nonetheless, application to commercial toothpaste samples yielded results consistent with label claims, demonstrating the method’s suitability for quality control of hydroxyapatite-containing formulations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01843-9.

Introduction

Hydroxyapatite (HA), a naturally occurring mineral form composed of calcium and phosphorous with a chemical composition of Ca5 (PO4)3 (OH), has positioned as a promising active in oral care products. It is the primary constituent of human bones and teeth conferring rigidity and structural integrity. Its hexagonal crystal lattice enables ionic substitutions, enhancing its functional properties. HA is biocompatible and used in toothpaste for remineralization, sensitivity reduction, biofilm control, and whitening [1, 2].

In oral health, HA serves as a biomimetic agent, assisting in the remineralization of enamel, reducing dentin hypersensitivity, and preventing dental caries. Clinical studies have demonstrated that HA-containing toothpastes are as effective as their fluoride-based counterparts in preventing caries, with the added advantage of eliminating the risk of dental fluorosis [3]. This has led to the incorporation of HA in various oral care products, including toothpaste, mouthwashes, and gels [4].

Regulatory bodies have recognized the safety and efficacy of HA in dental applications including the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and it has been approved as an anticaries agent in countries such as Japan and Canada [5]. The increasing consumer demand for fluoride-free and natural oral care solutions has further propelled the inclusion of HA in commercial toothpaste formulations [6].

Nanohydroxyapatite is a synthetic form of hydroxyapatite, and it has been used extensively in medicine for various purposes because of its biocompatibility and osteoconductive properties [7]. In dentistry, nHA has gained popularity as a natural alternative to fluoride. A unique characteristic of nanohydroxyapatite is its particle size, measuring only a few nanometers in diameter. Owing to their minuscule size, nHA particles can easily penetrate the microscopic pores on the surface of tooth enamel. Once applied, these particles bond with the tooth structure and help rebuild any damaged areas by remineralization. This process not only strengthens the teeth but also creates a protective barrier against dental caries and sensitivity [8].

Depending on the synthesis method, synthetic HA can be customized in terms of purity, crystal size, and morphology to improve its effectiveness. Given the increasing prevalence of HA in dental products, accurate quantification of its content in formulations is paramount for quality control and regulatory compliance purposes [9, 10].

Traditional analytical techniques, such as XRD and electron microscopy, primarily provide qualitative insights and may lack the sensitivity required for precise quantification. In contrast, ICP-OES emerges as a robust analytical method, capable of detecting and quantifying trace elements such as calcium and phosphorus with high sensitivity and precision with multivariate optimization. Unlike univariate optimization, which requires a large number of experiments that consume time and reagents, multivariate optimization is based on mathematical statistical tools to optimize conditions that involve many variables and the possibility of evaluating interactions between variables [11]. This study used a multivariate experimental design to maximize sensitivity for both Ca and P in digested toothpaste matrices, minimize matrix-induced signal suppression, and simultaneously optimize plasma operating parameters. This method allows for the direct calculation of nHA mass fraction when paired with a stoichiometric conversion framework (Ca/P molar ratio = 1.67). This quantitative connection was not previously established for commercial toothpaste formulations.

ICP-OES analysis of Ca and P in toothpaste is mostly available from dental research for enamel restoration and remineralization, but it lacks a thorough procedure and optimization. Although ICP-OES is frequently used for elemental quantification in food and pharmaceutical matrices, there are difficulties when using it to quantify nHA in complex toothpaste formulations such as matrix-induced spectral interferences from co-formulated Ca sources and the lack of a stoichiometric framework connecting elemental Ca/P ratios to nHA mass fraction in final products. Previous literature has not systematically addressed these limitations [12, 13].

In industrial practice, hydroxyapatite is added to the formulation and labelled as a certain percentage in the final product on the basis of the addition of raw material. This study aimed to establish a validated analytical framework employing multivariate optimized ICP-OES technology for the quantification of nano-hydroxyapatite in commercial toothpaste formulations, which was characterized earlier with different analytical techniques. This ensures product consistency, efficacy, and adherence to regulatory standards, ultimately contributing to enhanced consumer trust and oral health outcomes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Synthetic nano-hydroxyapatite powder (ingredient grade synthetic > 97%, from Ceramat), nitric acid (Suprapur, 65%), sulfuric acid (AR grade, 98%), hydrogen peroxide (AR grade, 30%), ICP multielement standard (Sigma Aldrich, Certipur) and water purified on a Milli-Q system from Millipore (IQ7003) were used in this study.

Characterization of nHA

Phase composition: XRD

The XRD patterns of the nHA powders from 20° 2θ to 60° 2θ were obtained via a Bruker D8 Focus, by copper anode (v-40kv and I-35 mA) as the X-ray source.

Functional group: FTIR

FTIR spectra of nHA powders from 400 to 4000 cm− 1 using Shimadzu IRAffinity-1 S, in transmission mode by Happ-Genzel apodization with 45 number of scans and a resolution of 2 cm− 1.

Morphology and microstructure: HRTEM

Transmission electron micrographs were obtained from crystals placed directly onto formvar- and carbon- coated nickel grids, and ultrastructural images of the crystals were recorded by TEM at an accelerating voltage of 80KV via an OEL JEM 2100 Plus.

Sample formulation

The formulation process was conducted internally, 2% w/w nHA powder was incorporated into the toothpaste formulation and served as a reference control sample and a placebo sample was also formulated without the addition of nHA.

Elemental analysis

Measurements were performed using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (make: Perkin Elmer, model: AVIO 220 Max, software: Syngistix software, version: 5.1.0.0293) with an autosampler system (make: Perkin Elmer, model: S23) according to the operating conditions (Table 1).

Multivariate optimization

The concurrent impact on plasma robustness resulting from variables, including nebulization gas flow, plasma flow rate, and applied power, was assessed using a 23 factorial design. Following the identification of the significant variables for the experiment generated by the 23 factorial design, Pareto and residual analyses were employed to ascertain the critical levels of these variables using Minitab® software, version 22.4 [14].

Validation

Validation of the optimized method was performed to evaluate linearity, matrix interference, accuracy, precision, limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ).

Linearity and matrix effects

The linearity from 5 mg L-1 to 50 mg L-1 (n = 5) was evaluated for Ca and P. The matrix effect was studied using matrix-matched and aqueous calibrations. The slopes obtained from both calibration curves were evaluated using statistical tests to determine the significance of the matrix effect.

Limits of detection and quantification

LOD and LOQ were derived from the equation LOD = (3* Blank RSD * BEC) /100 and LOQ = (10 * Blank RSD * BEC) /100, where BEC is the background equivalent concentration and it is calculated as the blank intensity divided by the slope of the analytical curve. The blank RSD is the relative standard deviation of the sample blank of 10 replicates [10].

Accuracy

The samples were spiked before the digestion stage, and the recoveries were evaluated to study the accuracy of the method. Recovery studies were conducted by introducing spikes into the placebo sample at concentrations of 5, 25, and 50 mg L− 1. The analysis was performed in three replicates, during which the intensity was recorded, and the assay along with the % RSD for recovery was computed.

Precision and Intermediate precision

Precision was assessed in terms of repeatability by analyzing the standard and reference sample solutions in six replicates each. On day two, the standard and reference sample solutions are analyzed in six replicates each to achieve intermediate precision under similar instrument operating conditions. The assay and the percent RSD were calculated, and the intensity was recorded.

Robustness

A thorough robust analysis of the method was conducted. The concentration of the acid added for digestion and plasma flow was slightly altered. The percent variation was computed by comparing the results with the precision result.

Measurement uncertainty (MU)

The MU of the analytical method was determined using the validation data in accordance with the EURACHEM/CITAC guideline. To determine the MU, the uncertainty source was identified, and each component was assessed separately.

Sample analysis

The validated method was employed for the quantification of nHA in commercially available toothpaste samples. In this study, commercial toothpaste samples were gathered from different brands that claimed and labelled the presence of nHA, and these samples were analyzed according to the optimized method, with the results quantitatively reported.

Results and discussion

XRD

The crystalline structure of nHA was investigated using X-ray diffraction, and the patterns were recorded in the 2-theta range of 20° to 60°. Diffraction was also used to identify the crystalline phases of nHA. The crystalline phase was identified by comparison with International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) Powder Diffraction File (PDF) data bank, where only one crystalline phase was used as an indicator of the purity of the nHA. Phase analysis conforms with the reference model of pure hydroxyapatite ICDD PDF No. 09- 0432 and is in full agreement with the corresponding values reported for hexagonal HA and no distinctive peaks of impurities such as calcium hydroxide and calcium phosphate were observed, which resemble the samples of phase-pure hydroxyapatite [15].

The crystallographic characteristics of the crystals were determined from the diffraction pattern data (Fig. 1). The more intense reflections observed in the diffractogram correspond to 31.839° (100%), 32.026° (81.2%), 33.044° (47.7%), 25.678° (41.5%), and 46.772° (23.7%), and their d values and FWHM readings are listed in (Table 3). Furthermore, the Scherrer equation [16] states that the crystallinity increases as the FWHM decreases, and we can see that the FWHM of all reflections is narrow. The lattice parameters c ≈ 6.93 Å and a ≈ 9.39 Å are extremely similar to those of typical hydroxyapatite. The crystallite size using the Scherrer equation, without instrument correction (K = 0.9), is consistent and falls between 25 and 60 mm. The pattern exhibits no broad amorphous halo around 20–35°, the peaks are sharp (FWHM ~ 0.17–0.29°) for major peaks, and the peak positions match standard hydroxyapatite reflections, confirming the nanocrystalline structure [17].

HRTEM

Hydroxyapatite particles tend to aggregate, resulting in distinct individual particles that are challenging to capture using electron microscopy. Nevertheless, we utilized contrast imaging in transmission electron microscopy to adjust the focus on individual particles, thereby making their outer boundaries visible for the effective measurement of length and width. As illustrated in the electron photographs, the nHA crystals exhibited a typical prism needle-like morphology (Fig. 2). Other calcium phosphates have different crystal morphologies than HA crystals do; depending on the phase, OCP, DCPD, TCP, and DCPA have plate-like morphologies with different sizes [18]. The diameter of the nHA was consistent and uniform, with dimensions significantly shorter than the length in all instances. Histogram analysis indicated that the sample contained a substantial fraction of similarly sized particles, ranging from 15 to 50 nm in length and 10–25 nm in diameter, with an average aspect ratio of 2.253 (Table 4). The crystallites did not exhibit significant variations in size or shape; rather, elongated, needle-shaped nanoparticles predominated, with an average length of 35.9634 nm and a diameter of 16.8681 nm. The measurements and calculated ratios conform to the guidelines established by the SCCS for oral care ingredients. This safety evaluation is applicable solely to hydroxyapatite (nano) with the following characteristics: - it is composed of rod-shaped particles, of which at least 95.8% (by particle number) have an aspect ratio of less than 3, and the remaining 4.2% have an aspect ratio not exceeding 4.9 [19].

When the TEM and XRD data are compared, they show a directional orientation and mutual consistency with nanocrystalline, rod-like hydroxyapatite. When combined with XRD crystallite size from strong mid-angle peaks (≈ 30–40 nm), TEM length (≈ 36 nm) and diameter (≈ 17 nm) are in the same tens of nanometers range, which is good agreement for actual materials. TEM length of 36 nm versus XRD of ~ 30–40 nm indicates that rods are nearly single-crystalline along their long axis [20].

FTIR

The FTIR spectrum (Fig. 3) displays all characteristic absorption bands associated with the hydroxyapatite structure. The prominent bands at 1023, 962, and 562 cm⁻¹ correspond to the ν₃ (asymmetric stretching) and ν₁ (symmetric stretching) vibrational modes of the phosphate group (PO₄³⁻) and exhibit maximum intensity (Table 5). The characteristic bending modes (ν₄) of the phosphate group were observed at 600 cm⁻¹. The well-resolved doublet at 600 and 563 cm⁻¹ is indicative of a well-crystallized hydroxyapatite phase, consistent with the clearly defined absorption bands and high degree of crystallinity observed in the spectrum. Minor impurity bands were identified at 877 cm⁻¹, attributable to HPO₄²⁻ ions, and a weak band at approximately 1418 cm⁻¹ was assigned to a trace amount of carbonate (CO₃²⁻), with a recorded intensity of 0.72 [21, 22]. Since B-type substitution is the most common form of carbonate incorporation found in biological enamel and dentin, the lack of a B-type carbonate substitution band—where CO₃²⁻ replaces PO₄³⁻ in the apatite lattice—is crucial to dental hydroxyapatite. This substitution, which takes place at 2–8 weight% in biological apatites, directly affects physicochemical properties by decreasing crystallinity, decreasing crystal size, and increasing solubility—parameters that control tooth mineral remodeling, turnover, and therapeutic responsiveness. The apatite lattice is structurally disrupted by B-type substitution, which results in smaller nano-sized crystals with needle-like or rod-like morphology and lower crystallinity. This is typically accompanied by a slight increase in the c lattice parameter and decrease in the a lattice parameter [23].

The nebulization gas flow rate influences the analyte residence time and aerosol formation. High nebulization gas flow rates encourage the formation of aerosols with tiny droplets, which favor the desolvation process and improve ionization, atomization, and excitation. Both the analyte residence time and the plasma process efficiency decrease when the nebulization gas flow is too high. Low nebulization gas flow rates increase the analyte residence time, but they also intensify the self-absorption phenomenon, which reduces the emission of the analytical signal [25].

From the residual plots (Fig. 6) of the factorial design, the normal probability plot shows that the data points fall close to the linear line, suggesting that the residuals are approximately normal. The versus fit plot shows the residuals against the fitted values. Randomly scattered points with no discernible pattern, centered around zero, indicate that the model is significant. The histogram shows the distribution of the residuals, and the bell-shaped curve indicates a normal distribution. The versus order plot shows that the residuals are independent as the points are randomly plotted throughout the graph in a nonlinear fashion, which indicates that the model assumptions are likely met in the order in which the data were collected. It is used to check for time-dependent patterns or trends in the residuals [26].

From the statistical analysis, the optimized instrument conditions with a nebulizer flow rate of 0.5 L min-1, a plasma flow of 12 L min-1, and a power of 1500 watts were selected and given the highest intensity response for calcium, and are correlated with the significance through ANOVA, Pareto and statistical analysis. With these optimized conditions, the method parameters were studied extensively.

Wavelength selection is one of the major areas that is directly related to the intensity response. The chosen wavelengths must be free of spectral interferences, have signal levels suitable for the concentrations of the corresponding elements in the sample, and be reachable by both the dispersive system and the detector. Typically, most instrument software can provide recommended wavelengths for each element, and multiple wavelengths are listed in the order of preference, on the basis of intensity, background, and common interferences. In the initial development process, three wavelengths for Ca (315.887, 317.933, and 393.366 nm) and P (178.221, 213.617, and 214.914 nm) were selected to evaluate the application for the toothpaste sample. The intensity response for the standard 5 mg L− 1 for Ca at 317.933 nm was the highest, followed by 315.877 and 393.366 nm, whereas the response was greatest at 213.617 nm and lowest at 178.221 nm for phosphorous, and the interference and background noise were very minimal at higher intensity wavelengths [27]. Since the expected Ca and P concentrations in digested toothpaste samples fall well within the mid to high mg L⁻¹ range after dilution, the radial view is chosen despite its lower sensitivity because it provides a high linear dynamic range and improves the instrument’s stability with high matrix samples. Because radial viewing configurations are more resilient to spectral and physical interferences than axial view, they perform better in these challenging matrices. Radial viewing offers a more stable and interference-resistant configuration suitable for digested toothpaste matrices because it does not require ultra trace detection [28].

The efficiency of different digestants was studied, and it was evident that the nitric acid digestant produced a high-intensity response for both Ca and phosphorous and showed better digestion suitability on the matrix with less interference and precipitation. In contrast, the combination of nitric acid and hydrochloric acid caused some precipitation and showed interference with the spectrum at a specific wavelength. Compared with nitric acid plus hydrochloric acid, the combination of nitric acid plus hydrogen peroxide had better results in terms of interference and precipitation, but the intensity response for both Ca and P was lower than that of nitric acid digestion for the sample and standard [29]. Nitric acid effectively dissolves many metals, and the resulting nitrates are all soluble, which prevents precipitation issues and causes far fewer spectral and chemical interferences than other common acids do. It is a strong oxidizing agent that helps to break down organic bonds in samples and creates stable solutions suitable for long-term storage without significant degradation [30]. Linearity and matrix effects

Sample transport or nebulization may be impacted by matrix interference between the toothpaste samples and calibration standards, such as density and viscosity. Matrix-matching calibration was used to examine the impact of the matrix to address these physical interferences. Five-point calibration curves were obtained for both the solvent calibration and matrix-matching calibrations to confirm the linearity and matrix effects. The developed method was linear in the predetermined concentration range of the calibration standards, according to a linear regression analysis of the calibration curve. Based on the anticipated sample concentrations within the method’s working range and LOQ, the calibration range of 5 to 50 mg L-1 was tested. In both calibration curves, linearity calibration produced correlation coefficients for Ca and P that were greater than 0.999. The slope and regression of the curves were compared using the F-test and t-test; no significant difference was discovered at the 95% confidence level, suggesting that the matrix effect was minimal. Consequently, aqueous standards were used for calibration in all subsequent experiments. The calibration linearity slope’s t-value of 142.6 denotes a high degree of statistical significance, indicating an extraordinarily strong correlation between concentration and response. Measurement uncertainty

In compliance with the GUM framework, measurement uncertainty (MU) was calculated using the bottom-up approach, where uncertainty contributions were methodically divided into Type A and Type B components. The statistical analysis of repeated measurements was used to assess type A uncertainty, specifically capturing variability related to method precision (repeatability). Non-statistical sources, such as contributions from volumetric glassware calibration, gravimetric measurements, stock standard preparation, and instrument-related factors, were used to estimate type B uncertainty components. The combined standard uncertainty (UC) was obtained by propagating each individual uncertainty component and combining them in quadrature in accordance with the law of propagation of uncertainty. Assuming a normal distribution, the expanded uncertainty (U) was then computed as U = k × UC, where k = 2 is the coverage factor corresponding to a roughly 95% confidence level. The validated method’s expanded uncertainty was found to be 9.87%, which ICP-OES deems acceptable for routine quantitative analysis [32].

Conclusion

The goal of the current study was to create a reliable analytical method for measuring the hydroxyapatite equivalent content of toothpaste formulations that contain nano hydroxyapatite. Before being added to the formulation, the raw nHA material was thoroughly characterized using XRD, FTIR, and HRTEM to confirm its crystallinity, chemical identity, and nanoscale morphology. These analyses establish the structural attributes of the starting material; however, they cannot be directly extrapolated to the finished toothpaste following digestion. The structural information is naturally lost when the formulation is broken down for ICP OES analysis, leaving only elemental Ca and P that can be measured. Consequently, the ICP OES assay is not specific to nano hydroxyapatite as a chemical entity; rather, it is designed to measure total elemental Ca and P and reflects the hydroxyapatite equivalent content. The optimized ICP OES method showed outstanding analytical performance, including high linearity, low quantification limits, and strong recovery values, despite this intrinsic limitation. Nebulization gas flow rate was found to be the most significant parameter by the factorial design approach, allowing for increased signal sensitivity and stability. Application of the validated method to commercial toothpaste samples produced results consistent with declared Ca/P-based claims, supporting its suitability for routine quality control where elemental quantification is required.

Overall, the method provides a reliable and analytically rigorous means of determining hydroxyapatite-equivalent content in toothpaste formulations, which can be assured supportive for quality control and claim substantiation.
