Standard and microbiology-focused oral hygiene instructions improve oral health knowledge and beliefs of young adults when provided in group settings
Authors: Mohammed A Almawiri, Aaron J Bonham, Joshua J Thomson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComprehensive dental care includes health education strategies that highlight the importance of disease prevention and reach as many people as possible. Bacteria are central to most oral diseases, yet few studies have explored the impact of including specifics of microbial pathogenesis on oral health education. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to determine if oral hygiene instructions (OHI) provided to undergraduate students in a group-setting positively impacted oral health knowledge and beliefs, and whether integration of microbiology-focused instructions and activities further improved these oral health measures.MethodsA pre-test/post-test experimental study was conducted among undergraduate life-science students. Participants were allocated to the control group, (n=35) receiving Standard OHI (SD-OHI) and the experimental group (n=40) receiving Microbiology-focused OHI (MICRO-OHI). Each group completed questionnaires before and after the educational intervention for measurement of changes in oral health knowledge, beliefs, and behaviors.ResultsDescriptive statistics were conducted on the pre-test/post-test responses for SD-OHI and MICRO-OHI. Both groups showed improved responses on items related to oral health knowledge and beliefs and high agreement with statements on intention to adhere to routine oral hygiene practices. Microbiology-related items had significant improvement in the MICRO-OHI group compared to the SD-OHI group (p<0.05). Qualitative analysis of responses to “Why should we brush our teeth?” revealed that a higher percentage of MICRO-OHI responses added emphasis on bacteria/biofilm removal compared to SD-OHI.ConclusionsBoth methods of group-based instruction increase health-promoting responses and the addition of microbiology-focused instructions has the potential to uniquely augment comprehensive oral healthcare strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09021-y.Keywords: Oral health, Health education, Oral hygiene, Public health, Microbiology, Patient education

Introduction

Oral diseases are among the most prevalent health conditions, affecting approximately 3.5 billion individuals worldwide according to the most recent Global Oral Health Report [1]. If left untreated, oral diseases can cause significant discomfort including impaired chewing, reduced appetite, sleep disturbance, and negatively impact overall general health [2]. One strategy suggested to combat the continued and growing prevalence of oral diseases involves implementation of oral health promotion programs with an emphasis on improving oral health literacy [3]. Health literacy refers to one’s ability to not simply learn, but also process and apply relevant health information for the purposes of prevention or management of a disease/condition [3]. It has been noted that low health literacy is linked to increased difficulty in understanding health-related content as well as its application in the form of positive health behaviors [3]. This is consistent with the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice model [4] which postulates that an increase in knowledge may lead to changes in attitude, in turn resulting in the adoption of favorable behaviors.

Oral health education is instrumental in improving oral health literacy and the eventual application of behaviors more favorable for disease prevention. Oral hygiene instructions (OHI), where recommended oral hygiene practices are described and demonstrated, are most commonly provided by dental healthcare professionals to patients in a one-on-one clinical setting. While education through OHI in this manner is important, time constraints and perceived patient disinterest during clinical appointments may hinder proper utilization of demonstration methods for increasing patient understanding of oral hygiene practices [5]. Additionally, the reach of education during dental appointments is restricted, as it must be given one-on-one and requires that the recipient has already established a dental home. OHI in this manner does not always fit disease prevention models since many patients’ first visit to a dentist occurs as a result of the onset of disease, even though recommendations are for all children to have an established dental home by age one [6, 7]. Therefore, to remedy the issues of time constraints during clinic visits, patient disinterest, access to care, and the need for oral hygiene education prior to disease onset, more broad reaching public health strategies involving engaging oral health education need to be directed at the public.

School-based education has been implemented to reach a broader audience, albeit with varying levels of achieved oral health literacy and clinical improvement [8–14]. While school-based education may increase oral health literacy in school-aged children, studies have shown that routine adherence to regular dental checkups drastically decreases after the age of 15 [15–17]. The variance in effectiveness of school-based education and the decreased adherence to recommended disease prevention practices as people age signal a need for alternative approaches to educational outreach.

Providing oral health education in group settings can reach a broader audience but may become less engaging than one-on-one interactions in a dental office. Methods to increase engagement in these settings would be required for better retention of information and positive changes in oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behavior. The inclusion of visual aids in health promotional initiatives has consistently yielded improvements in health literacy outcomes, especially in individuals with low health literacy [18]. The importance of visualization has also been demonstrated by incorporation of microbiological activities among primary school children. This enhanced their understanding of the bacteria involved in dental decay and reinforced the importance of preventive practices [19]. Similar results were obtained in studies where specific microbiological activities were included as part of OHI and given to pregnant mothers [20] and caregivers [21]. Together, these studies suggest that inclusion of microbiological activities as part of oral health education programs could not only increase oral health literacy but ultimately lead to the adoption of positive health practices.

In this study, we aimed to further address the impact of providing group-based OHI to young adults and the educational potential of incorporating microbiological information and engaging visual aids into standard OHI. We hypothesized that group-based educational interventions would increase students’ oral health knowledge, positively impact oral health attitudes, and increase intention to maintain recommended oral hygiene behaviors in the future. Additionally, we hypothesized that incorporation of microbiology into OHI would lead to a more in-depth understanding of the pathogenesis of oral disease and of the mechanisms by which routine oral hygiene practices aid in the prevention of oral disease.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was classified as exempt by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Detroit Mercy (IRB# 22-23-20) and was carried out in compliance with the ethical guidelines established by the Helsinki Declaration. Documentation of informed consent was waived.

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Experimental design and study participants

This study aimed to determine the effectiveness of two oral health educational sessions: Standard Oral Hygiene Instructions (SD-OHI) and Microbiology-focused Oral Hygiene Instructions (MICRO-OHI). A pre-test and post-test with control group quasi-experimental design strategy [22] was utilized in groups of undergraduate students to assess the impact of the two educational interventions on oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors.

Undergraduate students enrolled in four upper-level biology laboratory courses at the University of Detroit Mercy were invited to participate in the study during course time. Students in these courses had completed at least their first year of courses in a Biology/Biochemistry Degree program. SD-OHI and MICRO-OHI sessions were each randomly assigned to two of the laboratory courses.

Participation was voluntary and students were allowed to remain in the educational session regardless of participation, or not, in the study. The research information sheet along with confirmation of informed consent were included as the first question of the pre-test questionnaire. If a student did not wish to participate in the study, they would select “No” on the first question and it would take them to the end of the questionnaire. Participants were informed of the voluntary nature of the study and that they could cease participation in the study at any time by closing their browser without completing the questionnaire. Students that did not wish to participate did not have to remain in the class.

An overview of the sequencing of the pre-test, educational session, and post-test, along with the sections that were completed in each questionnaire, is shown in Fig. 1. Once the study overview was presented to the class, students completed the pre-test questionnaire. After completion of the pre-test, the educational session was presented by author JJT. Each educational session lasted approximately 30 min. Immediately following the session, participants completed the post-test questionnaire. Participating students used their own phone, tablet, or computer, to complete questionnaires that were hosted online using Qualtrics XM software (Provo, UT, USA).

Educational sessions

During the SD-OHI educational sessions, slides were presented that contained information covering the appropriate methods of oral care for disease prevention and the topics that would be covered during routine OHI at a dental appointment. Concepts discussed in the presentation included descriptions of dental diseases (caries, gingivitis, periodontitis), dental plaque with disclosing solution images, role of plaque in dental disease, and descriptions of oral hygiene practices (brushing, interdental care, fluoride, diet, and regular dental visits for prevention and/or treatment).

During the MICRO-OHI educational sessions, the SD-OHI slides were presented with the addition of specific microbiology components of dental disease and prevention. The microbiology information was included after a description of dental diseases and this included illustrations and electron micrographs of bacteria that make up dental plaque, acid-producing bacteria as a component of dental plaque, and specific description of Streptococcus mutans and how it contributes to caries initiation. Following presentation of this information, a microscope activity was performed to show bacteria from the tooth surface. Here, a swab from the presenter’s teeth was smeared onto a microscope slide, stained with Crystal Violet stain, and observed under oil-immersion. View through the eyepiece of the microscope was shared through a projector to the entire class. Visualizing the microscopic bacteria from teeth was used as a platform to discuss that bacteria from teeth are removed during oral hygiene practices and that they re-colonize tooth surfaces throughout the day. During this discussion, the presenter made sure to specifically discuss that these oral hygiene practices aim to prevent disease by maintaining low levels of the bacteria that cause disease within plaque biofilms, rather than the goal being to remove all bacteria.

Questionnaire sections

The pre- and post-test questionnaires used in this study were developed for use in this study (Supplementary File 1).

Current Oral Health Behaviors (COHB) was a 12-item section of the questionnaire included to assess self-reported oral hygiene practices of the participants prior to the educational session. Items in this section assessed brushing, flossing, mouth rinse use, fluoride exposure, sugar consumption, and frequency of dental visits. This section was included in only the Pre-Test questionnaire.

Oral Health Beliefs (OHB) was a 12-item section used to measure a participant’s personal perception of oral hygiene and beliefs on the influence of external forces/conditions on oral hygiene. Items in this section utilized 5-point Likert-scale type questions wherein a score of [1] represented “strongly disagree” and [5] “strongly agree”. This section of the questionnaire was answered in both the Pre-test and Post-test questionnaires.

Oral Health Knowledge (OHK) was an 18-item section intended to assess the participant’s understanding of routine oral hygiene practices recommended to prevent dental disease, pathogenesis of dental diseases, and the relationship between oral and systemic health. These items varied in format including open-ended free response, multiple choice, and Likert scale-type questions. This section of the questionnaire was answered in both the Pre-test and Post-test questionnaires.

Microbiology Knowledge (MICRO) was a 6-item section used to measure a participant’s understanding of the bacteria and bacterial processes or characteristics involved in the process of dental decay. Items varied in format including multiple choice, multiple answer, and 5-point Likert scale. This section of the questionnaire was answered in both the Pre-test and Post-test questionnaires.

Future Oral Health Behaviors (FOHB) was a 3-item section geared towards assessing a participant’s intentions to adopt recommended oral health behaviors. Two items in this section were matrix style questions that utilized a 5-point Likert scale, and one question was multiple-choice. This section was included in only the Post-test questionnaire and was intended for use as a metric of the effectiveness of each interventional program to instill intention for adhering to positive health behaviors.

As the final section of the post-test questionnaire, participants were given the option to respond to demographic questions.

Statistical analysis

The data in the present study were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and SAS software, Version 9.4 of the SAS System for PC (Copyright © [2023] SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Prior to analysis, incomplete responses, including responses without completed post-test, were removed. Descriptive statistics were carried out to determine frequencies and percentages of responses for all sections in both tests. Inferential analysis of pre-test and post-test items was conducted using independent sample t-test, Chi-square, Fisher’s Exact Test, repeated-measures ANOVA, McNemar-Bowker test, and multivariate mixed logistic models. Tests used are listed in tables. Analyses were performed using alpha = 0.05 to determine statistical significance.

Responses to open-ended questions were qualitatively evaluated using thematic analysis. Themes and subthemes were identified from the responses of both groups. Participant responses could include any combination of themes and subthemes as present.

Demographic composition was calculated based on answers provided by participants at the end of the post-test (Table 1). For items analyzing participant’s OHK, a significant interaction effect was found for two items: K5 “Fluoride helps to protect teeth from decay.” and K8 “Using a toothpaste containing fluoride, compared to one without fluoride, decreases chances of cavities.” (p < 0.05). Level of agreement in the responses of the MICRO-OHI group increased more dramatically than those of the SD-OHI group to the OHK items ‘fluoride helps protect teeth from decay’ (p = 0.036) and ‘using toothpaste with fluoride compared to one without reduces chances of cavities’ (p = 0.043). There was also a statistically significant effect over time for both groups on OHK items K1 “Plaque can reform on teeth after brushing and flossing within 12 hours.”, K2 “Children do not need to see a dentist until they start school.”, and K9 “People who smoke are more likely to have cancer in their mouth.” (p < 0.05).

Analysis of MICRO questions revealed that the SD-OHI group had more dramatic increases in level of agreement with the item M2 “The oral environment plays important roles in the prevention and development of dental caries” (p = 0.042), compared to the MICRO-OHI group. Likert scale items, M3 “To what extent do you understand the relationship between bacteria and cavities (tooth decay).” and M4 “To what extent do you understand that you can reduce cavity-causing bacteria in your mouth with good oral hygiene practices like brushing, flossing, and regular dental cleanings.”, both showed significant time effects meaning participants in both groups significantly increased their level of agreement following the educational session compared to pre-test (p < 0.05).

Table 4 contains responses to OHK and MICRO categorical items before and after the educational session. K16 “How often should you clean in between your teeth (flossing, etc.) to best prevent oral disease?” was the only item with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.023) between the two groups before the educational sessions, where more than 20.0% of participants in the pre-MICRO group responded that toothbrushing after every meal is recommended, while only 2.9% responded similarly in the pre-SD group. While there was no significant difference in response rate to item M5 “Which type of bacteria is most associated with causing dental decay (cavities)?” between pre-SD and pre-MICRO, there was a significant difference in the responses following the educational session. In particular, 90.0% of post-MICRO participants selected Streptococcus mutans as the bacterial species most associated with causing dental decay, compared to only 51.4% in the post-SD group.

Discussion

Effects of group-based OHI

The first goal of the study was to determine if group-based OHI was effective in positively impacting OHK, OHB, and behaviors. The findings in this study revealed that group-based OHI, both Standard OHI (SD-OHI) and Microbiology-focused OHI (MICRO-OHI), led to increases in OHK, positively impacted OHB, and increased participants’ intentions to maintain recommended oral hygiene behaviors in the future. Significant increases in agreement in post-test compared to pre-test statements on OHB and OHK were recorded for both SD-OHI and MICRO-OHI (Table 3). Specifically, there was significant positive impact in post-test responses for both groups on items K1, K2, K5, K9, B1, B9, and B10. Education on each of the above statements was delivered in the SD-OHI portion of presentations given to both groups, so the changes observed in knowledge and beliefs were likely due to reception of this information in the group-based setting. The ability to increase oral health knowledge in group settings observed in this study is consistent with studies in different age groups [23–25].

Pre-test reporting of current oral hygiene behaviors indicated potential for improvement as 25.3% reported brushing once or less than once a day and 48% that floss sometimes or never floss. Following the educational intervention, participants in both groups had very strong agreement with statement E2 “The educational session helped me to better understand the need for good oral hygiene practices.” (Table 7). This was supported with strong agreement from both groups in the post-test reporting of intentions to follow recommended oral hygiene practices such as brushing teeth twice a day (97.3%), flossing once at least once a day (93.3%), and receiving regular cleanings as recommended (97.3%). These comparisons shows the efficacy of the educational programs on the willingness to adopt recommended behaviors for oral disease prevention. While this study did not follow the participants into the future, the positive agreement with these intentional statements is an indicator towards behavior change [26].

The qualitative analyses of the open-ended question, “Why are we supposed to brush our teeth?“, provide further support to the conclusion that group-based OHI was effective (Table 5). The goal with this open-ended question was to assess whether the responses describe “what” brushing is specifically doing in terms of dental disease prevention, rather than the overarching “why” of having good oral health. Participants’ pre-test responses primarily emphasized theme (1.0) Oral Health and its subthemes as the reason for brushing our teeth, with few responses containing theme (2.0) Removal, indicating the role of brushing teeth in preventing disease. In the post-test, responses including theme (1.0) Oral Health decreased in both groups. A decrease in inclusion of theme (1.0) was expected as the focus of the OHI presentation was on what each of the different oral hygiene practices specifically accomplishes in order to prevent disease, rather than more general responses about its impact on overall oral health. This change in response content was reflected in the post-test as theme “2.0 Removal” and the subthemes of “(2.1) Plaque” and “(2.2) Bacteria/Biofilm were increased within both groups. The shift from general to more detailed explanations on the direct function of brushing, as an integral disease-prevention activity, underscores the positive effects of the educational session across both groups.

This study also highlighted that group-based OHI presentations could impact participant beliefs on topics that were not specifically addressed in the presentations. One OHB item, B9 “Water containing fluoride is safe to drink.”, had some of the lowest pre-test averages falling between “No Opinion” and “Agree” on Likert scale responses for SD-OHI (M = 3.4) and MICRO-OHI (M = 3.3). On post-test for both groups, responses had significantly higher levels of agreement with the statement. Interestingly, specific information on the safety of fluoride in water supplies was not addressed in either presentation. However, the mechanism by which fluoride works to enhance remineralization of enamel and decrease demineralization in low pH was discussed in both sessions. These findings illustrate that simply having a discussion of how something works to protect teeth was able to influence participant beliefs on its safety elsewhere. The changes in beliefs regarding the safety of fluoride from this study highlight the importance of receiving information from trusted sources, considering inconsistent fluoride information can cause confusion [27], while corroborating previous studies that measured improvements in patients’ attitudes or knowledge regarding the use of fluoride in oral hygiene practices to protect teeth following educational interventions [9, 10, 28, 29].

Effects of microbiology-focused instructions

The second goal of this study was to determine if the inclusion of microbiology-focused information in the educational intervention could lead to a better understanding of the disease mechanisms and necessity of routine preventative oral hygiene practices. Some items indicated a more detailed understanding of disease pathogenesis in the MICRO-OHI group. In particular, the MICRO-OHI group self-reported a significantly greater level of agreement with item E1 “The educational session helped me to understand the relationship between bacteria and cavities (tooth decay)” compared to the SD-OHI group after intervention (Table 7). Additionally, while both groups exhibited an increase in level of agreement after educational intervention with items regarding fluoride, K5 and K8, there were significantly larger increases in agreement in the MICRO-OHI group post-intervention (Table 3).

There were also significant differences between the two groups in responses to oral microbiology knowledge items, M5 and M6 (Table 4). M5 asked the multiple-choice question, “Which type of bacteria is most associated with causing dental decay (cavities)?”. The MICRO-OHI group significantly increased correct responses of “Streptococcus mutans” compared with SD-OHI. A similar result was observed for responses to the multiple answer item M6, that asked participants to select properties of bacteria that cause caries/cavities from a list of multiple choices, to test their understanding of the characteristics of the bacteria involved in the dental decay process. Specifically, there was a significant difference in selection of an incorrect property, “Stick to soft tissues”, between the two groups.

For open-ended responses to “Why are we supposed to brush our teeth?”, there seemed to be a larger increase from pre-test to post-test in the percentage of responses containing theme (2.0) Removal in the MICRO-OHI group (Table 5). More specifically, there was a higher percentage of responses that mentioned removal of specifically (2.2) Bacteria/biofilm instead of (2.1) Plaque in the MICRO-OHI group compared to SD-OHI. This is likely a byproduct of educational content received by the MICRO-OHI group, which was more detailed in terms of the microbiological aspect of plaque and disease pathogenesis. Although the detailed microbiological information was absent in the SD-OHI educational session, participants in that group nonetheless exhibited an understanding of the pivotal role of “removal” of plaque in the disease prevention process.

Among the explanations for these differences is that the emphasis on visualization through the microscope not only brought the bacteria from the tooth surface to light, but also further enhanced the learning process by directly engaging the participants. These findings are consistent with those of Mafra, Lima [19] who noted positive improvements in primary school children’s understanding of the bacteria involved dental decay after engaging them in microbiological activities. In our study, participants were young adults and still displayed increases in microbial knowledge, even though they did not perform the microscope activity individually. Similar results have been observed by both Thomson et al. [20] and Angarita-Diaz et al. [21], among pregnant mothers and caregivers, respectively. These studies, along with this study, together suggest that inclusion of microbiologically-focused information and activities, as part of oral health education initiatives, can not only increase oral health literacy, but lead to personal statements of intention to adopt recommended oral health practices.

Limitations of the study

The findings of this study should be considered with the following limitations. First, the study population was comprised of undergraduate biology students, which may have introduced sampling bias, and also made it difficult to determine the full extent to which the interventional program was successful in achieving the desired oral health knowledge outcomes. The baseline level of each domain in the study groups, as assessed in the pre-test questionnaire, was quite high/positive, most likely due to the population studied. However, as mentioned prior, there were still significant differences measured in certain areas that highlighted knowledge and beliefs that are still not fully realized, even in this population. While this is a limitation in this study, the group-based instruction did work to increase knowledge and could be an effective strategy to increase knowledge in similar age groups, as it has been shown that there are low levels of oral health knowledge and adherence to oral hygiene practices in first-year undergraduates [30].

The second limitation to this study is the length and nature of the pre-test and post-test questionnaires. The questionnaires used are yet unvalidated tools created for this study. They were lengthy and there is the possibility of fatigue effects that could have impacted post-test responses, especially in the open-ended responses [31]. Additionally, question format may have impacted results. Multiple-choice question format makes it possible to guess the “correct” response without having any true knowledge or in certain instances with minimal background knowledge on the subject matter. However, in the case of this study, the qualitative analyses of open-ended questions supplemented the descriptive data by not only providing additional confirmation but also showcasing how the intervention positively affected participants’ understanding of essential oral health practices. The differences between both, no matter how large or small, are readjusted when the qualitative analysis is taken into account, as the format of question aids us in determining the extent to which their understanding was truly affected.

Future directions

Additional testing in non-biology major populations is needed to fully ascertain the extent to which inclusion of microbiological information and activities can be useful in enhancing oral health knowledge and consequently lead the adoption of positive oral health behaviors. Target audiences for this type of group-based instruction should focus on populations with low oral health knowledge and high risk of oral disease, in particular families with low socioeconomic status and first-time mothers and their children [32]. This study should be utilized as a reference point for further research of a new approach to providing Oral Health Education (OHE), which incorporates methods that have been empirically tested regarding effective delivery and presentation of health information in a way that directly engages participants and enhances their learning process. The main challenges faced in this process are the translation of detailed or “scientific” knowledge and more importantly presentation of such knowledge in a way that matches the participants learning style. This study was premised on the idea of “seeing is believing” which was at the essence of microscope activity. As an extension of this model, future studies should develop OHE programs that meet multiple styles of learning and monitor longer term retention of knowledge gained, as well as evaluation of oral health outcomes.

Summary

This study has shown that educational interventions geared towards adults in group settings can be effective at increasing understanding of dental diseases and preventative measures, even when the target audience has a strong educational baseline in the area assessed. While traditional OHI information significantly increased OHK and OHB of participants, the inclusion of Microbiology-Focused information and activities was able to significantly impact responses to some questions reflecting participant knowledge, which was verified through quantitative and qualitative analyses. Although improvements were registered after the interventional programs on the domains of Oral Health Beliefs and Knowledge, and participants stated intentions to follow recommendations for routine oral hygiene practices, it remains unclear whether this will translate into sustained behavioral changes on the participants’ end and prevention of future disease. Consequently, future research should utilize the above-listed study as a reference point for assessment of this educational intervention on various clinical parameters, along with assessment of retention of information presented in the group setting. This would provide useful information on the effectiveness of incorporating MICRO-OHI as part of standard dental care.
