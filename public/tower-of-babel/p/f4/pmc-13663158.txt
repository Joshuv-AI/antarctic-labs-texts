Single-stage total correction of severe aortic stenosis and dysphagia lusoria via a single extended median sternotomy: a case report
Authors: Dahong Moon,, Se Jin Oh, You Jung Ok, Yong Won Seong, Hyeon Jong Moon, Jae Hwa Jeong, Jae-Sung Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aberrant right subclavian artery (ARSA) is the most common anomaly of the aortic arch and is usually asymptomatic. In a minority of patients, ARSA may cause dysphagia due to esophageal compression (dysphagia lusoria). The coexistence of symptomatic ARSA and severe aortic stenosis requiring surgical treatment is extremely rare, and the optimal management strategy remains unclear. A 43-year-old woman presenting with acute decompensated heart failure was diagnosed with severe aortic stenosis and concomitant dysphagia lusoria. Following medical stabilization in the intensive care unit, she underwent a single-stage total correction. To minimize surgical trauma and avoid multiple incisions, a conventional median sternotomy was slightly extended into the right supraclavicular region, serving as a single unified incision. Through this approach, transposition of the ARSA to the right common carotid artery was performed first, followed by mechanical aortic valve replacement with bovine pericardial patch repair of a focal annular defect secondary to decalcification and primary closure of a patent foramen ovale. Postoperatively, valve hemodynamics improved markedly, and dysphagia resolved completely, leading to a significant recovery of the patient’s nutritional status. This case demonstrates that a single-stage, single-incision approach is a highly effective and viable strategy, providing valuable clinical insight into the management of these combined rare pathologies.

Introduction

Aberrant right subclavian artery (ARSA), or arteria lusoria, is the most common congenital anomaly of the aortic arch, with a reported prevalence of 0.2–3% [1, 2]. Although most individuals remain asymptomatic, some develop dysphagia due to esophageal compression, a condition known as dysphagia lusoria. Surgical intervention is generally reserved for symptomatic cases, with reimplantation or transposition of the vessel being widely accepted techniques [3–6].

While treatment strategies for ARSA and severe aortic stenosis (AS) are well established individually, their coexistence is exceptionally uncommon and poses a challengein surgical planning. We report a case of severe AS combined with dysphagia lusoria successfully managed using a single-stage surgical approach. By slightly extending a conventional median sternotomy incision, we achieved total correction, aiming to provide valuable insight into the surgical management of this rare clinical scenario.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old woman with a history of congenital heart surgery in early childhood presented to the emergency department with acute dyspnea and diaphoresis. Although previous surgical records were unavailable, her own statement and a faded left thoracotomy scar strongly suggested a prior patent ductus arteriosus ligation. Due to her hearing impairment, communication was conducted in writing. On arrival, she was hypoxic (oxygen saturation of 84%) and decreased mental alertness. Despite supplemental oxygen, her respiratory status deteriorated with progressive hypercapnia, necessitating endotracheal intubation (Fig. 1). Chest radiography demonstrated pulmonary edema with bilateral pleural effusions. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed very severe AS with a peak velocity of 7.4 m/s and a mean pressure gradient of 137 mmHg, accompanied by moderate to severe aortic regurgitation and severe concentric left ventricular hypertrophy. Valve morphology suggested a heavily calcified unicuspid or bicuspid configuration. Computed tomography (CT) angiography demonstrated an ARSA arising from the proximal descending thoracic aorta and coursing posterior to the esophagus. On detailed questioning, the patient reported longstanding dysphagia, consistent with dysphagia lusoria. Her body weight and body mass index were 40.0 kg and 16.0 kg/m2, respectively.

After clinical stabilization, elective surgery was undertaken. To avoid the morbidity of multiple separate incisions, a conventional sternotomy was performed, incorporating a curvilinear extension into the right supraclavicular region to allow simultaneous access to the mediastinal and cervical structures (Fig. 2). Initially, a surgical plane was developed between the carotid sheath laterally and the trachea/ esophagus medially just above the sternal notch. Deeper dissection identifies the ARSA posterior to the esophagus. The vessel was followed proximally toward its origin from the descending thoracic aorta. To minimize bleeding during this cervical dissection before full heparinization for cardiopulmonary bypass, a reduced initial dose of systemic heparin (5,000 unit) was administered. The ARSA was ligated near its origin, secured with a Hem-o-lok clip and reinforced suture ligation, and subsequently divided. The distal segment was extensively mobilized to achieve sufficient length for a tension-free reconstruction. The right common carotid artery was then exposed and controlled proximally and distally. Following vascular reconstruction, standard cardiopulmonary bypass was established. Aortic cross-clamping was performed, and the native aortic valve was exposed. The valve was heavily calcified with a unicuspid configuration. During aggressive removal of deep-seated annular calcium, a localized tissue defect occurred in the posterolateral annulus. This defect was promptly covered with a small bovine pericardial patch, which provided adequate support for secure implantation of a 21-mm mechanical prosthesis (On-X). Concurrently, primary closure of a patent foramen ovale (PFO) was performed and the patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass.

The postoperative clinical course was uneventful. Postoperative echocardiography demonstrated a well-functioning prosthetic valve with a marked reduction in the transvalvular mean pressure gradient (from 137 mmHg preoperatively to 16 mmHg postoperatively). At the 1-year follow-up, the patient’s dysphagia had completely resolved, with her Mellow–Pinkas dysphagia score improving from Grade 2 to Grade 0. Due to the resolution of the esophageal obstruction, her oral intake improved significantly, resulting in a weight gain of 5.5 kg and excellent recovery of her nutritional status.

Discussion

In adult patients presenting with dysphagia, vascular causes are rarely considered early, as more common gastrointestinal or functional etiologies tend to be prioritized [7, 8]. This can often delay definitive diagnosis, particularly when symptoms are longstanding and nonspecific. In this case, CT angiography was pivotal in identifying the hidden vascular abnormality and linking it to the patient’s symptoms [9, 10]. Postoperative 3D-CT further confirmed the successful transposition and patency of the aberrant vessel (Fig 3). There was an intraoperative event that aortic annular defect occurred during excision of the heavily calcified aortic valve. Her aortic valve was a congenitally malformed unicuspid valve characterized by deeply embedded calcium nodules. Aggressive debridement of this calcium was required to ensure stable and secure prosthetic valve seating, which consequently led to an annular defect. The defect was confined to the posterolateral annulus and was reconstructed using a bovine pericardial patch before prosthetic valve implantation. Postoperative echocardiography and one-year follow-up demonstrated satisfactory prosthetic valve function without paravalvular leakage or annular complications. cc.

Regarding the ARSA management, the proximal stump was secured using both a Hem-o-lok clip and reinforced suture ligation. The clip served as an adjunct rather than the sole means of closure method. Given the high systemic pressure at the aortic origin, combining mechanical clip with a transfixion suture provide secure closure and minimizes the risk of clip migration or pseudoaneurysm formation, as confirmed on follow-up CT angiography.

Symptomatic dysphagia lusoria is generally treated surgically. Various techniques have been described, including simple ligation of the aberrant vessel, carotid–subclavian bypass, and transposition or reimplantation of the aberrant subclavian artery to the carotid artery [3–6]. Among these, transposition or reimplantation is currently favored because it relieves esophageal compression while preserving upper extremity perfusion. Although each condition has a well-established treatment pathway, there is a paucity of clinical guidance regarding their combined management. A staged approach (e.g., initial sternotomy followed by a later thoracotomy, video-assisted thoracoscopic incisions, or separate cervical incisions) might appear procedurally less demanding but subjects the patient to multiple anesthetic events, cumulative surgical trauma, and delayed symptomatic relief.

A single-stage strategy, conversely, addresses both pathologies simultaneously. In our approach, the vascular reconstruction was performed prior to full heparinization for cardiopulmonary bypass. This facilitated secure proximal control of the aberrant vessel and minimized risk of deep intrathoracic bleeding near the descending thoracic aorta. The single-incision approach provided excellent exposure for both procedures without the morbidity of separate incisions. This case suggests that the described strategy may be a viable option in selected patients. In addition to the restored valve hemodynamics, the patient experienced immediate and complete resolution of her dysphagia, leading to a clear improvement in her nutritional status. This underscores that correcting the cardiac lesion alone would have failed to address her primary, long-standing functional complaint.

This case highlights the importance of considering vascular anomalies in the differential diagnosis of unexplained dysphagia. More importantly, it demonstrates that single-stage correction of severe AS and ARSA via a single extended sternotomy is technically feasible and highly effective.

This report has several limitations. As a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with combined aortic valve and arch anomalies. In addition, direct comparison with a staged approach was not possible, and longer-term follow-up is required to confirm long-term patency of the carotid-subclavian anastomosis and durability of the annular reconstruction.
