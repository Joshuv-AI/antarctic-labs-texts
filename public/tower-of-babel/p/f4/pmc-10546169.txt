Index hospital cost of adverse events following thoracic surgery: a systematic review of economic literature
Authors: Daniel Jones, Srishti Kumar, Caitlin Anstee, Molly Gingrich, Alexander Simone, Zubair Ahmadzai, Kednapa Thavorn, Andrew Seely
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAdverse events (AEs) following thoracic surgery place considerable strain on healthcare systems. A rigorous evaluation of the economic impact of thoracic surgical AEs remains lacking and is required to understand the value of money of formal quality improvement initiatives. Our objective was to conduct a systematic review of all available literature focused on specific cost of postoperative AEs following thoracic surgery.DesignSystematic review of the economic literature was performed, following recommendations from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement.Data sourcesAn economic search filter developed by the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health was applied, and MEDLINE, Embase and The Cochrane Library were searched from inception to January 2022.Eligibility criteriaWe included English articles involving adult patients who underwent a thoracic surgical procedure with estimated costs of postoperative complications. Eligible study designs included comparative observational studies, randomised control trials, decision analytic or cost-prediction models, cost analyses, cost or burden of illness studies, economic evaluation studies and systematic reviews and/or meta-analyses of cost analyses and cost of illness studies.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts in the first stage and full-text articles of included studies in the second stage. Disagreements during abstract and full-text screening stages were resolved via discussion until a consensus was reached. Studies were appraised for methodological quality using the Critical Appraisal Skills Program checklist.Results3349 studies were identified: 20 met inclusion criteria. Most were conducted in the USA (12/20), evaluating AE impact on hospital expenditures (18/20). 68 procedure-specific AE mean costs were characterised (USD$). Introduction

Adverse events (AEs, defined as a deviation from the expected recovery from surgery) are common following thoracic surgery.1 Despite the improvement in perioperative patient management and refinement of surgical technique over the preceding decades, patients continue to experience a substantial burden of AEs, producing both short-term and long-term sequalae. Evidence supports a reduction in quality of life (QoL), prolonged hospitalisations with intense resource utilisation2 and impaired postoperative recovery associated with AEs.3 Furthermore, the association of earlier cancer recurrence following AEs4 and its link to increased mortality5 6 behove the thoracic surgeon to minimise any deviation from expected postoperative care.

Worldwide, lung cancer and oesophageal cancer represent leading causes of cancer mortality.7 Both malignancies are traditionally managed by thoracic surgeons, where surgical resection, in combination with preoperative and/or postoperative radiation, chemotherapy and immune therapy, is the mainstay of curative-intent treatment. In a North American context, the estimated need for surgery involving either lung or oesophageal cancer is rising, accounting for 1 in 10 procedures performed in high-income countries.8 Despite a substantial improvement in early detection as well as the increased recognition of the molecular machinery governing oncological changes within lung and oesophageal cells, patients often present to clinical attention with cancer in advanced stages, this being an independent marker for AEs.9 Our ability to minimise AEs in this context has increasingly been viewed as a measure of quality of hospital care, prompting international thoracic surgical societies to focus on harmonised data collection regarding AEs and perioperative outcomes, in order to allow for benchmarking and quality improvement (QI).10 11

Despite economic pressures placed on overburdened healthcare systems, to our knowledge, a rigorous evaluation of the economic impact of thoracic surgical AEs is lacking. Such an evaluation is required to understand the value for money of formal QI initiatives. Investment in QI requires the ability to calculate a return on investment, which requires an understanding of costs of AEs.12 Thus, given the far-reaching human and economic consequences of AEs and the need to justify investment in QI initiatives, the aim of our study is to perform a systematic review of the economic literature to assess cost associated with all AEs following thoracic surgery. Our study findings would highlight knowledge gaps in the literature, allowing future research into ways to reduce AEs following thoracic surgery.

Methods

Our systematic review follows recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.13 The protocol for the systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021260975), with data access available on request. There were no deviations from the registered protocol. The following PICO (population, intervention, comparison and outcomes) was used for our systematic review: Population (patients who have undergone thoracic surgery); Intervention: (patients who experienced AEs following surgery); Comparison (patients who did not experience AEs following surgery); Outcome (costs associated with AEs after surgery).

Information sources

The electronic search strategy was developed using an iterative process in collaboration with the research team and an experienced information specialist. An economic search filter developed by the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health was applied to identify relevant economic studies.14 The databases searched included Ovid MEDLINE Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process & Other Non-Indexed Citations and Ovid MEDLINE and Embase Classic+Embase. In addition, the Cochrane Library (HTA database, NHSEED, DSR, DARE and CENTRAL), Wiley version, were also searched. These databases were searched from inception to 6 January 2022. Details regarding the full search strategy are provided in online supplemental material 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they meet the following eligibility criteria (table 1): (1) adult patients who underwent a thoracic procedure and (2) studies describing or estimating costs associated with postoperative complications after a thoracic procedure. Eligible study designs included comparative observational studies, randomised control trials, decision analytic or cost-prediction models, cost analyses, cost or burden of illness studies, economic evaluation studies and systematic reviews and/or meta-analyses of cost analyses and cost of illness studies. Grey literature was not included in the search strategy. Only studies published after 1999 until January 2022 were included in the review and only English language studies were included. Studies published before year 2000 were not included due to economic and market variations. We included all operative approaches including an open, minimally invasive or hybrid approach. The outcomes of interest were total or subdivided costs associated with postoperative complications after a thoracic procedure.

Screening and selection

Screening of studies was performed in two stages. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts in the first stage and full-text articles of included studies in the second stage. The primary reason for exclusion of studies was recorded at the stage of full-text screening. The study screening process is only continued if there is an observed per cent agreement of over 80% agreement in study inclusion. Disagreements during abstract and full-text screening stages were resolved via discussion until a consensus was reached. Data abstraction and data collection process

Data were extracted by one of two members of the research team and then verified by the other member. Data abstracted from each study included study characteristics (location and respective currency, study design, sample size, length of follow-up), population characteristics (age, sex, procedure), costing methods (perspective, cost components, method to estimate costs, adjustment for confounding) and results (total cost, subdivided costs).

Methodological quality assessment

Studies were appraised for methodological quality using the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) checklist (online supplemental material 2). The checklist evaluates studies on issues of internal and external validity of study findings based on 12 questions.15 For each question, responses include ‘yes’ ‘no’ and ‘can’t tell’. Additionally, the definitions used for costs and analytical methods adopted to estimate attributable costs of complications were recorded; however, no comparison was performed across studies on analytical methods due to heterogeneity in study populations and cost definitions.

Synthesis

Results were reported narratively and in the form of tables. To facilitate comparison across studies, cost data for each included study was reported in 2020 US dollars (USD$). If necessary, costs were converted to US dollars using purchasing power parity for the year of included study and adjusted to 2021 costs based on the US consumer price index inflation calculator.16 17 Mean cost (and ranges) was reported for each complication by index procedure type. Mean costs and ranges for each complication were estimated based on costs reported across all applicable studies.

Patient and public involvement statement

None.

Results

Literature search and screening

The search yielded 3349 unique records after deduplication. After title and abstract screening, 280 studies met the inclusion criteria at the full-text screening stage. Of these, 260 were excluded as these did not describe costs associated with postoperative AEs following a thoracic surgery procedure. In total, 20 studies were eligible for inclusion in our study (figure 1).

Study characteristics

A summary of characteristics of the included studies is presented in table 2. All included studies were designed as retrospective cohort studies, and majority of studies (55%) were conducted in the USA. A large proportion of studies focused on patients who underwent lobectomy (35%) and esophagectomy (30%). On average, the age of patients in the included studies was 66 (55–73) years and the proportion of men varied between 5% and 92%.

Overview of study methods

All but one study (19/20)18 adopted a hospital perspective, assessing inpatient costs of care associated with postoperative complications. Most of these studies (15/20)19–33 focused on costs incurred during the index admission or within 30 days from date of admission. Four18 34–36 studies considered hospital costs incurred over a 90-day follow-up period, and one37 study estimated the annual hospital costs associated with postoperative complications.

Studies estimated costs using a gross-costing or a microcosting approach. A gross or top-down costing approach allocates a total budget to specific categories such as ‘per-inpatient day’ or ‘per-clinic visit’. Costs for each category are then estimated as a product of resource use and unit costs. Microcosting entails enumerating all inputs used in an intervention and deriving the total cost by assigning a unit cost to each input and aggregating them.38 Inputs could include labour (eg, nurse time) and capital (equipment, facility space). Microcosting is the foundational principle of activity-based costing where resource use is identified and cost for a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive activities and aggregated to estimate total cost.39 Five18 25 26 29 35 of the included studies used an activity-based costing approach to estimate costs. These studies incorporated both direct (personnel time, supplies, equipment) and indirect (building overhead, cleaning) hospital costs associated with an encounter.

Ten19–24 27 28 34 36 studies were based on reimbursement data on charges obtained from claims databases. Charges typically represent the amount that is billed by hospitals for services; however, this differs from costs, or the actual expenses that are incurred in the production of hospital services, such as personnel wages, supplies and utility.40 Only seven19 20 24 27 28 34 41 of these studies applied hospital-specific cost-to-charge ratios to convert charges to costs.

Almost all studies (17/20)18–22 24 25 27–29 32–37 41 estimated the attributable costs of a complication as they adjusted for confounding. Multivariate regression analysis was the most common approach used (15/20)18 20–22 24 25 27–29 33–37 41 to account for confounding, while two19 32 studies applied a propensity score-matching technique. Studies typically adjusted for patient demographic characteristics (age, sex) and baseline comorbidities in their analysis. Few studies (6/20)18 20 27–29 36 also accounted for co-occurring complications in the estimation of attributable costs for a postoperative complication. Two26 31 studies compared costs for patient cohorts with and without a complication without any adjustment for confounding, while one30 study did not specify the method used to compare cost data.

Quality appraisal

Results from the quality assessment showed the variability in study quality across the eligible studies (online supplemental material 3). All included studies provided a clear statement of aims, description of patient population and outcomes considered (CASP checklist item 1). For all included studies, the eligible patient population was identified using standardised definitions for index procedures and postoperative complications (CASP checklist item 2, 3). The outcome (ie, costs of postoperative complications) was measured using appropriate definitions in 12 of the included studies. For the remaining studies, it was unclear if the reported costs reflected the actual resources used by the hospital to provide services or charges to users (CASP checklist item 4). Seventeen studies applied appropriate statistical techniques such as regression analysis and propensity score matching to adjust for confounding (CASP checklist item 5b). Of these, seven studies reported having adjusted for baseline demographic characteristics and comorbidity (CASP checklist item 5a). None of the included studies provided information on lost to follow-up (CASP checklist item 6a). Based on clinical expert opinion, following patients until discharge from index admission or a follow-up period of 30-days was considered sufficient to capture the costs associated with managing postoperative complications after a thoracic surgery. All included studies followed patients at minimum for either 30 days or until discharge from index admission (CASP checklist item 6b). For CASP checklist item 7, a study was indicated as ‘yes’ if it reported any results on costs associated with postoperative complications. For CASP checklist item 8, study was marked as ‘yes’ if the study reported results on the mean or median incremental cost of a complication and associated uncertainty (95% CI/SD/SE/ range). Of the included studies, results were considered valid for 11 studies based on research design and analysis methods used (CASP checklist item 9). Of the included studies, 11 were based in the USA and were considered generalisable to the local Canadian population (CASP checklist item 10). For CASP checklist item 11, a study was considered to fit with existing evidence if reported results were aligned with those reported in studies focusing on similar target population and outcomes. We were unable to assess whether results of a study fit with available evidence due to substantial heterogeneity across studies in population and reported complications. All included studies were considered to have implications for practice (CASP checklist item 12).

Discussion

We report a first systematic review of the costs attributable to postoperative AEs following thoracic surgery, including individual and organ-related categories of AEs. We quantified cost of 68 procedure-specific AEs; the most common procedures included esophagectomy, lobectomy, gastrectomy and tracheostomy formation. Costs varied widely from thousands to multiple tens of thousands of dollars for individual postoperative AEs. The variation in costs represents differential severity of hospital resources required to address the AE as well as some heterogeneity in complication definition and the use of hospital-level cost estimates compared with actual charge/reimbursement. The costs for AEs remain highly significant.

Costs of AEs following esophagectomy were the most substantial and again highly variable. Oesophageal AL following esophagectomy is one of the most dreaded of such complications, associated with a reduction in a QoL, a prolonged length of stay and increased mortality. Our systematic review revealed that the cost of an AL was estimated on average to be $40 037 (USD). Furthermore, a diagnosis of sepsis following esophagectomy added huge costs ($31 671) as did renal failure ($42 368 per event). Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) palsy was also associated with a high cost of $27 517. It should be noted that this cost does not include longer term complications from sequalae ranging from dysphagia, recurrent aspiration pneumonia and psychological problems linked to RLN palsy. The increasing incidence of adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus must dictate further evolution of the thoracic surgical approach to this scourge including; improved surgical technique, better post-operative support as well as fundamental research.

Costs of AEs following lobectomy were also substantial and again highly variable. Our review identified the most expensive postoperative complications following lobectomy to include sepsis ($36 930), cerebrovascular complications ($33 322), emphysema ($31 296) and venous thrombosis ($28 474). Worldwide, lung cancer represents the leading cause of cancer-related death with surgery remaining the mainstay of treatment for early-stage disease.42 Despite modern advancements in management, our understanding of lung cancer and the treatment modalities remains incomplete, and the global burden of lung cancer represents a major barrier to increasing life expectancy globally.43 Postoperative complications following lung resection represent an additional obstacle to improving patient care, as AEs are common and have a profound effect on the patient outcomes.44

Our review has highlighted specific and costly AEs, identifying potential areas for value-directed QI interventions directed at optimal enhancement of quality of healthcare combined with reduction of costs. Our ability to minimise AEs has increasingly come to represent a marker of the quality of hospital care45; however, translating AE reduction into hospital savings provides a compelling argument for hospitals to support QI at the individual team or divisional level. As hospitals are commonly organised into departments and divisions, with teams of care providing similar services within divisions. Hospital-based or departmental systems for AE collection commonly focus on AEs common to all departmental disciplines (eg, surgical site infections for a department of surgery) and commonly lack the required fidelity to capture common thoracic-specific AEs such as prolonged air leak, arrhythmias, RLN injury and more.46 Thus, hospital support for individual teams-based QI may yield significant improvement in healthcare value, with improved quality and decreased costs. Furthermore, quality improvement for highly specialised physicians such as thoracic surgeons requires nationally or internationally coordinated quality networks to improve care47 as well as increased patient-focused care. Multicentre deployment of interventions focused on the most common and costly AEs will serve to enhance value-driven outcome care,48 and with ongoing follow-up and evaluation of response represents an integral component of a functional, responsive and resilient ‘learning’ health system. Additionally, the lack of studies involving a patient perspective, as revealed in our review, is concerning and warrants further investigation to help bridge the gap between QI learning systems and patient-centred healthcare outcomes.

Our systematic review analyses the available estimates providing a range of explicit, hospital costs attributable to postoperative AEs following common thoracic surgery. However, the overall cost of specific hospital acquired AEs and its relationship to societal cost, including outpatient and community costs, such as rehabilitation, home care services as well as the implicit costs to society, such as bereavement, work absence and income loss are not included, representing an important gap in our knowledge regarding the ‘true cost’ of AEs. Such costs are highly relevant and likely considerable, and when considered may better guide the provision of resources to support community-based care systems. Bridging hospital and community-level costs of AEs may support the delivery of high-quality care, enhance patient and provider satisfaction and diminish the cost transfer from hospital to community: a hallmark of value-based care.49 In addition, these costs are important for informing the value of surgical care as it can help determine the cost-effectiveness of surgical care, allowing for informed decisions regarding the allocation of healthcare resources on common and costly AEs. This will enable healthcare systems to generate return on investment in local or divisionally-based QI teams. The wide range of both incidence and cost in the examined publications indicate a difficulty in standardisation of clinical practice in the institutions studied. Further research more closely examining patient selection, perioperative care, technical and anaesthetic approach and team integration are all worthy of more intense research.

Limitations

There are several limitations to consider regarding the findings of our systematic review. The current research has identified gaps in the literature of important AEs; for example, no cost data were found in the literature for pleural effusion following esophagectomy (a common AE that requires additional pleural drainage procedures) and atelectasis following lobectomy. Attributable cost and possible sequelae are lacking. Further analysis and generation of models capable of identifying patients at risk for AEs will augment clinical experience while promoting a reduction in healthcare costs.

In addition, our study has identified a limitation regarding the measurement of exposure and outcomes. There was substantial variation across studies in the definitions used to identify postoperative complications. Furthermore, studies did not report costs of complications by grade of severity due to a small number of events. As a result, the wide range reported for the pooled mean costs of each complication reflects the heterogeneity in the definitions and severity of complications across included studies, limiting the accuracy of specific costs linked to specific AEs.

An additional limitation in our review relates to a narrow study perspective: all but one (95%) of studies examined only hospital costs associated with postoperative complications incurred during index admission. Studies that assessed costs over a longer follow-up period (90-day, 1 year) considered only hospital costs associated with the index and readmissions (table 4). Consequently, our reported costs of AEs represent an underestimation of the health system and societal (implicit) costs as they do not capture outpatient and out of pocket costs associated with management of postoperative complications.

Furthermore, we observed variability across studies in the definitions used to define costs and the robustness of methods used to account for confounding. Costs typically represent the number of resources required by the hospital to provide inpatient care services, while charges are the amount of money that the hospital reports on the bill and expects the patients and insurer to pay. From a USA perspective and setting, it is increasingly rare for the insurer to pay the full charges since Medicare, Medicaid and private insurers can obtain discounts of 50% or more. Charges can be translated into costs using hospital-specific cost-to-charge ratios. However, only some of the studies based on data on billed charges converted charges to costs using appropriate methods. Moreover, there was heterogeneity across studies in the methods used to adjust for confounding and the baseline clinical factors that were included in covariate adjustment.

Finally, our study pooled costs across studies conducted in a variety of settings. Our methodology for pooling costs across studies included calculating costs by way of standardising for differences in year of pricing and currency. However, there exist differences in healthcare systems across settings, which could not be adjusted for and may contribute to uncertainty in our findings.

Despite the above limitations, we found the included studies to be of good quality, with high scores on the CASP Cohort Study checklist indicating that they were conducted in a rigorous manner with few sources of bias. Despite minor variability in the study design and data collection methods, all studies had clear inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as appropriate measures of exposure. The findings of the included studies were generally consistent, although there were some differences in the magnitude and direction of the associations reported.

Conclusion

Our systematic review has revealed that hospital costs associated with postoperative AEs following thoracic surgery are substantial and variable, varying from thousands to tens of thousands of dollars depending on the AE. These costs are important for informing the value of surgical care as it can help determine the cost-effectiveness of the value of surgical care and making informed decisions regarding the allocation of healthcare resources to focus on common and costly AEs in order to generate return on investment in local or divisional-based QI teams. Our findings are significant in not only alerting policymakers to the economic burden of AEs but may also help mitigate expenditures, guide postoperative treatment pathways and promote measures to prevent the most costly and common complications. As such, our review has demonstrated that cost of AEs represents a significant but potentially modifiable clinical and financial target for perioperative care.
