Role of Genes in Dens Invaginatus: A Whole Exome Sequencing Study
Authors: Mary Grace, J Aarthi, Arun Elangovan, Madhan Chenchugopal, Sivakumar Gopalakrishnan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDens invaginatus (DI) is an abnormality of tooth structure that occurs due to invagination of enamel organ into the dental papilla before calcification. Dental anomalies have a diverse etiology, with contributions from both genetic and environmental factors that impact the dental lamina. The development of dental anomalies, including DI, could be significantly influenced by genes. Although the precise cause of DI is still unknown, it is thought to be caused by disruptions during the morphogenesis of teeth. Exome sequencing is a technique for genetic testing that can be used to identify genetic mutations or variations in genes that are important in specific diseases or conditions. The present study is the first of its kind to employ the method of whole exome sequencing (WES) to determine the genetic background of DI.MethodsBlood samples were collected from both the mother and child who had DI and analyzed using WES.ResultsA gene variant of KMT2D was identified as common in both mother and child, which could be a causative factor for DI. The variant KMT2D (c.15907C>T) was identified according to the analytic results obtained.ConclusionOur research advances knowledge towards the etiology of DI and to avoid the risk factors linked to it. Developing solutions for gene therapy and regenerative medicine may also benefit from an understanding of the process behind DI development.How to cite this articleGrace M, Aarthi J, Elangovan A, et al. Introduction

Invagination of enamel organ into the dental papilla causes a developmental anomaly of tooth structure named dens invaginatus (DI). In this condition, the portion of invagination starts from the crown structure and can extend into the root.1 DI has been described using various terminologies such as tooth inclusion, dilated composite odontome, invaginated odontome, dens in dente, and dentoid in dente.2 Various theories have been put forth in literature regarding the cause of DI. According to Kronfeld, invagination occurs when the inner enamel epithelium fails to develop, while the surrounding normal epithelium develops and covers the stationary region.3 Based on Rushton's theory, the invagination occurs when a portion of the internal enamel epithelium invades the dental papilla, proliferating quickly and aggressively.4 Oehlers' considered that, DI could occur when the enamel organ gets distorted and a portion of enamel organ protrudes, resulting in the formation of an enamel-lined channel which could eventually end at the cingulum or on the incisal tip.5,6 According to Atkinson, presence of external forces during the development of tooth germ is likely to cause DI.7

Based on the extent of invagination, DI can be of two types. The term “coronal type” refers to an invagination that starts in the crown and usually emerges from the palatal pit.8 These invaginations could lead to unusual variations in the structure of root canal.9 The radicular type of invagination is a rare condition that generally starts from the root surface.8 Altered tooth morphology and contemporaneous pulpal necrosis in an immature tooth might compromise root development. As a result, teeth with DI may have broad open or “blunderbuss” open apices, impeding endodontic treatment.1 Although several attempts have been made to classify DI,5,10–13 Oehlers' classification is the most commonly used system to classify DI.1,14 The prevalence of DI in adults has been reported between 0.3% and 10%.1,14 Ridell et al. stated that, the prevalence of types I and III is 15% and 5% and the prevalence of type II DI is 79%, respectively.15 The lateral incisors are the most commonly affected teeth, followed by the central incisors.16 The premolars and canines are the least affected. Various case reports have mentioned the presence of DI in mandibular teeth,17,18 supernumerary teeth,19 deciduous teeth,20–22 and multiple lesions in a single tooth.17,23,24 The significance of genetics in the development of DI is, however unknown.

When tooth development takes place, the growth molecules tend to regulate the folding and the enamel organ development.25 Tooth development can be disrupted if these genetically determined growth factors are lacking or modified.26–30 This theory strengthens the fact that DI could have a familial tendency.26 Literature suggests a possible association of DI with various genetically inherited anomalies.31–33

People with genetic mutations in PAX9, AXIN2, EDA, EDAR, WNT10A, WNT10B, EDARADD, LTBP3, LRP6, GREM2, SMOC, MSX1, AMBN, and COL1A2 have been seen to develop dental malformations such as oligodontia, amelogenesis imperfecta, anodontia, hypodontia, and dentinogenesis imperfecta, respectively.34–36 Various other developmental tooth anomalies, such as paramolar, enamel pearl, shovel-shaped incisors, talon cusp, Cusp of carabelli, and dens evaginatus (DE), have also been explored in order to rule out their genetic influence, which could be a reason for their occurrence. Shovel-shaped incisors are linked to EDAR gene variant that affects the size and shape of teeth.37 Talon cusp has been reported to be caused by BCOR (BCL6 Corepressor), since it has been observed in a family with oculofaciocardiodental syndrome.38 Candidate gene variants TLR3 and MDC1 have been identified in cases of DE.39

The genetic components connected to the development of DI have not yet been studied, despite the fact that autosomal and X-linked recessive patterns have been identified for this condition. Therefore, the current study used whole exome sequencing (WES) to examine the genetic basis of DI. The objective of this study is to determine the gene involved in the manifestation of DI.

Aim

To investigate the genetic background of DI using WES.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Sample Collection

The present study was conducted in in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Madha Dental College and Hospital, Kundrathur, Chennai, India. A male patient of age 11 years reported with the chief complaint of a fractured upper front tooth. Patient disclosed a history of trauma occurred while playing at his school 4 months before. On clinical examination, presence of DI was noted in the upper anterior teeth (11, 12, 21, and 22) (Fig. 1). Intraoral periapical radiographs of the 11, 12, 21, and 22 revealed Oehlers classification type I DI (Fig. 2).

In order to identify the role of genetic component in the inheritance of DI, the child's mother was examined. It was noted that the child's mother also presented with DI in her upper anterior teeth (11, 12, 13, 21, 22, and 23) (Fig. 3), which was classified as Oehler's type I following radiographic examination (Fig. 4).

In order to diagnose the role of genetics in the occurrence of DI a WES was planned.

WES Analysis

Blood samples (5 mL) of both the child (subject A-1) and mother (subject A-2) were collected in a VACUETTE® K2EDTA 6 mL tubes. These tubes contain dipotassium K2EDTA, which blocks the coagulation cascade, and are stored in −150°C until further use.

From the obtained blood samples, DNA was extracted. To enrich areas of importance from fragmented genomic DNA, exome sequencing of the clinically significant protein-coding regions of the approximately 22,500 human genes—of which more than 99% of regions are in the CCDS, RefSeq, and Gencode databases, was utilized. To prepare the DNA sample, 1.0 μg of genomic DNA was utilized as input material. The exome panel kit was used to construct sequencing libraries in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The libraries were sequenced using a high-throughput sequencing technology to a mean coverage of >80–100x. The study followed the Guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (MDCH/IEC/2024/20).

Data Analysis

The genome analysis toolkit (GATK) best practices framework was used to identify variations in the sample after the raw data was generated. The raw data quality was checked first using fast QC, and then Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA) read aligner was used to map and align the data to the human reference genome, GRCh38. The GATK Haplotype Caller technique was employed for variant calling following alignment. Utilizing SnpEff software, the variations were annotated. The minor allele frequency (MAF) of variations was annotated using the 1000Genome, gnomAD, dbSNP, and 100K Genome Asia databases. All probable nonsynonymous variations were annotated and functionally predicted using the dbNSFP database.

Discussion

To the best knowledge of the authors, this study is the first of its kind to identify the genetic factors linked to DI using WES. The presence of DI in both the child and parent could be more than just coincidental, and hence this genetic study was planned to investigate the same.

The results of WES analysis revealed the presence of genetic variant KMT2D gene in both individuals, indicating the likelihood of genetic inheritance of DI. The possible role of KMT2D gene in the enamel organ formation and initiation of tooth development could be due to its distinct expression in odontoblast and ameloblast layers during the late bell phase, suggests its role in the late stages of tooth development also.40 Through the process of in situ hybridization, the KMT2D mRNA gene was also discovered in the bud and cap stages of human dental epithelial cells during tooth development. Furthermore, the study also proposed that the aberrant expression of KMT2D may impair its functions and signaling in the dental epithelium, as well as have an epigenetic effect, which would affect the development of human teeth.41 An ectoderm-specific KMT2D conditional knockout (cKO) mouse model was used to investigate the role of KMT2D in amelogenesis (Lee et al.). These findings provide insights into the epigenetic mechanism that regulates amelogenesis, highlighting the critical role of KMT2D in normal enamel development.42 The findings of the study conducted by Pang et al. demonstrated that KMT2D was mostly expressed in the dental epithelium during the bud and cap stages in tooth germs of mouse, whereas the mesenchyme displayed very weak signals. It was reported that KMT2D gene was particularly distributed in the inner enamel epithelium, and no signals were detected in the dental papilla at early bell stages. KMT2D signals were seen in preodontoblasts and preameloblasts in the late bell stage.40

Ng et al. reported pathogenic mutations of KMT2D gene in patients affected with Kabuki syndrome (KS).43 Three variants of this gene were reported in patients [(exon 21, c.5104C>T, p. Arg1702*), (exon 35, c.10205delA, p. Leu3402Argfs*31), (exon 40, c.12840delC, p.Pro4281Leufs*103)], who were identified with pathogenic heterozygous mutations in KMT2D.44 In the present study, the gene KMT2D and its variant Exon c.15907C>T (p. Arg5303Cys) were identified in the child and his mother. However, no abnormal facial characteristics or syndromic features were found/ identified in them. The gene encoding for lysine methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D) protein, an important histone methyltransferase, has been found to be the primary cause of Kabuki syndrome.45 Patients with Kabuki syndrome are reported to have postnatal growth retardation, skeletal and dental abnormalities, along with dysmorphic face, midfacial hypoplasia, and cleft lip/palate.46 Screw-driver shaped upper incisors, ectopic upper molars, hypodontia, microdontia, congenital absence of teeth, and enamel hypoplasia are common dental findings in patients with Kabuki syndrome. Dental arch abnormalities such as high-arched palate, widely spaced teeth, and micrognathia are other features of Kabuki syndrome.46 These abnormalities pertaining to tooth number and crown morphology suggest the risk that KMT2D might be involved in the development and morphogenesis of the dental enamel organ.46

Mutations and deletions of KMT2D gene have also been reported in different types of cancer. Additionally, this gene has been shown to possess tumor-suppressive or protumorigenic function in different types of cancer. Thus, identification of this gene variant could possibly aid in early diagnosis and regular monitoring of such patients.47 According to Gowans et al., dental morphogenesis could be affected by the missense pathogenic variants in KIF4A, could affect dental morphogenesis, which in turn causes DI.48 However, this variant was not identified in the present study.

The present study was performed to identify the possible role of genetics in causation of DI through WES. This has led to the identification of variation in the candidate gene in both mother and child. It is expected that identifying the genetic origin of DI would be helpful for early prediction, diagnosis, and treatment planning. Acquiring insight into the genes that affect tooth morphology might help in understanding the mechanism behind the genesis of DI. Thus, novel approaches in treating DI through gene therapy and regenerative medicine may be made possible.

This research has a few limitations. Even though we identified the candidate gene variant, which can be a causative factor for DI, this study was conducted in a single family with two members. The current study's results strongly suggest the involvement of genetics through the KMT2D mutation, but do not provide evidence of how the variant may have been inherited. The current study is a novel exploration of the role of KMT2D gene in the clinical expression of DI. Future research could be conducted with a larger sample size to determine the function of gene variations, inheritance patterns, and the link between the candidate gene variants and DI.

Conclusion

The genetic basis of DI was examined in the current investigation, and the candidate gene KMT2D and its variation Exon c.15907C>T (p. Arg5303Cys) were successfully identified. Further research is needed to understand the role of the discovered candidate variations in DI. Examining the variant's function will help us better to understand its link to DI and tooth development.
