Optimisation and validation of line-of-therapy advancement algorithms in advanced/metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer: an I-O Optimise study
Authors: S. Lay-Flurrie, A. Greystoke, C. Rault, A. Wolf, M. Riaz, C. Leal, F. de Abreu e Lima, K. Van Naarden Braun, T. Evans-Cheung, M.B. Glassberg, L.J. Eccles, S. Cheeseman, C. Chouaid, P. Baas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDefining lines of therapy (LoTs) in real-world data is challenging, but it is essential for understanding the evolving treatment landscape for metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Validated algorithms are critical for determining LoTs; however, the performance of these algorithms across different data sources remains uncertain. This retrospective study aimed to optimise and evaluate the performance of several LoT algorithms for patients with metastatic NSCLC in Germany and the UK.Materials and methodsSix LoT algorithms were assessed in two real-world data sources: Frankfurt University Hospital, Germany, and the Real World Evidence Alliance at Leeds-Oncology, UK. The algorithms used various combinations of information on treatment (dispensation time, drug type, cycle) and progression of disease. Accuracy and positive predictive value were measured for each data source.ResultsAcross both data sources, all algorithms showed similar accuracy. On average, the algorithms correctly identified the full treatment pathway for >75% of patients (≥90% excluding the worst-performing algorithm). The most accurate algorithms were time- and drug based (94% accuracy), and time- and drug-cycle based (95% accuracy).ConclusionsAlgorithms using both drug type and timing information demonstrated high accuracy and consistent performance in defining LoTs. Adding disease progression information did not improve LoT definition. This study can help guide future real-world evidence generation.

Introduction

Metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has a poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of <10%.1 Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, requiring effective systemic anticancer therapies (SACT) to control disease progression, maintain quality of life, and extend survival.1

The evolving treatment landscape for metastatic NSCLC is complex, raising questions about optimal treatment sequencing. Current guidelines recommend SACT based on patient characteristics, disease progression, and previous treatment response.2,3 Real-world clinical practices can deviate from guidelines and require description.4,5 A lack of standardised definitions and treatment inconsistencies makes evaluating lines of therapy (LoTs) in real-world data (RWD) challenging.6 Generally, an LoT in the metastatic setting is considered a discrete therapeutic entity with start/stop dates, encompassing chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, or combinations thereof.6

Understanding patient pathways and outcomes through different LoTs is important for improving individualised treatment.5 The lack of detailed information on LoTs (e.g. composition, start/stop dates, reason for ending) complicates treatment sequence construction and outcome interpretation. Clinician assessment and algorithmic analysis of electronic medical records (EMRs) are used to reconstruct LoTs based on available, useful information (e.g. SACT type, start/stop dates, progression, cycle information). Clinician-determined LoTs are the gold standard, but are time-consuming, impractical for real-world studies, and prone to human error and bias. Algorithmic analyses in RWD research face challenges with data inconsistencies and complex treatment regimens.7

The portability of an algorithm (in this case, its ability to execute LoT determination across different data sources) is important for providing consistency and standardisation.8 In the context of RWD, it aids reproducibility and comparability of findings and clinical outcomes by enabling uniform application across datasets.9,10

Algorithm validation is crucial for successful application, and ensuring the reliability of study findings is essential for evidence-based health care.7 Emphasising high-quality validation studies aligns with the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices.11

For NSCLC, LoT validation studies have considered time-based10,12 or time- and drug-based algorithms,10,13 but the potential utility of additional cycle and progression information has not been studied. Here, we aimed to evaluate and validate the performance of six different LoT algorithms based on time, drug, cycle, and progression information. Performance was compared against clinician-determined results derived from medical chart review in patients with incident stage IV NSCLC. We used data from two separate sources based in Germany and the UK as part of the I-O Optimise (I-OO) observational research initiative.14

Materials and methods

Study data sources

This study used two single-centre EMR structured data sources to enhance the generalisability of the I-OO LoT algorithms’ performance assessment: Frankfurt University Hospital (FUH), Germany, treats >17 000 patients with cancer annually, and the Real World Evidence Alliance at Leeds-Oncology (REAL-O) is the largest integrated cancer centre in the UK, offering care to ∼750 000 patients locally, and regional cancer services to ∼2.7 million.

FUH EMR is a classical EMR data source with data being structured and manually coded by tumour documentation specialists. The FUH EMR includes prescription and treatment application information.

REAL-O EMR is an in-house EMR that captures comprehensive clinical data on all patients undergoing cancer treatment at the Leeds Cancer Centre within Leeds Teaching Hospital Trust. The REAL-O EMR consolidates electronic data from various separate systems within the Trust into a single EMR database, including data from the ChemoCare chemotherapy electronic prescribing system. In the REAL-O EMR, administered regimens are summarised by each LoT, and the resulting summary includes fields such as regimen, cycle (dates and drugs administered by cycle), course, and programme (preplanned sequence of treatment). Data on patients with lung cancer from the REAL-O EMR are routinely extracted into a lung cancer master file and reviewed by a clinician and data quality officer, with any discrepancies corrected in both the lung cancer master file and the EMR.

Study population and study period

This retrospective cohort study, in which each cohort was constructed separately, included all patients aged ≥18 years and newly diagnosed with stage IV NSCLC as defined by the seventh or eight edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control, with one or more SACT record during the study period. Patient selection was from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2018 at FUH and from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2019 at REAL-O. Follow-up continued until 30 May 2020 at FUH and 31 December 2020 at REAL-O. Although study dates were not identical, the minimum potential follow-up time was approximately equal between data sources.

Algorithm definition and development

SACT LoT sequence was determined through chart review and algorithm application at patient level. The performance of six algorithms was assessed against clinician-determined reference (gold standard) using a stepwise approach (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701): initial algorithm run, discrepancy examination, and algorithm optimisation (with clinical review and input); second algorithm run and evaluation of optimised outputs.

Determination of LoT sequences

This study focused on the SACT components of each LoT; therefore, LoTs utilising radiotherapy alone, or radiotherapy added to previously commenced SACTs, were not considered to be new LoTs.

Each LoT could comprise SACT agents alone or in combination. The first LoT was defined by the date of first dose or prescription of SACT agent(s) initiated within a 6-month period after incident stage IV NSCLC diagnosis according to TNM classification seventh and eighth editions (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701). The initial LoT included all systemic agents administered within the first 28 days after treatment initiation. A patient could have several LoTs, each differing in composition of the SACT agents.

Method 1: Clinician manual chart review (gold standard)

Clinician manual chart reviews were conducted at FUH and REAL-O. Clinicians and data quality officers had access to the full EMR.

Step 1 involved cohort identification, selecting anonymised patients meeting study criteria.

Step 2 entailed extracting relevant treatment and progression data from the EMR for clinician review. At FUH, the data management team extracted the relevant treatment and progression variables for each patient into a table, including patient ID, event (diagnosis, treatment start, progression), treatment, and additional progression information for clinician review. At REAL-O, data were extracted from the EMR into a table and included patient ID, regimen name, regimen number, and LoT number.

At FUH, step 3 involved two clinicians independently determining the LoT number for each patient, followed by step 4, in which results were compared and disagreements resolved, with third-party consultation if needed.

At REAL-O, step 3 involved a clinician, supported by a data quality officer, sequentially reviewing and updating implausible or missing LoT data in the analysis dataset. Clinicians, data quality officers, and third-party consultants were blinded to the LoT algorithm definitions. No formal guidance was provided to the clinicians regarding the LoT definition. The clinicians assigned the LoT based on their own clinical judgement and clinical experience including multidisciplinary team discussions.

Method 2: LoT algorithms definition

Six LoT algorithms, using combinations of time, drug, cycle, and progression variables (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701), were developed with input from a clinician group distinct from those conducting the manual chart review. The algorithms were designed to include a minimal number of variables, so they would be applicable to other data sources with more restricted data availability. The algorithms had access to a data extract in table format from the database which included the available data for the rules for each algorithm. These algorithms were tested in each data source and compared against clinician-determined LoT definitions from manual chart reviews (Table 1). Algorithms considered only a list of expected SACT agents to be given for NSCLC (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701). After the first LoT, subsequent LoT advancement was determined by time, drug, cycle, and/or progression-based rules, dependent on the algorithm applied. Evaluation of algorithm performance

Correct classification of an LoT (concordance) was defined as agreement between the algorithm and the clinician-determined LoT definition regarding the existence of the total number of LoTs in the treatment pathway and the International Nonproprietary Names (INN) of SACT agents within each LoT [any discrepancy in the composition (e.g. extra or missing SACT agents or differing INNs) resulted in misclassification].

Accuracy was measured by the number and proportion of patients with correctly classified whole pathways, calculated separately for each data source, according to clinician-determined classification. The best-performing algorithm was identified by calculating the crude average accuracy across the two data sources. In this study, positive predictive value (PPV) was calculated at each LoT (LoTx) and corresponded to the proportion of patients with concordant LoTx (i.e. both algorithm and clinician-determined reference identified the LoTx and same SACT agents within it) among patients who had started LoTx, adapted from Ehrenstein et al.7 This definition differs from that outlined by Sørup et al., which did not consider agreement of LoT agents.13 PPV was defined as the ratio of true positives to the sum of true positives and false positives, with true positives and false positives calculated as outlined in Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and LoT characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were represented by the mean, standard deviation, median, and quartiles, while categorical variables were represented by the number and percentage of patients in each category. Accuracy was calculated and presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived using Jeffreys interval.15

Discrepancies and optimisation

Initial algorithm performance was evaluated with clinician input, leading to algorithm refinements. Final optimised LoT algorithms were determined, and all outputs were reproduced for the post-optimisation versions. Limited pre-optimisation results are reported to contextualise post-optimisation results presented here.

Optimisation steps and adding agents

To address the issue of SACT agents being ignored by the algorithms, the list of SACT agents was updated to add new drugs that were not in the original predefined list (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701). To reduce over-splitting identified pre-optimisation [where patients had more LoTs defined compared with the clinician-determined reference due to splitting of combinations of treatments into separate LoTs, observed in algorithms #1a, #2, #4, and #5 (Figure 1)], an exception was added to the treatment gap rule. Treatment pathway determination

Clinician determined

At FUH, 53% of patients received only one LoT during the study period, compared with 71% at REAL-O (Table 2). FUH had more complex treatment pathways with 22.3% of patients receiving three or more LoTs compared with 4.2% at REAL-O.

Algorithm determined

The proportion of patients receiving each LoT varied slightly across the algorithms compared with the clinician-determined reference (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100701).

LoT algorithm performance

Accuracy

All six algorithms demonstrated a crude average accuracy of ≥75%, and >92% when excluding the time-based (#1b) algorithm, in identifying the treatment pathway at FUH and REAL-O, with low variability across the two data sources (Table 3). The time-based (#1b) algorithm had the lowest crude average accuracy at 75% (interquartile range) [69% at FUH (62%-75%); 80% at REAL-O (76%-85%)]. The highest crude average accuracy was achieved by the time- and drug-based, cycle-dependent (#2) algorithm at 95% [94% at FUH (90%-96%); 96% at REAL-O (93%-98%)]. Positive predictive value

The PPV for the first LoT across all algorithms was consistently high (≥79%) at both data sources (Table 4), with low variability (85%-98% at REAL-O versus 79%-95% at FUH). The time-based (#1b) algorithm had the lowest PPV for the first LoT in both data sources (79% at FUH, 85% at REAL-O). The highest PPV for the first LoT at FUH was achieved by the time- and drug-based, cycle-dependent (#2), the time-, drug- and progression-based (#4), and the time-, drug- and progression-based and cycle-dependent (#5) algorithms (95% for all), and by the time- and drug-based (#1a) and the time- and drug-based, cycle-dependent (#2) algorithms at REAL-O (98% for both). PPV decreased with each additional LoT. Excluding the time-based (#1b) algorithm, PPV was 84%-94% for the second LoT at REAL-O and 90%-92% at FUH, and 65%-88% for the third LoT at REAL-O and 84%-88% at FUH. Discussion

Validated algorithms are essential for generating reliable real-world evidence to inform health care decisions when managing metastatic NSCLC.7 This study validated and optimised six LoT algorithms for metastatic NSCLC using two data sources, comparing them with clinician-determined references. Our approach mimics a typical scenario when conducting a study using RWD where researchers only have extracts of data to determine LoT.

Strong alignment was observed, with all algorithms correctly identifying the entire treatment pathway for ≥75% of patients with stage IV NSCLC, with ≥90% accuracy for all except the time-only algorithm. PPV decreased with each LoT. Excluding the time-only algorithm, PPV was ≥93% and ≥95% for first LoT, ≥90% and ≥84% for second LoT and ≥84% and ≥65% for third LoT with FUH and REAL-O, respectively. Alignment remained high when more complex treatment pathways were analysed separately. PPV for first LoT was ≥87% for pathways with two LoTs and ≥89% for pathways with three or more LoTs at both data sources, with the exception of the time-only algorithm. PPV for second LoT was ≥88% for pathways with two LoTs and ≥81% for pathways with three or more LoTs, and for third LoT it was ≥85% for pathways with three or more LoTs, at both data sources, excluding the time-only algorithm for second and third LoTs for pathways with three or more LoTs. Reasons for errors in LoT determination beyond first LoT included (i) inaccurately assigning the next LoT after a long treatment gap combined with discontinuation of some but not all LoT regimens and (ii) early errors in the algorithm propagating to later LoT in more complex treatment pathways. Overall, increased pathway complexity with more LoTs reduced accuracy and PPV. Variability in follow-up length was observed between FUH and REAL-O. At FUH, information on number of treatment cycles was missing for 60% of patients and was estimated using regimen start and end dates. Regimen end dates were also missing for 30% of patients from FUH and were derived using the earliest of either the last contact date with the patient or the end of the study period. Importantly, missing regimen end dates at FUH only affected the final regimen records and therefore did not affect LoT assignment. Slightly higher accuracy was observed at REAL-O compared with FUH; the FUH population was younger and pathways were more complex overall. The PPV 95% CI observed at REAL-O was wider than that at FUH, likely due to differences in progression recording. FUH did not include mixed response (e.g. stable primary tumour but new metastases) as progression, whereas REAL-O did, leading to more frequent line advancements in REAL-O. The portability of algorithms between data sources can be uncertain owing to variability in factors including patient age, coding and documentation practices, standard of care, and infrastructure.7 Robust application of these algorithms in two data sources with similar geographies and EMR systems demonstrates their portability.

By testing several algorithms incorporating progression and cycle information, using a broad NSCLC population within a recent timescale, our study differs from existing literature.10,12,13,16 Gap rules between LoTs were developed with European experts to ensure appropriate application. While the literature shows variation in therapy gap windows leading to LoT advancement, we used a time gap of ≥42 days and a 28-day new treatment rule, aligning with recent studies.10,12,13,16,17 Wang et al. applied the cycle rule; however, we found minimal improvement in crude accuracy with cycle data addition, and progression data (not previously recorded in the literature but captured in our analysis by a formal record of progression) did not enhance algorithm accuracy.16

Grady et al. found their time- and drug-based algorithm more accurate for determining LoT in patients with metastatic NSCLC in an independent cohort with EGFR alteration.10 Applicability and portability to patients without oncogenic alterations may require assessment.

The drug-based algorithm of Sørup et al. outperformed a time-based one in identifying antineoplastic LoTs, with PPV decreasing as LoTs increased, aligning with our findings.13 We evaluated a wider range of algorithms, with similar drug rules, across two data sources, achieving comparable performance results, reinforcing our algorithms’ portability.

Our optimisation process aimed to correct LoT misclassifications due to missing SACTs, over-combination or over-splitting of SACT agents, and propagation of early pathway errors. Incorporating new SACTs, including targeted therapies, to the expected SACT list feeding the algorithm and adding exceptions for specific treatment scenarios achieved minor improvements in overall crude average accuracy of 1%-3% (results not shown), primarily in time- and drug-based algorithms. PPVs improved most notably for second and third LoTs; however, the trend of decreasing PPV with increasing LoTs persisted. PPV for the first LoT aligned well with accuracy results (i.e. there was minimal misclassification of SACTs between clinician- and algorithm-determined results). Subsequent results reflected accumulation of earlier and new errors, impacting later LoT performance.

The assumption that each progression should start a new LoT was challenged by our findings, which showed that progression records often did not align with LoT initiation. Patients with mixed responses might receive localised treatments, e.g. radiotherapy instead of moving to a new LoT.5 Clinicians at REAL-O and FUH noted that variations between progression records and LoT initiation were mainly due to mixed response events. This complexity in tumour behaviour affected the performance of algorithms that rely on progression timing to signal LoT transitions.

Variations between algorithm- and clinician-determined LoT advancement also included treatment switches due to intolerance, toxicity, or adverse events. Clinicians may pause or switch regimens without considering it a LoT advancement. Grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation occur in ∼15% of patients with NSCLC treated with immunotherapy,18 with higher risk when combining SACT agents.19 The original 42-day gap rule was insufficient to accommodate these cases, causing premature LoT advancement. Extending this gap reduced misclassification by accounting for treatment changes due to toxicity, aligning nonprogression-based algorithms more closely with clinical practice.

Our results suggest that within the context of these data sources, incorporating time- and drug-based rules into LoT algorithms is essential for accurate outcomes. The time variable was available for all REAL-O and 70% of FUH patients, with omissions at FUH in end dates for the last recorded regimen. Drug information was available for all patients. Adding cycle or progression information does not vastly improve quality and their absence is not detrimental to algorithm performance. This may be because cycle data were fuelled by the variables already captured through time and drug rules, but may also be due to the absence of regimen end dates for FUH. In terms of progression, it is evident that progression patterns did not consistently correlate with the treatment data.

Real-world studies inform health care decisions including payer evaluations, regulatory approvals, and reimbursement policies. Our results confirm that algorithms can be designed to accurately determine LoTs from EMRs even with minimal available data, and the study showcases how the portability of algorithms to other data sources can be tested to support claims regarding data quality and completeness. Following a similar approach, RWD studies reporting on treatment patterns and outcomes to support payer and regulatory approvals can be considered reliable sources of evidence. The strengths of this study include validating six LoT algorithms using two independent NSCLC populations totalling 514 patients, identifying LoT advancement rules, and providing expert comparison through manual chart review. Multiple algorithm evaluation revealed their strengths, weaknesses, and advancement rule interplay. Performance in non-EMR-based datasets, e.g. population-based registries and insurance claims, remains unknown, with optimisation potentially overly tailored to the datasets used, necessitating further external validation. Portability is influenced by many variables. However, differences between our data sources in follow-up time, clinician-determined LoTs, treatment complexity, and patient populations support our algorithms’ portability.

Regarding limitations, manual chart review lacked formal guidelines, relying on clinicians' judgement, potentially introducing inconsistencies. However, limited discrepancies in the determination of LoT number occurred; at FUH, third-party consultation was required only once (for a case with more than one LoT). The difference in data collection dates between FUH and REAL-O could introduce a time period bias; however, limited updates to treatment guidelines during this time and similarities in data collection would likely have a limited impact on algorithm performance results. We did not account for LoT duration; thus, the concordance of the tested algorithms did not rely on this. Most patients received only one LoT, limiting analysis of multiple LoTs. The 42-day treatment gap, although not ascertained through formal guidance, is a timeframe aligning with clinical interpretation of 6 weeks. The study assessed algorithms in patients with newly diagnosed stage IV NSCLC, not accounting for patients who relapsed from earlier stages with different treatment pathways. Evaluating these algorithms for other cancers with multiple LoTs and complex treatment regimens requires investigation. As earlier diagnosis in NSCLC increases and treatment complexity evolves, LoT algorithms must be adaptable to incorporate SACTs accordingly.

In conclusion, this study evaluated the performance of six algorithms independently on two data sources to predict LoT sequences using RWD in patients with newly diagnosed stage IV NSCLC. Despite a lack of international accuracy standards, most algorithms demonstrated high performance, even with complex treatment regimens. Balancing integration and complexity of advancement rules is crucial for accurately determining treatment pathways in patients with metastatic NSCLC. The time- and drug-based (#1a) algorithm demonstrated high accuracy and has potential broad applicability across EMR datasets, while the time- and drug-based, cycle-dependent (#2) algorithm performed best when cycle information was available, guiding future real-world evidence generation.
