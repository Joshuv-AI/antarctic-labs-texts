Multi-omics Analysis Reveals the Hormonal Basis of Differential Response to Nighttime Heat Temperature Between Superior and Inferior Rice Grains
Authors: Hui Fang, Xiangfeng Meng, Bei Tang, Hongwei Zhang, Yitian Ma, Zhangfei Xu, Bingrong Xu, Xiaoya Li, Baohua Wang, Yunying Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNighttime high temperature (NHT) poses a significant threat to rice production, particularly during the grain-filling stage. However, its impact on grain development remains poorly understood. In this study, we investigated the effects of NHT on yield and quality in two japonica rice varieties (N5055 and YND1) during early grain-filling stage. We found that NHT significantly reduced the grain setting rate, hundred-grain weight and amylose content, with inferior grains (IGs) being more severely affected than superior grains (SGs). Metabolomic analysis revealed 670 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), primarily associated with lipid, amino acid, and hormone metabolism. Notably, more hormone-related DAMs were detected in SGs than in IGs. Transcriptomic profiling identified 7750 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under control conditions, but only 576 under NHT, indicating a dramatic disruption of transcriptional programs. Eight hormone-related genes were significantly differentially expressed in SGs, underscoring the crucial role of hormonal regulation in the SG-specific NHT response. Hormonal profiling further revealed 12 differentially accumulated hormones, six of which exhibited distinct accumulation patterns between SGs and IGs. This demonstrates that SGs undergo unique hormonal reprogramming under NHT stress, characterized by significant upregulation of cytokinins. The key cytokinin response regulators, OsRR3 and OsRR6, were specifically correlated with cytokinin levels in SGs, but not in IGs. Our integrated analysis reveals that differential hormonal and transcriptional regulation underpins the contrasting responses of SGs and IGs to NHT, providing new insights into the metabolic and molecular basis of NHT tolerance in rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00903-x.Keywords: Oryza Sativa, NHT, Metabolome, Transcriptome, Hormones

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is one of the most important crops worldwide, and serve as a stable food sustaining nearly half of the globle population (Wang et al. 2024a). Among the key developmental stages governing rice yield and quality, the grain-filling stage is the final and most stress-sensitive phase, involving the conversion of sucrose to starch and the effective translocation of photosynthates to grains (Tang et al. 2018; Ren et al. 2023). This stage comprises three sequential processes: endosperm cell division (determing cell number), starch biosynthesis (regulating kernel weight), and kernel dehydration (affecting maturity and quality) (Zhao et al. 2021). During grain filling, even mild environmental perturbations can disrupt these processes, reducing thousand-grain weight, grain plumpness and amylopectin content, and ultimately causing yield loss (Gao et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024). Notably, the grain-filling stage is particularly vulnerable to temperature stress, given disruptions to enzyme activity (e.g., amylase) or nutrient transport exert irreversible impacts on yield (Liu et al. 2021; Mondal et al. 2025).

Climate change is elevating global average surface temperatures, posing a threaten to global rice yield and food production. While the effects of high temperatures on rice have been extensively studied, most research has focused on daytime heat stress, with limited attention to the unique impacts of nighttime heat stress (NHT) (Mthiyane et al. 2024). Since the late twentieth century, the rise in global nighttime minimum temperatures (0.204 °C per decade) has been twice that of daytime maximum temperatures (0.141 °C per decade), narrowing the diurnal temperature range and elevating nighttime temperatures (Impa et al. 2021). Unlike daytime heat, which typically coincides with high light intensity and photosynthetic activity, NHT primarily impacts non-photosynthetic processes, including respiration, nutrient translocation, and hormone homeostasis (Glaubitz et al. 2015; Shi et al. 2016; Impa et al. 2021). Prior studies have demonstrated that NHT inhibit crop growth and development by disrubing key reproductive physiological processes, including pollen development, viability, tube growth, fertilization, embryogenesis, and grain filling (Liu et al. 2023). It also upregulates dark respiration, increasing energy consumption and reducing photosynthate accumulation in grains (Impa et al. 2019; Riaz et al. 2024). Collectively, these disruptions reduce rice yield. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of plant responses to NHT is critical for advancing stress biology research and breeding programs aimed at sustaining rice production under climate change.

A key feature of rice panicles is the inherent developmental and physiological divergence between superior grains (SGs) and inferior grains (IGs). SGs are typically located on the upper primary branches of the panicle, flower earlier than IGs, and initiate grain filling immediately after fertilization. They preferentially acquire photosynthates and nutrients, leading to faster grain filling, more endosperm cells, and higher grain weight (Zhang et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2022). In contrast, IGs are located on the lower secondary branches of the panicle, flower later, and face intense nutrient competition from SGs. Thus, even under optimal conditions, IGs exhibit delayed grain filling initiation, slower starch accumulation, and reduced grain plumpness (Teng et al. 2022). The differences, mechanisms, and regulation strategies underlying SG-IG grain filling have been well documented (Chen et al. 2016; Mohapatra et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2019). This inherent developmental asynchrony renders IGs more susceptible to environmental stresses. Separating SGs and IGs enables dissection of grain type-specific stress response mechanisms, which is essential for developing targeted strategies to improve IGs tolerance, and thereby enhance overall panicle yield. However, our understanding of how NHT affects SGs and IGs remains insufficient.

Plant hormones are crucial chemical signals for intercellular communication, regulating growth, development, morphogenesis, and biochemical metabolism (McSteen and Zhao 2008; Kamiya 2010). They are well- established regulators of abiotic stress responses, including heat stress (Waadt et al. 2022; Khan et al. 2023). For example, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) alleviates high-temperature-induced damage by reducing lipid peroxidation and ROS (Reactive Oxygen Species) accumulation in spikelets (Sharma et al. 2018). It also promotes root growth under elevated temperatures (Rellán-Álvarez et al. 2015). Jasmonic acid (JA) and brassinosteroid (BR) hormones enhance heat tolerance by preserving stamen fertilization capacity (Yang et al. 2021). Gibberellins (GA), primarily via GA12, enable flexible growth responses to ambient temperature changes through long-distance transport (Camut et al. 2019). Ethylene is an important endogenous hormone that regulates diverse physiological and developmental processes, from seed germination to tissue senescence, and mediates responses to various abiotic stresses (Huang et al. 2023; Johnson and Ecker 1998; Zhu et al. 2024). However, the response of ethylene to temperature varies significantly across species and conditions (Cicchino et al. 2013; Hays et al. 2007; Jegadeesan et al. 2018; Pan et al. 2019; Savada et al. 2017; Sehar et al. 2023; Wu and Yang 2019). Abscisic acid (ABA), a key stress hormone, plays a crucial role in various stresses, particularly via ROS-associated stomatal regulation. Under heat stress, ABA levels typically increase due to enhanced biosynthesis, reduced degradation, or release from conjugated forms (Devireddy et al. 2021). ABA induces the transcription factors ERF74 and ERF75, which translocate from the plasma membrane to the nucleus under heat stress and directly regulate RBOHD to enhance thermotolerance in Arabidopsis (Yao et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2021). ABA also induces the expression of heat shock factor (HSF) and heat shock protein (HSP) genes, forming a complex regulatory network for thermotolerance (Chen et al. 2022). However, some reports indicate that ABA levels can decrease under heat stress in certain contexts(Tokić et al. 2023). Notably, ABA and cytokinins (CKs) act antagonistically to regulate grain filling: ABA promotes senescence and inhibits starch accumulation, whereas CKs stimulate endosperm cell division and delay senescence (Huang et al. 2018; Singh and Roychoudhury 2023). CKs are key plant hormones, playing a critical role in regulating growth,development and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Zhang et al. 2010; Xiao et al. 2025). Numerous studies have established that CKs are closely linked to grain size, grain weight, and yield formation. Changes in CKs metabolites or signaling regulators significantly affect grain development and filling (Zhang et al. 2012; Tao et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2022). Beyond normal development, CKs are also involved in plant responses to high-temperature stress. Exogenous application or elevated endogenous CKs levels enhance crop heat tolerance by regulating ROS homeostasis, strengthening antioxidant capacity, and delaying senescence (Mei et al. 2023; Angon et al. 2024). These findings collectively highlight the potential regulatory role of CKs in heat stress response and thermotolerance mechanisms during grain filling. However, most of these findings are based on daytime heat stress or whole-plant analyses. How NHT specifically modulates hormone dynamics in SGs versus IGs, and how these changes translate into differential grain filling outcomes, remains unclear.

To address these gaps, we integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic and hormonal profiling to investigate SGs and IGs responses to NHT during the early grain-filling stage in two japonica rice varieties. Our objectives were to: (1) clarify the differential impacts of NHT on SGs and IGs yield and quality traits; (2) identify key genes and metabolites involved in NHT response; and (3) elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Results

Nighttime Heat Temperature Reduce Rice Yield and Quality-Related Traits

To investigate the impact of NHT on rice yield and quality, two japonica rices, including Nangeng 5055 and Yangnongdao 1 (abbreviated as N5055 and YND1, respectively), were selected for the measurement of yield and quality-related traits after exposure to NHT during the early grain-filling stage. The results of the two-year high-temperature experiment showed that NHT had significant impact on the setting rate and hundred-grain weight (HGW) of the two varieties, whereas the effects on grain length and grain width are not consistent between the two varieties (Fig. 1; Supplemental Fig. 1). In contrast, the quality-related traits, the contents of amylose and amylopectin, are less affected by the NHT. Moreover, compared with SGs, IGs are more susceptible to the NHT (Fig. 1A). Specifically, for N5055, the setting rate and HGW of SGs and IGs were significantly reduced under the condition of NHT across the two-year experiments, with setting rate of SGs reduced 8.10% and 12.90%, and IGs reduced by 14.47% and 28.22% in 2022 and 2023, respectively (Fig. 1B, H). The HGW of SGs for N5055 reduced by 1.36 g and 2.18 g, and that of IGs decreased by 2.02 and 2.14 g, in 2022 and 2023, respectively (Fig. 1I). Conversely, whether the SG or IG of N5055, neither their grain length nor grain width has been significantly affected (Fig. 1D, E; Supplemental Fig. 1A, B), but the hundred-grain weight has significantly decreased, which indicates that the NHT has significantly impacted the grain filling of N5055. For YND1, the setting rate of SGs did not exhibit significant change, wheares that of the IGs decreased by 23.69% and 13.70% in 2022 and 2023, respectively (Fig. 1C, J). The HGW of YND1 significantly decreased in both SGs and IGs under NHT, with that decreased by 6.45 g and 1.18 g in SGs and by 6.70 g and 0.93 g in IGs, in 2022 and 2023, respectively (Fig. 1K). Interestingly, the grain length and width of YND1 for both SGs and IGs significantly increased, especially in 2022, with grain length increased by 0.13 mm to 0.89 mm and grain width increased by 0.10 to 0.65 mm, respectively (Fig. 1F, G; Supplemental Fig. 1C, D). Compared with SGs, the setting rate and size of IGs are more significantly affected. Under the NHT, although the grain size of YND1 increased, its hundred-grain weight significantly decreased, indicating a reduction in the weight of individual grains. This further suggests that the NHT affected the grain filling of rice. The difference in grain size changes between the two varieties under NHT stress is likely caused by their different genetic backgrounds. In addition, NHT hardly alters the amylose, amylopectin and total starch contents of IGs for both varieties, while in SGs, the significant change in amylose content was observed (Fig. 1L, M). These results indicate that rice SGs exhibited stronger tolerance to NHT, while IGs were more significantly inhibited in terms of seed setting rate, grain weight, and grain filling.

Metabolomic Analysis Under Nighttime High Temperature

The rice variety N5055 was selected for metabolomic analysis due to its consistent phenotypic response to NHT over a two-year period. After a 10-day NHT treatment, SGs and IGs were sampled and analyzed separately using UPLC-MS. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation among four sample groups (CK-S, CK-I, H–S and H-I), with six biological replicates per group. The first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 18.6% and 12.3% of the total variance, respectively. PC1 differentiated SGs from IGs, while PC2 seperated the control groups from the treatment groups (Fig. 2A). Using a threshold of a P-value < 0.05 and a VIP > 1, we identified 670 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), of which 476 were upregulated and 194 were downregulated (Fig. 2B, C; Supplemental Table 1). These DAMs were involved in various metabolic processes (Fig. 2B; Supplemental Fig. 2), including those essential basic life activities, such as fatty acid and lipid (117, 17.46%), amino acid (110, 16.42%), carbohydrate (45, 6.72%), nucleotide (40, 5.97%), and vitamin metabolism (36, 5.37%), and those regulating growth, development, stress responses, such as hormone and signaling transduction (41, 6.12%) and aromatic metabolism (32, 12.24%). These hormone-related DAMs included ethylene, abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellins (GA), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), cytokinins (CK), auxin (IAA), salicylic acid (SA) and their intermediates, suggesting their important roles in the rice response to NHT. Specifically, under control conditions, 208 DAMs were identified between SGs and IGs, with 67 DAMs accumulating more in SGs, while 141 more in IGs, indicating inherent metabolic differences during grain development. Under NHT stress, the number of DAMs between SGs and IGs increased to 268 (79 with higher accumulation in SGs; 189 in IGs). This significant increase over the control indicates that NHT disrupts rice metabolism, with a more pronounced effect on IGs. Conversely, when compared to their respective controls, 193 (81 up, 112 down) and 156 (75 up, 81 down) DAMs were identified in SGs and IGs, respectively (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, Veen and upset plots illustrated the unique and common metabolites across the different comparisons of SGs and IGs (Fig. 2D; Supplemental Fig. 3). 77 DAMs were common to both the SGs and IGs under NHT (i.e., CK-S vs. H–S and CK-I vs. H-I), wherese 34 were common to all four groups (Fig. 2D). Notably, 116 and 79 DAMs were specific to heat-stressed SGs and IGs (Fig. 2D), respectively, suggesting that SGs possess a greater capacity to mount a metabolic response to NHT. These specific DAMs were primarily associated with some fundamental metabolic processes essential for maintaining cellular activities (Fig. 2E–G). Notably, under NHT, the number of stress-related hormones differed significantly between SGs (8) and IGs (3). The hormonal changes in IGs were primarily in ABA and IAA, whereas SGs also involved SA, CKs and BR (Fig. 2F, G). These results indicate that differential hormone regulation in SGs and IGs under NHT contributes to their contrasting yield performance. Furthermore, this hormonal reprogramming appears to be a key mechanism in rice's response to NHT.

Transcriptome Analysis

RNA-seq was performed on SGs and IGs from the N5055 variety following a 10-day NHT treatment. A total of 76.16 Gb clean data was obtained, with an average of 6.35 Gb per sample. The error rate for all samples was approximately 0.03%, and the Q30 score was above 92%, indicating high data quality (Supplemental Table 2). Although PCA analysis did not clearly separate the four sample groups (Fig. 3A), potentially due to shared grain development regulatory genes between SGs and IGs, separate PCA analysis for each grain type effectively distinguished their control and treatment groups, confirming the reliability of data (Supplemental Fig. 4). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis of randomly selected 20 genes showed a strong correlation (r = 0.92) with the RNA-seq data, further validating the transcriptome results (Fig. 3B). Global transcriptome analysis identified 7750 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between SGs and IGs under control conditions (4372 up-regulated and 3378 down-regulated), revealling substantial transcriptomic differences during grain development (Fig. 3C; Supplemental Table 3). Under NHT, however, the number of DEGs between SGs and IGs decreased markedly to only 576, indicating that NHT profoundly disrupts transcriptomic programs underlying grain development. In contrast, under NHT, 2,420 (1125 up-regulated and 1295 down-regulated) and 3266 (1893 up-regulated and 1373 down-regulated) DEGs were identified in SGs and IGs, respectively, compared to their controls. The lower number of DEGs in SGs compared to IGs suggests that SGs were less affected by NHT. Among these DEGs, 32 were common to all four sample groups, while 434 were common to the control and stress comparisons for both SGs and IGs (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, 1986 DEGs and 2832 were unique to SGs and IGs, respectively. The distinct sets of unique DEGs suggest that SGs and IGs may employ different molecular mechanisms to respond to NHT. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in the biosynthesis of diterpenoids, flavonoids, amino acids and phenylpropanoids in SGs (Fig. 3E). In IGs, they were mainly enriched in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, the proteasome, diterpenoid biosynthesis, and the citrate cycle (Fig. 3F). Consistent with the metabolome analysis, these transcriptomic results indicate that SGs and IGs exhibit markedly different responses to NHT.

In addition, we also verified that ten DEGs were associated with hormone signal transduction pathways, specifically for ethylene (ETH), ABA, IAA, MeJA, SA and CKs (Fig. 3G). Of these DEGs, eight were differentially expressed specifically in SGs, while only OsPYL4, a ABA receptor, and OsJAZ7, involved in regulation of jasmonic acid mediated signaling pathway, were differentially expressed in both grain types. This indicates that differential expression of hormone signaling genes is a key molecular basis underlying the distinct performances of SGs and IGs under NHT.

Changes in Endogenous Hormone Levels in Response to Nighttime Heat Temperature

Both metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses identified DAMs and DEGs associated with hormone signaling pathways, indicating a crucial role for hormones in the rice response to NHT. Therefore, we profiled hormone levels in SGs and IGs under NHT. PCA analysis clearly distinguished the four sample groups,with high reproducibility among biological replicates within each group. PC1 and PC2 explain 39.4% and 34.4% of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 4A). We identified 21 endogenous hormones in the rice grains, including GA, ABA, JA, IAA, CK, ETH, melatonin (MT) and strigolactone (SL) (Fig. 4B; Supplemental Table 4).Twelve of the 21 hormones exhibited significant content changes in both SGs and IGs under NHT, indicating that this stress disrupts hormonal homeostasis of grains during the early grain-filling stage in rice. However, the response patterns of these hormones differed between SGs and IGs (Fig. 4C–N). Specifically, the levels of IAA, ICA (3-Indolecarboxylic acid), JA (Jasmonic acid) and CZ (Cis-zeatin) increased significantly in both grain types under NHT (Fig. 4C–F), while ABA and OPDA (12-Oxophytodienoic acid) decreased (Fig. 4G, H). In contrast, six other shormones, including ACC (Aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid), DZ (Dihydrozeatin), SA, SAG (Glycosylated salicylic acid), TZ (Trans-zeatin), and TZR (Trans-zeatin riboside), increased significantly in SGs but decreased in IGs (Fig. 4I–N). This opposing pattern demonstrates the distinct impact of NHT on the hormonal landscapes of SGs and IGs. These differentially regulated hormones are likely key factors contributing to NHT resistance of rice and the contrasting phenotypic performance. Furthermore, in SGs, TZ showed the highest increase fold (15.35), followed by TZR (6.75), JA (3.59) and DZ (3.57) (Supplemental Table S4). Notably, three of the four most upregulated hormones in SGs were cytokinins, suggesting a crucial role for this hormone class in the SG-specific NHT response.

Key Plant Hormone Related Genes Responding to Nighttime Heat Temperature

To further explore the role of hormone-related genes in rice NHT response, we employed Mantel's test to assess correlations between the expression of 10 hormone-related DEGs (Fig. 3G) and the concentrations of 12 differentially accumulated hormones (Fig. 4C–N) in both grain types. Notably, most hormones exhibited significant positive or negative correlations (Fig. 5A), indicating that synergistic or antagonistic hormone interactions may regulate the rice NHT response. Specifically, the four cytokinins (CZ, TZ, TZR, and DZ) were significantly negatively correlated with ABA and OPDA (a JA-related hormone), yet positively correlated with IAA. This correlation pattern remained consistent in SGs (Supplemental Fig. 5A), but was disrupted in IGs (Supplemental Fig. 5B). This implies that NHT likely disrupts the inherent cooperative hormone balance in IGs, thereby imparing their grain filling. Given the key role of cytokinins in the SGs response, we analyzed the correlation between two potential cytokinin-related genes, OsRR3 and OsRR6, which are cytokinin response regulators, and the four cytokinins (Nongpiur et al. 2024). In SGs, OsRR3 was significantly correlated with TZ (P < 0.05), DZ (P < 0.05), and TZR (P < 0.01), wherease OsRR6 only correlated with TZR (P < 0.05) in SGs (Fig. 5B). In contrast, no significant correlations were detected in IGs (Supplemental Fig. 5B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that OsRR3 and OsRR6 likely play key roles in rice NHT response, with their involvement restricted to SGs. Under NHT stress, both OsRR3 and OsRR6 expression were significantly induced in SGs (Fig. 5C, D), confirming their coordinated involvement in the cytokinin-mediated SG-specific NHT response. Intriguingly, both genes significantly correlated with SAG and ABA in SGs but not in IGs (Supplemental Fig. 5), implying they may regulate SA and ABA levels in SGs via a specific pathway under NHT stress.

To further characterize the transcriptional landscape of the CKs pathway in grains, we systematically analyzed the grain-expressed (FPKM ≥ 1) core genes involved in CKs biosynthesis (four IPT family genes), catabolism (six CKX family genes), and signal transduction (10 type-A/-B RRs genes) (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Figs. 6, 7 and 8; Supplemental Table 5). For CKs biosynthesis, among IPT family genes encode isopentenyltransferase, a rate-limiting enzymes for CKs production (Nguyen et al. 2021). Among these, only OsIPT7 and OsIPT8 showed mild downregulation in SGs under NHT, while other IPT members displayed no significant transcriptional alterations in either grain type (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Fig. 6). For CKs catabolism, CKX family genes, key enzymes for CKs degradation that encodes cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase (Zheng et al. 2023), showed no significant expression differences between control and treatment groups in either SGs or IGs (Supplemental Fig. 7). Regarding the remaining type-A/-B RRs, no obvious transcriptional changes were observed in SGs or IGs under NHT except for OsRR3 and OsRR6 (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Fig. 8). These results were further validated by RT-qPCR. Consistent with RNA-Seq data, most OsCKX family members showed no significant expression differences between control and treatment groups in either SGs or IGs (Supplemental Fig. 9), except for OsCKX2, which was significantly upregulated in SGs and downregulated in IGs under NHT. In addition, OsRR21 was significantly upregulated in IGs under NHT (Supplemental Fig. 10). These results indicate that NHT-induced transcriptional regulation of the CKs pathway in SGs is highly specific: the core response is mediated by OsRR3 and OsRR6, whereas CKs biosynthesis, catabolism genes, and other signal transduction components do not undergo significant transcriptional reprogramming.

In addition, OsPR−1 and OsPR−1a expression correlated significantly with SA content in SGs but not in IGs (Supplemental Fig. 5). This indicates that these genes, known to be SA-induced during plant disease resistance in rice (Yasuda et al. 2009), also function in the rice NHT response. In IGs, only OsPYL4 expression was significantly correlated with six hormones (Supplemental Fig. 5B); other genes showed no significant correlation, consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 3G). This suggests that, compared with SGs, IGs mobilize fewer hormone-related genes in response to NHT stress, resulting in more severe impacts.

Discussion

Global climate change is increasing nighttime temperatures, which has emerged as a significant factor affecting crop growth and yield during key development stages, such as flowering and grain filling (Bahuguna et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019; Coast et al. 2020; Impa et al. 2020). As one of the world’s most important food crops, understanding the molecular mechanisms of rice’s response to NHT is crucial. Through a two-year field experiment, we systematically evaluated the effects of elevated nighttime temperatures during the early grain-filling stage on yield and quality traits in SGs and IGs of two rice vatieties. We observed that under normal conditions, SGs of both japonica rice varieties had advantages over IGs in yield-related traits. This may be attributed to different regulatory mechanisms during the grain filling period, which result in higher yields for SGs. However, under NHT stress, the grain setting rate and hundred-grain weight of IGs in both varieties were more severely inhibited than those of SGs. For example, in variety N5055, the seed setting rate of IGs decreased significantly (by 14.47–28.22%) compared to that of SGs (8.10–12.90%). This suggests that the inherent grain filling disadvantage of IGs is further amplified under NHT stress. Notably, although the changes in grain size (length and width) were inconsistent between varieties, the general decrease in hundred-grain weight indicated that impaired grain filling was a core cause of yield reduction. In particular, in variety YND1, despite an increase in grain size, the hundred-grain weight decreased significantly. This strongly suggests that NHT disrupt the transport and/or accumulation of photosynthates rather than affecting grain morphology. To elucidate this phenomenon, we analyzed the expression patterns of the sucrose synthase family in SGs and IGs under NHT stress. This enzyme family contributes to grain assimilate accumulation by mediating starch and sucrose metabolism throughout the grain filling stage (Li et al. 2024). We observed that NHT stress significantly downregulated the transcript levels of OsSUS1, OsSUS2, and OsSUS5 were in IGs, wherease no significant alterations were detected in SGs (Supplymentary Fig. 11). Interestingly, the expression of OsSUS4 was markedly upregulated in SGs. Collectively, these findings indicate that NHT potentially impairs the supply and partitioning of assimilates by repressing the expression of sucrose metabolism-related genes in IGs. This differential transcriptional response may partially account for the marked inhibition of IG filling relative to SG filling under NHT stress,which ultimately exacerbates the deterioration of grain traits. Therefore, we deduce that NHT reduce rice yield by severely perturbing the grain filling process, especially in IGs, which inherently exhibit yield disadvantage. Consequently, stabilizing or further enhancing IGs yield under NHT stress is pivotal for adapting to global climate warming and ensuring future rice production. Moreover, NHT is known to significantly affect crop quality (Lu et al. 2014; Hafeez et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2024). In the present study, NHT significantly altered only the amylose content of SGs across both varieties, whereas the amylopectin and total starch contents in SGs, as well as all starch components in IGs, remained unaffected. However, our transcriptome data demonstrated that the expression of the OsSSIIIa gene, a starch synthase and a key enzyme mediating amylopectin synthesis (Ryoo et al. 2007), was significantly downregulated in both SGs and IGs under NHT stress (Supplymentary Fig. 12). Despite the downward trend in amylopectin content across both rice cultivars, this reduction did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may stem from statistical constraints, a limitation that could be mitigated by increasing sample size, given the observed downward trend in amylopectin content.

Multi-omics analysis is a powerful approach for elucidating plant response mechanisms to adverse stress and has been widely applied across multiple species (Naik et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2023; Singh et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025). Our multi-omics data revealed that NHT altered the abundance of numerous metabolites (SGs:193; IGs: 156 DAMs) and the expression of many genes (SGs: 2420; IGs: 3266 DEGs) in N5055 grains, indicating a complex remodeling process in response to stress. Lipid metabolism is one of the most significantly affected pathways, with lipids comprising 17.46% of the differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs). Lipids, particularly membrane lipids, are essential for maintaining the integrity and fluidity of cell membranes (Yu et al. 2021). High temperatures are known to disrupt cell membrane stability and induce lipid peroxidation (Narayanan et al. 2016). We observed changes in numerous lipid metabolites. Concurrently, transcriptomic data revealed significantly enriched of genes related to protein processing in the ER and proteasome in IGs under stress condition, strongly suggesting that NHT may disrupt ER homeostasis. This disruption likely triggers membrane lipid remodeling and impairs cellular function. Amino acid metabolism also exhibited significant changes (DAMs accounted for 16.42%). Several amino acids, such as proline and glycine betaine, play important roles in osmotic regulation and oxidative protection (Ghosh et al. 2022; Khalid et al. 2022). In our data, amino acid metabolites accumulated significantly in both SGs and IGs, possibly reflecting osmoregulate response to mitigate NHT stress. Importantly, the metabolome and transcriptome data provided strong cross-validation for hormone-related pathways. We detected significant changes in phytohormones, including ABA, IAA, JA, and CKs, at the metabolite level, alongside differential expression of hormone signaling-related genes (e.g., OsJAZ7 and OsPYL4) at the transcriptome level. This concordance greatly strengthens the reliability of our conclusions. Of the 10 hormone signaling genes identified, eight were differentially expressed specifically in SGs. This indicates that NHT precisely reprograms core hormone signaling genes, which coordinates downstream physiological and metabolic responses and ultimately contributes to the distinct phenotypes of SGs and IGs.

Hormone profiling demonstrated that NHT specifically triggered accumulation of multiple cytokinins (e.g., TZ, DZ and TZR) and the expression of their putative response genes (OsRR3 and OsRR6) in SGs, thereby offering novel insights into the site-specific NHT tolerance mechanism in grains. Notably, previous studies have established that exogenous cytokinin application can markedly enhance crop yield and stress resistance (Kosakivska et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2022). Our findings align with this observetion, indicating that SGs elevate endogenous cytokinin levels via the expression of cytokinin-responsive genes. Furthermore, these gene-specific transcriptional changes in the cytokinin signaling pathway further corroborate the metabolite-level observations of cytokinin accumulation in SGs under NHT stress. Specifically, the specific downregulation of OsIPT8 and upregulation of OsRR3/OsRR6 indicate a fine-tuned regulatory mode, whereby NHT does not activate the entire cytokinin pathway but instead targets key regulatory nodes to sustain cytokinin homeostasis in SGs. This regulatory pattern aligns well with previous reports demonstrating that cytokinin-mediated stress tolerance often depends on the precise modulation of core pathway components or genes rather than widespread pathway activation (Jameson and Song 2016; Mas-ud et al. 2025). Collectively, the coordinated upregulation of OsRR3 and OsRR6 in SGs likely enhances cytokinin signal transduction efficiency, thereby promoting endosperm cell proliferation and sustaining grain filling under NHT stress, which is a protective adaptive mechanism absent in IGs. Nevertheless, although our transcriptomic data did not detect other significant alterations in cytokinin pathway genes, future investigations could explore whether post-transcriptional regulation of cytokinin transporters contributes to the differential cytokinin partitioning between SGs and IGs. Taken together, the integration of cytokinin metabolite, transcriptomic, and pathway gene expression data establishes a putative molecular framework underlying SG-specific NHT tolerance.

Beyond the cytokinin-mediated pathway alone, the antagonistic interaction between cytokinin and ABA constitutes a core regulatory module for plant stress responses (Huang et al. 2018; Singh and Roychoudhury 2023). Typically, under NHT stress, ABA accumulates and initiates senescence-related processes, thereby inhibiting grain filling. Consistent with this antagonistic relationship, In the present study, we detected a significant negative correlation between cytokinin and ABA levels in SGs. Additionally, OsRR3 and OsRR6 expression was also negatively correlated with ABA levels. Therefore, we hypothesize that in SGs, NHT-induced OsRR3 and OsRR6 active cytokinin signaling, which likely represses ABA-mediated senescence programs. This protective cascade ensures sustained progression of grain filling, in contrast, IGs undergo premature developmental termination due to the absence of this protective regulatory mechanism.

Conclusion

This study investigated the impact of NHT on rice yield and quality. The results showed that NHT significantly reduced rice yield by disrupting grain filling, with IGs being more susceptible to this damage than SGs. This differential sensitivity originated from distinct molecular and physiological responses in the two grain types. Integrated multi-omics analyses revealed that SGs activated more robust defense mechanisms, characterized by specific upregulation of cytokinins and key regulatory genes such as OsRR3 and OsRR6. In contrast, IGs exhibited a less coordinated response, resulting in more severe impairment. Our findings establish cytokinin-mediated signaling as a potential mechanism for NHT tolerance and provide potential targets for breeding more resilient rice varieties adapted to a warming climate.

Materials and Methods

Planting and Nighttime High Temperature Treatment

Two japonica rice cultivars, N5055 and YND1, were selected for this study due to their widespread cultivation in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River in China, as well as distinct advantages in agronomic traits and genetic characteristics. Their seeds were first surface-sterilized in a carbendazim solution for 24 h, followed by soaking in sterile water for another 24 h. Subsequently, seeds were sown in seedling trays in a greenhouse (25 ℃/20 ℃, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod) at the Botanical Garden of Nantong University, Jiangsu Province, China (31°58′N, 120°54′E; 72 m altitude). At approximately 20 days after sowing, uniform seedlings were transplanted into 16-L plastic buckets filled with topsoil from a local paddy field (6 seedlings per bucket) with a total of 40 buckets per cultivar placed in an open field under natural conditions. Basal fertilization wa applied to each bucket on the transplanting day: urea (1 g N), calcium superphosphate (0.3 g P), and potassium chloride (0.5 g K). Additional urea was applied at the pre-tillering stage and the panicle initiation stage, supplying 0.5 g and 0.8 g of pure nitrogen per bucket, respectively. Except for a brief dry-down at the end of the tillering stage, a 1–2 cm water layer was maintained in each bucket throughout the growth period until one week before harvest. Diseases and pests were strictly controlled during the entire growth period. All plants were grown under natural environmental conditions during daytime. On the second day after anthesis (early grain-filling stage), plants were divided into control (CK, no movement, natural nighttime temperature) and nighttime high temperature treatment groups, with ≥ 3 biological replicates (≥ 5 buckets per replicate, ≥ 15 buckets per cultivar per treatment). The treatment group was transferred daily to a climate-controlled growth chamber from 18:00 to 08:00 the next day (maintained at ~ 4 ℃ above the ambient nighttime temperature of control under dark conditions), then returned to the open field (Supplemental Table 6). This 20-day NHT treatment was followed by open-field growth until maturity (Supplymentary Fig. 13). Consistently with the known disparity in filling efficiency between SGs and IGs (Yuan et al. 2018), the two grain types were sampled separately. SGs includes all grains on the upper primary branches excluding their second and thrid grains, plus the first grain on each upper secondary branch; IGs were comprised the second and thrid grains on the lower primary and secondary branches (Fig. 1A). 10 days after NHT initiation, SGs and IGs were sampled: at least 10 uniform panicles were pooled per biological replicate, with 3 or 6 biological replicates per group. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80℃ for multi-omics analysis. At maturity, SGs and IGs were harvested separately for to determine yield- and quality-related phenotypic traits. This experiment was replicated over two growing seasons (2022–2023).

Measurement of Rice Yield and Quality-Related Traits

During the rice flowering stage, at least 60 panicles with synchronized anthesis were selected and tagged (20 panicles per biological replicate, ≥ 3 biological replicates per cultivar per treatment) to ensure consistent developmental stages across samples. NHT treatment was initiated on the second day post-anthesis, consistent with the experimental design described earlier. At maturity, the tagged panicles were harvested and air-dried under natural conditions. For each panicle, the total number of grains was counted manually, followed by separate collection of SGs and IGs according to the defined classification (Fig. 1A). Empty, shriveled and plump grains were counted separately for SGs and IGs, and the seed-setting rate was then calculated as the ratio of plump grains to the total number of grains for each grain type. Grain length, width and hundred-grain weight were determined using 10, 20 and 100 grains, respectively, with the SC-G automatic grain counting instrument (Wansheng, Hangzhou, China). Regarding quality-related traits, amylose content was determined via the iodine colorimetric method using a Multiskan GO colorimeter (Thermo, USA) (Khoomtong and Noomhorm 2015), based on the different affinities of amylose and amylopectin for iodine. Briefly, dried grains from each group were ground into fine powder using a high-speed grinder, passed through a 100-mesh sieve for uniformity, and the resulting powder samples were sealed and sent to Sanshu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nantong, Jiangsu, China) for total starch content determination. Amylopectin content was drived by subtracting amylose content from total starch content.

Analysis of Phenotypic Data

All yield- and quality-related phenotypic data were processed and analyzed to ensure statistical robustness. Technical replicates were averaged per biological replicate prior to subsequent analysis. Student’s t-test was employed for pairwise group comparisons, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE) with ≥ 3 biological replicates to guarantee reproducibility. Data visualization was performed using OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA), GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software (v4.4.1).

Metabolomic Profiling

After 10 days of NHT treatment, SGs and IGs samples from rice variety N5055 were collected from both control and treatment groups. These samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Six independent biological replicates per group were included per group, resulting in a total of 24 samples. Specifically, all samples were collected in a single growing season (2023) to eliminate interannual climatic and environmental variation, thus ensuring the reliability of molecular data. Such reliability aligns with the stable phenotypic responses of N5055, which were validated in our two-year field trials. Metabolomic analysis was performed using a non-targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) approach. Metabolites were extracted according to the method described previously (Vasilev et al. 2016). LC–MS analysis was conducted by Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Tech Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes. The spray voltage was set to 3.50 kV for positive mode and − 2.50 kV for negative mode. The sheath and auxiliary gas flow rates was set to 30 arb and 10 arb, respectively. The capillary temperature was maintained at 325 ℃. Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 over the m/z range of 100–1000. For MS/MS acquisition, higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was used with a normalized collision energy of 30%. Spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15,000. The top 4 most intense ions were selected for fragmentation, and dynamic exclusion was applied to prevent repeated fragmentation of the same ions (Want et al. 2013). Raw data files were converted to mzXML format using MSConvert (Smith et al. 2006). The data were then processed using the XCMS package in R (Navarro-Reig et al. 2015) for peak detection, filtering, and alignment, resulting in a table of quantified features. Metabolite identification was performed by querying features against public databases including HMDB, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, and KEGG (Kanehisa and Goto 2000; Sud et al. 2007; Wishart et al. 2007; Horai et al. 2010; Abdelrazig et al. 2020). A mass tolerance of less than 30 ppm was applied. The LOESS (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing) signal correction method was used to normalize the data and reduce systematic bias. Following normalization, only features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) below 30% in the quality control (QC) samples were retained to ensure data quality. To identify significant metabolites, univariate (P-value < 0.05) and multivariate analyses were performed. The variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were derived from an OPLS-DA model, and fold change (FC) was calculated. These metrics—P-value, VIP, and FC—were used to evaluate the discriminatory power of each metabolite for group separation. Differential metabolites were defined as those with a P-value is < 0.05 and a VIP score > 1. The identified differential metabolites were subjected to pathway enrichment and topological analysis using MetaboAnalyst software 5.0 (Xia and Wishart 2011). Subsequently, these metabolites were mapped to KEGG pathwaysto interpret their higher-level systemic functions.

Transcriptome Sequencing and Analysis

The same set of samples used for metabolomic analysis was employed for transcriptome sequencing. After 10 days of NHT treatment, SGs and IGs samples from N5055 were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Three independent biological replicates were collected per group, yielding a total of 12 samples for transcriptome sequencing. All samples were also collected in the same growing season with metabolomic analysis. The sequencing service was provided by Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Tech Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The transcriptome sequencing workflow, described previously (Wang et al. 2024b), included RNA extraction, quality assessment, library construction, and sequencing. Total RNA was extracted from the grains using a standard method. RNA integrity was rigorously assessed using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. Library quality was confirmed using Illumina HiSeq software. After quality filtering, the resulting clean reads were aligned to the IRGSP-1.0 reference genome (Shang et al. 2023). Base call quality was assessed using the Phred score (Q), calculated as Q = -10log₁₀(P), where P is the probability of an incorrect base call. Per the Illumina 1.8 + encoding, the Phred score is derived from the ASCII value of the quality character minus 33. A higher Q score denotes higher base-calling accuracy. Read alignment was performed using HISAT2 (Kim et al. 2019), which rapidly and accurately maps clean reads to the reference genome to determine their genomic positions. gene, differential expression analysis is performed using DESeq. Gene expression levels were quantified, and differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014). Genes with an absolute log2(fold change) > 2 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Volcano plots were generated to visualize DEGs, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the BioDeep platform (https://www.biodeep.cn).

Determination of Endogenous Hormones

Endogenous hormone levels were measured in SGs and IGs from N5055 subjected to either control conditions or 10 days of NHT stress. Three independent biological replicates per group were analyzed, resulting in a total of 12 samples. A total of 34 endogenous hormones were quantified, representing nine major classes: auxins (IAA), cytokinins (CK), gibberellin (GA), abscisic acid (ABA), ethylene (ETH)、brassinosteroids (BR), jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), and strigolactones (SL). For extraction, frozen samples were ground into fine powder. Subsequently, 50 mg of powder per sample was homogenized in a methanol:water:formic acid (15:4:1, v/v/v) solution. The extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane (Anpel) and analyzed by liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) (Hui et al. 2018). Metabolites were identified by matching the mass spectrometry data against the MWDB plant hormone database, which was established using authentic standards (Šimura et al. 2018). Hormone content was calculated using the following formula: content (ng/g) = C × V × D × 1000/m, where C is the concentration from the standard curve (ng/mL), V is the extractant volume (mL), D is the dilution factor, and m is the sample mass (mg). The endogenous hormone mearusement was conducted by Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China).

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Plant RNA extraction was performed using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Takara Bio Inc; Japan). with each sample weighing about 100 mg. The total RNA’s integrity was assessed via 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA concentrations were measured using a Nanodrop2000 microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). One milligram of total RNA for each sample was immediately used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA using the FastKing One Step Genomic cDNA First Strand Synthesis Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). The gene-specific primers for RT-qPCR analysis were designed using Primer 5.0 software, and the primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 7. RT-qPCR was conducted using Fast Start Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with a 7500 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). The rice OsActin-1 gene (LOC4333919) was utilized as the internal reference gene. Each sample and internal reference gene were replicated at least three times. The relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Data were presented as mean ± standard error (SE) and Student’s t-test was used for significance analysis, with P < 0.05 considered significant.
