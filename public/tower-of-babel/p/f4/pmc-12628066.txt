An injectable hyaluronic acid–silanol hydrogel containing arginine and puerarin for immune modulation and enhanced diabetic wound healing
Authors: Renwen Wan, Zhiheng Lin, Mowen Xu, Wei Luo, Hengjie Jia, Zhufeng Hu, Zhengyuan Fang, Junming Sun, Yisheng Chen, Shiyi Chen, Zhiwen Luo, Lei Yi, Zhijie Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic wound healing is a significant complication of diabetes mellitus, characterized by prolonged healing times, heightened infection risks, and potential amputations, necessitating innovative therapeutic approaches. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of a novel injectable hydrogel, AP@HA-Si InjGel, which combines hyaluronic acid, silanol, and bioactive compounds to promote wound healing in diabetic patients. Utilizing a combination of in vitro assays, diabetic mouse models, and advanced techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing, we demonstrated that AP@HA-Si InjGel significantly accelerates wound closure, enhances collagen deposition, and modulates macrophage polarization towards a pro-healing M2 phenotype while suppressing the inflammatory M1 phenotype. The hydrogel exhibited excellent biocompatibility with over 90 % cell viability and significant antioxidant properties, reducing reactive oxygen species accumulation. Histological analysis confirmed that AP@HA-Si InjGel promoted neovascularization and improved extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby enhancing tissue regeneration. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed distinct macrophage subtypes, with an increased proportion of M2 macrophages and a favorable differentiation trajectory towards enhanced wound healing. Overall, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of AP@HA-Si InjGel as a comprehensive strategy for diabetic wound management, warranting further exploration in clinical settings to address the growing challenge of impaired wound healing in diabetic patients.Keywords: Diabetic wound healing, Hydrogel, Macrophage polarization, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Immunomodulation

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder affecting more than one in ten adults globally, leading to numerous complications and a significant decrease in quality of life [1,2]. Among these complications, diabetic wounds, especially chronic, non-healing ones, are of particular concern [3]. These wounds are characterized by delayed healing, high susceptibility to infections, and increased healthcare costs, significantly impacting patients' well-being [4]. The pathophysiology of diabetic wounds involves a complex, dysregulated microenvironment, characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, impaired immune response, inadequate vascularization, and reduced collagen synthesis. These factors contribute to prolonged healing, infections, and, in severe cases, amputations [5]. Despite a variety of available treatment options such as surgical debridement, negative pressure wound therapy, and wound offloading, clinical outcomes for diabetic wound healing remain unsatisfactory [6,7]. This highlights the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Recent advancements in biomaterials have provided promising alternatives to improve diabetic wound healing. Multifunctional injectable hydrogels, in particular, have garnered attention for their ability to deliver bioactive signals, promote tissue regeneration, and modulate immune responses [8]. These hydrogels offer versatility, providing both physical support and bioactive cues that are essential for guiding the cellular behaviors involved in wound repair[[9], [10], [11]]. By integrating a range of biological components, such as growth factors, cytokines, and antimicrobial agents, hydrogels can address the multifaceted challenges that complicate diabetic wound healing [12]. Biomaterials like hydrogels have emerged as promising candidates for wound healing applications, offering the dual benefit of physical protection and the delivery of multi-modal signals that guide cellular behaviors [13,14]. Unlike traditional cell-specific biochemical signals that primarily target specific cellular pathways, hydrogels can provide physical structural signals that regulate cell behavior through physical contact and cellular mechano-sensing pathways. These structural cues can influence various aspects of tissue regeneration, such as cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation [15]. Hydrogels with anisotropic structures, including nanofibril-sponge, nanofiber hydrogel films, and oriented fibrous hydrogels, have been particularly studied for their ability to enhance cell behavior. These structures provide aligned structural guidance that supports cytoskeletal extension and cell orientation, thereby improving moisture transport, enhancing cell adhesion, and offering functionalization opportunities for tissue repair [16,17].

Incorporating bioactive components such as hyaluronic acid (HA), silanol (Si), arginine (Arg), and puerarin (PUE) into these hydrogels further enhances their wound-healing properties. HA, a naturally occurring polysaccharide, plays a vital role in tissue regeneration by promoting cell migration and modulating inflammation. Its incorporation into hydrogels improves biocompatibility and supports the tissue repair process, accelerating wound healing by improving cellular infiltration and stimulating collagen deposition [18,19]. Silanol, a silicate-based compound, improves the mechanical properties of hydrogels, enhancing scaffold stability and promoting tissue regeneration by improving cell adhesion and proliferation [20,21]. Arginine, an amino acid, supports collagen synthesis, modulates the immune response, and plays a significant role in nitric oxide production, which regulates blood flow and tissue repair. It has been shown to stimulate fibroblast activity and enhance collagen deposition, both critical for wound healing [[22], [23], [24]]. Puerarin, a flavonoid derived from the plant Pueraria lobata, provides potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. It helps reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, which are key factors in the pathophysiology of diabetic wounds. Puerarin-based hydrogels have demonstrated the ability to promote tissue repair by reducing oxidative damage and enhancing cell survival under stress conditions [[25], [26], [27]].

Building upon the beneficial properties of these individual components, the AP@HA-Si InjGel is synthesized by integrating HA, Si, Arg, and PUE into a single hydrogel system. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of AP@HA-Si InjGel in promoting wound healing in diabetic models. By combining the unique properties of each component, we hypothesize that the hydrogel will modulate inflammation, enhance angiogenesis, and promote collagen remodeling, addressing the key barriers to effective diabetic wound healing. The synthesis of AP@HA-Si InjGel and its potential for enhancing tissue repair are explored through a combination of in vitro and in vivo experiments, including cell culture, immunofluorescence analysis, and single-cell RNA sequencing. The results from this study will provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of AP@HA-Si InjGel in diabetic wound healing (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of AP@HA-Si InjGel

AP@HA-Si InjGel was synthesized through a two-step crosslinking procedure. Initially, 1.0 g of hyaluronic acid (HA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature (∼25 °C). After complete dissolution, 2.0 mL of polyethoxysilane (silanol, Aladdin, China) was added dropwise to the solution under vigorous stirring for 2 h to initiate silanol-based pre-crosslinking. Following this, 0.5 g of L-arginine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 0.3 g of puerarin (Yuanye Bio-Technology, China) were added to the pre-crosslinked solution and stirred for an additional 1 h to ensure uniform distribution of the bioactive compounds within the hydrogel matrix. Chemical crosslinking was then induced by adding 0.2 mL of hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to the solution containing 1.0 g HA, 2.0 mL silanol, 0.5 g arginine, and 0.3 g puerarin in 100 mL deionized water. HDI contains reactive –NCO groups that form covalent bonds with –OH and –COOH groups on hyaluronic acid and silanol via urethane and urea linkages, resulting in a stable chemically crosslinked hydrogel network. The mixture was stirred for 1 h and then cast into polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) molds, followed by curing at room temperature for 24 h. After curing, the hydrogels were demolded and washed thoroughly with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove any unreacted components. To prepare samples for microstructure observation, the hydrogels were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles (freezing at −20 °C for 12 h followed by thawing at room temperature for 6 h) and subsequently freeze-dried. This protocol facilitates formation of dry, porous cross-sections suitable for scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Apreo 2, USA) imaging and structural analysis. The morphology was observed by SEM. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Thermo Axia, USA) mapping analyzed elemental distribution. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo K-Alpha) characterized chemical bonding. Rheological properties were evaluated using a rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar). Drug release kinetics were measured by UV–vis spectrophotometry (GENESYS 50, Thermo). Adhesion properties were tested on skin, glass, metal, and polymers.

Preparation of AP@HA-Si InjGel extract for injection

AP@HA-Si InjGel was sterilized under ultraviolet (UV) light for 30 min to eliminate any microbial contaminants. Following sterilization, the hydrogel was added to Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow the extraction of bioactive components from the hydrogel. After incubation, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to remove any particulate matter or undissolved components. The resulting filtered solution was stored at 4 °C for short-term storage or −20 °C for long-term preservation [28].

Cell isolation and culture

RAW 264.7 macrophage cells and L929 fibroblast cells were cultured in appropriate media. RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). L929 cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco, USA) with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, and the medium was changed every two days. Only early-passage cells (passages 3–6) were used for experiments.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China). L929 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000–5000 cells per well and incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h. After treatment with hydrogel extracts, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Multiskan GO, USA).

Live/dead cell staining

Live/dead cell staining was performed to assess cell viability after treatment with AP@HA-Si InjGel extracts. L929 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10^4 cells per well. After 24, 48, and 72 h of treatment with hydrogel extracts, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Live cells were stained with 2 μM Calcein-AM (Thermo Fisher, USA), which emits green fluorescence, while dead cells were stained with 4 μM Propidium Iodide (PI, Thermo Fisher, USA), which emits red fluorescence. The cells were incubated with the staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After staining, the cells were washed with PBS and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany). The percentage of live and dead cells was quantified using ImageJ software based on fluorescence intensity.

BrdU assay

BrdU incorporation assays were conducted to assess cell proliferation [29]. L929 cells were incubated with 10 μM BrdU for 4 h, followed by fixation with 4 % paraformaldehyde. After permeabilization and blocking, cells were incubated with an anti-BrdU antibody and stained with DAPI. The BrdU-positive cells were quantified using fluorescence microscopy.

ROS assay

The reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in L929 cells were assessed using a DCFH-DA assay (Beyotime, China). To induce oxidative stress, cells were pre-treated with 300 μM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 2 h prior to hydrogel extract treatment. After treatment with hydrogel extracts, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 20 min at 37 °C. ROS generation was visualized using a fluorescence microscope, and the intensity of the fluorescence was quantified using Image J software.

Wound healing assay

To assess the wound healing ability of AP@HA-Si InjGel, L929 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until they formed a confluent monolayer. A sterile pipette tip was used to create a scratch in the monolayer. Cells were then treated with hydrogel extracts. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.5 % crystal violet solution for 10 min to enhance contrast in imaging. Images of the wound area were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h. The extent of wound closure was calculated using ImageJ software by measuring the percentage of the wound area covered over time.

Macrophage polarization assay

In vitro Macrophage polarization

To evaluate the effect of AP@HA-Si InjGel on macrophage polarization, RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10^5 cells per well and cultured in standard growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin) for 24 h to allow proper attachment. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h to induce the M1 macrophage phenotype, simulating an inflammatory microenvironment. For control, cells were treated with standard growth medium without LPS. Following LPS stimulation, cells were treated with the AP@HA-Si InjGel extracts, which were prepared by dissolving the hydrogel in DMEM at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL for 24 h.

Immunofluorescence staining for Macrophage markers

After 72 h of incubation with the hydrogel extracts, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 10 min. To block nonspecific binding, cells were incubated with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h. Primary antibodies targeting M1 markers (iNOS and CD86) and M2 markers (CD163) were applied at a dilution of 1:500, and the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were anti-iNOS (Abcam, UK), anti-CD86 (Abcam, UK), and anti-CD163 (Abcam, UK). After overnight incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (green fluorescence) or Alexa Fluor 594 (red fluorescence) at a dilution of 1:500 for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, UK) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, UK). The cells were then washed with PBS and stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, Thermo Fisher, USA) for 5 min to visualize the nuclei. Immunofluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry analysis

To further confirm the polarization of macrophages, flow cytometry was performed to analyze the expression of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD163 (M2 marker). Following the treatment with hydrogel extracts, cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS. The cells were incubated with anti-CD86-PE (eBioscience, USA) and anti-CD163-FITC (eBioscience, USA) antibodies at 1:500 dilution for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSVerse, USA). Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA), and the percentage of CD86+ (M1) and CD163+ (M2) macrophages was determined.

Establishment of the T2DM model

Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 7 weeks, were randomly divided into two groups: the control group (n = 10) and the type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) model group (n = 70). The control group was fed a normal chow diet, while the T2DM group was fed a high-fat diet for 12 weeks to induce obesity and insulin resistance. After 12 weeks, the T2DM model was established through the administration of streptozotocin (STZ). The T2DM group received five consecutive daily intraperitoneal injections of STZ at a dose of 30 mg/kg/day, while the control group was injected with the same volume of sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5). STZ-induced damage to pancreatic β-cells resulted in elevated blood glucose levels, confirming the onset of diabetes.

Glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

The development of T2DM was assessed 8 days after the final STZ injection using an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT). Mice were fasted for 12 h overnight, with water provided ad libitum. Blood glucose levels were measured after a tail vein blood sample was collected for fasting glucose. Subsequently, glucose was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 2 g/kg body weight, and blood glucose levels were monitored at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection using a glucometer (Roche). The T2DM model was validated by observing impaired glucose clearance in the experimental group compared to the control group.

In vivo diabetic wound healing

Diabetic wound healing was evaluated using a full-thickness excisional wound model in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: Control, DM, HA-Si, and AP@HA-Si. The wound healing process was monitored over 14 days. Wounds were photographed, and the wound area was measured at days 7, 10, and 14 using ImageJ software. Histological analysis was performed on wound tissue sections stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome.

Immunofluorescence analysis for angiogenesis and collagen remodeling

For angiogenesis analysis, wound tissues were immunostained with a primary antibody against CD31, a marker for endothelial cells, followed by incubation with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, UK). The fluorescence intensity and vessel density were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany) and quantified using ImageJ software. For collagen deposition, immunofluorescence staining for type I and type III collagen was performed using anti-collagen I and anti-collagen III antibodies (Abcam, UK), and the secondary antibody was Alexa Fluor 594- or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (Abcam, UK). The fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software. To assess cell proliferation, tissues were stained for Ki67 (Abcam, UK), and for fibroblast differentiation, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, Abcam, UK) was used as a marker of myofibroblasts. VEGF (Abcam, UK) staining was performed to evaluate new vessel formation. All fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the intensity of the immunofluorescence staining was quantified with ImageJ software. The percentage of CD86+ (M1) and CD206+ (M2) macrophages was evaluated to assess macrophage polarization at both day 7 and day 14 time points. Statistical significance was assessed by comparing the data across experimental groups (Control, DM, HA–Si, and AP@HA–Si).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

All libraries were sequenced using Illumina sequencers running 150 bp on both ends. First, download the Cell Ranger (v.7.1.0) package from the official 10X Genomics website and follow the official tutorial to install it. Refer to the mouse genome provided by 10X Genomics official website. Use “Cell Ranger mkfastq” for data preprocessing, “Cell Ranger count” for gene expression quantitative analysis. Sequencing starts from the 3′ end.

The original gene expression data were imported into R software (v4.3.3) and analyzed by Seurat R software package (v4.4.0). After the double cells were removed, the DoubletFinder R package (v2.0.3) was used for quality control, and the thresholds were as follows:(1)300<nFeature<6000; (2) 500 < nCount <100000; (3) Mitochondrial gene expression does not exceed 25 % of the total number of genes in the cell; (4) The expression of erythroid genes does not exceed 5 % of the total number of genes in the cell.

The gene expression matrix of the remaining cells was generated was log-normalized using the "NormalizeData" functions in the Seurat R package. Then, the "FindVariable "function was used to identify the top 2000 genes with the largest variation. The “ScaleData” function was used for data standardized. To mitigate batch effects, the harmony package (v0.1.1) was used, and the first 30 principal components were selected for further analysis. Taking the first 30 principal components (with a resolution of 1.2) as input to the "FindClusters" function, different clusters of cells are eventually identified. In order to obtain specific gene expression scores for each cell type, we applied non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum tests between gene expression of focal cell types and gene expression of other cell types.

Enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler (v4.6.2) R package. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment [30] and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [31] were performed to identify significantly enriched biological pathways and functions. Pathways or functions with a p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Gene set score

We use the scgmt (v0.0.3, https://github.com/ZhaoLabs-SJTU/scgmt) package to calculate the signature activity score. We used AUCell (v1.22.0) to conduct quantitative analysis of pathway activity. Firstly, a predefined biological pathway gene set (Hallmark gene set) was input, and then the gene set enrichment degree of each cell was calculated by sequencing expression matrix. The AUC value range was 0–1, and the higher the value was, the more active the pathway was in the cell. We identify pathway-specific cell subgroups by threshold screening.

Cell development trajectory inference

CytoTRACE(v0.3.3) is a computational tool based on single-cell transcriptome data that enables the dynamic reconstruction of cell development trajectory by analyzing gene expression, thereby quantifying the degree of cell driness (i.e., differentiation potential).

In this study, we use the Monocle cell differentiation trajectory analysis, specific process includes: trajectory reconstruction: based on the single cell transcriptome data, reviewed 3 by machine learning algorithms to build the development relationship between cells, infer the potential differentiation path. Dimension reduction and visualization: adopt DDRTree (dimension reduction method based on graph) nonlinear dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional data, present trajectory, cell differentiation in low dimensional space while preserving development dynamic characteristics. Pseudo time analysis: along the reconstruction of pseudotime trajectory calculation, quantitative cell differentiation process, and monitor different subgroups distribution and dynamic change of cells on the track, reveals from the progenitor cells to the continuous transition process of terminal differentiation cells. The method for parsing cell fate determination and differentiation regulatory mechanism provides an important tool.

We further adopt Slingshot cell lineage inference algorithm, and through getCurves function fitting development path curve, to resolve dynamic cell differentiation.

Cell communication

We investigated interactions between different cell types at skin wounds using the CellChat (v1.6.1) R package, which enables quantitative inference and analysis of cell communication networks. We mainly used the "netVisual_circle" function of CellChat to present the corresponding results through hierarchy diagram, ligand-receptor heat map and chord diagram.

Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC)

SCENIC analysis was performed using SCENIC (v1.3.1). First, the co-expression module was calculated, and then the weight between the transcription factor and the target gene was evaluated. Next, AUCell was used to evaluate the activity of each regulator in each cell. Finally, ggplot2 was used to plot the rank diagram of the regulator.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Prism 9.5.1, USA) software. The results are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD), with individual data points shown. A t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, whereas two-way ANOVA and one-way ANOVA were employed for multiple group comparisons to determine differences. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The significance levels were classified as: (ns p > 0.05), (∗p < 0.05), (∗∗p < 0.01), (∗∗∗p < 0.001), and (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Result

Structural and physicochemical characterization of the AP@HA-Si InjGel

We developed a multifunctional injectable hydrogel (AP@HA-Si InjGel) through a chemical crosslinking strategy that integrates HA, Si, Arg, and PUE (Fig. 2A). The AP@HA–Si hydrogel underwent a sol–gel transition via chemical crosslinking at room temperature, forming a stable cohesive structure. This gelation behavior enabled in situ application, as illustrated by the pre-gel solution forming a free-standing hydrogel after crosslinking (Fig. 2B). The formulation demonstrated excellent injectability, producing self-supporting filaments when extruded (Fig. 2C). SEM revealed a highly porous, interconnected internal architecture composed of thin, layered walls (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Such microstructure facilitates nutrient exchange, cell infiltration, and therapeutic agent transport. EDS confirmed uniform distribution of elemental carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sodium (Na), and silicon (Si) across the network (Fig. 2E). XPS further verified the successful incorporation of each component, with clearly defined peaks corresponding to Si 2p, C 1s, N 1s, O 1s, and Na 1s (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Elemental quantification (Supplementary Fig. 1C) showed that carbon and oxygen predominated, while Si and Na were uniformly distributed. X-ray XPS analysis confirmed the presence of Si, C, N, and O elements (Fig. 2F), validating the successful integration of silanol, arginine, and puerarin components. Rheological measurements (Fig. 2G) revealed a dominant storage modulus (G′) over loss modulus (G″) in the linear viscoelastic region, indicating solid-like behavior with excellent mechanical stability. The hydrogel exhibited a sustained puerarin release profile over 14 days (Fig. 2H), supporting its long-term therapeutic potential. Enzymatic degradation assays demonstrated gradual matrix erosion, particularly in HA-rich formulations (Fig. 2I). Notably, the hydrogel showed robust adhesion to diverse substrates including skin, polymers, wood, glass, and metal (Fig. 2J), underscoring its conformability and broad applicability. Collectively, these findings highlight the AP@HA–Si InjGel's favorable physicochemical characteristics, biofunctionality, and translational suitability for wound healing applications.

Biocompatibility and antioxidant properties of AP@HA-Si InjGel

When hydrogel-based biomaterials are intended for therapeutic applications, excellent cytocompatibility is a prerequisite. To evaluate the biological safety of AP@HA-Si InjGel, we first investigated the effect of puerarin concentration on cell viability by fabricating hydrogels with low (0.1 g/100 mL), medium (0.3 g/100 mL), and high (0.6 g/100 mL) PUE contents. CCK-8 assays conducted at 24, 48, and 72 h revealed that only the High-PUE group exhibited significant cytotoxicity (p < 0.05), whereas the Low-PUE and Medium-PUE groups maintained high viability comparable to the control (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). Therefore, Medium-PUE was selected as the optimized concentration for subsequent hydrogel synthesis. Further cytocompatibility was validated through Live/Dead staining, where over 90 % cell survival was observed across all groups without significant differences relative to the control (Fig. 3A and B), suggesting minimal cytotoxicity of AP@HA-Si InjGel. Consistently, BrdU staining demonstrated comparable percentages of proliferating cells among the control, HA–Si, and AP@HA-Si groups (Fig. 3C and D), confirming that the hydrogel did not impair cell proliferation. Moreover, longitudinal CCK-8 analysis indicated that AP@HA-Si InjGel effectively supported cell growth over time, with no significant difference in viability compared to untreated cells (Fig. 3E). In addition to preserving cell viability and proliferation, AP@HA-Si InjGel promoted cell migration, as evidenced by accelerated wound closure rates in scratch assays compared to control and HA–Si-treated cells (Fig. 3F–H). Given that oxidative stress can severely impede wound healing, we further assessed the antioxidant capacity of AP@HA-Si InjGel under H2O2-induced stress. Fluorescence imaging revealed that AP@HA-Si InjGel markedly reduced intracellular ROS accumulation relative to the H2O2 and H2O2+HA–Si groups (Fig. 3I and J), highlighting its potent antioxidative function. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AP@HA-Si InjGel exhibits excellent cytocompatibility, enhances cell migration, and effectively mitigates oxidative stress, thereby offering significant therapeutic potential for regenerative medicine applications.

AP@HA-Si InjGel modulates macrophage polarization and mitigates inflammation

To investigate the immunomodulatory effect of AP@HA-Si InjGel, RAW 264.7 macrophages were cultured on standard medium, HA–Si hydrogel, or AP@HA-Si hydrogel for three days, followed by LPS stimulation for 24 h to mimic an inflammatory microenvironment. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that AP@HA-Si significantly suppressed macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, as indicated by reduced expression of iNOS and CD86 compared to the LPS group (Fig. 4A–B, D–E). Moreover, AP@HA-Si promoted polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, as evidenced by the increased expression of the M2 marker CD163 (Fig. 4C and F). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed these findings: the proportion of CD86+ macrophages was substantially lower in the AP@HA-Si group (43.7 %) than in the HA–Si (71.7 %) and LPS groups (73.0 %), while the proportion of CD163+ macrophages increased to 22.7 % in the AP@HA-Si group (Fig. 4G and H). These data collectively indicate that AP@HA-Si InjGel not only supresses pro-inflammatory M1 activation but also facilitates the transition of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This dual regulation of macrophage behavior suggests that AP@HA-Si InjGel has strong potential to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby promoting tissue repair and regeneration. Such an effect is particularly advantageous for chronic wounds or inflammatory diseases, where excessive M1 activation impedes healing. The ability of AP@HA-Si to rebalance macrophage polarization highlights its promise as an immunomodulatory platform in regenerative medicine.

AP@HA-Si InjGel accelerates diabetic wound healing in vivo

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of AP@HA-Si InjGel in vivo, a full-thickness excisional wound model was established in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice. Prior to modeling, the mice were fed a high-fat (high-sugar) diet for 12 weeks, which led to a significant increase in weight and the induction of insulin resistance, followed by intraperitoneal injections of STZ at a low dose (30 mg/kg) for 5 consecutive days. After the injections were completed, blood glucose levels were measured every 3 days. A fasting blood glucose level ≥11 mmol/L indicated successful model establishment (Supplementary Fig. 3). Mice were then divided into four groups: Control, DM (diabetes model without treatment), HA–Si, and AP@HA-Si (Fig. 5A). The wound healing process was monitored over 14 days. Macroscopic images showed that wounds in the DM group exhibited delayed healing compared to the Control group, whereas the AP@HA-Si-treated group demonstrated markedly accelerated wound closure (Fig. 5B). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the relative wound area in the AP@HA-Si group was significantly reduced compared to the DM and HA–Si groups at days 7, 10, and 14 (Fig. 5D). Histological assessment using H&E staining revealed faster re-epithelialization and narrower wound gaps in the AP@HA-Si group relative to the DM and HA–Si groups across all time points (Fig. 5C). Masson's trichrome staining indicated enhanced collagen deposition in the AP@HA-Si group, suggesting superior extracellular matrix remodeling. Measurements of wound length (Fig. 5E) and collagen volume fraction (Fig. 5F) further corroborated these findings, with AP@HA-Si-treated wounds showing significantly shorter wound lengths and higher collagen content compared to the DM and HA–Si groups. Collectively, these results demonstrate that AP@HA-Si InjGel effectively promotes wound closure, enhances tissue regeneration, and improves collagen remodeling in diabetic wounds, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for impaired wound healing.

AP@HA-Si InjGel enhances immunomodulation, angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and collagen remodeling in vivo

To further elucidate the regenerative mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of AP@HA-Si InjGel, we evaluated macrophage polarization, neovascularization, cellular proliferation, and collagen remodeling within the wound tissue using immunofluorescence staining. Macrophage polarization analysis demonstrated that AP@HA-Si significantly modulated the immune microenvironment. Compared to the DM and HA–Si groups, the AP@HA-Si group exhibited a notable decrease in the proportion of CD86+ M1 macrophages and a significant increase in CD206+ M2 macrophages at both day 7 and day 14 (Fig. 6A–C). This immunomodulatory shift toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype is crucial for promoting a pro-healing environment in chronic wounds. In parallel, neovascularization was markedly enhanced following AP@HA-Si treatment. Immunostaining for CD31, an endothelial cell marker, revealed a significantly higher vessel density in the AP@HA-Si group compared to other groups at both evaluated time points (Fig. 6D and E), indicating robust angiogenic stimulation. Co-staining with VEGF and Ki67 further confirmed that AP@HA-Si not only promoted vascular growth but also supported active cellular proliferation within the wound bed (Fig. 6F and G). Beyond immune and vascular modulation, AP@HA-Si InjGel also significantly influenced extracellular matrix remodeling. Dual immunostaining for collagen type I (COL I) and collagen type III (COL III) revealed enhanced collagen deposition and favorable matrix organization in the AP@HA-Si group at both day 7 and day 14 (Fig. 6H–J). Notably, the balanced increase in COL I and COL III suggests that AP@HA-Si supports the formation of a mature, mechanically competent tissue matrix, crucial for effective wound closure and long-term skin function restoration. Collectively, these results demonstrate that AP@HA-Si InjGel orchestrates a comprehensive regenerative response by modulating inflammation, enhancing neovascularization, stimulating cellular proliferation, and optimizing collagen matrix remodeling. These multifaceted biological effects likely underlie its potent ability to accelerate wound healing, particularly in the context of diabetic impaired tissue repair.

AP@HA–Si InjGel demonstrates excellent in vivo biosafety in C57BL/6 mice

To assess the systemic biosafety of AP@HA–Si InjGel, histological analysis of major organs—including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys—were performed on C57BL/6 mice 14 days post-treatment. Animals from all four experimental groups (Control, DM, HA–Si, and AP@HA–Si) were examined. H&E staining (Supplementary Fig. 8A) revealed preserved tissue architecture with no evidence of inflammation, necrosis, hemorrhage, or other pathological alterations in any group. Moreover, Masson's trichrome staining (Supplementary Fig. 8B) showed no abnormal collagen deposition or fibrosis. These findings indicate that AP@HA–Si InjGel exhibits excellent biocompatibility and systemic safety, supporting its suitability for in vivo therapeutic applications, particularly in the context of chronic wound repair and tissue regeneration.

Discussion

Diabetic wounds represent a significant complication of diabetes mellitus, imposing a considerable burden on patients and healthcare systems due to their association with increased morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, and heightened healthcare costs. The chronic nature of diabetic foot ulcers is attributed to a multitude of factors, including neuropathy, inadequate blood supply, and persistent inflammation, leading to impaired wound healing processes[[41], [42], [43]]. Despite advances in treatment strategies, including surgical interventions and various wound dressings, the efficacy of these approaches remains limited, highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies that can effectively address the complexities of diabetic wound healing [44,45].

This study investigates the phenotypic effects of a multifunctional injectable hydrogel, AP@HA-Si InjGel, on diabetic wound healing. The hydrogel's design aims to enhance tissue regeneration and modulate immune responses, which are crucial for effective healing in diabetic wounds. By employing a combination of in vitro and in vivo methodologies, the study evaluates the hydrogel's efficacy in promoting wound healing through mechanisms such as immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and collagen remodeling. The findings underscore the potential of AP@HA-Si InjGel as a promising therapeutic agent for improving healing outcomes in diabetic patients, thus addressing a critical clinical challenge [46,47].

The structural and physicochemical characterization of the AP@HA-Si InjGel demonstrates its potential as a multifunctional therapeutic agent for wound healing. The successful synthesis of the hydrogel, exhibiting a thermoresponsive sol-gel transition, enables in situ application and adaptability to complex wound geometries. The highly porous architecture, confirmed by scanning electron microscopy, facilitates nutrient exchange and therapeutic agent transport, essential for effective wound healing. Moreover, the rheological properties indicate a solid-like behavior, suggesting stability upon application. These features underscore the hydrogel's suitability for regenerative therapies, highlighting its capacity to support enhanced healing outcomes in diabetic wounds, where timely and effective treatment is critical [48,49].

The biocompatibility and antioxidant properties of AP@HA-Si InjGel position it as a promising candidate for regenerative medicine. The CCK-8 assays reveal that cytotoxicity is only observed at high concentrations of puerarin, with medium concentrations maintaining cell viability comparable to controls. Live/Dead staining further supports this finding, demonstrating over 90 % cell survival. Additionally, the hydrogel's ability to mitigate oxidative stress, as indicated by reduced reactive oxygen species accumulation, suggests its therapeutic potential in enhancing cell proliferation and migration, which are crucial for wound healing. The ability to promote cell survival under stress conditions is particularly significant in diabetic wounds, where oxidative stress is prevalent and contributes to impaired healing processes [50,51].

The modulation of macrophage polarization by AP@HA-Si InjGel represents a critical mechanism in promoting a pro-healing environment. The hydrogel effectively suppresses the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype while enhancing the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in macrophages. This dual modulation is evidenced by decreased expression of iNOS and CD86 alongside increased CD163 expression, which are markers of M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. Flow cytometry further confirms the significant reduction in pro-inflammatory macrophages in the presence of the hydrogel. By favoring an M2 polarization, AP@HA-Si InjGel not only diminishes inflammation but also fosters tissue repair, which is particularly beneficial in chronic wound scenarios where inflammation plays a pivotal role in delayed healing [52,53].

The efficacy of AP@HA-Si InjGel in accelerating diabetic wound healing is supported by in vivo studies, where treatment significantly reduces wound closure times compared to control groups. The histological analysis demonstrates enhanced re-epithelialization and collagen deposition, indicating improved extracellular matrix remodeling. This acceleration in wound healing is crucial for diabetic patients, as delayed healing can lead to severe complications, including infections and amputations. The findings suggest that integrating AP@HA-Si InjGel into standard wound care protocols could substantially improve clinical outcomes for diabetic patients, addressing a major healthcare challenge [54].

Importantly, the pro-angiogenic effects observed with AP@HA-Si InjGel are supported by both experimental outcomes and mechanistic plausibility based on its component functions. Immunofluorescence staining showed significantly enhanced expression of CD31 and VEGF in hydrogel-treated wounds, indicative of neovascularization and active endothelial proliferation. Mechanistically, both puerarin and L-arginine—core constituents of the hydrogel—have been independently reported to stimulate angiogenesis via distinct but complementary pathways. Puerarin, a natural isoflavone compound, has been shown to upregulate VEGF and HIF-1α expression promoting endothelial migration and vessel sprouting [55,56]. Meanwhile, L-arginine serves as the physiological substrate for nitric oxide synthase (NOS), leading to production of nitric oxide (NO), a potent vasodilator and angiogenic mediator[[57], [58], [59]]. NO facilitates endothelial cell proliferation and enhances vascular remodeling through eNOS-mediated pathways[[60], [61], [62]]. The synergistic interplay between these two bioactive compounds within the AP@HA-Si InjGel system may thus underlie the observed increases in vessel formation. Furthermore, the controlled release kinetics of L-arginine and puerarin from the hydrogel matrix ensures a sustained pro-angiogenic microenvironment conducive to granulation tissue formation and oxygenation. While our current study did not include in vitro tube formation or angiogenesis-blocking experiments, the combination of in vivo histological data and literature-supported mechanisms provides a reasonable biological basis for the angiogenic effects of the hydrogel. In future studies, pathway inhibition or transgenic models may be used to dissect the specific contributions of each pathway to vascular regeneration.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the lack of clinical validation restricts the translational applicability of the findings, as results obtained in animal models may not fully replicate human responses. Additionally, the relatively small sample size in the in vivo experiments may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Furthermore, potential batch effects from using multiple data sources in single-cell RNA sequencing could introduce variability in the transcriptomic analyses, impacting the reliability of cellular insights. Collectively, these limitations underscore the necessity for future studies to address these gaps through larger, more comprehensive clinical trials that can substantiate the efficacy and safety of AP@HA-Si InjGel in human subjects.

In conclusion, AP@HA-Si InjGel demonstrates substantial potential as a multifunctional therapeutic agent for diabetic wound healing, exhibiting impressive structural properties, biocompatibility, and immunomodulatory effects. The hydrogel's ability to promote macrophage polarization, enhance angiogenesis, and facilitate collagen remodeling illustrates its capacity to create a conducive environment for tissue regeneration. These multifaceted biological effects not only highlight the promise of AP@HA-Si InjGel in addressing the challenges of impaired wound healing but also emphasize the need for continued research to optimize its formulation and explore its clinical applications in regenerative medicine.
