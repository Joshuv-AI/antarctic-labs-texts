Switching Patients with Schizophrenia from Intramuscular Paliperidone Palmitate Once Monthly to TV-46000, a Long-Acting Subcutaneous Antipsychotic: Population Pharmacokinetic–Based Strategies
Authors: Itay Perlstein, Jonathan Meyer, Ziqi Yue, Vijay Ivaturi, Kelli R Franzenburg, Mark Suett, Rolf Hansen, Avia Merenlender Wagner, Arti Phatak, Rajendra Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPharmacokinetic differences between long-acting injectable antipsychotic (LAI) formulations, combined with a lack of clinical switch studies, contribute to clinician uncertainty when transitioning between LAIs. This analysis employed a population pharmacokinetic (popPK) modeling approach to characterize dosing conversions and switching strategies from intramuscular paliperidone palmitate once monthly (PP1m) to TV-46000, a long-acting subcutaneous formulation of risperidone, once monthly (q1m), with a secondary analysis of PP1m to TV-46000 every 2 months (q2m).MethodsFor PP1m and TV-46000, concentration–time profiles for paliperidone and TV-46000 total active moiety (TAM; risperidone + paliperidone) were simulated on the basis of published popPK models with virtual populations of 5000 patients. TAM exposure following oral administration served as a comparison benchmark.ResultsWhen switching from PP1m 234 mg at steady state (comparable to 6 mg/day oral risperidone), the most comparable switch involved initiating TV-46000 125 mg q1m 4 weeks after the last PP1m dose. Ratios of TV-46000 to PP1m for maximum, minimum, and average plasma concentration (Cmax, Cmin, and Cavg, respectively) post switch ranged from 1.0 to 1.4 after the first dose and 1.0–1.3 at steady state. Switching from other PP1m doses to TV-46000 q1m (comparable to 2–4 mg daily oral risperidone) using a 4-week interval demonstrated comparable TAM exposures. When switching from PP1m 234 mg to TV-46000 250 mg q2m, Cmax was higher and Cmin lower than that of q1m, though Cavg remained comparable to 5/6 mg daily oral risperidone.ConclusionSwitching to TV-46000 125 mg q1m 4 weeks after the last PP1m 234-mg injection yielded generally comparable pharmacokinetic parameters at steady state. The same was true for other TV-46000 q1m or q2m dosages and equivalent dosages of PP1m. Clinician discretion will ultimately determine how to switch on the basis of factors such as patient preference, convenience, and concerns about tolerability or symptom breakthrough.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03329-x.Keywords: Long-acting injectable antipsychotic, Paliperidone, Population pharmacokinetic modeling, Risperidone, Schizophrenia, TV-46000

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Pharmacokinetic differences between long-acting injectable antipsychotic (LAI) formulations, combined with a lack of clinical switch studies, contribute to clinician uncertainty when transitioning between LAIs.This analysis characterized dosing conversions and switching strategies from intramuscular paliperidone palmitate once monthly (PP1m) to TV-46000, a long-acting subcutaneous formulation of risperidone, once monthly, with a secondary analysis of PP1m to TV-46000 every 2 months. What was learned from the study? The most comparable switching scenarios based on population pharmacokinetic modeling involved initiating the comparable TV-46000 dose 4 weeks after the last dose of PP1m.Clinician discretion will ultimately determine how to switch on the basis of a variety of factors such as patient preference, scheduling convenience, and concerns about tolerability or symptom breakthrough.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe and disabling chronic mental disorder characterized by positive symptoms (e.g., delusions and hallucinations), negative symptoms (e.g., deficits in expression of affect), and cognitive symptoms (e.g., working memory deficits) [1]. Pharmacotherapy for schizophrenia consists of antipsychotic treatment, with formulations including oral antipsychotics (OAs), short-acting injectable antipsychotics, and long-acting injectable antipsychotics (LAIs) [2]. Although results from randomized controlled trials are mixed [3], real-world studies have shown that during maintenance therapy, LAIs are associated with greater adherence [4–7] and lower rates of relapse/hospitalization compared with OAs [4, 8–10]. Certain retrospective studies have also noted that LAIs are associated with lower rates of mortality and suicidal behavior [8, 11, 12] and lower inpatient/emergency department visit costs compared with OAs [13–15].

Clinicians may switch the treatment of patients receiving an LAI to a different LAI for several reasons: patient preference, tolerability or efficacy issues, different dosing intervals, or cost concerns [16]. In addition to differences in pharmacodynamic properties of antipsychotics, there are numerous clinical factors to be considered when switching between LAI preparations. Foremost among the patient-related variables of concern are illness severity, history of response to and tolerability of prior antipsychotic regimens, concomitant medications, comorbid medical and psychiatric conditions, and risk of relapse if the switching strategy results in prolonged periods of subtherapeutic antipsychotic exposure [17].

The risk of inadequate exposure during LAI switching relates to inherent pharmacokinetic (PK) differences between various LAIs, and a paucity of clinical trials on transitioning between LAIs to inform clinicians regarding optimal switch strategies [18]. As a result of the lack of direct switch studies, clinicians are left to their own judgement to ascertain the impact of varying PK properties among LAI preparations (e.g., time to peak concentration and time to steady state), in addition to factoring in any pharmacodynamic differences between antipsychotics involved in the switch [18]. Even when the switch involves identical molecules, the distinct PK aspects of LAIs make transitioning more complex than OA switches. One unique PK property of LAIs is rooted in the fact that the rate at which the antipsychotic is absorbed from the injection site is slower than the rate of hepatic or renal elimination (i.e., “flip-flop kinetics”); therefore, the plasma half-life for LAIs is determined by absorption rate. In most instances, this absorption is slow (days or weeks), and the antipsychotic is gradually released into systemic circulation when the injected particles slowly dissolve, or the antipsychotic diffuses from the oil vehicle [19]. The water solubility of the drug, delivery vehicle, and patient factors (e.g., body weight, subcutaneous adipose tissue, vascularity of site of injection) all influence absorption, with the net effect being that injection site absorption differs considerably between LAIs. Additionally, the characteristics of delayed release for some LAIs also complicate transitioning between LAI regimens, especially when oral supplementation or multiple initiation or loading injections might be required. For example, the standard initiation scheme for intramuscular (im) paliperidone palmitate once monthly (PP1m) involves a loading approach consisting of a 234-mg injection in the deltoid muscle followed by 156 mg im in the deltoid 8 days later, with the first maintenance dose given 5 weeks after the first injection [20].

As newer LAI technologies emerge, clinicians may be motivated to switch patients between LAIs for reasons such as patient convenience, dosing interval, or ease of administration. TV-46000 is a long-acting subcutaneous antipsychotic designed to deliver risperidone in once-monthly (q1m) or once-every-2-month (q2m) intervals, without the need for oral loading or initiation injections [21]. In addition, a single needle size can be used for any injections of subcutaneous formulations such as TV-46000 whereas needle size for intramuscular formulations varies according to the body mass index (BMI) of a patient [22]. Subcutaneous formulations may also be viewed as being less invasive and easy to use in clinical practice, which is preferred by some clinicians and patients, when compared to intramuscular formulations [23]. For risperidone formulations such as TV-46000, total active moiety (TAM) exposure is composed of approximately 19% risperidone and 81% 9-OH risperidone (also known as paliperidone). As both risperidone and paliperidone are pharmacologically active, there may be clinical interest in switching from PP1m to TV-46000 for reasons noted above; however, clinicians must have a working knowledge of the PK properties and dose strengths of each formulation to determine the appropriate switching interval and dosage. Data on how various switching strategies impact plasma TAM levels are thus necessary to ensure that patients receive the appropriate antipsychotic exposure during the transition.

While the TAM for a risperidone LAI is the sum of risperidone + 9-OH risperidone (paliperidone) levels, for paliperidone LAI formulations such as PP1m, the TAM is composed solely of 9-OH risperidone (paliperidone). The median time to maximum concentration in the blood (Cmax) for PP1m is approximately 13 days [20]. After the required loading doses [20], monthly maintenance doses of PP1m range from 78 to 234 mg, which corresponds to either daily 3–12 mg/day oral paliperidone [20] or 2–6 mg/day oral risperidone [24]. Simulation results from the PP1m PK model suggest that steady state for PP1m is achieved within 1 week of initiation for 52–84% of patients when using the loading procedure [25]. For TV-46000, the median Cmax ranges from 8 to 14 days, and > 70% of steady state for area under the curve (AUC) and Cmax TAM exposure based on sequential simulated concentration–time profiles are approached within 2 months of LAI initiation [21]. TV-46000 is released from the subcutaneous injection site after the first injection and achieves clinically relevant TAM plasma levels (> 10 ng/mL) within 6–24 h, obviating the need for oral supplementation or loading injections. The dose of TV-46000 ranges from 50 to 125 mg (q1m) or from 100 to 250 mg (q2m), corresponding to a daily oral dose of 2–5 mg risperidone, respectively [21].

For clinical use, simulations with popPK models indicate how drug levels change over time and how they may vary between patients. These models are tested for reliability and accuracy, allowing clinicians to make informed decisions about different dosing options [26]. While these models cannot replicate clinical trials, the insights they provide can guide future studies and assist clinicians when adjusting doses or switching between LAIs. Therefore, the objective of this study was to use population PK (popPK) models to compare exposures and evaluate simulated dosing conversions and switching strategies from PP1m to TV-46000 q1m or q2m.

Methods

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants.

PopPK Models Used in This Study

All involved study centers of the original clinical trials received approval by local ethics committees and informed consent was received for all patients. This analysis used published popPK models for PP1m [24] and TV-46000 [27]. TV-46000 PK data was obtained from five clinical trials (Table S1): three phase 1 studies and two phase 3 studies. The PP1m PK data was obtained from six phase 1 studies and five phase 2 and 3 clinical trials. Several PP1m models were considered for the switching simulations and included Samtani 2009 [24], Samtani 2011 [25], Coppola 2012 [28], Korell 2017 [29], and Russu 2018 [30]. The Samtani 2009 model [24] was selected because of the rich dataset for model development and because the other models considered either used or verified the Samtani 2009 model. Oral risperidone reference concentrations determined by the Russu 2018 model [30] (3 mg/day and 6 mg/day specifically) were compared with the target concentrations previously established by our modeling to determine consistency between both models. The covariate distributions from the original studies used for popPK model development were applied to generate the virtual subject population. This approach ensured that interpatient variability is appropriately captured and explored in the simulations.

The final PK model for TV-46000 is described in detail elsewhere [27]. Briefly, it was a sequential parent–metabolite model. The parent model’s structure is a one-compartment model, with double first-order, release-dependent absorption (fast [KA1] and slow [KA2], with transit rate constant and five transit compartments) and first-order elimination to 9-OH risperidone (i.e., paliperidone). The 9-OH risperidone PK was described by a one-compartment model, with first-order conversion from the parent and first-order elimination. Covariates retained in the parent model include BMI effect on KA1 and KA2 as well as effects of administration site and injection volume on KA1 (Fig. 1) [27].

The schematic for the final PP1m PK model of Samtani 2009 [24], depicted in Fig. 1, is a one-compartment model with first-order elimination. The absorption component of the model allowed a fraction of the dose (f2) to enter relatively quickly into the central compartment via a zero-order process. After a certain lag time for drug absorption, the remaining fraction then entered the systemic circulation via a first-order process. Covariates included in the models were randomly sampled from within the demographic ranges of the TV-46000 phase 1 and phase 3 clinical trials. To accurately simulate the PK of PP1m and match it to the published PK profile from Coppola (2012) [28], ethnicity was randomly assigned to virtual subjects based on percentages listed in the demographics table. Additionally, BMI and weight were randomized within the range specified by Coppola (2012) for each assigned ethnicity group.

Model-Based Simulations

Simulations were performed to predict PK exposures for TAM when switching to TV-46000 q1m and q2m at steady state with virtual populations of 5000 patients. Parametric simulations using popPK models often employ virtual populations of approximately 5000–10,000 subjects. This large sample size ensures robust characterization of variability and enhances the statistical power and precision of summary exposure metrics [31]. See Table S1 for details on how many participants were included from each arm of the TV-46000 clinical study (NCT03503318) [32] to generate the virtual populations. For the PP1m clinical study (NCT00147173) [33], a total of n = 349 participants were included here, of which n = 132 were from the placebo cohort and n = 93 from the 78 mg, n = 94 from the 156 mg, and n = 30 from the 234 mg treated cohorts. To assess the impact of switch timing on the exposure of TAM, different switch time points (4 to 6 weeks after the last injection of PP1m) were simulated. For comparative exposure evaluations, the TAM was derived from the simulated plasma concentrations of risperidone and 9-OH-risperidone for further plotting and noncompartmental analysis (NCA) purposes.

Comparable doses for oral risperidone and paliperidone to PP1m and PP1m to TV-46000 were based upon reference information from Samtani 2009 [24] and Russu 2018 [30]. For TV-46000 to oral risperidone and paliperidone, the prescribing information was used [20, 21]. For all the switching simulations, a fixed loading dose (234 mg) was simulated for PP1m at day 1 and 156 mg at day 8, followed by different maintenance doses which were comparable to TV-46000 and oral risperidone (Table 1). The switch occurred at steady state and after 12 maintenance PP1m doses. The types of switching strategies simulated were (1) a comparable-dose switch, in which the comparable TV-46000 dose is initiated 4 weeks after the last dose of PP1m for TV-46000 (Figs. 3, S1–S4) and (2) a transition and maintenance switch, in which the patient initially receives a lower dose of TV-46000 q1m for 6 months (6 doses) or of TV-46000 q2m for 2 months (1 dose) and then moves to the higher dose of TV-46000 comparable to oral risperidone (Fig. S5). The latter was done to decrease the initial post-switch Cmax.

PK parameters of TAM such as Cmax and minimal plasma concentration (Cmin) were derived using simulated concentration profiles, and median simulated plasma concentration–time profiles were plotted. Cavg,ss was calculated as the AUC for the dosing interval (AUC0–tau) divided by dosing interval time in hours. In addition, the percentage ranges (median Cmax divided by oral risperidone median Cmin minus 1, and median Cmax divided by oral risperidone median Cmax) were evaluated. The simulation and NCA were conducted by using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling software Pumas (version 2.4.1, Pumas AI Inc., Dover, DE, US).

Results

Generally comparable doses for oral risperidone and paliperidone to PP1m and PP1m to TV-46000 were based on reference information from the Samtani 2009 [24] and Russu 2018 [30] models. For TV-46000 to oral risperidone and paliperidone, the prescribing information was used [20, 21]. Comparable doses are shown in Table 1.

Within each oral-comparable reference range (median Cmin to Cmax), TV-46000 q1m ranges trended relatively higher than PP1m. In addition, TV-46000 q2m trended higher than TV-46000 q1m and PP1m; however, both PP1m and TV-46000 were still comparable to the reference range for oral risperidone (Fig. 2). Modeling of oral risperidone reference levels in this study and that of Russu et al. 2018 [30] (specifically 3 mg/day and 6 mg/day) showed generally similar reference ranges for oral risperidone. For 3 mg/day, the median Cmin to Cmax from the Green model used in this analysis [29] ranged from 13.5 to 37.0 ng/mL compared to 16.0–33.0 ng/mL in the Russu model; for 6 mg/day, this was 26.9–74.0 ng/mL compared to 32.0–68.0 ng/mL in the Russu model. The larger range of median Cmin to Cmax for the Green model compared with Russu model can be attributed to the difference in parameter estimates between the models, particularly in terms of between-subject variability. The variability was higher in the Green model used here (40%) compared with the Russu model (30%). In addition, the Green model is more recent and incorporates rich data from additional studies, which is reflected in its updated parameterization. TV-46000 q1m 125 mg and q2m 250 mg are approved as comparable with daily oral risperidone of 5 mg [21]; however, the TAM concentrations of these doses overlapped with both the Russu oral risperidone reference range of 6 mg/day and the reference ranges (5–6 mg/day) from the model used in this analysis.

A comparable-dose switch, in which the most comparable TV-46000 dose is initiated 4 weeks after the last dose of PP1m for TV-46000, was identified as a potential switching strategy. The highest available PP1m maintenance dose (234 mg) [20] was simulated to TV-46000 125 mg q1m (Fig. 3a). PP1m median Cmax, Cmin, and Cavg at steady state were 51.4, 34.1, and 43.7 ng/mL, respectively. TV-46000 125 mg q1m median Cmax, Cmin, and Cavg were 72.5, 33.8, and 60.4 ng/mL at first dose, and 65.1, 33.3, and 51.0 ng/mL at steady state. Ratios of TV-46000 125 mg to PP1m 234 mg steady state ranged from 1.0 to 1.4 at first dose with a Cavg ratio of 1.4, whereas TV-46000 to PP1m ratios at steady state were 1.0–1.3.

A switch from PP1m 234 mg maintenance dose to TV-46000 250 mg q2m was also simulated (Fig. 3b). For TV-46000 q2m, median Cmax, Cmin, and Cavg were 97.5, 28.9, and 65.1 ng/mL at first dose, and 81.5, 20.9, and 49.8 ng/mL at steady state. Ratios of TV-46000 250 mg q2m to PP1m 234 mg at steady state showed a larger Cmin–Cmax range than q1m (0.9–1.9 at first dose and 0.6–1.6 at steady state).

Other doses of PP1m (78 mg, 117 mg, and 156 mg) were also simulated to TV-46000 q1m 50 mg, 75 mg, and 100 mg and TV-46000 q2m 100 mg, 150 mg, and 200 mg. Other comparable-dose switches using different doses showed similar trends in TAM exposure parameters and steady state ratios compared with the comparable-dose switch for PP1m 234 mg to TV-46000 125 mg q1m or 250 q2m (Table 2, Figs. S2–S4).

Alternative switching scenarios were explored to minimize post-switch Cmax peaks exacerbated by residual PP1m and rising TV-46000 levels (Table S2, Fig. S5). This was a transition and maintenance switch, in which the patient initially receives a lower dose of TV-46000 q1m for 6 months or TV-46000 q2m for 2 months and then transitions to the higher TV-46000 dose comparable with daily oral risperidone. Using a transition and maintenance strategy reduced the post-switch Cmax peak by approximately 7–11% compared with the comparable-dose switching strategy.

For most switching scenarios, site of administration had minimal effect on the TAM exposure. However, for the switch from PP1m 156 and 234 mg to 100 and 125 mg TV-46000 q1m, respectively, there were numerically higher median Cmin to Cmax when switching from PP1m administered in the gluteal muscle to TV-46000 administered in the back of upper arm compared with switching from PP1m administered in the deltoid muscle to TV-46000 administered in the abdomen. Figure S6 shows the 234 mg PP1m switch to 125 mg TV-46000 q1m by administration site.

Discussion

The switching simulations to TV-46000 q1m 4 weeks after the last PP1m injection yielded PK parameters consistent with relevant daily oral risperidone ranges. When this switching strategy was used, TAM exposure (Cmin and Cavg) was most comparable for PP1m and TV-46000 (although the TV-46000 q1m initial Cmax peak is higher). One advantage of this approach is the relative ease of switching in terms of dose scheduling as patients can remain on the same monthly injection schedule. In addition, switching directly to the dose of TV-46000 that will be used for maintenance allows a straightforward treatment plan, without the need to schedule dose changes.

Clinicians also have the option to switch patients on PP1m directly to TV-46000 q2m, although the Cmax is slightly higher and Cmin slightly lower than that of PP1m and q1m. However, both TV-46000 q1m and q2m and PP1m q1m are within the Cavg reference range of oral risperidone, indicating that this switch can be considered for patients who may benefit from q2m dosing and for whom there is limited clinical concern about the impact of higher Cmax peaks than seen with a switch to the q1m formulation.

In the simulations of switching from PP1m, TV-46000 q1m and q2m demonstrated higher Cmax peaks than PP1m at first dose and steady state. The transition and maintenance switching strategy explored in this work can be used if clinicians are concerned about adverse events or tolerability issues that may be related to higher Cmax values. However, this approach is not available for the 2-mg oral comparable doses, as there are no doses of TV-46000 that are comparable to 1 mg oral risperidone daily that can be used as the initial lower dose. Switching simulations also indicated that TV-46000 q2m doses have slightly lower Cmin values compared with PP1m; however, the median Cavg values at steady state for both q1m and q2m were generally comparable to daily oral risperidone and PP1m. For both TV-46000 q1m and q2m across all dosing groups, the TAM exposure was still within or relatively close to the oral risperidone ranges of 2–5 mg/day as evidenced by the Cavg at steady state after switching, even though initial and steady-state Cmax peaks and initial Cavg were higher than 6 mg/day oral risperidone. Furthermore, a separate exposure–response analysis for impending relapse indicated similar treatment benefit for q1m and q2m regimens across all TV-46000 dose and exposure groups [34].

Although the site of administration did not affect the PK for most of the switching scenarios, there were some differences in PK when switching from PP1m 234 and 156 mg to TV-46000 q1m 125 mg and 100 mg, respectively. The median Cmin to Cmax was higher when switching from PP1m administered in the gluteal muscle to TV-46000 administered in the back of the upper arm compared to switching from PP1m administered in the deltoid muscle to TV-46000 administered in the abdomen. A potential reason for this is the difference in subcutaneous adipose tissue and vascularity of the back of the upper arm and deltoid compared to the abdomen and gluteal muscle. These factors can influence absorption and delivery of the antipsychotic into the bloodstream [19], but further data are required to determine whether the numerical difference in PK between administration sites is clinically meaningful when switching from PP1m to TV-46000.

When switching between two different LAIs, the pharmacologic properties of each formulation should be considered. The time to peak concentration and time to steady state, as well as the molecule binding profile and half-life of each formulation, are important [18]. Because of the long half-life of PP1m (25–49 days) [20] and similar pharmacodynamic properties of the PP1m and TV-46000 TAM, switching can generally be considered on the day that the pre-switch PP1m injection was scheduled. TV-46000 has a rapid initial absorption that is potentially associated with the initial release from the subcutaneous depot, reaching clinically relevant plasma concentrations within 6–24 h, and a second, slower process that is likely associated with the in-situ depot releasing risperidone in a sustained manner over 28 days (q1m) or 56 days (q2m) [26, 35]. This is supported by the switching simulations here, with TV-46000 sustaining clinically relevant plasma concentrations after switching from PP1m.

There are clinical considerations such as illness severity, patient comorbidities, concomitant medications, and prior history of antipsychotic response and tolerability that influence the decision to switch between LAIs and inform the switching approach. One key limitation of these analyses is that switching between different LAI formulations can only be simulated with popPK, as pivotal clinical trials of LAIs do not typically focus on switching between different formulations. Another limitation of the simulations presented here is that they are not based on real-world data but on data derived from virtual populations in phase 1 and phase 3 clinical trials. In addition, it is important to highlight the inherent subjectivity in determining comparability between different drug products and formulations. There is no universally standardized definition of “pharmacokinetic comparability”, which results in a more interpretative than absolute approach (except in the case of bioequivalence studies with human participants). It must also be noted that manufacturers use different models to compare their product with reference ranges of oral risperidone, which can lead to variability in the models employed for switching simulations. Finally, as a result of the use of simulated populations there are no patient-level safety or efficacy outcomes data available for the switching strategies explored in this analysis; thus, while popPK-informed data is an additional tool for clinicians, any switching strategy should always consider the clinical context of each individual patient.

Conclusion

Clinician discretion will ultimately determine how to switch, on the basis of a variety of factors such as patient preference, scheduling convenience, and concerns about tolerability or symptom breakthrough. Switching to TV-46000 125 mg q1m 4 weeks after the last PP1m 234-mg injection yielded generally comparable PK parameters at steady state.
