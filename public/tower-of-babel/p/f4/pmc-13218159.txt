Evaluating stroke recognition and prehospital quality metrics in emergency dispatch: a retrospective cohort study comparing mimics and confirmed strokes
Authors: Nedim Leto, Elisabeth Farbu, Paul Barach, Conrad Arnfinn Bjørshol, Martin Kurz, Soffien Ajmi, Annette Fromm, Christopher Kalhagen Bjerkvig, Thomas Lindner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAccurate identification of acute stroke by the Emergency Medical Dispatch Centre (EMDC) is essential for timely treatment and efficient resource use. However, stroke mimics—conditions resembling stroke can often lead to overtriage, complicating emergency care. We aimed to quantify EMDC stroke overtriage and compare characteristics of stroke mimics with confirmed stroke cases.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingPatients with suspected stroke identified by the EMDC in Western Norway between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2022.ParticipantsA total of 8,261 patients with EMDC suspected stroke were included. Stroke suspicion was determined using the Norwegian Medical Priority Dispatch System, which includes 11 stroke-related criteria. The study population was stratified into stroke mimics (non-stroke diagnosis) and confirmed strokes (transient ischaemic attack, acute ischaemic stroke or intracerebral haemorrhage discharge diagnosis) at hospital discharge. Data collected included dispatch codes, prehospital time metrics and hospital discharge diagnoses.Outcome measuresOutcomes of interest were the positive predictive value (PPV) of stroke identification based on EMDC dispatch criteria, the association between specific dispatch criteria and confirmed stroke and differences in prehospital time metrics.ResultsOf 8,261 patients with an ambulance dispatch due to EMDC suspicion of stroke, only 1181 (14.3%) were confirmed strokes, resulting in a PPV of 14.3% (95% CI 13.6% to 15.1%). Five dispatch criteria—breathing problems, acute vertigo, hemianopsia, ataxia/confusion and headache—had stroke mimic rates exceeding 90%. The FAST (Face-Arm-Speech-Time) criteria were significantly associated with confirmed stroke after adjusting for age and sex (OR 3.55, 95% CI 1.55 to 8.07). EMS response times were similar between groups (median 10 min (IQR 7–14) vs 9 min (IQR 6–14); p=0.47), whereas stroke mimics had significantly longer on-scene times (median 16 min (IQR 9–24) vs 10 min (IQR 7–16); p<0.001).ConclusionsEMDC stroke dispatch criteria demonstrated low PPV, reflecting substantial overtriage due to stroke mimics at the earliest stage of emergency assessment. FAST-based criteria were associated with improved discrimination for confirmed stroke. Confirmed stroke cases had shorter EMS on-scene times, suggesting more streamlined prehospital management. These findings highlight the operational impact of stroke mimics on emergency services and the need to refine dispatch strategies. Because sensitivity could not be assessed, overall diagnostic performance remains uncertain, and future studies should include all stroke presentations to evaluate system-level accuracy.Keywords: Stroke; Emergency Service, Hospital; Triage; Clinical Decision-Making

Introduction

Stroke is a time-critical emergency and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. In Norway, approximately 9000 patients experience a stroke each year, with 85% resulting in acute ischaemic strokes (AIS) and 14% intracerebral haemorrhages (ICH), which are associated with a 12% and 25% 3-month mortality rate, respectively.1

Early contact with the Emergency Medical Dispatch Centre (EMDC) personnel and the identification of stroke symptoms are important for minimising delays from symptom onset to hospital admission. The recognition by EMDC dispatchers can streamline the acute stroke care-pathway and reduce onset-to-treatment times, which are linked to improved patient outcomes.2,5 In Norway, the EMDC is the initial contact point for stroke patients.6 Dispatch urgency and problem codes are determined based on the EMDC operator’s assessment of the patient’s symptoms and medical severity.

Achieving high sensitivity in prehospital stroke identification is essential to avoid missing treatable strokes. Yet this often comes at the cost of substantial reductions in specificity. Consequently, a large proportion of patients assigned a stroke suspicion at the EMDC level ultimately prove to be stroke mimics, including conditions such as hypoglycaemia, migraine and functional neurological disorders.7 Meta-analyses estimate that 27%–43% of prehospital stroke activations are mimics, while a Norwegian prospective study reported that 38% of stroke unit admissions were due to non-stroke diagnoses, suggesting that rates may be even higher in the Norwegian healthcare system.7 8 These high mimic rates create operational strain by increasing ED overcrowding, which is associated with adverse clinical outcomes.9 At the same time, dispatcher performance remains inconsistent. Widely used algorithms such as the Medical Priority Dispatch System algorithm (MPDS) show suboptimal sensitivity in stroke identification and limited external validation in real world-settings.10

Despite the need for rapid identification, many EMDC-suspected strokes ultimately turn out to be stroke mimics—patients dispatched with suspected stroke who are later discharged without a stroke diagnosis after in-hospital evaluation. Stroke mimics are regarded as stroke overtriage. Currently, there is no standardised definition of stroke overtriage. In this study, stroke overtriage refers specifically to EMDC stroke dispatches that result in hospital discharge without a stroke diagnosis. This patient group represents the proportion of false positive stroke activations among all EMDC-suspected stroke cases.

Accurately distinguishing between AIS and ICH in the prehospital settings also remains a challenge. Clinical trials highlight timely reperfusion therapies, including intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and endovascular treatment (EVT). These interventions can significantly improve outcomes and save lives in patients with AIS.2 3 Similarly, trials of ICH treatment have demonstrated early benefits from blood pressure control together with implementing a bundle of stroke care protocol and targeted surgical intervention.5 11 Early precise prehospital evaluation of stroke patients is important in guiding initial management and ensuring timely transport to a facility equipped for appropriate diagnostic and treatment.

Previous publications from our health region have evaluated EMDC stroke sensitivity.12,14 Data on specificity, stroke overtriage and resource utilisation is lacking. We sought to determine the extent of EMDC stroke overtriage variation by comparing emergency care pathways and symptom characteristics in stroke mimics versus confirmed stroke patients. The goal is to inform system efforts to identify patient symptom patterns between stroke and stroke mimics to improve the accuracy of EMDC criteria and dispatch protocols.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study enrolled patients who met the following three criteria: (1) patient or a bystander contacted the EMDC, (2) dispatcher concluded with a suspicion of stroke and (3) patient was referred to a hospital ED by the Emergency Medical Service (EMS). The study period was from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2022 in the Western Norway Health Authority Region. The study conformed to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology standards.

Settings

The study population included patients from hospital trusts in Vestland and Rogaland counties (Western Norway), which together serve approximately 1,050,000 people across an area of 43,000 km². The EMDC assigns an emergency level and a diagnostic suspicion at the time of the initial call using the Norwegian MPDS.15 The level of emergency dispatch is established according to the dispatcher’s evaluation and concerns about the patient’s medical condition, as conveyed during the interaction with the caller. The Norwegian EMDCs use the highest urgency level (code red) for acute stroke suspicion cases, with a target of on-scene arrival within 12 min in urban and 25 min in non-urban areas.14

Nurses and ambulance coordinators work in EMDC teams and manage all aspects of a call, from the interview phase and through ambulance dispatch, on-scene arrival and follow-up. The four EMDCs in the study can deploy a fleet of 92 ambulances, three physician-staffed air ambulances and two physician-staffed search and rescue helicopters. The centre with the lowest call volume answers to 12,000 and the busiest up to 61,000 emergency calls per year.

The EMS works with eight primary stroke centres (PSC) and two comprehensive stroke centres (CSC) providing emergency care diagnostic and treatment for patients with a suspected stroke. All PSCs have a dedicated stroke unit and offer treatment with IVT for patients with AIS. The two CSCs offer, additionally to PSC services, EVT for AIS with large vessel occlusion and neurosurgical services. The transport distances between PSCs and CSCs are large (median 120 km, range 1–278 km), except in the city of Bergen, where a PSC is located one km from a CSC.

Data collection and definitions

The data for this study were collected from four EMDC databases and in-hospital medical records. For patients with an AIS and ICH hospital discharge diagnosis, additional data were retrieved from the Norwegian Stroke Registry (NSR). Data from the EMDC databases include ambulance dispatch criteria, EMS response times (time from EMDC alert to ambulance on-scene arrival), EMS on-scene times (time from ambulance on-scene arrival to departure), and EMS total prehospital times (time from EMDC contact to hospital admission). We collected data from in-hospital medical records on the age, sex and final hospital discharge diagnosis for the corresponding hospital admission.

The NSR, established in 2005, is a mandatory reporting system for Norwegian stroke patients (AIS and ICH) and collects data for quality monitoring and improvement.1 We extracted baseline variables, stroke risk factors (eg, prior transient ischaemic attack (TIA), stroke, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, hypertension and diabetes), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores at admission and onset-to-hospital times. Prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores were obtained from medical records, while the 3-month mRS scores were assessed at the outpatient clinic follow-up by trained stroke nurses.

For acute stroke evaluation, a CT was performed to exclude (cerebral haemorrhage or other) contraindications before IVT, and CT angiography was performed to assess for large vessel occlusion pathology, including occlusions eligible for EVT. In the 4.5–9-hour window from the symptom onset, CT perfusion imaging was added to evaluate for IVT eligibility criteria. MRI with Diffusion Weighted-Imaging, Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery and Time-of-Flight Magnetic Resonance Angiography evaluations were the preferred modalities for patients with unknown symptom onset (wake up strokes) and follow-up imaging. However, in patients not eligible for MRI, a CT scan was applied. Patients who were assessed only with CT were also included in our study.

Stroke mimics were defined as patients who were initially assessed by the EMDC as having a possible stroke and subsequently referred to the hospital ED. This patient group was discharged without a stroke diagnosis after in-hospital diagnostic assessment. Accordingly, they represent false positive stroke activations and overtriage. Stroke overtriage was defined as the proportion of EMDC dispatches to ambulances per criterion with a stroke mimic hospital discharge diagnosis. A TIA was defined as a focal neurologic deficit lasting less than 24 hours and with no documented infarction lesions on MRI. In AIS and ICH, the stroke diagnosis was based on clinical and radiological findings.

Study procedures

Patients were grouped into stroke mimics or confirmed strokes (TIA, AIS or ICH) based on hospital discharge diagnoses. The EMDC assigned diagnostic suspicion and emergency levels using the MPDS during initial calls, with stroke getting the highest priority. Stroke dispatch was based on 11 symptom-specific criteria (MPDS 28.01–28.08), where the 28.04a, b and c criteria are the Face-Arm-Speech Time (FAST) criteria.15 Patients were subsequently for selected analyses dichotomised into the FAST or non-FAST EMDC dispatch group according to the presence or absence of a FAST dispatch criterion. Each patient received only one dispatch code. Dispatch coding was performed at the discretion of the dispatcher, based on the clinical assessment during the emergency call. Patients presenting with both FAST and non-FAST symptoms were classified exclusively according to the single dispatch code assigned. Consequently, patients dispatched under a FAST criterion were included only in the FAST group, while those dispatched under non-FAST criteria were included only in the non-FAST group. No patients were double-counted or excluded due to symptom overlap. The patient data from four EMDCs were linked to hospital records, with AIS and ICH cases supplemented by the registry data from the NSR.

Study measures

The primary outcomes of the study were the positive predictive value (PPV) of stroke identification by EMDC dispatch criteria, percentage of stroke overtriage for each dispatch criterion, ambulance on-scene times, EMS alert times and EMS on-scene times. For the secondary outcomes, only patients with confirmed AIS or ICH were analysed for acute stroke symptom presentation, time from the patient was last known well to hospital admission, proportion of patients achieving functional independence, as defined by a 3-month mRS score of 0–2 after the stroke event.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as percentages and continuous variables as median (IQR). The Pearson χ² test was performed on categorical variables, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test was performed on two group comparisons, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was performed for multiple group comparisons on continuous variables. The PPV for confirmed stroke was calculated as the proportion of patients with a final hospital diagnosis of confirmed stroke (TIA, AIS or ICH) among all patients dispatched as suspected stroke, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The OR with 95% CI was calculated for potential associations between the FAST EMDC dispatch criteria (criteria 28.04a–c) versus other EMDC dispatch criteria and confirmed stroke case (TIA, AIS or ICH) hospital discharge diagnosis. To account for the two main potential confounders, the OR was adjusted for age and sex. P values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics V.29 (IBM). We performed multiple statistical tests without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons. The results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmation.

Patient and public involvement

Informed consent was not obtained, but all patients received an information letter describing their ability to withdraw from the study at any time without impacting their care.

Results

Baseline characteristics

We included 8,261 patients in our analyses, of which 85.7% (n=7,080) were patients with stroke mimics and 14.3% (n=1,181) confirmed strokes, including 1% (n=15) TIA, 85% (n=998) AIS and 14% (n=168) ICH. Patients with stroke mimics were younger than those with confirmed strokes (median age 73 (IQR 59–82) vs 76 (IQR 66–83); p < 0.001), and a higher proportion were female (50% (n = 3,515) vs 44% (n = 520); p<0.001). The most frequent stroke mimic diagnoses in the cohort were dizziness (R42; 14%), syncope and collapse (R55; 9%), urinary tract infection (N39.0; 6%), unspecified migraine (G43.9; 5%), headache (R51; 4%) and epileptic seizure (G40.9; 4%).

The risk factors presented in AIS and ICH patients were similar, except for previous myocardial infarction (12% vs 8%; p=0.035) and smoking (19% vs 11%; p=0.003). The prestroke mRS score of 0–2 was similar between the stroke types. A detailed presentation of risk factors is presented in online supplemental file.

Discussion

We examined the extent of EMDC patient stroke overtriage, by comparing emergency medical pathways and symptom characteristics in patients with stroke mimics vs patients with confirmed stroke cases at hospital discharge. We found that 85.7% of patients admitted to the hospitals with stroke suspicion were stroke mimics, while only 14.3% were confirmed strokes (TIA, AIS or ICH). This corresponds to a PPV of 14.3% (95% CI 13.6% to 15.1%). The use of FAST symptom criteria in the EMDC-phone-based assessment demonstrated higher specificity and PPV for stroke recognition compared with non-FAST dispatch criteria. Symptoms like breathing problems, fainting, dizziness, sudden onset of ataxia and confusion were very rarely associated with stroke in our study, though their presence remains diagnostically challenging due to the risk of posterior circulation stroke.16

The observed overtriage rate of 85.7% in the present study is substantially higher than the 27%–43% range reported in previous meta-analyses and the 38% rate reported in a Norwegian stroke unit-based study.7 8 This discrepancy is most plausibly explained by case-mix differences and the point in the care pathway at which patients were sampled. Whereas prior studies primarily included patients admitted with stroke suspicion after consultation with on-call neurologists, the present study includes all patients for whom stroke suspicion was raised at the EMDC level. Consequently, a large proportion of lower-probability cases evaluated and discharged from EDs without stroke unit admission were captured, inflating the observed proportion of stroke mimics. This distinction is clinically relevant and underscores the diagnostic uncertainty inherent in earliest-phase prehospital stroke assessment.

Although the study applied a contemporary tissue-based definition of TIA, not all patients in whom cerebrovascular stroke remained suspected after the ED physician’s on-scene neurological assessment underwent MRI. Consequently, some patients classified as stroke mimics may in fact have had minor AIS that were not detected on CT. Such diagnostic misclassification would bias the results towards an overestimation of EMDC overtriage and a corresponding underestimation of the PPV. Nevertheless, in Norwegian clinical practice, MRI is widely available during daytime hours, including at PSCs, and is frequently performed at a low threshold when clinically indicated.

Differentiating strokes from mimics remains a challenge in prehospital stroke care. FAST-positive dispatches were significantly more predictive of confirmed strokes (adjusted OR 3.55, 95% CI 1.55 to 8.07) than non-FAST dispatches. EMS response times were similar across groups, but on-scene and total EMS times were longer for stroke mimics, likely reflecting more complex assessments. This low threshold for stroke activation aligns with current guidelines, and for actual stroke patients this practice is associated with improved time metrics, faster administration of effective treatment and improved patient outcomes.17,21 The EMS time metrics should be interpreted as exploratory, given the potential for confounding due to the unadjusted analyses.

Despite our finding that non-FAST stroke symptoms rarely turned out to be true strokes, a minority of AIS and ICH cases presented with symptoms such as visual disturbances, acute vertigo, ataxia and loss of consciousness.16 An increased awareness of patients presenting with these symptoms, together with more focus on stroke reperfusion therapies and reducing time delays, are most likely the reasons for the increasing rates of stroke mimics admitted to EDs with similar symptoms.22 A recently published Swiss study which evaluated ED clinical assessment of stroke showed that final year medical doctors and even young residents could achieve a high discrimination ability between actual strokes and stroke mimics solely based on clinical assessment.23 This may also be relevant for EMDC operators and ambulance personnel, as enhanced training and clinical assessment may improve the identification of stroke mimics in the prehospital phase.

The MPDS, despite its global use, has not been evaluated in randomised control trials. A meta-analysis from 2018 reviewed the accuracy of medical dispatch rates and found low overall evidence for dispatch accuracy, including for MPDS.10 The authors concluded that it was not possible to suggest a superior dispatch system for early stroke identification.10 One prospective cohort study found the sensitivity of the MPDS to be 41%, while two smaller retrospective cohort studies found a sensitivity of 83% and 86%.24,26 In our health region, two studies have found stroke sensitivity rates of 58% and 62%, respectively.12 14 This indicates that despite large stroke overtriage, a significant proportion of stroke patients are not identified. This may be especially relevant for posterior circulation strokes.

Balancing over- and undertriage is fundamental in emergency stroke care, where the clinical risk of delayed treatment generally outweighs the resource burden of activating stroke pathways. Although some degree of overtriage is unavoidable and often warranted, no consensus exists regarding acceptable thresholds in stroke systems. Evidence from trauma systems may be used as benchmarks. These systems typically operate with a target of ≤5% under-triage and 25%–35% overtriage, reflecting a deliberate tolerance for resource intensive responses to avoid critical misses.27 Still, these systems in real-world practice perform inferior to their target goal with both higher under- and overtriage rates.28 Similar patterns are observed in the Norwegian EMS system for acute stroke, where significant overtriage persists while under-triage still remains an issue. A recent Norwegian national registry study demonstrated that despite a 54% increase in ambulance dispatches between 2019 and 2024, there was no corresponding increase in stroke incidence, nor improvement in the proportion of patients admitted within 4 hours of symptom onset or receiving IVT.29 Additionally, a qualitative study from the Oslo EMDC identified high workload, strict response time requirements, and limitations in criteria-based dispatch protocols as key contributors to suboptimal triage accuracy.30

The most important limitation of this study is that dispatch sensitivity cannot be estimated. Only patients identified by the EMDC as potential strokes were included, and thus all false-negative cases—true strokes not recognised at dispatch—are absent. This limits interpretation of the observed overtriage, as it remains unknown whether this high rate represents inefficiency or a necessary trade-off to minimise missed strokes. In emergency stroke care, a low triage threshold may be clinically justified if it meaningfully reduces under-triage. Comprehensive evaluation of EMDC stroke performance therefore requires inclusion of all stroke presentations, regardless of initial dispatch code, enabling simultaneous estimation of sensitivity, specificity and predictive values.

In addition to evaluate dispatch accuracy, we performed a subanalysis comparing symptom presentation and care pathways between confirmed AIS and ICH. ICH patients had higher stroke severity (higher NIHSS score), higher rates of direct hospital admissions and more often presented with reduced consciousness, neglect, hemianopia, dysarthria or aphasia and severe strokes, while AIS patients showed a higher rate of upper limb paresis. Most symptoms were prominent in both stroke groups and higher rates of direct ICH admissions were likely due to more severe stroke symptoms making it easier to identify these stroke patients by the EMDC teams. The findings are in line with a Swiss ED assessment study, showing low discrimination ability solely based on clinical assessment.23

Stroke mimics pose a major challenge for emergency services, especially as ageing and multimorbidity increase healthcare demand in Western countries.31 A Norwegian report found that a national stroke campaign led to more stroke mimics being admitted, without increasing true stroke cases or improving timely admissions and overall quality.32 Despite this, rapid EMDC contact and ambulance transport remain crucial for stroke patients, but many true strokes are still missed by dispatch systems, underscoring the need for better assessment tools to improve both specificity and sensitivity. Improving dispatch accuracy therefore requires more than algorithmic refinement; it demands enhanced dispatcher training in stroke heterogeneity and integration of advanced tools such as telemedicine or video-based assessments. These strategies may improve discrimination between true strokes and mimics. Furthermore, the increasing prevalence of stroke mimics in emergency care underscores the need for system-level adaptations. While clinical assessment by experienced physicians achieves high diagnostic accuracy, translating this expertise to the EMDC and ambulance setting requires structured education together with refinement of standardised protocols.23 In addition, improving differentiation between AIS and ICH should prioritise implementation of point-of-care imaging, biomarker analysis and AI-driven support tools.33,35 Implementation of these elements may also improve differentiation between stroke mimics and actual strokes.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective registry-based observational cohort study, the study does not allow causal relationships to be established. Second, we only included patients with an EMDC suspicion of stroke and did not collect data on patients with stroke not identified by the EMDC. Thus, the sensitivity in EMDC stroke dispatch could not be evaluated and should be considered as a major limitation. Third, there may be confounding factors that cannot be controlled for and were not considered in the analysis to the extent that clinical trials can. Additionally, no advanced regression models or clustering analyses were performed to adjust for potential confounding. Fifth, the study was conducted in a specific region of Norway, potentially limiting generalisability to other settings and countries which have very different EMS and public health systems, finances and emergency medical services coverage. Sixth, this study involved multiple statistical comparisons across dispatch criteria, symptom categories and EMS time metrics without formal adjustment for multiplicity; therefore, particularly given the exploratory nature of several sub-analyses, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmation. Finally, the variability in the quality and type of the collected data, absence of data pertaining to long-term and functional outcomes, prehospital information and complications, as well as other methodological obstacles may limit the external generalisability of registry data in clinical and epidemiological research. This inherent diagnostic uncertainty represents an additional limitation of using routine, registry-based clinical data. An example of this is the potential misclassification of transient TIA, where diagnosis in routine clinical practice depends on symptom resolution and imaging availability. Patients assessed with CT alone may be misclassified between TIA, small infarcts and stroke mimics.

Conclusions

The current Norwegian EMDC stroke dispatch criteria demonstrated low PPV, resulting in substantial rates of overtriage by the EMDCs caused by stroke mimics. In contrast, the FAST criteria were associated with higher specificity for stroke recognition. Shorter EMS on-scene times were associated with confirmed stroke diagnoses. Our study highlights the significant overtriage challenges in the current organisation of EMDC services and points out the need for revising and improving EMDC stroke assessment tools. The inability to assess sensitivity remains a key limitation, and future work should include all stroke presentations—not only EMDC-suspected cases—to evaluate overall dispatch performance. Further research is needed to improve early stroke identification by the EMDC. The clinical implications for dispatch systems and stroke care effectiveness remain to be clarified.
