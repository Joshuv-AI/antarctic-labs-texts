Prevalence of Oral Soft Tissue Lesions and Their Associations with Oral Hygiene Practices, Addictive Habits, and CD4 Counts Among Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, and Queer Individuals Living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus
Authors: Shrikanth Muralidharan, Pramila Mallaiah, Deepak Bangera, Arunkumar Acharya, Judy Annie Koshy, Monali Nikalje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Oral manifestations often reflect underlying immune suppression and disease progression. These lesions can profoundly affect the quality of life, leading to pain, difficulty in eating, and social stigma. In the lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ) population living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), additional sociobehavioral factors, such as discrimination, mental health challenges, and barriers to healthcare access, may exacerbate oral health issues.Material and Methods:This cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of oral soft tissue lesions and their associations with oral hygiene practices, addictive habits, and CD4 counts in 56 LGBTQ adults with HIV (mean age 34.8 ± 7.2 years). Participants were recruited from an outpatient clinic and underwent comprehensive oral examinations using World Health Organization criteria, self-reported questionnaires on hygiene and addictions, and a review of recent CD4 counts.Results:All participants reported addictions, predominantly smoking (44.6%), smokeless tobacco (37.5%), and alcohol (30.3%), with polyaddiction in 39.3% of the participants. Soft-tissue lesions were detected in 60.7% of patients, including candidiasis (26.8%), oral hairy leukoplakia (14.3%), aphthous ulcers (10.7%), linear gingival erythema (8.9%), and tobacco pouch keratosis (7.1%). Lesion prevalence was significantly higher among irregular brushers (100%, P = 0.034), poly-addicts (86.4%, P = 0.002), and those with CD4 counts <350 cells/mm3 (78.9% vs. 42.3% for ≥350 cells/mm3, P = 0.011). Specific associations included tobacco use and leukoplakia/keratosis (P = 0.029) and alcohol use and candidiasis (P = 0.041).Conclusions:Multidisciplinary interventions emphasizing oral hygiene education, addiction cessation programs, and integrated dental-HIV care are essential to reduce morbidity and enhance the overall well-being of LGBTQ individuals with HIV.

INTRODUCTION

The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic continues to pose significant global health challenges, with over 38 million people living with the virus as of 2023.[1] Oral manifestations are among the most common and earliest clinical indicators of HIV infection, often appearing before systemic symptoms and serving as surrogates for immune status.[23] These include opportunistic infections like candidiasis, viral lesions such as oral hairy leukoplakia, and non-specific conditions such as aphthous ulcers and gingival erythema.[4] In the era of antiretroviral therapy (ART), whereas the incidence of severe opportunistic infections has declined, oral lesions persist in 30%–80% of HIV-positive individuals, particularly in resource-limited settings or among those with suboptimal adherence.[56] Factors contributing to oral lesions in HIV include direct viral effects, immune dysregulation, and co-morbid behaviors such as poor oral hygiene and substance use.[78] CD4 lymphocyte counts, a key marker of immune competence, inversely correlate with lesion prevalence; counts below 350 cells/mm3 are associated with heightened risk of opportunistic mucosal diseases.[910] Addictive habits, including tobacco and alcohol, further exacerbate oral pathology by promoting inflammation, microbial dysbiosis, and carcinogenesis.[11] Smoking, for instance, is linked to hyperkeratotic lesions, while alcohol facilitates fungal overgrowth.[12] The lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ) community faces a disproportionate HIV burden, accounting for a significant portion of new infections in many regions due to structural inequalities, stigma, and limited access to prevention services.[13] In this population, oral health is often neglected amid broader health priorities, compounded by discrimination in healthcare settings.[14] Studies indicate higher rates of substance use and mental health disorders among LGBTQ individuals with HIV, which may amplify oral disease risk.[15] Despite this, research specifically addressing oral manifestations in LGBTQ HIV cohorts is sparse, with most studies focusing on general populations or pediatric groups.[316] This gap is critical, as oral lesions not only impair quality of life – causing pain, nutritional deficits, and aesthetic concerns – but also signal poor viral control and increased transmission risk.[17] Early detection and management can improve outcomes; however, barriers persist. This study aimed to bridge this knowledge deficit by assessing the prevalence of oral soft tissue lesions in LGBTQ adults with HIV and exploring their associations with oral hygiene, addictions, and CD4 counts. These findings may guide tailored interventions by integrating dental care into HIV programs to address disparities in this vulnerable group.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted among the LGBTQ population in and around Pune city. No subgroup classification was made, as most of the cases refused further specific identification apart from being a member of the LGBTQ community. The sampling was snowball sampling based on participant references. The sample comprised 56 LGBTQ adults living with HIV. The inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, confirmed HIV diagnosis based on self-reported status, and willingness to participate. The exclusion criteria included acute systemic infections, recent oral surgery (within 3 months), and cognitive impairment precluding informed consent. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (IBM Analytics, USA), assuming 50% lesion prevalence from prior studies, 95% confidence level, and 10% margin of error, yielding a minimum of 50 participants; 56 were enrolled to account for attrition. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring confidentiality and voluntary participation. The study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital with an ART center, and the snowball sampling method was followed. A validated, structured questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha 0.82) captured demographics (age, gender identity, and sexual orientation), oral hygiene practices (brushing frequency: twice daily, once daily, irregular; use of floss, mouthwash; dental visits in past year), and addictive habits (types: smoking, smokeless tobacco, alcohol; duration in years; poly-addiction as ≥2 substances). Clinical examinations were performed by the author under artificial light using mouth mirrors and probes, following the World Health Organization criteria for HIV-associated oral lesions. The focus was on soft tissues, diagnosing conditions such as candidiasis (pseudomembranous, erythematous), oral hairy leukoplakia, aphthous ulcers, linear gingival erythema, and tobacco pouch keratosis. Multiple lesions were noted when present. CD4 counts were extracted from their medical records and dichotomized as <350 or ≥350 cells/mm3 based on clinical thresholds. HIV duration was categorized as ≤5 or >5 years old. Although viral load data were available, they were not analyzed because of high suppression rates (>90%). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Analytics, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency/percentage for categorical variables) were used to summarize the data. Associations between lesions and variables (hygiene, addictions, and CD4) were tested. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for significant associations. P < 0.05 were considered significant; no adjustments for multiplicity were applied, given the exploratory nature of the study.

RESULTS

Participant demographics revealed a mean age of 34.8 ± 7.2 years, with diverse LGBTQ identities: 45% gay, 25% bisexual, 20% transgender, and 10% queer/other. All patients were on ART, with a mean HIV duration of 6.4 ± 3.8 years. Oral hygiene practices were notably poor. Only 19 (33.9%) brushed their teeth twice daily, 31 (55.4%) once daily, and 6 (10.7%) irregularly [Table 1]. Adjunct use (floss/mouthwash) was low at 9 (16.1%), and only 9 (16.1%) reported annual dental consultations. Addictive behaviors were ubiquitous; all 56 (100%) participants endorsed at least one habit, with smoking being predominant (25, 44.6%), followed by smokeless tobacco (21, 37.5%) and alcohol (17, 30.3%). Polyaddiction affected 22 (39.3%) patients, with a mean addiction duration of 8.2 ± 4.6 years. Soft tissue lesions were prevalent in 34 (60.7%) participants, with seven (12.5%) exhibiting multiple types. The distribution included candidiasis in 15 (26.8%) patients, oral hairy leukoplakia in 8 (14.3%), recurrent aphthous ulcers in 6 (10.7%), linear gingival erythema in 5 (8.9%), and tobacco pouch keratosis in 4 (7.1%). No cases of Kaposi’s sarcoma or non-Hodgkin lymphoma were observed, likely because of ART coverage. Significant associations were observed. Brushing frequency correlated with lesions (χ² =6.72, P = 0.034): prevalence was 26.3% in twice-daily brushers, 71.0% in once-daily brushers, and 100% in irregular brushers. Adjusted OR for irregular vs. twice-daily was 4.2 (95% CI 1.8–9.7). Polyaddiction showed higher lesion rates (86.4% vs. 44.1% in single-habit, χ² =10.14, P = 0.002; OR 3.5, 95% CI 1.5–8.1). Tobacco use (smoked/smokeless) linked to leukoplakia and keratosis (P = 0.029; OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.2–6.5), whereas alcohol associated with candidiasis (P = 0.041; OR 2.4, 95% CI 1.1–5.3). HIV parameters influenced the outcomes. CD4 counts <350 cells/mm3 (n = 19, 33.9%) had 78.9% lesion prevalence vs. 42.3% in ≥350 (n = 37, 66.1%; χ² =6.54, P = 0.011; OR 3.1, 95% CI 1.3–7.4). Candidiasis was markedly higher in patients with low CD4 (57.9% vs. 10.8%). Longer HIV duration (>5 years, n = 32) correlated with lesions (P = 0.048; OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.0–4.4). Multivariate analysis confirmed the independent effects of polyaddiction (P = 0.009) and low CD4 (P = 0.015). Table 2 shows the correlation between CD4 Count and soft tissue lesions.

DISCUSSION

The 60.7% prevalence of oral soft tissue lesions in this LGBTQ HIV cohort aligns with global estimates ranging from 40% to 70%, although variations exist due to ART accessibility and population differences.[135] Candidiasis, the most frequent (26.8%), is a hallmark of immune compromise, often emerging at CD4 <500 cells/mm3, consistent with our findings where 57.9% of low-CD4 participants were affected.[29] Oral hairy leukoplakia and aphthous ulcers, at 14.3% and 10.7%, reflect Epstein–Barr virus reactivation and mucosal inflammation, respectively, mirroring reports from Asian and African cohorts.[1018] Suboptimal oral hygiene, with only one-third brushing twice daily, significantly associated with lesions (P = 0.034), underscores how plaque accumulation fosters opportunistic pathogens in immunocompromised hosts.[711] This is exacerbated in HIV, where xerostomia from medications or infections reduces salivary protection.[12] Universal addictions in our sample – higher than general HIV studies (50%–70%) – highlight LGBTQ-specific stressors like minority stress contributing to substance use.[1315] Poly-addiction’s strong link (P = 0.002) suggests synergistic mucosal damage; tobacco’s association with keratosis/leukoplakia (P = 0.029) aligns with its carcinogenic effects, whereas alcohol’s tie to candidiasis (P = 0.041) may involve immune modulation and microbiome shifts.[819] The inverse CD4-lesion relationship (P = 0.011) reaffirms lesions as prognostic markers, with low counts predicting opportunistic dominance.[620] Longer HIV duration’s correlation (P = 0.048) implies cumulative immune wear, although ART mitigates this in adherent patients. Compared to non-LGBTQ HIV studies, our prevalence is higher, possibly due to stigma delaying care.[142122] Implications include advocating for LGBTQ-sensitive dental programs, integrating addiction counseling, and routine oral screening in HIV clinics to prevent progression to severe outcomes like malignancy.[23] Limitations include the small sample size, which potentially limits generalizability, and the cross-sectional design, which precludes causality. Self-reported data may introduce bias, and the absence of viral load analysis may overlook its role. Future multicenter longitudinal studies should incorporate microbiome analyses and intervention trials to validate these findings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates a high burden of oral soft tissue lesions in LGBTQ individuals with HIV, primarily driven by inadequate oral hygiene, pervasive addictions, and immunocompromise, as indicated by low CD4 counts. These modifiable factors offer opportunities for interventions. Policymakers and clinicians should prioritize multidisciplinary strategies, including community-based hygiene education, accessible addiction treatment, and seamless integration of dental services within HIV care frameworks. Such approaches could substantially alleviate oral morbidity, improve ART adherence, and enhance the quality of life in this marginalized population. Further research is warranted to explore the long-term impact and efficacy of targeted programs.
