Overexpression of Scr1 protein induces strong changes in the transcriptome and metabolome of Streptomyces coelicolor that affect antibiotic production
Authors: Carolina Riascos, Gemma Fernández-García, Javier García-Martín, Ángel Manteca, Ramón I. Santamaría, Margarita Díaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundXenobiotic response element (XRE) regulator proteins play an important role in gene regulation in Streptomyces. In these bacteria, they are often encoded together with a small protein containing a DUF397 domain. Previous work on 15 such pairs present in S. coelicolor has shown they act as pleiotropic regulators. They dependently affect antibiotic production and morphological differentiation on different culture media. Overexpression of the protein Scr1, which is encoded by gene SCO4441, induces a drastic increase in the production of the antibiotic actinorhodin in this bacterium. This study involves a transcriptomic and metabolomic study of this overexpression.ResultsRNA-Seq assay showed that overexpression of Scr1 in the wild-type strain of S. coelicolor modified the expression of 1,308 genes at 36 h of culture incubation and 1339 at 48 h. The data indicated that almost 70% of the differentially expressed genes were up-regulated. Among them, 21% of the overexpressed genes were related to antibiotic biosynthesis pathways corresponding to antibiotics such as actinorhodin, calcium-dependent antibiotic (CDA), ectoin, arsono, hopene, coelimycin-P1, and albaflavenone. Conversely, some pathways were under-expressed such as those corresponding to coelichelin and undecylprodigiosin production. Metabolome studies corroborated these results and showed that the most notable differences in the production of these metabolites occurred at 72 and 96 h of incubation. A total of 3,978 and 4,392 metabolites were detected at 72 and 96 h respectively, from which 54 and 45 adducts corresponding to 15 and 14 secondary metabolites were identified. Of these, 5-hydroxyectoine, actinorhodin, albaflavenone, coelichelin, and coelimycin P1 are a few examples. In addition, other metabolic processes were affected such as those associated with energy metabolism, protein synthesis, response to stress, and the transport of molecules.ConclusionsThe overexpression of Scr1 in S. coelicolor causes changes in the regulation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis that may be related to the alteration of several other important primary metabolites. This transcriptional and metabolomic analysis takes a step forward in elucidating the pleiotropic role of the Scr1 protein in developmental processes and its importance in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in S. coelicolor. A model of its regulatory network is presented.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02843-5.

Background

Streptomyces are filamentous Gram-positive bacteria with a high GC genomic content. Although they are found in different types of habitats, soil is the most common environment. These microorganisms are characterized by the production of a wide range of secondary metabolites with antibiotic, antifungal, antitumor, and immunosuppressant activities [1].

For more than 70 years, Streptomyces coelicolor has been used as a model for studying this genus due to the production of two-coloured antibiotics: actinorhodin (ACT) which is blue; and undecylprodigiosin (RED) which is red. These characteristics facilitate the observation of the effect of different regulators on their production [2]. In its linear chromosome of almost 9 Mb, with 7825 identified genes, there are more than 20 clusters encoding for known or proposed secondary metabolites and a large number of regulatory genes [3]. The expression of these genes is subject to complex regulatory processes, consisting of a network involving different types of regulators at the transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels [4–7].

XRE/DUF397 systems are pleiotropic regulators comprising two proteins [8]. One component is a Xenobiotic Response Element (XRE) protein of approximately 32 kDa, which is a transcription factor, with a DNA-binding domain at the N-terminal end that represses or activates gene transcription. The other is a small protein, called DUF397, with an approximate size of 8 kDa, which, as its name suggests, is made up of a ‘Domain of Unknown Function’. Some of these systems have been found to function as transcriptional regulators involved in antibiotic production and/or aerial mycelial development in Streptomyces. Thus, the S. coelicolor XRE protein encoded by SCO1979 binds upstream to genes actII-ORF4, redZ, and cdaR, the corresponding specific regulators of ACT, RED, and CDA pathways [9]. Moreover, the loss of function of the S. venezuelae BldB protein, a DUF397 protein, induces a dramatic increase in the transcription of antibiotic biosynthesis activator A (abaA) paralogues and a novel inhibitor of sporulation identified as iosA [10].

Based on a bioinformatic analysis, XRE/DUF397 pairs were initially classified as type 2 toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems. However, our research group showed that none of the S. coelicolor XRE/DUF397 systems behave as TA systems under the different conditions tested [8]. In contrast, these systems have been shown to regulate antibiotic production, with system Scr1/Scr2 (SCO4441/SCO4442) being the strongest positive regulator among them, both in S. coelicolor and in several other Streptomyces species [11]. Indeed, overexpression of only the XRE protein, Scr1, is sufficient to trigger pleiotropic responses when Scr2 is present in the host genome. However, this activation does not occur if Scr1 overexpression is performed in S. coelicolor ∆scr2 or ∆scr1/∆scr2 mutant strains [11].

In this work, we have conducted a comparative transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of S. coelicolor overexpressing the scr1 gene (Sscr1 strain) and the same wild-type (wt) strain harbouring the corresponding empty expression plasmid (S. coelicolor wt strain) to understand the regulatory network exerted by the Scr1 protein in Streptomyces.

Methods

Strains and media used

The Streptomyces strains used in this work were S. coelicolor M145 [2] harbouring the multicopy plasmid pNX4441 (Sscr1 strain, overexpressing the scr1 gene under the xysA promoter [12]) and S. coelicolor M145 containing plasmid pN702GEM3 (control strain – empty plasmid) [11]. SFM solid medium was used to obtain spores [13]. For the phenotypic analysis, RNA-Seq and metabolomic studies, NMMP medium [13] supplemented with 1% xylose and 20 µg/mL neomycin was used. Escherichia coli DH5α and E. coli ET12567 (a dam− strain) were used for the transformation experiments [14, 15].

Bacterial growth and antibiotic production analysis

Quantification of ACT and RED in liquid medium. Four replicate cultures of each strain were grown in 50 mL baffled flasks, containing 10 mL of NMMP medium [13], 1% xylose, 4.5 mM phosphate, and 20 µg/mL neomycin inoculated with 106 spores/mL. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C under agitation (200 rpm) and mycelium was collected at four time points (24, 36, 48 and 60 h) Aliquots of 700 µL were taken from each flask at the designated time points, and antibiotic quantification was conducted using a modified colorimetric method based on that described by Kieser [13]. All experiments were performed in quadruplicate.

CDA production assay. For CDA production, strains were cultured at 30 °C for 2 days. Subsequently, the plates were overlaid with 5 mL of soft agar containing 60 mM Ca(NO₃)₂ and inoculated with Bacillus subtilis as the test microorganism (0.2 mL, 0.25 OD), followed by incubation at 30 °C for 20 h. A replica plate lacking calcium was employed as a negative control.

RNA extraction

For RNA extraction three of the 10 mL replicate cultures indicated above were collected at two time points (36 and 48 h) by centrifugation. The pellets were then washed with 2 mL of PBS before being treated with 4 mL of RNA Protect (Qiagen). The samples were stored at -20 °C before use. Mycelium was macerated by incubating the samples in TE buffer with lysozyme (15 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 15 min. RNA extraction was performed using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA was finally eluted in 50 µL of RNase-free water and its quantity and quality were determined using a 2100 Bioanalyzer. RIN values higher than 9 were obtained for all samples. The RNA was precipitated using 0.25 volumes of NaCl (0.8 M) and 3 volumes of ethanol and sent to Macrogen (South Korea) according to the company’s instructions.

Library, sequencing, and RNA-Seq analysis

The RNA-Seq analysis was performed by the company Macrogen, the equipment used was Novaseq 6000 Illumina, the Ribo-Zero Bacteria kit was used to clean the rRNA samples, and the Illumina Stranded Total RNA library was used to obtain the sample library. Sequences were generated by paired-end sequencing reads.

The sequencing data obtained were converted to a FASTQ format using the bcl2fastq package and can be accessed from the NCBI SRA database (BioProject: PRJNA902857; BioSample accessions: SAMN31777774 and SAMN31777775).

A differential analysis was performed using the RaNA-seq programme available at https://ranaseq.eu/ [16]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to generate a scatter plot illustrating the distribution of samples along the first two principal components. The spatial configuration of points within the plot reflects the overall (dis)similarity between RNA extracts samples from both strains (Sscr1and control) and time points (36 and 48 h). Samples exhibiting errors or deviation may be positioned separately from the main cluster, while paired samples and those with the identical input condition are expected to appear in proximity within the graph. STRING (https://string-db.org/) [17] was used for performing a functional enrichment analysis and StrepDB (http://strepdb.streptomyces.org.uk/) (StrepDB - Database Commons, s.f.) for analysing annotated genes in Streptomyces genome. antiSMASH [18] was used for analysing the clusters of secondary metabolites.

In the differential expression analysis (Sscr1 vs. the control), log2FC values determined the degree of change in expression. Values lower than or equal to -1 (log2FC < -1) indicated which genes were being down-regulated and values greater than or equal to 1 (log2FC ≥ 1) indicated up-regulated genes. Changes that showed an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05, in addition to a change in log2FC, were considered statistically valid.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Specific primers targeting the genes of interest were designed using the Primer Quest Tool (IDT) (https://eu.idtdna.com/page). The RNA employed for the RNA-Seq experiment (see above) served as the template for cDNA synthesis, which was carried out using iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad). RT-qPCR was conducted utilising the TB GreenTM Premix Ex TaqTM (TAKARA), adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s instructions throughout all preceding steps. Technical triplicates were performed for each sample and reverse transcription (RT) and no-template controls (NTC) controls were included to confirm the absence of DNA contamination or environmental interference. The housekeeping rpsL (SCO4659) was used as the internal control. Amplification profiles were monitored using the CFX96 Touch Real Time PCR Detection System (BioRad). Primer efficiencies were checked via decimal serial dilutions of S. coelicolor genomic DNA with a known copy number. Relative expression of the genes of interest was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method [19].

Compound extraction and HPLC-MS metabolome analysis

For the metabolomic analysis, three replicate cultures were grown as above and harvested at four time points (36, 48, 72, and 96 h). Mycelium was separated from the liquid culture by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Compound extraction was performed by adding 0.56 volumes of ethyl acetate to the supernatant, vortexing three times for 2 min, and then centrifugation for 5 min at 10,000 rpm (the pellet was discarded). Cell extraction was carried out by adding one volume of acetone, followed by sonication in an ultrasonic bath (three cycles of 2 min on and 2 min off). The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The pellets were subjected to a second extraction using ethyl acetate, vortexed, and centrifuged as above. The supernatants were vacuum-dried by rotary evaporation (RV 10 Digital, IKA®-Werke GmbH &Co. KG, Germany), and the cell extracts were added to the same evaporating flasks in which the supernatant culture extracts were dried, and vacuum-dried by rotary evaporation.

The dry extracts were dissolved in 4 mL of methanol, transferred to 2 mL centrifugation tubes, and dried using a SpeedVac. The pellets were dissolved and combined in 400 µL of methanol: DMSO (1:1, v/v) solution. Five µL from each sample were analysed by liquid chromatography-electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (LC-HRESI-MS). A UPLC system (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), coupled with an ESI-UHR-Qq-TOF Impact II spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA), was used with negative ion mode acquisition in a m/z range from 40 to 2000 Da. Chromatographic separation was carried out using a Zorbax® Eclipse Plus C18 column (50 2.1 mm, 1.8 m) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The S. coelicolor analytes were eluted at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min using a gradient of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B) as follows: 0–10% (v/v) of B for 1 min, which was increased to 35% over 3 min, maintained at 35% for 1 min, then increased to 100% over 3 min, maintained at 100% during 2 min, decreased to 10% over 1 min and maintained at 10% (v/v) for 4 min.

Bioinformatic analysis of the metabolome

Raw data were processed using the software package DataAnalysis (version 4.3, Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). The chromatograms were calibrated using internal calibrants (sodium, phosphate acetate). Undecylprodigiosin, which is characterised by a 392.2709 m/z and an RT of 26.04, was used to test the reliability of the calibration, which had an average deviation of 0.0001 (i.e., 0.1 ppm).

The chromatograms were processed using MZmine 2 software (G0 Cell Unit, Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology, Onna, Okinawa, Japan) [20]. Each sample was compared against the wild-type strain containing the plasmid pN702GEM3 (three replicates each) according to the following workflow: mass detection was performed with the centroid mass detector using a noise level of 2.0E2; the ADAP chromatogram builder was set at a minimum group of 10, a group intensity threshold of 2.0E2, a minimum highest intensity of 5.0E2 and a m/z tolerance of 0.001; smoothing was performed with a filter width of 9; the chromatogram deconvolution used a local minimum search, a chromatogram threshold of 90%, a minimum RT range of 0.05, a minimum relative height of 1%, a minimum absolute height of 5.0E2, a minimum ratio of peak top/edge 2 and a peak duration range between 0.03 and 3, m/z centre calculation median; the isotopes were grouped using a m/z tolerance of 0.001 and an RT tolerance of 0.1, a monoisotopic shape, a maximum charge of 2; the representative isotope was selected as the most intense; using a join aligner with a m/z tolerance of 0.001, weight for m/z of 20, RT tolerance of 0.1, weight for RT of 20, the isotope pattern with a m/z tolerance of 0.001 was compared, minimum absolute intensity of 5.0E2 and a minimum score of 50%; and the duplicate peak filter used the single feature filter mode, an m/z tolerance of 0.001 and an RT tolerance of 0.1.

The MZmine results were exported to MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (Institute of Parasitology, McGill University, Montreal, QC, Canada) [21] for statistical analysis. The MetaboAnalyst parameters were as follows: the missing values were estimated as 1/5 of the minimum positive value of each variable; the abundance values were log10 transformed; and the data were scaled by “Pareto scaling”. The differences in abundance values (compared to the wild-type strain) were considered significant if the q-value was equal to or less than 0.05 and if the log2 Sscr1/control abundance was higher or lower than +/-1.

The secondary metabolites produced by S. coelicolor were described by Nett et al. [22]. The formula and monoisotopic exact masses of these secondary metabolites were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The exact masses of the ions of these compounds and their putative adducts were obtained according to Huang et al. [23]. This list of secondary metabolite masses was used to identify the putative secondary metabolites in the S. coelicolor metabolome, using a mass tolerance threshold of 0.001 Da. The retention time tolerance used to consider a specific mass as an isomer was 0.1 min (i.e., differences higher than 0.1 indicated different isomers).

Results

Transcriptomic analysis of the effect of Scr1 overexpression

As previously mentioned, the overexpression of only the Scr1 protein triggers the overproduction of ACT and RED in S. coelicolor host strains if Scr2 is present [11]. To gain insight into the mode of action of this overexpression, which causes the strong positive regulation of antibiotic production and differentiation observed, an RNA-Seq assay was performed. In this transcriptomic analysis, the Sscr1 strain (S. coelicolor M145 carrying the pNX4441 plasmid) was compared to the control strain (S. coelicolor M145 carrying the empty plasmid pN702GEM3) grown in NMMP medium. In this medium, whereas RED production (red colour in Additional File 1 A) was comparatively reduced in the Sscr1 strain (Additional File 1B), ACT (blue colour in Additional File 1 A) was increasingly produced over time (Additional File 1B). Additionally, an increase in the production of coelimycin P1 (yellow in Additional File 1 A) was observed at 36 h, along with greater CDA production at 48 h (Additional File 1 C).

RNA from both strains were isolated from three replicate cultures of each one grown to two incubation times: 36 and 48 h. A clear differential clustering of expression for each strain at each time point was observed (Fig. 1A) using principal component analysis (PCA) (see Methods). The normalized data were filtered by log2FC, which determines the degree of change in expression. Values lower than or equal to -1 (log2FC < -1) indicated genes that were down-regulated and values greater than or equal to 1 (log2FC ≥ 1) indicated those that were up-regulated. All changes in gene expression were determined with respect to the control (Sscr1 strain vs. control strain). Changes that showed an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05, in addition to a change in log2FC, were considered statistically valid (Fig. 1B).

From this analysis, the data showed that 1,308 genes were differentially expressed at 36 h and 1,339 genes at 48 h. Most of these genes were up-regulated and the changes were statistically significant (Fig. 1B). Of these genes, 336 were down-regulated and 972 up-regulated at 36 h. While at 48 h, 171 genes were found to be down-regulated and 1,168 up-regulated (Fig. 1B). These data indicate that almost 70% of the differentially expressed genes were up-regulated and that from 36 to 48 h, the number of up-regulated genes increased, and the number of down-regulated genes decreased.

Of the 2,122 genes differentially expressed in the transcriptional analysis of the Sscr1 strain, 433 up-regulated and 74 down-regulated genes were identified at both time points. Another 4 were only up-regulated at 36 h and then down-regulated at 48 h, and 14 were down-regulated at 36 h and then up-regulated at 48 h (Fig. 1C). One of the four genes that changed from being up-regulated to down-regulated is the SCO1593 gene, which encodes a putative transcriptional regulator. Similarly, SCO3956, which changed from being down-regulated to up-regulated, encodes the ABC transporter ATP-binding protein that interferes with the production of ACT and RED in S. coelicolor M145 [24].

In the functional enrichment analysis of the data obtained using STRING (https://string-db.org/) [17], a large percentage of genes associated with metabolic pathways were differentially expressed (Additional File 2). 21% of the annotated genes were classified in the category of ‘antibiotic biosynthesis pathway’ and these differences were observed mainly at 48 h. Also, genes associated with other pathways directly related to specific antibiotic production, such as ‘actinorhodin biosynthesis’ and ‘calcium-dependent antibiotic biosynthesis’, and other indirectly related metabolic pathways, such as ‘Acyl-CoA + malonyl-CoA = >polyketide’ or ‘fatty acid biosynthesis, initiation’, were found to be up-regulated. Other metabolic processes were also affected, some of which could be related to the synthesis of primary metabolites, the precursors of secondary metabolites (Additional File 2).

Scr1 overexpression has a dramatic effect on genes implicated in secondary metabolite biosynthesis

The RNA-Seq data showed that the genes encoding the four main antibiotics, RED, ACT, CDA, and coelimycin P1 produced by S. coelicolor were differentially expressed in the Sscr1 strain. In the RED pathway, 14 of the 21 genes of the secondary metabolic biosynthetic gene cluster were differentially expressed. The redX gene (SCO5878) was the only one overexpressed at 36 h, while 13 other genes located between SCO5883 and SCO5897 were all found to be under-expressed at 48 h (Fig. 2A). In the ACT pathway, 24 out of 25 genes were differentially expressed. The actII-ORF4 gene (SCO5085), the pathway-specific positive transcriptional regulator [25], was overexpressed only at 36 h. Eleven other genes were overexpressed at 36 and 48 h and the rest of the genes were overexpressed only at 48 h (Fig. 2B). With respect to CDA, 28 genes, including the three core biosynthetic genes, out of the 48 comprising its secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene cluster were up-regulated at 36 h; these same genes also exhibited overexpression at 48 h. Likewise, genes SCO3219 and SCO3227 encoding a putative lipase and an (S)-3,5-dihidroxifenilglicine transaminase, respectively, were also overexpressed. Finally, of the 33 genes involved in coelimycin P1 biosynthesis, 26 were overexpressed at 36 h and 25 at 48 h, including the biosynthetic genes. Twenty-two of these genes are overexpressed at both times (Fig. 2D). Coelimycin P1 was the first metabolite detected in the cultures used for RNA extraction due to its yellow colour at 36 h (Additional File 1). Coelimycin P1 is an unstable compound without antibiotic activity produced right after the synthesis of the antibiotic coelimycin A [26]. In the Sscr1 strain, only 5 genes of the secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene cluster of coelimycin P1 were not overexpressed.

In addition, the analysis of the S. coelicolor genome using the antiSMASH software [18] identified 24 additional clusters involved in secondary metabolism. Overexpression of Scr1 affects the expression of genes in all of these clusters, although the core of biosynthetic genes was not affected in several of them (Additional Files 3, 4, and 5). Interestingly, the affected genes were mainly up-regulated. Thus, most of the genes from the ectoine, deferoxamine, albaflavenone, nogalamycin, hopene, and arsono-polyketide pathways were up-regulated in the Sscr1 strain (Additional Files 3, 4, and 5). However, other secondary metabolites such as coelichelin genes were down-regulated at 36 h and up-regulated at 48 h in the Sscr1 strain (Additional File 3).

Therefore, the Sscr1 strain presented the altered expression of a high number of genes implicated in secondary metabolism and most of these genes were overexpressed. Figure 3 shows a genomic graphical representation of the genes encoding secondary metabolites that were differential expressed in the Sscr1 strain. This allowed us to observe that most of the core biosynthetic genes of secondary metabolites, such as ectoine, CDA, ACT, albaflavenone, coelimycin P1, hopene, and arsono, were up-regulated while only two genetic pathways corresponding to coelichelin and the RED were down-regulated. All these results indicate that the Scr1 protein acts mainly as a positive regulator of antibiotic production.

Effect of Scr1 overexpression on genes implied in other metabolic processes

Scr1 overexpression exhibited a pleiotropic effect associated with changes in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and other metabolic processes important for the physiology of Streptomyces. The main differentially expressed pathways are indicated below.

Ergothioneine biosynthesis. Four of the five genes implicated in this pathway (egtABCD (SCO0910 to SCO0913) were up-regulated at 36 h. This compound is a sulfur derivative from histidine that may protect S. coelicolor from oxidative stress [27] (Additional File 6).

Biotin biosynthesis. Three of the genes (SCO1244, SCO1245, and SCO1246) were overexpressed at 36 h. Biotin is an important cofactor of malonyl coenzyme A (malonyl-coA) biosynthesis, which is used as an elongation unit for the biosynthesis of fatty acids. Malonyl-coA also provides the units for the elongation of secondary metabolites such as ACT and RED in S. coelicolor (Additional File 6).

General carbon metabolic pathways. 79% of the genes implicated in different carbon metabolic pathways that were differentially expressed at 48 h were up-regulated in the Sscr1 strain. Therefore, most of the genes in the citrate cycle (SCO4388, SCO4827, SCO5042, SCO5999, SCO6269, SCO6270, and SCO7000) were overexpressed, except for SCO0922 and SCO4808. All genes associated with pyruvate metabolism (SCO0208 and SCO5261), propanoate metabolism (SCO2726, SCO3247, and SCO6832), and ketone body synthesis and degradation (SCO3079, SCO4502, and SCO5399) were also differentially expressed. The latter three genes encode acetyl-CoA c-acetyltransferase enzymes, which are shared with the two previously mentioned pathways and with the citrate cycle. These general pathways play a key role both in primary and secondary metabolism, providing precursors for secondary metabolites (Additional File 7).

Purine metabolism. Most of the genes related to this process were overexpressed at 48 h in the Sscr1 strain (Additional File 8). In addition, three oxidoreductases (SCO1132, SCO1133, and SCO1134) that convert xanthine into urate were overexpressed at both 36 and 48 h. However, five other genes, SCO6209, SCO6210, SCO6211, SCO6247, and SCO6248, whose proteins participate in the conversion of urate into urea, were down-regulated only at 36 h in the Sscr1 strain (Additional File 8). Purines are extremely abundant in soil where Streptomyces use them as a source of carbon and nitrogen. One of the final products of purine metabolism, allantoin, affects enzymes of some antibiotic biosynthetic pathways; therefore, the accumulation of urea and ammonium could also affect these routes [28].

Sulfur metabolism. Three genes, cysN (SCO6097), cysD (SCO6098), and cysC (SCO6099), involved in the assimilatory reduction of sulfate were highly overexpressed in the Sscr1 strain (Additional File 8). The first two genes encode sulfate adenyl transferase enzymes and the third an adenyl sulfate kinase. These enzymes allow S. coelicolor to use sulfate as a source of sulfur that can be directly incorporated into amino acids. They were found to have high FC values of 3.9, 4.4, and 4.0, respectively. Moreover, they could be responsible for supplying the elements necessary for the synthesis of certain essential amino acids in the production of secondary metabolites [29].

Fatty acid metabolism. All differentially expressed genes involved in fatty acid metabolism were up-regulated at 36 h (Additional File 9). Among these up-regulated genes, fabH2 (SCO3246), a synthase III; SCO3247, an acyl-CoA oxidase; and fabF3 (SCO3248), a type II synthase are particularly noteworthy. These three genes, together with SCO3245, a salicylate hydroxylase, and SCO3249, an acyl transporter, form the operon that synthesizes the unusual short C6 trans-2,3-epoxyhexanoyl fatty acid side chain forming part of CDA [30]. Moreover, one of the important steps in fatty acid biosynthesis is catalysed by an acetyl-CoA carboxylase and a carboxyltransferase, both forming a catalytic complex. The reaction involves two steps: a first step carried out by the acetyl-CoA carboxylase and a second step catalysed by carboxyltransferase. In strain Sscr1, the gene for one acetyl-CoA carboxylase, accC (SCO2777), and the genes for four acetyl-CoA carboxytransferrases (SCO3079, SCO4502, SCO5399, and SCO6027) were also overexpressed (Additional File 9). In Streptomyces, these enzymes are often responsible for the synthesis of malonyl-CoA required for fatty acid and polyketide biosynthesis [31]. Therefore, fatty acids are precursors of many of the secondary metabolites in S. coelicolor and the genes associated with this category are numerous and dispersed and included in other more specific categories described throughout this work.

Biosynthesis and metabolism of amino acids. At 48 h, 33 genes involved in amino-acid biosynthesis and metabolism were differentially expressed (Additional File 10). Of these, 7 genes were down-regulated and the rest were up-regulated in strain Sscr1. Thus, genes responsible for the biosynthesis of the amino acid arginine were under-expressed (SCO1570, SCO1577, SCO1578, SCO1579, SCO1580, and SCO7036), while most of the genes involved in its metabolism were overexpressed. On the other hand, several genes involved in tryptophan biosynthesis were overexpressed (SCO3211, SCO3212, SCO3213, and SCO3214). Tryptophan is one of the rarest amino acids in proteins and its synthesis is costly [32]. It is also part of some of the CDA molecules and, although it is not part of the ACT structure, some authors have proposed that tryptophan promotes both CDA and ACT production [33]. Other genes overexpressed at 48 h were related to valine, leucine, and isoleucine metabolism (SCO2777, SCO6832). The degradation of these amino acids is important in the generation of fatty acid precursors that can subsequently be used for the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites.

Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis. Transcriptional analysis revealed that 16 genes associated with aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis were overexpressed in the Sscr1 strain after 48 h (some of these are SCO0499, SCO1501, SCO1508, SCO1594, SCO1595, SCO3397, SCO3778, SCO3795, SCO3961, and SCO5699). The aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases expressed belong to amino acids such as L-alanine, L-aspartate, L-threonine, L-serine, L-methionine, N-formylmethionine, L-valine, L-lysine, L-proline, L-histidine, L-phenylalanine, and L-tryptophan. Differential expression of these genes was not observed at 36 h. These synthases are important in providing precursors for the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Additional File 11).

Effect of Scr1 overexpression on transport and regulation

In addition to the metabolic processes that were altered by Scr1 overexpression, the RNA-Seq assay also detected differential expression of several important genes involved in molecular transport and gene regulation, as detailed below.

ABC transporters: three types of ABC transporters were differentially expressed in the strain Sscr1: mineral and organic ion transporters; oligosaccharide, polyol and lipid transporters; and phosphate and amino acid transporters (Additional File 12). The expression pattern of these genes varied greatly from one type to another. For example, in the Sscr1 strain, genes for three molybdate transport-associated proteins (SCO3704, SCO3705, and SCO3706) were overexpressed only at 36 h. The same occurred for the betaine transporter (SCO4831), an osmoprotectant; except in this case, the gene for the substrate-binding protein (SCO2933) was not differentially expressed. Additionally, the genes for the three proteins responsible for the multiple sugar transporter were under-expressed. Thus, the gene encoding a putative sugar-binding protein (SCO2404) was under-expressed at 36 h, while those genes encoding the permease protein (SCO2406) and the ATP-binding protein (SCO2405) were under-expressed at 48 h. In the case of adenosine and the genes of four proteins associated with transport, the first, a lipoprotein (SCO4884), was under-expressed at 36 h, while the genes for the other three proteins, two permease proteins (SCO4887 and SCO4888) and an ATP-binding protein (SCO4886), were overexpressed at 48 h. As for orthophosphate transport, the genes for the three proteins responsible for its transport (SCO6814, SCO6415, and SCO6416) were overexpressed at 36 and 48 h and showed high FC values. Finally, the genes for proteins responsible for D-methionine transport were also overexpressed at 36 and 48 h (SCO1557, SCO1558, and SCO1559) (Additional File 12).

Genes associated with XRE/DUF397: Of the other 14 XRE/DUF397 systems present in the S. coelicolor genome, only SCO2513/SCO2514 had both XRE and DUF genes under-expressed at 36 and 48 h in the Sscr1 strain. Two other XRE genes were also differentially expressed in this strain: SCO1979 overexpressed at 36 h and SCO4301 under-expressed at 36 h. In addition, 4 DUF genes were differentially expressed in strain Sscr1. SCO6128 and SCO6235 were both under-expressed at 36 h, while SCO5124 was under-expressed at 36 and 48 h. On the other hand, the DUF gene SCO4177 was overexpressed at 48 h in strain Sscr1. Overexpression (1.8 times) of scr1 RNA itself was observed in strain Sscr1 (Additional File 13).

Validation of RNA- seq data by qRT-PCR

Four genes (scr1, actI-ORF3, malE and thrB) were selected for qRT-PCR assay to validate the differential expression patterns identified via RNA-Seq. As shown in Additional File 14, the comparative evaluation of both dataset reveals consistent expression trends. The scr1 gene is overexpressed at the both time points, actI-ORF3 shows overexpression exclusively at 48 h; malE exhibits underexpression solely at 36 h; and thrB is significantly underexpressed at 48 h. These results demonstrate a strong correlation between the RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR analyses.

Metabolomic analysis of the effect of Scr1 overexpression

To further investigate the regulatory network exerted by the Scr1 protein in S. coelicolor, a metabolomic analysis was also performed comparing the Sscr1 strain with the control strain (wild type carrying the empty plasmid). The metabolomes of culture samples grown in NMMP supplemented with 1% xylose and collected at 36, 48, 72, and 96 h were analysed. The S. coelicolor genome has been described to have the potential to produce at least 27 secondary metabolites [22]. Of these, we were able to identify different m/z ion adducts for 17 based on their exact masses (± 0.001 Da maximum allowed variation using high-resolution electrospray ionisation, LC-HRESI-MS): 2-methylisoborneol, 5-hydroxyectoine, actinorhodin, albaflavenone, coelichelin, deferoxamine, eicosapentaenoic acid, flaviolin, geosmin, 2-methylisoborneol, germicidin, isorenieratene, melanin, methylenomycin B, g-butyrolactone Scb1, tetrahydroxynaphthalene, and undecylprodigiosin. Twelve of them (2-methylisoborneol, g-butyrolactone Scb1, geosmin, germicidin, 2-methylisoborneol, 5-hydroxyectoine, albaflavenone, actinorhodin, eicosapentaenoic acid, melanin, tetrahydroxynaphthalene and undecylprodigiosin) showed significant differences in their abundance in S. coelicolor overexpressing Scr1 than in the control strain. The results are listed in Figs. 4 and 5, and Additional File 15 and are detailed below.

At 36 h, 596 metabolites showed significant differences in abundance between S. coelicolor overexpressing Scr1 and S. coelicolor harbouring the empty plasmid (Fig. 4A), among which four secondary metabolite adducts were identified (geosmin, 2-methylisoborneol, butyrolactone Scb1, and germicidin B) (Fig. 4E; Additional File 15). The most notable differences were observed at 72 and 96 h, with 3,978 and 4,392 metabolites showing differences in abundance, respectively (Fig. 4C and D). The extracted ion chromatograms of the m/z ions corresponding to the identified secondary metabolite adducts showing differences in abundance indicated that most of them were up-regulated in the strain overexpressing scr1 (compare the red lines of the Sscr1-overexpressing strain with the green lines of the control strain in Fig. 4I-L). Moreover, the abundance of these secondary metabolites was much higher at 72 and 96 h than at 36 and 48 h (note that the intensity scale in the 36-hour chromatogram is 10⁴, increasing to 10⁵ at 48 h, whereas at 72 and 96 h, it reaches 10⁶; Fig. 4I-L). The secondary metabolites with adducts showing significant differences in abundance in the S. coelicolor strain overexpressing scr1 compared to the control strain are summarised in the Venn diagram in Fig. 5. Transcripts of the genes encoding the biosynthetic pathways for coelimycin P1, actinorhodin, coelichelin, albaflavenone, and geosmin (heatmaps in Fig. 6A and E), along with the quantities of the different adducts of these compounds identified (chromatograms in Fig. 6A and E), were significantly up-regulated at one or more developmental time points in the Sscr1 strain, particularly at the later time points (48 h for transcripts; 72 and 96 h for metabolites, indicated by arrows in Fig. 6A and E).

Expression of genes involved in undecylprodigiosin production decreased at 48 h (heatmaps in Fig. 6F). However, the abundance of the most prominent undecylprodigiosin adducts did not differ between the Sscr1 and control strains (arrowheads in Fig. 6F) (Additional File 15). This result could suggest that down-regulation of the undecylprodigiosin biosynthetic pathway in the Sscr1 strain was not sustained long enough to result in differences in the production of this compound. Nevertheless, some undecylprodigiosin [M–H]⁻ isomers (with retention times of 15.6 and 19.4 min at 48 h, and 18.2 min at 96 h), and other undecylprodigiosin adducts such as [M + Cl]⁻ (retention times of 9.9 and 26.5 min at 48 h, and 17.8 min at 96 h) and [M + FA–H]⁻ (retention time of 12.5 min at 96 h) (Additional File 15), were not visible at the scale shown in Fig. 6F, yet displayed significant differences between the Sscr1 and control strains.

The expression of genes involved in germicidin biosynthesis was particularly altered at 36 h (heatmaps in Fig. 6G); some were up-regulated in the control strain, while others were up-regulated in the Sscr1 strain. Correspondingly, different germicidin variants showed varying abundances between the Sscr1 and control strains. Most of the germicidin A, B, and D adducts identified were significantly more abundant in the Sscr1 mutant (arrows in Fig. 6G) (Additional File 15).

Expression of genes encoding the deferoxamine biosynthetic pathway was up-regulated in the Sscr1 strain at 36 and 48 h (heatmaps in Fig. 6H). However, gene up-regulation in this strain was not substantial, and some biological replicates of the Sscr1 mutant at 48 h showed expression levels similar to those of the control strain (see the heatmaps in Fig. 6H). The abundance of the identified deferoxamine adduct was higher in the control strain (indicated by an asterisk in Fig. 6H) and the overall deferoxamine production was extremely low (note the weak signal, in comparison to the other compounds and the baseline in the chromatogram of Fig. 6H).

Discussion

S. coelicolor has 15 XRE/DUF397 paired systems predicted, through a bioinformatic analysis, to be type II toxin-antitoxin proteins [8, 34–36]. All of these systems have been overexpressed in S. coelicolor and none of them have been shown to have a toxic effect under the different conditions tested [8]. In fact, one of these systems, composed of the proteins encoded by SCO4441/SCO4442 (scr1/2) behaves as a potent activator of antibiotic production when overexpressed [11]. This system is present in most of the sequenced Streptomyces and Scr1, which is the positive regulator, has orthologues in different families of the phylum Actinomycetota, such as Glucomycineae, Micromonosporineae, Pseudonocardineae, and Frankineae [8], but none of these proteins have been previously studied. The positive effect of Scr1 requires the presence of the Scr2 protein [11] but no direct interaction of both proteins has been demonstrated, even after using different strategies such as two-hybrid analysis, in vitro co-immunoprecipitation, and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) analysis (Santamaria et al. data not published).

Transcriptional analysis of the Sscr1 strain, a strain that overexpresses the SCO4441 protein, led to the conclusion that the regulatory network of this protein is intricate and affects the expression of at least 1,308 genes at 36 h and 1,339 genes at 48 h, with most of them being up-regulated. Analysis of these results showed that at 36 h, the up-regulated genes were mainly involved in primary metabolism that supports growth and development. Some of the genes were also up-regulated at 48 h (Additional Files 9–11), while the up-regulation of many genes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis was observed mainly at 48 h (Fig. 2 and Additional Files 3–5). Also, there was intense up-regulation of some of the CDA biosynthetic genes at 48 h and genes associated with coelimycin P1 production, of which almost all were overexpressed more than 4-fold at 36 and 48 h. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which have demonstrated that the most pronounced differences between coelimycin and calcium-dependent antibiotic (CDA) emerged approximately 36 h after initiation [37, 38].

Through the metabolomic assay, we confirmed the data obtained in the transcriptomic assay regarding the production of secondary metabolites. There was a good correlation between the two approaches, with an increase in the production of 12 out of the 17 adducts detected (Figs. 4 and 5). A comparison of transcriptomic and metabolomic data shows that, while the peak expression of most secondary metabolite biosynthetic pathways occurs at 48 h, the highest abundance of secondary metabolites synthesised is observed at 72 and 96 h (Fig. 6). This result is not surprising, as the synthesis of secondary metabolites requires prior gene expression and protein translation of the corresponding biosynthetic pathway.

Conclusions

Scr1 overexpression in S. coelicolor exerts the pleiotropic regulation of several important primary metabolic processes such as energy metabolism, protein synthesis, response to stress, and molecule transport processes, among others. However, the main differences found were related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis, with Scr1 being mainly a good positive regulator of these compounds. In this complex regulatory network of Scr1, some primary processes are associated with secondary metabolite production straightforwardly (for instance tryptophan biosynthesis, fatty acids metabolism, acetyl-CoA), whereas for other regulatory networks, there is still a lack of information about how they work, such as ABC transporters (Fig. 7). However, this transcriptional and metabolomic analysis takes a step forward in elucidating the pleiotropic role of the Scr1 protein in developmental processes and its importance in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in S. coelicolor.
