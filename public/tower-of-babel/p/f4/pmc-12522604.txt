Access to essential medicines during the Sudanese civil war: a cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEssential medicines are those that meet the primary healthcare needs of the population. During the current Sudan war, access to these medicines is critical for evaluating the quality of the healthcare system in the aftermath of this crisis. This study aims to evaluate the availability and affordability of essential medicines in primary healthcare centers in Sudan. Additionally, it seeks to assess patients’ perceptions of the affordability, accessibility, acceptability, and impact of war on medicines in public healthcare facilities.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study across 35 primary healthcare centers in five of the seven safe states in Sudan. At each center’s dispensary unit, a standardized checklist was used to evaluate the availability, affordability, and storage conditions of 19 essential medicines chosen from Sudan’s national essential medicines list. Additionally, 975 patients from all dispensary units in the selected states were interviewed upon exiting to assess their perceptions of the accessibility, acceptability, accommodation, affordability, and impact of war on these medicines. Data were collected using the Kobo Toolbox and analyzed with SPSS version 26.ResultsThe overall availability of essential medicines was 45.1%. Treatment costs exceeded the daily wage for insured patients in 8 out of 19 medications and for uninsured patients in 18 out of 19. The average median price ratio was 39.3 with insurance and 187.1 without insurance. Accessibility, affordability, accommodation, and acceptability ratings were 3.16/5, 1.02/4, 4.24/6, and 5.42/6, respectively. Only 14.3% of outlets had functioning storerooms, though dispensary areas were generally in good condition.ConclusionSudanese patients during the recent war had limited access to essential drugs due to high prices and low availability. The outcomes for the patients’ access variables (accessibility, accommodation, acceptability, and affordability) were comparable to those in low-income countries. Immediate action is critical to guarantee access to affordable medicines, especially since many families have lost their primary sources of income.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02647-4.

Background

On April 15, 2023, violent clashes began in Sudan between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) [1]. This has resulted in one of the most rapidly escalating crisis, with 15,550 reported fatalities and over 8.8 million people displaced from their homes [2]. The ongoing armed conflict has severely impacted the healthcare system, rendering a staggering 68% of hospitals in conflict-affected areas non-operational [3]. Hospitals still operating in these areas report severe shortages of blood, fluids, and other life-saving commodities, as well as critical shortages of water, electricity, fuel, and food for patients [4]. Disruptions to medical supplies have compounded the crisis, leading to critical shortages of essential medicines [5]. These medicines, selected based on disease prevalence, efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness, are vital for primary healthcare [6]. Despite being a key UN Sustainable Development Goal, access to essential medicines remains a significant challenge. Nearly 2 billion people—about one-third of the global population—do not have regular access to these medicines [7]. In Africa, the situation is even more severe, with over half of the population lacking access [7, 8].

Availability and affordability are essential for ensuring equitable access to essential medicines [9]. Studies on the availability of essential medicines in Sudan are generally scarce. However, a study conducted in 2007 across six Sudanese regions found that the availability of essential medicines in primary healthcare (PHC) center pharmacies was relatively high, averaging 82%. Despite this, the study noted that the storage conditions for these medications were of low quality [10]. Another study conducted in October 2022 at primary health centers in Khartoum State, just before the current conflict, found that the overall availability of essential medicines for adults was 36.8% [11]. This decline reflects Sudan’s economic and political challenges. The 2007 study was conducted before South Sudan’s separation, during a period of oil revenue stability. After the separation, the general economic situation had worsened, further impacting healthcare access.

The affordability of medicines is another major barrier to healthcare access in Sudan. While health insurance plays a crucial role in reducing out-of-pocket expenses, coverage remains uneven, and financial protection is limited, leaving many patients struggling with medicine costs. In Sudan, Social Health Insurance covered 71.5% of families in Khartoum State and 50.7% of individuals in other states by 2017, with an additional 5.5% covered by other schemes such as police, military, and para-statal organizations [12]. The Federal Ministry of Finance contributed the majority of health insurance funding (72%), while household contributions remained low (9%) [12]. However, out-of-pocket payments remain the dominant source of healthcare financing, accounting for approximately 70% of total health expenditure (US$84.0 per capita), while general government health spending is significantly lower at only 22.3% (US$26.9 per capita) [13]. As a result, many insured patients still face high medicine costs. A 2022 study revealed that the cost of treatment for 10 out of 19 drugs exceeded the daily wage for insured patients [11]. A 2013 study in Sudan found that the affordability of the lowest-priced generics in the public sector was generally good for some conditions, with standard treatments costing a day’s wage or less [14].

The ongoing war in Sudan has severely disrupted the healthcare system, impacting the availability and affordability of essential medicines at primary healthcare facilities. Given that 52% of the population lives in poverty, many Sudanese depend heavily on public health services [11, 15]. In this context, measuring the accessibility of essential medicines is crucial for understanding healthcare equity. Inadequate access in public facilities could exacerbate the strain on the healthcare system and widen existing disparities.

Our study aimed to evaluate the availability and cost of essential drugs, as well as the quality of services at PHC dispensary pharmacies during the current conflict. Additionally, it examined patients’ perceptions of affordability, accessibility, acceptability, and the impact of war on medicines in public healthcare facilities. Access to essential medicines is both a public health concern and a key policy issue. Ensuring stable supply, financial protection, and efficient healthcare delivery is crucial, especially in crises. Sudan’s challenges offer insights for national policies and global health strategies in conflict settings.

Methods

Study concept

The study partially met its objective by utilizing Penchansky and Thomas’ concept of health service accessibility, which highlights the importance of evaluating all five dimensions of access: availability, accessibility, accommodation, affordability, and acceptability (Table S1) [16]. The availability of essential medicines in public PHC outlet pharmacies was evaluated using the standardized methodology from the World Health Organization & Health Action Initiative (WHO/HAI) [14]. The other dimensions were assessed using a standardized questionnaire derived from a previous study conducted in Sudan in 2022 [11].

Study design and settings

This is an observational descriptive, cross sectional, facility-based study. It was conducted between June 25 and July 22, 2024 during the current Sudan war. The study focused on primary healthcare centers across five of the seven safe states in Sudan: River Nile, Kassala, Gadarif, Blue Nile, and North Kordofan. While this focus may introduce selection bias, it was necessary to ensure feasible data collection. The selection process began with identifying the target states. Next, major localities within each state were chosen, and finally, the central city within each selected locality was identified for the study. The selected cities are Atbara, El Damar, and Shendi in River Nile State; Kassala in Kassala State; Gadarif in Gadarif State; El Damazin in Blue Nile State; and El Obeid in North Kordofan State.

We conducted this study in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Study population

The study included all government-run primary healthcare centers managed by physicians in the five selected states, which had on-site pharmacies or medicine dispensaries. Centers that were categorized as below the primary care level in the healthcare pyramid were excluded, including PHC units staffed by community health workers, dressing stations managed by nurses, and dispensaries overseen by medical assistants.

We included patients aged 18 years and above who were obtaining medicines from the center’s outlet dispensary. Patients who were severely ill—those with conditions causing significant physical distress or inability to participate in the survey—were excluded from data collection.

Sample size and sampling technique

This study focused on primary healthcare centers and their adult patients as sources of data.

The estimated sample size of health centers was chosen within 95% confidence interval and 15% margin of error (e), from 156 totals of eligible centers (N). With a 95% confidence interval (CI), 50% response distribution, and 5% margin of error; a sample of 384 was considered as the minimal sampling to represent the study population. By multiplying the sample size by a design effect of 2 to account for the clustering in the multi-stage sampling method, we adjusted for the increased variability within clusters. Ultimately, we reached a final collected sample size of 975 during the study period.

Multi-stage cluster sampling was utilized to select the sample facilities and patients. In the first stage, seven representative localities were chosen from the five states, representing the main localities within each state, followed by the selection of the central city within those localities. The second stage involved selecting PHC centers using probability proportional to size: 7 centers from Gadarif, 7 from Kassala, 6 from Atbara, 5 from El Obeid, 4 from El Damar, 4 from El Damazin, and 2 from Shendi were randomly selected. In the third stage, patients were selected. Due to the approximately equal patient rates at each center within the cities, the sample size was divided relatively equally across the seven cities, with at least 125 responses collected from each city, except for El Obeid, which had a significantly lower patient rate. Systematic random sampling was used to select the patients.

Data collection tools and techniques

For the dispensary unit’s assessment, the WHO has developed standardized survey forms to assess pharmaceutical situations in countries. These forms, differentiated for three healthcare levels, are included in the WHOs operational package for assessing pharmaceutical situation. The form for Level II focuses on indicators for evaluating the situation at the facility level [18]. These indicators assess the achievement of strategic pharmaceutical objectives: improved access, quality, and rational use. Access is measured by the availability and affordability of essential medicines, particularly for the poor and in the public sector. Quality is indicated by the presence of expired medicines and the adequacy of storage conditions, as testing samples can be costly. Rational use is evaluated by examining prescribing and dispensing practices, along with the implementation of standard treatment guidelines (STGs) and essential medicines lists (EMLs) [18].

The dispensary survey forms focused on several key elements: basic demographic information, the availability of selected medications, the cost of each medicine’s dosage form, the adequacy of storage conditions in both storerooms and dispensary units, and the sources of the drugs in the pharmacy.

This research is based on a medication list from a previous study conducted in 2022 in Khartoum State [11]. The standardized list includes fifteen key medicines (19 dosage formulations) and was developed in accordance with WHO recommendations from their operational package for pharmaceutical situation assessment and the Sudan National Essential Medicines List − 2019 (NEML) [18, 19]. The 2019 NEML remains valid as the most recent version officially endorsed by the Ministry of Health in Sudan.

For the patients’ questionnaire, a standardized questionnaire was adapted from the 2022 Khartoum study [11]. This questionnaire was based on Penshansky and Thomas’ concept of access to medicines, which outlines five dimensions for a comprehensive analysis of medicine access from the patient’s perspective: availability, accessibility, accommodation, affordability, and acceptability (Table S1). The impact of the war on these dimensions was also evaluated in a separate section.

The Kobo Toolbox application, an offline mobile data collection tool commonly used for epidemiological surveys, was employed to gather patient data. The data collection process included face-to-face interviews with study participants, filling out checklists provided by pharmacy personnel, and observing dispensary units and storerooms to evaluate the adequacy of storage conditions.

Availability index

According to WHO/HAI, the availability of essential drugs is defined as the proportion of surveyed institutions that have a specific drug available out of the total number of surveyed institutions [20]. The mean availability of the selected medicines was calculated following WHO/HAI guidelines. Additionally, for each surveyed PHC facility, we calculated the proportion of assessed medicines that were available, providing a facility-level availability index. This allowed for a comparison of medicine availability across different study states.

Affordability index

As defined by WHO/HAI, the affordability of an essential medication during a specific course of treatment is calculated by dividing the total medicine price for treating a condition with standard dosages by the minimum daily wage for lowest-paid unskilled government worker [20]. In this study, the price of each essential drug for the full treatment course was collected in Sudanese pounds (SDGs) and then divided by 311 SDGs, which represents the daily wage of the lowest-paid unskilled government worker in Sudan, as sourced from the World Salaries website [21]. If the total price of drug treatment is less than this minimum daily income threshold, the drug is considered more affordable, and vice versa.

We used the median price ratio (MPR) as another way to evaluate the price of medicines, which represents the ratio of a medicine’s unit price to the international reference price (IRP) [20]. The formula for calculating MPR is as follows: MPR = (median unit price of the target drug within the survey range/international reference price) × 100%. When comparing drug purchase price levels using MPR values, MPR = 1 is typically the threshold. An MPR below 1 indicates that the investigated drug price is lower than the international average standard, and an MPR above 1 suggests it is higher.

Data management and analysis

Patient data was downloaded from Kobo Collect into an Excel sheet and manually cleaned. The analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26. Missing variables were coded, and descriptive statistics were generated for patient characteristics, drug availability, the impact of war, and mean prices, with results presented in tables and figures. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and independent sample t-tests were employed to explore differences in index scores based on various sociodemographic characteristics, while Spearman’s rho correlation tests were used to identify associations between study variables. Scores for accessibility, acceptability, affordability, and accommodation were calculated based on participants’ responses to specific questionnaire items, with higher scores reflecting better outcomes. Affordability was assessed using two items (financial adversity due to health and medication expenses, with a total score of 4), while accessibility (perceived distance, ease of transportation, and directions to the dispensing unit, with a total score of 5), acceptability (service quality, staff treatment, and respect for privacy, with a total score of 6), and accommodation (comfort, wait times, and suitability of opening hours, with a total score of 6) were each evaluated using three items. The total scores were determined based on the number of items used to assess each dimension, with each item contributing to the final score based on response scaling. This approach was consistent with the methodology used in the 2022 Khartoum study [11], ensuring comparability and consistency in measurement.

Additionally, the internal consistency of the subscales was assessed in the 2022 study through a pilot, where the Cronbach alpha statistic was calculated to be 0.68, further validating the reliability of the scoring system.

Results

Demographics and general information

This study was conducted in 2024 across 35 primary health care centers in five safe states of Sudan. A total of 975 participants were involved, with an average age of 42.3 ± 15.9 years. Among the participants, 672 (68.9%) were female. The majority were married, accounting for 709 (72.7%) of the participants, and 238 (24.4%) held a university degree, though 525 (53.8%) were unemployed. Nearly half, 482 (49.4%), had a monthly income of less than 200,000 SDG (332.50 USD). Most participants, 705 (72.3%), had not been displaced, and 653 (67.0%) had health insurance. Additionally, 293 (41.5%) of participants stated that the quality of service deteriorated after the war. Factors affecting accessibility, affordability, accommodation, and acceptability

Sex, age, state, marital status, educational level, occupational status, and health insurance were significantly associated with accessibility, affordability, accommodation, and acceptability (p < 0.05). Income was significantly associated with the affordability score (p = 0.000). Displacement status was associated with both the acceptability score (p = 0.001) and the accommodation score (p = 0.003) (Table S2 and S3).

Binary logistic regression analysis of overall access dimensions

Regarding the association between sociodemographic factors and patients’ accessibility to medicines, patients residing in Kassala and River Nile States had significantly higher odds of accessing medicines compared to those in other states (Kassala: Exp(B) = 1.88, 95% CI = 1.27–4.21, p = 0.015; River Nile: Exp(B) = 1.86, 95% CI = 1.21–3.28, p = 0.006). Marital status also demonstrated a significant association, with divorced individuals having lower odds of accessing medications compared to married individuals (Exp(B) = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.20–0.96, p = 0.029). Additionally, non-displaced individuals were more likely to access medicines compared to those who were displaced (Exp(B) = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.03–2.04, p = 0.021) (Table S4).

The median price ratio (MPR)

The average median price ratio (MPR) for the 19 medications is 39.3 with insurance and 187.1 without insurance. This means that, on average, essential medications cost 39.3 times more than their international reference prices. The most significant price differences were observed in ringer lactate, priced at 388.89 for insured patients and 1999.09 for uninsured patients, and cotrimoxazole suspension, priced at 82.14 for insured patients and 719.34 for uninsured patients. None of the drugs had a price that was less than double the international price for uninsured patients; however, artesunate injection, ciprofloxacin eye drops, and acetylsalicylic acid tablets were priced at less than double the international price for insured patients (Table 5).

Conservative conditions, sourcing, and dispensing staff

The storerooms and dispensaries of each pharmacy outlet were assessed for proper storage conditions. Only 14.3% had a functioning storeroom, all of which (100%) had temperature control methods, but only 20% maintained temperature charts. Additionally, only 40% of storerooms had windows or air vents, were free of moisture, and systematically stored the medications. Regarding the dispensary areas, most units had temperature control methods in place (94.3%), but only 11.4% maintained temperature charts (Table S6 and S7).

The primary source of medications was the Medical Supply Funds, accounting for 51.4%, while 28.6% were obtained through commercial sources. Most medications were dispensed by a pharmacy aide or health assistant, comprising 77.1% of the cases. Notably, 5.7% of the units had untrained staff or nurses responsible for dispensing medications, which is concerning.

Discussion

This study evaluated patients’ access to essential medicines at the primary healthcare level in five of the seven safe states in Sudan in 2024.

The availability of essential medications in this study was 45.1% considerably less than the 80% target of the WHO [22]. This availability is also lower than the 52.8% reported in Yemen during the conflict [23]. The situation is further exacerbated by additional challenges. Nearly half of the participants depended on public transportation to reach primary health centers. They reported that transportation costs had increased and routes had become more dangerous due to the war. These statements come from those who were able to reach the health centers, suggesting an unknown number of individuals might have been unable to access care because of transportation issues. Evidence indicates that transportation barriers significantly hinder healthcare access, especially for low-income individuals [24].

In Sudan, the average household size is 5.5 [25]. In this study, most participants had a monthly income of less than 200,000 SDGs (332.50 USD), which amounts to 60.5 USD per person, or 2.02 USD per day. This indicates that they live in extreme poverty, as defined by the World Bank, which classifies extreme poverty as living on less than $2.15 per day [26]. This situation is likely a consequence of the Sudanese military conflict, which has led to the unemployment of half the population and loss of the Sudanese pound to 50% of its value [27]. The deterioration of economic conditions and loss of employment have been accompanied by rising household healthcare expenses and medication costs, as reported by participants and confirmed by calculating the MPR. For example, the MPR for Ringer lactate for uninsured patients increased from 480.14 in 2022 to 1999.09 in 2024 [11]. While inflation rose from 138.8 in 2022 to 200.1 in 2024 [28], no adjustment was made to maintain consistency with the WHO/HAI methodology. The sharp increase in MPR is also driven by war-related factors, including currency devaluation, the destruction of local pharmacies, and severe supply chain disruptions. In fact, the MPR for most of the assessed medications has at least doubled. In addition to the devaluation of the Sudanese pound, the total monetary flow within the system has significantly decreased as funds have been redirected to defense spending and external support has been withdrawn [29]. This recent conflict has resulted in an estimated loss of USD 700 million to its already underfunded health system [30]. With out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure in Sudan at 56.9% [31], 62.9% of participants reported having to forgo essential items to afford their medications. It is important to recognize that the conflict has exposed the underlying structural issues in Sudanese health financing forcing us to face them and resulting catastrophic consequences in the public [29]. A significant issue highlighted in this study is the substantial disparity in drug affordability between insured and uninsured patients. The findings demonstrate a pronounced difference in the cost of antimalarial for these two groups, underscoring the essential role of health insurance in enhancing the affordability and accessibility of vital treatments, especially for high-cost medications like antimalarial. This is particularly critical given that malaria remains a major public health concern in Sudan [10].

The study uncovered a significant issue with the storage conditions of pharmaceuticals, noting that most primary healthcare centers lack dedicated storerooms. This is primarily because dispensaries receive only a modest supply of drugs, which are stored within the dispensary area itself, rendering storerooms unnecessary. These findings align with other studies conducted in Sudan, indicating a more profound, systemic issue in the management of dispensary storerooms [8, 11].

However, the study also found that the storage conditions within the dispensary rooms were generally adequate and well-equipped to maintain the drugs in their proper state. Another critical concern is that, despite the strict recommendations for recording cold storage temperatures [32], most dispensaries and storerooms do not have temperature charts in place. This oversight could lead to drug damage and a reduction in their shelf life. It was observed that in a small percentage (5.7%) of the units, dispensing was handled by nurses or untrained staff. The presence of untrained staff in medication dispensing raises concerns about the rational use of medicines and patient adherence [33]. While task shifting has been proposed as a strategy to address workforce shortages in conflict-affected settings, its implementation requires structured training programs to ensure patient safety. The findings highlight the need for policies to enhance the pharmaceutical workforce by increasing training opportunities for dispensary staff and strengthening regulatory oversight.

Participants reported good acceptability and accommodation, evidenced by the high quality of care, respect for privacy, cleanliness of facilities, and suitable opening hours. This contradicts the generally poor findings regarding the quality of care in primary care units across Africa [34–36].

Access to medications was influenced by several factors, with residents of River Nile and Kassala States having significantly better access compared to other states. Following the war, the Ministry of Health was moved to Port Sudan, where international organizations now deliver essential medications [37]. The ease of transportation between Port Sudan and these states may have a role in the accessibility to medications in them [38]. However, other factors like population density should also be considered. Interestingly, marital status was associated with access to medications, with divorced individuals having significantly less access. The reasons for this can only be speculated upon, potentially relating to baseline access and service utilization prior to divorce. While it is unclear which specific aspects of access are impacted by divorce and in what manner, it is known that married individuals tend to utilize healthcare services more effectively [39–41]. Non-displaced individuals had better access to medications compared to those who were displaced. This disparity might be attributed to their familiarity with the local healthcare system, the geographic location they selected prior to the conflict, and the fact that their reference for evaluating the acceptability and accommodation of healthcare services remains consistent with the same centers. In contrast, displaced individuals may compare these services to those in their original residences, which are often in the capital.

This study offers important insights into the availability and access to essential medications in Sudan following the military conflict. The research employs the widely accepted WHO/HAI methodology, frequently cited in the literature, offering valuable context and enabling comparative analysis. However, it is important to recognize that the data were gathered from patients who had access to public health centers in relatively secure states, and the dispensaries evaluated were also located in these safer regions. Consequently, the findings mainly reflect the war’s impact in these areas, with the situation in conflict zones expected to be considerably worse. Additionally, while various statistical analyses were conducted, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons, which may increase the risk of Type I errors. A 15% margin of error, used in a pre-war Khartoum study, was applied here due to logistical constraints. This may have introduced some variability in representativeness.

Conclusion

Access to essential medicines in Sudan during the war was severely limited, with availability falling below WHO targets. High costs made affordability a major barrier, especially for uninsured patients. While access factors from the patient’s perspective were similar to other developing nations, the war’s devastating impact on the health system demands urgent intervention. Expanding health insurance, strengthening local drug production, and improving supply chains are crucial to restoring medicine accessibility. Targeted subsidies can ease costs for uninsured patients, while coordinated efforts from government and aid organizations are essential to addressing the war’s impact on the health system.
