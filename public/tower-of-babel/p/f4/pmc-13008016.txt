Ending universal school meals: student perspectives on how policy reversal shapes hunger, stigma, and learning.
Authors: Dania Orta-Aleman, Monica D Zuercher, Tessa Pulido, Juliana F W Cohen, Punam Ohri-Vachaspati, Marlene B Schwartz, Anisha I Patel, Michele Polacsek, Caitlin D French, Lorrene D Ritchie, Wendi Gosliner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, federal waivers allowed schools participating in federal school meal programs to provide free breakfast and lunch, regardless of income. When these waivers expired in June 2022, most states returned to means-tested eligibility. This study examined middle and high school students' experiences transitioning from Universal School Meals (USM) to a means-tested system.MethodsWe conducted 36 semi-structured interviews with students in Texas (n = 21) and Arizona (n = 15) to explore their experiences before, during, and after USM implementation.ResultsStudents reported seven themes after USM ended: (1) witnessing food insecurity among peers; (2) hunger interfering with learning; (3) increased financial stress and anxiety; (4) meal denial and debt; (5) reduced participation; (6) renewed stigma and social hierarchies; and (7) desire for USM restoration, perceiving means-testing as unfair. Students described that USM reduced economic distinctions and supported learning. Post-USM, they observed peers skipping meals, sharing food, struggling to concentrate, and experiencing public embarrassment when unable to pay.ConclusionStudents described the return to means-testing as inequitable and harmful. Policies that restore or expand USM could reduce stigma, improve nutritional access, and support educational outcomes. Moreover, USM should be considered as a public health and educational investment rather than a temporary measure.Keywords: school health, school meals, food insecurity

Introduction

School meal programs in the United States represent a $22 billion annual federal investment in child nutrition, serving approximately 30 million students daily through the National School Lunch and School Breakfast Programs.1 These programs provide meals of higher nutritional quality than the average child's diet, making them one of the healthiest food sources for school-aged children.2-4 Evidence shows these programs can address childhood food insecurity, improve health and nutrition outcomes, and promote educational success.2,5-8

Current federal school meal policies use a means-tested framework that segments students into distinct income eligibility categories determining the cost of the school meal: full-price, reduced-price (family incomes between >130% and ≤185% of the federal poverty level [FPL]), and free (≤130% of FPL). Documented barriers associated with this means-tested framework include administrative burden, stigmatization, lower participation among eligible students, and accumulation of meal debt for families and schools.9-12 Complex meal application processes and social stigma prevent many food-insecure children from accessing available nutrition support in schools.10,13 In addition, many food-insecure households just above the income threshold are excluded from receiving needed support from this means-tested system, leaving families underserved, particularly in high-cost living areas.7

Universal School Meals (USM) is an alternative policy approach that eliminates income eligibility requirements and provides free meals to all students regardless of their family's economic status. Previous research on USM implementations via policies such as the Community Eligibility Provision (CEP) in select school districts and states has shown increased participation, improved food security, reduced administrative costs, decreased stigmatization, and improved academic performance.5,14,15 However, evidence of the effect of CEP on some outcomes is mixed: studies examining effects on school attendance have yielded inconclusive results across grade levels, and effects on academic performance vary by context.5,6,15-17

The nationwide adoption of USM during the COVID-19 pandemic, enabled by federal waivers in the 2021-2022 school year (SY), represented an unprecedented policy shift, giving all students attending schools participating in the National School Lunch and/or School Breakfast Programs access to free breakfast and lunch, regardless of household income.18 Early evaluations of these waivers report increased participation rates, reduced meal debt, decreased stigma, and positive effects on household food security.7,10-12,19-21 At the same time, studies have documented operational challenges during the waiver period, including increased food service workload, staffing shortages, supply chain disruptions, and food waste, particularly when participation expands faster than food service capacity.5,10-12,16,22 When these federal waivers expired in June 2022, most states returned to the traditional means-tested system.

While previous studies have reported students' experiences with school meal programs, including our own work documenting student perspectives on the benefits and challenges of USM in California,10 and other research examining student experiences with stigma and meal participation,11,23 these studies were conducted either among students currently receiving USM or under the traditional means-tested system. No prior study has examined students' perspectives after receiving USM for over a year and then returning to the traditional means-tested system. Students' perspectives on the transition from USM to the traditional means-tested system offer a unique contribution to the academic literature and policy discourse. As firsthand observers, students witness how policies influence participation and the social dynamics of school meals, including stigma, embarrassment, and social inclusion, which are not easily captured by administrative data or adult observations.

Building on our prior research documenting students' perspectives on USM in California, this qualitative study examines how middle and high school students in Texas and Arizona experienced the transition from universal free meals during the COVID-19 waiver period back to traditional means-tested systems in 2022-2023 SY. Texas and Arizona were selected as comparison states for a larger evaluation of California's USM program based on their demographic, socioeconomic, and political comparability and because both states returned to means-tested meal programs after federal waivers expired. We explore how students perceived the role of school meals in their daily lives, the effects of universal provisions on participation and social dynamics, and the material and psychological consequences of policy reversal. In doing so, we provide new evidence on the lived experience of one of the largest shifts in US child nutrition policy. These findings can inform current and future school meal policy decisions by demonstrating how universal approaches may better support equity, participation, and educational outcomes.

Data and methods

Study design, participants, and recruitment

We conducted a qualitative study in Texas and Arizona to examine students' perspectives on the transition from USM to a means-tested system after the expiration of the federal COVID-19 waivers in June 2022.

A professional research firm identified parents of middle and high school students via a proprietary panel and purposive sampling. Recruitment aimed for diversity in gender, race, ethnicity, grade level, language, geographic location, and eligibility for free or reduced-price meals (FRPM), with state-specific quotas. Parents were contacted by email or text message and invited to complete an initial survey that included sociodemographic characteristics and a brief screener to determine their child's eligibility.

Eligibility for the study required students attending a public or charter middle or high school in Arizona or Texas that were not participating in CEP at the time of the study, fluency in English or Spanish, and written parental consent and student assent. Each student received a $100 gift card for participation. The [blinded] Institutional Review Board reviewed the study and determined it to be exempt from full review.

Data collection

We conducted 36 in-depth interviews (Arizona, n = 15; Texas, n = 21) via Zoom between June and July 2023. The sample size of 36 interviews was determined a priori based on the study's scope and the expectation that this number would be sufficient to achieve thematic saturation, consistent with empirical guidance.24,25 We assessed analytic saturation retrospectively during the coding process. During analysis, no new themes emerged after coding approximately 25 transcripts, indicating that thematic saturation had been achieved. The interview guide explored students' experiences before, during, and after USM, perceptions of program accessibility, and the social and academic implications of policy changes. Our research team developed this interview guide based on a review of relevant literature, the primary research questions, questions adapted from instruments used in previous studies, and a focus group guide that our team used in previous years to collect similar qualitative data from students.26,27 The interview guide explored students' experiences before, during, and after USM, perceptions of program accessibility, and the social and academic implications of policy changes. The interviews were conducted by two adult female researchers from the professional research firm who are trained in qualitative interviewing.

All interviewers received comprehensive training in qualitative interviewing and met regularly with the research team to ensure consistency. The research team members reviewed the interviews to provide feedback and ensure quality standards were maintained. Interviews lasted approximately 45–60 minutes, were audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. The interview guide is available as Supplementary material.

Data analysis

We used an immersion–crystallization approach28 to thematic analysis,29 alternating between in-depth engagement with the transcripts and iterative reflection on emerging patterns.30 Two researchers developed an initial codebook using deductive codes derived from the research questions and inductive codes emerging from the data. They double-coded five transcripts in ATLAS.ti (version 23.2), resolving discrepancies through discussion until consensus was reached. Once coding was deemed consistent, one researcher (blinded) coded the remaining transcripts independently. While single coding of remaining transcripts is a limitation, initial inter-coder agreement was strong, and regular team debriefing sessions helped maintain coding consistency. Codes were synthesized into themes and subthemes through team deliberation until analytic saturation was achieved. To strengthen trustworthiness, we compared findings across states and maintained an audit trail documenting coding decisions and analytic memos throughout the process.

Researcher positionality

The research team includes scholars with expertise in nutrition policy, food security, and school health, several of whom have conducted prior research on USM programs. This positionality may have influenced the framing of interview questions and the interpretation of findings. The interviewers were external to the research team and did not have a predetermined view on USM policy. We acknowledge that our positionality may shape how we interpreted students' accounts, and readers should consider this context when evaluating our findings.

Results

Nearly half of the 36 participants identified as female (47.2%) (Table 1). The sample was racially and ethnically diverse, including 47.2% non-Hispanic White, 27.8% Hispanic, 22.2% non-Hispanic Black, and 2.8% non-Hispanic Asian students. Just over half (55.6%) were in middle school, and the remainder attended high school (44.4%). Regarding household income, 30.6% were eligible for FRPM, 33.3% were classified as near eligible (household income between 185% of the FPL and 300% of the FPL), and 36.1% were from higher-income households (household income >300% of the FPL).

Theme 1. Students report witnessing food insecurity and hunger after the end of USM

Many students understood school meals as an important safety net to reduce food insecurity, recognizing that many of their peers “don't have enough food at home” (AZ, P29). During USM, students observed how universal access addressed food security concerns: “everyone would be fed and everyone would have enough energy for the remaining classes” (AZ, P27).

Students perceived that the end of USM reintroduced food insecurity. Students described coping mechanisms such as sharing their meals with friends who could not pay: “A couple of my friends, they couldn't get lunch because they didn't have any money… I shared mine with them” (TX, P21). One student from Arizona shared: “I have a few friends that didn't have the money to pay for it every day, but wanted to eat at school. They would skip lunch or they would ask me to use my free lunch to get them something or ask around for money” (AZ, P35).

Theme 2. Students report hunger interfering with learning and concentration after USM ended

During USM implementation, students recognized how universal access promoted both nutritional health and academic success for all students. They understood that USM ensured consistent access to healthy meals: “It [USM] ensures that the people who eat lunch are getting a healthy meal” (TX, P5). Students connected this nutritional access directly to learning: “We’re at school for a long time and since we’re learning, we need energy in our bodies and food…lunch is halfway through the day, we need it to get through the other half of the day” (TX P2).

USM meant that all students, regardless of family income, could access the nutrition necessary for academic engagement. As one student explained: “it [USM] meant everyone would be fed and would have enough energy for the remaining classes” (AZ, P27).

Students perceived that the end of USM may have dismantled these conditions. One student captured the immediate academic consequence of the end of USM: “Now [that school meals are no longer free for all], some people just stay hungry for the rest of the day. They can't concentrate in class because they’re hungry” (TX, D5).

Students articulated how hunger now interfered with learning for those unable to afford meals: “I feel like it's hard for kids to stay focused if they’re hungry” (TX, P17). Students described food insecurity as displacing academic priorities: “Food's kind of important to stay focused, and if kids are more worried about being hungry, then they’re more worried about the being hungry part than to actually do their school” (AZ, P35).

Theme 3. Students report increased financial stress and anxiety after the end of USM

Students described experiencing psychological anxiety and financial stress about food access after USM ended. Students articulated the constant worry: “Kids are worrying if every day…if they have enough money to eat some lunch, and if they don't, they just don't eat at all” (TX, P22).

Students described emotional reactions to having insufficient funds to pay: “One time I didn't have money, and I went to buy lunch, and then they denied it…my cheeks got all red” (TX, P8). The financial stress extended to family relationships, with students recognizing how their meal needs created pressure on parents: “Some parents can't provide $3 every day because they don't get a paycheck every day” (AZ, P29).

Students contrasted this stress with the economic relief families experienced during USM. Students had consistently noted how USM “puts less stress on parents financially” (TX, P5) and provided relief during difficult times. Under USM, students experienced freedom from financial worry: “[With USM] you didn't have to pay, so it was just less stress, thinking about money” (AZ, P23).

Theme 4. Students report being turned away from meals and accumulating debt after USM ended

Students perceived that the end of USM reintroduced debt management systems, creating uncertainty about meal access. Students described being denied food due to lack of funds and witnessing others experience denial: “People in front of me didn't have enough money… they just told them, “You can't get food, you don't have enough,” and they sent them their way” (AZ, P37). One student shared: “For our school, after going over your balance…you don't get lunch until you pay it off… It was definitely sad because I couldn't get food even when I was hungry” (TX, D10).

Students described how meal debt accumulated quickly, creating significant financial burdens for families: “A lot of the students end up having hundreds of dollars of debt because they would go for breakfast and lunch daily” (AZ, P24).

Theme 5. Students report significantly reduced participation following the end of USM

Students reported that ending USM appeared to reduce students' meal participation due to reintroduced financial barriers: “Now there's less people having school lunch since they probably don't have the money” (TX, P20). Students connected participation drops directly to financial barriers: “More people brought lunches from home…families couldn't afford the lunch from school” (TX, D3).

This represented a reversal from the increased participation during USM implementation: “The school lunch line was going out of the cafeteria” (TX, P22).

Theme 6. Ending USM reintroduced stigma, embarrassment, and social hierarchies

During USM, students experienced reduced stigma associated with meal participation. With no cost attached, students did not have to reveal their economic status: “It made some people who apply for free and reduced less embarrassed because they didn't have to indicate that in any way. Nobody was paying, so they didn't have to worry” (TX, P14).

The destigmatization effect was particularly pronounced, with students noting: “[With USM], they felt more comfortable because it didn't show social class. Everybody got it for free, so it didn't really matter” (AZ, P35).

Students reported that ending USM reintroduced stigma and embarrassment among those who could not afford meals. Students witnessed scenes of public rejection that exposed peers' economic circumstances in front of classmates: “It's probably embarrassing for the student [to get turned away]. I don't think that's right” (TX, P19).

Students perceived that the end of USM reestablished social hierarchies based on economic status. Students described experiencing social judgment and explicit bullying: “I’ve heard people make mean jokes, say that they’re broke” (TX, P19). The social cruelty extended to direct attacks on family economic status: “their friends are discouraging them, saying mean stuff to them about eating school lunch like, “Your family is poor” (AZ, P29).

Students recognized that stigma created additional barriers for peers who might be eligible for FRPM but were reluctant to apply: “Some kids don't do the free and reduced form, even if they are on the lower class side of making money. Some of them… wouldn't go seek help because I think maybe they were a little embarrassed on asking for it” (AZ, P24).

Theme 7. Students express a desire to return to USM and perceive means-testing as unfair

Many students expressed strong gratitude for the time when lunches were universally free. One participant called the experience of meals being free for all as “E to the P-IC…It was awesome.” (TX, P8). Another said: “I think it was amazing, and I think they should do it every year no matter what” (TX, P6).

Given the negative consequences they experienced or observed, many students explicitly expressed a desire for the return of USM: “Make it free again” (TX, P8). One student explained: “It should be all the students get free meals, not just the ones that qualify for it.” (AZ, P37).

Students described the end of USM as unfair: “I think it's unfair that some people have to pay and some others don't. I think all places should have free lunches” (TX, P20).

Students also noted disparities in a system where access depended on income thresholds that did not always align with families' real financial circumstances. As one student put it, “A lot of kids that should have gotten free meals didn't qualify” (AZ, D35).

Discussion

This study provides one of the first qualitative examinations of how students in two states described their experiences transitioning from a USM policy to a means-tested framework. The findings suggest that, from students' perspectives, USM policies was perceived as supporting access to nutritious meals, food security, psychological wellbeing, and social inclusion. Students characterized USM as a stabilizing intervention in their daily lives, that met dietary needs, reduced stress, improved their ability to focus and learn, minimized stigma, and promoted a sense of belonging within the school. Following the return to means-testing in Texas and Arizona, students perceived that many of these benefits were diminished or lost.

Our findings align with previous research documenting positive student experiences with USM during the COVID-19 waiver period.10 Similar to a study with students in California, students in Texas and Arizona appreciated USM for increasing accessibility, reducing stigma, and promoting food security.10 The present study extends the literature by examining the de-implementation consequences when students returned to means-tested systems after waivers expired. Our research suggests immediate negative impacts when USM terminated: renewed food insecurity among peers, reintroduced stigma and embarrassment, increased anxiety about meal access, and the emergence of school meal debt.

These qualitative findings gain further significance in light of recent quantitative evidence. Ramponi et al. found that federal-level USM de-implementation was associated with significant declines in both breakfast and lunch participation rates, and that states adopting statewide USM policies mitigated these declines.19 A systematic review by Spill et al. similarly concluded that USM implementation was associated with increased meal participation, decreased obesity prevalence, and reduced suspensions, though the authors noted a critical absence of studies examining stigma.16 Our qualitative findings help address this evidence gap by documenting the lived experiences underlying these quantitative trends.

Students described school meals as an important resource for food security. They described how food insecurity and hunger reemerged immediately after USM ended, with classmates going without meals, relying on shared food, or being distracted by hunger, suggesting that means-tested approaches may be insufficient to address food insecurity for all students. Research demonstrates that food insecurity during childhood, even during short periods of time, is associated with increased rates of obesity, diabetes, and mental health problems, as well as reduced academic performance and long-term economic outcomes.31-35 Students' descriptions of classmates going hungry or skipping meals due to payment barriers are consistent with the mechanisms through which these adverse health and psychological outcomes develop.

Students articulated clear connections between meal access and learning ability. USM had created equal learning conditions, ensuring “everyone would be fed and everyone would have enough energy for the remaining classes.” Students perceived that the end of USM dismantled many of these conditions. Students reported that hunger interfered with concentration, and for some, the cognitive burden of food insecurity displaced academic priorities. Their understanding of the importance of food for learning and their observations of peers' academic difficulties when hungry demonstrate the important association between nutrition and education, which is supported by evidence.36,37 Quantitative research is broadly consistent with students' observations: universal free meal access has been associated with increased meal participation and reductions in disciplinary incidents, with some studies also reporting improvements in test scores, though evidence on academic performance and school attendance remains mixed and varies by grade level and context.6,15-17,38 Students' accounts of peers who could not focus because “they’re more worried about being hungry than to actually do their school,” illustrate how financial barriers to meal access may undermine conditions necessary for equitable educational engagement.

Students also recognized that the benefits of USM extended beyond nutrition. When school meals were free for all, they became normalized, free of the social hierarchies that accompany means-testing. The reinstatement of eligibility thresholds reintroduced visible divisions, excluding students whose families earned slightly above the cutoff yet still struggled financially, and discouraging participation even among those who could afford to pay but faced temporary barriers such as forgotten lunches or delayed deposits to meal accounts. This aligns with broader research on universal vs targeted social programs, which shows that universal approaches typically achieve greater coverage, reduce administrative burden, and avoid stigmatization.39,40 Research on school meals and stigma has shown that means-tested meal programs create visible markers of economic status that deter participation, and that universal approaches can eliminate this barrier by normalizing meal participation for all students.10,11,13,20,41 Notably, McLoughlin et al. found that even among schools offering USM, those with lower participation reported more stigma than those with higher participation,41 suggesting that stigma persists as a barrier even under universal provision. The students in our study described an intensified version of this dynamic; when USM were removed, stigma not only persisted but reemerged as a defining feature of the school meal experience.

Our findings also highlight important mental health dimensions that receive less attention in school meal policy discussions. Students described experiencing and witnessing significant psychological distress related to meal payment requirements, including anxiety at the possibility of being denied food, embarrassment, and social isolation. The end of USM introduced daily uncertainty for many students about whether they could afford lunch, sometimes deterring them from entering the cafeteria line altogether. Daily uncertainty about meal affordability and account balances represents chronic stress that can adversely affect both mental health and academic performance.42 Students contrasted this experience with the relief families experienced during USM, when students could eat “without worrying if they had enough money.”

The reemergence of meal denial and school meal debt is a notable finding. Students described going hungry themselves or watching peers go hungry and/or experiencing embarrassment when accounts were insufficient, experiences they described as both distressing and disruptive to their learning. Meal debt often accumulated quickly, creating further hardship for families and placing financial strain on schools. National data confirm that meal debt is an escalating problem, affecting over 69% of school districts nationwide, totaling more than $176 million annually.43 The problem has been intensifying in recent years: median district debt reached $6900 by November 2024, a 103% increase since the 2017–2018 SY.44

Previous research has identified stigma associated with means-tested programs as both a participation barrier and a negative outcome.10,20,45 Students in our study described how USM eliminated visible markers of economic status, making it easier to participate without fear of judgment. Their observations that USM made students “feel in place” and reduced social distinctions align with prior evidence suggesting that universal approaches can mitigate stigma and promote more inclusive school environments.10,11,13,20 Following the end of USM, students reported renewed economic divisions, including bullying, social cruelty, and shame that deterred participation even among eligible students. These accounts are consistent with broader research on how stigma functions as a social determinant of health, influencing both program utilization and psychological wellbeing.45,46 Students reports of being “broke” or told “your family is poor” for eating school meals reflect forms of social exclusion that may further reduce participation among students who need these programs the most.

Students' desire for USM's return reveals how they understood access to school meals as a matter of equity. Many viewed it as unfair to single out certain students for payment while others ate for free, especially when income thresholds excluded families still struggling financially. Their perspectives align with broader research showing that universal programs can avoid the inequities and inefficiencies inherent in targeted approaches.

The de-implementation of USM occurred in the broader context of rolling back many pandemic-era social supports, including expanded unemployment benefits, the enhanced Child Tax Credit, and emergency Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) allotments. Research on the expiration of these COVID-era policies has documented increases in food insecurity and financial stress among families with children.47-49 The simultaneous withdrawal of multiple safety-net supports may have compounded the effects students described, as families faced converging economic pressures at a time when pandemic-related costs and disruptions persisted.

These qualitative findings, considered alongside the quantitative evidence, point to several policy considerations. First, the negative consequences of de-implementation reported by students suggest that the sustainability of USM warrants further evaluation and investment. Second, implementation challenges documented in prior research, including increased food service workload and food waste would need to be addressed through adequate funding for food service infrastructure, staffing, and capacity-building. Third, for states and districts that cannot immediately adopt USM, incremental approaches such as expanding CEP eligibility, eliminating reduced-price co-pays, or investing in alternative breakfast delivery models may help reduce stigma and increase participation.

Limitations

This study's focus on middle and high school students in Texas and Arizona may limit generalizability to other age groups and geographic contexts. Importantly, Arizona implemented a reduced-price meal co-pay elimination policy in January 2023, six months before our interviews, providing free meals to students qualifying for reduced-price meals, though this represents a relatively small proportion of the student population. This may have tempered reports of financial stress and meal debt for that subgroup. Students' retrospective accounts of USM may be subject to recall bias, although the significance and recency of the policy change likely enhanced recall accuracy. This study did not use a specific theoretical framework to guide data collection and analysis. Future similar studies would benefit from employing policy implementation science frameworks to strengthen study design and provide additional analytic structure. Additionally, the absence of a formal framework and the reliance on single coding of the majority of transcripts after initial inter-coder agreement are methodological limitations. Although we used multiple strategies to enhance trustworthiness, including team deliberation and an audit trail, we did not compute formal inter-coder reliability statistics. We also did not present results stratified by meal eligibility status, which represents a missed opportunity that future research should address.

Conclusion

Students' perspectives revealed the perceived impacts of the transition from universal to means-tested school meals: the reemergence of food insecurity and hunger among peers, increased financial stress and anxiety about meal access, the return of stigma and social hierarchies in cafeterias, reduced meal participation, and impaired concentration and learning due to hunger. Students consistently identified USM as providing benefits beyond nutrition alone, including reduced psychological stress, elimination of visible economic divisions, and normalized participation in school meal programs. These perspectives offer qualitative evidence that complements quantitative findings on the effects of USM on participation, food security and academic outcomes. Considered alongside quantitative evidence from multiple states, these findings suggest that universal approaches may represent a more equitable model for school nutrition policy. Future policy decisions may benefit from weighing the reported costs of means-testing, including stigma, reduced participation, meal debt, and educational disruption, against the investment required to sustain universal provision.
