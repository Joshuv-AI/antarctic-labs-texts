Combined effects of depression and visual impairment on cardiovascular disease incidence in a Chinese population-based cohort
Authors: Yuzhe Zhou, Shuyang Guo, Zhuoning Wu, Shunming Liu, Di Lu, Tongtong Niu, Lei Liu, Renzhe Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression and visual impairment (VI) are prevalent in aging populations and known risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD). Their combined impact on CVD incidence remains poorly defined. This study investigated the individual and combined effects of depression and VI on incident CVD in a large Chinese cohort. We included 18,633 participants aged ≥ 45 from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2018). Participants were categorized into four groups: control, depression only, VI only, and both. Incident CVD was identified during follow-up. Cox regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). In the fully adjusted model, all three exposure groups had significantly higher CVD risk than the control group. The highest risk was for depression only (HR: 1.60; 95% CI: 1.36–1.89), followed by co-occurring depression and visual impairment (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.40–1.73), and visual impairment only (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.13–1.38). A significant negative multiplicative interaction was observed (HR for interaction: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.65–0.93; p = 0.006). Depression and VI are independently associated with increased CVD risk. Their co-occurrence shows an antagonistic interaction, with combined risk not exceeding that of depression alone. Screening for depressive symptoms in older adults, especially those with VI, may aid CVD prevention.Keywords: Depression, Visual impairment, Cardiovascular disease, Cohort, Population-based studySubject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, placing an immense burden on public health systems1. While traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia are well-established, there is growing recognition of the role of psychosocial and functional factors, particularly in aging populations2.

Depression is a highly prevalent mental health condition and has been consistently associated with incident CVD3. The pathophysiological mechanisms are multifaceted, involving hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, increased systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation4,5. Behaviorally, depression is often associated with poor health behaviors, such as smoking, physical inactivity, and non-adherence to medication, which further elevate cardiovascular risk6.

Visual impairment (VI) is another common condition in older adults, affecting millions worldwide. While often viewed as an outcome of cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., diabetic retinopathy, retinal vascular occlusion), VI is increasingly recognized as a marker associated with CVD7–9. VI can lead to a reduction in physical activity, increased social isolation, and difficulties in managing chronic conditions (e.g., inability to read medication labels or monitor blood glucose)10.

A significant co-morbidity exists between depression and VI. Vision loss is a major life stressor that can precipitate depressive episodes, while individuals with depression may neglect eye care11,12. Given that both conditions share common behavioral and inflammatory pathways linked to CVD, it is plausible that their co-existence may influence CVD risk. However, the nature of this joint association—whether additive, synergistic, or driven primarily by one condition—remains unclear and is a focus of this analysis.

Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the individual and combined effects of baseline depressive symptoms and visual impairment on the risk of incident CVD using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).

Methods

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a national, population-based longitudinal study conducted among residents aged 45 years and older. The sampling and study design of CHARLS have been described in detail elsewhere13. This study was a secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available CHARLS data.

VI was assessed using self-reported questions. Participants were asked about their ability to see things at a distance and their ability to see things up close (e.g., reading) (with glasses, if applicable). Response options were ‘excellent’, ‘very good’, ‘good’, ‘fair’, and ‘poor’. Participants who answered ‘fair or poor’ to either question were classified into the VI group, consistent with methodologies from other CHARLS-based studies.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 10). This scale is well-validated for use in the Chinese population. The total scores ranged from 0 to 30, and the participants were classified into the Depression(+) group when the total score was ≥ 10 consistent with previous CHARLS-based studies14–17. For brevity, we refer to this condition as depression throughout the manuscript.

This study employed a dynamic cohort design. From the CHARLS waves 2011–2020, we identified the first survey wave for each participant that met the following criteria as their individual baseline: aged ≥ 45 years, free of cardiovascular disease (CVD), with complete data on depressive symptoms and visual impairment, and surviving at least two years beyond that survey point. Participants who were lost to follow-up after their individual baseline were excluded.The primary outcome was incident CVD, defined as a new-onset, doctor-diagnosed case of heart disease (e.g., myocardial infarction, angina) or stroke reported in any subsequent survey wave (up to 2020).

Covariates were obtained from each participant’s individual baseline survey and included age, sex, education level (below primary, primary, middle school, high school +), marital status (married/partnered vs. unmarried/no partner), residence (urban vs. rural), BMI, smoking, drinking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CVD medication situation, Hypertension medication situation, Diabetes medication situation, dyslipidemia medication situation.

All participants were classified into four groups: Depression(–) VI(–) (Control), Depression(+) VI(–), Depression(–) VI(+), and Depression(+) VI(+). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed (skewed) variables were expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. For categorical variables, the chi-square (χ2) test was used, or Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were less than 5. For continuous variables, the independent samples t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) was used to compare two groups. If comparing more than two groups, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used, respectively. Cox proportional-hazards regression analyses were used to determine the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for incident CVD. The Depression(–) VI(–) group served as the reference. To formally assess whether the association of co-existing depression and VI with CVD risk differed from the sum of their individual associations, we included a multiplicative interaction term (depression × VI) in the Cox model. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and upheld using Schoenfeld residuals.Model 1 was adjusted for age. Model 2 was adjusted for all sociodemographic factors. Model 3 was the fully adjusted model. Stratified analyses were conducted by age (45–59 years and 60 + years) and sex. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses. First, we performed a complete-case analysis restricted to participants with no missing covariates to assess the influence of multiple imputation. Second, to evaluate the potential impact of exposure misclassification, we repeated the main analysis using a fixed-baseline design, with the 2011 wave as the single baseline for all participants. Third, given that death is a competing risk in older populations, we applied the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to account for non-cardiovascular mortality as a competing event.

Results

A total of 18,633 participants free of CVD at baseline were enrolled (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the study cohort, stratified by depression and VI status, are detailed in Table 1. Across the four groups, all baseline characteristics differed significantly (all P < 0.05). Participants with both depression and VI (Depression(+) VI(+)), who comprised 28.23% of the sample, were on average the oldest (58.44 ± 9.69 years), had the highest proportion of females (61.24%) and rural residents (69.35%), and the lowest educational attainment (56.86% below primary). This group also demonstrated the highest baseline prevalence of hypertension (24.18%), diabetes (6.63%), and use of cardiovascular medications (2.85%).

The results of the Cox proportional-hazards regression for incident CVD are presented in Table 2. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), after controlling for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle variables, comorbidities, and medication status, all three exposure groups showed a significantly increased risk of incident CVD compared to the Depression(–) VI(–) control group. The risk was highest for participants with depression only (HR: 1.60; 95% CI: 1.36–1.89, P < 0.001). Significant risks were also observed for those with both depression and visual impairment(HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.40–1.73, P < 0.001) and those with VI only (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.13–1.38, P < 0.001). The proportional hazards assumption was tested and found to be satisfied for all models (all P > 0.05).

Formal test for interaction. We further tested for a multiplicative interaction between depressive symptoms and visual impairment on CVD risk. The interaction term was statistically significant across all models, with a hazard ratio of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.65–0.93, P = 0.005) in the fully adjusted model. This negative interaction on the multiplicative scale, together with the observation that the joint effect (HR = 1.56) was not substantially larger than the effect of depression alone (HR = 1.60), suggests depressive symptoms may be the predominant driver of CVD risk in this comorbid relationship, with visual impairment conferring limited additional risk beyond that already associated with depression.

Kaplan-Meier analyses (Fig. 2) showed that the cumulative incidence of CVD differed significantly across the four groups (log-rank P < 0.001). Over the 10-year follow-up, participants with both depression and visual impairment (VI) exhibited the highest cumulative incidence. Notably, the curve for the depression-only group (Depression[+]VI[-]) closely paralleled that of the combined group, reflecting that depression is a predominant risk factor for CVD. This pattern was further elucidated by Cox regression, which revealed a significant negative multiplicative interaction between depression and VI (HR for product term: 0.78; P = 0.006). This finding indicates that while the coexistence of depression and VI identifies a population at the greatest absolute risk for CVD, the additional presence of VI does not amplify the risk beyond that conferred by depression alone, resulting in a combined effect that is less than multiplicative.

We further explored these associations through stratified analyses by age and sex (Tables 3 and 4). In the age‑stratified analysis (Table 3), all three exposure groups showed significantly elevated risks of incident CVD in both the 45–59 and ≥ 60 age groups. In the older cohort (≥ 60 years), the hazard ratio for the “Depression only” group was 1.50 (95% CI: 1.18–1.91, P < 0.001), which was slightly lower than that observed in the younger group (HR: 1.66, 95% CI: 1.33–2.08, P < 0.001). The interaction between depression and VI remained significant in the younger group (HR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.57–0.94, P = 0.014) but not in the older group (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.65–1.08, P = 0.178). In the sex‑stratified analysis (Table 4), all exposure groups demonstrated significantly increased risks in females. Among males, significant risks were also observed for the “Depression only” (HR: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.32–2.13, P < 0.001) and “Both” (HR: 1.56, 95% CI: 1.34–1.82, P < 0.001) groups. The risk for the “VI only” group in males was attenuated but remained statistically significant (HR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.33, P = 0.036). The depression‑VI interaction was significant only in females (HR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.59–0.96, P = 0.021).

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, after excluding participants with missing covariate data (Table 5), the associations remained consistent with the primary analysis: the fully adjusted HRs were 1.52 (95% CI: 1.25–1.87, P < 0.001) for depression only, 1.25 (95% CI: 1.11–1.41, P < 0.001) for VI only, and 1.51 (95% CI: 1.33–1.72, P < 0.001) for both conditions. The interaction term was statistically significant across all models, with a hazard ratio of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.64–0.99, P = 0.039) in the fully adjusted model. Second, we re-analysed the data using 2011 as the baseline wave, with multiple imputation for missing covariates (Table 6). In these analyses, the direction and magnitude of the associations remained similar to the primary results, although the interaction term was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.68–1.02, P = 0.080). Finally, we applied Fine-Gray competing risk models to account for non‑CVD mortality as a competing event (Table 7). The subdistribution hazard ratios were nearly identical to those from the primary Cox models, indicating that competing risks did not materially bias the estimates.

Discussion

In this large, longitudinal, population-based study of Chinese adults, we demonstrated that both depressive symptoms and visual impairment are independently associated with an increased risk of incident cardiovascular disease. After adjusting for a comprehensive set of confounders, the co-existence of these two conditions was associated with a 56% increased risk (HR 1.56, 95% CI: 1.40–1.73). A formal test for multiplicative interaction yielded a significant antagonistic interaction term (HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.65–0.93, P = 0.006). This, coupled with the observation that the joint effect (HR 1.56) was not substantially larger than the effect of depressive symptoms alone (HR 1.60), indicates that the combined association is less than expected under a multiplicative model and suggests that depressive symptoms may be the predominant driver in this comorbid relationship.

Several explanations may account for this antagonistic interaction. First, visual impairment itself represents a high-risk condition, and the additional cardiovascular contribution of depressive symptoms may be constrained by a ceiling effect. Second, individuals with both conditions may have more frequent healthcare contact and greater family or social support, potentially mitigating excess CVD risk. Third, overlapping biological pathways—such as systemic inflammation, HPA axis dysregulation, and reduced functional reserve—may lead to partial saturation of shared risk mechanisms. Fourth, individuals with both conditions may adopt more health-protective behaviors due to heightened perceived vulnerability. Finally, differences in measurement, residual confounding, or selective survival cannot be excluded. These findings highlight the complexity of multimorbidity interactions and underscore the importance of examining both additive and multiplicative scales when evaluating joint effects. From a clinical perspective, while the co-occurrence of depressive symptoms and visual impairment confers substantially elevated CVD risk, the presence of depressive symptoms—whether alone or in combination—may represent a particularly critical target for screening and intervention.

The combined association of depressive symptoms and visual impairment (HR 1.56) nonetheless represents a clinically important comorbidity pattern. While not synergistic in a statistical sense, these two conditions may still create a “vicious cycle” detrimental to cardiovascular health. Visual impairment can lead to functional dependence and social isolation, which are strong triggers for depressive symptoms. In turn, depressive symptoms reduce motivation for self-care, compounding the functional limitations imposed by visual impairment. This combination may lead to profound neglect of health behaviors including diet, exercise, smoking cessation, and medical management, thereby accelerating the onset of CVD. Our finding that the added presence of visual impairment did not increase risk beyond depressive symptoms alone implies that the pathways through which depressive symptoms elevate CVD risk are already fully engaged in individuals with elevated depressive symptoms, and the additional burden of visual impairment operates through similar or overlapping mechanisms rather than entirely distinct ones. These findings support the potential value of integrated mental health and vision screening in primary care settings, particularly for older adults with either condition.

Our finding that depressive symptoms are independently associated with incident CVD (HR 1.60, 95% CI: 1.36–1.89) aligns with a substantial body of evidence (Barger and Struve, 2022; Gan et al., 2014). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the CES-D 10 scale, a well-validated screening instrument; a cutoff score of ≥ 10 was applied to define clinically relevant depressive symptoms, consistent with previous CHARLS-based studies (Andresen et al., 1994; Lei et al., 2014). This relationship is supported by Mendelian randomization studies suggesting a causal link to coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, and small-vessel stroke (de Geus, 2021; Lu et al., 2021). The mechanisms are complex, involving behavioral factors such as poor medication adherence and physical inactivity, as well as biological pathways including HPA axis dysregulation, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction (Nemeroff and Goldschmidt-Clermont, 2012). A large-scale meta-analysis further demonstrated that severe mental disorders, including major depressive disorder, significantly increase CVD risk and mortality (Correll et al., 2017). Despite this strong evidence, the practical integration of mental health into cardiology remains limited. A recent evaluation of 65 CVD guidelines found that while 71% acknowledged depression as a risk factor, only 23% provided recommendations for both screening and treatment (Blatch Armon et al., 2025). This highlights a critical gap that our study reinforces.

Notably, our study confirmed that visual impairment is also independently associated with incident CVD (HR 1.25, 95% CI: 1.13–1.38). This underscores the strong connection between cardiovascular and eye health, as they share common risk factors including aging, smoking, hyperglycemia, and hypertension (De La Cruz et al., 2021; Du et al., 2025). Indeed, studies have shown that better cardiovascular health is associated with lower odds of ocular diseases such as age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy (Crews et al., 2017; Flaxman et al., 2021). Although the individual associations of depressive symptoms and visual impairment with CVD have been examined previously, evidence on their combined association and potential interaction remains limited. A better understanding of this relationship may inform more integrated prevention strategies for CVD in aging populations.

The stratified analyses provided additional insights, though these findings warrant cautious interpretation. The attenuation of the depressive symptoms–visual impairment interaction in older adults (≥ 60 years: HR 0.84, P = 0.178) compared to middle-aged adults (45–59 years: HR 0.73, P = 0.014) suggests that the antagonistic effect may be more pronounced in younger cohorts. This pattern may reflect selective survival, whereby older adults with both conditions who remain in the cohort represent a more resilient subgroup. Alternatively, the relative contribution of visual impairment to CVD risk may increase with age, diminishing the proportional excess risk attributable to depressive symptoms. Regarding the sex-stratified findings, the association between visual impairment alone and incident CVD was attenuated in men (HR 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.33, P = 0.036) but robust in women (HR 1.35, 95% CI: 1.17–1.56, P < 0.001), and the antagonistic interaction was significant only in women (HR 0.75, 95% CI: 0.59–0.96, P = 0.021). These observed sex differences are consistent with prior literature documenting higher CVD risk associated with sensory impairment in women (Liljas et al., 2016; Crews et al., 2017). However, given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

The current study has several notable strengths. It leverages a large, nationally representative longitudinal cohort in China, providing valuable insights into the combined association of depressive symptoms and visual impairment with incident CVD in a middle-aged and older population. We employed a dynamic cohort design in which exposure status was updated across multiple survey waves, mitigating potential bias from exposure misclassification that relies on a single baseline assessment. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and satisfied for all models (all P > 0.05). Sensitivity analyses using complete case analysis and competing risk models yielded consistent results, further supporting the robustness of our primary conclusions.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, both key exposures were assessed using self-reported measures. Visual impairment was defined by subjective functional rating rather than objective visual acuity testing, and depressive symptoms were assessed using the CES-D 10 screening scale rather than a structured clinical interview. Second, exposure assessment occurred at discrete survey intervals (every 2–3 years); changes in status between surveys could not be captured, which may lead to non-differential misclassification and bias our hazard ratios toward the null. Third, incident CVD was based on self-reported physician diagnoses without verification by medical records, introducing the possibility of recall bias and outcome under-ascertainment. Fourth, although we applied Fine–Gray competing risk models and found results nearly identical to the primary Cox models, competing events remain a consideration in aging populations. Finally, despite adjusting for a comprehensive set of covariates, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors such as physical activity, socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, and medication adherence cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In this large, population-based cohort study, both depressive symptoms and visual impairment were independently associated with increased risk of incident CVD. Their co-occurrence conferred substantially elevated risk (HR 1.56), though an antagonistic interaction (HR 0.78) indicated a less-than-multiplicative joint effect, with depressive symptoms appearing as the predominant driver. These findings support integrated screening for both conditions in primary care and suggest that managing depressive symptoms—particularly in older adults with co-existing visual impairment—may represent an actionable target for CVD prevention. Future studies using objective visual acuity measures, clinical depression diagnoses, and adjudicated CVD outcomes are warranted.
