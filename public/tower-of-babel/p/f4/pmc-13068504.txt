Pain‐Related Smoking Expectancies Across Tobacco Product Use Patterns, Including Heated Tobacco Products: Psychometric Validation of the Japanese Pain and Smoking Inventory
Authors: Keiko Yamada, Takashi Kimura, Naoki Mizunuma, Takahiro Tabuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimWith the rapid proliferation of heated tobacco products (HTPs) in Japan, understanding pain‐related smoking expectancies across tobacco use patterns has become increasingly important. In this study, we aimed to profile these expectancies among adults with pain using the Pain and Smoking Inventory (PSI) and to evaluate its psychometric properties in a Japanese context.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional internet‐based survey in 2024 among 300 Japanese adults with pain who smoked daily. Participants were categorized as combustible‐only, HTP‐only, switchers (from cigarettes to HTPs), or dual users. Psychometric evaluation included assessment of factor structure, internal consistency, and convergent validity. We used a structural equation model to examine associations between the latent PSI score and pain‐related outcomes. We then conducted analyses of covariance to compare PSI scores across tobacco use groups (adjusted for age and gender).ResultsPsychometric validation indicated high internal consistency (α = 0.93), mixed but essentially unidimensional fit, and expected correlations with nicotine dependence, pain, and psychological measures. The structural equation model demonstrated significant positive associations between the latent PSI factor and pain intensity (β = 0.29, p < 0.001) as well as pain‐related disability (β = 0.39, p < 0.001). Exploratory group comparisons revealed higher PSI scores among dual users than switchers, reflecting stronger perceived pain–smoking relationships. HTP‐only users also showed descriptively elevated profiles similar to combustible and dual users.ConclusionsPain‐related smoking expectancies differed across tobacco product use patterns, with dual users showing higher‐risk expectancy profiles. The PSI may serve as a useful tool for screening and tailored intervention in pain and tobacco use contexts.Keywords: pain, psychometrics, smoking cessation, tobacco products, tobacco use

Introduction

The landscape of tobacco use is shifting rapidly with the proliferation of novel products, including heated tobacco products (HTPs). Japan represents the largest HTP market globally, underscoring the need to characterize how product use patterns relate to pain‐related smoking expectancies in real‐world contexts [1, 2]. Although HTPs have been presented as alternatives to cigarettes, independent evidence on the related health risks is mixed and incomplete. Persistent toxicant exposure as well as adverse effects have been reported, with their psychological implications—particularly among people living with pain—remaining insufficiently understood [3, 4, 5].

A substantial body of evidence indicates a robust, bidirectional interplay between pain and smoking, with nicotine able to exert acute analgesic effects via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. However, chronic smoking is associated with the development and exacerbation of chronic pain, creating a paradox that may shape smokers' beliefs and motivations [5, 6, 7, 8]. Within this context, pain‐related smoking expectancies—i.e., beliefs about the efficacy of smoking for pain relief and the potential for harm—are a critical psychological construct. Prior work has linked these expectancies to pain and tobacco use parameters, using the Pain and Smoking Inventory (PSI) to assess perceived pain–smoking interrelationships [9, 10]. In Japan, preliminary evidence suggests that a subset of adults with chronic pain reports using tobacco specifically for pain relief, highlighting the need for context‐specific measurement and application [11].

Given the evolving product landscape, it is unknown whether pain‐related smoking expectancies differ among tobacco product use patterns (combustible‐only, HTP‐only, switching from cigarettes to HTPs, dual use). Dual users, in particular, may hold more conflicted or amplified expectancies than exclusive users, with implications for dependence and cessation [4, 12, 13].

In this study, we primarily profiled pain‐related smoking expectancies across tobacco product patterns among Japanese adults with pain in an exploratory cross‐sectional analysis using the PSI [9]. Because the PSI has not been previously validated in Japanese, embedded psychometric assessments (factor structure, internal consistency, convergent validity) representing the first psychometric evaluation of the Japanese PSI are reported for the Japanese version used herein. We hypothesized that profiles would differ according to product use pattern, with dual users showing higher‐risk expectancies relative to switchers and some exclusive‐user groups [4, 12, 13].

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study followed a cross‐sectional design, with data collected between November 15 and 18, 2024, through an internet‐based survey conducted by Rakuten Insight Inc., a Japanese internet research firm. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age 20–69 years, (2) smoking five or more combustible or HTPs per day over the past 30 days, and (3) experiencing physical pain over the past 30 days. The pain criterion was intentionally broad, without restrictions on pain duration, etiology, or diagnosis. This inclusive approach was chosen to maximize the ecological validity of the PSI in a general population of smokers experiencing pain, consistent with the broad applicability intended by the original developers of the PSI. Accordingly, the sample included individuals with both acute and chronic pain conditions; 147 participants (49.0%) met the criterion for chronic pain (lasting ≥ 3 months), while the remainder reported pain of shorter duration (Table 1). The threshold of ≥ 5 cigarettes/day was selected to mirror the PSI pilot's sampling frame and to exclude low‐level smokers with unstable patterns.

To recruit participants, an initial screening survey was administered to a maximum of 10 000 registered panelists; the exact number of distributed e‐mail invitations was determined using a computer algorithm managed by the agency (screened). Respondents were assessed for eligibility based on the responses to 30‐day recall questions regarding their pain and smoking habits. Individuals who met the criteria were deemed eligible and subsequently invited to participate in the main survey via a second e‐mail. Recruitment remained open until exactly 300 participants had completed the main survey, at which point the system automatically closed the first phase of data collection; responses from all 300 complete responses were analyzed.

For test–retest reliability assessment, an e‐mail invitation for a second survey was sent to all 300 initial participants 2 weeks later (between November 29 and December 2, 2024); recruitment was closed as soon as 100 participants successfully completed the retest. All participants provided digital informed consent before beginning the survey.

Measures

Pain and Smoking Inventory (PSI)

The primary measure was the Japanese version of the PSI, a 9‐item instrument used to assess perceived interrelationships between pain and cigarette smoking. Each item is rated on a 7‐point Likert scale (0–6), with higher scores indicating stronger pain–smoking expectancies [9]. To our knowledge, no validated Japanese version of the PSI existed prior to this study; accordingly, the present study represents the first psychometric evaluation of the Japanese PSI. After obtaining permission from the original PSI developer [9], the Japanese version was produced following established guidelines for translation and cultural adaptation of patient‐reported outcome measures, including dual forward translations, reconciliation, independent back translation, developer review, and item‐level clarification until conceptual equivalence was confirmed [14, 15]. Large language models (ChatGPT‐4o, DeepL) were consulted as auxiliary lexicons under human oversight; no generated text was adopted without independent human review and developer approval. Scoring was carried out by summing the nine items to arrive at the total score, with higher scores reflecting stronger perceived pain–smoking interrelationships.

Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND)

The FTND is a six‐item standard instrument for assessing the intensity of physical addiction to nicotine. Total scores range from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater nicotine dependence [16, 17].

Tobacco Dependence Screener (TDS)

The TDS is a 10‐item “yes/no” questionnaire designed to screen for tobacco dependence according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Third Edition, Revised, and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition criteria. A total score of 5 or higher indicates dependence [18].

Pain Intensity

A numerical rating scale (NRS) was used to assess average pain intensity over the previous week, with scores ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain) [19].

Pain Disability

The Pain Disability Index (PDI) was administered using the validated 5‐item Japanese short form, which assesses pain‐related interference across five domains: family/home responsibilities, recreation, social activity, occupation, and self‐care (total score range 0–50; higher scores indicate greater disability) [20, 21].

Anxiety

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐2 (GAD‐2) scale was used to screen for anxiety [22, 23]. Scores range from 0 to 6, with a score of 3 or greater considered a cutoff for identifying generalized anxiety disorder [23].

Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire‐2 (PHQ‐2) was used to screen for depression. Scores range from 0 to 6, with a score of 3 or greater suggesting the presence of major depressive disorder [22, 24].

Pain Self‐Efficacy

The short version of the Pain Self‐Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ‐4) was used to assess participants' confidence in performing various activities despite their pain [25, 26]. This scale comprises four items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 24 [25, 26]. Higher scores reflect stronger self‐efficacy [26].

Demographic and Smoking Characteristics

Participants provided demographic information, including age, gender, and educational attainment. Pain duration was also assessed and categorized into five groups: < 1 month, ≥ 1–< 3 months, ≥ 3–< 6 months, ≥ 6–< 12 months, and ≥ 12 months. Smoking variables included average daily consumption and primary product type. Participants were categorized into four primary groups: (1) exclusive combustible cigarette smokers, (2) exclusive HTP users, (3) switchers (former cigarette smokers who switched to HTPs), and (4) dual users (both combustible cigarettes and HTPs).

Exploratory Single‐Item Questions

For exploratory purposes, we included two single‐item questions identical to a previous report by Yamada et al. [11]. Participants were asked to respond “yes” or “no” to the following statements, presented in Japanese: Smoking relieves my pain “(タバコを吸うと痛みがやわらぐ; reported as “Felt pain relief during smoking” in our tables) and “I smoke tobacco for the purpose of suppressing pain” (痛みをおさえる目的でタバコを吸う; reported as “Using tobacco for pain relief”).”

Statistical Analysis

No formal a priori power analysis was performed. However, the sample size of 300 exceeded the commonly cited rule‐of‐thumb minimum for factor analytic methods, including at least 10 participants per item for a 9‐item scale, and it fell within the “good” range for factor analysis sample size [27, 28]. For group comparisons, the unequal and small subgroup sizes—particularly the HTP‐only group (n = 11)—limited statistical power to detect between‐group differences for some outcomes. Post hoc sensitivity analyses indicated that with n = 300, the study had approximately 80% power to detect medium effect sizes (f = 0.25) in one‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) at α = 0.05 across four groups, but was underpowered for small effects.

First, descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants' characteristics. Psychometric evaluation of the Japanese PSI included the following: (a) assessment of dimensionality using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which suggested a two‐factor solution with insufficient subscale reliability; confirmatory factor analysis using a bifactor model subsequently supported a dominant general factor structure, (b) internal consistency (using Cronbach's alpha for the total scale, and McDonald's omega hierarchical [ωH] and omega hierarchical subscale [ωHS] for the bifactor general and group factors, respectively), and (c) convergent validity with the FTND, TDS, NRS, PDI, PSEQ‐4, PHQ‐2, and GAD‐2.

EFA suggested two groupings with insufficient subscale reliability (ω < 0.70), and thus a bifactor confirmatory factor analysis was estimated; fit indices (χ2, comparative fit index, Tucker–Lewis index, root mean square error of approximation) and ωH supported use of the PSI total score as the primary measure [29, 30]. Test–retest reliability of the PSI total score was estimated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two‐way mixed, single‐measure, absolute agreement) for the PSI total score between the two time points [31]. Item‐level characteristics were examined using multidimensional item response theory models to inform item discrimination and information functions.

To test the study aims, an exploratory structural equation model (SEM) was used to estimate associations of the latent PSI general factor (G) with pain intensity (NRS) and pain‐related disability (PDI), allowing for a correlation between the residual errors of NRS and PDI, with covariate adjustment (age, gender). Group differences were examined using ANCOVA comparing the four product‐type groups on the PSI total and bifactor‐derived scores (G, Factor A [Smoking Cessation Barriers & Coping], Factor B [Pain‐Triggered Craving & Analgesic Expectancies]), with age and gender as covariates. Because the latent general factor (G) was estimated with negative polarity (a mathematically arbitrary default in latent variable estimation), the extracted G factor scores were multiplied by −1 (reverse‐scored) in subsequent analyses. This transformation ensured that higher G scores consistently reflected stronger pain‐smoking expectancies, aligning with the scoring direction of the raw PSI total score. For the binary exploratory outcomes, we used a modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for age and gender. The switcher group was the reference category to benchmark against a potentially lower‐risk profile. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals) were inspected; robust standard errors were applied when indicated. For multiple group contrasts against the prespecified reference group (switchers), post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunnett's adjustment. Adjusted p‐values with 95% CIs are reported.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and Mplus version 8.11 (Muthén & Muthén, Los Angeles, CA, USA). A p < 0.05 (two‐tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The final sample included 300 daily smokers with pain. The mean age was 53.2 (SD = 9.0) years, and most participants were men (n = 241, 80.3%). Participants were categorized into four groups based on their primary tobacco product use: combustible‐only smokers (n = 152, 50.7%), HTP‐only users (n = 11, 3.7%), switchers (n = 93, 31.0%), and dual users (n = 44, 14.7%). Nearly half of the sample (n = 147, 49.0%) reported pain lasting 3 months or longer, meeting the criteria for chronic pain. The mean PSI total score for the entire sample was 11.1 (standard deviation = 11.3). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Table S1.

Psychometric Properties of the Japanese PSI

Structural Validity and Reliability

The Japanese version of the PSI demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.93 for the total scale. An initial EFA suggested a two‐factor structure (see Table S2). However, because the ωHS reliability of these potential subscales was insufficient (0.28 and 0.60, respectively), a bifactor model was tested. The bifactor model (Table 2) showed a mixed fit to the data; the comparative fit index (0.980) and Tucker–Lewis index (0.955) indicated excellent fit, and the root mean square error of approximation was relatively elevated (0.092) (χ2 = 56.76, p < 0.001). The G factor accounted for a large portion of the explained common variance (86.6%) and showed excellent reliability (ωH = 0.90), supporting use of the total score as a primary measure of a unidimensional construct. Test–retest reliability for the total score over a 2‐week interval was moderate (ICC = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.36–0.65, p < 0.001).

Item Characteristics

Multidimensional item response theory analyses indicated that all nine PSI items exhibited good discrimination properties and provided substantial information across the latent trait continuum (see Table S3). Item information functions confirmed that each item contributed meaningfully to the overall measurement of the general pain‐related smoking expectancy factor.

Concurrent Validity

As shown in Table 3, the G score of the PSI demonstrated good convergent validity, being significantly and positively correlated with measures of nicotine dependence (FTND: r = 0.16; TDS: r = 0.34), pain intensity (NRS: r = 0.25), pain‐related disability (PDI: r = 0.37), anxiety (GAD‐2: r = 0.32), and depression (PHQ‐2: r = 0.18). The G factor was also significantly and negatively correlated with pain self‐efficacy (PSEQ‐4: r = −0.18).

Primary Analyses

Structural Equation Model (SEM)

The SEM results are depicted in Figure 1. The model showed that the latent PSI factor had a significant positive association with both pain intensity (NRS; β = 0.29, p < 0.001) and pain‐related disability (PDI; β = 0.39, p < 0.001). The residual errors of pain intensity and pain‐related disability were significantly and positively correlated (β = 0.38, p < 0.001). Age showed a significant positive association with pain intensity (β = 0.14, p < 0.01).

Group Comparison by Tobacco Product Use Patterns

We conducted ANCOVA to compare outcomes among the four tobacco product use patterns, adjusting for age and gender. The model assumptions were met. In post hoc pairwise comparisons versus the switcher group, Dunnett's adjustment was used. The dual‐user group had a higher mean PSI total score than the switcher group (14.8 vs. 8.5; adjusted p < 0.001). The reverse‐scored G score was also higher in the dual‐user group than that in the switcher group (0.29 vs. −0.23; adjusted p < 0.05).

Compared with the switcher group, the cigarette‐only group had significantly higher means on the GAD‐2 (1.8 vs. 1.3; adjusted p < 0.05) and PHQ‐2 (1.9 vs. 1.3; adjusted p < 0.05). Although the HTP‐only group showed descriptively higher means on the PSI, PDI, GAD‐2, and PHQ‐2 compared with the switcher group, none of these differences were statistically significant after adjustment, likely owing to the small sample size (n = 11).

In the exploratory analysis of the binary outcomes using a modified Poisson regression model, the dual‐user group showed significantly higher prevalences compared with the switcher group. Specifically, dual users were more likely to report feeling pain relief during smoking (PR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.01–6.12, p < 0.05) and using tobacco specifically for pain relief (PR = 12.40, 95% CI: 1.19–129.35, p < 0.05). No other statistically significant group differences were observed for pain intensity (NRS), nicotine dependence (FTND, TDS), or pain‐related self‐efficacy‐short version (PSEQ‐4) beyond those noted above (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides the first psychometric validation of the Japanese version of the PSI and demonstrates that the scale's function is essentially unidimensional, with a dominant G, in Japanese adults with pain. The PSI showed high internal consistency and expected associations with nicotine dependence, pain intensity, pain‐related disability, and psychological distress, aligning with prior validation work. In structural equation modeling, higher pain–smoking expectancies on the latent PSI factor were associated with greater pain intensity and disability, consistent with cognitive–behavioral frameworks that link such expectancies to worse pain outcomes.

Building on these findings, group comparisons according to tobacco product use pattern showed heterogeneity among users with pain. Dual users of combustible cigarettes and HTPs reported the strongest perceived link between pain and smoking, indicating a heightened cognitive risk profile relative to switchers and other exclusive users [2, 3].

A particularly notable contribution of this study is the differentiation of expectancy profiles across product patterns. The highest PSI scores were among dual users, suggesting that dual use may serve to sustain nicotine intake for pain‐related distress rather than representing a transitional stage toward cessation [3, 4]. This pattern could reinforce dependence by consolidating both physiological and cognitive pathways of reinforcement. Additionally, exclusive HTP users showed descriptively elevated PSI and disability profiles. Although not directly measured, this non‐switcher group may include individuals who initiated nicotine use with HTPs, pointing to a potential emergent risk group. These observations challenge a uniform harm‐reduction narrative, suggesting that HTPs may create a new pathway to nicotine dependence and maladaptive pain‐coping beliefs among people who might otherwise not smoke cigarettes.

Heterogeneity within HTP users warrants attention beyond a simple “HTP user” label [2, 32]. Switchers from cigarettes exhibited the lowest PSI levels, consistent with harm‐reduction motivations, whereas exclusive HTP users showed a distinct and potentially higher‐risk profile, with PSI and disability values similar to those among cigarette‐only and dual users. This pattern underscores the need to differentiate switchers from other exclusive HTP users in both research and clinical screening.

Several study limitations should be noted. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference; bidirectional relationships between pain and smoking expectancies cannot be disentangled. Second, use of an internet panel sample may limit representativeness. Although the sample was predominantly male and centered on middle‐aged adults, which is broadly consistent with national survey data showing that smoking in Japan remains far more common among men and is particularly prevalent among men in their 40s and 50s [32], no formal comparison with national survey data was conducted. Additionally, internet panel samples may differ from the general smoking population in ways not captured in basic demographic data. Third, the sample size of exclusive HTP users (n = 11) restricted statistical precision and generalizability; the findings for this subgroup should be considered as exploratory. Furthermore, we did not directly measure whether these exclusive HTP users initiated nicotine use with HTPs (i.e., native HTP users) or had other unmeasured past tobacco use histories. Thus, the inferences regarding this group remain speculative. Fourth, reliance on self‐report measures introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. Fifth, the broad pain inclusion criterion (current pain of any duration or etiology) limits the ability to characterize expectancy profiles specific to particular pain conditions or chronicity stages. Future studies may benefit from examining PSI properties within defined pain populations (e.g., chronic musculoskeletal pain, neuropathic pain). Sixth, although we assessed depressive and anxiety symptoms using brief screening measures (PHQ‐2, GAD‐2), information on formal psychiatric diagnoses and other medical comorbidities was not collected. Underlying psychiatric conditions (e.g., major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders) and medical comorbidities may substantially influence both pain perception and smoking‐related expectancies [33, 34]. The absence of these data limits our ability to disentangle the contributions of psychiatric and medical comorbidity to the observed expectancy profiles. Future studies should consider incorporating structured psychiatric assessment and comorbidity indices to clarify these relationships.

Seventh, no formal a priori power analysis was performed. Although the total sample size (N = 300) was adequate for the primary psychometric analyses, the small and unequal subgroup sizes—particularly the HTP‐only group (n = 11)—limited statistical power for between‐group comparisons. Thus, the associated findings should be interpreted with caution.

Eighth, sleep‐related factors (e.g., insomnia, sleep quality) were not assessed in the present study. Emerging evidence suggests that sleep disturbance is associated with greater pain severity, is also linked to smoking behavior and sleep disruption among smokers, and may partly explain the association between pain and nicotine dependence [35, 36, 37]. Future studies should incorporate sleep measures to clarify these potential confounding and mediating pathways. Finally, the moderate test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.52) suggests that perceptions of pain–smoking relationships may fluctuate over time, highlighting the need for longitudinal validation.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the validated Japanese PSI offers a practical tool to quantify pain–smoking expectancies within Japan's evolving tobacco marketplace. Clinically, assessment should extend beyond smoker/non‐smoker status to product patterns and motivation—particularly coping with pain—in chronic pain care [10]. Individuals identified as dual users or exclusive HTP users may benefit from integrated strategies addressing nicotine dependence and maladaptive coping beliefs. From a public health perspective, the present findings highlight that reduced‐exposure products do not inherently confer reduced risk. Initiating use of HTPs may foster dependence and expectancy patterns similar to those associated with combustible smoking, supporting targeted risk communication and regulation [38, 39].

In conclusion, this study establishes the Japanese PSI as a reliable and valid instrument and identifies high‐risk psychological and behavioral profiles among dual users and exclusive HTP users, indicating a heightened cognitive risk profile relative to switchers and other exclusive users [2, 3]. Future longitudinal and intervention studies should test causal pathways and evaluate whether modifying pain–smoking expectancies improves outcomes in pain and tobacco use.
