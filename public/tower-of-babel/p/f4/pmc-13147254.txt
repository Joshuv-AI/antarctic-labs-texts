A leap into the future: excluding the ischaemic origin of chest pain through artificial intelligence
Authors: Fabrizio Imola, Michela Cece, Lucia Fatima di Napoli, Domenico Mario Giamundo, Giuseppe Biondi-Zoccai, Francesco Versaci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChest pain remains one of the most common and challenging presentations in cardiovascular medicine. Clinical evaluation—including structured history-taking, recognition of anginal equivalents, and focused physical examination—continues to anchor early risk estimation. Artificial intelligence (AI) may augment cardiovascular specialist care through refined pre-test risk stratification by integrating clinical information, high-sensitivity troponin, electrocardiogram (ECG) data, and multimodal imaging. Deep-learning algorithms applied to ECGs identify subtle ischaemic patterns and support rule-out strategies with a high negative predictive value. Artificial intelligence-enhanced coronary computed tomography and cardiac magnetic resonance expand diagnostic capability by characterizing plaque, perfusion, and alternative non-ischaemic aetiologies. Multimodal models leveraging electronic health records produce dynamic risk estimates, while AI tools increasingly support identification of non-coronary but clinically relevant causes of chest pain. The clinical value of AI will ultimately depend on rigorous validation, thoughtful implementation, and clinician governance. When appropriately integrated, AI has the potential to improve consistency, equity, and accuracy in the assessment of chest pain.

Introduction

Chest pain is a heterogeneous and potentially life-threatening symptom that requires rapid, accurate evaluation.1 Despite advances in biomarkers and imaging, clinical assessment remains the essential first step: patient-reported characteristics, anginal equivalents, comorbidities, and psychosocial factors all influence the estimated likelihood of ischaemia. Physical examination provides additional diagnostic clues regarding haemodynamic stability, heart failure signs, pulmonary pathology, and pericardial involvement.

Even when initial clinical assessment suggests low ischaemic probability, clinicians must remain alert to alternative non-benign diagnoses—such as pulmonary embolism, aortic syndromes, myocarditis, or severe gastrointestinal conditions.2 Artificial intelligence (AI) does not replace clinical judgement; it may augment the quality and integration of clinical data as input for downstream risk-prediction models.3

From clinical suspicion to diagnostic strategy

Diagnostic pathways diverge according to the care setting—emergency department, inpatient unit, or outpatient clinic.2 In urgent scenarios, triage, 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), and high-sensitivity troponin testing guide early decisions but remain constrained by imperfect sensitivity and specificity. Subsequent steps may include serial biomarker testing, coronary computed tomography (CT), or stress imaging, depending on estimated risk and local resources (Figure 1; Tables 1 and 2).

Outpatient assessment involves a different time horizon, favouring selective non-invasive imaging, structured follow-up, and explicit return precautions. Across settings, AI can refine pre-test probability, identify very-low-risk patients suitable for accelerated discharge, and recommend the most appropriate next test. Embedded within clinical workflows, AI can transform scattered data into actionable insights.

Artificial intelligence: concepts and methodology

Artificial intelligence encompasses computational methods capable of modelling complex, non-linear relationships between clinical variables, test results, and outcomes.3 Supervised machine-learning algorithms—central to ischaemic rule-out—are trained on labelled datasets that define acute coronary syndromes or major adverse cardiovascular events using standardized criteria.

Modern decision-support systems integrate classic statistical learners (e.g. logistic regression, gradient boosting) with deep neural networks for image and signal analysis. Clinically relevant performance metrics include sensitivity, negative predictive value, and negative likelihood ratio, especially in acute care settings. Model reliability depends on robust development procedures including internal validation, external validation in independent cohorts, and ideally, prospective impact studies.

Artificial intelligence for pre-test risk stratification

Artificial intelligence-based models increasingly overcome the limitations of traditional risk algorithms that rely on age, sex, and typical/atypical symptom classification.4 Machine-learning systems integrating clinical data and first-sample high-sensitivity troponin concentrations can identify extremely low-risk patients eligible for single-visit discharge. Non-traditional features—such as healthcare utilization patterns, temporal characteristics of pain, medication adherence, and socioeconomic factors—further refine granularity.

Outputs are continuous probability estimates, which permit flexible threshold selection tailored to clinical context. Derivation-to-validation comparisons consistently show improved calibration and discrimination relative to established scores. The greatest clinical value emerges in simulated care pathways, where model use reduces unnecessary admissions and invasive procedures. Remaining challenges include model development for atypical presentations and adaptive algorithms that recalibrate using site-specific data.

Artificial intelligence-enhanced electrocardiogram interpretation

Deep learning applied to the 12-lead ECG is transforming this ubiquitous test into a high-sensitivity ischaemia-detection tool.5 Neural networks trained on large annotated datasets recognize subtle configurations of ST deviation, T-wave abnormalities, and conduction disturbances that may elude expert readers. Sensitivity for ST-elevation myocardial infarction and occlusion myocardial infarction (OMI) may equal or exceed that of cardiologists, improving early detection and triage.

These models can be tuned to maximize sensitivity and negative predictive value, making them ideal for rule-out strategies when combined with high-sensitivity troponin. Integration into hospital systems enables real-time automated alerts, prioritizes concerning tracings, and provides virtual expert interpretation in settings without on-site cardiology coverage. Remaining issues include signal quality, equipment variability, and interpretability.

Artificial intelligence in coronary computed tomography imaging

Artificial intelligence is expanding coronary CT imaging from an anatomical tool into a comprehensive phenotyping platform.6 Deep-learning algorithms automate vessel segmentation, quantify atherosclerotic burden, and characterize plaque composition, including high-risk features predictive of future events. Artificial intelligence also accelerates image reconstruction and facilitates non-invasive fractional flow reserve estimation through neural-network surrogates of computational fluid dynamics.

In patients with chest pain and non-obstructive coronary arteries, AI-derived CT features reveal diffuse atherosclerosis and remodelling patterns that help explain symptoms. In acute settings, AI-assisted CT can simultaneously evaluate coronary disease, pulmonary embolism, aortic syndromes, and thoracic pathology, making it a multimodal exclusion tool. Challenges include generalizability, regulatory approval, and workflow integration.

Artificial intelligence in cardiac magnetic resonance

Artificial intelligence algorithms have transformed cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) into a quantitative imaging modality with automated segmentation, fibrosis detection, and perfusion mapping. These tools reduce operator workload and interobserver variability while enabling more precise physiopathological interpretation.7

In patients with chest pain and non-obstructive coronaries, AI-assisted pixel-wise perfusion mapping captures gradients and severity of ischaemia, supporting diagnosis of microvascular dysfunction. Neural networks further assist in identifying myocarditis, cardiomyopathies, infiltrative disease, and oncologic cardiotoxicity. Artificial intelligence-accelerated acquisition and reconstruction shorten scan times and reduce artefacts, making CMR more feasible in acute care environments. Quantitative CMR parameters are increasingly incorporated into AI-driven prognostic models to guide follow-up and therapy.

Multimodal and combined algorithms

The integration of biomarkers, ECG signals, imaging data, and clinical variables into unified machine-learning models represents a major advance towards personalized diagnosis.8 Rather than interpreting each test in isolation, multimodal algorithms combine features non-linearly to produce continuous ischaemic risk estimates translatable into actionable recommendations (e.g. safe discharge, non-invasive imaging, early coronary angiography).

Embedding these tools into electronic health records with contextual alerts and real-time dashboards may reduce practice variability and testing redundancy. Cost-effectiveness analyses suggest potential reductions in unnecessary imaging and shorter hospital stays, provided that implementation includes workflow redesign and rigorous outcome monitoring.

Non-ischaemic chest pain

A substantial proportion of chest pain presentations are non-ischaemic, yet this domain remains clinically ambiguous. Artificial intelligence-based CT analysis systems can identify pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, and pleuro-pulmonary pathology in a single examination.9 Artificial intelligence applied to ECG and CMR differentiates myocarditis, pericarditis, cardiomyopathies, and myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries. Unsupervised learning can reveal phenotypes associated with long COVID or cardiotoxicity.

Artificial intelligence may also help distinguish functional or anxiety-related chest pain from organic conditions, reducing unnecessary invasive investigations without trivializing psychological contributors. Patient communication remains essential to contextualize algorithmic outputs and ensure appropriate follow-up.10

Present and future perspectives

Chest pain management will increasingly diverge across outpatient clinics, emergency departments, and inpatient units, with generative AI tools tailored to urgency, resource availability, and risk profiles.11 Outpatient predictive models may guide selection for coronary CT or stress imaging; wearable-linked AI ECG systems may reduce unnecessary emergency visits. In emergency departments, multimodal algorithms combining symptoms, vital signs, troponin, ECG, and imaging will support rapid decisions on observation, admission, or discharge.

Barriers to widespread AI adoption remain substantial, including lack of standardized implementation protocols, limited external validation, computational costs, and bias—particularly affecting women, frail older adults, and patients with multimorbidity. Ensuring that AI augments rather than replaces clinical judgment will be critical to equitable deployment.

Conclusions

Artificial intelligence already provides meaningful support in evaluating chest pain, with promising implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic decision-making. These benefits will expand in the coming years, provided that clinicians maintain active control of this technological evolution in a logic of AI-augmented care, rather than being overtaken by it.
