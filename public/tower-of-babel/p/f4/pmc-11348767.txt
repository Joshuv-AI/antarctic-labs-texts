Comparing body composition measures in children with end stage liver disease using noninvasive bioimpedance analysis
Authors: Maryam Ekramzadeh, Seyed Ali Moosavi, Amirali Mashhadiagha, Ali Ghorbanpour, Nasrin Motazedian, Seyed Mohsen Dehghani, Homa Ilkhanipoor, Alireza Mirahmadizadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic liver disease (CLD) in children, often leads to cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease (ESLD). CLD poses significant challenges in management and prognosis. Assessing body composition, including sarcopenia, is increasingly recognized as important in understanding outcomes in this population.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational study, involving children aged 2 to 18 years with ESLD awaiting liver transplantation. Socio-demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected, and body composition was assessed using Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA). Sarcopenia was defined using age-specific cut-off points for appendicular skeletal muscle mass (aSMM) and fat-free mass (FFM).ResultsThe study included 57 children (42.1% girls, 57.9% boys; median age: 10.9 years) with liver cirrhosis. Of them 11 (19.3%) died during the study. The mean duration of living with end-stage liver disease prior to participation was 5.43 years [IQR: 3.32, 8.39]. The most common etiology was biliary atresia (24.6%), followed by cryptogenic (22.8%). Deceased children exhibited significantly higher sarcopenia prevalence, lower basal metabolic rate and growth scores compared to survivors (P < 0.05), (771.0 vs. 934.0, P = 0.166) (65.0 vs. 80.5, P = 0.005). Total body and limb-specified lean mass were lower in deceased children, although not statistically significant. Similarly, total mineral (90% normal) and bone mineral content were lower in deceased children, with a significant difference observed only in water-to-FFM percentage (72.5 vs. 73.1, P = 0.009).ConclusionThis study highlights the high prevalence of sarcopenia among children with ESLD and its association with adverse outcomes, including mortality. Bioimpedance analysis emerges as a promising, non-invasive method for assessing body composition in pediatric ESLD, warranting further investigation and integration into clinical practice.Keywords: End-stage liver disease, Sarcopenia, Bioimpedance analysis, Cirrhosis, Pediatric, Body composition

Introduction

Chronic liver disease is a forerunner to cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease (ESLD), during which scar tissue replaces healthy hepatocytes, making it incapable of functioning adequately. Therefore, individuals present with jaundice, itching, fatigue, ascites and peripheral edema, variceal bleeding, and changes in level of consciousness [1]. According to the Global Burden of Disease project, 5400 people lost their lives due to CLD in 2017 in Iran (8.12 age-standardized deaths per 100,000), making it one of the most costly digestive diseases [2]. Orthotopic liver transplantation (LT) has been the only curative treatment in both adults and children with terminal liver failure for over thirty years [3].

Assessment of body composition is believed to be helpful in children with ESLD. Many factors including nutrition influence body composition. Undernutrition and resultant impaired growth manifests as decreased muscle tissue and expanded fat mass in the pediatric population [4, 5]. Previous studies have revealed that sarcopenia is a poor prognostic factor since it affects the length of stay on the waiting list and increases the risk of postoperative mortality in adults [6]. However, the underlying mechanism is not yet elucidated in children [7]. In contrast to assessing sarcopenia using a Computed Tomography (CT) scan which puts children at risk of X-ray exposure, body composition assessment could be more practical and applicable and also less expensive [8]. There are different tools to assess body composition, including lean mass, fat mass and body water analysis. It is not yet clear which tool(s) would be predictive in the prognosis of ESLD [9].

Consequently, more studies should be conducted assessing prognostic roles of body composition measures, specifically among vulnerable population of children. Our goal is to evaluate the distribution of body composition measures including anthropometric parameters and sarcopenia using a Body Composition Analyzer, among population with ESLD on the waiting list of LT.

Methods

Study population and design

This prospective observational study was conducted from April 2021 to April 2022 in Motahari and Imam Reza Clinics affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran. Patients were selected from the Shiraz Pediatric Liver Cirrhosis Cohort study (IR.SUMS.REC.1398.142). We recorded the patients’ status from follow-up information. The participants aged 2 to 18 years, of both genders, with ESLD, who were on the LT waiting list, and were able to stand on their feet were eligible to enter the study. Patients were excluded if their parents or legal guardians did not consent to participation or in case of any medical contraindication to test, such as the presence of a cardiac pacemaker or prosthetic devices.

Data collection and instruments

The socio-demographic characteristics along with medical and laboratory data of participants were extracted from the pediatric liver cirrhosis registry (IR.SUMS.REC.1399.530) and missing were collected through interviews. Laboratory findings include complete blood count (CBC), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine; and full liver function test (LFT) including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase, total and direct bilirubin, total protein, albumin and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT).

Body composition factors were measured with the Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) technique using InBody 770 Body Composition Analyzer (InBody USA, Cerritos, CA) [10]. This six-frequency BIA testing device provides both comprehensive and segmental extremity quantitative assessment of body composition and body water balance parameters, including skeletal muscle mass (SMM), body fat mass (BFM), intracellular water, or extracellular water.

To conduct the BIA investigation, we employed a conventional method (based on device protocol). This method entailed utilizing a fixed frequency of 50 kHz and placing four electrodes at a distant position (two on the hand and two on the foot on the same side of the body). The patient was after at least 8 h of overnight fasting positioned in a lying position, with the lower limbs spread out at a 45° angle and the upper limbs spread out at a 30° angle. Once the measurements reached a stable state, the values of resistance and reactance were recorded.

The following quantitative variables were assessed: Body Composition Analysis (protein, minerals, SMM, BFM, fat-free mass (FFM) and bone mineral content all in kilograms), Obesity Analysis (body mass index [BMI], percent body fat and obesity degree), Segmental Lean Analysis, Body Water Composition (including intracellular, extracellular, and total body water [TBW] in Liters) and Segmental Body Water Analysis. Segments comprise right and left arms, trunk, and right and left legs. Other parameters are basal metabolic rate (BMR), waist-hip ratio, visceral fat area, body cell mass (BCM) as well as anthropometric parameters in centimeters such as the circumference of waist, arm, and arm muscle. Nutrition, obesity, and body balance evaluation were the quantitative variables.

The Growth Score utilized in this study is an index provided by the InBody device, integrating weight, muscle mass, and body fat mass to assess children’s body composition and growth status. Calculation involves determining standard weight based on height and a target BMI, adjusting for age- and sex-specific norms. Validation studies conducted by InBody demonstrate high agreement with Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry for pediatric body composition measurements, ensuring reliability in clinical settings [10].

Statistical analysis

The SPSS statistical software version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to carry out the analyses. Data were reported using the count (percentage) and the median [interquartile range (IQR)]. To compare the survived children and deceased cirrhotic children for demographic, clinical, laboratory, and body composition characteristics, we applied Wilcoxon rank-sum, Fisher’s exact, and Pearson’s Chi-square tests. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Fifty-seven children with liver cirrhosis (42.1% girl, 57.9% boy; median age at diagnosis of 10.9 [IQR = 7.8, 15.1] years) were included in this study, with an average of 5.43 years [IQR: 3.32, 8.39] of living with end-stage liver disease prior the participation. The most common etiology among children was biliary atresia 14 (24.6%), followed by cryptogenic 13 (22.8%). Overall, 27 children underwent liver transplantation (19 children from the surviving group and 8 from the deceased group). The most common complications were esophageal varices (63.2%) and ascites (28.1). Till April 2023, 11 children expired and 46 children survived, without any statistical difference in terms of years lived with end-stage liver disease (6.62 [IQR: 3.85, 9.75] vs. 5.33 [IQR: 3.24, 7.85]; P = 0.542). Additionally, heart failure, multi-organ failure and LT rejection were responsible for 63.6% of deaths. Moreover, the average days from enrollment to death were 163 days [IQR: 105, 421] (Table 2).

Discussion

Assessment of body composition compartments and sarcopenia in children with CLD is not commonly performed as a standard procedure. Nonetheless, a number of recently conducted studies have indicated a significant occurrence of sarcopenia, along with a connection between sarcopenia and various outcomes, notably post-transplant outcomes [12, 13]. In our study, we used a sarcopenia cut-off point based on a study [11], which provided reference values for FFM and aSMM in Caucasian children and adolescents using a BIA device.

Our findings revealed a sarcopenia prevalence of 37% and 63% based on FFM and aSMM, respectively, in children with End-Stage Liver Disease (ESLD). Intriguingly, a significantly higher prevalence of sarcopenia was observed among deceased children compared to their living counterparts. These results are consistent with those of a previous study by Rezende et al. on children and adolescents with CLD using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA); the frequency of sarcopenia was identified in 40% (34/85) of the patients. When assessing the variables that characterize sarcopenia individually, 50.6% (43/85) of the patients had muscle mass deficit [14].

The present study demonstrated that the BMR was lower, and the growth score was significantly lower in deceased children than those measured in living children with ESLD. These results are consistent with a study by Mager et al. on the pediatric population after LT aged 0.5–17 years, which concluded that sarcopenia occurred in 41% of these patients. Persistent sarcopenia was associated with poorer growth and recurrent hospitalization in children post-LT [15].

Also, a study focused on non-obese children and adolescents, aged 9 to 15 years, who were diagnosed with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) using the BIA method; it was revealed that the Mean Muscle to Fat Ratio (MFR) was noticeably lower in individuals with NAFLD compared to those without this condition. The researchers concluded that NAFLD was significantly linked to relatively reduced skeletal muscle mass (SMM) in non-obese children and adolescents. They suggested that increasing SMM, through methods like weight training, could be considered as one of the treatment strategies for pediatric NAFLD in the absence of obesity [16].

Consistent with these prior studies, our results underscore the prevalence of either sarcopenia or myopenia among the pediatric population with ESLD, highlighting their negative impact on outcomes both pre- and post- LT [17].

In a previous study by Mangus et al. sarcopenia was evaluated using pre-transplant computed tomography (CT) on children younger than 19 years of age with end-stage liver, kidney, or intestine failure. They concluded that significant sarcopenia and increased fat stores were present in end-stage organ failure patients, supporting the notion of an active physiological mechanism to store fat while losing muscle mass. Sarcopenia may be related to total protein loss from a catabolic state, or from decreased synthesis (liver), wasting (kidney), or malabsorption (intestine). In our study, we compared two groups of children both living with ESLD. We were not able to compare the result with normal population; however, several fat content indices were measured, demonstrating that lower values in deceased children are generally seen compared to the living children. Also, the total protein content was lower in deceased children. However, it is worth noting that protein content was overall deficient in more than 50% of both deceased children and those who survived, which is consistent with the study of Mangus et al. [18]. The observed patterns in fat content indices and total protein content in children with ESLD, especially those who did not survive, may be influenced by the complex interplay of liver dysfunction, altered metabolic priorities, and the catabolic state associated with end-stage organ failure. These factors could collectively contribute to a scenario where fat storage is favored over muscle maintenance, and protein synthesis is compromised, leading to the observed lower values in the studied parameters.

In the current study, although the median BMI-for-age percentile was lower in deceased children compared to survivors, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, measurements such as waist circumference, arm circumference, and arm muscle circumference were observed to be lower in deceased children, but again, these differences were not statistically significant. The lack of statistical significance in these differences could be attributed to the complexity of assessing nutritional status in children with ESLD. ESLD is often associated with fluid retention, indicated by conditions such as ascites and edema. These factors can significantly influence weight and BMI measurements, potentially masking the true impact of nutritional deficits. Since BMI is a composite measure of weight and height, any alterations in fluid balance can obscure the interpretation of these metrics. [19].

In our study, the total body and the limb-specified lean mass were lower in deceased cirrhotic children; however, these differences did not attain statistical significance. This aligns with findings from a previous study, indicating that a majority of sarcopenic patients had a normal weight, while only a small number of them had low weight as indicated by the BMI/A index. Nevertheless, this index is not an effective parameter for assessing the anthropometric status since it solely evaluates the overall body mass without taking the distribution of body compartments into account. This limitation may fail to accurately reflect the true anthropometric status of pediatric patients with CLD [14].

It’s noteworthy that muscle dysfunction can serve as a significant indicator of the severity of liver disease. As a result, future studies in this population should consider exploring the potential inclusion of sarcopenia as part of conventional prognostic criteria. Establishing sarcopenia as a relevant marker for evaluating nutritional status in individuals with CLD could enhance our understanding of the impact of muscle mass on outcomes in this patient population [14]. Therefore, there is a need for further research to validate and integrate sarcopenia assessments into the broader framework of prognostic criteria for pediatric patients with CLD.

In the present study, the total mineral and the bone mineral content were lower in deceased children; however, the total mineral content was normal in more than 90% of children in both groups. A previous systematic review by Jeong et al. concluded that sarcopenia was a serious risk factor for bone diseases in the background of CLD due to alteration of metabolism and bone remodeling, strongly correlating with osteoporosis. They suggest that sarcopenia should be screened in patients with liver cirrhosis and further studies should be conducted on the reference cut-off of muscle mass for diagnosis in various populations [20].

Due to the perception of sarcopenia primarily affecting adults, there is a lack of specific age and sex-related normative data for assessing SMM in children. Furthermore, the absence of a universally accepted tool to evaluate muscle function poses a challenge in defining sarcopenia in childhood. Previous studies addressing childhood sarcopenia have utilized various body composition assessment methods such as DEXA, BIA, CT scan, or magnetic resonance imaging. These studies aimed to identify potential indicators of sarcopenia, such as psoas muscle area, SMM z-score, and MFR [21].

Another limitation arises from the lack of established cut-off points to classify muscle mass and muscle strength deficits in pediatric patients with CLD. To overcome this, our study relied on the research conducted by McCarthy et al. [11], which presents skeletal muscle mass reference curves for children and adolescents using a BIA device. Although BIA may have limited validity when compared to DEXA for measuring muscle mass, recent studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between the two methods in evaluating body composition. BIA is preferred in the pediatric population due to its simplicity, speed, non-invasiveness, and applicability to children [11, 21]. Despite this, the absence of direct comparisons with DEXA or similar gold standard methods in our study limits the generalizability of our findings. Incorporating such comparisons in future research would further enhance the understanding of BIA’s utility in assessing sarcopenia in pediatric ESLD patients and strengthen the evidence base for its clinical use in this population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study sheds light on the clinical characteristics and body composition of children with liver cirrhosis, emphasizing the prevalence of sarcopenia as a significant concern in this population. The analysis revealed no significant differences in various clinical parameters between surviving and deceased children, underscoring the complexity of end-stage liver diseases in pediatric population. However, the higher prevalence of sarcopenia among deceased children highlights the importance of early detection and intervention to improve outcomes in this vulnerable population. Besides, with emerging evidence suggesting sarcopenia as a significant factor warranting further investigation; employing methods like bioimpedance analysis (BIA) over CT or X-ray due to its non-invasiveness is particularly promising.
