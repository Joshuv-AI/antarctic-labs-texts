Non-Hermitian topological superfluidity in a three-dimensional Fermi gas with spin-orbit coupling
Authors: Pingcheng Zhu, Lihong Zhou, Jianxin Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe experimental realizations of artificial spin-orbit coupling (SOC) and non-Hermitian potentials in ultracold atoms provide a versatile platform for exploring their interplay in quantum many-body systems. Here, we investigate a two-component Fermi gas in a three-dimensional cubic lattice with Rashba SOC and a complex-valued interaction arising from two-body loss. Based on a non-Hermitian mean-field theory, this work elucidates the interplay of dissipation and SOC in the pairing behavior of Fermi gases. Dissipation not only drives a phase transition from a superfluid to a normal state and induces a reentrant superfluid but also, synergistically with SOC, significantly expands the stability regions of both the normal and metastable superfluid phases. Moreover, the introduction of a Zeeman field can drive the system into a topological superfluid phase characterized by a nontrivial topological invariant. These results thereby uncover a rich spectrum of pairing phenomena in this dissipative quantum system.

Introduction

Real physical systems are inevitably coupled to their environment and thus are inherently open quantum systems. In recent decades, with the deepening of research on non-Hermitian quantum systems, non-Hermitian physics has emerged as a vibrant frontier in modern physics, with profound implications across condensed matter, photonics, and atomic systems. Such systems exhibit a wealth of exotic phenomena, such as exceptional points,1,2,3,4,5 the non-Hermitian skin effect,6,7,8,9,10,11,12,13 and novel superfluidity.14,15 Ultracold atomic gases, with their high controllability, provide an ideal platform for studying non-Hermitian physics. Here, the interaction can be tuned via Feshbach resonances,16 laser-induced synthetic spin-orbit coupling (SOC)17,18,19,20 can be implemented, and non-Hermiticity can be realized experimentally via laser- or electron-beam-induced one-body11,21,22,23,24,25,26 or two-body dissipation.27,28,29,30

In particular, two-body dissipation refers to a class of decay processes in which two particles are simultaneously lost from the system due to their mutual interaction. Formally, this type of dissipation is described by introducing a complex-valued interaction into the system’s Hamiltonian, and the strength of two-body dissipation can be tuned by adjusting the depth of the optical lattice.27,28,29,30 Theoretically, in the few-body regime, it has been shown that the non-Hermiticity arising from two-body dissipation suppresses the formation of both two- and three-body bound states.31,32 This contrasts sharply with other types of non-Hermiticity—such as an imaginary magnetic field or non-Hermitian SOC acting at the single-particle level—which tend to facilitate bound-state formation.33,34,35 In the many-body context, theoretical studies have demonstrated that two-body dissipation can induce intriguing forms of fermionic superfluidity, including dissipation-induced reentrant superfluidity36,37,38 and phase transitions,28,30,39,40,41 as well as other novel physics.42,43

Extensive research has explored the many-body effects of two-body dissipation in both lattice and continuum systems. However, most of these studies focus on the interplay between non-Hermiticity and interactions; the role of SOC and Zeeman fields in such dissipative settings has rarely been addressed. In particular, SOC Fermi gases serve as a unique platform for studying topological superfluidity44,45,46,47 and exotic pairing mechanisms.48 Therefore, investigating nontrivial phenomena arising from the interplay among SOC, Zeeman fields, and non-Hermiticity is highly desirable.

In this work, we consider a two-component Fermi gas in a three-dimensional cubic lattice with Rashba SOC, a Zeeman field, and a complex-valued s-wave interaction. Within a non-Hermitian mean-field approach, we determine the ground state of the system at zero temperature and construct the corresponding phase diagram. We demonstrate that such a system exhibits rich many-body physics affected by two-body dissipation, SOC, and the Zeeman field. A fundamental feature is the reentrant behavior of the superfluid (SF) state under weak interactions in the absence and presence of SOC or the Zeeman field. Although in lattice systems either SOC or a Zeeman field alone tends to suppress pairing by increasing the single-particle band gap, their combined effect can be strikingly different. This interplay robustly drives and stabilizes a topological SF (TSF) phase, which is characterized by a nontrivial topological invariant. Our results enrich the physical picture and pave the way for understanding open quantum many-body systems.

Phase diagram

To construct the zero-temperature phase diagram, we derive the gap equation from the stationary condition of the thermodynamic potential ∂ΩMF/∂Δ0 = 0,(Equation 12)NU=∑k1+h2/E04Ek++1−h2/E04Ek−.

The order parameters are obtained by solving the gap equation for different choices of interaction strength U1, dissipation γ, SOC strength α, and Zeeman field h. To distinguish different phases, we resort to a method based on the energy difference between the SF state and the normal state as ΔE = ΩMF(Δ0) − ΩMF(0).36,38 In the following numerical calculations, we adopt the hopping integral t and the lattice constant a as units of energy and length, respectively.

In Figures 1A–1C, we present the mean-field zero-temperature phase diagram in the (U1, γ) plane for three typical SOC strength. The yellow region corresponds to the normal (N) phase, characterized by Re(Δ0) = 0. The blue region represents the stable SF phase, distinguished by Re(Δ0) ≠ 0 and ΔE < 0, and is an effective ground state of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. The light blue region denotes the metastable superfluid (MSF) phase, identified by Re(Δ0) ≠ 0 and ΔE > 0, and corresponds to a local rather than global minimum of the real part of the grand thermodynamic potential. This phase has a finite lifetime; studying it paves the way for discovering novel quantum states of matter, such as time crystals and topological orders. In the weak interaction case, as dissipation increases, the system exhibits a nonmonotonic evolution: it transitions from SF to MSF phase, then to the N phase, and finally re-enters the MSF phase. In the strong-interaction regime, fermions form tightly bound, bosonic molecules that are essentially localized. In this regime, dissipation effectively acts as a one-body loss for these molecules. As the loss time of these molecules is very short, the remaining molecular population can then undergo Bose-Einstein condensation; this allows the superfluid order to persist even in the presence of dissipation. The dissipation and SOC respectively drive two key phenomena: a reentrance of the MSF state in the weak interaction regime and a significant expansion of the parameter space for both the N and MSF phases. The reentrant behavior can be understood as follows: at small dissipation, particle loss dominates and depletes the paired states, suppressing superfluidity; at larger dissipation, the strong two-body dissipation suppresses particle hopping between neighboring sites, favors on-site pair formation, and thereby enhances the SF order parameter,36,38 which is also a direct consequence of the quantum Zeno effect.27,28 On the other hand, in the strong-dissipation regime, quantum jumps are suppressed, and the system can be trapped in the MSF phase for a long timescale, making it a possible effective steady state under continuous monitoring. We point out that the phase transition between the MSF phase and N phase does not belong to the conventional U(1) gauge symmetry breaking transition but rather arises from the emergence of exceptional points unique to non-Hermitian systems, at which the Hamiltonian cannot be diagonalized. In fact, when ReΔ0 = 0, the Hamiltonian possesses parity-particle-hole (CP) symmetry CPHMF(k)(CP)−1 = −HMF(k), where CP = σx ⊗ τzK, σx and τz are Pauli matrices acting on particle-hole subspace and spin subspace, respectively, and K is complex conjugation. There is a spontaneous CP symmetry breaking at these exceptional points.

The expansion of the N phase across all these figures illustrated in Figures 1A–1C is that, as the SOC strength increases, the areas of both the N and MSF phases expand, while the region of the stable SF phase shrinks. This suppression of superfluidity by SOC can be understood from the single-particle energy spectrum. Rashba SOC splits the Fermi surface into two helicity bands with momentum-dependent spin textures. This reduces the phase-space overlap between time-reversal partner states (k↑, −k↓), which is crucial for conventional s-wave pairing, thereby suppressing the SF order parameter. The suppression of superfluidity by SOC is further manifested in the phase diagrams of Figures 1D and 1E, which map the phase structure in the (α, γ) space for both weak and strong interaction regimes. They show that with increasing SOC strength, the system undergoes a transition from SF to MSF and then to the N phase. This behavior is consistent with the trend shown in Figure 1F, collectively demonstrating that SOC inhibits pairing. For a given SOC strength, the reentrant SF behavior manifests through the phase transition sequence shown in Figures 1D and 1E with increasing dissipation.

To gain deeper insights into the phase transition in the weak-interaction regime, in Figures 2A and 2B, we present the real part of the order parameter Δ0 and the condensate energy ΔE as functions of dissipation γ with different SOC strength. As shown in Figure 2A, the real part of the order parameter vanishes as dissipation increases, indicating that the SF state is destroyed and the system enters the normal state. To elucidate the intricate interplay of dissipation, the Zeeman field, and SOC, we present the zero-temperature phase diagram in the plane of the Zeeman field strength and dissipation (h, γ) for two different SOC strengths in Figure 3. In particular, we identify a TSF state characterized by a non-zero topological invariant. The topological invariant of the two-dimensional subsystem at fixed kz is characterized by the Chern number computed within the biorthogonal basis. For a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian H(k) with right and left eigenstates |unR(k)⟩ and |unL(k)⟩, respectively, the Berry connection is defined as An(k)=i⟨unL(k)|∇k|unR(k)⟩. The Chern number for the occupied bands is given by C(kz)=12π∬BZdkxdky[∇k×An(k)]z. In our numerical calculations, we employ the discrete Fukui-Hatsugai-Suzuki method on a discretized Brillouin zone to evaluate the Chern number.51 A nonzero C(kz) indicates a TSF phase with chiral edge states. In Figure 3, regions with C ≠ 0 (indicated in green region) mark the TSF phase, which supports C = −1 and −2 in our model (Appendix B). The interplay of SOC and the Zeeman field splits the single-particle energy spectrum into distinct spin-mixed branches, enabling effective p-wave pairing and facilitating the emergence of the TSF phase. For weak SOC as shown in Figure 3A, the TSF phase is confined to a narrow window between the N and trivial SF phases. Upon increasing SOC, this topological region broadens markedly, accompanied by a concomitant shrinkage of the SF phase. This behavior can be attributed to the Rashba SOC operating through different mechanisms under varying Zeeman field strengths. For a weak Zeeman field, the time-reversal symmetry breaking is not particularly pronounced, and the Fermi surface mismatch between spin species is minimal. Here, the dominant pairing channel remains the zero-momentum, spin-singlet-like BCS state, which is suppressed as the increase of SOC. For a large Zeeman field, the Fermi surfaces are severely shifted, causing a pronounced Fermi surface mismatch. This strongly suppresses and can completely destroy the conventional BCS pairing, with the interplay of SOC and Zeeman field pushing the system toward a novel phase. In this regime, the SOC plays a constructive and essential role. Increasing the SOC strength in this regime enhances the efficiency of this intra-helicity pairing channel. It increases the phase space available for forming these resilient pairs, which are immune to the large Zeeman field. This can be viewed as SOC engineering a path to superfluidity, such as a helical SF or a TSF, both of which explicitly rely on strong spin-orbit interaction to exist. The crossover between these two regimes marks a transition in the dominant pairing mechanism. At a critical Zeeman field, the system moves from a topologically trivial BCS-like SF toward a non-trivial SF phase. In the latter, the order parameter may even originate from inter-band pairing and exhibit topological properties. Discussion

In this work, we have systematically studied the many-body phase diagram of a two-component Rashba spin-orbit coupled Fermi gas with two-body loss in a cubic lattice. Our results reveal that with increasing dissipation, the system in the weak interaction regime sequentially enters three distinct phases both in the absence and in the presence of SOC: SF, N, and MSF phases. The intriguing property of the many-body phase is the reentrant behavior of the SF state due to the quantum Zeno effect. In addition, the introduction of Rashba SOC can enlarge the single-particle energy gap, thereby extending the N phase region and suppressing the SF phase in the phase diagram. More importantly, the coexistence of Rashba SOC and a Zeeman field promotes the emergence of TSF state which is robust against local impurities and disturbances as long as the perturbation is not strong enough to close the bulk energy gap and induce a topological phase transition. The system exhibits two distinct pairing mechanisms in different Zeeman field regimes. This difference can be quantified, for instance, by projecting the pairing amplitude onto the different helicity bands. In this work, we utilize the momentum distribution to distinguish between different SF phases. These phases can also be probed by other experimentally accessible techniques, such as measuring the excitation spectrum via momentum-resolved radio-frequency spectroscopy or Bragg spectroscopy. Furthermore, edge-state imaging serves as another probe: in a TSF phase, applying a potential trap (e.g., a harmonic trap) leads to localized density accumulation at the boundary—a spatial signature of chiral edge states. In contrast, a MSF phase may exhibit additional density modulation between lattice sites due to molecule formation. Our work opens several future directions. It would be interesting to study the dynamic properties and the fate of Majorana edge modes in the presence of dissipation. Furthermore, exploring other exotic pairing regimes, such as finite-momentum Fulde-Ferrell-Larkin-Ovchinnikov states or breached-pair phases, in this non-Hermitian setting promises additional richness.

Limitations of the study

The non-Hermitian effective Hamiltonian neglects the quantum jump terms, is valid only when the time is much shorter than the inverse of the dissipation rate, and fails to describe the cumulative effects of quantum jumps during long-time evolution. The calculation takes the β → ∞ limit and focuses on the effective ground state, thus unable to investigate thermodynamic properties and phase transitions at finite temperatures.
