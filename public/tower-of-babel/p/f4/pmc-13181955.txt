Mitochondrial genome of the invasive weed Amaranthus palmeri: structural features, RNA editing, and evolutionary insights
Authors: Jiahao Zheng, Xinyu Bai, Tingting Zhu, Ang Li, Jing Yan, Huiru Li, Zhechen Qi, Shaojun Dai, Xiaoling Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmaranthus palmeri is one of the most aggressive invasive weeds worldwide, causing severe yield losses in major crops and exhibiting widespread herbicide resistance. Although its nuclear genome has been extensively studied, the mitochondrial genome, which plays essential roles in stress responses and reproductive development, remains unexplored.ResultsIn this study, we report the first complete assembly and annotation of the A. palmeri mitogenome. The circular mitochondrial genome is 348,126 bp long (GC content 42.8%) and contains 54 genes: 30 protein-coding, 21 tRNA, and 3 rRNA. Comparative analysis with A. tricolor revealed differences in codon usage, repeat elements, RNA editing (296 versus 311 C-to-U sites), and intracellular gene transfer. A total of 1,243 repeat elements were identified in A. palmeri, compared to 1,176 in A. tricolor. We analyzed genome structure and synteny among seven species in the family Amaranthaceae, and reconstructed phylogenetic trees for these seven and 16 additional representative taxa (23 total).ConclusionsThis study presents the first assembly and annotation of the mitochondrial genome of A. palmeri, revealing codon bias, repeat elements, RNA editing, and intracellular gene transfer. These results provide valuable insights into mitochondrial genome evolution and inform the development of mitochondrial markers for species identification, population monitoring, and lineage tracking, thereby supporting the management of A. palmeri.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08675-8.Keywords: Amaranthus palmeri, Mitochondrial genome, Genome assembly, RNA editing, Repeat elements, Intracellular gene transfer, Phylogenetic analysis, Invasive species, Amaranthaceae

Introduction

Amaranthus palmeri is among the most notorious invasive species affecting global agriculture [1, 2]. Native to the deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, this annual broadleaved weed from the dioecious subgenus Acnida has steadily expanded its range across North America since the early 20th century, driven primarily by agricultural expansion and human activities [3, 4]. Its ability to invade agricultural fields and co-occur with crops has facilitated its global spread to South America [5], Asia [6, 7], Europe [8–10], Africa [11], and Australia [12, 13]. A. palmeri poses a serious threat to crop production, causing yield losses of up to 60–94% in key crops such as cotton, soybean, maize, and sorghum [1, 8, 14]. This impact is further amplified by its prolific seed production, efficient dispersal mechanisms, strong reproductive capacity, and significant environmental adaptability [14–16]. Additionally, A. palmeri has evolved widespread resistance to multiple herbicides [17, 18], rendering its control increasingly difficult [19].

Although nuclear genomic studies have examined herbicide resistance in A. palmeri [20, 21], its mitochondrial genome remains poorly characterized, a gap given that mitochondrial haplotypes provide maternally inherited markers for tracking spread and introgression and complement nuclear analyses. Furthermore, mitochondrion is a crucial organelle for aerobic respiration and cellular energy metabolism [22, 23]. In plants, increasing evidence also highlights their roles in stress tolerance and reproductive development, particularly in ROS homeostasis [24], mitochondrial–nuclear retrograde signaling [25], and their association with cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) [26–31].

Mitogenomes offer unique insights due to their maternal inheritance, low mutation rates, and dynamic structural features, such as repeat-mediated recombination. These characteristics make the mitogenomes valuable tools for phylogenetic reconstruction and population-level diagnostics [32]. Structural variation in plant mitogenomes is largely regulated by nuclear-encoded factors, and interactions between the nucleus and mitochondria—including intracellular gene transfer (IGT) events—play a crucial role in maintaining mitogenome integrity and shaping plant evolution and trait development [33, 34].

The widespread invasiveness of A. palmeri necessitates a deeper understanding of its mitogenome and the evolutionary mechanisms that contribute to its adaptability. In this study, we present the first complete assembly and annotation of A. palmeri mitogenome, providing detailed analyses of its repeat elements, RNA editing sites, codon usage bias, and IGT events. We further compare the mitogenome structure and synteny of A. palmeri with those of A. tricolor and other related species, and construct phylogenetic trees to elucidate their evolutionary relationships.

Materials and methods

Plant material and DNA extraction

Mature seeds of A. palmeri collected from Fengtai District, Beijing, China (39.76°N, 116.38°E), were cultivated under controlled greenhouse conditions at Shanghai Chenshan Botanical Garden (31.08°N, 121.18°E). Leaf samples were subsequently collected from these greenhouse-grown plants for experimental analysis. For long-read sequencing, Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAGEN® Genomic DNA Kit, and libraries were constructed with the LSK109 library optimized for Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT). Then, Single-molecule long-read sequencing was subsequently carried out using the ONT PromethION platform. DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit fluorometer, while quality and purity were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry, respectively. Following sequencing, initial data quality control was performed using Guppy (v3.2.2), where low-quality reads were filtered out based on a mean Q-score threshold of ≥ 7. Subsequently, NanoComp was employed to visually compare and summarize the quality metrics of the resulting high-quality dataset [35]. A total of 2.1 Gb of sequencing data was obtained from the sequencing facility, including 91,002 reads.

For short-read sequencing, Genomic DNA was fragmented using a Covaris ultrasonicator, followed by construction of adapter-ligated libraries. The libraries were then PCR-amplified and circularized into single-stranded DNA. Sequencing was conducted on the MGISEQ-2000 platform. Raw reads were processed using fastp (v0.20.0) to remove low-quality sequences [36], adapter contamination, and PCR duplicates, thereby ensuring high-quality data for downstream analyses. Finally, A total of 2.2 Gb of sequencing data was generated, comprising 15,505,272 reads.

Assembly and annotation of mitogenomes

The mitochondrial genome of A. palmeri was primarily assembled using long-read sequencing data, with high-accuracy Illumina short reads applied for polishing and error correction to improve assembly accuracy. The long reads were assembled using Flye v2.9.4 [37] with default parameters, generating a graphical assembly result in GFA format. The assembly graph was visualized using Bandage v0.9.0 [38]. Assembly refinement was conducted using Pilon v1.24 [39], which corrected residual base mismatches, small indels, and potential misassemblies using the short-read data. The assembled mitogenome was annotated with GeSeq v2.03. Reference species included Amaranthus tricolor (a close relative of A. palmeri), Chenopodium quinoa and Spinacia oleracea (additional members of Amaranthaceae), and Arabidopsis thaliana (a well-annotated model). Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were identified using tRNAscan-SE v.2.0.11, while rRNAs were annotated using BLASTn. Any annotation errors in the mitogenome were manually corrected using Apollo v1.11.8 and Geneious Prime v2025.0.2 [40]. Finally, OGDRAW v1.3.1 was used to visualize the annotated mitogenome.

Codon usage bias

In this study, PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [41] was utilized to process the protein-coding sequences of the mitogenomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor. The A. tricolor mitochondrial genome sequences (OP177683.1, OP177684.1, and OP177685.1) were obtained from GenBank and correspond to three circular mitochondrial DNA molecules published by Bi et al. (2024) [42]. The sequences used for downstream analyses, including synteny and phylogenetic analyses, without additional re-assembly or annotation. The extracted protein-coding genes (PCGs) were subsequently analyzed using MEGA v11.0 [43] to calculate the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values for each codon [44]. An RSCU value > 1 indicates that the codon is preferentially used by amino acids, whereas an RSCU value < 1 indicates the opposite trend. The results were then visualized using ggplot2 in R, generating bar charts that clearly illustrate the codon usage patterns.

Analysis of repeat elements

In this study, three types of repeat sequences in the mitogenomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor were analyzed. First, interspersed repeat sequences were identified using REPuter (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) [45, 46], with parameters set as follows: a Hamming distance of 3, a maximum computed repeat limit of 5000, and a minimum repeat size of 30. Next, tandem repeat (TR) sequences were detected using Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) v4.09 (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.unix.help.html) [47], employing its default parameter settings. For the identification of simple sequence repeats (SSRs), MISA (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) web service was utilized [48, 49]. The parameters were configured to define the minimum number of occurrences for various types of nucleotide repeats: at least 10 repeats for mononucleotide SSRs, at least 6 repeats for dinucleotide SSRs, and at least 5 repeats for trinucleotide, tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide SSRs. Following the detection of repeat sequences, the data were systematically categorized and quantified. Then the results were visualized using ggplot2 in R.

The prediction of RNA editing events

To predict cytidine-to-uridine (C to U) RNA editing events in the mitogenomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor, Deepred-mt was employed [50]. This tool utilizes a convolutional neural network model specifically designed for predicting C to U RNA editing sites in mitogenomes. PCGs extracted from the mitogenomes in previous analyses were used as input data for Deepred-mt. The software generated predictions for potential C to U RNA editing sites, along with their corresponding probability values. To ensure high confidence in the predictions, only editing sites with a probability value exceeding 0.9 were retained for further analysis. The number of predicted C to U RNA editing sites for each PCG was then quantified, and the results were visualized using ggplot2 in R.

Detection of intracellular homologous sequences

In this study, the plastome data of A. tricolor (NC065013) were retrieved from the NCBI database [51], while the plastome data of A. palmeri (MT312257) were obtained from previous research conducted by our group [52]. To identify homologous sequences between the mitochondrial and plastomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor, the BLASTn program [53] was employed. The resulting homologous sequence data were visualized using the “Advanced Circos” module within the TBtools suite v2.221 [54]. Additionally, the BLASTn program was employed to align the plastid and mitogenome sequences of A. palmeri against its nuclear genome. The alignment results were visualized using the R package RIdeogram, which enabled the clear mapping of chloroplast and mitochondrial genes onto the nuclear chromosomes.

Phylogenetic analysis

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of A. palmeri, we performed a phylogenetic analysis based on the coding sequences (CDS) of 24 core mitochondrial protein-coding genes. The mitogenomes of the selected species were retrieved from the NCBI database, with accession numbers provided in Table S1. The dataset included species from the orders Caryophyllales and Santalales. Within the family Amaranthaceae, nine species were included: A. palmeri, (A) tricolor, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Chenopodium quinoa, Spinacia oleracea, Suaeda glauca, Beta macrocarpa, (B) vulgaris subsp. maritima and B. vulgaris subsp. vulgaris. Three species from the family Caryophyllaceae were included: Silene vulgaris, S. latifolia, and Agrostemma githago. The family Nyctaginaceae was represented by Mirabilis himalaica, M. jalapa, and Bougainvillea spectabilis. Additional representatives included Tetragonia tetragonoides (Aizoaceae), Opuntia cochenillifera and Pereskia aculeata (Cactaceae), and three species from Polygonaceae: Fallopia aubertii, F. multiflora, and Fagopyrum esculentum. Nepenthes ventricosa × N. alata (Nepenthaceae) was was also included as a representative of Caryophyllales. Finally, Malania oleifera (Santalaceae, Santalales) was used as the outgroup for rooting the phylogenetic tree.

The CDS of 24 core mitochondrial genes were extracted from these genomes using the PhyloSuite [41]. The initial alignment was performed with MAFFT v7.417 using the auto strategy, which automatically selects the optimal algorithm based on data size and sequence characteristics [55, 56]. A custom Python script was used to concatenate the aligned coding sequences of the 24 core mitochondrial genes. Phylogenetic inference was conducted using IQ-TREE v2.0.6 [57–59] with the ModelFinder Plus (MFP) procedure to automatically select the best-fit substitution model (TVM+F+R3) [60]. Node support was assessed through 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates with BNNI optimization to reduce the risk of overestimating branch supports [61]. The resulting tree was visualized in FigTree v1.4.4, with Malania oleifera designated as the outgroup.

Synteny analysis

In this study, mitogenome data of A. palmeri, A. tricolor, and five other related species—including A. philoxeroides, C. quinoa, S. oleracea, Beta vulgaris subsp. maritima, and M. crystallinum—were used to investigate conserved genomic regions and structural variation through synteny analysis. The BLASTn program was used to identify collinear blocks based on sequence similarity among the mitogenomes of the seven species. Homologous sequences ≥ 500 bp were retained as conserved collinear blocks. These blocks were visualized using NGenomeSyn to generate multi-genome synteny maps [62].

Results

Characteristics of the mitogenome of A. palmeri

The mitogenome of A. palmeri exhibits a single circular structure, which is characteristic of plant mitogenomes. This mitogenome comprises a total length of 348,126 bp, with a guanine-cytosine (GC) content of 42.8% (Fig. 1). A total of 54 genes were annotated within this mitogenome, comprising 30 PCGs and 24 non-coding genes (Table S2). The PCGs include 24 core genes, consisting of 9 NADH dehydrogenase genes, 5 ATP synthase genes, 4 cytochrome C biosynthesis genes, 3 cytochrome C oxidase genes, one membrane transport protein-encoding gene, one maturase gene, and one ubiquinol-cytochrome C reductase gene; along with 6 variable genes, which include 2 large ribosomal subunit genes and 4 small ribosomal subunit genes. Notably, all PCGs are present as single copies. The remaining 24 genes consist of 21 tRNA genes and 3 rRNA genes. Among these, trnH-GUG and trnE-UUC are duplicated. Additionally, several genes were found to contain introns: the nad2 and nad7 genes each contain 4 introns, the nad4 gene contains 2 introns, and the nad5, cox2, ccmFc, and trnY-AUA genes each contain 1 intron.

Codon usage bias of PCGs

Analysis of codon usage in the PCGs of the mitogenomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor revealed significant codon usage bias (Table S3, Table S4). Notably, stop codons exhibited pronounced preferences in both species. UAA was the most frequently used stop codon, with relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values of 1.3 and 1.7 in A. palmeri and A. tricolor, respectively. In contrast, UAG was rarely used as a stop codon, with RSCU values of 0.5 and 0.4 in A. palmeri and A. tricolor, respectively. A strong preference for the codon GCU was observed for Alanine, with an RSCU value of 1.6. Only a small proportion of codons displayed no significant bias, indicating a high degree of codon usage specificity in both mitogenomes. In A. palmeri, tryptophan and methionine were exclusively encoded by UGG and AUG, respectively. Similarly, in A. tricolor, tyrosine, tryptophan, and methionine were encoded by ACA, UGG, and AUG, respectively. Furthermore, nearly all preferred codons (RSCU > 1) in the mitogenomes of both species ended with A or U, with the exception of the UUG codon (Fig. 2).

Statistics of repeat elements

In the mitogenome of A. palmeri, a total of 44 SSRs were identified (Fig. 3). The majority of these were mononucleotide SSRs, accounting for 40, while the remaining SSRs included 2 dinucleotide SSRs and 2 trinucleotide SSRs (Table S5). Additionally, 10 tandem repeat units were detected, with the longest unit spanning 131 bp. Furthermore, 895 interspersed repetitive sequences were identified, comprising 417 forward repeats and 478 palindromic repeats (Table S6).

Statistics of the number of various types of repeat sequences in the mitogenomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor

In the mitogenome of A. tricolor, 39 SSRs were identified, with mononucleotide SSRs being the most abundant at 35. The remaining SSRs consisted of 2 dinucleotide SSRs and 2 trinucleotide SSRs (Table S7). A total of 13 tandem repeat units were detected, with the longest unit extending to 141 bp. Additionally, 769 interspersed repetitive sequences were identified, including 371 forward repeats and 398 palindromic repeats (Table S8).

Analysis of RNA editing sites

In the mitochondrial protein-coding genomes of A. palmeri and A. tricolor, 29 genes were identified to contain C to U RNA editing sites (Fig. 4). The total number of editing sites was similar between the two species, with 296 sites in A. palmeri (Table S9) and 311 sites in A. tricolor (Table S10). Among the mitochondrial genes, ccmB, ccmC, mttB, ccmFn, nad2, nad4 and nad7 exhibited the highest RNA editing activity, each harboring 20 or more editing sites. In contrast, genes such as atp1, cox3, and rps13 showed minimal RNA editing activity, with only 2 or 3 editing sites each. Notably, some differences in RNA editing activity were observed between the two species. For instance, the nad1 gene in A. palmeri displayed low RNA editing activity, with only 5 editing sites, whereas in A. tricolor, the same gene exhibited significantly higher editing activity, with 17 editing sites.

The analysis of sequence homology between the mitochondrial and plastomes identified mitochondrial plastid DNAs (MTPTs). In A. palmeri, 35,377 bp of matching fragments were identified, representing approximately 25% of the total plastome and 10% of the mitogenome (Fig. 5a, Table S11). Similarly, in A. tricolor, 48,763 bp of matching fragments were detected, accounting for approximately 32% of plastid genome and 13% of the mitogenome (Fig. 5b, Table S12).

Phylogenetic analysis

The maximum likelihood tree based on 24 conserved mitochondrial protein-coding genes (CDSs) revealed phylogenetic relationships among the 23 sampled angiosperm taxa (Fig. 7), with all nodes receiving strong bootstrap support (> 96%). The inferred topology is largely congruent with the APG IV system, underscoring the reliability of mitogenomic data for phylogenetic inference at both family and genus levels. A. palmeri was clearly nested within Amaranthaceae and was most closely related to A. tricolor and Alternanthera philoxeroides. These three species belong to the subfamily Amaranthoideae, while Chenopodium quinoa, Spinacia oleracea, Suaeda glauca, and Beta species formed a distinct clade representing Chenopodioideae. This pattern reveals a clear phylogenetic split between Amaranthoideae and Chenopodioideae within Amaranthaceae s.l. (clade a & b, Fig. 7).

Synteny analysis

The mitogenomes of six Amaranthaceae species, including A. palmeri and A. tricolor, along with one Aizoaceae species (M. crystallinum) also belonging to the order Caryophyllales, were aligned, and collinear blocks were visualized. The analysis revealed significant variation in the sizes and positions of local collinear blocks among the mitogenomes of the seven species (Fig. 8). The highest number of collinear regions was observed between A. palmeri and A. tricolor, which belong to the same genus. In contrast, other Amaranthaceae species not belonging to the same genus exhibited fewer collinear blocks. The greatest collinearity was observed between A. palmeri and (A) tricolor, consistent with their congeneric relationship. Species from other Amaranthaceae genera exhibited fewer collinear blocks. Very few blocks were detected between M. crystallinum (Aizoaceae) and (B) vulgaris subsp. maritima (Amaranthaceae). These patterns are consistent with established taxonomy and phylogenetic distance.

Discussion

Characterization of the mitogenome

Plant mitochondria and chloroplasts originated from endosymbiotic events over 1.4 billion years ago and have since evolved into semi-autonomous organelles with their own genomes [63]. Unlike plastomes [64], which are relatively conserved in size and structure, plant mitogenomes display remarkable variability [65], ranging from 66 kb in Viscum scurruloideum [66] to 5.52 Mb in Sitka spruce [67]. This variability is largely attributed to differences in non-coding DNA content [68]. Consistent with other mitogenomes, the majority of A. palmeri mitogenome consists of non-coding regions, accounting for 73.19% of the total length, compared to 85.998% in A. tricolor (Table S14). Structurally, plant mitogenomes are known for their complexity and can exist in circular, linear, branched, or mixed conformations [65]. The mitogenome of A. palmeri was assembled as a typical single circular molecule, with a total length of 348,126 bp. In the context of other Amaranthaceae species, the genome size of A. palmeri is intermediate. It is smaller than those of (A) tricolor (382,432 bp), (B) macrocarpa (385,220 bp), and S. glauca (474,330 bp), but larger than those of (C) quinoa (315,003 bp), S. oleracea (329,613 bp), and A. philoxeroides (283,258 bp). The observed structural complexity of plant mitogenomes is often positively correlated with genome size, as exemplified by (A) tricolor and (B) schreberi [69], where increased genome length is associated with more intricate genomic architecture.

The GC content of the A. palmeri mitogenome is 42.8%, which falls within the narrow range observed among the nine other Amaranthaceae species analyzed in this study (42.5%–44.1%). This conservation at the family level is consistent with and provides further support for the previously proposed notion that angiosperm mitogenomes, in contrast to those of gymnosperms, exhibit a higher degree of structural stability [70]. A. tricolor shows a similar GC content (42.5%); however, given their distinct domestication histories and placement in different subgenera (Albersia vs. Acnida), this similarity is best interpreted as a reflection of overall mitogenome compositional conservation within Amaranthaceae rather than evidence of shared evolutionary pressures or adaptive convergence. Notably, the limited availability of mitogenome data within Amaranthus currently precludes robust inference regarding associations between GC content, evolutionary trajectories, or ecological adaptation. Broader taxon sampling will be required to evaluate such relationships in future studies.

Repeat sequences and extensive homologous recombination

Repetitive sequences play an essential role in shaping the evolutionary trajectory of plant mitogenomes by contributing to genome plasticity, regulating gene expression, and influencing the variability of epistatic traits [71–73]. In the A. palmeri mitogenome, we identified 44 simple sequence repeats (SSRs), while 39 SSRs were detected in A. tricolor. These repeats may serve not only as indicators of genome complexity but also as useful molecular markers for species identification and germplasm development. Beyond SSRs, the presence of dispersed repeat sequences can promote homologous recombination events, which in turn mediate structural rearrangements such as inversions and translocations. These rearrangements are considered important mechanisms contributing to mitogenome expansion, structural diversification, and regulatory flexibility [74].

Our synteny analysis across A. palmeri and six other Amaranthaceae species revealed widespread genomic rearrangements, indicative of frequent structural reorganizations during mitochondrial genome evolution in the family. Moreover, a strong correlation was observed between phylogenetic proximity and the degree of gene order conservation, consistent with prior studies in other eudicot lineages such as Rosoideae and Amygdaloideae [75, 76].

In addition to structural variations, post-transcriptional modifications—especially RNA editing—play a critical regulatory role in plant mitochondrial gene expression [77, 78]. C-to-U RNA editing was prevalent in both A. palmeri and A. tricolor, with 296 and 311 editing sites identified, respectively. Notably, over half of these edits occurred at the second codon position, a pattern also reported in other species [76, 79–81], and likely to affect amino acid coding and protein function. This positional bias suggests that RNA editing may act as a fine-tuning mechanism in regulating protein structure and enzymatic activity.

We observed that RNA editing in both species led to the creation of premature stop codons in essential genes such as atp6 and atp9, which may impact respiratory efficiency and mitochondrial performance [82]. The substitution of amino acids with different polarities following editing—seen in both A. palmeri and Ilex metabaptista [83]—further underscores the potential of RNA editing to modulate organellar protein function. These observations align with growing evidence that RNA editing contributes not only to transcriptome plasticity but also to phenotypic diversity and adaptive capacity [84].

In addition to RNA editing, codon usage bias in plant mitochondrial genes may also play a crucial role in adaptive evolution by modulating gene expression and protein synthesis. Several studies have revealed a strong preference for A/U-ending codons in mitochondrial protein-coding sequences (CDSs) across diverse taxa. For instance, in Medicago species, mitochondrial genes exhibit significant codon bias shaped predominantly by natural selection rather than mutational pressure [85]. Similar patterns are observed in Gossypium species, where genes such as atp9 and atp8 show non-random codon usage favoring A/U-ending codons, potentially enhancing the efficiency or stability of mitochondrial respiratory proteins [86]. Moreover, in Ophioglossum vulgatum, codon bias correlates with gene expression levels and evolutionary rates across different tissues, indicating that codon usage may be fine-tuned in response to specific metabolic or physiological demands [87].

These findings collectively suggest that codon usage bias in plant mitogenomes may represent an additional regulatory layer that facilitates environmental adaptation. Editing-induced codon alterations may further refine this bias by shifting toward preferred codons, potentially affecting translational speed, accuracy, or protein folding dynamics. Such fine-tuning could be especially important under selective pressures like abiotic stress or herbicide exposure, thereby contributing to evolutionary plasticity.

IGT and its evolutionary implications in plant mitogenomes

IGT, the movement of genetic material between different cellular compartments—particularly among mitochondria, plastids, and the nucleus—is a common and evolutionarily significant phenomenon in plants. Among these, mitochondria exhibit particularly high levels of DNA exchange, with plastid-to-mitochondrion transfers being especially prevalent [88, 89]. These transfers contribute substantially to mitogenome expansion and structural diversification, enhancing genome plasticity and potentially supporting adaptive responses to environmental change [90]. Examples from other species highlight the scale of such transfers. In Selenicereus monacanthus, a 46,496 bp plastid-derived sequence was identified in the mitogenome [91], and in Capsicum pubescens, the size of the transferred plastid fragment reached 57,207 bp [92]. Beyond organelle-to-organelle exchange, both mitochondrial and plastid sequences can also be integrated into the nuclear genome, as observed in model species such as Nicotiana tabacum and Arabidopsis thaliana [93, 94]. These nuclear insertions—referred to as NUMTs and NUPTs—are thought to reduce recombination pressure within organelles, promote genomic stability, and, in some cases, result in functional gene capture that enhances plant fitness and adaptability [95–97].

In our study, A. palmeri exhibited evidence of extensive IGT. Full-length or nearly complete sequences of the plastid gene psbE and mitochondrial gene nad9 were found integrated into the nuclear genome (Table S5). This observation is particularly notable given the species’ invasive success. A similar pattern of elevated IGT activity was reported in the invasive aquatic plant Eichhornia crassipes, where organelle-to-organelle transfer rates reached 6.74%—substantially higher than in many other monocots [98]. These findings raise the possibility that high IGT activity may be linked to enhanced genome flexibility and ecological competitiveness in invasive taxa.

Furthermore, both A. palmeri and A. tricolor mitogenomes contained substantial regions derived from plastid DNA (MTPTs). Additionally, the nuclear genomes of these species are known to numerous NUMTs and NUPTs, including partial or full gene sequences. The presence of these organellar fragments suggests that repeated and ongoing gene transfer events may be shaping the mitochondrial landscape of these species. Given the potential of IGT to generate novel gene combinations and regulatory elements, its role in adaptive evolution warrants further investigation.

The phylogenetic relationships between two Amaranthus species and other species

The phylogenetic relationships inferred from 24 conserved mitochondrial protein-coding genes confirmed the placement of Amaranthus (including A. palmeri and A. tricolor) and Chenopodium (including C. quinoa) within the Amaranthaceae family, which is consistent with modern taxonomic classifications [99, 100]. This result supports the view that mitochondrial genome evolution in angiosperms largely reflects established phylogenetic patterns and validates the utility of mitogenomic data for resolving evolutionary relationships at the genus and family levels [101]. Notably, the two Amaranthus species formed a clade closely related to C. quinoa, consistent with the taxonomic integration of the former Chenopodiaceae into the Amaranthaceae family. This finding reinforces the close phylogenetic relationship between these lineages and supports the revised family-level classification based on both molecular and morphological data. Such consistency between mitochondrial data and higher-level taxonomy underscores the reliability of mitogenomic information for phylogenetic reconstruction, particularly at the genus and family levels [102].

Despite these insights, the limited availability of complete mitogenome sequences for Amaranthus restricts our ability to fully resolve intrageneric relationships. Although Amaranthus comprises over 80 recognized species, only three mitogenomes—A. palmeri, A. tricolor, and the recently reported A. tuberculatus [103]—have been published to date, accounting for just 3.75% of the genus. This underscores the considerable gap in organelle genomic resources for Amaranthus, limiting comprehensive evolutionary and comparative analyses across the genus. As more mitogenome sequences become available, especially from morphologically and ecologically diverse species, a clearer picture of lineage diversification, adaptive radiation, and genome evolution across the genus can be achieved. Expanding mitogenome sampling within Amaranthus will not only refine our understanding of its evolutionary history but also provide a valuable framework for addressing applied questions. These include the development of molecular markers for species identification [104], tracing the origins and spread of invasive lineages such as A. palmeri, and informing breeding strategies for agriculturally important species. Ultimately, integrating mitogenomic data with nuclear and plastid datasets may also contribute to improved phylogenetic resolution and a more comprehensive understanding of evolutionary patterns within the genus.

Conclusions

We assembled and annotated a 348,126 bp mitochondrial genome of A. palmeri, revealing abundant repeats, predicted RNA editing, and extensive intracellular gene transfers. Comparative analyses among Amaranthaceae species showed substantial structural rearrangements, indicating a dynamic mitogenome architecture. Whether this variation contributes to ecological performance or spreads remains to be tested. These results provide a resource for future genetic studies and clarify patterns of mitogenome evolution in this lineage.
