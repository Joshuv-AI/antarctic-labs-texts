Understanding the association between body mass index and chemotherapy dose reductions in women treated for stage I-IIIA breast cancer
Authors: Jenna Bhimani, Kelli O’Connell, Grace B Gallagher, Victoria S Blinder, Rachael Burganowski, Isaac J Ergas, Jennifer J Griggs, Narre Heon, Sankeerth Jinna, Tatjana Kolevska, Yuriy Kotsurovskyy, Candyce H Kroenke, Cecile A Laurent, Raymond Liu, Kanichi G Nakata, Sonia Persaud, Janise M Roh, Sara Tabatabai, Emily Valice, Peng Wang, Elisa V Bandera, Erin J Aiello Bowles, Lawrence H Kushi, Elizabeth D Kantor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistorically, breast cancer patients with obesity have been more likely than normal-weight patients to receive dose-reduced chemotherapy, although contemporary dosing practices of patients with obesity remain unclear. Among 9708 women treated with adjuvant chemotherapy for stage I-IIIA breast cancer at two integrated healthcare delivery systems (2005–2019), we used generalized linear models to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios between body mass index (BMI) and comorbidity and chemotherapy dosing (first cycle dose reduction (FCDR), dose proportion < 90%) and average relative dose intensity (ARDI) < 90%). A strong, independent association was observed between BMI and FCDR (PRBMI:40+ vs 18.5-<25: 9.74; 95% CI: 7.43–12.8; p-trend < 0.001) and ARDI < 90% (PRBMI:40+ vs 18.5-<25: 2.69; 95% CI: 2.35–3.09; p-trend < 0.001). This association weakened over time, although remained significant, particularly in the heaviest patients. Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was associated with FCDR (PRCCI:3+ vs 0: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.19–2.11; p-trend = 0.009) and ARDI < 90% (p-trend < 0.001), as were individual comorbidities. Understanding dose reductions and their impact will be critical to optimizing care and improving survivorship.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Biomarkers

Introduction

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) provides guidelines for chemotherapy dosing for breast cancer, with most cytotoxic drugs dosed according to body surface area (BSA)1. Consequently, women with obesity (body mass index [BMI] of 30+ kg/m2) would be expected to receive higher absolute doses than normal weight women. However, measures such as ideal body weight have been historically incorporated into chemotherapy dosing calculations due to concerns regarding toxicity in patients with obesity at such high absolute doses, among other factors2,3.

Previous studies have shown high BMI to be associated with a higher likelihood of first cycle dose reduction (FCDR), reflecting clinician intent prior to administration of any chemotherapy, and a reduced average relative dose intensity (ARDI), additionally reflecting responsive dose reduction due to issues around toxicity and tolerance over the course of chemotherapy4–10. However, given concern that dose reductions may negatively impact survival11–13, guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) in 2012 recommended the use of actual body weight for dosing calculations14. Thus, dose reductions in the first cycle are not advised for treatment-naïve patients with breast cancer and obesity.

Several comorbid conditions including diabetes, renal disease, and cardiovascular disease are associated with higher toxicity and chemotherapy intolerance, which may prompt dose reductions in the clinical setting15. While BMI is associated with multiple comorbidities, particularly metabolic syndrome, this relationship is not absolute16–18 and it is unknown to what degree physicians account for BMI and comorbidities in chemotherapy dosing decisions.

This study evaluated the association between BMI prior to initiation of chemotherapy and pre-existing comorbidity with chemotherapy dose reductions within a large cohort enrolled in an integrated healthcare delivery system, for which we had detailed chemotherapy data and extensive information on pre-existing conditions. We also evaluated how the association between BMI and dosing has changed over time in the context of the 2012 guidelines.

Results

Population description

Overall, 9708 people received adjuvant chemotherapy, 31% of whom were normal weight (BMI of 18.5–< 25 kg/m2), 31% were overweight (with a BMI of 25–< 30 kg/m2); 20% had class I obesity (BMI of 30–< 35 kg/m2) and 17% had class II+ obesity (BMI of 35+ kg/m2) (Table 1). The majority of participants were ages 50–64 (47.1%), with 24.3% aged 40–49 and 17.9% aged 65–74. The most common race and ethnicity was Non-Hispanic White (57.2%), with 18.1% of participants Asian, 8.0% Non-Hispanic Black, and 15.3% Hispanic. The majority of patients (75.1%) had a CCI score of 0. Women with stage I disease made up 35.9% of the study, with stage II and IIIA constituting 52.9% and 11.2%, respectively.

Main results: BMI

Overall, 6.4% of participants had a FCDR and 19.1% had an ARDI < 90% (Table 2). BMI was strongly associated with FCDR, with multivariable models showing that those with a BMI 35–<40 kg/m2 were almost 5-fold more likely to experience FCDR than women with a BMI 18.5–<25 kg/m2 (PR: 4.64; 95% CI: 3.49–6.16), and those with BMI 40+ kg/m2 were almost 10-fold more likely to experience FCDR (PR: 9.74; 95% CI: 7.43–12.77) (p-trend < 0.001). ARDI < 90% was also strongly associated with obesity (PR for BMI between 35–< 40 kg/m2 vs BMI between 18.5–< 25 kg/m2: 1.93; 95% CI: 1.68–2.22; PR for BMI of 40+ kg/m2 vs BMI between 18.5–<25 kg/m2: 2.69; 95% CI: 2.35–3.09, p-trend < 0.001).

Main results: comorbidity

In multivariable models including age and BMI, women with a CCI ≥ 3 were more likely to have FCDR than women with a CCI of 0 (PR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.19–2.11, p-trend 0.009). A similar association was seen for the association between CCI ≥ 3 and ARDI < 90% (PR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.18–1.64, p-trend < 0.001). When alternatively examining individual comorbidities in relation to dose reductions, we found associations were strongest for renal impairment (FCDR PR: 4.49; 95% CI: 2.72–7.41; ARDI < 90% PR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.46–2.93), hepatic impairment (FCDR PR: 2.33; 95% CI: 1.61–3.35; ARDI < 90% PR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.04–1.70), anemia (FCDR PR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.32–2.15; ARDI < 90% PR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.13–1.51) and thrombocytopenia (FCDR PR: 2.76; 95%CI: 1.52–5.01; ARDI < 90% PR: 2.03; 95% CI: 1.51–2.74) (Fig. 1). Diabetes was additionally associated with ARDI (PR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.03–1.28), but not FCDR, as were neuropathy (ARDI PR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.06–1.37), and neutropenia (ARDI PR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.33–2.12). Cardiovascular disease was associated with FCDR but not ARDI (FCDR PR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.71).

Results over time

Between January 2005 and December 2007, women with BMI 35–< 40 kg/m2 were more likely to have a FCDR than women with BMI 18–< 25 kg/m2 (PR: 15.5; 95% CI: 6.71–35.9) (p-trend < 0.001); this likelihood was greater for women with BMI40+ kg/m2 (PR 32.3; 95% CI: 14.1–73.9, p-trend < 0.001) (Table 3). The strength of these associations decreased in women diagnosed between January 2008 and April 2012 but remained strong and statistically significant (FCDR: BMI 35–< 40 kg/m2; PR: 5.93; 95% CI: 3.93–8.94; BMI 40+ kg/m2: PR: 12.54; 95% CI: 8.43–18.65, p-trend<0.001). The strength of association declined again from May 2012 to December 2014 and was no longer significant for BMI 35–< 40 kg/m2 by January 2015–December 2019 (PR: 1.30; 95% CI: 0.67–2.50, p-trend = 0.02) but remained significant for BMI 40+ kg/m2 (PR: 2.69; 95% CI: 1.40–5.15). A similar pattern was observed for ARDI (Table 4). Joinpoint analyses further revealed that while dose reduction in obese patients declined over the study period, it was not confined to the period following the guidelines but rather was apparent from the earliest point in the study (Fig. 2). Notably, we did not observe a change in association between CCI and dosing over time (results not shown).

Additional analyses

To further explore the associations with BMI and assess robustness of observed associations, we conducted a sensitivity analysis alternatively adjusting for individual comorbidities and found results were unchanged, and thus we retained CCI in the main model. We also restricted analyses to those with a CCI score of 0 (Supplementary Table 1) and found associations between BMI and FCDR were even larger among those with no comorbidity (PR for BMI 40+ kg/m2: 14.3; 95% CI: 10.4–19.7), and similarly retained statistical significance for the ARDI (PR: 3.03; 95% CI: 2.57–3.58). Results for the association between BMI and CCI and both FCDP < 85% and ARDI < 85% were comparable to FCDP < 90% and ARDI < 90% (Supplementary Table 2). Results were also comparable when restricted to those for whom the intended regimen was determined via algorithm (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this large cohort of 9708 patients with comprehensive chemotherapy dosing and comorbidity data inclusive of a broad spectrum of regimens received in real-world practice, BMI was strongly associated with chemotherapy dose reductions in the first cycle and over the course of treatment. The strength of these associations weakened over time, reflecting changing practice patterns responsive to implementation of guidelines on BSA and dosing. That said, women with class III obesity remain markedly more likely to be dose reduced even in more recent years, independent of adjustment for comorbidity, age and other patient factors suggesting need for further understanding the factors driving decision making and implications on clinical outcomes. Results were generally comparable for first cycle dose reductions and ARDIs although with results notably stronger for FCDR. We also found that presence of comorbid conditions as measured by the CCI was also associated with dose reductions with both FCDR and ARDI. However, the magnitude of associations was much lower for CCI than for BMI, suggesting that BMI is a greater driver of dose reductions than comorbidity at outset of treatment. Individual comorbidities at the time of diagnosis had varying degrees of association with dose reductions and were most pronounced in patients with renal impairment, hepatic impairment, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, conditions for which dose reductions are clinically indicated and thus were expected findings in this study.

We found a very strong association between higher BMI and FCDR in our large and contemporary sample, in keeping with previous studies3–5,8,9,14,19, although these were largely published in 2008 or earlier (prior to the guideline update) and had much small sample sizes. Here, with a range of drug combinations captured, we can see that this association remained but was of smaller magnitude when examining associations with dose reduction in subsequent cycles (ARDI < 90%). While the FCDR reflects clinician intent and potentially perceived risk of toxicity, the ARDI further includes additional dosing changes and delays that likely arise in response to side effects and other toxicities. We thus further explored the data post-hoc to better understand the factors underlying this difference. In line with prior work, we observed very little evidence of dose escalations in patients with obesity who initially received reduced doses (results not shown). Therefore, dose increases do not explain the differences in associations with FCDR and ARDI. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis to explore if associations for ARDI were consistent among those women who started at the full dose of chemotherapy, and observed that in this group, PRs were slightly attenuated although there was no overall change in significance or interpretation (results not shown), suggesting BMI is still a driver of dose reductions in subsequent cycles even among those starting at the full dose, possibly due to intolerance and toxicity. Aligned with prior work, our data suggest neutropenia in normal weight women may lead to further delays and dose reductions more so than among women with obesity. All of these data speak to the need to better understand holistically the best strategy to achieve minimum effective dose across all BMI groups4,14,20.

Interestingly, although a higher CCI was associated with a higher likelihood of dose reductions, this was not as marked as for BMI, suggesting the presence of comorbidity does not explain observed BMI-dosing associations. As expected, the comorbidities associated with both the FCDR and ARDI were renal, hepatic, anemia, and thrombocytopenia as seen in previous studies, all of which are associated with increased toxicity and would be expected to prompt dose reductions9,15,21. Notably these comorbidities, when alternatively accounted for in multivariable models, also did not account for the BMI-dosing association.

We observed changes in patterns of dosing patients with obesity over time, with a substantial decrease in dose reductions occurring over time, as well as among overweight patients, which for FCDR likely reflects the emergence of evidence regarding full-BSA determined dosing, with notable declines in the proportion of patients with obesity who received dose reductions occurring even prior to the release of the 2012 guidelines, which promoted fully dosing women with a BMI 25+ kg/m2 in the first cycle of chemotherapy14. This may reflect emerging evidence shifting practices before guideline implementation; notably, a further decline in the likelihood of FCDR occurred from January 2015 to December 2019 suggesting further guideline adherence occurring over time. However, there is still a significant association between BMI and use of FCDR in the most recent study period, which may reflect uncertainty in the dosing of the heaviest patients or may reflect other comorbidities not otherwise covered. Even so, we have accounted for comorbidity, both through a composite measure capturing a broader set of comorbidities and through alternative analyses adjusting for individual comorbidities. The association between BMI and dosing also remained in analyses restricted to those with a CCI of 0, although we did not further explore whether this changed following the release of the guidelines given concerns about small sample size.

There are some limitations to this work. It is possible that some of our intended regimens were misclassified by the algorithm; however, we used extensive chart abstraction to mitigate this in all cases with ambiguity. Although we conducted a validation process for those with chart abstracted regimen determination, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to the group whose intended regimens were exclusively algorithm-determined to confirm results were comparable without any subjectivity of chart abstraction (Supplementary Table 3)22. It is possible that some comorbidities were not captured in our data; therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to determine if associations held when the cohort was restricted to those who had been enrolled for >1 year and thus were more likely to have complete comorbidity data. Findings were very similar to the main results (results not shown). As with any observational study, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, despite extensive adjustment in multivariable models. Associations for patients receiving alkylating agents may have been affected by the inclusion of carboplatin for which there may be more heterogeneity in approach on dosing; in this study, we calculated dosing based on actual body weight. However, exclusion of platinum-based drugs did not affect effect estimates (results not shown). We used the value of 90% to define significant dose reduction, in keeping with other studies4,9, as a balance of potential impact on patient outcomes and sample size. Even so, to ensure robustness of results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the cut-point of 85%19–21 (Supplementary Table 2); associations broadly remained comparable. We did not have sufficient power in the underweight group to include them in time-stratified and supplementary analyses and thus they were excluded from these stratified analyses, but in future work, it would be helpful to understand how treatment patterns of underweight individuals is also shifting with time. Further work may also seek to examine the individual drugs and drug classes which continue to be dose reduced among patients with obesity.

Our ability to examine dose reductions is unique in the context of this large study. We were able to achieve a granularity describing intended regimen22 beyond other comparable studies and in a significantly larger population of women treated for breast cancer, across drug combinations described for early-stage breast cancer in the NCCN guidelines. We also included detailed demographic data, with individual comorbidities, and were able to conduct stratified analyses by time. We were able to conduct sensitivity analyses using granular data on individual comorbidities to carefully reflect the rule of comorbidity separately from BMI. We conducted this research in an integrated healthcare setting that has been shown to be demographically similar to its underlying population and mitigates the potential for confounding by insurance coverage23,24.

In summary, in this large cohort of women treated for breast cancer in the real-world setting of cancer care, BMI was strongly associated with dose reductions, particularly first cycle dose reductions but also throughout the course of chemotherapy. Among those who received a full dose for the first cycle, dose reductions were still observed throughout remaining cycles suggesting that prescribers were responding to toxicity and drug intolerance in this population. Associations attenuated over time, reflecting changes in line with dosing guidance, although these remained strong for patients with the highest BMI, which may suggest continued concern regarding administration of large doses of these medications even if seemingly appropriate per guidelines. Further research is required to determine if first cycle reductions facilitate higher cumulative doses, the role of dosing in survival, and the impact on health systems and administrative planning of chemotherapy modification from cycle to cycle.

Methods

Study population

We evaluated the associations between BMI and comorbidity in relation to chemotherapy dosing in the Optimal Breast Cancer Chemotherapy Dosing (OBCD) study. The OBCD Study has been described in detail elsewhere22. In brief, it is a cohort study of 34,109 women, all members in Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) and Kaiser Permanente Washington (KPWA) diagnosed with primary stage I-IIIA breast cancer from 2004 to 2019. Both KPNC and KPWA are members of the Health Care Systems Research Network, a consortium of integrated healthcare delivery systems, with a Virtual Data Warehouse (VDW) which stores electronic health plan data with standardized definitions25. We obtained diagnostic and treatment data from the VDW, supplemented with other electronic health record (EHR) data, including chart review. Eligibility criteria included the following: diagnosis of an invasive primary breast cancer stage I-IIIA, with no prior history or same-day diagnosis of any cancer (except non-melanoma skin cancer), enrolled at KPWA or KPNC at time of diagnosis, had available medical records, and did not opt out of research studies. KPWA also included a subsample of women diagnosed with stage I-II breast cancer who were part of a previous study (COMBO, Commonly used Medications and Breast Cancer Outcomes)26.

We restricted this analysis to the 13,252 women who received chemotherapy. We excluded women who received chemotherapy in the context of randomized controlled trials (n = 674), and anyone not receiving adjuvant chemotherapy (n = 1306). Due to needed information for dosing calculations, we also excluded women whose intended regimen could not be identified (n = 319) or who received a non-NCCN-guideline drug combination (n = 242), since their intended chemotherapy dosing could not be calculated. Likewise, we excluded women who changed regimens from a guideline to a non-guideline drug combination (n = 100), those who received oral cyclophosphamide (n = 92), those who initiated chemotherapy after 2019 (n = 158), those with missing BSA (n = 62), and those with missing dose or infusion date for any drug (n = 119). Lastly, from this cohort of 10,166 we restricted the sample to include women with complete data on BMI (n = 2 excluded) and covariates planned a priori for inclusion in models (n = 456 excluded), resulting in a final analytic sample size of 9708. Given that one of these covariates, neighborhood income, was only available from 2005 onward, all analyses are restricted to patients diagnosed from 2005 onward.

IRB approval was obtained from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (IRB#17-566), Rutgers University (IRB#Pro2018000402), KPNC (IRB#1279761), and KPWA (IRB#1197161) with a waiver of informed consent at KPNC and KPWA.

Exposures

The two primary exposures of interest were body mass index (BMI) and the Charlson Comorbidity Index27 (CCI) at diagnosis. For BMI, the weight closest to the date of diagnosis was identified within the time window beginning 12 months before diagnosis to the date of chemotherapy initiation, and any adult height closest to date of diagnosis was identified. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2 and categorized as underweight (range in kg/m2 < 18.5); normal weight (18–<25); overweight (25–<30); class I obesity (30–<35); class II obesity (35–<40); and class III obesity (≥40).

For the CCI, we incorporated any diagnoses in the year prior to diagnosis and categorized values into 0; 1; 2; and 3+. In sensitivity analyses, we also separately examined individual comorbidities also diagnosed prior to treatment onset, including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, neuropathy, renal impairment, hepatic impairment, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, for which definitions are available elsewhere28. This was done to reflect individual comorbidities which may drive physician-led dose reduction more so than aggregate measures such as CCI.

Outcomes

Dose reductions were measured in two ways: (1) first cycle dose proportion (FCDP) of <90%, constituting a first cycle dose reduction (FCDR), reflecting clinician intent at the outset of chemotherapy, and (2) average relative dose intensity (ARDI) < 90%, reflecting relative dose across all drugs and completed cycles of chemotherapy administration. Both measures rely upon a comparison of the observed and expected dosing for the specific intended regimen, with expected dosing for most drugs determined by a patient’s BSA as outlined in the NCCN guidelines.

FCDR

Dosing at the outset is represented by the FCDR, which was calculated by dividing the observed dose by the expected dose based on BSA (derived from the Mosteller formula)14,29. The value for each drug given in the first cycle was averaged to determine the first cycle dose proportion (FCDP), and FCDR was defined as a binary variable (receipt of <90% of FCDP or ≥90% of the FCDP), with this threshold selected to both reflect substantive dose reduction while balancing study power4.

ARDI

Dosing over the course of chemotherapy is represented by the relative dose intensity (RDI)4,21,30,31, which was calculated per standard chemotherapy dosing analyses. For each drug, we calculated the RDI by dividing the cumulative dose received by the corresponding weeks of treatment and dividing this by the cumulative planned dose for each drug received by weeks of treatment. This was then averaged across drugs to calculate the ARDI. Participants were categorized as ARDI < 90% or ARDI ≥ 90%. Where participants experienced an early discontinuation, i.e., fewer cycles than intended were completed, we included data only for cycles completed for both the numerator and denominator so as not to obscure the effect of dose reductions. If participants changed regimen once, the ARDI was averaged across each regimen; if they changed twice, their data were censored at the end of the first regimen change. The ARDI accounts for treatment delays by incorporating treatment duration into calculations.

Data on chemotherapy drugs and doses, number of cycles, and cycle lengths as administered were available from health records. However, identification of the intended regimen at the outset was more complex and our methodology for this has been described previously22. Briefly, participants were classified as receiving a drug combination within or outside of the NCCN guidelines for early breast cancer from 2004 to 2019. Among those identified as having received guideline drug combinations, participants were further classified into intended NCCN-recommended administration schedules (cycle number and interval) using algorithms that allowed for some scheduling variation32. These intended administration schedules could then be used to calculate the expected dosing. In cases of cycle length ambiguity, medical chart abstraction by trained abstractors was used to identify the intended administration schedule from clinical notes. We identified regimens with non-guideline administration schedules, as well as non-guideline drug combinations (although the latter group is not included in this study, as it would be impossible to determine the expected dosing in this group).

Statistical analyses

The associations of BMI or CCI in relation to FCDR and ARDI were estimated using generalized linear models of the Poisson family with a log-link function and robust standard errors to estimate prevalence ratios (PR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs).

Patient and tumor factors were included as independent variables in separate, minimally-adjusted models, including age, stage, year of diagnosis, or study site (KPNC/KPWA). Statistically significant variables in minimally adjusted models were included simultaneously in a multivariable model (variables included in each model are available in table footnotes). P-trends are presented for both BMI and CCI.

We explored the relationship between BMI and dosing over time in two ways. First, we examined whether the associations between BMI and dosing (FCDR and ARDI) varied by time of diagnosis (January 2004–December 2007; January 2008–April 2012; May 2012–December 2014; January 2015–December 2019), with the middle cut-point of May 2012 representing the period before and after the release of the guidelines. Results are presented in each period separately with interaction evaluated using a Wald test. Of note, in these time-stratified analyses, we excluded women who were underweight due to small numbers. To further understand patterns over time and inflection points at which dosing patterns changed, we also explored a Joinpoint model, plotting the proportion experiencing a FCDR by year. Notably, while such an approach allows for a more flexible approach to capture changing patterns, limitations in sample size precluded the separate examination by obesity class. Thus, we used both approaches to better understand how dosing patterns changed over time.

We also conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to ensure robustness of our results. First, to better ensure independence from comorbidity, we conducted sensitivity analyses assessing whether the inclusion of individual comorbidities changed the association between BMI and dosing, each examined in a separate model that did not include CCI. We included single-organ system comorbidities as in clinical practice these are often used to make decisions regarding dose reductions, e.g., presence of pre-existing cardiac disease. To further address this issue and the potential for residual confounding, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis evaluating the association between BMI and dosing among the group with a CCI of 0. To ensure that associations were robust regardless of the cut point used to determine “underdosing,” we also evaluated associations between BMI and CCI with another commonly used cut point of 85% (for both the FCDP and ARDI). Last, to ensure that associations were robust regardless of how the intended regimen was determined, we also conducted an analysis restricted to those whose regimen was identified by algorithm only.

All analyses were conducted using Stata.v.17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).
