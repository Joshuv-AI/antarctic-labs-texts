The burden of infertility in north-eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, a high-fertility region: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mike-Antoine Maindo Alongo, Noël Labama Otuli, Jean-Jeannot Juakali Sihakiyolo, Salomon Batina Agasa, Sarah Missimbu Mayindu, Bienvenu Antony Ilongosi, Louise Bamawa Bahaisi, Katenga Bosunga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfertility affects millions of couples worldwide and regions with high fertility rates are the most affected. This study aims to determine the proportion of infertile couples during gynecological consultations in Kisangani, identify the aetiological factors involved, and assess the responsibility of partners.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in seven health facilities in Kisangani, following a two-month campaign of free gynecological consultations in 2023. The ratios were subjected to Pearson’s chi-square test or Exact Fisher with a significance level of p < 0.05. The data was analysed using Epi Info™ software version 7.2.2.6.ResultsOf the 1,434 female patients who consulted for a gynecological problem, 697 (48.61%) were infertile, predominantly secondary (78.78%) with a mean duration of 3.43 ± 2.79 years. Among the female reproductive system factors involved in infertility, ovarian factors was identified among 43.21% of cases, tubal factors among 64.63% of cases and uterine factors among 19.69% of cases. Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) was found to be associated with primary infertility, while bilateral obstruction and bilateral hydrosalpinx were associated with secondary infertility. Bacterial vaginosis was identified in 53.54% of patients, while 43.4% exhibited antisperm antibodies. A positive diagnosis for Chlamydia trachomatis was associated with secondary infertility. Among males, 44.55% of them had a sperm abnormality wich was in association to primary infertility. Oligospermia was the most prevalent semen abnormality (21.03%) and associated to primary infertility. Regarding partner responsibility, women was solely responsible in 25.23% of cases and men in 18.16%. Both partners were responsible in 29.06% of cases and in 27.53% of cases, the cause of infertility remained unexplained.ConclusionInfertility represents a significant public health concern in Kisangani, with approximately one-quarter of cases remaining unexplained. Given the high prevalence of bacterial vaginosis and anti-sperm antibodies, it is imperative to conduct studies to ascertain whether there is a correlation between these factors and unexplained infertility.

Introduction

Infertility is defined as the absence of clinical conception after 12 months or more of unprotected sexual intercourse [1, 2]. Infertility is frequently overlooked in many resource-limited countries and regions, such as sub-Saharan Africa, due to the numerous competing health needs and relatively high fertility rates and large families that can mask infertility in the population [3]. It can be a distressing experience for both men and women, often leading to psychological disorders such as depression. This is particularly observed in Africa, where children hold significant socio-cultural and economic value. In some communities with high fertility rates, the stigma of being childless is so strong that women who are unable to conceive are socially isolated and neglected [4, 5]. In the initial stages of marriage, a question such as “When are you having children?” may be perceived as a harmless joke. However, as couples navigate the challenges of infertility, such remark can become hurtful and insensitive [6]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the financial burden associated with infertility treatment represents a considerable obstacle. In many of these countries, public funding for infertility is either scarce or non-existent, and is frequently excluded from health insurance programmes [7]. Despite the high costs involved, the limited financial coverage of assisted reproduction means that infertility in resource-limited countries is perceived as a condition that does not merit financial support [8].

Infertility affects nearly 80 million people worldwide, with the World Health Organization (WHO) estimating that about 1 in 6 couples suffers from infertility globally [9]. However, the prevalence of infertility varies considerably across the globe. It is noteworthy that regions with a high fertility rate paradoxically have the highest prevalence of infertility [10, 11]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of infertility can exceed 25% of couples in some regions [12]. If infertility prevalence varies widely in Nigeria, from 14.8–51.8% in some regions, it is estimated at around 25% in Cameroon [13–15]. In South Africa, the estimated prevalence of infertility among women of childbearing age is approximately 32% [16]. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), there is a paradox between the high total fertility rate of 6.6 [17] and the location in the so-called ‘infertility belt’ of sub-Saharan Africa, where infertility exceeds 30% in some regions [12, 18].

Despite the absence of a national study on infertility in the DRC, this phenomenon is not well documented in hospitals. A study conducted in Kasai, situated in the south of the DRC, revealed a the prevalence of infertility is of 11% [19]. This is in contrast to observations from different clinics, where the majority of cases consult with the intention of seeking conception.

In contrast to developed countries, secondary infertility is more prevalent in developing countries, where an early first marriage reduces the risk of primary infertility [20]. The causes and types of infertility vary across different African countries [21]. In the DRC, secondary infertility is the predominant form [22]. Among women, tubal affections are commonly reported, with a prevalence ranging from 67.6 to 73.31% [23, 24]. Additionally, sperm abnormality affects 57.5% of cases [25]. The lack of access to assisted reproductive technology (ART) centres further compounds the issue of infertility in Africa, and mostly in DRC [26]. It is, by then, of great importance in our conditions to investigate the types and causes of infertility in order to generate data that will be useful for planning and interventions.

This study aims to determine proportion of infertile couples in gynecological consultations in Kisangani, identify the aetiological factors involved, and assess the responsibility of partners.

Methods

Type, period and setting of study

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in the city of Kisangani, in seven health facilities: the four General Referral Hospitals of Kabondo, Tshopo, Mangobo, and Makiso-Kisangani, the Foyer hospital and COKIS referral health center, and the Hôpital du Cinquantenaire de Kisangani. The selection of these health facilities was based on the subdivision of the city of Kisangani’s health zones and the density of their populations. Kisangani is divided into five health zones, four of which are located on the right bank of the Congo River (Kabondo, Makiso-Kisangani, Tshopo, Mangobo and Makiso health zones) and one on the left bank (Lubunga health zone). However, due to security constraints, no health facility on the left bank was selected. Non-probabilistic sampling of health structures was chosen to make free consultations more accessible to the majority of patients on the right bank of Kisangani city.

A free gynaecological consultation campaign was implemented in selected medical facilities between the dates of 2 August and 2 October 2023. Women aged 13 and above residing in Kisangani and its surrounding areas were the one targeted. The team comprised a gynecologist, four general practitioners with gynaecological consultation skills, five nurses, and three medical biologists. The staff of each facility provided assistance to the team.

The study population and sample

Of the 2,113 patients registered for the consultation campaign, 1,818 attended the subsequent consultation. No quota sampling was used for health facilities. Throughout the study period, 1,434 patients who sought consultation for gynaecological issues were evaluated. The aforementioned patients constituted the study population.

A simple random sampling technique was employed (Fig. 1). The study sample comprised patients who satisfied the following criteria:

Inclusion criteria: Sick a consultation for fertility issue [1], be in reproductive age and be aged 18 and over.

Non inclusion criteria: Sick a consultation for fertility issue [1] but aged less than 18.

Exclusion criteria: Sick consultation for reasons other than trying to become pregnant.

A total of 697 patients met the established criteria and were included in the calculation of the intra-hospital proportion of infertile women.

The 697 patients underwent the standard infertility investigations. If their partners had not accompanied them during the initial consultation, they were invited to the hospital for clinical and para-clinical standards investigations.

To identify the aetiological factors of the couple’s infertility and the responsibility of the partners, only the 523 couples who underwent the standard infertility investigations were considered. Of the aforementioned couples, 73 were seen at the COKIS referral health centre, 124 at the Kabondo General Referral Hospital, 124 at the Foyer Hospital, 23 at the Tshopo General Referral Hospital, 32 at the Mangobo General Referral Hospital, 59 at the Makiso General Referral Hospital and 88 at the Hôpital du Cinquantenaire de Kisangani.

Subsequently, all patients who had consulted in health facilities other than the Hôpital du Cinquantenaire de Kisangani were referred to the latter for treatment and follow-up. All standard paraclinical investigations of infertility, as well as the assessment of infectious diseases and the subsequent follow-up of infertile patients, were conducted for free. The consultation forms were classified according to the relevant health facility and in alphabetical order, in order to facilitate the process of data organisation and retrieval.

Data collection

In order to collect the relevant data, we carried out a documentary analysis, using predesigned consultation forms. These forms included the socio-economic, demographic, clinical and paraclinical parameters of the patients and their partners.

Infertility investigations

In order to investigate the couple’s infertility, a two-step process was employed. Firstly, the investigation of the woman’s infertility was conducted in three stages. The initial stage of the investigation entailed an examination of the woman’s medical history. The second stage involved a comprehensive physical examination, with a particular focus on the gynaecological examination. Finally, at the third stage, standards para-clinical investigations were realized. Secondly, the partner was invited to undergo a clinical examination and sperm analysis.

For female patients, samples were collected at the laboratory of the health facility where the consultation was held and subsequently transported by laboratory technicians to the provincial public health laboratory for analysis. Patients were invited to collect their results 48 h after the sample was taken. For men, samples were collected and analysed at Hôpital du Cinquantenaire de Kisangani’s laboratory.

Vaginal swabs

To assure the validity of the vaginal swab collection method, a standardized protocol was implemented. Prior to collecting vaginal discharge, medical biologists ensured that patients had not used antibiotics or vaginal medication in the previous 14 days, nor had they engaged in sexual intercourse or taken a vaginal bath in the 24 h prior to collection. In case any of these conditions was not fulfilled, the collection was postponed. To save time and to avoid causing the patient any distress, samples were collected without the use of a speculum. Samples collected without speculum have same comparable sensitivity and specificity to the one collected using speculum on detecting BV, Candida albicans and Trichomonas vaginalis [27]. In order to ensure reliability of the vaginal swab collection method, the swab was inserted deep into the vaginal cavity by the same medical biotechnologist, following the parting of the labia. Two vaginal swabs were collected: one for immediate examination and the other for gram staining. The swabs for immediate examination were preserved with physiological serum in an isothermal cooler and sent for testing for Candida albicans and Trichomonas vaginalis. The sample for gram staining was applied to the slides and heat fixed, placed in a slide holder at the patient’s consultation site laboratory, and then sent to the main laboratory for analysis. The Gram-2 Kit (BioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) was employed for the purpose of performing the Gram staining. The staining process entailed four distinct steps. (a) The slides were stained with Gentian violet for one minute, after which they were rinsed. (b) The slides were then etched with Lugol for one minute, after which they were rinsed. (c) The slides were rapidly flooded with a bleaching agent and alcohol for ten seconds, after which they were rinsed. (d) The slides were counterstained with Fuchsine for one minute, after which they were rinsed. The slides were examined using an optical microscope with a 100× magnification objective. The Nugent scoring system [28] was employed in the laboratory to diagnose bacterial vaginosis (BV) by scoring all Gram-stained slides. Five microscopic fields per Gram-stained slide were scored for the presence and quantity of Lactobacillus (Gram-positive rods), Gardnerella vaginalis/Bacteroides (Gram-variable coccobacilli), and Mobiluncus (Gram-negative curved rods) cell types. The vaginal smear was classified according to the Nugent score, which indicated a healthy vaginal microbiota (score 0–3), an intermediate vaginal microbiota (score 4–6), or BV (score 7–10). Two independent readers scored all slides in a single-blind manner. Should there be a discrepancy in the categorisation of a slide, the two reviewers would re-evaluate the slide and engage in discussion. Should a consensus remain elusive, a third party would be consulted to make the final determination. All Gram-stained slides were analysed at Hôpital du Cinquantenaire de Kisangani’s laboratory by medical biologists who had received specialised training in Nugent scoring.

Ultrasound

Two ultrasound examinations were conducted on women: the first by transabdominal ultrasound on the day of the consultation and a further examination by transvaginal ultrasound scheduled to take place between days 2 and 4 of the menstrual cycle. The gynecologist was the sole practitioner to perform the procedure. The second ultrasound was performed in order to count the ovarian follicles.

Hysterosalpingography (HSG)

The HSG was performed in the digitised X-ray room of Hôpital du Cinquantenaire de Kisangani between the 7th and 12th day of the menstrual cycle. Prior to the procedure, a systematic plasma beta-human chorionic gonadotrophin (b-hCG) test was conducted to rule out early pregnancy. Contraindications to the HSG included pregnancy, active genital infection, recent uterine surgery, genital bleeding, and known allergy to iodinated contrast. Our protocol prohibits the use of intravenous sedation during the HSG. To alleviate pain and discomfort during and after the test, and to prevent tubal spasm, 50 mg of diclofenac or 1000 mg of paracetamol and 10 mg of hyoscine were administered orally one hour prior to the test. The HSG was performed by an experienced radiologist, assisted by a gynaecologist, utilising the conventional metal cannula system and Pozzi pliers. Once the cannula was in situ, a volume of between 5 and 20 milliliters of a water-soluble iodinated contrast medium (XENETIX® 350 (350 mg I/mL), GUERBET, France) was administered in a gentle manner. A series of five X-rays were taken, including an unprepared pelvis shot and four facial images (beginning of filling, face repletion, early evacuation, late peritoneal shaking) during the administration of the radiopaque product. Frontal images were taken after contrast media was discharged into the pelvic cavity. Profile images were taken only if necessary. As clinicians are more reliable at diagnosing hydrosalpinx and tubal obstruction (findings which are most crucial to identify) [29], the gynaecologist was the first to read the X-ray images. If a more subtle finding suggestive of salpingitis isthmica nodosa or uterine adhesions was suspected, a radiologist was required.

Hormonal assay

Hormone dosage was mainly performed for women with irregular cycles, galactorrhea, amenorrhea and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) based on the revised Rotterdam criteria. The revised Rotterdam criteria include two or more of the following: oligomenorrhoea/anovulation, clinical or biochemical hirsutism, and the morphology of polycystic ovaries on ultrasonography (≥ 12 follicles in each ovary measuring 2 to 9 mm in diameter) [30]. Hormonal assays carried out included antimülleran hormone (AMH), prolactin, and progesterone. AMH was carried out between day 2 and 4 of menstrual cycle and Progesterone at day 20. The AMH was performed at that period more for organizational reasons.

Antisperm antibody (ASA)

Two different tests were employed to detect antisperm antibodies: a rapid serum test using Hightop ASA lgA and lgG Rapid test (Qingdao HIGHTOP Biotech Co., Ltd., China) and a mixed antiglobulin reaction test (MAR test) using SpermMAr (FertiPro N.V., Conception Technology Inc.). The MAR test was performed if the serum test was positive. For the direct MAR test, we utilised the partner’s sperm, while the indirect MAR test was conducted using the patient’s serum. The tests were conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions.

TORCH test

We used Hightop TORCH lgM/lgG Rapid test (Qingdao HIGHTOP Biotech Co., Ltd., China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Chlamydia test

We used Hightop chlamydia Ig M and Ig G rapid test (Qingdao HIGHTOP Biotech Co., Ltd., China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

HIV and syphilis

In accordance with the recommendations of the national HIV programme, all patients were counselled and screened at the initiative of the healthcare provider following a clinical examination. Following the patient’s consent, the test was carried out. The SD HIV/Syphilis Duo (Standard Diagnostics, Inc., Korea) was used to identify the presence of HIV and syphilis. In the event that the Duo test returned a positive result for HIV, two further tests were conducted in order to confirm the presence of HIV. The Uni-Gold™ Recombigen® HIV-1/2 (Trinity Biotec Plc, Ireland) and VIKIA® HIV 1/2 (Biomérieux, France) tests were employed.

Sperm analysis

The semen analysis was conducted following a period of sexual abstinence of between three and five days. The results were then interpreted in accordance with the laboratory manual provided by the WHO [31].

Data analysis

The data was analysed using Epi Info™ software version 7.2.2.6 (CDC, Atlanta, USA). Qualitative data are presented as proportions, while quantitative data are presented as means with their standard deviations (SDs). The ratios were subjected to a Pearson’s chi-square test or Exact Fisher with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Ethical issue

This study was ethically approved by the Ministry of Public Health of DRC (reference number: 701/FBL/DPS/TSHOPO/SEC/0173/2023) through the head of the health division of the Tshopo province and by the Ethical Committee board of each health facility. Prior to any consultation, all patients were informed that the consultations were for research purposes and that the de-identified data collected would be used for scientific publications. This was done in order to obtain their consent. In the event of refusal, the file was reclassified following the conclusion of treatment.

Prevalence of infertility

In relation to sexual behaviour, the study found a statistically significant association between seeking medical attention for maternity and various factors. Responsibility for infertility of the couple

The study revealed that in 25.23% of cases, the woman was solely responsible for the couple’s infertility, while in 18.16% of cases, the man was responsible. In 29.06% of cases, both partners were responsible. In 27.53% of cases, the cause of infertility remained unexplained (Fig. 1).

Causes of infertility

Table 3 displays the potential causes of infertility after standard investigations and infectious assessments, including testing for anti-sperm antibodies. Ovarian factors were identified in 43.21% of patients and were significantly associated with primary infertility (p-value = 0.0172). Micropolycystic ovaries were present in 31.55% of cases and were significantly associated with primary infertility (p-value = 0.0012). Additionally, organic ovarian cysts were found in 7.46% of patients, and hyperprolactinaemia in 5.74%. Discussion

It is well documented that the prevalence of infertility is highest in low-income countries, with sub-Saharan Africa representing a significant example [12]. In numerous regions of Africa, it is estimated that up to 40% of women have reached the end of their reproductive years without having a child [32]. In their study, Polis et al. [16] estimated the prevalence of infertility in low-to-middle-income countries by applying a current duration approach to Demographic and Health Survey data. The findings indicated that the prevalence of infertility was 31.1%. An infertility belt has been described in Africa, extending from Angola to Tanzania and including Cameroon, Gabon, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Nigeria. The infertility belt exhibits a high prevalence of infertility, with rates exceeding 30% in certain regions [18].

In our study, we found a high prevalence of patients consulting for infertility, at 48.61%, which is consistent with the expected prevalence in the infertility belt of Africa. A lower prevalence of 11% was observed in the southern region of the DRC in Kasai [19]. In the DRC, the majority of couples experiencing infertility tend to consult general practitioners and traditional practitioners before consulting a gynaecologist. In the majority of cases, a gynaecologist is only consulted when previous treatments have been unsuccessful [22]. It can be postulated that the availability of free gynaecological consultations during the outreach may have been a significant factor in motivating several infertile women to seek medical advice, which could explain the high prevalence of infertility observed in our study.

Among women experiencing infertility, 78.78% had secondary infertility, while 21.22% had primary infertility. The results of this study are in agreement with those of Bosenge et al. [22] in Kisangani, who reported in 2023 that 67% of infertile couples had secondary infertility and 33% primary infertility. These findings are consistent with those from other Sub-Saharan African studies [19, 24, 33, 34]. The proportions of patients with primary and secondary infertility vary according to the aetiological particularities of each geographical region. The high prevalence of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) among women with secondary infertility in our study and in other sub-Saharan environments is mainly due to the high incidence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), unsafe abortions and complications of childbirth, among other factors. PID can lead to infertility in several ways, including the production of antisperm antibodies, tubal obstruction and other complications [23, 33]. The mean duration of infertility in this study was 3,43 ± 2,79 years which is lower than 6.09 ± 4.80 years found by Bosenge et al. [22] in 2023. In contrast to numerous illnesses that necessitate medical intervention regardless of personal preferences, infertility is only regarded as a significant concern when a couple desires to conceive but encounters obstacles. Individuals who are subfertile but lack a strong inclination towards biological parenthood or fortune may not actively pursue medical interventions. The provision of free gynaecological consultations by a specialist may be a significant factor influencing the decision of patients to seek medical advice at an earlier stage, which in turn may contribute to the relatively short average duration of desire to conceive observed in this study.

The mean age of infertile women was found to be 33.91 ± 6.69 years, while the mean age of infertile men was 41.33 ± 6.05 years. In Kisangani, Bosenge-Nguma et al. [22] found a mean age of 32.85 ± 6.02 years, while Mubikayi et al. [19] in the southern region of the country reported a mean age of 33.85 ± 5.82 years at Mbujimayi and in the western of the DRC, Mboloko et al. [35] found a mean age of 33.85 ± 5.82 years at Kinshasa. Sule et al. [14] found a mean age slightly higher (35 years) in Nigeria. In our communities, the majority of girls generally get married before the age of 26, which is the age of high fertility. Given the high fertility rate in our communities, they conceive earlier [14]. Nevertheless, the observed decline in fertility at the age of 30, coupled with the high prevalence of infections affecting fertility in our communities [23], provides a rationale for the average age of infertile women in our study. Among men, a significant association was observed between age and infertility, with men aged ≥ 35 years exhibiting a significantly higher prevalence of infertility. The mean age of these men was 41.33 ± 6.05 years. A review of the literature revealed that the probability of conception is 30% lower for men over the age of 40 than for men under the age of 30. This is attributed to the negative effects of advanced paternal age on sperm parameters, reproductive success, and offspring health [36].

Among women, housewives constituted the largest proportion of cases of infertility, with 44.76% of cases. However, there was a significant association between seeking consultation for conception and being civil servants, health professionals, and housewives. In a study conducted in 2023 in Sudan by Abdullah et al. [37], the housewives group was found to be the largest, yet no significant differences were observed between the various groups. The high number of housewives can be attributed to the fact that African society is traditionally oriented towards marriage. Furthermore, our study identified an association between the status of being a housewife, a health professional, or a government employee and the occurrence of infertility. This is likely due to several modifiable factors of infertility, such as stress, diet, long working hours, and so forth, which are known to affect fertility [37].

For men, working as civil servant or in private sector, being a lawyer or health professional was significantly associated to infertility. Civil servants, workers in private sector lawyers and health care professionals are individuals who possess a high level of knowledge and education. A systematic review study revealed that, with regard to the cognitive capacity to accept infertility, men with higher levels of cognition exhibited heightened anxiety and diminished intentions to adhere to treatment among subfertile men [38]. Given that psychological stress has been demonstrated to reduce sperm count, progressive motility, and increase abnormal morphology [39], the situation is further exacerbated by the reluctance to seek a solution. This could elucidate the exposure of these workers to infertility.

The study revealed that women residing in peripheral communes (suburbs) of Kisangani and men residing within the city centre were significantly more likely to seek consultation for infertility. The city centre of Kisangani is home to the majority of the city’s wealthy residents, as well as the bulk of its commercial activities and recreational facilities. This attracts a considerable number of individuals from the suburbs, resulting in an elevated risk of exposure to high-risk sexual behaviours and sexually transmitted infections. These factors are among the contributors to tubal obstructions and the deterioration in sperm quality [25, 40]. This may be an explanation for these findings.

Women having a medium or medium high socio-economic status and men having a medium high or high socio-economic status were significantly more likely to seek consultation for infertility. It is evident that in Africa, an increase in socio-economic status is frequently accompanied by a transformation in lifestyle and dietary habits, leading to a greater prevalence of sedentary behaviours and a greater consumption of industrially produced products containing endocrine disruptors, heavy metals and high levels of fat. These changes are contributing to the onset of infertility [37, 41, 42]. Furthermore, wealthier men are more likely to engage in riskier sexual behaviours (HRSB), such as having multiple partners and less condom use, which can lead to infertility [43].

The study revealed that both married women and men were significantly more likely to seek consultation for infertility. A systematic review revealed that infertile couples reported that the marital relationship of either member was affected by the experience of infertility and the coping strategies employed in response to it. Moreover, additional factors, including sexual satisfaction, the age of the infertile couples, the level of education, and the congruence of couples’ perceptions of infertility, were found to be associated with the quality of the marital relationship. Furthermore, a significantly less stable marital relationship was observed in infertile females compared to fertile females [44]. This may result in the development of HRSB, which in turn may lead to infertility and explain the observed results.

The study revealed a statistically significant correlation between seeking medical attention for conception and various factors related to sexual behaviour among women. These included being in a polygamous relationship, having first sexual intercourse before the age of 18, admitting to having unprotected sex with a non-regular partner in the previous 6 months, and having a history of treatment for genital infections in the previous 6 months. A number of factors have been identified by various authors as being indicative of high-risk sexual behaviour (HRSB). These include the age at which the individual first engages in sexual activity, the use of unprotected intercourse, the number of sexual partners, the use of illicit substances, and the associated risks of infection, pregnancy, or even familial conflicts or legal issues. Those with HRSB are at an elevated risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and other infections, which can result in a number of complications, including infertility [45, 46]. This justifies the results obtained in this study.

The results of our study indicate that a history of abdominal-pelvic surgery is associated with infertility. A similar result was found in 2013 in Nigeria by Famurewa et al. [47]. They also found that there was an association between abdominal-pelvic surgery and tubal pathologies, probably due to adhesions, which explains the infertility and its duration.

Concerning the responsibility of the partners in the occurrence of the couple’s infertility, our findings indicate that in 25.23% of cases, the woman was solely responsible for the couple’s infertility, while in 18.16% of cases, the man was responsible. In 29.06% of cases, both partners were responsible. In 27.53% of cases, the cause of infertility remained unexplained. A study conducted in 2023 in Kisangani by Bosenge-Nguma et al. [22] revealed that 31.62% of couples were infertile due to male factors alone, 39.71% due to female factors, and 13.23% due to a combination of male and female factors. In 15.44% of cases, the cause of infertility remained unexplained.

Among women patients, the study identified ovarian factors in 43.21%, uterine factors in 19.69% and tubal factors in 64.63%. The results indicated that ovarian factors were significantly associated with primary infertility. However, the uterine and tubal factors were not found to be associated with any type of infertility. In a similar study, Bosenge-Nguma et al. [22] found ovarian factors among 35.2% of patients, uterine factors among 31.5% of patients and tubal factors among 46.3% of patients. In previous studies in 2019 and 2014 respectively, Nguma et al. [24] in Kisangani and Kalume et al. [23] in Goma found that 67.6% and 73.31% of infertile women had globally a tubal issue, respectively. The analysis of the various causes of female infertility revealed that micropolycystic ovarian dystrophy was significantly associated with primary infertility, while bilateral hydrosalpynx and bilateral tubal obstruction were associated with secondary infertility. Kalume et al. [23] also identified a correlation between tubal obstruction and secondary infertility in eastern Congo. In their study, Yu O et al. [48] observed an increase in the incidence of PCOS in individuals aged 16 to 20 and a decrease in those aged 26 to 30. These findings provide compelling evidence that PCOS begins at an earlier age and can explain its association with primary infertility, as observed in our study.

The investigation of infections among infertile women revealed that 53.54% of the patients had bacterial vaginosis. In their meta-analysis, Noortje et al. [49] estimated the prevalence of bacterial vaginosis (BV) in infertile women to be 19%, with a wide range from 10% in Ireland to 36.6% in Egypt. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Mulinganya et al. [50] found a prevalence of 26.3% during pregnancy in the eastern DRC. The high prevalence observed in this study may be attributed to HRSB found among patients in the study population.

The prevalence of HIV-positive serology was observed in 10.9% of cases, with no statistically significant difference observed between women with primary or secondary infertility. This prevalence is considerably higher than the 0.8% observed among women aged 15 and above in the DRC [51]. However, this prevalence is comparable to that observed among sex workers in Kinshasa, where a prevalence of 7.2% was found [52]. In Rwanda, Dhont et al. [53] observed a higher prevalence of 16% among infertile women. A total of 7.65% of patients exhibited positive serological results for syphilis, with no statistically significant difference observed between women with primary or secondary infertility. This prevalence is higher than that observed at the national level in the DRC (3.3%) among pregnant women, but lower than that observed by Katenga and Maindo in Kisangani (10.9%) [54]. In Rwanda, Dhont et al. [53] observed a prevalence of 6.7%, which is approximately the same as that observed in this study among infertile women and with no statistically significant difference between different types of infertility. As observed in this study, Dhont et al. [53] noted a prevalent HRSB, although this was significantly more common among secondary infertile women. This may be indicative of the high prevalence of HIV and syphilis in our study.

The prevalence of chlamydia-positive serology was observed in 24.67% of cases, with a significant association noted with secondary infertility. In Bukavu, eastern DRC, Mongane et al. [55] observed a prevalence of 23.4% and an association with tubal factor of infertility, which was predominantly observed in cases of secondary infertility.

Following an investigation into TORCH infections, it was observed that 16.63% of cases were positive for toxoplasmosis (TOX), 44.36% for rubella (RV), 15.87% for cytomegalovirus (CMV), 17.21% for herpes simplex I (HSV I) and 15.68% for herpes simplex II (HSV II). No significant association was observed between these infections and any type of infertility. In a study conducted in China by Han et al. [56], the prevalence of TOX, CMV and RV was found to be 3.20% (95% CI: 3.18–3.22%), 77.67% (95% CI: 77.62–77.71%) and 76.03% (95% CI: 75.98–76.07%), respectively. Abdo et al. [57] in the United Arab Emirates reported a prevalence of 12.4% for HSV-2. A multitude of factors, including nutritional status, vaccination status, socio-cultural habits, geographical and climatic variations, route of transmission, age, and others, influence the prevalence of TORCH infections. This is why the prevalence of these infections varies across the globe [56]. Furthermore, in our study, we only considered active infections by looking for IgM antibodies, which may explain the low prevalence of certain TORCH infections in our study.

The study demonstrated that 43.40% of patients exhibited positive anti-sperm antibody results, with no discernible association with any specific type of infertility. This prevalence is slightly higher than that reported by Maindo et al. [33] five years ago in Kisnangi. In the global context, studies have demonstrated that ASA serology is positive in 1–36% of infertile couples. In Egypt, Emad M. Siam et al. [58] found a prevalence rate of 20%, while Adejuno et al. [59] in Nigeria reported a rate of 28.2%. The high prevalence of anti-sperm antibodies (ASA) observed in this study may be related to the high prevalence of infections found during the study. Indeed, previous research has demonstrated a correlation between infections and the production of anti-sperm antibodies [33].

The findings of this study revealed that 44.55% of men exhibited sperm analysis abnormalities, which were significantly associated with primary infertility. The most prevalent abnormalities observed was oligospermia with 21.03% of cases and was significantly associated with primary infertility. In Kisangani, Bosenge-Nguma et al. [23] observed that 44.8% of men exhibited sperm abnormalities, with oligospermia and asthenospermia being the most prevalent abnormalities at 25.6% each. Juakali and Labama [25] observed that 57.6% of men exhibited sperm abnormalities, with oligospermia and asthenospermia being the most prevalent. The discrepancy between our findings and those of Juakali and Labama can be attributed to the fact that the aforementioned studies only considered male taxi-bike drivers. Given that the testicles are more exposed to heat, there is a greater risk of sperm abnormalities.

Conclusion

This study has highlighted the scale of the problem of infertility in couples in the town of Kisangani in the north-east of the DRC. The high prevalence of infertility in Kisangani, with one-quarter of cases being unexplained, demonstrates the seriousness of the situation. In light of the high prevalence of infections, including bacterial vaginosis, and anti-sperm antibodies observed in this study, studies should be conducted to investigate the correlation between unexplained infertility and these factors

Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to be carried out in the DRC. The study was carried out in several health facilities in the same town and involved a full gynaecological consultation, free of charge, in order to ensure access to a large number of patients from all social strata. However, it is important to note that this study has certain limitations. Indeed, the results of this study cannot be generalised to the entire Congolese population, which is a diverse and cosmopolitan group with specific habits and customs that can significantly influence many aspects of infertility in different parts of the country.
