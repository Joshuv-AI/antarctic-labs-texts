Self-healing hydrogel loaded with M2pep-modified recombinant apoferritin enhanced chemotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma via reprogramming tumor-associated macrophage
Authors: Jiayi Qin, Pei Chen, Xin Li, Qingsong Tang, Qingwei Zhao, Haiqin Huang, Yangbo Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy plays an important role in the clinical systemic management of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, the efficacy of chemotherapy is remarkably suppressed by the tumor microenvironment. After observing the prevalent M2-tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) in clinical HCC specimen, we developed a dual protein drug delivery system, namely, genetically engineered M2 macrophage targeting peptide (M2pep) modified recombinant apoferritin (HFn) for the delivery of "old drug" chloroquine (CQ), and bovine serum albumin (BSA) for the delivery of PTX for the synergistic treatment of HCC. To avoid additional efficacy limitations caused by systemic exposure, BSA@PTX and M2-HFn@CQ were incorporated into a self-healing hydrogel (COT@BP/M2HC) composed of carboxymethyl chitosan, oxidized sodium alginate and tannic acid. The experimental results demonstrated this COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel had good biocompatibility and biodegradability, adhesion, injectability and self-healing properties. It was further confirmed that a single peritumoral injection of COT@BP/M2HC successfully promoted the polarization of M2 TAMs to M1 TAMs, and the M1/M2 ratio increased by 4.63 times compared with the BSA@PTX group, thereby achieving 74.7 % tumor growth inhibition in HCC tumor-bearing mouse models. These findings revealed a potential TAM polarizing effect of CQ and provided a reliable combination strategy for improving HCC chemotherapy efficacy.Keywords: COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel, Tumor associated macrophage reprogramming, Apoferritin, Combination therapy, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most frequent primary liver malignancy, ranking third in cancer- related death worldwide (Bray et al., 2024). A considerable number of HCC patients are diagnosed at the advanced stage and are unable to undergo radical surgery, and can only receive targeted therapy, immunotherapy, chemotherapy and combined local treatments such as TACE and HAIC, etc (Yang et al., 2024a). Systemic chemotherapy is not a first-line treatment option due to its poor sensitivity and extremely high toxic side effects (Salem, 2022). However, the development of drug delivery systems (DDS) has brought revolutionary changes to the role of chemotherapy drugs in the treatment of liver cancer, and the therapeutic effect of chemotherapy drugs on HCC deserves further exploration. Recent studies have also shown that some chemotherapeutic drugs can not only play the role of cytotoxic chemotherapy, but also stimulate immune cells, such as paclitaxel and doxorubicin (Zhang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, for HCC, the performance of clinical chemotherapy is significantly compromised due to the presence of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) (Han et al., 2023). Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are critical regulators of immune evasion and tumor progression which are enriched in TMEs, accounting for more than 50 % of all immune cells (Gong et al., 2025a). They can generally be divided to classically activated macrophages (M1 phenotype) and alternatively activated macrophages (M2 phenotype). M1 macrophages activate immune response, generate pro-inflammatory molecules or tumor necrosis factor–α, and normalize tumor blood vessel to exert antitumor activity (Liu et al., 2023). However, most TAMs display an M2 phenotype and promote tumor activity by recruiting immunosuppressive cells and producing inhibitory cytokines such as interleukin-10, transforming growth factor-β and vascular endothelial growth factor. Thus, polarizing M2-TAMs toward the M1 phenotype is a highly-regarded approach to reverse the immunosuppressive TME. Chloroquine (CQ) is an affordable antimalarial drug with long-term clinical use experience and good biosafety. In addition to malaria, CQ has also been widely reported as a potential antitumor drugs due to the interruption of autophagy (Kroemer and Zitvogel, 2020). Recent studies have found that CQ could regulate the metabolic pattern of TAMs, thereby polarizing M2-TAMs to M1 phenotype (Chen et al., 2018). Although TAM repolarization from M2 phenotype to M1 phenotype has shown great potential in colon adenocarcinoma and melanoma (Rodell et al., 2018), there are currently few reports on CQ regulating TAM to improve the sensitivity of liver cancer patients to chemotherapy. Seriously, due to the poor solubility of CQ, this small molecule drug is difficult to target to the tumor site (Lu et al., 2025), resulting in poor therapeutic effect.

The local drug delivery is a desirable approach to increase drug accumulation and meanwhile avoid potential systemic toxicity (Zhu et al., 2024). Regional administration has been explored in the clinic for the treatment of HCC, such as TACE or HAIC (Sutanto et al., 2025). However, the therapeutic efficacy of TACE/HAIC remains suboptimal due to poor drug targeting, necessitating repeated treatments, and significant chemotherapy-related toxicities, with the iodized oil in TACE particularly susceptible to ectopic embolization that severely impairs normal liver function reserve (Peng et al., 2025). Hydrogel systems are another attractive platform for local drug delivery (Xia et al., 2023; Raza et al., 2019). Based on prolonged drug retention and less frequent dosing, excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability (Jie Li et al., 2023; Pansuriya et al., 2024), hydrogel-based vehicle is an ideal local DDS with great potential in the treatment of HCC. Despite these benefits, conventional hydrogel systems often suffer with inadequate tissue targeting specificity and uncontrollable drug release properties.

Nanomaterials hold great potential as drug carriers in cancer therapy (Yalamandala et al., 2024; Moorthy et al., 2025), and many studies have shown that nanocomplex with multiple interactions can enhance drug targeting and control drug release (Huang et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024a), thereby improving therapeutic efficacy (Huynh et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2025).The endogenous apoferritin (HFn) is a natural protein with excellent biocompatibility and becomes a promising vehicle for drug delivery (Liu et al., 2024b). The nanoscale particle size of HFn (approximately 12 nm) enables it to evade phagocytosis and clearance by the human RES system and enhances tumor accumulations via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Meanwhile, HFn can specifically bind to transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) overexpressed in malignant liver cancer cells, thereby achieving deep tumor penetration and subsequent transfer to endosomes and lysosomes. Studies have also demonstrated that HFn has good stability, structural plasticity, and functional diversity, and can be efficiently expressed in Escherichia coli, with low cost and large-scale production (Li et al., 2025). HFn has been widely applied in the delivery of various therapeutic drugs, including chemotherapeutic drugs, gene drugs, and contrast agents. Research have shown that M2 macrophages express high-resolution receptor type B (SR-B1) on their surface which can be specifically recognized by M2 macrophage targeting peptide (M2pep) (Zheng et al., 2025). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is the most abundant protein in human blood and widely used in drug delivery due to its advantages such as easy purification and low cost (Zhang et al., 2025). In addition, it was demonstrated that the therapeutic efficacy of drug-incorporated BSA nanoparticles (NPs) was significantly enhanced due to favorable tumor targeting capability of BSA compared to conventional PTX (Liu et al., 2021). Therefore, we selected BSA@PTX as a chemotherapeutic agent, but the chemotherapeutic effect in HCC still needs further evaluation. Based on the multiple advantages of HFn and BSA, the dual protein-based self-healing hydrogel may be an ideal chemotherapy adjuvant, which can significantly improve the efficacy and prognosis of HCC treatment.

To verify this hypothesis, the distribution of TAMs in TME was characterized in HCC clinical tissues initially. Next, we innovatively designed an M2pep-conjugated recombinant HFn nanocarrier (M2-HFn) for CQ targeted delivery by genetic engineering (Scheme 1). The M2-HFn@CQ NPs may be primarily targeted to tumor tissues via the TfR1 receptor. Then, the M2-peptide chemical modification enabled the targeting of CQ to M2 macrophages in TME, and once internalized into the cell, M2-HFn@CQ NPs could escape from lysosomes via the proton sponge effect, followed by releasing CQ into the cytoplasm to effectively polarize M2 macrophages to M1 type. In addition, PTX was incorporated BSA NPs (BSA@PTX) as a chemotherapeutic agent. To further improve the retention of M2-HFn@CQ and BSA@PTX NPs in tumors, we prepared a self-healing hydrogel (COT) composed of carboxymethyl chitosan(CMCS), oxidized sodium alginate(OSA) and tannic acid (TA). The in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated this COT@BP/M2HC self-healing hydrogel not only enhanced the retention of NPs and controlled release of drugs, but also contributed to the improvement of synergistic therapeutic effects. Our work suggested that repolarization TAMs was an effective strategy to improve the therapeutic sensitivity of chemotherapeutic drugs against HCC via dual protein-based self-healing hydrogel with excellent targeting and biocompatibility, which may address the challenges of other chemotherapy-insensitive tumors with potential for future clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

pET30a (+) plasmid and M2pep(YEQDPWGVKWWY) were constructed by GenScript (Nanjing, China). Imidazole and Nickel sulfate hexahydrate were provided by Macklin Biochemical Co. (Shanghai, China). Sodium Alginate (Mw 98,412.83–110,335.5 Da, viscosity: 200 ± 20 mPa.s, G/M ratio: 1) was purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Paclitaxel, Carboxymethyl Chitosan (Mw 100,000-200,000, degree of substitution 87–90 %) and Tannic Acid were ontained from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Bovine serum albumin, LB Broth, Kanamycin, Sodium dodecyl sulfate, Tris and Ni-NTA resin were purchased from SanGong Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 rapid staining solution, Calcein-AM, Propidium iodide and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Dulbeccos Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and Fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Wuhan servicebio technology CO., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Human primary antibody CD86 and CD163 were provided by Beijing Zhong Shan-Golden Bridge Biological Technology CO., Ltd. (Beijing, China). FITC fluorescent dyes, Mouse Primary antibody iNOS, Arg-1, CD86, CD206 and Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) HD constant dye kit were provided by Pro-teintech Group Inc. (Wuhan, China). Alexa Fluor 594-coupled goat anti-rabbit IgG was obtained from Abcam (MA, USA).

Characterization and the expression of TAMs in HCC clinical tissue

Clinical samples were collected from patients with a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of HCC by surgical resection who had not received any tumor-related treatment before the diagnosis. Specimen was collected for H&E and immunohistochemistry analysis. The samples were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The patients signed an informed consent form, and the experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The human studies were carried out in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Preparation and characterization of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogels

Preparation and characterization of BSA@PTX NPs

BSA were dissolved in 100 mL water, and PTX solution was slowly dripped into BSA solution (BSA: PTX weight ratios of 10:1, 20:1 and 30:1) and followed added anhydrous ethanol under stirring at 200 rpm at room temperature for 30 min. Then, the excess ethanol was removed using a rotary evaporator at 37 °C. and the mixture was dialyzed to remove the unloaded PTX against deionized water for 24 h (MWCO 8000–14,000 Da). Finally, the BSA@PTX NPs was obtained after freeze drying. The NPs were disrupted with ethanol solution and sonicated for 20 min to determine the content of PTX in the NPs. The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 8000 rpm for 15 min and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading were calculated according to the following formula.

The particle size and Zeta potential of BSA@PTX NPs were investigated by a particle and zeta potential analyzer (Malvern, U.K.). In addition, the particle size distributions on days 1, 3, 5 and 7 were measured to examine the stability of the BSA@PTX NPs. The morphology of the BSA@PTX NPs was visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Thermo Scientific, USA). The BSA@PTX NPs solution was added to a dialysis bag (MWCO 8000–14,000 Da) and immersed in 20 mL of pH 7.4, pH 6.8, pH 5.0 PBS solution (containing 30 % ethanol solution) and incubated with rotation at 100 rpm at 37 °C. The dissolution medium (1 mL) was collected at a set time interval and then replaced by 1 mL fresh medium. The amounts of PTX released was measured by HPLC.

Preparation and characterization of M2-HFn@CQ NPs

The M2-HFn protein nanocarrier was obtained from the E. coli protein expression system. Briefly, the pET30a (+)/M2-HFn recombinant plasmid was constructed by modifying the gene encoding the M2-type macrophage-targeting peptide (M2pep) on the 5′ end of the coding sequence of the Ferritin heavy chain gene by genetic engineering technology. The screened plasmid was transformed into BL21 (DE3) E. coli competent cells by CaCl2 transformation method, and positive engineering bacteria were selected by kanamycin-resistance experiment. These engineering bacteria were cultured in LB- kanamycin-containing medium with 1 M IPTG solution at 20 °C,150 rpm for 6 h. Then the bacterial cell was harvested after centrifugation at 4 °C, 8000 rpm for 10 min, and ultrasonically disrupted in an ice bath by the cell ultrasonic disruptor (400 W, 1 s, 2 s, 20 min). The disrupted bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 4 °C 10000 rpm for 35 min. The supernatant was retained and incubated in a 60 °C water bath for 20 min to remove any impurity precipitate. Ultimately, the target M2-HFn protein was purified by nickel column affinity chromatography with a gradient imidazole eluent of 25–100 mM. The molecular weight of M2-HFn was characterized by SDS-PAGE.

The pH-dependent depolymerization-recombination property facilitate drug encapsulation in recombinant HFn easily (Jiang et al., 2020). In this study, CQ was encapsulated into M2-HFn through pH depolymerization method. M2-HFn was depolymerized by adjusting the pH of this M2-HFn solution to 2.0 with 0.1 M HCl. The M2-HFn solution and CQ PBS solution were mixed and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Then, pH value of the mixture was adjusted to 8.0 using 0.1 M NaOH solution to recover the cage structure of M2-HFn and achieve the M2-HFn@CQ NPs. The particle size of M2-HFn@CQ NPs was determined by DLS and the morphology was observed by TEM. Additionally, the stability of the M2-HFn@CQ NPs was examined by measuring the particle size distributions on days 1, 3, 5 and 7. Subsequently, the in vitro release of CQ was investigated in PBS solutions.

Preparation and characterization of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogels

Sodium periodate was served as an oxidant to synthesize oxidized sodium alginate (Emami et al., 2018). Specifically, 0.5 g of SA was dissolved in 50 mL of purified water and reacted with 0.54 g of sodium periodate solution for 6 h in the dark. Then, 0.75 mL of ethylene glycol was added to the mixed solution and neutralized the remaining sodium periodate. Next, the reacted solution was dialyzed in a dialysis bag (MWCO 3500 Da) for 72 h and lyophilized to obtain OSA. The FT-IR spectra of SA and OSA were measured. 1 g of CMCS was dissolved in 10 mL of purified water and then mixed with 2 mL of 2 % tannic acid solution, 2 mL of 5 % OSA solution to form a blank hydrogel (COT). COT@BP/M2HC hydrogels was prepared by mixture of BSA@PTX and M2-HFn@CQ with the blank gel solution. The morphology of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The adhesion and injectability of this COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel were investigated. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G") were determined on a rheometer (Malvern, UK) by frequency and the shear strain scanning. The prepared hydrogel was separated into 4 portions and then placed back into the container and self-healing property of the hydrogel observed after 5 min. The swelling behavior and dissolution properties were also analyzed.

Cell experiments

In vitro cytocompatibility and cytotoxicity assay

The biocompatibility of BSA and M2-HFn was determined using the CCK-8 assay. 100 μL of HepG2 or RAW264.7 cell suspension was incubated in DMEM medium containing 10 % FBS in a 96-well plate and placed in an incubator at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. The culture medium was discarded and replaced with fresh medium containing BSA or M2-HFn. After culturing for 48 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for another 4 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm through Varioskan Flash (Thermo Scientific, USA) to calculate the cell viability. Further, the cytotoxicity of BSA@PTX NPs on HepG2 cells and that of M2-HFn@CQ on RAW264.7 cells were evaluated by the CCK-8 assay described above, respectively. The concentration of PTX in the culture medium was 0.01–4 mM, and the concentration of CQ was 0.02–1000 nM.

Live/dead staining was used to analyze the biocompatibility of the hydrogels. The hydrogel extracts were prepared according to previously reported methods (Amiryaghoubi et al., 2020). The hydrogels were immersed in 10 mL of DMEM at 37 °C for 24 h and filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to obtain sterile hydrogel extracts. HepG2 or RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in confocal petri dishes and 1 mg/mL, 5 mg/mL, and 10 mg/mL of hydrogel extracts were added, respectively. After incubation for 48 h, the cells were stained with Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) and then visualized using inverted fluorescence microscope. In addition, the cytotoxicity of the hydrogels was evaluated by live/dead staining. HepG2 cell suspensions in DMEM medium were placed in confocal petri dishes. PTX, BSA@PTX NPs, blank hydrogels, or COT@BP hydrogels were added to the culture medium, and cell viability was evaluated according to the above method. Further, the cytotoxicity of COT@BP hydrogels on HepG2 cells were evaluated by the CCK-8 assay described above. The concentration of PTX in the culture medium was 1 mM and hydrogel extracts were prepared as described above.

Wound-healing analysis

Cell scratch assay was used to determine the inhibitory effect of PTX on HepG2 cells. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells each well in 37 °C, 5 % CO2 incubator for 24 h. A 200 μL pipette tip was used to make horizontal line in the center of every well. Subsequently, HepG2 cells were co-incubated with PBS, PTX, BSA@PTX, and blank gel extract for 2 h and the scratched width was measured at 12 and 24 h using a microscope (CKX53, Olympus Corporation, Japan). Cellular uptake assay

To evaluate the active targeting ability of M2-HFn NPs on M2-TAMs, RAW 264.7 cells were inoculated in confocal petri dishes, and IL-4 (40 ng/mL) was added to stimulate the polarization of RAW 264.7 cells into M2 macrophages, while LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) were added to induce RAW 264.7 to polarize into M1 macrophages as a control. FITC-labeled M2-HFn was added to the above dishes and incubated for 8 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde solution at room temperature for 10 min. The cellular nucleoli were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, and the uptake of cells in different polarization states was observed by CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica, Germany). BSA was also stained with FITC and FITC- BSA@PTX NPs were prepared as described above. HepG2 cells were inoculated in confocal petri dishes and treated with FITC-BSA@PTX NPs for 4, 12, or 24 h, respectively. The cellular uptake of BSA@PTX NPs in the HepG2 cells was estimated as mentioned above.

Macrophage polarization detection

To assess the reprogramming effect of drugs on macrophage phenotype, we used immunofluorescence to detect the expression of characteristic proteins in M1 and M2 macrophages. RAW264.7 cells were inoculated at a density of 1 × 104 in confocal culture dishes. When the cell density reached about 70–80 %, LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) were added to induce RAW 264.7 to polarize into M1 macrophages as a M1 control, while PBS treated cells were also used as the control. IL-4 (40 ng/mL) was applied to induce RAW 264.7 cells for 24 h and then cell suspensions were stimulated with free CQ, and M2-HFn@CQ for 48 h. The cells were subsequently washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde solutions. After incubation with PBS solution containing 0.2 % Triton X-100 for 10 min, the cells were blocked with a closure solution containing 1 % BSA for 2 h. Subsequently, cells were stained with primary antibody (iNOS, Arg-1) overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-coupled goat anti-rabbit IgG for 1.5 h at room temperature. Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min. The expression of macrophage polarization was imaged by CLSM, and the proportion of positive cells were analyzed with Image J software.

Animals experiments

In vivo antitumor effect evaluation

BALB/c mice (18–20 g) were selected to perform the in vivo animal experiments. 1 × 106 HepG2 cells were injected subcutaneously into the dorsum of BALB/c mice to establish a HCC tumor mouse model. The animal studies were conducted according to the ARRIVE guidelines. Animal experiments were approved by the Tab of Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. When the tumor reached the volume of ∼150 mm3, the tumor bearing mice were randomly divided into 5 groups and peritumorally injected at the location of HepG2 tumors with a single dosage of PBS, blank hydrogel, M2-HFn@CQ, BSA@PTX, and COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel. The animal body weight and tumor size were also monitored every day. After 9 days of treatment, the mice were sacrificed on day 10, and the tumor tissues were excised and weighed. The isolated tumors were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde solution for 48 h and followed by dehydration, embedding, sectioned and stained with H&E for histological evaluation. For the immunofluorescence (IF) staining analysis, post-treatment tumors were repaired by immersion in boiling citrate buffer solution and then covered with primary antibody (CD206 or CD86). Sections were treated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the cellular nuclei were counterstained with DAPI in the dark. The stained sections were observed by CLSM and semi-quantitative analysis was determined using Image J software.

In vivo biosafety evaluation

To evaluate the systemic toxicity of the treatment strategy, major organs including hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were collected on day 10 after treatments described above for histological analyses with H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software with two-tailed Student's t-test. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Distribution of TAMs in the TME of HCC patients

TME plays a critical role in the chemotherapy insensitivity against HCC (Zhao et al., 2023). TAMs are the main components of the tumor suppressive microenvironment, and a disproportionate ratio of M2/M1–TAMs may contribute to the insensitivity of HCC to conventional chemotherapy in clinical practice. To determine the geographical distribution of TAMs in HCC microenvironment, liver cancer samples were surgically resected and collected from patients diagnosed with HCC (Fig. 1A-B). Immunohistochemistry was used to detect tumor-associated macrophage biomarkers in the biopsy specimens. As shown in Fig. 1C, increased CD68+ cells indicated the macrophage infiltration in HCC tumors, and the strong expression of M2-TAMs marker (CD163) was detected in the HCC tissue, indicating that M2-macrophages are prevalent in the TME of HCC (Fig. 1D). Based on these clinical analysis results, we designed a therapeutic nanomedicine as a chemotherapeutic adjuvant to reverse immunosuppression in HCC and exert a potent antitumor effect against HCC.

Preparation, characterization and in vitro cytotoxicity of BSA@PTX NPs

Abraxane® is an albumin-based PTX nanoparticle formulation that is clinically available for the treatment of patients with regionally advanced or metastatic cancers (Murphy et al., 2025). Although Abraxane enhances the effectiveness of PTX compared to Taxol® (Cremophor EL-based PTX), Abraxane exhibits only a moderate enhancement in pharmacokinetic properties (Kratz, 2008). In addition, Abraxane requires the organic solvent and high-pressure homogenizer to prepare lyophilized nanoparticle. In fact, BSA is a natural serum protein that has been extensively explored in drug delivery due to its non-biotoxicity, water solubility and non-immunogenicity (Huang et al., 2019). Therefore, BSA was applied to physically encapsulate PTX to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

When the ratio of BSA: PTX (W/W = 10–30) was increased, the particle size of BSA@PTX NPs was increasing and the potential was decreasing. The highest encapsulation efficiency (87.09 ± 1.62 %) and drug loading (4.17 ± 0.07 %) was observed when the ratio was 20:1. Therefore, the weight ratio of this BSA@PTX NPs to BSA: PTX was 20:1 in the subsequent experiments. As depicted in Fig. 2A-B, the spherical morphology of the BSA@PTX NPs was observed by TEM, and its size was about 100 nm, which was consistent with that determined by DLS (105.12 ± 1.36 nm). At this time, the PDI of BSA@PTX NPs was 0.163 ± 0.012 nm and the zeta potential was −20.67 ± 1.45 mV. The drug release of PTX was determined by dialysis method. In order to investigate the release profile of BSA@PTX NPs, the NPs were exposed to different pH PBS solutions mimicking physiological (pH 7.4) and acidic (pH 6.5 or pH 5.0) environment. As shown in the Fig. 2C, the drug release rate of PTX from BSA@PTX NPs increased with pH decreasing. The release amount within 12 h was 1.8 and 1.4 times higher than that of pH 7.4 under the conditions of pH 5.0 and pH 6.5, respectively, indicating that BSA@PTX NPs possessed the acid sensitive release property, which is rapidly released in the tumor microenvironment, and can release drugs more quickly after entering the cells to exert anti-tumor effects. Moreover, the particle size was measured by DLS to analyze the stability of BSA@PTX NPs. The NPs particle size remained stable within 7 days (Table 1), with a particle size of 107.62 ± 0.45 nm, and the PDI at 7 days was 0.203 ± 0.011. As a result, these NPs were easy to manufacture and had enhanced drug loading capacity and stability, which was contributed to improved anti-tumor efficacy.

Albumin-based drug delivery systems have been developed because of their prolonged blood circulation time and high drug accumulation capability in tumors. As shown in Fig. 2D-E, the cellular uptake of PTX was evaluated with FITC-labeled BSA@PTX NPs by CLSM. When HepG2 cells was incubated with FITC-labeled BSA@PTX NPs, the green fluorescence of NPs was observed at 4 h, whereas the strongest green fluorescence appeared at 24 h. These favorable properties are mediated by the interaction of the albumin receptor (gp60) with the albumin-binding protein SPARC in the tumor microenvironment and the EPR effect (Battogtokh et al., 2015).The cytotoxicity of BSA against HepG2 and RAW264.7 cells was determined by CCK8 assay. BSA was not cytotoxic to either cell line in the concentration range of 0.25–4 mg/mL (Fig. 2F), demonstrating that blank NPs did not contribute to the cytotoxic effect of BSA@PTX NPs. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 2G, the HepG2 cell viability displayed a decreasing trend with increasing PTX concentration, and the half inhibitory concentration (IC50) was 1.65 mM within 48 h. Given that HepG2 migration plays a significant role in tumor progression, we further performed the inhibitory activity of BSA@PTX NPs on the migration of HepG2 cells by wound healing experiment. As depicted in Fig. 2H-I, the inhibitory activity of BSA@PTX NPs and free PTX on cell migration was significantly higher than that of the control group (P < 0.0001). Furthermore, after 24 h, the inhibitory effect of BSA@PTX on cell migration was significantly higher than that of free PTX(P < 0.05), and the cell migration rate of the control group was about 3 times that of PTX NPs. Therefore, we deduced that PTX NPs may be able to effectively inhibit tumor growth.

Preparation, characterization and reprogramming function of M2-HFn@CQ NPs

Although HFn is widely applied as a drug carrier because of its tumor cell targeting properties in cancer therapy, its macrophage targeting remains to be improved. M2 macrophage-targeting peptide (M2pep) was conjugated to the surface of HFn nanocages to construct M2-HFn NPs with active M2 macrophage targeting ability using genetic engineering technology. As shown in Fig. 3A, the recombinant plasmid pET-30a (+)/M2-HFn was obtained by cleavage of the pET-30a (+) plasmid with Nco I and Not I enzyme and then under the action of T4 DNA ligase. The engineering bacteria of pET-30a(+)/M2-HFn were inoculated into the medium with 0.1 M IPTG at 20 °C, 150 rpm for 16 h, and the supernatant and precipitate resuspension of broken bacteria were collected and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The recombinant fusion protein was successfully expressed as there was no band in the self-expressed protein of the empty host bacterium, which was soluble and had a molecular weight of about 27 kDa (Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, the target protein was isolated and purified with different concentrations of imidazole eluent, and the results showed that the target M2-HFn protein was effectively eluted from the nickel column, and achieved a good purification in 100 mM imidazole eluent.

The encapsulation efficiency of CQ in M2-HFn@CQ NPs was determined to be 88.54 ± 3.21 %, and the drug loading was 8.32 ± 1.87 %. Subsequently, the CQ released from M2-HFn@CQ NPs were investigated under physiological and acidic conditions. As shown in Fig. 3E, 4.86 % of CQ was released from NPs in pH 7.4 after 6 h, while the release of CQ was significantly (P < 0.001) increased (52.53 %) in pH 5.0 and almost completely released after 48 h, suggesting that the NPs displayed a pH-dependent release behavior and are expected to achieve controllable drug delivery in the slightly acidic environment of tumors. The pH-sensitive CQ release is due to the fact that M2-HFn could disassemble and open the nanocage under acidic conditions. Previous studies have shown that the natural protein HFn has good biocompatibility (Gong et al., 2025b). As expected, the cell viability of HepG2 and RAW 264.7 cells treated with M2-HFn for 48 h were greater than 90.0 %, demonstrating that the M2-HFn NPs have significant biosafety even at a high dosage of 8 mg/mL (Fig. 3F). However, in the presence of drug CQ, the survival rate of RAW 264.7 cells in the free CQ group decreased significantly to 66 %(P < 0.01), while in the M2-HFn@CQ NPs group was still maintained at 90 % at the CQ concentration of 20 nM (Fig. 3G). An excellent delivery system not only requires to minimize the cytotoxicity caused by the delivery vehicle but also to accumulate effectively in tumor tissues. HFn can selectively target tumor tissues through TfR1-mediated specific binding (Zhang et al., 2021). With further modification of M2pep, NPs have ability of internalization M2-TAMs rapidly via M2pep ligand binding effect (Tian et al., 2021). We next evaluated the cellular uptake of M2-HFn in IL-4 activated RAW264.7 cells using CLSM. LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells were incubated with FITC-labeled M2-HFn as a control to trace the endocytosis process. As displayed in Fig. 3H, after 8 h of incubation, the green fluorescence intensity observed in IL-4-activated RAW264.7 cells was higher than that in LPS-activated RAW264.7 cells, indicating that the NPs have a higher affinity for M2-RAW264.7 cells due to the M2pep, thereby showing the ability to target and deliver the drug to M2-macrophages.

The excellent phagocytosis property of M2-HFn@CQ NPs on M2-TAMs laid a strong foundation for subsequent repolarization experiments. M2 TAMs are prevalent in TME of HCC that exert the immunosuppressive effects, making it difficult to improve chemotherapy sensitivity. RAW264.7 cells was employed to validate the polarization of M2 phenotype to M1 phenotype by M2-HFn@CQ NPs under CLSM. PBS and IL-4 groups were taken as controls. M1 and M2 macrophages were identified by evaluating the expression levels of Arg-1 and iNOS. Fig. 3I-J showed that M2-HFn@CQ NPs dramatically elevated the expression of iNOS compared with the control group (P < 0.001), a typical marker of M1 phenotype macrophages while significantly decreased the expression of Arg-1 compared with the IL-4 group (P < 0.05), a typical marker of M2 phenotype macrophages. The positive rate of M1 phenotype in the M2-HFn@CQ NPs group was 11.4 ± 0.8 %, which was 4.5-fold and 1.2-fold higher than that of the IL-4 group and free CQ group, respectively. These results confirmed that M2-HFn@CQ NPs showed a superior performance in the repolarization of the M2 TAM to the M1 TAM and potentially enhance the efficacy of PTX.

Preparation, characterization of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogels and in vitro antitumor effect

Although several protein-based delivery systems have been developed, only a few have been investigated in oncology clinical trials (Mohanty et al., 2023). In general, they dissociate easily in the blood and rapidly eliminated by the mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS) or renal filtration, thereby leading to reduced therapeutic efficacy and increased toxicity. Given the exceptional drug storage capacity and sustained-release properties of hydrogels (Wu et al., 2024), this study aimed to leverage the advantages of hydrogels in improving the therapeutic effects of chemotherapeutic drugs by developing functional hydrogels encapsulating protein-based NPs. CMCS and SA, natural polysaccharides with good biocompatibility and degradability, have been widely applied in constructing hydrogels (Yang et al., 2024b). Therefore, in this study, as shown in Fig. 4A, SA was oxidized to OSA by sodium periodate to generate aldehyde groups which then cross-linked with the amino groups in CMCS to form imine bonds to construct an injectable hydrogel and integrated with tannic acid (TA), a natural polyphenol known for its unique molecular structure that facilitate hydrogel adhesion to tumor tissues through hydrogen bonds. BSA@PTX and M2-HFn@CQ NPs were further incorporated to develop COT@BP/M2HC engineered hydrogels to promote tumor attachment and prevent their clearance, thereby improving the efficacy of comprehensive treatment.

Compared with the SA spectrum, the new characteristic peak at 1752 cm−1 appeared in FTIR spectrum of OSA, which corresponded to the stretching vibration absorption peak of the aldehyde group (Hajiabbas et al., 2020), indicating the successful synthesis of OSA (Fig. 4B). SEM images (Fig. 4C) showed that the COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel consisted of a well-connected microporous network, which enabled the hydrogel to fully absorb NPs to protect the drugs from leakage but maintain controllable drug release. In addition, the fact shown in Fig. 4D provided evidence for the adhesion and injectable property of the hydrogel. We also examined the rheological behavior of the hydrogel (Fig. 4E-F). For the oscillating strain sweep, hydrogel was applied with different strains with a fixed angular frequency, the storage modulus (G') of the hydrogel was always higher than the loss modulus (G"), indicating excellent structural stability and elasticity. For the frequency sweep, the value of G consistently exceeded G″ over a wide range of frequencies. The improvement in storage modulus was mainly attributed to the covalent/noncovalent interactions in the gel matrix, including hydrogen bonding with –OH in TA and dynamic Schiff base bonding reactions with amino groups in CMCS and aldehyde groups on OSA (Tu et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025). Due to the dynamic Schiff base and hydrogen bonds mentioned above, the hydrogels showed self-healing properties (Ding et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 4G, the sliced four semicircle hydrogels healed into an integral hydrogel within 5 min. The swelling capacity of the hydrogel was evaluated to assess its water solubility and bio-environment adaptability, thereby providing favorable conditions for prolonging the retention time. As shown in Fig. 4H, the swelling rate of the hydrogel continued to increase and finally reached about 416 % after 2 h, showing excellent hydrophilicity. The slow degradation of hydrogels helps to release drugs continuously and reduce the number of injections. Herein, we investigated the dissolution behavior of hydrogel and found that it dissolved slowly within the first three days and gradually degraded by more than 90 % within 9 days (Fig. 4I). This suggested that COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel had a tighter network structure which could resist dissolution and maintain long-term stability. Considering the secondary pain caused by frequent intravenous injections, this hydrogel was expected to have good potential in anti-tumor applications, laying the foundation for subsequent in vivo.

For potential applications in localized tumor drug delivery, COT hydrogels are required to possess not only favorable mechanical properties but also excellent biocompatibility. So, the impact of COT hydrogels against HepG2 and RAW264.7 cells was investigated by live/dead staining experiment. Green fluorescent signals represented living cells and red represented dead cells. Fig. 4J revealed that the majority of HepG2 cells cultured with COT hydrogels emitted green fluorescence and maintained normal morphology. The same result was observed for RAW264.7 cells cultured in COT hydrogels even at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, confirming the good cytocompatibility of the COT hydrogel carrier. Then, the cytotoxicity of COT@BP hydrogel against HepG2 cells was examined. The results exhibited that the red fluorescence signal of HepG2 cells was enhanced and the green fluorescence signal was weakened in all drug-treated groups (Fig. 4K). COT@BP hydrogels exhibited powerful killing effect on HepG2 cells, as demonstrated by decreased number of live cells and increased number of dead cells, severe disrupted cellular morphology, and massive formation of necrotic aggregates, with the cell viability being only 42.0 % ± 3.9 % as determined by CCK-8 assay (Fig. 4L).

In vivo therapeutic, TAMs repolarization and biosafety evaluation

To determine whether CQ enhanced the anticancer effect of the chemotherapeutic drug PTX, we carefully explored the tumor suppression of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel in vivo by establishing HCC subcutaneous mouse model. The H22 tumor cells were subcutaneously injected into the dorsum of Balb/c mice to construct a subcutaneous HCC mouse model. Fig. 5A exhibited the treatment and monitoring scheme. Mice with similar tumor volumes (∼150 mm3) were randomly divided into five groups and received one peritumoral injection treatment of various treatments including PBS, blank hydrogel, M2-HFn@CQ NPs, BSA@PTX NPs, and COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel. The dosage of CQ and PTX was 75 and 15 mg kg−1, respectively.

The tumor size was recorded every day. At the end of the experiment, the relative tumor volume of mice in the COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel group was significantly smaller than that in the BSA@PTX group (P < 0.05), M2-HFn@CQ group (P < 0.01), and PBS group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, all mice were sacrificed to remove the tumors. The tumor tissues were weighed to calculate the tumor inhibition rate. As shown in Fig. 5D, compared with the PBS group, both the COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel and the BSA@PTX group revealed tumor inhibition effects in the HCC subcutaneous tumor model. In contrast, the COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel showed a higher tumor treatment effect than the BSA@PTX group, with the tumor inhibition rate increased to 74.7 % (P < 0.05), while the tumor inhibition rate of M2-HFn@CQ NPs was only 36.6 % (P < 0.01). Images of enbloc dissected tumors showed a similar trend (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the collected tumor tissues were stained with H&E to assess the therapeutic effects. As shown in Fig. 5F, tumor sections collected from PBS or COT hydrogel groups showed a large population of tumor cells and no obvious cell apoptosis. Compared with the PBS group, although monotherapy induced tumor histological damage, the combination therapy of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel resulted in the most severe tumor cell apoptosis. These results confirmed that M2-HFn@CQ could synergistically improve the chemotherapeutic efficacy of BSA@PTX in promoting cancer tissue apoptosis by combining with COT hydrogel.

As one of the most important immunosuppressive cell subsets, M2 TAMs play a complicit role in tumor progression by reducing the sensitivity of anti-tumor chemotherapy. To further confirm the synergistic antitumor effect of the M2-HFn@CQ on BSA@PTX in liver cancer, tumor-associated macrophages in the tissues were stained with anti-CD206 and anti-CD86 antibodies to label M2-type macrophages and M1-type macrophages, respectively. Fig. 5G exhibited representative images of tumor-associated macrophage markers, showing that changes in the number of tumor macrophages occurred after various treatments. CD86+ cells in the COT@BP/M2HC group showed higher regional density, while CD206+ cells were the most abundant in the PBS and COT groups. The number of M1-TAMs in the COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel group increased dramatically, of which 78.65 % were M1-type TAMs, an increase of 3.36 times compared with the PBS group (P < 0.01) and a significant increase of 116.51 % compared with the BSA@PTX group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5H). The M2-type TAMs biomarker CD 206 (Fig. 5I) decreased accordingly (P < 0.05), suggesting that TAMs in the tumor have been successfully reprogrammed. Besides, we calculated the M1/M2 ratio (Fig. 5J) and verified that the ratios of CD86 and CD 206 signals in the COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel group were 8.62, 7.62, 2.54, and 4.63 times higher than those in the PBS, COT, M2-HFn@CQ and BSA@PTX groups, respectively. These above results demonstrated that peritumoral injection of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel is a promising strategy to ehance the efficacy of chemotherapy in the treatment of liver cancer.

Additionally, no significant variation in animal body weight was observed in each group throughout the treatment (Fig. 5B). H&E stained sections revealed no obvious histological damage to the main organs of the mice (Fig. 6), indicating that the combination treatment strategy had good biocompatibility and no remarkable toxicity.

Conclusions

This study based on the prevalent M2-TAMs in the TME of clinical HCC biopsy specimens, focused on the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and proposed an innovative synergistic combination strategy for enhanced chemotherapy in HCC. We firstly prepared a dual-protein drug delivery system, one applied M2 peptide-modified HFn for CQ delivery and the other employed BSA for PTX delivery. Then, a hydrogel composed of CMCS, OSA and TA was constructed for the co-delivery of BSA@PTX and M2-HFn@CQ NPs. This hydrogel had multiple advantages, such as good biocompatibility and biodegradability, adhesion, injectability and self-healing properties, that were capable of enhancing the long-term retention and sustained release of dual-protein NPs. The M2-HFn@CQ NPs was primarily targeted to tumor tissues through the TfR1 receptor, and successfully internalized into M2-TAMs with the help of M2-pep to achieve secondary targeting and escape from the lysosome. After entering the cytoplasm, TAM function-regulating drug CQ was released, resulting in enhanced anti-tumor effect of PTX. In addition, BSA@PTX NPs were contributed to the improved efficacy of PTX which could target HCC and achieve pH-controlled drug release via BSA NPs. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel effectively reduced tumor cell migration and stimulated the reprogramming of M2-TAMs to M1 TAMs, thus overcoming the poor response of chemotherapy drugs in HCC. More importantly, a single peritumoral injection of COT@BP/M2HC hydrogel significantly improved the therapeutic effect with the tumor suppression rate increased to 74.7 % compared with BSA@PTX monotherapy (P < 0.05). Currently, there is no uniform consensus on the comprehensive treatment of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma. The positive role of chemotherapy in treating liver cancer has long been ignored due to the serious toxic side effects and poor targeting capabilities. In summary, this work demonstrated that the dual-protein self-healing hydrogel drug delivery system can significantly enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy for liver cancer with good biocompatibility and delivery efficiency, indicating that after addressing these two issues, chemotherapeutic agents can achieve favorable antitumor effects in liver cancer treatment. This provides a new option for comprehensive treatment strategies in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma and holds promise for reshaping the current landscape of advanced liver cancer therapy. We believe such proteins and carbohydrate polymers could serve as a powerful modality for drug delivery, cell transplant, or even tissue engineering, which may help address the challenges of other chemotherapy-insensitive or immunotherapy-resistant tumors and have great potential for clinical translation in the future.
