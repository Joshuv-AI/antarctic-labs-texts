A 45-Year-Old Man With Dyspnea and Hypoxemia With Bilateral Pulmonary Infiltrates
Authors: Claire A. Grosgogeat, Melissa Griffith, Carlyne Cool, Bridget A. Graney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case PresentationA 45-year-old man presented with dyspnea and acute on chronic hypoxemic respiratory failure with progressive bilateral pulmonary infiltrates after surgical lung biopsy.His past medical history was pertinent for antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) treated with systemic anticoagulation. This diagnosis was made after aortic valve replacement for an aortic valve mass. Histopathology of the mass was consistent with a thrombus (Libman-Sacks endocarditis).Shortly after his diagnosis of APS, he developed progressive dyspnea and chest pain. CT imaging demonstrated bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, and review of prior available imaging confirmed these changes had been present during the evaluation period of his aortic mass.These symptoms progressively worsened and he presented to an outside hospital where he was found to have acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. He underwent bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and was diagnosed with bland pulmonary hemorrhage related to anticoagulation and was empirically treated with prednisone after infection was excluded. He improved clinically and symptomatically but his abnormal imaging findings were persistent on hospital follow-up with his pulmonologist. It was recommended that he undergo surgical lung biopsy for definitive diagnosis of his persistent pulmonary infiltrates. He was discharged home the day after his biopsy on 3 to 4 L/min of supplemental oxygen.Two days post-operatively, he presented with worsening dyspnea and hypoxemia. CT chest scan demonstrated worsening bilateral infiltrates. Notably, he decreased his dose of prednisone 1 week before the biopsy. He was transferred to our hospital for further evaluation.

Physical Examination Findings

On arrival to the emergency department, the patient was afebrile with a heart rate of 74 beats/min, respiratory rate of 16 breaths/min, blood pressure of 129/83 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 92% on 6 L/min supplemental oxygen.

He was not in distress and did not have increased work of breathing or conversational dyspnea. His pulmonary examination revealed bibasilar crackles. Cardiac rate and rhythm were normal; the skin did not have rashes or bruising on exposed areas; there was no lower extremity edema; and neurologically, the patient was awake, alert, and oriented with no obvious deficits.

Diagnostic Studies

On admission to our hospital, we obtained and reviewed his prior evaluation including histopathology slides for reexamination.

His initial coagulability and serologic workup revealed a normal activated partial thromboplastin time (28.7 seconds), positive beta-2 glycoprotein I IgG antibody (> 112 U/ml), positive beta-2 glycoprotein I IgA antibody (28.3 U/ml), positive phosphatidylserine antibody (> 100 U/ml), positive cardiolipin antibody IgA (34.7 APL-U/ml), and positive cardiolipin antibody IgG (> 110 GPL-U/ml). All other autoimmune serologies were negative.

CT angiography pulmonary embolism protocol at this presentation demonstrated diffuse, bilateral multifocal ground-glass and nodular opacities without central cavitation (Fig 1). Review of his prior CT imaging confirmed multifocal patchy ground-glass and peripherally based consolidative opacities bilaterally. What is the diagnosis?

Diagnosis: Recurrent diffuse alveolar hemorrhage due to APS

Discussion

Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) is a clinicopathologic syndrome of pulmonary microvasculature hemorrhage with resultant RBC accumulation within the alveolar space due to disruption of the alveolar-capillary basement membrane. It typically presents with hemoptysis, anemia, diffuse lung infiltrates, and acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. Importantly, hemoptysis can be absent in roughly one-third of cases. Systemic vasculitis with involvement of the pulmonary capillaries (capillaritis) is the most common cause of DAH. Other etiologies of DAH include bland pulmonary hemorrhage, isolated pulmonary vasculitis, and diffuse alveolar damage.

Compared with other systemic autoimmune diseases with resultant capillaritis, DAH due to primary APS is rare. However, it is an often-fatal complication of APS, particularly given the therapeutic challenges of pulmonary hemorrhage in hypercoagulable patients requiring systemic anticoagulation. These competing factors commonly lead to delayed diagnosis. Patients are diagnosed with bland pulmonary hemorrhage due to systemic anticoagulation, rather than APS-induced capillaritis. DAH is more common in catastrophic APS than primary APS (approximately 12% vs 1-2% incidence, respectively). DAH is also now included within the clinical domain (domain 3: microvascular) for diagnosis of APS based on the 2023 American College of Rheumatology and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology APS Classification Criteria.

The pathophysiology of DAH in APS is not completely understood but is presumed to be an antibody-mediated capillaritis, possibly due to antiphospholipid antibodies inducing endothelial cell activation with resultant fibrinoid necrosis of the alveolar-capillary basement membrane. Another proposed mechanism is microvascular thrombosis with resultant alveolar hemorrhage. Capillaritis is more commonly identified than microvascular thrombosis on histopathology, but both are described.

Although less common than other autoimmune etiologies, APS should be on the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with DAH. Patients should be evaluated for signs and symptoms of APS including unexplained venous thromboembolic events such as pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis, stroke, arterial thrombotic events, obstetric complications, and valvular disease. If any of these are evident, serologic workup for APS including lupus anticoagulant, cardiolipin, and beta-2 glycoprotein antibodies should be obtained, and the individual referred to rheumatology for further evaluation.

A definitive diagnosis of DAH can be made with bronchoscopy or lung biopsy. With bronchoscopy, serial BALs are performed in the same wedged location. Diagnosis is confirmed by a rising RBC count in serial aliquots. Serial aliquots can be falsely negative in subacute or recurrent DAH. In addition, the yield of serial aliquots decreases over time, and the best diagnostic value occurs within the first 48 hours of hemorrhage. In cases of subacute or recurrent DAH, it may be helpful to identify hemosiderin-laden macrophages in pooled lavage specimen centrifugate. Typically, lung biopsy is reserved for determining the etiology of DAH—confirming the presence or absence of capillaritis—rather than making the initial diagnosis of hemorrhage. It can also be useful when there is a high suspicion of DAH despite negative serial aliquots. Histopathology demonstrating hemosiderin-laden macrophages confirms the diagnosis of DAH. Specimens should be closely examined for features of capillaritis as the causative etiology of DAH because this has important diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic implications.

Treatment of DAH is targeted at the underlying condition. For patients with capillaritis, immunosuppression is used to address and control the autoimmune destruction of the alveolar-capillary membrane. First-line therapy is high-dose corticosteroids. Second-line therapy is a steroid-sparing agent to achieve long-term disease control. Despite aggressive immunosuppression, relapse of DAH in APS is common, especially with triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies. Management of DAH from APS is often further complicated by systemic anticoagulation or thrombocytopenia due to APS.

Clinical Course

After further evaluation, this case demonstrates that BAL can be negative for DAH. Furthermore, this case demonstrates that, in patients for whom there is a high clinical suspicion for capillaritis, surgical lung biopsy should be performed to confirm the underlying etiology. The patient’s prednisone was increased to 0.5 mg/kg/d. In collaborative care with rheumatology, he was started on rituximab for maintenance immunosuppressive therapy. With attempts to taper prednisone, he had recurrent hospitalizations for hypoxemia and pulmonary infiltrates concerning for ongoing active capillaritis and resultant hemorrhage. Due to lack of disease control with rituximab and prednisone, IV immunoglobulin was added to his treatment regimen. He is undergoing a very slow prednisone taper and has not had recurrent hemorrhage with the combination of rituximab and IV immunoglobulin. Follow-up imaging has shown complete resolution of multifocal ground-glass opacities and ground-glass nodules, compatible with treatment response.

Clinical Pearls

1. Primary APS can lead to capillaritis and DAH; therefore, APS should remain on the differential diagnosis as an etiology of DAH in a person suspected of or diagnosed with APS who has diffuse or patchy opacities on chest imaging. 2. Diagnosing DAH in primary APS requires high suspicion. Hemoptysis is often absent. A BAL that confirms DAH cannot distinguish capillaritis from bland hemorrhage, which is a major diagnostic challenge in anticoagulated patients with APS. A bronchoscopy often suffices for a presumptive diagnosis, but only surgical lung biopsy can definitively diagnose pulmonary capillaritis. 3. Early recognition and multidisciplinary involvement, especially rheumatology, is crucial to effective management of DAH due to APS.
