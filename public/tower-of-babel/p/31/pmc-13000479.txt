Association between Sugar-sweetened beverage consumption and gallstones: A cross-sectional analysis from NHANES 2017–2023
Authors: Xin Wang, Chenao Zhang, Junpeng Xiong, Qiao Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) are a major source of added sugars and may be related to gallstones, yet the quantitative relationship has not been well characterized. To evaluate the association between SSB consumption and gallstone prevalence, assess potential non-linear dose–response patterns, and exploratorily examine subgroup differences.MethodsWe analyzed 9001 U.S. adults in National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017–2023. SSB exposure was derived from two 24-h dietary recalls; gallstones were ascertained by self-report. Survey-weighted logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) with progressive covariate adjustment. Non-linearity was tested using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were also performed.ResultsEvery 100 kcal/day increment in SSBs was associated with 10% higher odds of gallstones (adjusted OR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.05, 1.15). Compared with non-consumers, ≥300 kcal/day related to an 81% higher odds (OR = 1.81; 95% CI: 1.37, 2.40). RCS indicated a significant non-linear relationship (P nonlinearity = 0.04). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses yielded directionally consistent results with the primary analysis.ConclusionsHigher SSB intake was positively and non-linearly associated with gallstone prevalence in this cross-sectional analysis. Longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm temporality and clarify underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Gallstones are a common gastrointestinal condition that imposes a substantial burden on global health systems and economies (Stinton et al., 2010). In the U.S., nearly 800,000 cholecystectomies are performed annually, accounting for $6.5 billion in healthcare costs (Wang et al., 2024a). Although often asymptomatic initially, 10%–20% of cases develop major complications such as biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, choledocholithiasis, pancreatitis, and cholangitis (Gupta et al., 2024). Worldwide, cases increased from 127 million (1990) to 193 million (2019), with parallel rises in mortality, underscoring the need for effective prevention (Li et al., 2022). Established risk factors include female sex, older age, pregnancy, physical inactivity, higher body mass index (BMI), and greater total energy intake (Lammert et al., 2016). Notably, obesity and dietary excess are modifiable and therefore represent important considerations for disease prevention.

With shifting dietary patterns, widespread SSB intake has become an important driver of excess energy consumption (Nguyen et al., 2023). SSBs encompass beverages with added/free sugars—including sodas, non-100% fruit drinks, sports/energy drinks, sweetened dairy products, and packaged tea/coffee (Sousa et al., 2020). Between 1990 and 2021, high consumption of SSBs among youth exhibited a substantial global increase, rising from 6.58% to 11.13% (Ge et al., 2025). Over the same period, overweight and obesity increased across most countries and regions (Collaborators GBDAB, 2025). Frequent SSB intake is linked to reduced satiety and higher energy intake, promoting weight gain and metabolic disturbances (e.g., insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome) and elevating cardiovascular risk (Calcaterra et al., 2023; Imamura et al., 2015). These metabolic consequences provide a plausible biological basis for an association between SSB intake and gallstones. Supporting this hypothesis, a large U.S. prospective cohort study reported that higher ultra-processed food intake was associated with a higher incidence of gallstones, with SSBs as a key contributor and obesity as a potential mediator (Uche-Anya et al., 2024).

Despite indirect evidence, few studies have quantified the association between SSB intake and gallstone prevalence in a nationally representative sample. To address this gap, we analyzed NHANES 2017–2023 to examine the association between SSB intake and gallstone prevalence and to conduct exploratory subgroup analyses across key demographic and metabolic strata. These findings provide population-level evidence to support hypothesis generation and to inform future longitudinal research on SSB intake and gallstone burden.

Methods

Study design and population

NHANES is a nationally representative survey administered by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)and approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. We used NHANES 2017–2023 because gallstones items were fielded only in these cycles. Initially, 27,493 participants were identified. Exclusions were: <20 years or pregnancy; missing gallstone status; missing SSB intake data; or missing prespecified covariates. Measures

Ascertainment of gallstones

Gallstones were ascertained based on self-reported clinician diagnosis. Specifically: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had gallstones?”

Measurement of SSB

SSB intake was estimated from up to two 24-h dietary recalls; when both were available, we used their mean as a pragmatic measure of short-term intake (Wang et al., 2024b; Herrick et al., 2018). This measure is subject to substantial day-to-day variability and may not accurately reflect individuals' usual long-term dietary patterns. Reported items were standardized using United States Department of Agriculture food and nutrient databases. In this study, SSBs were defined as a broad category including regular soda, non-100% fruit drinks, sports drinks, sweetened dairy beverages, smoothies, grain-based drinks, flavored carbonated water, and sweetened coffee or tea (Alves-Costa et al., 2024). These beverages share the common feature of containing added free sugars, and this definition was used to assess total intake of sugar-containing beverages (Herrick et al., 2018). Energy and nutrients were computed from reported amounts paired with NCHS nutrient composition data. Dietary assessment procedures follow NHANES manuals and analytic guidelines (see official documentation).

Measurement of covariates

Covariates were grouped a priori into five domains informed by prior evidence (Lammert et al., 2016). Demographics: age, sex, BMI, race. Socioeconomics: education, marital status, poverty-income ratio (PIR). Clinical: self-reported diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD). Behaviors: smoking and alcohol use. Nutrition: total energy intake. Diet was assessed using two 24-h recalls across the 2017–2023 cycles; mean intake across recalls was analyzed. Detailed acquisition methods are described in the NHANES Survey Methods Manual and Analytic Guidelines.

Statistical analysis

We compared baseline characteristics by gallstone status. All analyses applied dietary survey weights (wtdr2d) to yield nationally representative estimates. Continuous variables were summarized as weighted medians (IQR, 25th–75th) and tested with survey-adjusted procedures. Categorical variables were shown as counts with weighted percentages and compared using survey-weighted nonparametric tests (e.g., Kruskal–Wallis).

Associations between SSBs and gallstones were estimated using survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression. We specified three models: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, PIR, marital status); Model 3 (Model 2 plus BMI, smoking, alcohol, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, total energy). To account for potential confounding by overall dietary energy intake (including energy from other food sources, such as grains and protein-rich foods), Model 3 adjusted for total energy intake. Specifically, this model was intended to estimate the energy-adjusted (energy-independent) association between sugar-sweetened beverage intake and gallstones, reflecting differences beyond overall caloric intake. SSBs were modeled continuously (per 100 kcal/day) and categorically (0, 1–299, ≥300 kcal/day). Linear trend across categories was tested. Non-linearity was evaluated with RCS; knot number was chosen by minimizing Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). We conducted exploratory subgroup analyses to assess potential effect modification and interpreted interaction tests cautiously given multiple comparisons. Additionally, we conducted the following sensitivity analyses: (1) fitting an alternative model without adjustment for total energy intake; (2) repeating the primary model using a redefined three-category SSB intake classification; (3) performing multiple imputation for missing covariates using the “mice” package and refitting the regression models across the imputed datasets; and (4) repeating the analyses after excluding participants with implausible total energy intake. Analyses used R 4.5.0; two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Compared with non-cases, participants with gallstones were more often female, ≥60 years, less educated, had lower PIR, and were more frequently widowed/divorced/separated. They also had higher BMI, were more commonly former smokers and non-drinkers, and more often had diabetes and CVD. Notably, total energy intake was lower in the gallstone group. Association of SSB intake with gallstones

The association between sugar-sweetened beverage intake and gallstones was evaluated using survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models (Table 2). In the fully adjusted Model 3, each 100 kcal/day higher intake was significantly associated with higher odds of gallstones (OR 1.10; 95% CI 1.05, 1.15). In categorical analyses, intake ≥300 kcal/day (vs 0 kcal/day) was significantly associated with higher odds of gallstones (OR 1.81; 95% CI 1.37, 2.40), whereas the 1–299 kcal/day category was not statistically significant (OR 1.22; 95% CI 0.94, 1.58). A significant linear trend was observed across intake categories (P for trend <0.01). We then applied a three-knot RCS to test non-linearity (Fig. 2). The fully adjusted RCS indicated a significant positive non-linear relationship (P nonlinearity = 0.04). Curve showed a positive monotonic association between SSB intake and the odds of gallstones, characterized by a sharper rise at lower intake levels followed by a gradual plateau. Sensitivity analysis

The main findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S1–S4). In the model without adjustment for total energy intake, SSB intake remained statistically significantly associated with gallstones (per 100 kcal/day: OR 1.10; 95% CI 1.05, 1.15). Using multiple imputation for missing covariates yielded statistically significant and comparable estimates in the fully adjusted model (per 100 kcal/day: OR 1.08; 95% CI 1.03, 1.13). Results were also consistent using the redefined three-category SSB intake groups, with the highest category (>240 kcal/day vs 0) remaining statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (OR 1.99; 95% CI 1.46, 2.73). After excluding implausible total energy intake reporters, the highest intake category (≥300 vs 0 kcal/day) remained statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (OR 1.82; 95% CI 1.37, 2.42).

Discussion

In a nationally representative cross-sectional sample of 9001 U.S. adults, SSB consumption was positively associated with gallstones. Each 100 kcal/day higher intake was associated with higher odds of gallstones (adjusted OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.15), and compared with non-consumers, ≥300 kcal/day was associated with higher odds (OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.37,2.40). Findings were robust to covariate adjustment and sensitivity analysis. Overall, these cross-sectional findings indicate an association between SSB intake and gallstones, which should be evaluated in longitudinal studies to clarify temporality.

To our knowledge, this is the first NHANES analysis directly evaluating the SSB gallstones link with formal subgroup and interaction tests. Results align with prior literature while adding novel elements. Recent work has highlighted associations between highly processed, high-sugar diets and gallstone pathogenesis. For instance, Uche-Anya et al. found higher gallstone risk with greater UPF intake (HR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.24, 1.36), implicating SSBs—especially in younger women (Uche-Anya et al., 2024). Likewise, NHANES 2017–2018 analyses linked high-carbohydrate dietary patterns to gallstones (OR = 1.329; 95% CI: 1.326, 1.331), reinforcing sugar-related pathways (Zhang et al., 2024). Unlike prior work, we isolate SSBs as the exposure and visualize non-linearity via RCS, addressing a key gap.

Our findings are also consistent with growing evidence linking SSB consumption to adverse metabolic and health outcomes. Hur et al. found that women who consumed ≥2 servings of SSBs daily had more than double the risk of early-onset colorectal cancer before age 50 compared to those consuming <1 serving per week (RR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.10, 4.35) (Hur et al., 2021). Likewise, the Framingham Heart Study reported a 2.5-fold higher risk of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) among participants consuming SSBs at least once per week (OR = 2.54; 95% CI: 1.36, 4.73), independent of weight change (Park et al., 2022). Another NHANES-based study further distinguished between sugary and artificially sweetened beverages, showing that even non-caloric sweeteners may be associated with a higher prevalence of NAFLD, possibly due to residual obesity or microbiome-related mechanisms (Tseng et al., 2023). Together with our findings, its broader metabolic correlates and epidemiologic relevance merit further evaluation in longitudinal studies.

Several biological pathways have been proposed in prior literature that may help contextualize the observed association between sugar-sweetened beverage intake and gallstones. Excessive SSB consumption promotes energy surplus and obesity, which in turn contribute to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, both of which are recognized risk factors for gallstones (Tseng et al., 2023). These metabolic abnormalities impair gallbladder motility, increase bile cholesterol supersaturation, and promote biliary stasis, all of which facilitate cholesterol crystal formation (Teff et al., 2009; Aeberli et al., 2011; Malik and Hu, 2011). Uche-Anya et al. further quantified that 12%–39% of the SSB–gallstone association may be mediated by obesity, supporting our proposed mechanism (Uche-Anya et al., 2024). Additionally, high-fructose beverages may stimulate hepatic de novo lipogenesis, elevate triglyceride and Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, and alter bile composition (White, 2013). Emerging evidence also suggests that SSB-induced chronic low-grade inflammation and gut dysbiosis may disrupt enterohepatic bile acid circulation, further exacerbating gallstones risk (Grosso et al., 2022; Muli et al., 2021). These mechanisms remain speculative in the context of the current study, and longitudinal and experimental investigations are needed to clarify their relative contributions.

Traditionally, dietary guidance for gallstones has focused primarily on limiting high-fat and high-cholesterol foods (Madden et al., 2024). In our study, SSB intake, a potentially underappreciated dietary factor, was significantly associated with gallstone prevalence. This association was statistically significant in the overall sample and directionally consistent across most subgroups, providing additional support for the robustness of the main findings. Dose–response analyses suggested increasing odds with higher intake, with elevations observed even at lower intake levels, and no clear threshold was identified in the present analysis. However, due to sparse data at higher intake levels, the confidence intervals at the extremes of the spline curve were wide, and inferences for these ranges should be interpreted cautiously. These findings may be influenced by exposure measurement error and partial missing data, and they warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts using more precise usual-intake assessment methods. Notably, although a association was observed among adults with diabetes, the interaction term reached only borderline statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously in the context of multiple subgroup comparisons. Rather than indicating a definitive effect modification, this finding is best viewed as hypothesis-generating and may help inform future targeted investigations.

This study offers several strengths. It is based on a large sample with detailed dietary data. Comprehensive adjustment for confounders improves the validity of our estimates, and the use of RCS modeling provides novel insights into dose–response patterns. Nonetheless, Several limitations should be noted. (1) The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. SSB intake reflects recent exposure, whereas gallstone status was based on self-reported lifetime diagnosis, leading to temporal mismatch and possible reverse causation; the pattern of lower total energy intake but higher SSB consumption among gallstone cases may reflect post-diagnosis dietary modification or differential reporting. (2) Gallstones were identified by self-reported physician diagnosis; although widely used in NHANES, asymptomatic cases may be underdiagnosed, and reporting may vary by sex or age, resulting in potential differential misclassification. (3) Because SSB consumption is episodic, averaging two 24-h recalls may not fully capture usual intake. The NCI method was not applied, and residual within-person variability may have caused exposure misclassification and attenuation bias. Future studies should incorporate multiple dietary assessments or apply usual-intake modeling approaches to more accurately evaluate the long-term association between SSB consumption and gallstone risk. (4) SSBs were analyzed as a broad category without distinguishing beverage subtypes, which may introduce exposure heterogeneity and non-differential measurement error. (5) Total energy intake may function as both a confounder and a mediator. In a cross-sectional design, these pathways cannot be readily disentangled, so the findings should be interpreted in an integrative manner. (6) Finally, the conclusions of this study are based on the U.S. adult population. Of 27,493 NHANES participants, only 9001 adults were included after exclusions and missing data, which may have introduced selection bias and limited generalizability. Therefore, the applicability of the findings to other populations with different dietary patterns or genetic backgrounds may be limited.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study observed a significant positive association between SSB intake and gallstone prevalence, with evidence of a non-linear pattern. These findings highlight SSB intake as a potentially relevant dietary exposure for further investigation, pending confirmation in longitudinal studies. Prospective and mechanistic studies are warranted to establish temporality and clarify underlying mechanisms.
