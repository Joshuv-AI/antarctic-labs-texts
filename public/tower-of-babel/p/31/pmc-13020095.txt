Development and validation of a risk score for serious infection in patients with rheumatic diseases receiving prolonged high-dose glucocorticoids
Authors: Se Rim Choi, Ju Yeon Kim, You-Jung Ha, Jin Kyun Park, Eun Ha Kang, Eun Young Lee, Yun Jong Lee, Eun Bong Lee, Jun Won Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough previous studies have demonstrated that high-dose glucocorticoids (GCs) significantly increase the risk of infection in patients with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases (AIRDs), the actual risk for individual patients varies depending on other clinical factors. This study aimed to develop and validate a scoring system for identifying patients at high risk of serious infection following high-dose GC treatment for AIRDs.MethodsPatients with AIRDs treated with prolonged (≥ 4 weeks) high-dose GCs (≥ 30 mg/day prednisone) were included from two referral hospitals. Primary outcome was 1-year incidence of serious infection. Cox regression with LASSO regularization was applied to select covariates for the final model from 19 prespecified factors. A scoring system was developed based on β-coefficients of covariates in the final model.ResultsIn the derivation cohort (n = 1635), serious infection occurred in 153 patients, with an incidence rate (per 100 person-years) of 10.5 (95% CI 9.0–12.3). The LASSO regularization identified older age, interstitial lung disease, decreased renal function, anemia, low serum albumin, ANCA-associated vasculitis, and concomitant rituximab and cyclophosphamide as key risk factors. The resulting scoring system, termed the CORAL score (0–20), demonstrated good predictive performance (AUROC 0.726 [95% CI 0.681–0.771]) for serious infections. Based on the score, patients were stratified into low- (score ≤ 6) or high-risk group (score > 6). Patients in the high-risk group (n = 343) had significantly higher rates of serious infection (HR 4.85 [3.53–6.67]) and all-cause mortality (HR 4.55 [2.91–7.11]) than those in the low-risk group. The predictive accuracy of the CORAL score was consistent in the external validation cohort (n = 672).ConclusionIn patients with AIRDs receiving prolonged high-dose glucocorticoids, the CORAL score successfully identified those at high risk of serious infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03756-3.

Introduction

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are essential immunosuppressive agents for the treatment of various autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases (AIRDs) [1]. Despite the advent of immunomodulatory agents targeting specific immune pathways, GCs —especially at high dosages— remain an essential component of treatment to induce remission in many AIRDs [2]. GCs mainly exert immunosuppressive effects by altering the transcription of genes encoding proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, cell adhesion molecules, and key enzymes involved in host inflammatory reactions [3, 4]. Since GC receptors are expressed in a wide range of immune cells, their effect extends across various components of the host immune system.

While GCs potently suppress the abnormal immune responses observed in AIRDs, their broad immunosuppressive effects can compromise the natural defense mechanisms against a wide range of infections [5, 6]. Previous studies have shown that this risk increases with higher dosages, suggesting that high-dose GC treatment for remission induction in AIRDs is a key risk factor for serious infection [7–11]. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the incidence and risk factors of infectious complication in AIRDs patients receiving prolonged high-dose GC therapy, as this knowledge is essential for predicting prognosis and optimizing treatment outcome in daily clinical practice. However, the paucity of studies on this issue makes it difficult to quantify the risk of serious infections in patients with AIRDs.

Therefore, we aimed to identify risk factors for serious infection and to develop a prediction model for patients with AIRDs receiving prolonged high-dose GC treatment. From this model, we derived a clinically applicable scoring system, the CORAL score, to identify patients at high-risk of serious infection.

Methods

Study design and patients

This study used data from two independent cohorts of patients with AIRDs at tertiary referral hospitals in South Korea. Data for the derivation and validation cohorts were extracted from the electronic medical record warehouses of Seoul National University Hospital and Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, respectively. Patients with rheumatic diseases who received high-dose GCs for at least 4 consecutive weeks for the treatment of their rheumatic disease were included. High-dose GC was defined as a daily dose of ≥ 30 mg of prednisone equivalent, based on evidence that such dosages fully saturate genomic GC receptors in humans [1]. Patients with rheumatic diseases were first identified using International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes (Supplementary Text 1). To confirm AIRD diagnoses, experienced rheumatologists (SRC and JWP) performed a detailed review of medical records and prescription data. For individuals assigned more than one ICD-10 codes for AIRD, all available clinical information was reviewed to adjudicate the final underlying diagnosis. GC prescriptions were then evaluated in this population to identify patients who received prolonged high-dose GC therapy. If a patient had multiple episodes of prolonged high-dose GC treatment, only the first episode was included. Patients were excluded if they had malignancy at the time of high-dose GC treatment, a history of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, or were under 18 years of age. Patients with a follow-up duration of less than 28 days after initiation of high-dose GC therapy and those who experienced serious infection during the period were also excluded. Included patients were identified between January 2004 and December 2019 in the derivation cohort and between January 2004 and December 2021 in the validation cohort. Finally, a total of 1635 and 672 patients were analyzed in the derivation and validation cohorts, respectively. The flow of inclusion is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) of Seoul National University Hospital (2009-065-1156) and Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (B-2403-888-101). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the IRB waived the requirement for patient consent.

Data collection

The observation scheme used in this study is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The index date was defined as the starting date of high-dose GC treatment (day 1), and the entry period was defined as days 1–28. The observation period spanned 52 weeks from day 29 onward to ensure that all included patients were exposed to prolonged high-dose GC therapy. Follow-up was censored at the occurrence of the outcome event, the last available follow-up date, death, or 52 weeks from the start of follow-up, whichever came first. Demographic characteristics and clinical information regarding underlying disease, comorbidities, and laboratory findings were collected from medical records at the index date. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease study equation [12]. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) was determined based on the formal radiologic interpretation of chest computed tomography scans performed before cohort enrollment by radiologists at each participating institution. We also collected data on all prescribed medications, hospital admissions, emergency room visits, and deaths during the observation period. Previous GC exposure and cumulative dose were assessed during the 6 months preceding the index date. The initial dose of high-dose GC was defined as the GC dose prescribed at the index date. Concomitant immunosuppressive medication was defined as any medication prescribed during the entry period. GC pulse therapy, defined as 250–1000 mg/day of intravenous methylprednisolone for 1–5 days, was recorded separately. When pulse therapy was administered at the beginning of the entry period, the initial dose of high-dose GC was defined as the GC dose prescribed after completion of the pulse regimen. The patients’ causes and dates of death were obtained from the electronic medical database of each hospital, as well as the Statistics Korea database.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the 1-year incidence of serious infections, defined as infections leading to hospitalization and/or the use of intravenous antimicrobial therapy (Supplementary Table 1). To identify as many cases as possible, we collected data on hospitalization and prescribed intravenous antimicrobial agents to the study population at each center. Also, we reviewed all medical records from the enrolled hospitals to capture serious infection cases that occurred outside of them. Potential cases were initially identified by screening all hospital admissions and emergency department visits in which intravenous antimicrobials were administered. Two investigators (SRC and JWP) independently reviewed all medical records, laboratory data, and therapeutic antimicrobial use for the selected cases. Cases were confirmed as serious infections when both assessors reached consensus. Secondary outcomes included incident infections according to pathogen type (bacterial, fungal, and viral infections), infection-related death, and all-cause death.

Statistical analysis

Based on the fixed sample size in the derivation cohort, we determined the maximum number of parameters that can be included in the prediction model for time-to-event outcomes. With considering the rate of serious infection of 0.13 reported by Stuck, et al., and assuming a Cox-Snell R2 of 0.1, we could maximally include 20 variables in the model, targeting a shrinkage of 10% to minimize overfitting [13]. Some laboratory values contained missing values, with their proportion less than 10% for all variables. Missing values were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbors algorithm.

We performed Cox regression with the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) penalty to select covariates from 19 prespecified candidate predictors (Supplementary Text 2). These candidate predictors were determined by the investigators based on literature review and clinical judgement. Covariates included in the final multivariable model were selected at the one-standard-error penalty (𝝀1se) to favor parsimony. The discrimination performance of the prediction model was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and the area under the curve (AUC) of time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves at 1 year. The calibration performance of the prediction model was assessed using the mean absolute error between predicted and observed probability at day 365.

For better clinical applicability, we developed a scoring system based on the corresponding β coefficients of the final model [14]. Briefly, all β coefficients were standardized by setting the smallest absolute value to 1; the coefficients for the other variables were divided by the smallest absolute β and then rounded to the nearest integer. The final score was calculated by summing the scores of individual covariates. We applied to conditional inference tree analysis to identify an optimal cut-off value of the final score for risk stratification. In addition, we performed decision curve analysis to evaluate the clinical utility of the scoring system for identifying patients at high risk for serious infection [15]. The detailed methodology for decision curve analysis is described in Supplementary Text 3. Threshold probability represents the level of predicted risk at which a clinician would opt to initiate a preventive intervention to reduce the risk of serious infection, despite the potential risk of adverse events. Threshold probabilities between 10% and 20% were evaluated.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the derivation and validation cohorts are presented in Table 1. In the derivation cohort, patient’s mean (standard deviation [SD]) age was 46.1 (16.1) years and 1107 (67.7%) were female. The most frequent underlying AIRD was systemic lupus erythematosus (33.5%), followed by idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) (15.8%), Behçet disease (13.5%), and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody-associated vasculitis (AAV) (11.7%). Their mean (SD) disease duration was 1.8 (4.2) years, and 1140 (69.7%) patients were diagnosed with an AIRD within one year of the index date. The mean (SD) GC dose (prednisone equivalent) was 55.8 (28.0) mg/day, indicating that most patients received substantially higher dose than those specified in the inclusion criteria. Concomitant cyclophosphamide and rituximab were administered to 237 (14.5%) and 58 (3.5%) patients, respectively.

Incidence rate of serious infection

During the 1454.3 person-years (PYs) of follow-up, 163 cases of serious infection occurred in 153 patients (9.4%), with an incidence rate (per 100 PYs) of 10.5 (95% CI 9.0–12.3). Patients with AAV showed the highest incidence rate (23.1, 95% CI 16.4–31.6), followed by IIM (12.5 [95% CI 8.9–17.7]) and other vasculitis (11.1 [95% CI 6.2–20.0]) (Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, patients with Behçet disease (BD) had a significantly lower incidence of serious infection than those with other diseases. These patients were less likely to have ILD, leukopenia, and decreased renal function. The proportion of patients who received cyclophosphamide or rituximab were also significantly lower in those with BD (Supplementary Table 3).

The anatomical sites and causative pathogens of serious infection cases are presented in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5. Bacterial infections were the most frequent (n = 107, 65.6%), followed by fungal (n = 32, 19.6%), and viral (n = 24, 14.7%) infections. Among bacterial infections, lower respiratory tract (n = 50, 46.7%) was the most common anatomical site, followed by skin and soft tissues (n = 15, 14.0%) and gastrointestinal tract (n = 11, 10.3%). Exploratory analyses using chi-square tests showed no significant association between AIRD diagnosis and pathogen group (p = 0.353) or infection site (p = 0.981). The mean (SD) time to infection was 110.7 (98.9) days. Twenty-eight (18.3%) patients died from serious infection during the observation period.

In the validation cohort, a total of 59 serious infection cases occurred during the 584.2 PYs of follow-up, with an incidence rate of 10.1 (95% CI 7.9–12.9). The overall incidence rate did not significantly differ between the two cohorts (P = 0.497), nor among the individual disease subgroups.

Effect sizes of risk factors for serious infection

The effect of each pre-specified clinical factor on the risk of serious infection in the univariable analysis is presented in Table 2. Among demographic features, older age (≥ 60 years) was significantly associated with an increased risk of serious infection. AAV, ILD, leukopenia (< 4000/µL), lymphopenia (< 800/µL), anemia (Hb < 10 g/dL), low serum albumin level (< 3.5 mg/dL), and decreased renal function (eGFR < 60mL/min/1.73m2), were also associated with the higher risk of serious infection. In addition, patients treated with cyclophosphamide or rituximab were more likely to develop serious infections. By contrast, comorbidities such as diabetes, chronic hepatitis, and previous cancer, and initial GC dose at the index date were not significantly associated with the outcome.

We also assessed the impact of each underlying AIRD on the incidence of serious infection. In the univariable analysis, AAV was significantly associated with higher risk of infection (hazard ratio [HR] 2.49 [95% confidence interval [CI] 1.71–3.61]). However, this effect became statistically insignificant after adjustment for age, renal function, and immunosuppressive treatment (adjusted HR 1.46 [95% CI 0.90–2.37]). Likewise, although patients with BD showed a lower risk of serious infection in the univariable analysis (HR 0.30 [95% CI 0.14–0.63]), this effect disappeared after adjustment for other risk factors (adjusted HR 0.56 [95% CI 0.26–1.21]) (Supplementary Table 6).

Development of scoring system to identify high-risk patients for serious infection

LASSO variable selection process involving 19 pre-specified variables identified 8 covariates at the optimal 𝝀1se (Supplementary Fig. 3): age ≥ 60 years, AAV, ILD, anemia, low serum albumin, decreased renal function, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab. These selected variables were subsequently included in the multivariable Cox model (Table 2). The final model showed a good performance (AUROC 0.726 [95% CI 0.681–0.771]) (Fig. 1A). Calibration at 1 year showed a mean absolute error of 0.073, indicating acceptable agreement between predicted and observed survival. Internl validation using bootstrapping also showed acceptable result, with an optimism-corrected AUROC of 0.703 [95% CI 0.665–0.757]). To assess whether model performance differed across clinically relevant subgroups, we evaluated discriminatory ability according to AAV status, ILD status, and use of cyclophosphamide or rituximab. The model showed comparable performance in patients with (n = 191) and without (n = 1444) AAV (AUROC 0.754 vs. 0.699); with (n = 200) and without (n = 1435) ILD (0.724 vs. 0.697); and among users (n = 290) and non-users (n = 1345) of cyclophosphamide or rituximab (0.720 vs. 0.686) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Risk stratification based on the scoring system

The risk of serious infection increased with higher CORAL score in a dose-dependent manner, especially in patients with the score > 6 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Conditional inference tree analysis to identify the optimal threshold score to classify high-risk group also showed the consistent result (Supplementary Fig. 6). Based on the analysis, patients with the CORAL score ≤ 6 (n = 1292, 79.0%) were classified into the low-risk group, and those with > 6 (n = 343, 21.0%) into the high-risk group.

One-year incidence of serious infection in the low-risk and high-risk groups were 6.05 (95% CI 4.73–7.62) and 30.66 (95% CI 24.35–38.10), respectively. The risk of serious infection in the high-risk group was significantly greater than that in the low-risk group (HR 4.85 [95% CI 3.53–6.67]) (Fig. 2A). This result was consistent when each type of serious infection was analyzed as a separate outcome variable (Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, infection-related mortality and all-cause mortality rates during the observation period were also significantly higher in the high-risk group (HR 10.88 [95% CI 4.79–24.71] and 4.54 [95% CI 2.91–7.11], respectively) (Fig. 2B). Finally, decision curve analysis demonstrated that applying the classification system to identify the high-risk group provided greater net benefit than intervening in all patients or in none across the range of predefined threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Sensitivity analysis

Since some clinical factors, such as AAV and rituximab use, could be correlated with each other, we included each of these interaction terms (AAV × rituximab and AAV × cyclophosphamide) in the final model and assessed their effects. In this analysis, neither interactions term was statistically significant (p = 0.413 and p = 0.618, respectively), and the AUROC was also comparable to that of the original model (AUROC 0.727 [95% CI 0.682–0.772] for the AAV × rituximab model and 0.725 [95% CI 0.679–0.770] for AAV × cyclophosphamide model) (Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, to investigate the effect of disease activity, we conducted an exploratory analysis in which baseline ESR and CRP levels were individually incorporated into the final model among patients with available data in the derivation cohort (n = 1167 for ESR, n = 1228 for CRP). In these analyses, the performance of each model was not significantly different from that of original model (AUROC 0.722 for the ESR-adjusted model and 0.716 for the CRP-adjusted model) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Finally, because the performance of the final model might have been influenced by prophylactic trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), we reassessed the model after excluding patients who received prophylaxis (n = 197). In this sensitivity analysis, the AUROC was 0.724 (95% CI 0.675–0.774), which was consistent with the original analysis.

External validation of the CORAL score

In the validation cohort, 1-year incidence of serious infection in the low- and high-risk groups were 6.39 (95% CI 4.25–9.24) and 17.90 (95% CI 12.13–25.34), respectively. The final model also showed a favorable performance with an AUROC of 0.763 (95% CI 0.700–0.826) (Fig. 1B). Similar to the analysis in the derivation cohort, the risk of serious infection increased in a dose-dependent manner with higher scores. Patients in the high-risk group (n = 192, 28.6%) also had a significantly greater risk for serious infection compared with those in the low-risk group (HR 3.15 [95% CI 1.89–5.26]) (Fig. 3A). In addition, the between-group difference in all-cause mortality was consistent with that observed in the derivation cohort (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

Although infection is one of the leading causes of death in patients with AIRDs receiving intensive immunosuppressive treatment, it is challenging for physicians to assess the risk of serious infection in these patients [9, 16]. This complexity arises because various factors, including comorbidities, age, and treatment regimens, may contribute to the risk [8, 17, 18]. In this study, we developed and validated a novel scoring system to predict the risk of serious infections in patients with AIRD receiving prolonged high-dose GCs using readily available factors in daily clinical practice.

This study showed that the 1-year incidence of serious infection in our study population was approximately 10%, which was higher than that reported in previous studies [13–15]. In these studies, the incidence of serious infection varied widely, ranging between 2.3 and 9.1 per 100 PYs [17–20]. Many clinical factors may contribute to this variation. Importantly, the risk of infectious complication is AIRDs is highest during the early phase of the disease, when intensive immunosuppressive therapy is administered to stabilize disease activity [20–22]. Accordingly, we defined the observation period as the induction period, with prolonged high-dose GC serving as an essential component in most cases.

Since the actual risk of serious infection in individual patients is determined by multiple factors with varying impacts, we developed a risk scoring system that quantifies the effect of each factor based on the β coefficient from the final prediction model. The CORAL score, which is composed of eight readily accessible clinical variables, demonstrated a favorable performance and successfully identified patients at high risk of serious infection after the prolonged high-dose GC treatment. The annual incidence of serious infection in the high-risk group was approximately 30%, representing a 5-fold higher rate than in the low-risk group. This result demonstrates the clinical applicability of our scoring system, indicating that intensive monitoring and preventive strategies should be considered for patients identified as high risk. Furthermore, the CORAL score demonstrated comparable performance in the independent validation cohort, thereby minimizing the risk of overfitting.

Notably, most predictors in the CORAL score reflected co-morbid conditions and immunosuppressive treatment; only AAV represented an underlying AIRD variable. Although reported incidence rates of infection vary across AIRDs, previous studies have not thoroughly accounted for difference in treatment intensity and comorbid conditions [20]. In our analysis, the underlying AIRD was not an independent predictor of serious infection after adjustment for these factors. Furthermore, the effect sizes of other covariates were substantially greater than that of AAV in the final model. These findings suggest that, from a clinical perspective, the risk of serious infection in individual patients is more strongly influenced by comorbid conditions and treatment regimens rather than by underlying disease itself. They also suggest that the CORAL score can be applied to patients with various AIRDs in daily clinical practice. However, to establish a more accurate and clinically relevant model, the effects of disease-specific clinical factors should be further investigated in future large-scale studies. In addition, although the relatively higher weight assigned to ILD compared with the small contribution of AAV and rituximab treatment may be explained by the uniformly administered prolonged high-dose GC treatment in our study population, these findings warrant further validation.

Interestingly, we found that the CORAL score could predict not only serious infection, but also all-cause mortality in the study population. Serious infections may be associated with the long-term prognosis of AIRD patients by deteriorating their clinical course, triggering the exacerbation of underlying AIRDs, or hindering appropriate immunosuppressive therapy for the underlying disease [23–25]. This association is closely intertwined with the disease activity of AIRDs; patients requiring aggressive immunosuppression for severe AIRDs are more predisposed to serious infections, and subsequent infections may lead to suboptimal treatment. Recent cohort studies including patients with AAV have provided further support for this notion, indicating that infections occurring during the early stages of the disease substantially elevate the risk of 1-year mortality 2–4 folds [26, 27].

However, although our scoring system accurately identified patients at high risk of serious infections and mortality, the optimal preventive strategies for these patients remain unclear. Notably, a few recent studies have suggested that prophylactic use of TMP-SMX against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia lowers the incidence of serious infections in patients with AAV [28–30]. In contrast, the performance of our prediction model did not change after excluding patients who received prophylactic TMP-SMX. Unlike previous studies, our study population showed heterogeneity in underlying diseases, comorbidities, and concomitant immunosuppressive agents. Therefore, the impact of prophylactic TMP-SMX may differ depending on specific clinical factors, warranting further investigation in future studies. In addition, another recent study has shown that prophylactic dose of azithromycin reduced respiratory infection in patients with primary antibody deficiencies [31]. Although it is uncertain whether similar efficacy can be achieved in patients with AIRDs, this approach also warrants further investigations, considering that the lower respiratory tract was the most common site of serious infection in the study population.

This study has some limitations. First, although the CORAL score was well validated in the independent cohort, both cohorts were composed exclusively of patients from South Korea. Therefore, further validation studies including patients with diverse ethnicities and nationalities are necessary. Second, although previous studies have suggested that hypogammaglobulinemia, low CD19 + B cell counts, or low CD4 + T cell counts are associated with the development of severe infections, these factors were not addressed [29, 32–34]. However, because these laboratory tests are not routinely performed in daily practice, their inclusion as covariates could limit the feasibility of the prediction model. Third, there is a possibility of bias due to unmeasured confounding factors owing to the retrospective nature of our study. For instance, data on body weight, and vaccination status were not available. In addition, information regarding hospitalization or administration of intravenous antimicrobials at other hospitals may not have been captured in our database, which could have led to an underestimation of incidence of severe infection in the study population. The result of this study could also be influenced by time-varying factors. For example, the risk of infection may be affected by subsequent dosing schedule of GC after the entry period. Although we intentionally included only baseline clinical factors to enhance clinical applicability, not accounting for subsequent dose modifications could have introduced bias, underscoring the need for further investigation in future studies. Finally, our combined analysis may not fully capture disease-specific variation in infection risk. The number of serious infection events within individual disease subgroups was insufficient to support disease-specific analyses in the current study. Future studies with larger, disease-specific cohorts will be needed to validate the model.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed the CORAL scoring system and validated its performance in identifying patients at high risk of serious infection after prolonged high-dose GC treatment for AIRDs. Although this scoring system warrants further validation, its implementation could potentially help physicians estimate the risk of serious infections among patients with AIRDs in daily clinical practice.
