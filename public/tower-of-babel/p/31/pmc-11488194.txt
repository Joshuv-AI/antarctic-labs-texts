Beyond trauma: a review of content and linguistic characteristics of nontrauma narratives in posttraumatic stress disorder
Authors: Aubrey A Knoff, Jennifer J Vasterling, Mieke Verfaellie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Using narratives to reflect on experiences, emotions, and thoughts is associated with better health, enhanced mood, and improved symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Prior research examining narrative characteristics thought to reflect cognitive styles associated with PTSD has focused on trauma narratives, but the characteristics of nontrauma narratives in relation to PTSD are not fully understood.Objective: We reviewed the PTSD literature examining linguistic characteristics of nontrauma narratives, focusing on affective content, personal pronouns, and cognitive processing words.Method: We searched online databases for both laboratory and social media studies examining these characteristics of nontrauma narratives in relation to PTSD diagnostic status and/or PTSD symptom severity.Results: Following SWiM guidelines [Campbell et al., 2020. Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM) in systematic reviews: Reporting guideline. British Medical Journal, 368, l6890], there was moderate evidence for differential use of emotion words in nontrauma narratives in relation to PTSD symptom cluster severity. More severe avoidance/numbing symptoms were associated with greater use of negative emotion words and less use of positive emotion words. Results were mixed for other linguistic elements reviewed.Conclusions: Differential use of emotional language in trauma narratives generalises to nontrauma narratives in individuals with PTSD. Additional research is needed to elucidate the use of personal pronouns and cognitive processing words in nontrauma narratives.KEYWORDS: PTSD, nontrauma narrative, narrative characteristics, linguistic characteristics, linguistic inquiry and word count

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is associated with maladaptive appraisals of the world and self, characterised by heightened sensitivity to threat and negative self-evaluations. Negative cognitive styles surface in information processing biases (Vasterling & Arditte Hall, 2018) such as selective attention to threat (Naim et al., 2015), overgeneral autobiographical memory (e.g. Sutherland & Bryant, 2008), and interpretation of ambiguous stimuli as threatening (Amir et al., 2002; Elwood et al., 2007). Maladaptive appraisals may also manifest in the verbal expressions of people with PTSD. How individuals use narratives to reflect on experiences is important, as descriptions of negative experiences that are better integrated with the self are associated with improved health outcomes in healthy adults (Pennebaker, 1997; Smyth, 1998). Moreover, constructing narratives about specific, positive memories has been associated with better health and enhanced mood in healthy adults (Burton & King, 2004) and attenuation of PTSD symptoms (Contractor et al., 2020a), particularly when narratives are vivid and coherent (Contractor et al., 2020b). Thus, how both negative and positive experiences are translated into language may influence well-being.

In relation to PTSD, narrative content has been examined primarily in descriptions of trauma memories. Evidence suggests, however, that aspects of everyday language (e.g. use of emotion words) are related to psychosocial and physical wellbeing (Karan et al., 2017; Vine et al., 2020). Thus, examining narratives beyond trauma memories potentially expands understanding of thought processes that characterise PTSD, ultimately identifying possible treatment targets within a variety of narratives. The purpose of this synthesised review was to examine linguistic expressions of nontrauma narratives in PTSD. We first briefly review studies examining trauma narratives, as their results guided our examination of nontrauma narrative characteristics.

Trauma narrative characteristics

Trauma memories associated with PTSD elicit a ‘here and now’ quality (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Cognitive models suggest that trauma memories in PTSD lack context and are poorly integrated into autobiographical memory, rendering emotional and perceptual aspects of the memory more prominent and non-volitional (Brewin, 2014; Ehlers & Clark, 2000). As such, greater recall of sensory and perceptual details and heightened negative emotionality measured by negative emotion word usage are associated with greater PTSD symptom severity and/or PTSD diagnosis (Crespo & Fernández-Lansac, 2016). Verbal expressions of trauma memories in PTSD also contain more first-person singular pronouns (Kleim et al., 2018; Son et al., 2021), possibly representing general distress and negative emotionality (Tackman et al., 2019). Cognitive processing content, reflecting effort to mentally analyse and integrate experience, also holds implications for narrative generation in PTSD. The trauma literature is somewhat mixed, but largely suggests that greater use of cognitive processing words is related to fewer PTSD symptoms (Jaeger et al., 2014; Jelinek et al., 2010) and greater symptom improvement over time (Kleim et al., 2018; Procaccia & Castiglioni, 2022). Such heightened emotional, sensory, cognitive processing, and self-referential features of trauma-related memories in PTSD may reflect a focus on negative and threatening content in attention, memory, and interpretation.

Extension of trauma memory characteristics to nontrauma narratives

Negative appraisals of the world and self in people experiencing PTSD may also characterise nontrauma narratives. For example, the retrieval of nontrauma memories could be altered via inappropriate assimilation of new information to fit previously held schemas, resulting in unhelpful beliefs (e.g. self-blame), or via modification of existing schemas to accommodate trauma information, leading to inaccurate or overgeneralised views (Resick & Schnicke, 1993). Similarly, narrative features typical of trauma memories may characterise future-oriented nontrauma narratives generated by people experiencing PTSD because memory provides possible content for imagining future events (Schacter & Addis, 2007).

Guided by findings from the trauma literature, our objective was to characterise PTSD-associated linguistic expressions in nontrauma narratives that are related to conceptualizations of PTSD focused on cognitive styles characterised by negative thinking and appraisals (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Specifically, we examined objective scoring protocols quantifying affective content (i.e. positive and negative emotion words), first-person pronouns reflecting self-related processing, and cognitive processing words relating to insight, causal thinking, and mental flexibility. In addition to traditional laboratory-based studies encompassing narratives differing in cued valence (i.e. positive, negative), we reviewed studies using social media. Social media studies offer access to unrestricted narratives, thus potentially broadening knowledge about verbal expression associated with PTSD to more naturalistic settings. We included studies comparing individuals with and without PTSD diagnoses and those examining relationships between PTSD symptom severity and narrative content, reflecting the dimensionality of posttraumatic stress responses (Brancu et al., 2016). We hypothesised that PTSD-associated linguistic features of trauma memories would also characterise nontrauma narratives and predicted the presence of more negative emotion and first-person pronouns, and fewer cognitive processing words in nontrauma narratives in relation to PTSD.

Methods

Literature review

We searched Google Scholar and Dimensions databases. In Google Scholar, we paired ‘PTSD’ with: ‘non-trauma memories’, ‘negativity bias’, ‘social media’, ‘LIWC’ (for Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count), ‘spontaneous thought’, and ‘future thinking’. We also paired ‘linguistic analysis’ and ‘content analysis’ with the combination term ‘future thinking’ AND ‘PTSD’. We searched Dimensions using search terms ‘PTSD AND non-trauma narratives’, ‘PTSD AND LIWC’, and ‘PTSD AND ‘Future Thinking’. We searched ‘non-trauma’ and ‘nontrauma’ to capture terminology differences. We reviewed citations from relevant articles for any additional publications.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Studies were included if: (1) PTSD diagnosis was determined by Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fourth edition (DSM-IV; American Psychiatric Association, 1994) criteria through a psychometrically validated structured (e.g. Clinician Administered PTSD Scale, Blake et al., 1990, 1995) or semi-structured (e.g. PTSD Symptom Scale – Interview, Foa et al., 1993) clinical interview; (2) PTSD diagnosis was based on a clinical diagnosis (confirmed with a validated instrument) with ≥1 criterion A event; (3) PTSD symptom severity was assessed using a psychometrically validated self-report questionnaire (e.g. PTSD Checklist; Blanchard et al., 1996); or (4) for social media studies, participants self-reported PTSD within their posts (e.g. Tweets, forum posts). We only reviewed studies on adult populations and those using objective narrative coding protocols. LIWC text analysis software (Pennebaker et al., 2007, 2015; Pennebaker & Francis, 1999) was used in all but two studies: one due to software unavailability in Norwegian (Eid et al., 2005) and one social media study that used differential language analysis (Preotiuc-Pietro et al., 2015).

Narrative characteristics

All characteristics considered were examined by at least two studies and included: (1) overall affective content and the use of negative and positive words separately; (2) singular (e.g. I, me, my) and plural (e.g. we, our) first-person pronouns; and/or (3) cognitive processing words reflecting insight (e.g. think, know), causal thinking (e.g. because, effect), and flexibility (e.g. perhaps, or). LIWC variables represent proportions (e.g. number of emotion words divided by the total number of words). Though sensory and perceptual words have been studied in the context of trauma memories, we did not review these words because only one study of nontrauma narratives examined this characteristic (Rubin, 2011).

Synthesis approach

Our review was guided by the Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis (SWiM; Campbell et al., 2020) recommendations. We extracted effect sizes from the laboratory-based studies only, due to insufficient information in social media studies. We transformed effect sizes into a Common Language Effect Size (Lenhard & Lenhard, 2022) that represents the likelihood that (1) a randomly sampled datapoint from one group will have a higher score than the other group, or (2) of two randomly selected datapoints, the datapoint with a higher score on variable A will also have a higher score on variable B (Table 2). Studies reviewed were first grouped by the narrative characteristic examined and then by study design (i.e. laboratory and social media studies). We also distinguished between differences in narrative characteristics based on PTSD diagnosis and associations between characteristics and PTSD symptom severity. We relied upon consistency to provide strength of evidence for each narrative characteristic, which we based on the number of studies yielding a significant effect and comparison of effect sizes.

Emotions

Emotional content was reported in five studies of nontrauma memories, three studies of nonmemory narratives, and two studies of social media posts. Laboratory studies of memory and nonmemory narratives comparing PTSD vs no-PTSD groups (Kleim et al., 2018; Myers et al., 2022; Römisch et al., 2014; Rubin, 2011) did not provide compelling evidence for PTSD-related differences in overall affective content, or use of positive and negative emotion words. Only one nonmemory study found greater negative emotional content in the description of film clips, which was a small effect (Orsillo et al., 2004). Studies examining associations with symptom severity demonstrated small correlations between PTSD symptom severity and emotional word usage in nontrauma narratives (Table 2). Use of fewer positive emotion words was related to more severe avoidance/numbing symptoms in narratives written a few years posttrauma (Manne, 2002), whereas use of more negative emotion words in some narratives was related to more severe avoidance/numbing a few weeks posttrauma (Eid et al., 2005). In both studies, negative word usage was not significantly associated with severity of overall PTSD symptoms or other symptom clusters. Overall, small correlations from dimensional analyses in laboratory studies provided moderate evidence of a relationship between emotional content and PTSD symptom severity in nontrauma narratives.

Results from the two social media studies were inconclusive (Table 2). Daily life narrative posts on a PTSD forum by media users with PTSD contained more negative emotion words compared to posts on a forum for firefighters without PTSD and a control group from the general population not characterised by their psychological or physical health (Todorov et al., 2020). Coppersmith et al. (2015) found that Twitter users self-reporting PTSD did not differ significantly from those not reporting PTSD in their use of positive and negative emotion words; however, the study did not account for whether Tweets contained trauma content.

Pronouns

Two laboratory and two social media studies examined first-person pronoun use (Table 2). Compared to participants without PTSD, those diagnosed with PTSD used a higher proportion of singular first-person pronouns in describing their most positive event but a lower proportion in describing their most ‘important’ event occurring the year prior to or after their trauma (Rubin, 2011). These differences in pronoun usage both equated to a large effect. In a prospective study of assault victims, first-person singular pronouns in descriptions of negative memories generated within weeks of the trauma events did not significantly predict PTSD symptom severity six months later (Kleim et al., 2018). Social media studies produced more consistent results, finding elevated self-focus in online posts from users self-reporting PTSD. Specifically, online forum users reporting PTSD used first-person singular pronouns more frequently and first-person plural pronouns less frequently in daily life narratives than trauma-exposed and general population controls not reporting PTSD (Todorov et al., 2020). Similarly, a study of Twitter posts found that language associated with self-reported PTSD was self-focused, with frequent use of phrases such as ‘I had’, ‘I was’, ‘I am’, and ‘I would’ (Preotiuc-Pietro et al., 2015).

Cognitive processing words

Overall, studies examining cognitive processing words in nontrauma narratives were mixed across the seven laboratory and two social media studies evaluating this characteristic (Table 2). Laboratory study findings suggested that overall cognitive processing words reflecting cause, insight, and flexibility in nontrauma narratives were not distinctive features of PTSD as a diagnosis (Myers et al., 2022; Papini et al., 2014; Römisch et al., 2014; Rubin, 2011; Rubin et al., 2016). Regarding associations of cognitive processing words with PTSD symptom severity, whereas Kleim et al. (2018) found that fewer cognitive processing words as a general category showed a small association with greater PTSD symptoms severity months later, Manne (2002) did not find a significant association between use of causation and insight words and PTSD symptoms severity. In another study, less usage of flexibility words showed a strong correlation with more severe reexperiencing symptoms but not with other symptom clusters (Papini et al., 2014). In contrast, social media studies found that online forum posts by users self-reporting PTSD contained more cognitive processing words (Todorov et al., 2020), whereas there were no significant differences among users reporting versus not reporting PTSD in shorter form (i.e. Twitter) posts (Coppersmith et al., 2015). Overall, there were inconsistent findings between laboratory and social media studies, as well as across dimensional analyses.

Discussion

Language is a powerful tool that provides opportunities for people to communicate, shape, organise, and interpret their experiences. Though there is extensive research investigating associations between PTSD and trauma narrative linguistic characteristics, less is known about language use in nontrauma narratives in PTSD. This knowledge gap limits our understanding of how experiences beyond traumatic events are communicated through and shaped by language in PTSD. Overall, our review indicates that some PTSD-related linguistic characteristics are not confined to narratives about the trauma experience. In particular, there is consistent evidence that nontrauma narratives generated by individuals with more severe PTSD symptoms contain more negative and less positive emotion words, a pattern that might reflect negative cognitive appraisals characteristic of the disorder.

Characteristics of nontrauma narratives

Across the 12 reviewed papers examining nontrauma narratives, emotional and cognitive processing words were the most commonly examined narrative features. In both content areas, studies comparing PTSD to no-PTSD diagnostic groups provided no evidence of significant group differences. In contrast, studies examining PTSD symptoms dimensionally revealed moderate evidence that emotional word usage was significantly correlated with PTSD symptom severity. Specifically, the use of more negative emotion words and fewer positive emotion words was related to higher avoidance/numbing symptom severity (Eid et al., 2005; Manne, 2002). The extension of more negative emotionality to nontrauma memories is consistent with the overgeneralisation of current threat and negative emotion emerging as a consequence of threat appraisals that maintain PTSD symptoms (Ehlers & Clark, 2000).

Associations between emotion content and PTSD symptoms in nontrauma narratives may also represent the tendency to focus attention on negative information and/or away from positive information. Contextual factors appeared to influence how a negativity bias is expressed. For example, comparison of findings by Manne (2002) and Eid et al. (2005) suggests that as the temporal distance from a trauma increases, a transition from heightened negative to reduced positive emotional content may occur in nontrauma narratives. PTSD-associated alterations in the use of emotional words in nontrauma narratives could also reflect the diversity of emotion words individuals with PTSD use to describe experiences (i.e. emotional vocabulary), a language feature that is highly correlated with emotional tone of narratives and is related to psychological and physical health (Vine et al., 2020). The effects reported in the reviewed studies were small and additional research is needed to more fully understand the contextual factors and other language features that influence these emotion-PTSD relationships.

Altered language use in nontrauma narratives was not limited to emotion, but findings in the other categories were generally heterogenous. Though more consistent in social media studies, we found some evidence for greater use of first-person singular pronouns in association with PTSD. An elevated self-focus may influence other aspects of narrative generation, as creating a narrative from the first-person perspective can be associated with greater vividness, negative emotion, and depression (Berry-Blunt et al., 2021; Nigro & Neisser, 1983; Tackman et al., 2019; Vella & Moulds, 2014). For individuals with PTSD, heightened emotion may be problematic because the emotional expression of experiences would theoretically be dominated by negative language as a result of a pervasive negativity bias. Exaggerated self-focus may also sustain a negative emotional state, whereas reduced use of first-person pronouns can help regulate emotions (Nook et al., 2017).

Among heterogenous findings, notable was a small effect indicating that less use of cognitive processing words overall was associated with greater PTSD symptoms, though this relationship may be apparent specifically in the use of cognitive flexibility words. These results may reflect difficulty processing and integrating experiences because of intrusive memories and reactions. Difficulty processing experiences could also exacerbate existing reexperiencing symptoms, leading to a disruptive cycle. More broadly, increasing use of cognitive processing words in writing over time is associated with better health outcomes (Pennebaker et al., 2003), which suggests that a paucity of cognitive processing language may be detrimental.

Several potential reasons for the mixed findings across all characteristics studied deserve mention. Correlations of narrative characteristics in relation to PTSD symptom severity were more likely to yield significant effects than were group comparisons. This pattern of results may reflect that correlational studies better capture the dimensionality of PTSD. Prior work has shown that, compared to trauma-exposed individuals with low PTSD symptoms, those with subthreshold PTSD have worse outcomes (Brancu et al., 2016). Additionally, continuous measures provide increased statistical power to detect effects, relative to dichotomous comparisons (Cohen, 1983; MacCallum et al., 2002). As such, apparently mixed results regarding diagnostic group comparisons versus dimensional analyses may reflect the insensitivity of group designs to the effects of PTSD as a continuous clinical phenomenon.

The contrasting findings across pronoun and cognitive processing word use may also reflect differences in narrative generation methods and participant sampling procedures. Social media posts are likely driven by internal cues in contrast to laboratory-based studies that capture responses to external cues generated in an artificial, albeit more controlled, environment. It is also possible that updates to variables across different LIWC versions used across studies may account for some of the heterogeneity in findings.

Future directions

This review revealed several studies that focused on the narrative characteristics of nontrauma memories in PTSD, with fewer examining other forms of narrative. One noticeable gap is examination of narrative characteristics in descriptions of imagined future events. Previous research on future event narratives in PTSD has documented reduced content specificity (Brown et al., 2013; Kleim et al., 2014) and detail (Brown et al., 2014; Verfaellie et al., 2023) but no studies have examined linguistic characteristics of such narratives. Given that memory is thought to support future-oriented thinking (Schacter & Addis, 2007), characteristics of nontrauma memory narratives would be hypothesised to extend to nontrauma narratives about the future in PTSD.

The relative importance of avoidance vs. numbing symptoms to the observed relationships with use of emotion words remains unclear given that avoidance and numbing symptoms were analysed as one cluster, reflecting the taxonomy in use when the studies were conducted. Dysphoric symptoms such as restricted range of affect and anhedonia could be theorised to drive reductions in use of positive emotion words and greater use of negative words. Future work is needed to examine the independent contributions of avoidance compared to numbing and other dysphoric symptoms.

Another outstanding question concerns the extent to which PTSD symptoms contribute to narrative alterations independently of depression and other component emotions of PTSD (e.g. anxiety). PTSD incorporates dysphoric symptoms and is often associated with comorbid depressive disorder. Individuals with depression generate fewer positive words and more negative words (Rude et al., 2004), as well as more first-person pronouns (Brockmeyer et al., 2015; Edwards & Holtzman, 2017). These content and linguistic features are likewise correlated with the severity of depression (Tolboll, 2019) and anxiety symptoms (Anderson et al., 2008). The studies we reviewed did not systematically control for comorbid depressive or anxiety disorders, though Preotiuc-Pietro et al. (2015) found that PTSD users referred more frequently to traumatic experiences and psychological distress compared to users with depression. Thus, examining the contributions of anxiety and dysphoric symptoms to the observed PTSD-associated narrative characteristics could help elucidate underlying emotional mechanisms.

Our analysis did not account for sample characteristics other than PTSD as the studies that reported on demographic characteristics included primarily white (3 out of 5 studies) or female participants (4 out of 7 studies), with average ages between 20 and 40. Factors such as age and race (e.g. Rai et al., 2024) may moderate the linguistic characteristics associated with PTSD. Thus, the findings of the current review may only be relevant to specific subgroups of individuals with PTSD, though support for this idea would require further study.

Additionally, it will be important to examine social media posts of individuals with validated PTSD diagnoses due to concerns for diagnostic validity in the absence of verification by evidence-based assessment. Although speculative, it may also be beneficial to minimise self-selection into social media studies, given the potential for individuals who choose to participate in and self-disclose on social media platforms to have a higher desire to process events. A hybrid approach could address these questions by conducting laboratory-based psychological assessment of PTSD of individuals who consent to examination of their social media posts for linguistic characteristics.

Conclusions

This review of nontrauma narrative characteristics provided moderate evidence that severity of PTSD symptom clusters is related to differential use of emotion words in nontrauma narratives. The findings pertaining to other linguistic characteristics in these narratives were inconsistent. Results generally suggested that the negative cognitive styles that characterise PTSD extend beyond trauma narrative and more broadly affect narrative generation of experiences, which has implications for how people with PTSD think and use language to communicate their views of the world and themselves.
