Toward Practical Embryo Genomic Selection in Cattle: An Optimized Workflow Enabling High‐Accuracy Breeding Value Prediction Without Compromising Embryo Survival
Authors: Jingyu Zhang, Zhize Liu, Hang Shen, Mengmeng Zhao, Jun Teng, Yubing Wang, Guangyao Liu, Boxuan Ye, Zhenni Zhang, Qin Zhang, Jianhui Tian, Lei An, Yinjuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe implementation of embryo genomic selection (EGS) is constrained by the lack of a standardized workflow that balances high genotyping accuracy with the preservation of embryo viability, particularly given embryonic heterogeneity. To address this, we systematically optimized the critical steps of trophectoderm biopsy and whole‐genome amplification, establishing a diameter‐stratified strategy that links embryo diameter to a biopsy area window designed to protect embryo recovery while ensuring sufficient DNA input for reliable genotyping. Our results demonstrated high concordance (> 98%) between embryo and calf genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs), supporting the reliability of predictions. Crucially, we defined a diameter‐based biopsy framework: for embryos < 150 μm, biopsy areas < 1000 μm2 supported viability, whereas larger embryos (≥ 150 μm) tolerated up to 1500 μm2, with a minimum threshold of 840 μm2 guaranteeing > 90% genotype call rates. By preventing oversampling in small embryos and undersampling across embryo sizes, this diameter‐guided control improves both embryo survival and GEBV prediction accuracy. This study provides a validated technical pathway for EGS that addresses the major practical bottleneck in livestock breeding. By ensuring reliable selection without compromising developmental potential, our work facilitates the rapid introgression of superior genetics and marks a significant step toward making EGS scalable, accelerating genetic progress in cattle and other species.Keywords: biopsy area, embryo diameter, embryo genomic selection, genomic estimated breeding values, laser‐assisted biopsy, whole‐genome amplification

Introduction

The field of animal breeding is undergoing a profound paradigm shift [1], as the target of genetic selection is progressively moving from traditional postnatal individuals to the early embryonic stage. This transformation is driven by the integration of several key multidisciplinary technologies, including minimally invasive embryo biopsy techniques [2], whole‐genome amplification (WGA) [3] of trace cellular material, and high‐multiplex molecular genotyping [4]. The application of genomic selection to early embryos theoretically enables the precise screening of genetically superior embryos for the next generation prior to embryo transfer. This technology can not only reduce the waste of maternal resources but also significantly shorten the breeding generation interval, thereby accelerating genetic progress at the population level.

However, this transformative technology, termed embryo genomic selection (EGS), still faces a core challenge for large‐scale implementation: the integration of these steps into a reliable, efficient, standardized workflow. This system must simultaneously meet two critical criteria: first, maximizing embryo viability to ensure successful implantation and developmental potential of biopsied embryos [5]; and second, ensuring high‐accuracy in genotyping to prevent the omission or incorrect selection of superior genotypes due to biases in whole‐genome amplification or genotyping errors from low‐input samples [6].

As the primary step in EGS, embryo biopsy has been extensively studied and optimized. In particular, advances in human preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) have provided significant insights and a solid foundation for developing embryo biopsy techniques in livestock [7]. To date, trophectoderm biopsy at the blastocyst stage has been shown to be superior to blastomere biopsy at the 4‐cell or 8‐cell stages [8]. Concurrently, laser‐assisted biopsy, owing to its distinct advantages over manual dissection with microknives, has been integrated with micromanipulation techniques to ensure high embryo survival rates [9]. Notably, reliable indicators for assessing the number of biopsied cells remain lacking, which is particularly pronounced in animal embryos with high lipid content. The indistinct cell boundaries in such embryos substantially increase the technical difficulty of biopsy procedures.

WGA, the second step in EGS, has also been extensively investigated. Multiple displacement amplification (MDA), a classic WGA method, is known for its simplicity, high DNA yield, and relatively high‐fidelity [10, 11]. We have previously compared MDA and multiple annealing and looping‐based amplification cycles (MALBAC) using trace somatic cells, and found that MDA outperformed MALBAC in terms of the detection rate in microarray analyses and genotype consistency [12]. Nevertheless, the application of MDA to trophectoderm cells remains scarcely explored, and its suitability for embryo genomes requires systematic evaluation.

The third and pivotal step of EGS is the conversion of embryonic genomic information into reliable GEBVs [13], which form the basis for selecting embryos with optimal genetic potential. This step predominantly employs statistical models from conventional genomic selection (GS) [14], with the Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) and its genomic extension (GBLUP) [15, 16, 17] serving as the foundational algorithms. These models analyze high‐density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers [18, 19, 20, 21] to precisely quantify the genetic relationships between the target individual and a reference population with known phenotypic data, thereby enabling accurate GEBV prediction. This integration of genomic data with quantitative genetics principles allows for the prediction of genetic merit independent of phenotypic information, significantly accelerating breeding cycles. Nonetheless, a significant challenge impedes the application of EGS [22]: the lack of statistical methods tailored to the unique context of embryonic genomes. The applicability and predictive accuracy of standard models like GBLUP for embryos therefore necessitate rigorous, systematic validation.

EGS enables pre‐transfer ranking of embryos based on genomic merit rather than morphology alone, thereby supporting more informed embryo selection [23, 24]. In turn, this can improve the efficiency of recipient allocation and decision‐making in multiple ovulation and embryo transfer (MOET) programs [25, 26]. At the breeding‐program level, its principal expected advantage is to accelerate genetic progress by enabling earlier selection, shortening the generation interval, and increasing selection intensity [27].

This study aims to evaluate the concordance between EGS and conventional GS, systematically establish quantitative biopsy sampling methodologies, and assess the feasibility of MDA for amplification of trace amounts of embryonic cells. Ultimately, this study seeks to optimize EGS workflows and provide robust theoretical and technical foundations for large‐scale implementation of EGS in animal breeding.

Materials and Methods

In Vitro Production of Bovine Embryos

Healthy ovaries from slaughterhouse‐derived cows were collected and transported to the laboratory in physiological saline (0.9% NaCl) maintained at 37°C and delivered within 2 h. Cumulus‐oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from antral follicles (2–8 mm in diameter) under sterile conditions using a 20‐gauge needle. COCs exhibiting homogeneous cytoplasm and at least three layers of compact cumulus cells were selected for in vitro maturation (IVM). Selected COCs were cultured in BO‐IVM medium (IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) for 21 h at 38.5°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

For sperm preparation, frozen–thawed semen was washed twice with BO‐SemenPrep medium (IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min and then resuspended in BO‐IVF medium (IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) at a final concentration of 1 × 106 sperm/mL. Oocytes and spermatozoa were co‐incubated in BO‐IVF medium (IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) for 18 h at 38.5°C under 5% CO2 to allow fertilization.

Presumptive zygotes were transferred to BO‐IVC medium (IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) and cultured for 144 h under tri‐gas conditions (5% O2, 5% CO2, 90% N2) at 38.5°C. Cavitated blastocysts (expanded blastocysts with a distinct inner cell mass and trophectoderm) were collected on Day 7 for further analyzis or cryopreservation.

Trophectoderm Biopsy

Trophectoderm biopsy was performed using a laser system (Hariolab, Guangzhou, China) and micromanipulators (Olympus, Hachioji, Japan) mounted on an inverted microscope (Olympus IX71). The laser‐assisted biopsy approach was adopted based on our previous studies, which showed that laser biopsy caused less damage to mouse and bovine blastocysts than mechanical biopsy. In the same optimization experiments, we established the operating setting used in the present study: 400 mW power and a pulse duration of 500 μs, corresponding to a spot size of approximately 11.79 μm [28]. An approximately 10‐μm opening was created using laser assistance in the zona pellucida at the polar trophectoderm (TE) region opposite the inner cell mass (ICM), followed by gentle aspiration of several TE cells through a 15‐μm inner diameter biopsy pipette. A subset of biopsied blastocysts was cultured in BO‐IVC medium (IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) for 24 h, while others were transferred immediately to recipients. The cultured embryos were morphologically evaluated under an inverted microscope at 2, 4, and 24 h post‐culture. Cavitation (formation of a distinct fluid‐filled cavity) observed by 24 h was used as the primary criterion for successful re‐expansion.

Embryo Diameter Measurement and Diameter‐Guided Biopsy Area Selection

Prior to biopsy, each Day 7 cavitated blastocyst was imaged using the same inverted microscope used for micromanipulation. Embryo diameter was defined as the maximum external diameter of the blastocyst, measured from the outer trophectoderm edge to edge, excluding the zona pellucida. Diameter measurements were performed using the software of the Hariolab laser system and its integrated 40× objective. Embryos were stratified into small (< 150 μm) and large (≥ 150 μm) diameter groups.

Biopsy was always performed at the polar TE region opposite the ICM. The target biopsy area was determined according to embryo diameter to balance post‐biopsy recovery and downstream genotyping robustness: for embryos < 150 μm, the planned biopsy area was 840–1000 μm2 (upper limit to avoid oversampling‐related viability loss); for embryos ≥ 150 μm, the planned biopsy area was 840–1500 μm2. In all embryos, 840 μm2 was used as the minimum area threshold to reduce the risk of insufficient DNA input and call rate< 90%.

During each biopsy, the aspirated TE cell cluster was briefly imaged inside the biopsy pipette and the biopsy area was estimated in real time based on the image (as described in Figure S3B,D) to confirm that the sample tissue was within the predefined area range; if the estimated area was below the minimum threshold, additional TE tissue was gently aspirated; if the estimated area exceeded the upper limit, only tissue up to the upper limit was collected. After sampling, any excess cell mass outside the zona pellucida was gently pushed back into the zona pellucida using the biopsy pipette.

Nuclear Staining and Cell Counting

The biopsied cell clusters were incubated with Hoechst 33 342 (1:10 dilution in PBS; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37°C for 5 min to stain nuclei. Following staining, samples were washed twice for 3 min each time in phosphate buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) supplemented with 0.1% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Sigma‐Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) to remove excess dye. For nuclear visualization, cell clusters were mounted on glass slides with 4 μL of DAPI‐containing antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, California, USA) and gently compressed with a coverslip. Fluorescent imaging was performed using an epifluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51) equipped with a DAPI filter set (excitation 350 nm, emission 460 nm). The number of Hoechst‐positive nuclei was counted.

Whole‐Genome Amplification and DNA Quality Assessment

The MDA‐based whole‐genome amplification strategy was selected based on our previous validation experiments. In that work, five individual cells isolated from digested bovine ear tissue were used for MDA amplification, and the GEBVs derived from these single cells exhibited a high correlation with those obtained from the bulk ear tissue. On this basis, MDA was used in the present study for amplification of DNA derived from TE biopsy samples [12]. The biopsied cell clusters were lysed in 4 μL of Storage Buffer (REPLI‐g Advanced DNA Single Cell Kit; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and subjected to room‐temperature denaturation for 10 min followed by whole‐genome amplification (WGA) using the REPLI‐g Advanced DNA Single Cell Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol: a 2 h incubation at 30°C with Phi29 DNA polymerase, terminated by enzyme inactivation at 65°C for 3 min. Parallel samples were preserved in Storage Buffer at −80°C for 15 days before undergoing the same WGA processing.

Amplified DNA products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (100 V, 30 min) to confirm fragment distribution (200–10 000 bp) and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, USA). Fresh and cryopreserved samples were compared to evaluate DNA degradation patterns.

Genotyping and Sequencing

The remaining amplified DNA was subjected to either genotyping using the GeneSeek Genomic Profiler Bovine 100 K (Neogen, Lansing, USA) or 1× whole‐genome sequencing (MolBreeding, Shijiazhuang, China). Genotype call rates were calculated using PLINK 1.9, whereas sequencing reads were aligned to the bovine reference assembly (UMD_3.1.1 assembly) using Bowtie2 v2.5.2. Mapping rates and genome coverage were computed using SAMtools v1.19.2.

Sample Collection and Genotyping

Tissue samples (hair follicles or ear notches) were collected from calves born following embryo transfer and immediately preserved in 75% ethanol. Genomic DNA was extracted and genotyped with the GeneSeek Genomic Profiler Bovine 100 K SNP chip (Neogen) following standard protocols.

Embryo‐derived WGA DNA profiles and calf genotypes were merged using PLINK 1.9 (‐‐bmerge command). Genotype concordance rates were calculated for each calf‐embryo pair with PLINK 2.0. Following genotype imputation with Beagle v5.5, the phased dataset was filtered using PLINK 1.9 with Minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05 and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium < 0.00001.

Statistical Analysis and Visualization

Inter‐group differences (e.g., fresh vs. cryopreserved WGA samples) were assessed using either Student's t‐test (unpaired, two‐tailed) for continuous variables with a normal distribution, or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.3.1. Statistical significance was annotated with asterisks (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Results

From Embryo to Calf: Validating Genomic Predictions in Cattle

To investigate whether MDA‐based whole‐genome amplification (WGA) of trace embryonic genomes can fully replace postnatal individual samples for genotyping and GEBV assessment, Holstein embryos were biopsied, subjected to WGA, and subsequently transferred, resulting in 13 live‐born calves. Tissue samples from these calves, together with the corresponding embryo‐derived WGA DNA, were genotyped using the GGP‐100K SNP microarray.

In contrast to results obtained from trace somatic cells, the call rate of embryo‐amplified DNA samples did not consistently exceed 90%, with only 58.4% of samples meeting this threshold (Figure 1A,B). Moreover, high genotype concordance (> 95%) with corresponding calf samples was observed exclusively in embryo‐derived samples exhibiting call rates > 90% (Figure 1C, Figure S1A).

Further genomic evaluation was performed using HIBLUP v1.4 (a software tool for genomic prediction) using the GBLUP model for seven QC‐passed samples (call rate > 90%). Genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) were calculated for five traits: milk yield, fat percentage, fat yield, protein percentage and protein yield. Correlation analysis revealed GEBV concordance > 0.98 for all traits in QC‐passed embryo–calf pairs (Figure 1D), demonstrating that high‐fidelity amplified embryonic genomes can serve as equivalent alternatives to postnatal individual genomes for genetic assessment.

In parallel, pregnancy rates at 38 days were compared between biopsied embryos and non‐biopsied embryos to evaluate the impact of biopsy on developmental competence. The mean pregnancy rate was 58.89% for biopsied embryos, which was not statistically different from the mean pregnancy rate of 69.19% observed in the non‐biopsied group (Student's t‐test, p > 0.05; Figure S1B). These results confirm that our biopsy protocol does not compromise embryonic implantation potential.

Because call rate is a direct indicator of WGA/genotyping completeness and is known to correlate with allele dropout and genotyping errors associated with low‐input WGA [3, 6, 29], we used a call rate threshold of > 90% as a quality‐control criterion for downstream analyses. In our dataset, embryo samples below this threshold showed markedly reduced genotype concordance with the matched calves (Figure 1C, Figure S1A), and were therefore excluded from GEBV estimation to avoid biased estimates driven by missing or erroneous genotypes. Consequently, the > 0.98 embryo–calf GEBV concordance reported in this study was observed in embryo samples that passed this QC threshold.

Optimization of MDA Amplification Pipeline for Trace Holstein Embryo Cells

Given that embryo biopsy material is typically retrieved as a compact cell cluster rather than dispersed individual cells, incomplete lysis may occur and could contribute to suboptimal call rates; therefore, this study systematically optimized the standard MDA protocol. We hypothesized that extending the denaturation time might enhance DNA accessibility; therefore, we conducted experiments to evaluate this modification. To mitigate the risk of DNA over‐denaturation associated with prolonged exposure, the original protocol (10 min denaturation at room‐temperature) was modified to include 30 min denaturation on ice (Figure 2A). Amplified samples underwent whole‐genome sequencing. All sequenced datasets from both experimental streams (denaturation optimization and the subsequent cryopreservation experiment) met stringent quality control thresholds, with effective mapping rates > 99%, Q20 > 98%, and Q30 > 95% (see Figure S2A–C for full metrics), ensuring that subsequent analyses were based on high‐integrity data. Following this QC step, reads were aligned to the ARS‐UCD1.2 bovine reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.5.2. Subsequent analysis revealed no statistically significant improvement in either mapping rate (Figure 2B) or coverage depth (Figure 2C) compared to standard protocol.

Concurrently, logistical constraints in routine field and commercial workflows prompted an evaluation of cryopreservation as a strategy for sample storage and transport. Biopsied samples were preserved in the kit‐provided storage buffer. After 15 days of storage at −80°C, samples were processed using the same MDA workflow and sequencing pipeline (Figure 2D). Although the mapping rate showed no significant difference (Figure 2E), cryopreserved samples exhibited significantly reduced coverage depth compared with controls (Figure 2F). This result suggests that freezing and/or freeze–thaw handling can adversely affect DNA integrity and subsequent amplification efficiency, potentially leading to reduced coverage depth.

Cell Number Analysis in Embryo Biopsy

Previous studies have indicated that stable amplification outcomes typically require at least 5 to 10 cells [12], and insufficient cellular input can lead to inconsistent amplification outcomes across samples. However, accurate cell counting during biopsy procedures remains technically challenging. We therefore explored the use of operationally measurable parameters during sampling as a surrogate measure for estimating the number of cells retrieved. To validate this approach, a regression model between tissue dimensions and cell count was established. Initially, biopsied tissue was completely aspirated into sampling needles with standardized bore diameters. Tissue depth (length within the needle) was measured as a potential proxy for the cell count (Figure S3A,C). Tissue depth was recorded during biopsy, followed by Hoechst staining and nuclei counting. The results revealed only a weak linear correlation (R 2 = 0.0475) between tissue depth and cell number (Figure 3A), indicating that tissue depth is inadequate as a substitute metric.

(B) Linear regression (solid line) and Pearson correlation's square (R 2) between biopsy tissue area and cell number (n = 21). (C) Representative immunofluorescence image of nuclei stained with Hoechst 33 342 (blue). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus BX51) with a 40× objective. Scale bar = 50 μm.

We hypothesized that tissue deformation during aspiration might confound depth‐based measurements. Consequently, tissue area was evaluated as an alternative parameter. Tissue area was measured by capturing microscopic images of the aspirated cell cluster within the biopsy pipette and calculating the area of the resulting pseudo‐rectangular structure to obtain a pseudo‐rectangular region bounded by the zona pellucida (left), the inner edge of the pipette orifice (right), and the elongated cell mass (upper and lower boundaries) (Figure S3B,D). Similarly, Hoechst‐stained cell counts were plotted against optically measured biopsy areas. Again, a weak correlation was observed (R 2 = 0.1582; Figure 3B), indicating that neither depth nor area reliably predicts cell number in aspirated trophoblast clusters.

Interactive Effects of Diameter and Biopsy Area on Post‐Biopsy Recovery

The post‐biopsy recovery rate serves as a critical indicator for evaluating biopsy outcomes. Although our attempts to quantify cell numbers did not yield a strong correlation, our findings supported using the biopsy area to define an operational upper limit. Specifically, determining the maximum biopsy area that embryos can tolerate could enable reliable tissue sampling without the need for precise cell counting. This approach was supported by a significant association between biopsy area and post‐biopsy embryo recovery, with successfully re‐expanded embryos predominantly exhibiting biopsy areas of < 2000 μm2 (Figure 4A). To further investigate area‐dependent recovery, biopsy areas were stratified into three groups: middle area (500–1000 μm2), large area (1000–1500 μm2), and extra‐large area (1500–2000 μm2). 24‐h re‐expansion rates were as follows: 100.0% for the middle group, 81.1% for the large group, and 78.9% for the extra‐large group (Figure 4B). Comparative analysis revealed consistently higher re‐expansion rates in the middle than large area groups at all timepoints (2 h, 4 h, 24 h; p < 0.05) (Figure 4C). In contrast, large area and extra‐large area groups showed a significant difference only at 2 h (p < 0.05), with no detectable differences at 4 h (p = 0.362) or 24 h (p > 0.999).

Concurrently, blastocyst diameter significantly influenced recovery, with large‐diameter embryos (> 150 μm) exhibiting consistently higher re‐expansion rates than their small‐diameter counterparts (< 150 μm) at 2 h, 4 h, and 24 h (Figure 4D). This diameter‐dependent resilience also applied to variations in biopsy area manipulation: large‐diameter embryos maintained 100% re‐expansion at 24 h across middle‐to‐large biopsy areas (500–1500 μm2) with no significant differences at any timepoint (Figure 4E), whereas small‐diameter embryos exhibited severely compromised recovery after large‐area biopsies (Figure 4F). Collectively, these findings indicated that large embryos (> 150 μm) tolerate biopsy areas up to 1500 μm2 without loss of viability, while small embryos (< 150 μm) necessitate restricted biopsy areas to under 1000 μm2 to prevent irreversible damage.

To further validate the impact of the area‐guided biopsy protocol on embryonic developmental potential, this study was conducted in a large‐scale commercial bovine embryo production and transfer system. In vitro produced blastocysts were stratified by diameter and subjected to biopsy sampling according to a predefined area protocol. Both biopsied and non‐biopsied embryos were transferred to recipients. Pregnancy status was assessed on Day 38 post‐transfer, demonstrating a significantly higher mean pregnancy rate in the biopsied group (80.67%) versus the non‐biopsied group (57.33%) (t‐test, p < 0.05; Figure S4B). These results confirm that the area‐based biopsy protocol did not reduce implantation potential in this commercial setting and, in our dataset, that it was associated with a higher Day‐38 pregnancy rate compared with that of non‐biopsied embryos.

In addition, because embryos in practical EGS programs are often cryopreserved after biopsy while awaiting genotyping and GEBV computation, we compared pregnancy outcomes after transfer of frozen–thawed embryos between biopsied and non‐biopsied groups under commercial farm conditions. The D38 pregnancy rate of vitrified/warmed biopsied embryos was 51.11% (n = 45), which was comparable to that of vitrified/warmed non‐biopsied embryos (48.35%, n = 1743) (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.7637; Figure S4C).

These data indicate that, at the workflow level, TE biopsy does not reduce post‐thaw transfer outcomes in our field dataset.

Optimizing Trophectoderm Biopsy Area to Ensure Genotyping Success in EGS

In EGS, genotyping accuracy is a key determinant of selection efficiency. Having established an upper limit for the biopsy area‐based on post‐biopsy recovery rates, we next sought to determine the minimum required biopsy area by evaluating genotyping outcomes following amplification. In vitro fertilization (IVF) blastocysts underwent either a middle area (500–1000 μm2) or a large‐area (1000–1500 μm2) biopsy, with aspirated cell clusters subjected to WGA using the MDA method (Figure 5A). Amplified DNA met all quality criteria for genomic microarray analysis, with concentrations of 2000–2800 ng/μL (Figure 5B), purity ratios of A 260/A 280 = 1.7–1.9 (Figure 5C) and A 260/A 230 = 1.8–2.2 (Figure 5D).

Genotyping with the GGP‐100 K SNP chip revealed that, in the middle area group, one sample had a sub‐threshold call rate (< 90%), whereas in the large‐area group, all samples achieved call rates above 90% (Figure 5E,F). Overall, larger biopsy areas were associated with more robust genotyping performance. Consistent with this, quadrant analysis defined 840 μm2 as an operational minimum in our dataset: all samples with call rates > 90% were observed only when the biopsy area exceeded 840 μm2 (Figure 5G), supporting this cutoff as a practical lower bound to reduce QC failure risk of embryonic genotyping.

Discussion

Through a comprehensive study involving in vitro production of bovine embryos, biopsy techniques, WGA, and genetic evaluation, we demonstrate that EGS can provide reliable genetic assessments, which are crucial for making informed breeding decisions. Importantly, our results demonstrate that a high degree of consistency (exceeding 98%) can be achieved between embryo‐derived GEBVs and calf‐derived GEBVs for critical traits such as milk yield, fat percentage, and protein yield. However, this consistency hinges on the absence of amplification issues during the WGA process. This underscores the necessity of optimizing WGA protocols to ensure reliable genetic assessments and to avoid potential errors that could arise from amplification failures.

There has been extensive research on the entire process of embryo biopsy, WGA, and genotyping, particularly in the field of human PGD, which has great significance for the early identification of genetic diseases [30, 31]. However, there is a significant difference between human PGD and breeding in terms of their requirements for WGA. Human genetic disease detection typically considers a limited number of mutation sites, whereas breeding requires high‐density molecular markers at the whole‐genome level, a requirement that imposes stricter requirements on the coverage and accuracy of WGA. Many WGA methods are available, and some have been studied for their effectiveness in amplifying livestock embryo biopsy samples, such as primer extension preamplification (PEP) [32], degenerate oligonucleotide primed PCR (DOP‐PCR) [24], MDA [33], and MALBAC [29]. Among these, the MDA method, which relies on phi29 DNA polymerase, is considered the most effective due to its simplicity, speed, and high‐fidelity. Despite the solid foundation established by related studies, there has been little research on whether the genotyping results of amplified samples can replace those from newborns. The results of this study show that the genotyping results from amplified samples are highly authentic and can serve as a reliable proxy. Although complete substitution has not yet been achieved, these findings provide a robust foundation for the future large‐scale application of EGS.

Given the challenges highlighted in our study regarding amplification inconsistencies, we subsequently carried out experiments to optimize the WGA process and refine biopsy sampling methods. These experiments have provided valuable insights and potential solutions to enhance the reliability of EGS protocols. Various experimental parameters have been adjusted in numerous studies aimed at optimizing the MDA process [34]. Specifically, to address the non‐uniform amplification issue inherent in MDA, more advanced technologies have been developed. These include microreactor MDA [35], emulsion MDA [36, 37], and micro‐channel MDA [38], which have shown promise in improving the uniformity and accuracy of the amplification process. However, while these advancements have led to significant improvements in the detection of copy number variations (CNVs) [39], their impact on single nucleotide variants (SNVs) [40] detection has been more limited.

Despite attempts to modify the denaturation protocol and assess the impact of cryopreservation, we found that these adjustments did not substantially improve the sequencing mapping rates or coverage of the amplified DNA. This highlights the complexity of working with embryonic cells and suggests that the limited quantity of starting material may inherently increase amplification variability. Consequently, optimizing the biopsy procedure to ensure sufficient cellular material is collected while maintaining high recovery rates becomes crucial. Furthermore, the observed reduction in sequencing coverage from cryopreserved samples underscores the necessity of prompt amplification following the biopsy to ensure genomic data quality.

Embryo biopsy, which first found application in PGD, has been of great significance for the screening of human genetic diseases. Initially, embryo biopsy was performed primarily during the cleavage stage, especially at the 4‐cell or 8‐cell stage, because the blastomeres were large in volume. On the other hand, at that stage, the blastomeres are not compacted and are relatively loosely connected, which makes them easy to collect [41, 42]. Studies on embryo biopsy stages show that trophectoderm biopsy at the blastocyst stage is better than blastomere biopsy, as more cells can be biopsied while minimizing the impact on the embryo [43, 44]. Furthermore, not all cleavage‐stage embryos can develop into the blastocyst stage. Therefore, selecting blastocysts for biopsy is not only a sampling process but also a strategy to screen for high‐quality embryos. This is highly significant for improving the success rate of embryo transfer and the efficiency of the entire breeding process. However, trophoblast cells are tightly connected. Moreover, for embryos with high lipid content, such as bovine embryos, the color is darker and cell boundaries are not distinct, making cell counting difficult. Therefore, our investigation into using biopsy tissue depth or area as a proxy for cell number revealed weak correlations, indicating that these metrics are not reliable substitutes for direct cell counting.

Interestingly, we discovered a significant relationship between biopsy tissue area and embryo recovery rates, with smaller biopsy areas associated with higher recovery rates, especially in embryos with smaller diameters. We established a tiered biopsy strategy for embryo genomic selection (Figure 6). Initial embryo screening by diameter dictates that under‐150‐μm embryos require biopsy areas under 1000 μm2 to ensure viability. For eligible embryos, biopsy areas should meet or exceed the empirically derived 800 μm2 threshold (approximately 840 μm2), a threshold that secures over 90% genotype call rates, while preferably targeting 1500 μm2 where developmentally permissible to maximize amplification reliability. Critically, the 800 μm2 minimum prevents genetic data loss from undersampling, whereas the 1500 μm2 optimum enhances genotyping consistency without compromising recovery in large embryos exceeding 150 μm in diameter. This approach balances tissue conservation against genomic information fidelity.

Schematic diagram of biopsy protocols for blastocysts of different diameters. (A) For blastocysts < 150 μm, the optimal biopsy area is 840–1000 μm2. (B) For blastocyst > 150 μm, the optimal biopsy area is 840–1500 μm2.

An intriguing observation was the significantly higher pregnancy rate achieved using the optimized biopsy protocol compared with that of the non‐biopsied group. This improvement is particularly notable given that the initial version of the workflow showed no significant difference in pregnancy rates compared with non‐biopsied embryos. The breach in the zona pellucida created during biopsy may facilitate embryo hatching and subsequent implantation—a mechanism analogous to assisted hatching in clinical embryology [45]. However, further validation with larger cohorts is necessary to confirm whether the biopsy procedure itself contributes consistently to enhanced pregnancy outcomes. Post‐thaw viability is a key translational consideration for EGS, because embryos are commonly vitrified after biopsy while genotyping and GEBV computation are completed and then warmed for transfer [46]. In our farm dataset, pregnancy rates after transfer of frozen–thawed embryos were comparable between biopsied and non‐biopsied groups (Figure S4C), suggesting that TE biopsy in this workflow does not compromise post‐thaw reproductive performance at the field level.

Commercial‐scale EGS requires procedures that are rapid, standardized, and robust [23, 47, 48]. In this context, the diameter‐guided biopsy windows established here offer a practical framework for MOET pipelines by defining biopsy areas that balance genotyping success with post‐biopsy recovery. When combined with a > 90% call rate QC threshold before GEBV calculation, this workflow allows embryo evaluation to proceed only when genotyping data meet a defined reliability standard. Such an approach may improve the reliability of pre‐transfer embryo evaluation by increasing confidence that genomic assessments are based on sufficiently robust genotyping data.

The results of this study have broader implications for the field of animal breeding. By enabling the selection of genetically superior individuals at the embryonic stage, EGS can accelerate the genetic progress of livestock populations, contributing to improved production efficiency, disease resistance, and overall animal welfare. The integration of advanced molecular techniques with traditional breeding approaches represents a promising avenue for the sustainable development of the livestock industry. However, several challenges remain to be addressed, including the standardization of the biopsy procedure, the improvement of DNA amplification fidelity, and the development of robust quality control measures to ensure the reliability of genetic assessments. Future research should focus on these areas to further enhance the feasibility and effectiveness of EGS in practical breeding. Overall, our study provides a foundation for the continued advancement of EGS and underscores its potential to transform animal breeding practices.
