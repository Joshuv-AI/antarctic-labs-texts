Protocol for inducible expression of small non-coding RNAs using a tet-on system in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Regina Nostramo, Anita K Hopper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryA valuable approach to understanding small non-coding RNA (sncRNA) function is to regulate their expression. However, inducible expression of sncRNAs can be challenging. Here, we present a protocol to express sncRNAs in Saccharomyces cerevisiae using a two-plasmid inducible tet-on system. We describe the steps for generating plasmids and inducing and assessing sncRNA expression. This protocol can be used to express and elucidate the function of tRNA introns and has the potential to identify functions of a wide range of sncRNAs.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Nostramo et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Genetics, Model Organisms, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

With the advancement of RNA sequencing technologies, small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs) are being discovered at a rapid rate, necessitating novel methodologies to determine their functions. A standard approach to understanding the function of gene products is to assess the impact of their absence or overproduction. While sncRNAs can often be easily deleted from genomes like Saccharomyces cerevisiae, overexpression can be more challenging. Unlike protein-coding genes, which contain defined promoters, terminators and transcriptional start and stop sites needed for RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription, genes encoding sncRNAs lack these components. Furthermore, many sncRNAs are embedded within other gene transcripts.

We were interested in determining whether tRNA introns could function as sncRNAs in S. cerevisiae and needed a methodology to induce their expression. Since tRNA introns are embedded within tRNA genes, overexpression of any given intron-containing tRNA would have the desired effect of increasing levels of the free intron. However, it would also increase levels of the mature tRNA, altering the overall cellular tRNA pool, which could have global impacts on the proteome.2 To circumvent this issue, we developed a 2-plasmid, tet-on system to inducibly express the tRNAIle intron, without altering mature tRNAIle levels.1 Since this publication, we have utilized this system to successfully express other tRNA intron sequences, specifically the tRNATrp intron and the tRNASerCGA intron. Thus, this system is amenable to express likely any sncRNA.

The protocol consists of four major sections. The first section describes how to obtain and/or design the necessary plasmids to express a sncRNA of interest. Section two explains how to transform these plasmids into S. cerevisiae. The third section details how to induce expression of the sncRNA. Lastly, section four describes how to assess the expression and localization of the sncRNA. Together, this protocol will result in tightly controlled expression of a sncRNA in S. cerevisiae, the effects of which can be analyzed by a range of downstream approaches.

Plasmid acquisition and design

Timing: variable

This system employs two plasmids, as depicted in Figure 1. Plasmid #1 is a low-copy plasmid (YCp - Yeast centromere-containing plasmid) that contains the reverse tetracycline transactivator (rtTA) gene under the control of a strong constitutive promoter, and the T7 polymerase gene, modified to contain a nuclear localization signal (NLS), under the control of a tetracycline responsive element (TRE). Plasmid #2 is a high-copy plasmid (YEp - Yeast episomal plasmid) that contains the sequence of the sncRNA of interest under the control of a T7 promoter and terminator.

Overview of the two-plasmid tet-on system to inducibly express a sncRNA of interest

rtTA, reverse tetracycline transactivator; DOX, Doxycycline; TRE, tetracycline responsive element; tetO7, 7 consecutive tetracycline operator sequences; NLS, Nuclear localization signal; ITS, initially transcribed sequence.

When yeast cells bearing these two plasmids are treated with doxycycline (DOX), a DOX-rtTA complex forms, which alters the confirmation of the rtTA and allows its binding to the TRE. This results in RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription of the T7 polymerase gene. After translation, the T7 polymerase localizes to the nucleus where it binds to the T7 promoter of Plasmid #2. This results in expression of the sncRNA of interest.

Subcellular fractionation to assess sncRNA localization

Timing: 14–16 h to grow yeast cultures; 2–3 h for fractionation procedure

This part of the protocol describes how to assess sncRNA subcellular localization using subcellular fractionation. In this system, the sncRNA of interest is transcribed by a nuclearly-localized T7 polymerase. Therefore, T7 polymerase-mediated transcription of the sncRNA of interest takes place in the nucleus. If small enough in size, the RNA should passively diffuse through the nuclear pores to the cytoplasm. The following rapid fractionation protocol, which was modified from Neumann et al.,11 will allow for the separation of yeast cells into cytoplasmic and organellar fractions, from which RNAs and proteins can be isolated. Subsequent northern blot or RT-qPCR analysis can then be used to assess sncRNA localization and western blot analysis can be used to assess protein localization to demonstrate fraction purity.

Expected outcomes

This protocol will result in reliable, inducible expression of likely any sncRNA. However, the extent of sncRNA expression will vary depending on factors such as the yeast strain, the sequence of the sncRNA (particularly the 5′ end), the length of the RNA, and the dose and time of DOX treatment. Alterations in the T7 terminator sequence in Plasmid #2 also results in sncRNA products of varying lengths and expression levels (Figure 7). Examples of how sncRNA expression can be impacted by each of these factors are provided below and should serve as a guide to optimize expression of any sncRNA. Regardless of the extent of sncRNA expression, the ability to compare cells treated with and without DOX makes this a powerful approach to determine sncRNA function.

Since the sncRNA to be expressed is under the control of a T7 promoter and terminator, it is important to first validate and optimize T7 polymerase expression in response to DOX treatment. For reasons presently unknown, T7 polymerase mRNA levels vary widely based on the yeast strain. For example, in wild-type cells (BY4741) expressing Plasmid #1 and Plasmid #2, either as an empty vector or containing the sequence of the tRNAIle intron, we observed a 150-fold increase in T7 polymerase mRNA levels following 4 h treatment with 10 μg/mL DOX, as compared to untreated controls (Figure 8A).1 In a BY4741 strain lacking the endogenous tRNAIle introns, T7 polymerase mRNA levels increased by only 50-fold under these same conditions, despite expressing the same Plasmids #1 and #2 (Figure 8B).

Impact of yeast strain on DOX-induced T7 polymerase mRNA levels from plasmid #1

Wild-type (BY4741) (A) or tRNAIle intron deletion (B) strains expressing Plasmid #1 (Figure 2) and Plasmid #2 (Figure 3) were treated with 10 μg/mL DOX for 4 hrs. T7 polymerase mRNA levels were assessed by RT-qPCR. Data expressed as mean ± SEM. n = 4 per group.

Despite the large differences in DOX-induced T7 polymerase mRNA levels between these two strains, levels of the ITS-tRNAIle intron expressed from Plasmid #2 displayed much less variation. For example, when Plasmid #2 contained the sequence of the tRNAITS-Ile intron, treatment with 10 μg/mL DOX for 3 h elicited a 14-fold increase in tRNAITS-Ile intron levels in wild-type cells and a 10-fold increase in cells lacking the endogenous tRNAIle introns (Figure 9A). However, this was not the case for all yeast strains. In order to study the contributions of different RNA decay pathways on the tRNAIle intron-mediated regulation of gene expression, we utilized this tet-on overexpression system to express the tRNAITS-Ile intron in yeast strains bearing deletions or temperature sensitive mutations in components of different RNA decay pathways, such as the nuclear and cytoplasmic 5′ to 3′ exonucleases Rat1 and Xrn1, respectively, and the RNA exosome components Dis3, Rrp6 and Ski7.12,13,14 Interestingly, strains defective in these various RNA turnover components displayed considerable variation in the levels of the tRNAITS-Ile intron expressed from Plasmid #2. For example, DOX-induced tRNAITS-Ile intron levels were increased by 57-fold in dis3-1 cells, 20-fold in rat1-1 cells, 8-fold in rrp6Δ cells, 5-fold in xrn1Δ cells, and 0.2-fold in ski7Δ cells (as compared to untreated controls) (Figure 9B).

The RNA sequence also impacts expression levels. For example, DOX-induced levels of tRNAITS-Ile intron displayed small variations when 9 nucleotide stretches in the interior of the 60 nucleotide tRNAIle intron sequence were mutated to the complementary nucleotide (i.e. G to C). For example, changing nucleotides 25–33 of the tRNAIle intron resulted in a 10-fold increase in tRNAITS-Ile intron levels in response to DOX treatment as compared to 14-fold with the wild-type tRNAIle intron sequence (Figure 10A). Changing nucleotides 30–38 of the tRNAIle intron had an even larger inhibitory effect on expression, with only a 7-fold increase in tRNAITS-Ile intron levels following DOX treatment. Altering the sequence of the tRNAIle intron also impacted the length of RNAs expressed, as shown by northern blot (Figure 10B). This is despite changes only to the sequence of the tRNAIle intron in Plasmid #2, but not the overall length of the intron.

Alterations at the 5′ end of the RNA sequence have the greatest impact on expression. This is largely due to the high number of abortive transcripts that are produced by T7 polymerase when the initially transcribed sequence (ITS) of the transcription start site, specifically GGGAGA, is absent.5,6 To circumvent this issue, the addition of 2–3 G’s or the GGGAGA sequence at the 5′ end of the RNA of interest can greatly improve expression. For example, DOX treatment elicited a 0.5-fold increase in tRNAIle intron levels when the 5′ end of the this RNA, which normally begins with a single G, was either unamended or amended to start with 2 additional G’s (Figure 5). When the ITS sequence of GGGAGA was added at the 5′ end, DOX treatment elicited a 7-fold increase. The effect was much different when the 19-nt tRNASerCGA intron sequence was inserted into Plasmid #2. Northern blot analysis shows that the endogenous tRNASerCGA intron sequence, which does not begin with a G, produces a small amount of a product that is >100 nts in length (Figure 11). The addition of 3 G’s or the ITS sequence to the 5′ end yields a major product that is much shorter in length and expressed at much higher levels than the unmodified intron. This product is substantially longer than the endogenous 19-nt intron and much closer in length to the approximately 100-nt end-processed, intron-containing tRNASerCGA. In addition, the presence of the ITS sequence results in many additional read-through transcripts at lengths upwards of >100 nts. Further optimization of the 5′ and T7 terminator sequences, as was done for the tRNAIle intron, would presumably result in an RNA closer to the expected size.

Lastly, variations in the expression of the desired sncRNA can also be expected to be dependent on the T7 terminator sequence in Plasmid #2. The native T7 terminator sequence is inefficient, allowing for read-through transcription to serve as a mechanism of T7 phage regulation of gene expression.7 Mutations to the native T7 terminator sequence in Plasmid #2 will allow for enhanced expression and/or products that are closer to the desired length. The best T7 terminator sequence will vary depending on the sequence of the sncRNA of interest. For example, of the four different T7 terminator sequences that were placed downstream of the tRNAITS-le intron, three (Var1, Var2, Var3) resulted in the expression of a product of the expected size, but at much differing levels. Two terminators (Hyb6 and Var1) yielded products closer in size to that produced by the native T7 terminator (Figure 7A). Similar findings were observed with the ITS-tRNASerCGA and ITS-tRNATrp introns (Figures 7B and 7C). Alteration of the T7 terminator sequence yielded products of differing lengths and abundance.

Using this two plasmid tet-on system, it is expected that the desired sncRNA is transcribed in the nucleus since the T7 polymerase gene in Plasmid #1 has been modified to contain a nuclear localization signal. If the size of the desired sncRNA is below the nuclear pore diffusion limit, it should passively diffuse to the cytoplasm after transcription. This was the case for the tRNAITS-Ile intron, as shown by subcellular fractionation (Figure 12).1 Following DOX treatment, the tRNAITS-Ile intron accumulated in both the cytoplasmic and organellar fractions, with the intron species closest in size to the endogenous tRNAIle intron predominantly found in the cytoplasmic fraction. For larger sncRNAs, subcellular fractionation should be utilized to confirm localization to the cytoplasm.

Limitations

Although this system can be utilized to inducibly express any sncRNA, the extent of RNA expression can be greatly affected by the sequence and length of the sncRNA. For many sncRNAs, this can be overcome by adjusting the variables described in detail above, such as the sequence upstream of the 5′ end of the sncRNA, the T7 terminator sequence and the DOX dose and treatment time. There are likely scenarios where sncRNA expression cannot be adequately optimized to yield sufficient expression. For example, in response to DOX treatment, cells lacking the Ski7 gene displayed no induction of tRNAIle intron expression (Figure 9B).

Another limitation of this approach is that the T7 polymerase is prone to transcription read-through, making it difficult to generate a sncRNA with a defined 3′ end. Alterations to the T7 terminator sequence can greatly impact the site of transcription termination, as discussed in detail above. However, depending on the sncRNA sequence and length, this approach may yield single or multiple sncRNA products that are longer than desired. Although sncRNAs below the diffusion limit of nuclear pores should be able to passively diffuse to the cytoplasm after their transcription in the nucleus, longer sncRNAs may not be able to access the cytoplasm and be trapped in the nucleus.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The sncRNA is not expressed following DOX treatment.

Potential solution

There are several possibilities for the lack of sncRNA expression. To identify and solve the problem, follow these steps in the order listed.

•Ensure that the T7 polymerase is expressed following DOX treatment. Some yeast strains may display poor T7 polymerase expression in response to DOX treatment and may require higher DOX doses and/or longer treatment times to induce expression.

Ensure that the T7 polymerase is expressed following DOX treatment. Some yeast strains may display poor T7 polymerase expression in response to DOX treatment and may require higher DOX doses and/or longer treatment times to induce expression.

•If the T7 polymerase is expressed, amend the sequence upstream of the sncRNA in Plasmid #2. Add the ITS sequence (GGGAGA) or 2–3 G’s to the 5′ end of the sncRNA sequence. Refer to step 2 in “before you begin” section.

If the T7 polymerase is expressed, amend the sequence upstream of the sncRNA in Plasmid #2. Add the ITS sequence (GGGAGA) or 2–3 G’s to the 5′ end of the sncRNA sequence. Refer to step 2 in “before you begin” section.

•Alter the T7 terminator sequence. See Table 1 for examples of potential terminator sequences. Alternatively, design additional T7 terminator sequences. Examples of additional T7 terminators and their impacts on transcription termination can be found in Calvopina-Chavez et al. (2022).8

Problem 2

Yeast strains which previously expressed the desired sncRNA in response to DOX treatment, no longer respond to DOX.

Potential solution

One solution is to start with fresh yeast cultures and repeat the transformation with Plasmids #1 and #2. In some instances, mutant yeast strains can lose the ability to respond to DOX over time, particularly if the strain grows poorly or is deficient in an important cellular component. We observed this phenomenon with cells that lacked the endogenous tRNAIle introns. Over time, these cells would lose the ability to express the tRNAITS-Ile intron from Plasmid #2 in response to DOX treatment.

Problem 3

The sncRNA is not the correct size.

Potential solution

This issue can be addressed by altering the T7 terminator sequence. Several T7 terminator sequences and their impacts on tRNAIle, tRNATrp and tRNASerCGA intron expression are shown in Table 1 and Figure 7. Additional T7 terminator sequences and their impacts on transcription termination can be found in Calvopina-Chavez et al.8

Problem 4

The rtTA is not expressed in the presence or absence of DOX.

Potential solution

Confirm the presence of Plasmid #1 in the yeast strain. If present, sequence the plasmid to confirm that there are no mutations in the rtTA gene.

Problem 5

The T7 polymerase is not expressed in response to DOX treatment.

Potential solution

Confirm the presence of Plasmid #1 in the yeast strain by PCR using primers designed to amplify the T7 polymerase and/or the rtTA. If present, sequence the plasmid to confirm that there are no mutations in the T7 polymerase gene. If the sequence is correct, alter the dose and time of DOX treatment. Alternatively, utilize a different yeast strain background. For mutant strains, utilize a different mutant allele or generate a mutant strain using an alternative approach (i.e. auxin-inducible degron vs. deletion).
