Comprehensive analyses of pathogen loads and immune responses alongside the gastrointestinal tract upon long-term Salmonella Typhimurium-infection of clinically resistant mice
Authors: Noah A. Maisch, Soraya Mousavi, Anja A. Kühl, Daniela Paclik, Stefan Bereswill, Markus M. Heimesaat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractC57BL/6 mice serve as in vivo-model mimicking acute enteritis caused by Salmonella Typhimurium (S. Tm) but succumb to infection within a week. CBA mice, however, were shown to be clinically resistant to S. Tm-infection, survive the acute phase and are hence suitable to study chronic infection. We performed a comprehensive survey of enteropathogenic loads and immunopathological responses in intestinal compartments during the early and late phase of S. Tm-infection. Upon oral challenge of CBA mice, the enteropathogens colonized the intestines with highest loads in the cecum and colon. Whereas all mice survived the 4-week observation period and did not display any symptoms, marked inflammatory cell damage became overt in the cecum as early as 24 h post-infection (p.i.) that was accompanied by increased numbers of large intestinal innate immune cells including neutrophils, macrophages, and monocytes on days 1 and 14 p.i., whereas recruitment of adaptive immune cells such as T and B lymphocytes and regulatory T cells to the cecum and colon was pronounced on days 14 and 28 p.i. Depending on the time point S. Tm-infection resulted in differential cytokine secretion alongside the intestinal tract. Collectively, our results underscore CBA mice as a model to study chronic S. Tm-infection including long-term fecal pathogen shedding.

Introduction

Salmonella enterica is a Gram-negative, facultative intracellular bacterial species, that is divided into more than 2,600 serovars. S. enterica serovars Typhimurium (S. Tm) and Enteritidis, for instance, are of particular importance as a major contributor of food-borne infections worldwide, imposing substantial health care and socioeconomic costs due to their broad spectrum of hosts and transmission through animal products [1–3]. In the European Union alone, S. enterica was the second leading cause of food-borne bacterial infections in 2023 with a total of 77,486 reported cases (i.e., 18 per 100,000 capita) causing 14,801 hospitalizations [4]. Immunocompetent individuals that have been infected with S. enterica usually present with acute gastroenteritis characterized by diarrhea, abdominal pain, vomiting, headache, muscle pain, fever, and chills [2, 5, 6]. Most commonly, the gastroenteritis is self-limited with a duration of three to seven days and therefore no antibiotic treatment is needed. Yet, some patients develop severe enterocolitis lasting up to weeks while others suffer long-lasting colonization of the stool with more than 1% of patients becoming chronic fecal shedders of Salmonella bacteria after one year [6–8].

For a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogen-host interactions, distinct in vivo Salmonella infection models have been developed. The streptomycin pretreatment mouse model for S. Tm infection, for instance, is widely used and valued for its robustness and reproducibility [9]. Application of the antibiotic streptomycin to mice prior infection is essential to overcome colonization resistance by providing ecological niches for the pathogen within the complex commensal gut microbiota and thereby facilitating intestinal infection and inflammation [9–11]. However, unlike humans mainly suffering from gastroenteritis, mice in the streptomycin mouse model predominantly develop colitis upon oral S. Tm challenge [12]. Within this context, mouse strains can be broadly classified as susceptible or resistant with both developing colitis but exhibiting markedly different disease outcomes [13, 14]. A key determinant of resistance is the macrophage-encoded solute carrier family 11a member 1 (Slc11a1), also known as natural resistance-associated macrophage protein 1 (Nramp1). Slc11a1 is responsible for the deprivation of magnesium within the Salmonella-containing phagosomes leading to an earlier initiation of the immune response and thus limiting systemic spread of S. Tm with potential fatal outcome [14, 15]. In its absence, as in C57BL/6 mice carrying a loss-of function allele of Slc11a1, hosts develop acute and severe disease and succumb to infection within one week, rendering them unsuitable for long-term studies [16]. In contrast, strains harboring a functional Slc11a1 allele, such as CBA mice, survive the acute phase and therefore enable the study of prolonged infection courses, including chronic inflammation and fibrosis in Salmonella-induced colitis [17–20].

Typically, Salmonella infections are characterized by three overlapping phases: (i) oral uptake and colonization of the small and large intestines within hours post-infection (p.i.); (ii) a plateau phase lasting days to weeks in which Salmonella bacteria replicate in phagocytic cells within the intestinal tract, mesenteric lymph nodes as well as systemic organs; and finally (iii) induction of innate and adaptive host immune responses to eradicate the invaded pathogens [21, 22]. Initial epithelial invasion within the intestines is mediated by the Salmonella Pathogenicity Islands-1 (SPI-1) encoded type-III secretion system (T3SS-1), which facilitates translocation across the intestinal epithelium [22, 23]. Following entry, Salmonella proceeds into the subepithelial Peyer's patches where members of the mononuclear phagocyte system such as monocytes and macrophages become infected [22]. Subsequently, intracellular survival and replication within phagocytes is facilitated by the SPI-2-encoded type III secretion system (T3SS-2), which is essential for systemic dissemination and persistence [24, 25]. Early during infection, intestinal epithelial cells and innate immune cells are activated by distinct pathogen-associated molecular pattern molecules (PAMPs) such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and flagellin via toll-like receptors (TLRs) [22]. In consequence, infiltration of the lamina propria with neutrophilic granulocytes as well as macrophages occurs coincidently with the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and interleukin (IL)-6 [9, 21]. Despite the important role of the innate immune system in the first hours of infection, the adaptive immune response mediated by T lymphocytes in Peyer's patches is initiated rapidly, can be detected within the first hours of infection, and is essentially involved in clearance of the enteropathogens, whereas B lymphocytes are first and foremost required to ensure formation of immunity against secondary infection by antibody production [26–28].

The outcome of Salmonella infection in mice is governed by a tightly regulated interplay between innate and adaptive immune responses, which is critically influenced by host genetic factors. While susceptible murine strains rapidly succumb to acute disease, resistant mouse strains such as CBA mice survive the acute phase of infection and therefore serve as a model of acute and chronic colitis and associated complications, mimicking distinct features of human inflammatory bowel diseases [17, 29].

However, despite their increasing use in studies on chronic inflammation and colitis-associated immunopathology, comprehensive temporal analyses of histopathological changes, immune cell dynamics, and cytokine responses in resistant CBA mice remain limited [17, 29]. In particular, information on the kinetics of the infection process is scarce. Therefore, the present study provides a systematic analysis of long-term S. enterica subsp. enterica ser. Typhimurium infection in resistant CBA mice with respect to pathogen loads and pro-inflammatory including histopathogical sequelae alongside the gastrointestinal tract over time.

Material and methods

Mice and streptomycin pretreatment

CBA/J wildtype mice were purchased from Janvier Laboratories (Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) and housed in the Forschungseinrichtungen für Experimentelle Medizin, Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin, Germany under specific pathogen free (SPF) conditions. Mice were maintained in IVC cages within an experimental semi-barrier under standard conditions (i.e., 22–24 °C room temperature, 55 ± 15% humidity, 12 h light/12 h dark cycle) and had ad libitum access to autoclaved water and standard chow (food pellets: ssniff R/M-H, V1534-300, Sniff, Soest, Germany). Eight-week-old female and male mice were orally challenged with a single dose of 20 mg streptomycin (Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) in a total volume of 0.3 mL sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) by gavage 24 h prior infection (day -1).

Salmonella infection and assessment of fecal shedding

S. enterica subsp. enterica ser. Typhimurium (Salmonella Typhimurium; S. Tm) strain SL1344 (DSM24522) was thawed from frozen stocks and grown on columbia agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood and selective XLD agar plates (both from Oxoid, Wesel, Germany) for 24 h at 37 °C. On day 0, mice were perorally infected with 3 × 106 colony-forming units (CFU) S. Tm in a total volume of 0.3 mL sterile PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) by gavage. At defined time points p.i., fecal and gastrointestinal luminal S. Tm bacteria were quantified by counting of CFU after growth from serial dilutions of respective samples on columbia agar plates supplemented with 5% sheep blood and selective XLD agar plates (both from Oxoid, Wesel, Germany) for at least 24 h at 37 °C. The detection limit of viable pathogens was 100 CFU per g fecal matter.

Sampling procedures

On days 1, 14, and 28 p.i., mice were sacrificed by CO2 asphyxiation. Ex vivo biopsies from the terminal ileum, cecum, and distal colon as well as luminal samples from the stomach, duodenum, terminal ileum, cecum, and distal colon were derived under aseptic conditions. From each mouse, respective intestinal samples were collected in parallel for subsequent microbiological, histopathological, and immunological analyses.

Histopathology

For histopathological analyses, ileal, cecal, and colonic ex vivo biopsies were immediately fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin and 1–2 µm sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Small intestinal histopathological sequelae of S. Tm infection were quantitated using a standardized scoring system (light microscopy, 100× magnification) as reported earlier [30]. To evaluate the severity of histomorphological changes in cecal and colonic samples, another established scoring scheme was applied [31].

In situ immunohistochemistry

Quantitative in situ immunohistochemical analyses were performed in ex vivo biopsies derived from the terminal ileum, cecum, and colon following immediate fixation in 4% formalin and embedding in paraffin as reported previously [32, 33]. In brief, to detect apoptotic epithelial cells, neutrophils, macrophages/monocytes, T lymphocytes, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and B lymphocytes, ileal, cecal, and colonic paraffin sections (3 µm) were stained with primary antibodies against cleaved caspase-3 (Casp3; Asp175, Cell Signaling, Beverly, MA, USA; 1:200), myeloperoxidase (MPO7; no. A0398, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; 1:500), F4/80 (no. 14-4801, clone BM8, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA; 1:50), CD3 (no. N1580, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; 1:10), FOXP3 (clone FJK-165, no. 14-5773, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA; 1:100), and B220 (no. 337 14-0452-81, eBioscience; San Diego, CA, USA; 1:200), respectively. Positively stained cells were quantitated by a blinded independent investigator applying light microscopy. The average number of respective positively stained cells in each sample was determined within at least six high power fields (HPF, 0.287 mm2; 400 × magnification).

Inflammatory cytokines

Ileal, cecal, and colonic samples (approximately 1 cm2) that were longitudinally cut and washed in sterile PBS were transferred to 24-flat-bottom well culture plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 500 µL serum-free RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), penicillin (100 µg mL−1; Biochrom, Berlin, Germany) and streptomycin (100 µg mL−1; Biochrom, Berlin, Germany). After an 18-h incubation period at 37 °C, respective culture supernatants were tested for IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-10 by the Mouse Inflammation Cytometric Bead Assay (BD Biosciences, Heidelberg, Germany) using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Heidelberg, Germany).

Statistical analyses

Medians and significance levels were calculated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6; San Diego, CA, USA). Normalization of data was assessed by the Anderson-Darling test. The Student's t test and Mann-Whitney test were used for pairwise comparisons of normally and not normally distributed data, respectively. For multiple comparisons, the one-sided ANOVA test with Tukey post-correction (for normally distributed data) and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post-correction (for not normally distributed data) were applied. Two-sided probability (p) values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. Data were pooled from two independent experiments.

Results

Time course of luminal S. Tm loads in distinct gastrointestinal compartments

Following oral infection with 3 × 106 viable S. Tm bacteria, all CBA mice survived until day 28 p.i. and did not display overt Salmonella-induced clinical signs such as diarrhea, fecal blood or significant weight loss during the observation period (data not shown). To assess the gastrointestinal pathogen loads over time p.i., we performed cultural analyses of distinct luminal samples at defined time points. As early as 24 h p.i., viable S. Tm bacteria were found alongside the gastrointestinal tract with approximately four orders of magnitude higher median loads in the large intestines (approximately 108 CFU g−1 luminal cecal and colonic contents) compared to those obtained from more proximal compartments such as the stomach, duodenum, and ileum (respective median S. Tm numbers up to 104 CFU per g; p < 0.05–0.001; Fig. 1A). On day 14 p.i., however, the cecal S. Tm numbers were still higher compared to those detected in the stomach, duodenum, and ileum (p < 0.05–0.001), whereas the colonic pathogen loads were higher than the duodenal bacterial counts only (p < 0.01; Fig. 1B). At the end of the observation period, the numbers of the S. Tm bacteria cultured from the cecum and colon were still higher if compared to the duodenal pathogens (p < 0.05–0.01) but did not differ from those derived from the stomach and ileum when compared with each other in the multivariate analyses (not significant (n.s.); Fig. 1C).

During the time course of infection, S. Tm loads in distinct gastrointestinal compartments determined by the luminal bacterial numbers remained stable in the stomach, duodenum, and ileum until day 28 p.i. (Fig. 2). In the cecum, however, the median pathogen counts had declined from 108 CFU g−1 on day 1 p.i. by approximately 1.5 orders of magnitude until the end of the observation period on day 28 p.i. (p < 0.05–0.01 versus days 1 and 14 p.i.; Fig. 2), whereas in the most distal compartment, the colonic luminal S. Hence, all infected CBA mice survived for 4 weeks and did not display clinical signs of S. Tm infection. The applied enteropathogens could establish along the gastrointestinal tract with the highest loads in the cecum and colon, and bacterial numbers slightly declined in the large intestines until 28 days p.i.

Microscopic inflammatory changes in the small and large intestines over time following oral S. Tm infection of CBA mice

In order to assess microscopic inflammatory sequelae of Salmonella infection we quantitated histopathological changes in the mucosa of distinct small and large intestinal tissues by defined scores (Fig. 3). In the ileum, if at all, only minimal and focal inflammatory cell infiltrates, but with an intact epithelial layer could be observed in single animals on days 14 and 28 p.i. (n.s.; Fig. 3A). In the cecum, however, pronounced histomorphological changes became overt shortly after infection given significantly increased histopathological scores as early as 24 h p.i. (p < 0.05–0.001 versus naive), with highest median values on day 14 p.i. (Fig. 3B) indicative for marked inflammatory cellular infiltrates in the cecal mucosa and submucosa, marked hyperplasia of the epithelium with marked goblet cell loss, multiple crypt abscesses and extended ulcerations with pseudopolyps (not shown). In the colon, no overt histopathological changes were visible 24 h p.i. Two weeks later, however, increased histopathological scores could be determined (p < 0.001 versus naive and day 1 p.i.; Fig. 3C) indicative for moderate inflammatory cell infiltrates in the colonic mucosa and submucosa with moderate hyperplasia of the colonic epithelia and goblet cell loss, cryptitis and ulcerations (not shown).

We next determined apoptotic epithelial cell damage in distinct intestinal segments by quantitating intestinal epithelial cells that stained positive for cleaved caspase-3. In line with the results from the histopathological survey, no significant increases in apoptotic cell numbers were detected in the ileum over time post Salmonella infection (n.s.; Fig. 4A), whereas those obtained in the cecum had multifold increased on days 1, 14, and 28 p.i. (p < 0.05–0.001 versus naive mice) with highest medium numbers on day 14 p.i. (Fig. 4B). In the colon, increased apoptotic epithelial cell numbers were detected on days 14 and 28 p.i. (p < 0.01–0.001 versus naive), but not at the earliest time point (Fig. 4C). Hence, S. Tm infection resulted in marked microscopic inflammatory including apoptotic cell damage in the cecum as early as 24 h p.i., whereas in the ileum neither histopathological changes nor enhanced epithelial cell apoptosis could be observed over time. In the colon S. Tm induced epithelial apoptosis rather at later time points (i.e., at day 14 p.i.).

Innate and adaptive immune cell accumulation in the small and large intestines of CBA mice over time following oral S. Tm infection

Next, we analyzed the S. Tm-induced inflammatory immune cell responses over time applying quantitative in situ immunohistochemistry. Whereas S. Tm infection did not result in pronounced accumulation of neutrophilic granulocytes to the ileum over the entire observation period (n.s. versus naive; Fig. 5A), markedly increased neutrophil numbers were observed in the mucosa of the cecum and colon derived from mice on days 1 and 14 p.i. (p < 0.01–0.001 versus naive), which were reduced to basal levels at the end of the experiments (n.s. versus naive; Fig. 5B and C). When staining the intestinal tissue sections against F4/80 (Fig. 6), we found S. Tm-induced increases in cecal and colonic macrophages and monocytes on days 1, 14, and 28 p.i. (p < 0.001 versus naive; Fig. 6B and C) with higher counts at the later time points (days 14 and 28 p.i.) compared to day 1 p.i. (p < 0.01–0.001; Fig. 6B and C). Like the neutrophils, ileal macrophage and monocyte numbers were rather unaffected by the pathogenic challenge (n.s.; Fig. 6A).

Furthermore, we surveyed the adaptive immune cell numbers alongside the intestinal tract over time following S. Tm infection. In line with the analyzed innate immune cell subsets, we did not observe S. Tm-induced recruitment of adaptive immune cells such as T lymphocytes (n.s. versus naive; Fig. 7A), Tregs (Fig. 8A), and B lymphocytes (Fig. 9A) to the terminal ileum. Discussion

Summary of the main results

Our present detailed microbiological and immunological investigation of chronic Salmonella infection in clinically resistant CBA mice revealed that following oral S. Tm challenge i.) CBA mice did not present any overt clinical signs such as enteritis or wasting symptoms and survived the 4-week observation period; ii.) the enteropathogens could stably establish within the ileum, cecum, and colon with the highest loads in the large intestine; iii.) marked microscopic inflammatory damage in the cecum including epithelial cell apoptosis became evident as early as 24 h p.i.; iv.) that were accompanied by differential pro- and anti-inflammatory immune responses.

Intestinal pathogen loads, clinical and histopathological outcome upon S. Tm infection

Our S. Tm infection experiments confirmed the effective and stable establishment of the enteropathogens within the intestinal tract of streptomycin pretreated CBA mice over time. The observed cecal and colonic luminal S. Tm loads of up to 108 CFU g−1 and of approximately 104 CFU g−1 in the ileum lumen are well in line with previous findings in infected C57BL/6 and 129Sv/J mice [29, 34]. Despite the high intestinal enteropathogenic burdens all mice survived and did not show any overt S. Tm-induced clinical manifestations such as enteritis and wasting symptoms until day 28 p.i. Our results are confirmed by a previous study from Johnson et al. who surveyed the outcome in different resistant mouse strains such as CBA, DBA, and 129S1/SvlmJ mice upon oral challenge with S. Tm SL1344 bacteria [17]. Nonetheless, the absence of clinical signs such as enteritis and wasting symptoms was surprising to us given the marked microscopically overt inflammatory changes observed in the colon, but also the cecum harboring the highest intestinal luminal S. Tm burdens. Remarkably, infected mice presented with pronounced Salmonella-induced histopathological changes and apoptotic cell responses in the cecum as early as 24 h p.i., whereas respective pathogen-induced sequelae were detected in the colon later-on (on days 14 and 28 p.i.). This delayed gut mucosal tissue-damaging effect of S. Tm in the colon versus the cecum is in line with previous findings in resistant 129SvJ mice that had been orally challenged with the same S. Tm strain like in our study [29]. The authors of another work hypothesized that the differences in the time course of developing S. Tm-induced tissue damages in different compartments of the large intestines might be due to distinct features of cecal and colonic tissue architecture. For instance, the colonic mucus was described as relatively thick contributing to a relatively tight epithelial barrier whereas the mucus in the cecum displayed gaps to the underlying epithelium enabling bacterial penetration [35].

Furthermore, we observed pronounced compartment-specific and time-dependent differences in apoptotic cell numbers in the intestinal epithelia as quantified by cleaved caspase-3 staining. Consistent with previous reports, S. Tm induces apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells via caspase-3-dependent mechanisms operating at distinct stages of infection [36]. While SPI-1 effector SipA has been shown to induce apoptosis rapidly following bacterial invasion, T3SS-2 associated apoptosis occurs at later time points, approximately 24–28 h p.i. [37, 38]. In this context, the early and pronounced increase in apoptotic cells observed in the cecal epithelia likely reflects the known tropism of S. Tm for the cecum in the streptomycin pretreatment model [34]. Furthermore, the persistent elevation of epithelial apoptosis in the cecum and colon until d28 p.i. is consistent with persistent bacterial colonization and ongoing host-pathogen interaction in these regions.

Interestingly, the distal small intestines were rather unaffected by Salmonella infection given a lack of Salmonella-induced increases in apoptotic ileal epithelia and histopathological scores p.i. In support, a previous study revealed that only 10% of the streptomycin pretreated mice develop enteropathy in the terminal ileum after oral S. Tm infection [9].

Intestinal inflammatory immune responses following S. Tm infection over time

Innate immune cells in the intestinal tract

When surveying host immune responses over time following oral S. Tm infection of resistant CBA mice, we observed a marked accumulation of distinct innate immune cells such as neutrophils, macrophages, and monocytes in the cecum and colon as early as 24 h p.i. This is consistent with previous findings from Grassl and coworkers underscoring pronounced cecal and colonic inflammatory cell accumulation 6 and 24 h after infection of clinically resistant 129SvJ mice with the same Salmonella strain [9]. Furthermore, maximum neutrophil numbers in the cecal and colonic lamina propria declined back to basal levels until day 28 following infection of CBA mice which is well in line with results from infected 129SvJ mice displaying only few neutrophils in the cecal mucosa and submucosa by days 21 and 40 p.i. [9].

Numbers of other innate immune subsets such as F4/80+ macrophages and monocytes were also elevated in both large intestinal tissue sites as early as 24 h p.i. and the cellular accumulation into the cecal and colonic lamina propria was further accelerated until day 14 and remained on elevated levels until the endpoint on day 28 p.i. This transition from an early neutrophil-dominated acute inflammatory response towards a more sustained mononuclear cell-driven phase of intestinal inflammation upon Salmonella infection has also been shown by others [39]. Of note, neither innate immune cell numbers changed in the terminal ileum in response to murine S. Tm infection.

Adaptive immune cells in the intestinal tract

We also observed profound activation of the adaptive immune system over the course of S. Tm infection in resistant CBA mice, which became, however, more evident at later time points (days 14 and 28 p.i.), as reflected by increased numbers of T lymphocytes, regulatory T cells, and B lymphocytes. Overall, this supports a transition from an early innate immune response towards a more adaptive immune phase during infection.

As stated earlier, a pronounced T cell response is crucial for the host's clearance of the enteropathogen. Here, our observations are well in line with earlier findings reporting the role of T cells including Th1, Th17, and of CD8+ T cells, in orchestrating the intestinal immune responses through the activation of macrophages and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IFN-γ [22, 26, 40, 41]. Interestingly, a previous study investigating S. Tm SL1344 in C57BL/6 mice without prior administration of streptomycin showed infection-induced activation of CD4+ T cells in the Peyer's patches of the small intestine as early as 3 h post-challenge [26, 27]. However, in our experiments, we did not observe increases in CD3+ T lymphocytes at the earliest assessed time point (i.e., 24 h p.i.) in any of the three compartments. The accumulation of T cells in the cecum and colon until the end of our experiment may reflect ongoing bacterial colonization as well as antigen exposure and local immune activation at primary sites of S. Tm. However, T cells were also shown to exert a direct immunopathological effect during an early hit of the immune cascade as well as to have a secondary impact through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ [42, 43].

Coincident with the delayed accumulation of T cells, we further observed significant increases of Tregs within the cecum and colon of the infected mice. Considering the mild clinical symptoms of our mice, Tregs may play a crucial role in limiting excessive immune-mediated tissue damage during S. Tm infection of resistant CBA mice. Supporting this, a study using Treg-deficient mice has demonstrated that the absence of Tregs results in decreased survival, increased apoptosis of colonic epithelial cells, and impaired intestinal barrier integrity without affecting bacterial persistence [42].

We further observed distinct S. Tm-induced increases of B cells within both the cecum and colon until days 14 and 28 p.i. This is in line with previous findings attributing both antibody-dependent and -independent roles to B cells in overcoming Salmonella infection [44].

Alike the assessed innate immune cell populations we were not able to observe any enhanced adaptive immune cell responses in the terminal ileum during S. Tm infection, however.

Salmonella-induced intestinal cytokine secretion

Furthermore, we assessed pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine secretion in distinct intestinal compartments over time following S. Tm infection of resistant mice. In line with the immune cell dynamics described above, we observed a robust Salmonella-induced Th1-type immune response, as indicated by enhanced secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α in the large intestinal tract on days 1 and 14 p.i. and even at the end of the observation period in case of cecal TNF-α. This is in line with previous findings showing CD4+ T lymphocytes as main producers of IFN-γ in response to Salmonella infection being first detected at day 3 p.i. and peaking between days 7 and 14 p.i. [22, 26]. In the early phase of infection, IFN-γ production has also been attributed to innate immune cell subsets including neutrophils [26, 45]. This is underscored by the marked increase in neutrophilic cell numbers in the cecum, but also the colon as early as day 1 p.i. observed in our present study. The TNF-α concentrations that were elevated in the large intestinal tract during the entire time course of Salmonella infection were accompanied by increased numbers of macrophages and monocytes infiltrating the cecum and the colon of infected mice. Besides the (from the host perspective) benefit of early inflammatory defense against the invading enteropathogens, TNF-α produced by activated macrophages and monocytes, but also T lymphocytes can be made responsible for further perpetuation of the inflammatory cascade leading to induction of intestinal epithelial apoptosis and cell death and subsequent gut epithelial barrier dysfunction (“leaky gut”) [46–48].

Given the orchestrated regulation of the enteropathogen-induced inflammatory immune cascade, we also assessed anti-inflammatory IL-10 secretion in the intestinal tract and found increased IL-10 concentration in the cecum as early as 24 h p.i., and additionally at the end of the experiment in both, the ileum and cecum, whereas no changes were measured in the colon. One needs to take into consideration, however, that the measured concentrations were rather low and should be discussed with caution. Nevertheless, these findings are partially supported by previous studies reporting elevated IFN-γ and TNF-α levels in the cecum in the absence of enhanced IL-10 secretion [29]. Interestingly, IL-10 mRNA expression levels have been shown to correlate with the S. Tm C5 strain burdens in the spleen of both susceptible C57BL/6 and resistant CBA mice by days 3–5 p.i. [49]. Furthermore, the authors stated that strong IL-10 expression correlates with the severity of the infection [49], which we could not confirm on protein level, however, but might have been due to differences in the applied S. Tm strains.

Conclusion

Taken together, our study demonstrates that in the absence of overt symptoms such as enteritis the resistant CBA mice infected with S. Tm SL1344 strain represent a robust model for investigating both acute and chronic Salmonella infection in a temporal- and compartment-specific manner. Despite the absence of clinical signs infected animals exhibited sustained pro- and anti-inflammatory innate and adaptive immune responses accompanied by distinct histopathological alterations in the cecum and colon, whereas the terminal ileum remained largely unaffected. Notably, the coexistence of low clinical severity with measurable immune activation resembles the situation of patients suffering of long-time fecal Salmonella shedding. Given the ability of Salmonella to disseminate systemically in both humans and mice, future studies should further address the systemic consequences of infection in resistant CBA mice, with particular emphasis on extra-intestinal dissemination and persistence.
