Nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence: relationship between cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation
Authors: Nazlı Hacıalioğlu, Esra Boyraz Şeker, Feridun Kaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToday, rapidly advancing artificial intelligence technologies provide significant changes in the health field. These technologies, which have a wide range of applications in nursing practices, enhance the quality of healthcare and make the care process more efficient. Therefore, nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence and the effective use and adoption of these technologies in patient care are significant. In this study, two hypotheses were tested: (1) whether cognitive flexibility is associated with positive/negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, and (2) whether emotion regulation is associated with positive/negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence.MethodThis study employed a correlational and cross-sectional research design. It was completed with 377 nurses working in a province in the East of Turkey who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. Data were collected using the Introductory Information Form, Attitude Towards Artificial Intelligence Scale, Cognitive Flexibility Inventory, and Emotion Regulation Scale. These measurement tools were administered to the nurses using the face-to-face interview method. Multiple linear regression analyses were used to evaluate the data.ResultsAs a result of the multiple regression analyses conducted, it was found that cognitive flexibility and reappraisal together were associated with 7% of the variance in positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence (F(2, 374) = 12.961, p < 0.001), while they explained 4% of the variance in negative attitudes (F(2, 374) = 7.098, p < 0.001). In both of the tested models, reappraisal was found to be significantly associated with the attitudes, whereas cognitive flexibility was not.ConclusionThe study concludes that nurses’ emotional regulation skills play an important role in shaping their attitudes toward artificial intelligence, while cognitive flexibility does not contribute significantly. Nurses with higher emotional regulation skills tend to report a more positive attitude toward artificial intelligence. In this respect, education and awareness programs prioritizing the improvement of these skills in nurses may contribute to the adoption of artificial intelligence technologies.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Attitudes, Cognitive flexibility, Emotion regulation, Nursing

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) includes all kinds of technological tools that are informed by various data inputs and can make intelligent decisions [1, 2]. Today, rapidly advancing AI technologies are revolutionising the field of health and affecting nursing practices [3, 4]. Nurses Fritz and Dermody [5] define AI as a rational computer algorithm that can analyse human actions over time and make decisions based on these analyses.

Artificial intelligence is used in many areas, such as the creation of medications and treatment plans, and protection from disease risks in the field of nursing [6]. AI has many applications, including virtual patient training, coding of health data, epidemic tracking, and medical imaging analysis [7]. These applications improve the quality of health care provided to individuals and make patient care more efficient [8, 9]. The former research has revealed that nurses are frequently assigned tasks other than nursing practices and perform practices requiring physical strength in clinical areas. These practices increase the workload and affect nursing services and the quality of life of nurses [10]. It is stated that by integrating AI into nursing services, the workload of nurses will decrease while the quality of care, the quality of life of nurses, and patient and nurse satisfaction will increase [11]. In addition, nurses need to turn to AI applications for the reliability of medical records and the effective use of human resources. In this respect, nurses have to adapt to the change and transformation in the world without lagging behind the technology. Tarsuslu et al. [12] state in their study that nurses’ positive attitudes toward AI will be important in reducing workload and improving patient care services. Therefore, nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence are critical for the adoption of AI technology and its effective use in patient care. Previous studies have examined the general attitudes toward AI among nurses and nursing students. However, to date, no study has comprehensively addressed the impact of nurses’ cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation strategies on their attitudes toward AI.

The use of artificial intelligence in nursing practice involves not only professional skills but also psychological elements. Nurses’ adoption of AI technologies is influenced by psychological processes such as cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation [13–15]. These psychological characteristics can shape the process of accepting AI technologies by interacting with factors such as perceived benefits and ease of use. Cognitive flexibility is defined as individuals’ awareness of alternative solutions, being flexible to new situations, and the ability to adapt [16]. In the field of nursing, cognitive flexibility is critical in terms of adapting to change and improving the quality of professional care [17]. Nurses with high cognitive flexibility easily adapt to developing technology and seek alternative solutions to complex situations in the care process. They also tend to accept artificial intelligence not only as a tool but also as a part of health services [18, 19].

Individuals with high cognitive flexibility are more flexible and open to exploring creative learning methods. A study conducted with students found a positive and significant relationship between cognitive flexibility and attitudes toward artificial intelligence [20]. Similarly, other studies have emphasized that cognitive flexibility plays an important role in evaluating the opportunities offered by technology and accepting these technologies [21, 22].

Another important factor affecting nurses’ attitudes towards AI is emotion regulation skills. Emotion regulation skills include the processes of monitoring, regulating, evaluating, and changing individuals’ emotional reactions [23]. Nurses have emotion regulation experiences because they frequently encounter emotionally intense events in their working lives [24]. This may affect their attitudes towards AI. To illustrate, a nurse who believes that AI will improve the quality of patient care may develop an emotional trust towards artificial intelligence and exhibit a positive attitude [25]. On the other hand, a nurse with poor emotion regulation skills may perceive AI as a threat and develop a negative attitude [26, 27].

The related literature has several studies examining nurses’ and nursing students’ attitudes towards AI. In one of the studies, it was found that most of the nurses did not have information about AI technologies, but they thought that if these technologies were used in patient care, they would positively affect the nursing profession [28]. Morales and Xu found that nurses developed positive attitudes when they perceived that AI increased efficiency and reliability, and their desire for integration into practices and work performance increased [29, 30].

Research conducted with nursing students has revealed that students see AI as a practical and advantageous tool in the field of nursing, have a positive attitude towards AI technologies, and tend to adopt these technologies [31–37]. All of these studies show that general psychological adjustment abilities and expectations regarding technology may influence attitudes toward AI. However, no comprehensive study addressing both of these variables together in a nursing sample has yet been found in the literature.

On the other hand, when the literature is examined, it is also found that there are opposite views and negative attitudes towards AI. For example, in a study conducted in Saudi Arabia, it was found that most healthcare professionals feared losing their jobs due to AI and were therefore concerned about the integration of artificial intelligence into the healthcare field [38]. Similarly, in another study conducted in Indonesia, it was found that nurses were concerned that AI would replace them in the future and take away their duties [39]. These negative attitudes are thought to stem from contextual factors such as a lack of organizational support, insufficient training in AI technologies, and individuals having had limited exposure to these systems in the past. In this context, the study focuses on the following research questions:

Is cognitive flexibility associated with nurses’ positive and negative attitudes towards artificial intelligence?

Is cognitive flexibility associated with nurses’ positive and negative attitudes towards artificial intelligence?

Do emotion regulation strategies have a significant effect on nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence?

Do emotion regulation strategies have a significant effect on nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence?

The existing literature shows that nurses’ perceptions of artificial intelligence are mostly addressed in terms of technical competence and professional roles. However, no previous study has specifically examined how nurses’ cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation strategies are associated with their attitudes towards AI. Failure of nurses to psychologically adapt to technological developments may lead to decreases in patient care quality and disruptions in service delivery. Therefore, it is critical to examine psychological factors. In this context, studies in which psychological processes are taken into account are needed to understand nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence. This study was conducted to examine the relationship between nurses’ cognitive flexibility levels, emotion regulation skills, and their attitudes towards AI.

Method

Study design and setting

The study employed the relationship-seeking and cross-sectional research model to examine the role of cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation in determining nurses’ attitudes towards AI. The total number of nurses working in Erzurum province is approximately 510. Of these, 377 participated in the study, while the remaining nurses could not be reached on the data collection days due to leave or absence. As a result, 377 of the 510 eligible nurses completed the questionnaire (response rate = 73.9%). Studies tested with power analysis suggest that a sample size of 107 is sufficient to detect small to moderate effects when considering two predictive factors [40, 41]. The study sample consisted of 377 nurses working in a second-tier hospital in Erzurum province who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. Of the participants, 90.5% were female and 9.5% were male. The average age of the nurses was 33.3 (SD = 7.17, min.-max. = 20–55), 48.8% of whom were college graduates, and 87.3% of whom were found to use artificial intelligence at least several times a month. The inclusion criteria for the study were nurses who had at least one year of clinical experience at the relevant hospital, were over 18 years of age, agreed to participate in the study, and had no communication issues (permanent or contract staff). The exclusion criteria included nurses who did not meet the specified inclusion criteria. Ethical approval (Approval No: 2024 − 716) was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine in order to conduct the study. The necessary official permits were obtained from the Erzurum Provincial Health Directorate.

Data collection

The research data were collected between January and March 2025. This time frame was chosen because it is far from the annual vacation periods during the summer months, when nurses are more accessible in terms of patient workload, and the clinical workload is relatively lower. During the visits made by the researchers, face-to-face interviews were conducted with nurses at in the research setting. They were informed about the purpose and procedure of the study. They were also assured that they could withdraw at any time and their data would be kept confidential. Informed consent forms were obtained from the nurses who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. Data were collected by the questionnaire method. The participating nurses were given necessary explanations about how to fill out the questionnaire. Then, the questionnaire forms were distributed to them under the researcher’s supervision, and they were kindly requested to fill them in. During the process, necessary explanations were made by the researcher to those who had questions. The questionnaire form consisted of two parts. The first part included the consent form explaining the content of the study and, the second part included demographic information and items. Participant data were kept confidential and no personal information, e-mail, telephone number or other identifying information was collected. The application of the data collection forms took an average of 10–15 min and the completed forms were collected by the researcher. The completed forms were then collected by the researcher. When it was decided that the form had reached a sufficient sample size, the data collection process was ended.

Measurement tools

Introductory form

An introductory information form, which is thought to affect the role of cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation in determining the attitudes of the research population towards AI, was designed by the researchers in line with the literature. The form includes questions about participants’ gender, age, education level (categorized as high school, associate’s degree, or graduate degree), and AI usage status (AI usage frequency is categorized as follows: (1) a few times a year and (2) at least once a month).

The general attitudes towards artificial intelligence scale (GAAIS)

This scale was originally developed by Schepman and Rodway [42] to assess individuals’ general attitudes towards AI. It consists of 20 items in total, with 12 items evaluating positive attitudes towards AI and 8 items assessing negative attitudes towards AI. The items are rated on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The developers reported that the scale has high internal consistency. The Cronbach’s alpha value for positive attitudes towards AI was calculated as 0.88, and for negative attitudes towards AI, it was 0.83 [42]. Adaptation studies of the scale for Turkish culture were carried out by Kaya et al. [43]. In this adaptation process, the scale’s construct validity was tested on a Turkish sample, yielding strong evidence of validity (χ² = 557.01, df = 169, χ²/df = 3.30, CFI = 0.92, NNFI = 0.91, SRMR = 0.067, RMSEA = 0.081). Reliability analyses from the same study revealed internal consistency values of 0.82 for the positive attitudes towards AI and 0.84 for the negative attitudes towards AI [43]. The scale has different scoring approaches in other studies. These include reverse-scoring the negative attitude items in case they can be interpreted as positive attitudes, or evaluating them directly as negative attitudes. In this study, the negative attitude items were not reverse-scored. In the current study, the internal consistency coefficients were found to be 0.92 for the positive attitudes toward AI and 0.87 for the Negative attitudes toward AI, indicating strong reliability of the scale.

Cognitive flexibility inventory (CFI)

The Cognitive Flexibility Inventory was originally created by Dennis and Vander Wal [44] to assess individuals’ levels of cognitive flexibility. The original version includes 20 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale and is structured around two primary factors (Alternatives and Control). Scores on the inventory can range from 20 to 140, with higher scores reflecting greater cognitive flexibility. The Turkish adaptation of the inventory was conducted by Sapmaz and Doğan [16]. Unlike the original format, the Turkish version employs a 5-point Likert scale across the same 20 items while maintaining the original two-factor structure. In this version, total scores range from 20 to 100, with elevated scores indicating a higher degree of cognitive flexibility. In terms of reliability, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Turkish form was calculated as 0.90, indicating high internal consistency. Confirmatory factor analysis also supported the construct validity of the scale, with the following fit indices: χ² = 406.98, df = 167, χ²/df = 2.44, NFI = 0.96, CFI = 0.98, RMR = 0.05, and RMSEA = 0.05. In this study, the total score of the scale was used and similarly to the original form, a strong reliability was found with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89.

Emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)

The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) was originally designed by Gross and John [45] and later adapted into Turkish by Eldeleklioğlu and Eroğlu [46]. Comprising 10 items in total, the scale is rated using a 7-point Likert format. It includes two subscales: Suppression (4 items) and cognitive reappraisal (6 items). Rather than producing a total score, the scale yields separate scores for each sub-dimension. When examining the scale factors, it can be stated that reappraisal is an antecedent-focused strategy leading to more adaptive outcomes, while suppression is response-focused. The possible score range is 4 to 28 for suppression and 6 to 42 for reappraisal. In the study where the questionnaire was developed, the internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) values for the suppression subscale ranged from 0.73 to 0.76, while for the reappraisal subscale they ranged from 0.80 to 0.82. Additionally, test-retest reliability was reported as 0.69 for both dimensions [45]. In the Turkish adaptation conducted by Eldeleklioğlu and Eroğlu [46], the internal consistency coefficients were reported as 0.73 for suppression and 0.78 for reappraisal. Test-retest reliability values were calculated as 0.72 for suppression and 0.74 for reappraisal. In the current study, reliability analysis found Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.75 for the suppression subscale and 0.83 for the reappraisal subscale, reflecting acceptable internal consistency.

Statistical analysis

The analyses conducted within the scope of the study were performed using the SPSS 21.00 software package. Firstly, the data set was analyzed for missing data and outlier data. In addition to these, it was examined in terms of normal distribution and covariance [47]. Following the screenings conducted, all returned questionnaires were complete. The scores obtained from the scales were checked by converting them into standard z scores. It was determined that the standardized scores (z values) were between − 3 and + 3, so there were no outliers in the data set [48]. After these procedures were completed, skewness and kurtosis values were examined to test whether the variables were normally distributed, and it was seen that the fact that these values were between − 2 and + 2 was evidence that the data were normally distributed [49]. The correlation coefficients obtained from the study were interpreted according to Cohen [50]. As Cohen states, correlation coefficients can be interpreted as follows: r = 0.10 to 0.29 indicates a small effect size, r = 0.30 to 0.49 indicates a medium effect size, and r ≥ 0.50 indicates a large effect size. Then, two separate multiple linear regression analyses were performed for positive and negative attitudes towards artificial intelligence to test the research questions. Some additional assumptions were checked before performing multiple linear regression analysis. The Durbin-Watson statistic was employed to assess the presence of autocorrelation in the residuals of the multiple regression analysis. Based on the guidelines provided by Durbin and Watson [51], acceptable values for this statistic range between 1 and 3. In the current analysis, the Durbin-Watson value was calculated as 1.12 for the positive GAAI model and 1.65 for the negative GAAI, indicating that the assumption of independence of residuals was met. To evaluate multicollinearity among the independent variables, correlation coefficients, variance inflation factor (VIF), and tolerance values were examined. For this assumption to hold, the intercorrelations among predictor variables should remain below 0.90 [47], the VIF should not exceed 5, and the tolerance value should be above 0.20 [52, 53]. In this study, the highest observed correlation among the predictor variables was 0.49, which falls well below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.90. Additionally, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for both models were found to range from 1.10 to 1.42, remaining within the acceptable limit of less than 5. Tolerance values were also within the acceptable range, varying between 0.71 and 0.91, thus exceeding the minimum recommended value of 0.20. These findings indicate that the assumption of multicollinearity was not violated. In addition to these assumptions, the presence of multivariate outliers was analyzed with Cook’s D values. Cook’s D values greater than 1 should be considered outliers [54]. It was found that there was no value greater than 1 in Cook’s D values calculated for both models. Similarly, Tabachnick [48] suggested that standardized DFBeta values should be less than 1. In both models, no value greater than 1 was found. The analyses were conducted after it was seen that there was no multicollinearity problem.

The results of the correlation analysis

Preliminary analyses were conducted before the research questions were tested using multiple regression. Since the skewness and kurtosis values of the variables were within ± 2, they were determined to exhibit a normal distribution. At this point, descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were performed, and the results are presented in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, there was a negative and low-level significant relationship between positive attitudes towards AI and negative attitudes towards AI (r = -0.227, p < 0.001). In addition to this finding, it was observed that there were positive and low-level significant relationships between positive attitudes toward AI and cognitive flexibility (r = 0.183, p < 0.001) and the reappraisal dimension of emotion regulation (r = 0.244, p < 0.001). However, there was a negative and statistically insignificant relationship between positive attitudes toward AI and the suppression dimension of emotion regulation (r = -0.006, p = 0.911). When negative attitudes towards AI, which is another dimension of attitudes towards artificial intelligence, were analysed, it was observed that there were negative and low-level significant relationships between negative attitudes toward AI and cognitive flexibility (r = -0.104, p = 0.045) and reappraisal dimension of emotion regulation (r = -0.191, p < 0.001). However, there was no statistically significant positive relationship between negative attitudes toward AI and the suppression dimension of emotion regulation (r = 0.038, p = 0.467). Overall, although cognitive flexibility and reappraisal were significantly associated with positive and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, the correlation coefficients demonstrated small effect sizes. On the other hand, suppression did not appear to have a significant effect.

According to the results of the regression analysis, the nurses’ cognitive flexibility skills and emotion regulation reappraisal skills explained approximately 7% of the change in the positive attitudes towards AI, and the model obtained was found to be significant (F(2, 374) = 12.961, p < 0.001). According to the model results in Table 3, while the predictive power of emotion regulation’s reappraisal skills was significant (β = 0.203, p < 0.001), the predictive power of cognitive flexibility skills (β = 0.083, p = 0.147) was found to be insignificant. In other words, the high level of individuals’ ability to reevaluate their emotions was related to their higher level of positive attitudes towards AI, while cognitive flexibility skills did not have a significant contribution to positive attitudes towards AI.

The analyses also revealed that nurses’ cognitive flexibility skills and emotion regulation reappraisal skills explained approximately 4% of the change in the variable of negative attitudes towards AI and the model obtained was significant (F(2, 374) = 7.098, p < 0.001). According to the model results in Table 3, while the predictive ability of emotion regulation’s reappraisal skills was significant (β = -0.184, p = 0.002), the predictive ability of cognitive flexibility skills (β = -0.013, p = 0.820) was found to be insignificant. That is, it was determined that individuals’ high emotion reappraisal skills were related to their lower level of negative attitudes towards AI, while cognitive flexibility did not have a significant contribution to negative attitudes towards artificial intelligence. The low R² values observed in the variables of positive and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, as revealed by the research findings, suggest that factors other than cognitive flexibility and reappraisal account for a substantial portion of the variance in attitudes.

Discussion

This study was conducted to determine the relationship between nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence and cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation. The related literature has no study examining the relationship between nurses’ cognitive flexibility levels and emotion regulation skills, and their attitudes towards artificial intelligence (AI). For this reason, the findings obtained from the study were discussed with the study results obtained from different study groups.

Cognitive reappraisal, one of the constructive strategies, allows individuals to restructure their emotions in the face of negative situations and makes them more open to change and innovation [63, 64]. This strategy contributes to individuals who tend to develop resistance to change perceiving new situations not as threats but as opportunities for development [72]. AI technologies, which are rapidly being integrated into today’s healthcare services, raise both technical and ethical concerns. That requires nurses to have the ability to cope with these concerns and adapt to new technologies [65, 66]. The study found that as the scores for the reappraisal subdimension of emotion regulation increased, the nurses’ positive attitudes towards AI also improved. This finding is consistent with studies in the literature showing that cognitive reappraisal may facilitate adaptation to technology. For instance, Weinert et al. [73] reported that reappraisal plays a mediating role in adaptation to digital health applications. Similarly, Lin et al. [74] found that individuals who resist digital systems adapt more easily to technology if they have high emotion regulation skills [67, 68]. These findings are compatible with the present study. The extent to which individuals find a technology useful and usable is one of the key factors determining their attitudes. Openness to innovation and the ability to cope with uncertainty play an important role in shaping these perceptions [75]. It has also been reported in various studies that individuals with a high level of acceptance develop more positive attitudes towards new technologies [69, 70]. Nurses who can regulate their emotions tend to be more open to technological changes [76]. This may facilitate the development of more positive and adaptive attitudes toward AI. Regarded all related research together, it can be said that the ability to re-evaluate emotions facilitates nurses’ perceptions of AI not only as a professional threat but also as a tool that supports patient care. Overall, it can be said that the re-evaluation strategy plays a decisive role in nurses’ development of positive attitudes toward AI.

The study found that as the nurses’ reappraisal scores, one of their emotion regulation skills, increased, their negative attitudes towards artificial intelligence diminished. The literature also indicates that reappraisal effectively reduces negative emotions and attitudes [70]. A study in the field of nursing suggests that emotion regulation plays a crucial role in the acceptance of technological systems [71]. In particular, it was found that healthcare workers with high emotional regulation skills experienced fewer difficulties in transitioning to digital systems, accepted change more easily, and exhibited a more open, flexible, and collaborative attitude towards new technologies [15]. All these findings suggest that nurses who can effectively manage their emotions tend to view technological innovations not as a threat but as a tool that facilitates patient care. It is believed that AI applications can reduce nurses’ workload and prevent emotional burnout, thereby contributing to a reduction in negative attitudes towards these technologies [24]. Emotion regulation skills are considered an important factor in increasing empathy and professional satisfaction, especially for nurses who are in constant communication with patients. The findings reveal that emotional regulation skills are important not only in terms of technology adaptation but also in terms of empathy and professional relationships. More specifically, developing skills such as stress management, emotional awareness, and reappraisal can contribute to nurses’ resilience and facilitate the transition to technology at the institutional level [76]. This case highlights why emotional skills should not be disregarded when integrating AI into the healthcare system, as improving these skills can both increase nurses’ resilience and facilitate the transition to technology at the institutional level. Such improvement can enable them to integrate AI into the healthcare system more effectively and efficiently. Given these findings, developing emotion regulation skills in nurses may be associated with exhibiting less negative attitudes toward AI.

The research findings revealed that cognitive flexibility showed low but significant correlations with attitudes towards AI. However, in multiple regression analyses, when the emotion reappraisal variable was controlled, cognitive flexibility was found to be unable to be independently related to attitudes. This suggests that the effect of cognitive flexibility on attitudes towards AI may not be direct but may instead emerge indirectly through mediating processes such as emotion regulation or other psychological variables not measured in the study. Some studies have reported that individuals with high levels of cognitive flexibility develop more positive attitudes towards AI [55]. Additionally, it has been stated that cognitive flexibility contributes to nurses’ ability to adapt quickly to changing clinical conditions and benefit more effectively from AI-supported decision-making processes [56, 57]. However, the findings of the current study are not consistent with this literature. This discrepancy can be explained by factors such as sample characteristics, measurement tools used, or the effect of mediating variables. Therefore, the effect of cognitive flexibility on attitudes towards artificial intelligence may not be direct but may emerge indirectly through intermediary mechanisms such as emotion regulation. Considering that AI-based simulation training and practical experience can support nurses’ cognitive flexibility, more research on these kinds of interventions is needed [22, 58]. These findings show that further research is needed on the role of cognitive flexibility in the AI adaptation process.

Cognitive flexibility is one of the characteristics that individuals must possess in order to exhibit appropriate behaviors in emerging situations [59]. Although this study found low but significant correlations between cognitive flexibility and attitudes towards AI, multiple regression analysis indicated that cognitive flexibility was not significantly associated with attitudes. This finding suggests that the effect of cognitive flexibility on attitudes towards AI may not be direct but rather indirect or may emerge through interaction with other variables. However, studies conducted with nursing students in the literature have reported that cognitive flexibility significantly predicts positive attitudes toward technology [60]. It has been shown that individuals with high cognitive flexibility are more open to change and cope more effectively with uncertainty in problem-solving processes [44]. The results of this study are not consistent with other studies. While cognitive flexibility in students may lead more directly to openness to technology, the emergence of this effect in nurses working in clinical settings may depend on more mediating and contextual factors. In this context, variables such as age, professional experience, and clinical stress must also be taken into account. Furthermore, considering the dynamic and multidimensional nature of nursing practice, it is assessed that cognitive flexibility may indirectly affect adaptation to technology [61, 62]. This situation may explain why cognitive flexibility was not significantly related to attitudes toward AI in our study, which does not fully align with the direct relationships reported in the previous literature. Overall, cognitive-behavioural workshops and professional development programs aimed at increasing cognitive flexibility may serve as an indirect supportive factor contributing to nurses’ healthier adaptation to AI, even if they do not directly change attitudes. These findings indicate the need for further research on the role of cognitive flexibility in the AI adaptation process.

In this study, no significant relationship was found between nurses’ suppression subscale scores for emotion regulation and their positive and negative attitudes towards AI. Suppression of emotional expression refers to the conscious inhibition of expressing feelings through facial expressions, tone of voice, or body language [45]. Although suppression prevents a person from expressing their emotions, it does not necessarily change their thoughts or attitudes toward technology. In other words, even if a person feels insecure or anxious, their attitude toward AI may remain the same because they do not express these feelings. To illustrate, a nurse may feel anxious or insecure when first encountering an AI-supported patient care system. However, by suppressing the expression of these emotions, they may maintain a professional, calm, and reassuring demeanour toward their patients. Additionally, suppression may sometimes be an automatic response and may not influence the individual’s conscious approach to technology. Therefore, there may not be a meaningful relationship between suppression and attitudes towards AI. The literature lacks studies directly examining the relationship between suppressing emotional expression and attitudes toward AI. These findings shed light on a clear need for further research on the relationship between suppressing emotional expression and attitudes toward AI.

Limitations

The study was conducted in Erzurum province of Turkey, and the results can be generalized only to nurses working in hospitals there. The sample of the study consists largely of female nurses (90.5% female, 9.5% male). A criticism that male nurses are not sufficiently represented may be stated at this point. Therefore, in future studies, a more balanced gender distribution should be provided and the validity of the results should be tested for different groups.

The cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Hence, it is recommended that longitudinal or experimental designs be used in future studies. An important limitation of the study is that the data collection tools were administered face-to-face. Although confidentiality was ensured during this process, it should be noted that participants may have felt compelled to give socially acceptable responses.

Finally, one limitation of this study is that the findings were interpreted by comparing them with results obtained from different study populations. To increase the generalizability of the results, future studies should use larger samples from the same target population in various regions of the country and conduct multi-center studies. It is expected that including more diverse groups in future studies will strengthen both the reliability and applicability of the findings. Another limitation stems from individuals who declined participation due to time constraints, as well as employees who were on leave during the data collection period and, therefore, unable to participate in the study. It is believed that addressing these limitations in accordance with the recommendations could enhance the reliability and generalizability of future studies.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between nurses’ attitudes towards AI and cognitive flexibility and emotional regulation skills. The findings showed that nurses’ emotion reappraisal skills were in a positive relationship with their attitudes toward AI, but cognitive flexibility did not reveal the same effect when reappraisal was taken into account. This finding highlights the critical role of emotion regulation in understanding individual attitudes towards AI technologies and suggests that further research is needed to better understand this relationship.

Research findings suggest that interventions aimed at developing nurses’ emotional reappraisal skills may help support more positive attitudes toward AI and contribute to the successful integration of AI into patient care. By improving emotion regulation, nurses can collaborate more easily with AI tools, which may contribute to improved care efficiency and patient outcomes. This can increase efficiency in care processes and improve patient outcomes. In this respect, nursing education curricula should devise and follow content that is both technical and related to psychosocial skills such as cognitive flexibility and emotional regulation. This will enable nurses to enhance their skills to work in collaboration with AI technologies, integrate these technologies into clinical decision-making processes, and improve the quality of patient care.

Besides, it is important to strengthen institutional infrastructures that will support the transition to AI applications in healthcare institutions, for managers to adopt inclusive leadership approaches that take into account employee concerns, and to develop practical support programs for nurses. Establishing ethical standards for AI applications at the national level and disseminating professional development programs will also contribute to this process. Furthermore, professional organizations in the nursing field conducting guidance and seminar activities will increase nurses’ practical competencies.

All in all, nurses should be able to work effectively with AI and develop skills such as cognitive flexibility, emotional awareness, and re-evaluation. To support this process, it is imperative to expand practical training programs and establish supportive institutional infrastructure. This study has a cross-sectional design. Regarding the nature of cross-sectional studies and a larger sample, future studies may yield different results.
