TRIM29 suppresses malignant progression in lung squamous cell carcinoma by shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment
Authors: Xuanyu Zhou, Weikun Jia, Hequn Jiang, Huafei Zhong, Jie Sun, Ruidong Ma, Zhiqiang Wu, Zhiwu Lin, Ling Lu, Hu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
TRIM29 (tripartite motif containing 29) is a multifunctional protein with context-dependent roles in cancer, acting as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor. In this study, we investigated the role of TRIM29 in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and its impact on the tumor microenvironment. We found that TRIM29 is highly expressed in early-stage LUSC, primarily due to promoter hypomethylation and copy number amplification. High TRIM29 expression was significantly associated with improved overall survival, disease-specific survival, and progression-free survival in LUSC patients. However, TRIM29 did not remain an independent prognostic factor after adjusting for clinical covariates, as well as tumor purity and keratinization-related programs. Functionally, TRIM29 suppressed tumor cell proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in vitro, and inhibited tumor growth and metastasis in a syngeneic mouse model. Mechanistically, TRIM29 activated tumor-suppressive pathways (e.g., p53) and epithelial markers (e.g., KRT5), while repressing oncogenic pathways (e.g., KRAS, IL6-JAK-STAT3). Unadjusted bulk transcriptome analyses suggested that TRIM29-high tumors exhibit an immune-cold phenotype with reduced immune infiltration. However, these associations attenuated after adjustment for tumor purity and keratinization programs. Notably, the syngeneic model provided orthogonal evidence that TRIM29 overexpression can decrease intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration. Protein-protein interaction analysis identified KRT5 and TRAT1 as key interactors, suggesting that TRIM29 may preserve squamous differentiation while simultaneously attenuating anti-tumor immunity. Our findings support a context-dependent dual role of TRIM29 in LUSC, where it combines tumor-intrinsic suppression of malignant progression with potential remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. This study provides new insights into the complex role of TRIM29 in LUSC and underscores its potential as a therapeutic target, warranting further validation in cell-type–resolved and clinically annotated cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48026-z.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1,2. Although current therapies, including surgery, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, have improved outcomes, the 5-year survival rate continues to show a dramatic decline, from 65% in stage I to only 5% in stage IV disease3–5. This stark prognostic disparity underscores both the aggressive nature of advanced lung cancer and the critical gaps in our understanding of its pathogenesis. Therefore, identifying the molecular drivers of disease progression is essential for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) constitutes approximately 20%–30% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cases and represents around 80% of all lung cancer diagnoses 6 7. Despite considerable advancements in cancer therapies, there remains a notable lack of effective, LUSC-specific treatments8. Current treatment strategies primarily rely on platinum-based chemotherapy regimens9, such as carboplatin-based combinations10. Recent evidence indicates that carboplatin resistance in LUSC is driven, at least in part, by metabolic reprogramming, in which the lineage-specific oncogene ΔNp63α transcriptionally activates the serine synthesis pathway to support nucleotide biosynthesis and antioxidant defense under chemotherapy-induced stress, thereby promoting tumor survival and treatment resistance11. Recent therapeutic advancements have introduced epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) monoclonal antibodies (e.g., necitumumab) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., pembrolizumab, pislelizumab, sintilimab), which have shown some promise12–14. However, despite these innovations, durable responses and long-term survival benefits remain infrequent15. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms underlying in LUSC is critical for developing novel therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes.

Recent advances in cancer immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1, have revolutionized the treatment of lung cancer by reactivating anti-tumor T cell responses7. However, over 60% of patients exhibit either intrinsic or acquired resistance, primarily due to the immunosuppressive mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME)16. The TME is a complex ecosystem composed of various cell types, including immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix (ECM) components. These elements interact to influence tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance17. Immune cells, such as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), natural killer (NK) cells, and dendritic cells, typically recognize and eliminate malignant tumors18, but tumors evade immune responses through immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-L1) and the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)19. CAFs promote tumor progression by secreting pro-tumorigenic factors, remodeling the ECM, and fostering immune evasion and drug resistance. Endothelial cells in tumors form aberrant blood vessels, facilitating tumor growth and metastasis while impairing drug delivery20. ECM remodeling by enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), supports invasion and creates a physical barrier, restricting immune infiltration and drug penetration21,22. Current TME-targeting strategies show limited efficacy in advanced NSCLC, highlighting the urgent need to identify key regulators of immune evasion pathways.

In recent years, several studies have developed molecular signatures associated with immunotherapy response to better understand the relationship between intrinsic tumor programs and the tumor microenvironment. For example, the Mitochondrial Pathway Signature (MitoPS), derived from mitochondrial-related pathways, has been shown to predict immunotherapy response in lung adenocarcinoma, with low MitoPS scores correlating with increased immune infiltration and improved treatment efficacy23. Similarly, a DNA damage repair-associated signature (DAS) in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma has demonstrated that patients in the low-DAS group exhibit a more favorable prognosis and higher levels of immune infiltration24. Additionally, in thyroid cancer, a dynamic network biomarker (DNB) approach has identified ASPH as a key regulator of tumor progression that is closely associated with distinct immune microenvironmental features25. Collectively, these studies highlight that intrinsic molecular features of tumors not only determine their biological behavior but also shape the immune microenvironment, thereby influencing the effectiveness of immunotherapy.

TRIM29, also known as the ataxia-telangiectasia group D complementing gene (ATDC), located on chromosome 11q23, is a multifunctional member of the TRIM family with proposed E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. It orchestrates various cellular processes, including carcinogenesis, through context-dependent mechanisms26,27. TRIM29 has been implicated in tumor progression in several cancer types, including nasopharyngeal cancer via the PTEN/AKT/mTOR pathway, bladder cancer via the miR-29/PTEN/DNMT3A/PKC-NF-κB pathway28,29, thyroid cancer via the PI3K/AKT pathway, and cervical and pancreatic cancers through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway30. Interestingly, TRIM29 also exhibits tumor-suppressive functions in some contexts. For example, it inhibits breast cancer metastasis by blocking TWIST1-mediated EMT31 and suppresses skin cancer development through interactions with keratin and FAM83H to regulate keratin distribution32. Notably, TRIM29 demonstrates context-dependent role in gastric cancer. On one hand, it promotes tumor progression by enhancing metastasis; paradoxically, it also stimulates anti-tumor immunity by triggering the ubiquitination of IGF2BP1 and downregulation of PD-L1 expression33,34.

In this study, we demonstrate that TRIM29 may play a dual role in LUSC, simultaneously restraining tumor progression and modulating immune responses. Specifically, TRIM29 is highly expressed in early-stage LUSC due to promoter hypomethylation and copy number amplification. This elevated expression is accompanied by an immune-cold transcriptional phenotype, as inferred from bulk transcriptome analyses with potential confounding by tumor purity and keratinization programs. Upon adjusting for tumor purity and keratinization-related programs, the correlation between TRIM29 expression and key immune features was attenuated, underscoring the importance of cautious interpretation of bulk deconvolution results. Moreover, our data suggest that the interaction of TRIM29 with KRT5 and TRAT1 may contribute to the maintenance of classical squamous morphology, while concurrently linking to immune-related signaling alterations. This is supported by orthogonal evidence from our syngeneic model, which showed decreased intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration upon TRIM29 overexpression. Pathway analyses revealed that TRIM29 activates tumor-suppressive programs (e.g., p53) and epithelial differentiation markers (e.g., KRT5), while repressing oncogenic pathways (e.g., KRAS, IL6-JAK-STAT3), epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and inflammatory responses (e.g., interferon signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokines), thus exhibiting a bifunctional regulatory role in tumor biology. Taken together, these findings suggest that TRIM29 may regulate both tumor biology and immune function in LUSC through multiple mechanisms. They also provide a rationale for future studies utilizing cell-type–resolved profiling and clinically annotated cohorts to elucidate its context-dependent immunomodulatory effects, offering new insights for optimizing immunotherapeutic strategies in lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Gene expression analysis

To characterize the expression profile of TRIM29 in both normal and tumor tissues, we performed an integrated analysis of multi-platform genomic data. Baseline expression levels in normal tissues were first established using mRNA sequencing data (nTPM) from 50 human tissues obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, v23.0; https://www.proteinatlas.org/)35. The results were visualized as rank-ordered bar plots after DESeq2 normalization. A comparative analysis across 33 cancer types was conducted using TIMER2.0 (http://compbio.cn/timer2/)36. A focused investigation of LUSC and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) was performed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets (n = 553 LUSC; n = 600 LUAD). Finally, external validation was performed using two independent GEO datasets (GSE2088: 48 LUSC samples, GPL962 platform; GSE140343: 51 LUAD samples, GPL20795 platform)37. These datasets were selected based on sample size (> 40), availability of paired normal-tumor samples, and platform compatibility. All analytical visualizations were generated using R version 4.2.2 with ggplot2 version 3.4.0.

Protein expression analysis

To validate TRIM29 expression at the protein level, a multi-platform approach was employed. First, quantitative proteomics data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) were analyzed using the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html)38. Normalized spectral counts from 111 histologically normal adjacent lung tissues were compared with 111 LUAD and 111 LUSC samples, following log2 transformation and batch correction to enable cross-cohort comparisons. Concurrently, immunohistochemistry (IHC) reference data were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) using the validated anti-TRIM29 antibody (HPA020053). IHC images of normal lung tissue (n = 3 donors) were compared with tumor specimens from the Cancer module (LUAD: n = 9; LUSC: n = 7). All images were evaluated under standardized conditions (20x magnification, brightfield microscopy), with expression levels categorically assessed using the database’s established classification system (-: negative; +: weak; ++: moderate; +++: strong).

Clinical characterization

To investigate the expression patterns of TRIM29 and their clinical relevance in LUSC, gene expression profiles and clinical records from 553 TCGA-LUSC cases were integrated with 107 samples from the GSE74777 cohort. Samples with missing values and normal tissues were excluded from the analysis. Expression data were normalized using log2 transformation, followed by inter-cohort harmonization. TRIM29 expression was dichotomized based on the cohort-wide median to preserve sample size and ensure statistical power for multivariable adjustment. After confirming an approximate normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test, TRIM29 expression levels were categorized into high and low groups based on the cohort-wide median cutoff. Association analyses between TRIM29 expression groups and clinicopathological features were performed using the R packages limma (v3.50.3) for differential expression testing and ComplexHeatmap (v2.10.0) for visualization. Clinical parameters, including T stage (T1-T4), N stage (N0-N2) tumor status (Tumor Free/With Tumor), survival status (Alive/Dead), and pathological stage (Stage I, II, III, IV), were aligned with TRIM29 expression strata. Comparative visualization was generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.2), producing boxplot arrays to illustrate expression distributions across clinical categories.

Survival analysis

To assess the prognostic value of TRIM29 in LUSC patients, we performed a multi-platform survival analysis. Initially, clinical survival data for 504 LUSC cases were obtained from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas via cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org)39, including overall survival (OS), disease-free interval (DFI), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) endpoints. These clinical outcomes were then integrated with RNA-seq expression data from 553 TCGA-LUSC patients, after confirming normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. TRIM29 expression levels were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the cohort-wide median cutoff. Survival analyses were conducted using the survival (v3.2-10) and survminer (v0.4.9) R packages, and Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for all four survival endpoints. To assess the potential of TRIM29 as an independent prognostic factor, we selected seven clinicopathological variables, including age, sex, clinical features, and TRIM29 expression levels, from 504 TCGA cases with complete records. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were applied, and hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each survival metric.

To address potential confounding by tumor purity and keratinization programs in LUSC, multivariable Cox models were adjusted for ESTIMATE-derived tumor purity and KRT5 expression (log2[TPM + 1]) as a surrogate for keratinization/epithelial programs. Time-to-event was measured in months, with events coded as 1 (death/event) and 0 (censored) for each endpoint. The incremental predictive value of adding TRIM29 to the clinical baseline model was assessed using categoryless net reclassification improvement (NRI) at 36 months (t0 = 36), implemented via the nricens package (cut = 0). All survival models were fitted separately for OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI using endpoint-specific cohorts due to non-overlapping availability of the four endpoints.

Copy number variant analysis

To investigate the genomic characteristics and clinical relevance of TRIM29 copy number variations (CNVs), we initially extracted TRIM29 CNV profiles and subtype distribution frequencies from the CNV summary submodule within the mutation module of the GSCA platform. Correlation analyses between CNV patterns and gene expression were subsequently performed using the TCGA PanCancer Atlas dataset, accessed via cBioPortal. The integrated visualization tools of the platform were used to quantify expression-level variations across different CNV subtypes, including amplification (Amp), deletion (Dele), and wild-type (WT). Prognostic associations were evaluated using the CNV & Survival submodule of GSCA, where Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for OS, DFI, DSS and PFI endpoints stratified by TRIM29 CNV status (Amp vs. Dele vs. WT).

DNA methylation analysis

To investigate the epigenetic regulation of TRIM29, a multi-platform approach was employed. Pan-cancer and LUSC-specific methylation profiles were first extracted from the Differential Methylation submodule of GSCA platform. The β-values from the Illumina HM27 and HM450 platforms were compared across tumor and adjacent normal tissues, with results visualized through boxplots depicting the median and interquartile range (IQR) of methylation levels. Subsequently, the cg13625403 probe, located in the promoter CpG island of TRIM29 (chr1:241,687,502 − 241,687,503), was selected for targeted analysis using the TCGA PanCancer Atlas dataset accessed via cBioPortal. Probe-level β-values from the Illumina HM27 and HM450 platforms were jointly correlated with matched RNA-seq expression data (log2-transformed RSEM values) using Pearson’s rank correlation. Prognostic implications were evaluated using the Methylation & Survival module of GSCA, where patients were dichotomized into high/low-methylation groups based on cohort-wide median β-value thresholds (HM450: median β = 0.38; HM27: median β = 0.42). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for OS, DFI, DSS and PFI, with directly extracted from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas clinical files.

Immune infiltration analysis

To comprehensively evaluate the association between TRIM29 expression and the TME, we first analyzed immune cell infiltration using the CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 signature matrix, which represents the gene expression profiles of 22 human immune cell types. Gene expression data from TCGA-LUSC were analyzed after excluding normal tissue samples. Immune cell proportions were visualized using bar plots and violin plots to compare TRIM29 high- and low-expression groups. Next, correlations between TRIM29 expression and immune cell infiltration levels were examined using the TIMER2.0 platform, with a specific focus on M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, plasma cells, resting CD4 + T cells, and CD8 + T cells. To further assess the clinical relevance of these findings, joint survival analyses were conducted, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted. The ESTIMATE algorithm was then employed to calculate immune scores, stromal scores, and tumor purity across various TRIM29 expression groups. Additional characterization of the immune landscape was performed using ssGSEA to quantify immune cell infiltration levels and immune function scores. These findings were validated using multiple independent algorithms, including CIBERSORT-ABS, EPIC, and MCP-counter, and the results were visualized in bubble plots. Finally, ssGSEA was applied to analyze the expression differences of immune checkpoint genes between TRIM29 high- and low-expression groups.

To minimize confounding from tumor purity and epithelial/keratinization programs in bulk RNA-seq data, all immune-related analyses were restricted to primary tumor samples from TCGA-LUSC (TCGA barcode “01”). CIBERSORT results were subjected to per-sample quality control, retaining samples with CIBERSORT P-value < 0.05. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a subset of data restricted by root mean square error (RMSE). For between-group comparisons and association testing, multivariable linear regression models were fitted for each immune cell fraction (from CIBERSORT) and each ssGSEA signature score, with TRIM29 expression group as the main predictor and tumor purity (ESTIMATE-derived TumorPurity), KRT5 expression (log2[TPM + 1]), age, sex, and pathological stage as covariates. Multiple testing correction for immune-cell and immune-signature analyses was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (FDR). Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using the VIF/GVIF from the car package.

Biological function analysis

To investigate the functions of TRIM29-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and their potential molecular mechanisms, transcriptomic analysis was performed to identify DEGs between TRIM29 high- and low-expression groups (median-split). DESeq2 (v1.30.1) was used with stringent thresholds (|log2FC| > 1.0, adjusted p < .05) and batch effect correction. The DGEs were then functionally characterized using three integrated approaches. First, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted on upregulated and downregulated DEGs using the clusterProfiler package (v3.18.1). These analyses provided functional annotations, revealing biological processes and pathways, which were visualized via bubble plots (showing -log10[p-value] vs. gene ratios) and bar charts. Second, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on KEGG and Hallmark gene sets to identify significantly enriched pathways (|NES| > 1.5, FDR < 0.25). Third, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed by integrating TRIM29-associated genes from STRING (https://cn.string-db.org, version 12.0) with the DEGs to investigate the potential underlying mechanisms of these interacting genes40. Interactions were retained using a minimum required interaction score of ≥ 0.7 (high confidence), with edges representing STRING functional associations based on the combined score, incorporating evidence from multiple channels (Experiments, curated Databases, Text-mining, Co-expression, and genomic-context evidence including Neighborhood, and Co-occurrence).

Cell culture and Cell viability analysis

Mouse lung squamous cancer cells KLN205 and Human embryonic kidney cells HEK-293T were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). All the cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and mycoplasma contamination was routinely tested and confirmed to be negative. HEK-293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 16000-044, Gibco). KLN205 cells were cultured in MEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS. All the cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Cell viability was assessed via a CCK8 assay. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and treated with carboplatin. After 2 h of incubation with CCK8 solution (C0039; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), the absorbance was measured at 450 nm to assess cell viability via microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, Winooski, USA).

Plasmid construction and Lentiviral infection

The construct encoding human TRIM29 was generated via PCR amplification and subsequently inserted into the pLVX-puro vector (632164, Clontech, Mountain View, USA), confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Lentivirus was generated as described previously41. Briefly, HEK-293T cells were transfected with the pMD2.G and psPAX2 packaging plasmids, along with the appropriate backbone plasmid, using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000008; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) when the cells reached 80–90% confluence. Viral supernatants were collected 72 h post-transfection, filtered, and used to infect cells at 50% confluence in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene (C0351, Beyotime). To establish stable cell lines, the cells were treated with 2 µg/mL puromycin (A1113803, Invitrogen) 48 h post-infection.

Immunoblot analysis

Cells were lysed in EBC250 lysis buffer (250 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, 50 mM NaF, 0.5% Nonidet P-40) supplemented with 0.5 mM Na3VO4, 0.2 mM PMSF, 20 µg/mL aprotinin, and 10 µg/mL leupeptin, pH 8.0. Equal amounts of total protein were separated by10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (FFP28; Beyotime). The membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (A0181 and A0208, Beyotime) were applied, followed by detection via enhanced chemiluminescence (P0018S, Beyotime). Antibodis against GAPDH (60004-1-Ig; 1:5000), TRIM29 (17542-1-AP; 1:1000), ZEB1 (21544-1-AP; 1:1000), Snail (13099-1-AP; 1:1000), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP; 1:1000), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP; 1:1000) were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China).

Transwell and wound-healing assays

Cells were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the inner chamber, while 600 µL of complete medium was added to the outer chamber. Cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. After incubation, non-migratory cells on the inner side of the chamber were carefully removed by cotton swabs. Chambers were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet in 70% ethanol for 15 min and imaged under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti-S/L 100).

The wound-healing assay was mainly conducted as previously described42. Cells were grown to 90% confluency, then treated with Mitomycin C (1 µg/mL, S8146, Selleck, China) for 1 h to exclude the impact of cell proliferation on wound-healing. Gently and slowly scratch the monolayer cell with a plastic pipette tip. After scratching, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS and incubated in medium containing 1% serum at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. At specified time points, cells were imaged using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti-S/L 100), and wound closure was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Syngeneic mouse model

Animal care and experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chengdu Medical College, and the procedures were carried out according to the guidelines established by the China Council on Animal Care. DBA/2 mice (8 weeks old, male) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Stable KLN205 cells (5 × 105 cells per flank) were subcutaneously injected into the mice. The mice were euthanized 30 days after the injection. The tumors were excised, weighed, fixed, embedded, and sectioned. The tumor sections were subsequently subjected to Immunofluorescence analysis. Lungs were collected, fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, followed by histological analysis. Metastatic nodules in lung sections were quantified for each mouse. Since this was an exploratory animal study, no statistical tests were used to determine the sample size, and no mice were excluded from the analysis.

Immunofluorescence analysis

For immunofluorescence, the tumor sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS, and blocked with 4% bovine serum albumin in PBS. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD8 (Abcam, ab316778, 1:200). After incubation, the cells were treated with fluorescein (FITC)-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (711-095-152, 1:1000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, ) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. Coverslips were mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium (P0126; Beyotime). Images were acquired via laser confocal microscopy (TCS SP5 II system; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.4.2) and validated web platforms to evaluate differences and correlations between groups. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > .05), which guided the choice of subsequent statistical tests. For normally distributed quantitative data, a Student’s t-test was used (with equal variance confirmed by an F-test), while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank testing under right-censoring conditions, stratified by TRIM29 expression groups based on the median cutoff. For correlation analysis, Pearson’s coefficient was used to assess linear relationships (for normally distributed variables), and Spearman’s rank coefficient was applied to evaluate monotonic associations. Multi-test correction for enrichment analyses (GSEA/GO/KEGG) was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR adjustment, with significance thresholds set at p < .05 for single tests and FDR < 0.25 for gene set analyses. All assumptions, including homoscedasticity and proportional hazards, were verified prior to analysis.

For immune deconvolution and signature analyses, multivariable linear regression was used to test associations between TRIM29 expression and immune features, adjusting for tumor purity, KRT5 expression, age, sex, and stage. p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across immune features, including the 22 CIBERSORT cell fractions and ssGSEA cell and function signatures. For survival modeling, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted with the same set of covariates (age, sex, stage, tumor purity, and KRT5). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. The categoryless NRI at 36 months (t0 = 36) was computed to quantify the incremental predictive value of adding TRIM29 to the clinical baseline model.

Results

TRIM29 is highly expressed in LUSC

To investigate the role of TRIM29 in the cancer microenvironment, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of TRIM29 expression across normal and malignant tissues using integrated multi-omics analysis. In normal tissues, elevated TRIM29 mRNA expression was observed in 14 out of 50 analyzed tissue types, with the highest levels found in the esophagus, skin, and vagina. In contrast, lung tissues exhibited comparatively lower expression (Fig. 1A). Pan-cancer differential analysis revealed that TRIM29 is significantly upregulated in 10 malignancies, including cervical (CESC), colon (COAD), esophageal (ESCA), and LUSC. Conversely, TRIM29 expression was downregulated in 6 cancer types, such as breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), prostate cancer (PRAD), LUAD, and renal cell carcinomas (KICH/KIRC) (Fig. 1B). Subsequent independent validation using both TCGA and GEO datasets yielded consistent results: TRIM29 mRNA was significantly upregulated in LUSC and downregulated in LUAD compared with matched normal tissues (Fig. 1C and D). Protein-level analysis using CPTAC, and immunohistochemical validation from HPA corroborated these findings, showing that TRIM29 protein expression was significantly higher in LUSC specimens than in LUAD and normal lung tissues (Fig. 1E and F). Collectively, these results establish that TRIM29 is highly expressed in lung squamous carcinoma.

TRIM29 highly expressed patients is associated with better prognosis in LUSC

TRIM29 expression was significantly upregulated in LUSC compared with LUAD, prompting our subsequent focus on investigating its role in LUSC. To examine the correlation between TRIM29 expression levels and clinical characteristics of LUSC patients, we summarized clinical data and performed an initial evaluation using the TCGA-LUSC and GSE74777 datasets (Table 1). This analysis revealed no significant differences in gender, age, pathological stage, survival status, tumor status, or TNM classification between the high and low TRIM29 expression groups (Fig. 2A). However, subgroup analyses uncovered expression patterns: TRIM29 levels progressively declined from T1 to T4 stages (Fig. 2B) and displayed a biphasic pattern in nodal status, decreasing in N1 but increasing in N2 compared to N0 (Fig. 2C). Notably, elevated TRIM29 expression was observed in tumor-free patients and survivors compared to their respective controls (Fig. 2D and E), suggesting a preferential expression in early-stage disease. Similar to the T-stage, a gradual decrease in TRIM29 levels was observed from pathological stage I to III (Fig. 2F). Survival analysis demonstrated that LUSC patients with higher TRIM29 expression experienced benefits in OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI (Fig. 2G and J). However, Cox regression modeling confirmed that tumor status is an independent prognostic factor across all survival endpoints, while TRIM29 expression did not achieve independent prognostic significance (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5). Collectively, these findings suggest that elevated TRIM29 expression correlates with early-stage clinical features and improved survival outcomes in LUSC, although it does not serve as an independent prognostic marker.

We further evaluated the prognostic value of TRIM29 using multivariable Cox models, incorporating tumor purity and keratinization-related programs. After adjusting for age, sex, pathological stage, ESTIMATE-derived tumor purity, and KRT5 expression, TRIM29 remained non-independent across overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI), and progression-free interval (PFI) (Table S1). Specifically, the adjusted hazard ratios were as follows: OS HR = 0.860 (P = .357), DSS HR = 0.777 (P = .332), DFI HR = 1.553 (P = .194), and PFI HR = 0.955 (P = .821) (Table S1). Additionally, categoryless NRI at 36 months (t0 = 36) revealed no incremental predictive value when adding TRIM29 to the clinical baseline model (NRI ≈ 0 across all endpoints; Table S1).

Hypomethylation and copy amplification is responsible for TRIM29 highly expression

We previously observed that TRIM29 was highly expressed in early-stage LUSC but was downregulated in late-stage. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we first analyzed TRIM29 CNVs in LUSC. The predominant CNV types were heterozygous amplification (20.16%), heterozygous deletion (33.53%), and normal copy number (45.51%) (Fig. 3A). Notably, TRIM29 expression was significantly positively correlated with its copy number amplification (Fig. 3B and C). Further analysis revealed that patients with TRIM29 amplification had prolonged survival across all examined endpoints compared to those with deletions or wild-type CNVs (Fig. 3D - G).

TRIM29-high tumors display an immune-cold transcriptional phenotype in LUSC

To investigate the role of TRIM29 in the tumor microenvironment of LUSC, the immune landscape of LUSC was first analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm and associated R packages. The analysis identified the top six immune cell populations within the LUSC TME: M0 macrophages (17.9%), M1 macrophages (8.6%), M2 macrophages (13.8%), plasma cells (9.3%), resting memory CD4 + T cells (11.2%), and CD8 + T cells (8.5%) (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. S1A). Immune infiltration scores were then assessed for the TRIM29 high- and low-expression groups using the ESTIMATE algorithm. The results revealed lower mesenchymal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores in the TRIM29 high-expression group compared to the low-expression group (Fig. 5B). Comprehensive immune profiling showed that 20 immune cell types, including B cells, CD8 + T cells, and M1 macrophages, were downregulated in the TRIM29 high-expression group, while 12 populations, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), neutrophils, and regulatory T cells, were upregulated, as determined by CIBERSORT and ssGSEA (Fig. 5C and D). Seven independent algorithms (XCELL, TIMER, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, EPIC, CIBERSORT-ABS, and CIBERSORT) consistently demonstrated a negative correlation between TRIM29 expression and the infiltration levels of most immune cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Functional assessment further revealed that immune activities were downregulated in the TRIM29 high-expression group, alongside the upregulation of APC co-inhibition, proinflammatory responses, MHC class I molecules, and type I interferon responses (Fig. 5E).

Immune checkpoint analysis showed significantly elevated expression of CD276, CD44, TNFRSF18, TNFSF18, and VTCN1 in the TRIM29 high-expression group, while other checkpoints were markedly downregulated (Fig. 5G), suggesting that TRIM29 expression is negatively correlated with the expression of most immune checkpoints. Moreover, a significantly association was observed between TRIM29 expression and the abundance of specific immune cell types. Notably, high TIRM29 expression was positively correlated with M0 macrophages (r = .178, p = 9.45 × 10− 5) and M2 macrophages (r = .164, p = 3.18 × 10− 4), but negatively correlated with M1 macrophages (r = -.235, p = 2.02 × 10− 7), CD4 + T cells (r = -.125, p = 6.32 × 10− 3), CD8 + T cells (r = -.239, p = 1.31 × 10− 7) and plasma cells (r = -.186, p = 4.49 × 10− 5) (Fig. 6A - F). Additionally, elevated TRIM29 expression in LUSC patients was significantly associated with prolonged overall survival, particularly in an immune microenvironment characterized by abundant CD8 + T-cell infiltration and reduced infiltration of plasma cells and M2 macrophages (Fig. 6G - L). However, unadjusted multi-algorithm analyses suggested that TRIM29-high tumors exhibit an immune-cold phenotype with reduced immune infiltration. After adjusting for tumor purity (ESTIMATE-derived), keratinization proxy (KRT5), and clinical covariates (age, sex, stage), the association between TRIM29 expression and CD8 + T-cell infiltration was attenuated and no longer significant (CIBERSORT “T cells CD8”: β = −0.0121, P = .148, FDR = 0.641; Table S2). Additionally, ssGSEA immune function signatures were not significant after adjustment (Tables S6–S7). These results suggest that the immune differences observed in bulk transcriptomes may be partially explained by confounding from tumor purity and squamous differentiation programs.

Because immune infiltration estimates from bulk transcriptomes can be confounded by tumor purity and keratinization programs, we refrained from performing formal mediation or causal modeling linking TRIM29, immune infiltration, and survival. Instead, we focused on multivariable adjusted association analyses (Tables S2–S7) and multivariable survival models (Table S1) to account for these confounding factors.

TRIM29 suppresses immune infiltration, immune function and tumor development through multiple pathways

Immune profiling revealed a widespread suppression of both immune cell populations and immune functional activity in TRIM29-expressing tumors. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, comparative transcriptomics identified 554 upregulated genes and 759 downregulated genes between cohorts with high and low expression of TRIM29 (Fig. 5F). Pathway enrichment analyses revealed distinct biological associations: downregulated genes in TRIM29-high tumors were predominantly enriched in processes related to lymphocyte and leukocyte proliferation, activation of adaptive immunity activation (including T cell differentiation: Th1/Th2/Th17), and immune synapse formation (such as leukocyte adhesion, MHC complex assembly) (Fig. 7A and B). In contrast, upregulated genes were associated with epithelial morphogenesis programs, including keratinocyte differentiation and epidermal development (Fig. 7C and D).

Systematic pathway interrogation confirmed global immune suppression in the TRIM29 high-expression group, as evidenced by impaired antigen presentation, disrupted cytokine signaling, and compromised intercellular communication, such as cell adhesion molecules (Fig. 7E). Hallmark analysis further revealed, at the transcriptomic level, the co-suppression of both oncogenic pathways (e.g., KRAS signaling) and immune effector modules (e.g., interferon α/γ responses, IL6-JAK-STAT3/IL2-STAT5 signaling) in the TRIM29 high-expression group, suggesting that TRIM29 suppresses the expression of these key signaling pathways. In contrast, the Hallmark analysis also highlighted the activation of tumor-suppressive pathways, such as p53 signaling, and epithelial integrity programs, (e.g., apical junction formation) in the TRIM29 high-expression group, underscoring a complex regulatory role for TRIM29 that counterbalances its immune-suppressive effects (Fig. 7F - H).

By balancing the accuracy and number of nodes obtained under different parameter settings on the STRING web server, PPI network reconstruction identified seven direct TRIM29 interactors (KRT5, TRAT1, HINT1, DES, BBOX1, TRIM23, and DVL2) (Fig. 8A).These interactors mediate differential in two key areas: immune suppression through TRAT1-associated repression of lymphocyte activation effectors (CD8A/B, CD2, ITK, CD3D and LCK) (Fig. 8B and C), and epithelial oncogenesis enhancement via KRT5-mediated upregulation of keratinization markers (KRT16/17/3) and tumor progression factors (COL17A1, PKP1, EGFR) (Fig. 8D and E). Moreover, differentially gene expression analysis revealed that KRT5 expression was significantly upregulated, while TRAT1 expression was downregulated in the TRIM29 high-expression group. KRT5, a canonical marker of squamous differentiation, contributes to epithelial structural integrity43, while TRAT1, a T-cell receptor–associated transmembrane adaptor, modulates T-cell activation and the balance between effector and regulatory responses44,45. These findings suggest that TRIM29 may cooperate with KRT5 and TRAT1 to maintain the classical squamous morphology of LUSC and contribute to shaping a tumor microenvironment characterized by low immune infiltration. However, since the PPI data were sourced from the STRING platform, and detailed clinical information (such as stage, smoking history, treatment background) was not provided, we are unable to fully explore the consistency of the immunosuppressive microenvironment across different clinical contexts. Nevertheless, this analysis underscores the potential role of the immunosuppressive microenvironment in the overall sample. Future studies incorporating patient clinical data for stratified analyses will be crucial in validating the consistency of the immunological microenvironment’s role across different patient groups. Given the strong enrichment of keratinization/epithelial programs (including KRT5) in TRIM29-high tumors, we incorporated KRT5 as a covariate in the adjusted immune and survival analyses to reduce confounding by squamous differentiation programs (Tables S1–S3).

TRIM29 suppresses LUSC malignant progression and inhibits immune cell infiltration

To investigate the functional role of TRIM29 in LUSC progression, we ectopically expressed TRIM29 in mouse LUSC tumor cell line KLN205. As shown in Fig. 9, overexpression of TRIM29 markedly inhibited cell growth (Fig. 9A). Moreover, TRIM29 significantly increased the expression of E-cadherin and downregulated key EMT marker, including N-cadherin, Snail, ZEB1 (Fig. 9B), suggesting that TRIM29 may suppress cell migration and tumor metastasis. Consistent with these findings, ectopic expression of TRIM29 also significantly inhibited cell migration (Fig. 9C and D). In a syngeneic mouse model of LUSC, TRIM29 overexpression suppressed both tumor growth and metastasis (Fig. 9E and G). Notably, TRIM29 also significantly reduced the infiltration of CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 9H). While bulk RNA-seq–based immune estimates can be confounded by tumor purity and keratinization programs, this syngeneic model provides orthogonal evidence that TRIM29 overexpression reduces intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration. These findings suggest a context-dependent role of TRIM29 in tumor-intrinsic phenotypes and microenvironment remodeling.

Discussion

TRIM29 exerts highly context-dependent functions in cancer, and its role appears to vary with tumor type, cellular context, and interacting molecular networks46. Across a range of malignancies, among them gastric, pancreatic, and cervical cancers, TRIM29 is frequently upregulated and has been linked to enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metastatic potential30,34. In contrast, studies in esophageal, breast, and cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas have suggested a tumor-suppressive role, with TRIM29 downregulated through epigenetic silencing31,32,47. In LUSC, earlier reports have identified pronounced TRIM29 overexpression in tumor tissues and cell lines relative to normal bronchial epithelial cells48. In the present study, we found that TRIM29 expression is upregulated in LUSC due to promoter hypomethylation and copy number amplification. Notably, high TRIM29 expression was significantly associated with prolonged overall survival, suggesting a potential tumor-suppressive role for TRIM29. However, in multivariable Cox models adjusted for clinical covariates, tumor purity, and keratinization-related programs, TRIM29 did not remain an independent prognostic factor and provided no incremental predictive value over clinical baseline models (Table S1). This suggests that its prognostic utility as a standalone biomarker in TCGA-LUSC may be limited. Mechanistically, TRIM29 appears to exert a dual function in both tumor suppression and immune modulation. On the one hand, TRIM29 upregulates tumor suppressor genes such as p53 and markers of epithelial differentiation, while downregulating oncogenic pathways, including KRAS and the IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway, as well as EMT process. Notably, IL-6/STAT3 signaling is well-documented in promoting tumor invasion and metastasis by upregulating factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and EMT-related transcription factors, such as Twist. Therefore, the inhibitory effect of TRIM29 on this pathway may be a key mechanism underlying its tumor-suppressive function in LUSC.

On the other hand, our findings, in conjunction with previous studies, suggest that TRIM29 negatively regulates several aspects of the immune response. It has been shown to suppress type I interferon and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression via the NF-κB pathway49, as well as to diminish NK cell cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion50. Additionally, TRIM29 may influence the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), promoting a shift toward pro-tumoral M2-type macrophages at the expense of M1-type macrophages51–53. In this study, we also highlight the suppressive effect of TRIM29 on T cell proliferation, differentiation, and activation, in addition to its downregulation of several immune checkpoint genes. Importantly, immune infiltration and immune function inferences derived from bulk transcriptomes can be confounded by tumor purity and squamous differentiation/keratinization programs. After adjusting for tumor purity (ESTIMATE-derived), KRT5, and clinical covariates, the association between TRIM29 and CD8 + T-cell fraction was attenuated and no longer significant, and ssGSEA immune function signatures were not significant after adjustment (Tables S2–S7). These findings suggest that transcriptome-inferred immune patterns should be interpreted with caution, as the immune-cold phenotype observed in TRIM29-high tumors may be partially driven by confounding factors. Nevertheless, our syngeneic model provides orthogonal evidence that TRIM29 overexpression can decrease intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration (Fig. 9H), supporting the hypothesis of a context-dependent immunomodulatory role for TRIM29 that may not be fully captured by bulk deconvolution approaches. Taken together, these findings point to a potential role for TRIM29 in shaping an immune-restrained microenvironment in LUSC. This interpretation is supported indirectly by recent evidence from OSCC, in which spatial redistribution of MDSCs was shown to drive immune suppression and therapeutic resistance through ANXA1-FPR signaling. Although the underlying mechanism may differ from that in LUSC, these findings reinforce the importance of MDSC-associated immune remodeling during squamous tumor progression54. While this broad-spectrum immune suppression could facilitate immune evasion, it is important to note that in LUSC, TRIM29 expression remains associated with a better prognosis. This suggests a finely tuned balance between immunomodulation and tumor control.

The dual role of TRIM29 in tumor suppression and immune modulation can be better understood by exploring its selective regulation of these opposing functions. In our study, TRIM29 was shown to upregulate tumor suppressor genes, such as p53, while concurrently downregulating oncogenic pathways, including IL6-JAK-STAT3 and KRAS signaling. The immune modulation by TRIM29, particularly its suppression of immune responses, may be mediated through selective inhibition of immune checkpoint genes and the modulation of tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization, favoring an M2 phenotype. While unadjusted analyses suggested reduced CD8 + T-cell infiltration in TRIM29-high tumors, adjusted analyses revealed that this relationship is not independent of tumor purity and keratinization programs (Table S2), underscoring the importance of cell-type–resolved validation. Additionally, TRIM29 may influence immune signaling dynamics by interacting with immune-related proteins such as TRAT1 and KRT5. Notably, TRIM29 could mediate this dual role through differential activation of intracellular signaling pathways. For instance, TRIM29 may suppress NF-κB-mediated immune responses in immune cells while concurrently promoting p53-mediated tumor suppression in epithelial cells, indicating a cell-type specific action of TRIM29 in the tumor microenvironment. This selective modulation of immune suppression and tumor control may be influenced by factors such as epigenetic modifications, changes in the tumor microenvironment (e.g., cytokines, immune cell infiltration), and metabolic shifts, warranting further investigation to fully elucidate the mechanisms at play.

Furthermore, TRIM29 may regulate both epithelial structure and immune signaling through interacting with cytoskeletal and immune-related proteins, particularly KRT5 and TRAT1. KRT5 is highly expressed in LUSC, demonstrating significant subtype specificity with a sensitivity of 93.9% and a specificity of 98.9%43. TRAT1 has been shown to be downregulated in association with poor prognosis and reduced immune cell infiltration in NSCLC45. In our study, differential expression analysis revealed that KRT5 was significantly upregulated, while TRAT1 was downregulated in the TRIM29 high-expression group. Based on these findings, we propose that TRIM29 may help maintain epithelial identity in LUSC by upregulating KRT5, while simultaneously promoting immune evasion through the suppression of TRAT1. Interestingly, a study in colorectal cancer suggested that TRIM29 enhances tumor progression by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of KRT555, which contrasts with our observations in LUSC. This discrepancy points to a tissue-specific mechanism of TRIM29–KRT5 regulation, further underscoring the context-dependent nature of TRIM29’s functional role across different tumor types.

However, conflicting reports suggest that TRIM29 may facilitate tumor progression by promoting the autophagic degradation of E-cadherin, with high TRIM29 expression correlating with poor prognosis in LUSC48. Additionally, TRIM29 has been shown to directly bind to and antagonize p53-mediated tumor suppressive functions56. These findings stand in contrast to our results, which indicate a positive correlation between TRIM29 expression and p53, suggesting a tumor-suppressive role of TRIM29 in LUSC. Such discrepancies may stem from differences in data sources, experimental models, tumor subtypes, or inherent tumor heterogeneity. Moreover, TRIM29 has been implicated in acting as a tumor suppressor in early-stage estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer, particularly in patients under the age of 55 who have not received systemic therapy, where high TRIM29 expression is associated with improved survival outcomes57. This observation aligns with our findings in LUSC and underscores the tumor-type specificity of TRIM29’s function, which may be modulated by epigenetic context, the immune microenvironment, and lineage differentiation.

Recent advancements have underscored the significant impact of immune cell infiltration on both tumor progression and therapeutic response. Studies have demonstrated that immune cells within the TME are integral to regulating tumor behavior and its sensitivity to treatment. Furthermore, recent reviews highlight that tumor-infiltrating immune cell states can vary across molecular subtypes and exhibit stage-dependent dynamics, with profound clinical implications for prognosis and treatment response58.Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a powerful tool, enhancing diagnostic and prognostic capabilities in lung cancer. Notably, AI technologies have improved the detection of early pulmonary nodules and facilitated more accurate treatment decision-making. In addition to imaging-based applications, AI-driven analytical frameworks have been employed to imaging-based applications, AI-driven analytical frameworks have been employed to quantify cancer stemness indices from large-scale datasets, providing complementary insights into tumor progression, treatment resistance, and prognosis59. With applications in natural language processing and image recognition, AI technologies hold great promise for revolutionizing lung cancer diagnosis and prognosis, potentially improving treatment strategies and reducing mortality rates.

In conclusion, our study supports a tumor-suppressive role for TRIM29 in LUSC by inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and EMT. While unadjusted bulk transcriptomic analyses suggested that TRIM29-high tumors exhibit an immune-cold phenotype with reduced immune infiltration, these associations were attenuated after adjusting for tumor purity and keratinization-related programs, indicating potential confounding effects. Notably, our syngeneic mouse model provides complementary evidence that TRIM29 overexpression can decrease intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration. Collectively, these findings suggest that TRIM29 functions as a context-dependent “molecular rheostat,” balancing tumor-intrinsic suppression with microenvironment remodeling. Further mechanistic investigations using cell-type–resolved approaches are necessary to better elucidate the complex role of TRIM29 in LUSC.
