Disparities in Adult Influenza Vaccination in the United States: Analysis of 2023 BRFSS Data
Authors: Christian Smolen, Joseph Fava, Paul E Kilgore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite longstanding recommendations for universal adult influenza vaccination, coverage remains suboptimal in the United States (U.S.). The objective of this study is to examine current influenza vaccination coverage and identify demographic, socioeconomic, and geographic disparities in vaccination among U.S. adults.MethodsCross-sectional analysis of 2023 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data from 402,005 adults across 52 states/territories. Survey-weighted logistic regression models assessed independent predictors of self-reported influenza vaccination in the past twelve months, including demographics, socioeconomic factors, healthcare access, and health status.ResultsOverall vaccination coverage was 42.2% (95% CI 42.0–42.4%), well below the Healthy People 2030 target of 70%. Coverage increased markedly with age, from 29.2% among 18–24 year-olds to 66.9% among those ≥ 80 years of age. Women had higher coverage than men (45.4% vs. 38.7%, p < 0.001). Strong socioeconomic gradients emerged: coverage among college graduates (54.3%) exceeded that of those with less than a high school education (32.6%) and those earning ≥ $200,000 (53.5%) exceeded those earning < $15,000 (34.4%). Insurance status showed the largest disparity—45.1% of insured versus 16.2% of uninsured adults were vaccinated. Geographic variation was substantial, ranging from 19.1% in the Virgin Islands to 56.6% in Massachusetts. In multivariable analysis, the strongest predictors were advanced age (≥ 80 years: adjusted OR = 3.51, 95% CI 3.18–3.87), insurance coverage (adjusted OR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.57–1.93), and recent healthcare engagement (checkup within past year; adjusted OR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.53–1.73).ConclusionsInfluenza vaccination coverage remains far below national targets, with pronounced disparities across age, socioeconomic status, insurance coverage, and geography. These findings suggest that efforts to advance equity may benefit from addressing structural barriers, such as expanding insurance coverage, workplace vaccination programs, and targeted outreach to underserved populations; because the data are observational, these represent potential implications rather than conclusions about causal effects.Keywords: Adult, Disparities, Immunization, Influenza, Survey, Vaccine, Vaccination

Key Summary Points

Despite longstanding recommendations for universal annual adult influenza vaccination in the United States (U.S.), only about half of Americans receive the vaccine on an annual basis.Using data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data, this study evaluates current influenza vaccination coverage and attempts to identify patient factors that impact vaccination rates among U.S. adults.Key findings included overall patient-reported vaccination coverage of 42.2% with significantly higher rates among older versus younger adults.Other strong predictors of receiving influenza vaccination included having a higher education level, higher household income, health insurance, and seeing a personal healthcare provider in the past year (p < 0.001 for each factor).

Introduction

Influenza remains a significant public health threat in the United States (U.S.), causing substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burden annually [1]. During the 2022–2023 influenza season, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated that influenza was associated with 31 million illnesses, 14 million medical visits, 360,000 hospitalizations, and 21,000 deaths in the U.S [2, 3]. Beyond the direct health effects, influenza imposes considerable economic costs, including lost work productivity, increased healthcare utilization, and strain on healthcare systems, particularly during peak transmission periods [4]. Older adults, young children, pregnant women, and individuals with chronic medical conditions face disproportionately high risks of influenza-related complications, including pneumonia, exacerbation of underlying chronic diseases, and death [5].

Annual influenza vaccination remains the most effective preventive measure against influenza infection and its complications [5]. Vaccination has been consistently shown to reduce the risk of influenza illness by 40–60% when the vaccine is well matched to circulating strains [6]. Beyond individual protection, influenza vaccination contributes to community-level immunity, reducing viral transmission and protecting vulnerable populations who cannot be vaccinated or who may have suboptimal immune responses [2, 7, 8]. Vaccination reduces the severity of illness among those who become infected despite vaccination, decreasing hospitalizations and deaths [9].

Vaccination coverage varies considerably across different population subgroups and geographic regions. Historical trends have consistently shown higher vaccination rates among older adults, particularly those aged 65 years and older, compared to younger age groups [10]. However, even among high-risk populations for whom vaccination is most strongly recommended, coverage remains inadequate [11]. Understanding the factors that influence these coverage patterns is critical for developing targeted interventions to improve vaccination uptake.

This study addresses these knowledge gaps by leveraging data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), the nation's largest continuously conducted health survey system. The BRFSS provides robust, population-representative data with sufficient sample sizes to examine vaccination patterns across diverse demographic subgroups and geographic areas [12]. This study aims to estimate current influenza vaccination coverage, identify independent predictors of influenza vaccination, assess the contemporary relevance of traditional vaccination predictors in the post-COVID-19 pandemic period, and identify emerging patterns that may inform future public health interventions.

Methods

Overview

This study is a cross-sectional analysis of survey data obtained by the BRFSS in 2023. Survey data included noninstitutionalized adults aged 18 years and older living in the U.S. with access to landline or cellular telephone service. Participants who completed or partially completed the interview through the demographics section were included in the analytic sample. The BRFSS is an annual state-based survey employing a complex, stratified probability sampling design with two sampling frames. For landline samples, telephone numbers are selected using disproportionate stratified random sampling, in which numbers are drawn from strata defined by the expected density of working residential numbers and one adult is randomly selected within each sampled household; cellular samples are obtained by random sampling of cell-phone numbers, with each respondent treated as a single-adult household. Each participating state and territory determines its own annual sample size before the data-collection year, governed primarily by available funding together with a target precision for key state-level prevalence estimates; national estimates are produced by aggregating the state-based samples and applying the final survey weights. Because this study is a secondary analysis of an existing surveillance dataset rather than a primary study with a recruited cohort, no a priori sample-size or power calculation was performed, and the analytic sample comprised all eligible 2023 respondents (n = 402,005); given this large sample, the precision of all estimates is reflected in the reported standard errors and 95% confidence intervals. The influenza vaccination item ascertained receipt of a seasonal influenza vaccine during the 12 months preceding the interview and did not capture vaccination in prior seasons. The primary outcome was influenza vaccination status in the past 12 months, assessed by the question: "during the past 12 months, have you had either a flu shot or a flu vaccine that was sprayed in your nose?" Responses were dichotomized as vaccinated (yes) versus not vaccinated (no), with refused and "don't know/not sure" responses classified as missing data. Independent variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity), socioeconomic factors (education level, annual household income, employment status, and marital status), healthcare access indicators (health insurance coverage, personal healthcare provider, cost barrier to medical care in past 12 months, and time since last routine checkup), health status measures (self-rated general health status, body mass index [BMI], and presence of chronic conditions), and geographic variables (state of residence and metropolitan status).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses accounted for the complex survey design using appropriate survey procedures [13–15]. Descriptive statistics were calculated to characterize the study population and estimate the overall influenza vaccination prevalence. Weighted percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for categorical variables. BRFSS reporting guidelines dictated that the researchers suppress estimates when the relative standard error (RSE) surpassed 30% or when the unweighted denominator fell below 50 respondents. Bivariate analyses examined the association between each independent variable and influenza vaccination status. Chi-square tests were used to assess statistical significance, with survey design-adjusted Rao-Scott F-statistics.

To identify predictors of influenza vaccination while accounting for the complex survey design, we developed hierarchical logistic regression models using a stepwise approach, fitting survey-weighted generalized linear models at each stage [16, 17]. Three sequential models were developed:

- Model 1: adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, race/ethnicity).

- Model 2: adjusted for Model 1 variables plus health insurance and healthcare provider.

- Model 3: Fully adjusted model including demographic, socioeconomic, healthcare access, and health status variables.

The models incorporated sampling weights to adjust for unequal selection probabilities and the clustered nature of the data, using pseudolikelihood estimation methods appropriate for complex survey designs [18]. Model selection was based on the Akaike information criterion adjusted for survey design [17]. Model fit was assessed using survey-adjusted pseudo-R2 statistics. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF > 10 indicating problematic collinearity. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% CIs were calculated. Statistical significance was determined at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Complex survey-weighted analyses accounting for the BRFSS stratified sampling design were conducted using survey weights, strata, and primary sampling units as specified in the BRFSS documentation to generate nationally representative estimates. Variance estimation was conducted using Taylor series linearization to account for the stratified cluster sampling design [19]. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024) in RStudio (version 2024.09.0, Build 387).

Response Rates and Data Quality

Response rates were calculated using the American Association for Public Opinion Research Response Rate Formula #4 [20]. The median survey response rate for all participating states and territories in 2023 was 44.7% (range 21.7–63.1%). Response rates varied by sampling frame, with rates calculated separately for landline and cellular telephone samples, then combined using weighted proportions based on sample composition. Data quality was monitored through monthly data submissions, cumulative data quality checks, and state-specific data quality reports. Partially complete interviews were included if respondents completed at least through question 12 of the demographics section.

Missing data patterns were examined for all variables. Participants with missing data on the outcome variable were excluded from the analyses. For independent variables, complete case analysis was employed [21], with sensitivity analyses conducted to assess potential bias from missing data [22]. The impact of missing data was minimal, as the analytic sample retained > 95% of the original sample, suggesting limited potential for selection bias.

The BRFSS is conducted under the authority of individual state health departments with oversight from the CDC. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to interview participation. The study used publicly available, de-identified secondary data and was therefore exempt from institutional review board approval.

Discussion

Vaccine Coverage Overall

This nationally representative study of 402,005 U.S. adults in 2023 revealed that influenza vaccination coverage remains substantially below national targets, with only 42.2% of adults receiving the vaccine. This aligns closely with CDC's FluVaxView estimates of 44.9% for the 2023–24 season, with minor differences likely attributable to timing of data collection and survey method [12]. It also represents a continuation of the plateau in adult influenza vaccination that has persisted for over a decade, with coverage estimates ranging from 37.1–50.2% since 2010. Of note, estimates have been steadily declining from 50.2% in 2020–21 to the aforementioned 44.9% in 2023–24 [12]. Despite decades of public health efforts, vaccination coverage has failed to approach the Healthy People targets, suggesting that current approaches may be insufficient to overcome entrenched barriers to vaccination [23].

The modest coverage we observed stands in stark contrast to vaccination rates among children (55.4%) and healthcare personnel (75.4%) in 2023–24, suggesting that adult vaccination faces unique challenges related to perceived vulnerability, healthcare engagement, and structural barriers. Our finding of declining vaccination with each successive younger age cohort is consistent with established patterns [24], but the magnitude of the age gradient—a 3.5-fold higher odds of vaccination in adults aged ≥ 80 years compared with adults aged 18–24 years—underscores that young and middle-aged adults represent a critically underserved population that contributes disproportionately to community transmission, as modeling studies show these groups often sustain influenza epidemics through higher age-specific reproductive numbers [25].

Socioeconomic Disparities

The pronounced socioeconomic gradients we observed align with existing literature consistent associations between education, income, and influenza vaccination [26–28], though the direction and magnitude of associations vary by how socioeconomic status is measured [29]. Our findings contribute robust U.S.-specific evidence that both education and income operate independently, with education showing strong effects (college graduates vs. less than high school: AOR 1.94) even after adjusting for income, insurance, and healthcare access.

The income gradient we observed, with vaccination coverage ranging from 34.4% among those earning < $15,000 to 53.5% among those earning ≥ $200,000, extends earlier work showing dose–response relationships across finer income categories. Notably, income effects persisted after adjusting for insurance coverage, suggesting that financial barriers operate through multiple pathways beyond direct payment for vaccines—potentially including transportation costs, inability to take time off work, and residence in neighborhoods with limited vaccination infrastructure.

Insurance Coverage and Healthcare Access

Our finding that insurance coverage conferred 74% higher odds of vaccination, making it among the strongest predictors in the model, aligns with extensive literature documenting insurance as a critical structural determinant of preventive services [23, 29, 30]. Despite the Affordable Care Act's requirement that health plans cover influenza vaccination without cost-sharing, uninsured adults face substantial barriers, with only 16.2% vaccinated in our study compared to 45.1% of insured adults. This 2.8-fold difference exceeds disparities reported in some earlier studies, possibly reflecting increasing stratification of healthcare access or differential impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on insurance coverage among vulnerable populations [31–34].

The powerful association we observed between time since last routine checkup and vaccination—with those having a checkup within the past year showing 62% higher odds compared to those with a checkup 1–2 years ago—is consistent with research showing that provider recommendations and standing orders remain among the strongest predictors of adult vaccination across populations [35]. The dose–response relationship, with progressively lower odds among those with more distant or no checkup history, suggests that infrequent healthcare users represent a challenging population to reach through clinic-based vaccination strategies alone.

Sex Disparities

The persistent sex disparity we observed, with males showing 16% lower odds of vaccination even after adjusting for all other factors, replicates well-established patterns in preventive health behaviors. Previous research has attributed sex differences in vaccination to multiple mechanisms, including gender differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, health attitudes, perceived vulnerability to illness, time availability for preventive care, and differential provider recommendations [36]. Our finding that the sex gap was most pronounced among middle-aged adults (18–64 years) and narrowed substantially among older adults may reflect age-related changes in healthcare engagement patterns, with older men accessing healthcare more regularly through management of chronic conditions.

Geographic Variation

The substantial state-level variation we observed, with a 37.5-percentage point range from the Virgin Islands (19.1%) to Massachusetts (56.6%), exceeds variability reported in some earlier studies and suggests important state-level contextual factors influencing vaccination [37, 38]. The clustering of high-coverage states in the Northeast and low-coverage states in the South and Mountain West likely reflects multiple intersecting factors, including state Medicaid expansion status, healthcare infrastructure density, political and cultural attitudes toward vaccination, and state-level vaccination promotion efforts. Recent research using county-level data has linked geographic variation to social vulnerability indices encompassing poverty, unemployment, housing quality, and minority status, suggesting that state-level estimates may mask even greater within-state heterogeneity.

Health Status and Perceived Vulnerability

Our finding that poorer self-rated health predicted higher vaccination, with those in fair or poor health showing 35–36% higher odds compared to those in excellent health, supports a perceived vulnerability model wherein individuals with chronic conditions demonstrate heightened risk awareness and responsiveness to vaccination recommendations. The positive associations we observed with respiratory chronic conditions (COPD and asthma) suggest that guideline-concordant recommendations for high-risk groups are reaching at least some priority populations. However, the absence of a significant association with heart disease—despite strong clinical evidence supporting flu vaccination for cardiovascular patients—indicates missed opportunities for provider recommendations and patient education in this high-risk group [39].

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, the large, nationally representative sample of over 400,000 adults provides robust statistical power for detecting disparities across demographic and socioeconomic subgroups while maintaining precision in state-level estimates. The BRFSS represents the gold standard for adult vaccination surveillance in the U.S., with established reliability and validity documented over four decades. Second, the use of complex survey weights ensures findings are representative of the U.S. adult population, addressing selection bias from differential response rates. Third, our hierarchical modeling approach allowed us to disentangle the independent effects of correlated predictors and assess the relative importance of different domains (demographics, socioeconomic status, healthcare access, health status) in explaining vaccination disparities. Fourth, the comprehensive set of predictors examined, spanning demographic, socioeconomic, healthcare access, and health status domains, provides a holistic understanding of factors influencing vaccination.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First and most importantly, vaccination status was self-reported. Although validation studies have demonstrated moderate to good agreement between self-reported and documented influenza vaccination [40–42], the potential for recall, misclassification, and social desirability bias must be acknowledged and may cause overestimation of coverage. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and the temporality of associations cannot be established; although some predictors (e.g., education, insurance status) plausibly precede vaccination, all findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Third, the median response rate of 44.7% raises concerns about potential non-response bias, though BRFSS weighting procedures are designed to minimize such bias. Adults with lower socioeconomic status and those less engaged with healthcare—populations at highest risk for under vaccination may be underrepresented despite weighting adjustments. Fourth, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. The BRFSS does not capture several determinants known to influence vaccination, including vaccine attitudes and confidence, receipt of a provider recommendation, prior-season vaccination, perceived risk, trust in healthcare, misinformation exposure, political ideology, and fine-grained measures of access such as distance to a vaccination site, clinic hours, and out-of-pocket cost; the observed associations may therefore be partly attributable to these unmeasured behavioral and access-related factors. Our models explained only 10.5% of the variance, indicating that most individual variation in vaccination stems from factors not captured in the BRFSS.

Fifth, the reliance on telephone surveys excludes households without telephone access, though telephone coverage exceeds 99% in the U.S. Nonetheless, telephone coverage is lower among some underserved populations, potentially contributing to the underestimation of disparities. Sixth, ecological inference from state-level variation must be made cautiously, as state-level estimates aggregate diverse subpopulations and cannot fully capture individual-level processes. Seventh, race and ethnicity, recognized as critical dimensions of health equity, were not included in our analytic models due to inconsistencies in variable coding across survey years, representing an important gap that should be addressed in future research. Eighth, the study examines only the 2023 influenza season, limiting our ability to assess trends over time or seasonal variations in predictors. Relatedly, the BRFSS item captures vaccination only during the 12 months preceding the interview and does not record prior-season vaccination history; because prior-season vaccination is a strong predictor of current-season uptake, its absence represents a further source of unmeasured confounding.

Future Research Directions

Future research should address several key questions. First, longitudinal studies examining changes in vaccination behavior over time, including responses to new policies (e.g., Medicaid expansion), public health interventions (e.g., community-based vaccination programs), and external shocks (e.g., COVID-19 pandemic), could elucidate causal mechanisms and inform more effective interventions. Second, qualitative research exploring barriers and facilitators to vaccination among underserved populations—including young adults, males, low socioeconomic status groups, and uninsured adults—could inform the design of culturally tailored interventions addressing population-specific needs. Third, intervention studies rigorously evaluating the effectiveness of novel vaccination strategies such as mobile clinics, workplace programs, text message reminders, financial incentives, and peer-to-peer messaging would build the evidence base for scalable solutions.

Fourth, research examining the role of vaccine attitudes, misinformation and disinformation, trust in healthcare, and political ideology in mediating socioeconomic and demographic disparities would inform communication strategies and counteract vaccine hesitancy. Fifth, county-level and neighborhood-level analyses using linked data from multiple sources could provide a finer-grained understanding of geographic variation and identify local hot spots for targeted interventions. Sixth, comparative effectiveness research examining different healthcare delivery models (e.g., integrated health systems vs. fragmented care, pharmacy-based vs. clinic-based vaccination) could inform healthcare system optimization. Finally, economic evaluations quantifying the cost-effectiveness of different strategies to increase vaccination coverage, particularly among underserved populations, would inform resource allocation decisions.

Conclusions

Influenza vaccination remains substantially below national targets among U.S. adults. Pronounced disparities exist across age, sex, socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and geographic regions, consistent with structural inequities in the U.S. healthcare system. Age and healthcare access were the strongest predictors in our models, and persistent socioeconomic gradients suggest that education and income remain associated with vaccination even after accounting for insurance coverage and healthcare access. Progress toward the Healthy People 2030 goal of 70% coverage among adults may require comprehensive, multilevel approaches addressing these barriers, though the observational design of this study means such strategies should be evaluated rather than assumed effective.
