Diagnostic Performance for Detection of Glaucomatous Structural Damage Using Pixelwise Analysis of Retinal Thickness Measurements
Authors: Hongli Yang, Juan Reynaud, Glen P. Sharpe, Dawn Jennings, Cindy Albert, Trinity Holthausen, Xiue Jiang, Shaban Demirel, Steven L. Mansberger, Marcelo T. Nicolela, Stuart K. Gardiner, Balwantray C. Chauhan, Claude F. Burgoyne, Brad Fortune
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the diagnostic accuracy of thickness measurements of individual and combined macular retinal layers to discriminate 188 glaucomatous and 148 glaucoma suspect eyes from 362 healthy control (HC) eyes on a pixel-by-pixel basis.MethodsFor this retrospective study, we manually corrected the segmentations of posterior pole optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans to determine the thickness of the nerve fiber layer (NFL), ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), the ganglion cell complex (GCC), and the total neural retina (TR). For each eye, the total number of pixels with thickness values less than the fifth percentile of the HC distribution was used to create a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for each layer and for layer combinations.ResultsUsing total abnormal pixel count criteria to discriminate glaucoma from HC eyes, the individual layers with the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC) were the NFL and GCL; IPL performance was significantly lower (P < 0.05). GCC had a significant higher AUC (94.3%) than individual the AUC of the NFL (92.3%) (P = 0.0231) but not higher than AUC of the GCL (93.4%) (P = 0.3487). The highest AUC (95.4%) and sensitivity (85.1%) at 95% specificity was found for the Boolean combination of NFL or GCL. The highest AUC is not significantly higher (P = 0.0882) than the AUC of the GCC but the highest sensitivity is significantly higher than the sensitivity of the GCC. This pattern was similar for discriminating between suspect and HC eyes (P = 0.0356).ConclusionsUsing pixel-based methods, the diagnostic accuracy of NFL and GCL exceeded that of IPL and TR. GCC had equivalent performance as NFL and GCL. The specific spatial locations within the posterior pole that exhibit best performance vary depending on which layer is being assessed. Recognizing this dependency highlights the importance of considering multiple layers independently, as they offer complementary information for effective and comprehensive diagnosis.

Glaucoma is the most common optic neuropathy worldwide and a major cause of irreversible blindness resulting from degeneration and death of retinal ganglion cells. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) enables objective three-dimensional visualization and morphometry of the anatomical changes caused by glaucoma, including loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and their axons, and the characteristic deformation of optic nerve head (ONH) tissues. The density of retinal ganglion cell bodies is normally highest within the macula, adjacent to the rim of the fovea, where OCT easily reveals this nuclear layer to be several times thicker than it is in more peripheral retina. This results in an increased dynamic range for detection and monitoring of glaucoma by OCT layer thickness measurements. It may also facilitate enhanced diagnostic performance of these measurements by enabling image segmentation algorithms to be more accurate and reliable. Similarly, degeneration of retinal ganglion cell axon bundles is typically assessed by OCT and other imaging modalities in the peripapillary region, where the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) is normally thickest and progressive thinning may be detected most reliably.1,2

Another diagnostically important characteristic of retinal layer thinning caused by glaucoma is that it commonly occurs in stereotypical localized spatial patterns, such as the well-known “arcuate bundle defect.” Confidence in detecting glaucomatous structural loss is increased when all aspects correspond spatially, that is, thinning of corresponding sectors of the RGC layer, the inner plexiform layer (IPL), the RNFL and the ONH rim tissue, along with loss of associated microvasculature, which can be assessed noninvasively using OCT-angiography.3

Many studies have shown that glaucomatous structural damage can be detected with high accuracy using OCT measurements of tissue thinning in the macula area, either for individual inner retinal layers4–8 (such as the nerve fiber layer [NFL], ganglion cell layer [GCL], or IPL) or for combined layers, such as the ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer,6,9,10 or ganglion cell complex (GCC), defined as NFL, GCL, and IPL combined4,11–13. However, most of these previous studies used either average thickness measurements for relatively wide spatial areas (global average over entire OCT scan area) or regional averages for sectors with predetermined shape/location. Similarly, few previous studies4,7 have compared diagnostic ability of individual retinal tissue layers (NFL, GCL, IPL, etc.). To our knowledge, no previous study has assessed and compared diagnostic performance on the basis of analyzing thickness measurements of individual retinal layers, combinations of layers, and using the data from individual pixels (rather than larger spatial regions). Some of this gap may be due to historical limitations of individual layer segmentation accuracy and the labor required to correct errors of automated segmentation algorithms, as well as previous limitations of OCT scan density. In this study, our goal was to assess the diagnostic ability of macular OCT scans to detect glaucomatous structural damage without using pre-determined spatial boundaries (sectors or cluster of points) We compared diagnostic performance of individual layers to each other and to combinations of layers in terms of discriminating eyes classified as having glaucoma (perimetric loss) from healthy eyes, and discriminating eyes classified as being glaucoma suspect (no perimetric loss) from healthy eyes.

Methods

Study Participants

This cross-sectional study compares OCT, clinical and demographic data from participants enrolled in two separate longitudinal observational studies of glaucoma (GL) and glaucoma suspects (GLS), as well from a separate study of Healthy Controls (HC) that was designed to generate a normative reference database.14,15 For this study, OCT data from only one eye of each participant, selected at random, was used. The GL and GLS cohorts (see details below) were combined from two separate ongoing studies: (1) the Portland Progression Project,16–18 a longitudinal study, supported by the National Institutes of Health, based at the Legacy Devers Eye Institute in Portland, Oregon, whose aims are to determine the risk factors predicting the rate of progression and to evaluate and improve relevant diagnostic tools in ocular hypertensive and early glaucoma eyes, and (2) longitudinal glaucoma studies at the Eye Care Centre, Nova Scotia Health Authority, Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada. The aims of Halifax longitudinal study, supported by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, are to longitudinally assess the rate of progression in glaucoma eyes using OCT structural parameters and visual field-testing parameters. Enrollment of participants for both the Portland and Halifax studies was based not only on these criteria but also on the willingness and likelihood of each recruit to remain in the longitudinal study, itself a subjective criterion; however, this subjectivity is not based on any ocular findings. Data for the HC cohort was obtained from a previous multi-centered normative study14,15 (eight centers total: five in the United States, two in Germany, and one in Canada). Subjects for HC cohort were recruited by advertisement in local media, on bulletin boards, and from a registry of subjects who had taken part in other studies. Approximately equal numbers of subjects were recruited in each decade group from 20 to 90 years.

All procedures in all of these studies adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, complied with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, and were approved and monitored by the Institutional Review Board or its equivalent at each center. All participants provided written informed consent after having the risks and benefits of participation explained to them.

Inclusion criteria for the HC subjects included age 18 to 90 years, no history of glaucoma, intraocular pressure (IOP) ≤ 21 mm Hg, best-corrected visual acuity ≥20/40, refraction less than ±6.00 diopters sphere and ±2.00 diopters cylinder; Glaucoma Hemifield Test within normal limits (from standard automated perimetry, using the HFA II perimeter [Carl Zeiss Meditec Inc, Dublin, CA, USA], 24-2 test pattern and SITA Standard test strategy), and the availability of acceptable ONH, circumpapillary RNFL, and macular OCT scans. Exclusion criteria were unusable stereo photographs or insufficient OCT scan quality (scan quality score < 20), clinically abnormal optic disc appearance, any intraocular surgery (except uncomplicated cataract surgery) or any vitreal, retinal, choroidal, or neuro-ophthalmological disease.14,15

Our reference standard test for glaucoma included the following functional test as described below.19 GL eyes (combined from Portland Progression Project and Halifax cohorts) had a clinical diagnosis of glaucoma as judged by the patient's clinician and confirmed for this study by visual field criteria of pattern standard deviation > 1.99 dB) or GHT outside normal limits on two consecutive tests with the Humphrey Field Analyzer 24-2 pattern. GLS eyes (derived from the Portland Progression Project only) had ocular hypertension (untreated IOP greater than 22 mm Hg), or a suspicious optic disc appearance (neuroretinal rim notching or narrowing; or disc hemorrhage) along with one or more other risk factors for glaucoma (e.g., age > 70, systemic hypertension, migraine, diet-controlled diabetes, peripheral vasospasm, African ancestry, a self-reported family history of glaucoma, or glaucoma in the fellow eye), but no glaucomatous loss on repeated visual field testing, as described above for the HC cohort. Exclusion criteria for GL/GLS eyes were other causes of significant visual field loss, or inability to produce reliable test results.16,18,20 For subanalyses reported in the Results section, GL eyes were further subdivided into two sub-groups: early GL (MD ≥ −4 dB) and moderate or advanced GL (MD < −4 dB).

OCT Acquisition, Segmentation, and Pixel-Based Quantification of Macular Layer Thickness

In this study, the index test19 is defined as pixel-wise quantification of different macular layer thickness values, adjusted for age and distance between the fovea and ONH, as detailed in this section. The OCT acquisition protocol was identical for all GL, GLS, and HC eyes across all three studies and has been described in detail previously.17,21 Briefly, spectral domain OCT imaging of the ONH, circumpapillary RNFL and macula was performed on each study eye using the same type of instrument and software version (Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany; HEYEX software version 5.4c, Glaucoma Module Premium Edition). For each eye, before image acquisition, refractive correction and values of anterior corneal surface curvature (measured by keratometry) were entered into the OCT instrument database. The OCT scan acquisition protocol used the instrument's interactive Anatomic Positioning System whereby the operator identifies the position of the fovea using live B-scan views, and an estimate of the center of the ONH by marking a pair of BMO points within each of two orthogonal B-scans crossing the ONH. These steps thus also established the fovea-BMO centroid (FoBMO) axis for each eye, which determines the orientation of three subsequent scan types obtained and used for this study.17 (Glaucoma Module Premium Edition In: Spectralis OCT Glaucoma brochure: Heidelberg Engineering).

The first scan type collected was the ONH scan using an enhanced depth imaging mode, which is comprised of 24 radially oriented B-scans, each containing 768 A-lines, spanning 15° total width and centered on the ONH BMO centroid. The real-time averaging was set to 25 repetitions. A separate circular B-scan (12° diameter, 1536 A-lines, average of 100 repetitions), which was also centered on the ONH BMO centroid using the anatomic positioning system, was used to measure circumpapillary RNFL thickness. Finally, the third OCT scan type acquired was the “posterior pole horizontal” pattern, which is the primary focus of this study. This pattern covers a rectangular area of the posterior pole, 30° wide by 25° height, centered on the fovea (identified in the anatomic positioning system) and oriented parallel to the FoBMO axis according to the anatomic positioning system protocol (Fig. 1). It is comprised of 61 equally spaced B-scans (∼120 µm apart) each containing 768 A-lines, spanning 30° total width and averaged in real time using 10 repetitions.

FoBMO macular scan pattern and the macular B-scan (Left) 61 B-scans, 768 A-lines / B-scan, 30° × 25° area centered on the fovea. (Right) The B-scan corresponds to scan on the left (the scan location highlighted with green line).

All OCT scans were required to have an averaged scan quality score ≥ 20 and be free of large truncation or mirror artifacts. All individual B-scans within each of the posterior pole horizontal scans were examined to meet these inclusion criteria. Approximately 5% of OCT scans were excluded for failing to because of one or more of these criteria. For all OCT scan types, the image segmentation began with the instrument's automated set, which was then inspected and manually corrected as necessary by trained observers using custom software (ATL 3D Suite, Portland, OR, USA).17,22,23 For the posterior pole horizonal scans covering the macula, five retinal layer boundaries were used to calculate layer thicknesses along each A-line, including the internal limiting membrane (ILM), external boundaries of the NFL, GCL, inner plexiform layer (IPL), and neurosensory retina [i.e., the anterior aspect of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)] (Fig. 2). Thus a continuous thickness map (containing a maximum of 768 × 61-pixel values) was generated for each of three individual layers of the macular inner retina: NFL, GCL, and IPL, as well as for the ganglion cell complex (GCC, from ILM to posterior boundary of IPL), and total neural retinal thickness (TR, from ILM to the inner boundary of RPE). For each eye, a circular mask with 3° radius centered on the BMO centroid was used to exclude A-lines within or immediately adjacent to the ONH from all calculations of retinal layer thickness values. A visualization of the ONH mask can be seen in Figure 3.

Data Analysis

Because the primary purpose of this study was to compare the diagnostic performance of various individual inner retinal layers, as well as combinations of layers, we applied a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and determined the area under the ROC curve (AUC), as well as the sensitivity at 95% specificity to compare the accuracy of each layer or layer combination to discriminate GL and GLS eyes from HC eyes. We also used a variety of criteria to define a thickness “abnormality,” each of which was then used to generate separate ROC curves (see below) to assess the potential advantages of each criterion.

The first step of the analysis was to adjust each observation (each thickness value at each pixel) by age and distance between the ONH and fovea (i.e., the linear FoBMO distance), given that both factors are well known to influence peripapillary24 and macular retinal thickness21 (our data showed age and FoBMO distance were significantly associated with macular layer thickness in more than 50% of pixels for most of the individual and combination layers). Thus each measured thickness value at each pixel was adjusted to the equivalent value for age 70 years and the average FoBMO (Fovea-BMO centroid) distance of the HC eyes, based on linear regression among the HC eyes. For this cohort of HC eyes, the slope of thickness versus age for the NFL, GCL, IPL, GCC and TR, respectively, was −0.02, −0.06, −0.04, −0.12, and −0.24 µm/year. Using the adjusted thickness values for each pixel, we defined an “OCT structural abnormality” to be any observation that was below the fifth percentile of the HC eye distribution. We then counted the number of abnormal pixels within each eye and generated ROC curves on the basis of those results, as well as other criteria, as follows: (1)Total abnormal pixel count of each individual eye/layer. This approach represents primarily the lateral spatial extent of an abnormality, but would not necessarily capture increasing depth of anatomical defects as severity worsens. We generated a family of ROC curves based on the abnormal pixel count, whereby the performance of each parameter (individual inner retinal layer thickness) was represented by one ROC curve.(2)Total abnormal pixel count of each individual eye using combinations of layers. This approach used both physical layer combinations, GCC, and TR, as well as the results of Boolean combinations of individual inner retinal layers, in order to count the number of abnormal pixels per eye, including: (2A) “NFL and GCL” (if both layer thicknesses were abnormally thin at a given pixel, this parameter was defined as being abnormal at that pixel); (2B) “GCL and IPL”; (2C) “NFL or GCL” (if NFL or GCL thickness was abnormally thin at a given pixel, then this parameter was defined as being abnormal for that pixel); (2D) any two of the three layers: NFL, GCL, IPL.

The cumulative Z-score was defined as the sum of these pixel-wise Z-scores for all abnormal pixels (i.e., those below fifth percentile of normal). As before, we generated a ROC curve for each of the individual macular inner retinal layers (NFL, GCL, and IPL), as well as for the parameters of physically combined layers (GCC and TR) and each of the Boolean logical combinations described above for criterion number 2. We determined sensitivity at 95% specificity and area under the ROC curve, then also estimated their 95% confidence intervals using bootstrapping.

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 3.1.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For both AUC and sensitivity at 95% specificity, 95% confidence intervals were empirically generated using 2000 bootstrap resamplings of the data. The AUC of the NFL was set as the reference and compared with the AUC of all other criteria using the DeLong method.25 Similarly, the sensitivity at 95% specificity for the NFL was set as the reference and compared with the sensitivity of all other criteria using the bootstrap method.26

Results

A total of 698 eyes of 698 participants were included in this study, comprising 362 HC eyes (Age range from 20 to 90), 148 GLS eyes (Age range from 38 to 92) and 188 GL eyes (Age range from 49 to 92) (Table 1). The distribution of visual field Mean Deviation (VF MD) among the GL and GLS eyes is depicted in Supplemental Figure S1. Among the GL eyes, 86 (45.7%) demonstrated early VF defects (VF MD ≥ −4 dB, median −1.92 dB, range −3.94 to 0.72 dB), and 102 eyes (54.3%) demonstrated moderate-advanced VF defects (VF MD < −4 dB, median −7.27 dB, range −27.01 to −4.02 dB). Compared to the HC group, GL subjects were older and GL eyes had a longer FoBMO distance, as well as thinner global average values for macular NFL, GCL, IPL, GCC and TR thickness (Table 1). The GLS group was also older than the HC group, had a more negative spherical equivalent refractive error, longer FoBMO distance, slightly larger global average thickness of NFL, but smaller global average values for macular GCL, and TR thickness (Table 1). Compared to the GLS group, GL subjects were older and GL eyes had lower IOP, shorter FoBMO distance, and thinner global average values for macular NFL, GCL, IPL, GCC and TR thickness. Compared to HC eyes, GL and GLS eyes had very were very slightly, but significantly lower Scan Quality Scores. However, further evaluation indicated that Scan Quality Score was not a confounding factor for individual layer thickness: in a multivariate regression model, individual layer thickness values were associated with age only but not with Scan Quality Score. The demographics of early GL subjects were very similar to moderate-advanced GL subjects, however, early GL eyes had thicker global average values of macular NFL, GCL, IPL, GCC, and TR thickness, on average (Table 2).

The results for diagnostic performance based on total abnormal pixel count (Criteria 1 and 2) are listed in Table 3 and plotted as ROC curves in Figure 5. For GL eyes, the individual inner retinal layers with the best performance were the NFL and GCL; the GCL had the highest AUC (93.4% [91.2%–95.6%]), and second-highest sensitivity (at 95% specificity, 74.5% [65.4%–83.0%]). The NFL had a higher sensitivity (79.5% [69.7%–86.7%]) but a slighter lower AUC (92.3% [89.3%–95.3%], although neither of the values for NFL was significantly different than those for the GCL (sensitivity P = 0.3996; AUC: P = 0.4887). Diagnostic performance of the IPL in GL eyes was significantly lower than that of either NFL or GCL based on values for both AUC (88.7% [85.7%–91.6%], vs. NFL P = 0.0303; vs GCL P < 0.0001) and sensitivity (58.5% [43.1%–66.5%], vs. NFL P = 0.0037; vs. GCL P = 0.0397).

Discussion

The results of our study using pixel-based thickness measurements of individual inner retinal layers demonstrate that the diagnostic performance of the NFL and GCL exceeded that of the IPL but were similar to each other for detecting glaucomatous structural damage. Using thickness measurements of combined layers and Boolean combinations of individual layers with the same pixel-based analyses, we found that the GCC has equivalent performance as NFL and GCL and that the Boolean combinations of individual layers provided only a modest improvement in diagnostic accuracy. As a result of using a pixel-based approach (rather than averaged thickness values from predetermined regions or sectors), we also found that the specific spatial locations within the posterior pole that exhibit best performance depend on which layer is being assessed. These results can be used by clinicians to select appropriate posterior pole OCT scan parameters and to refine their interpretation in the management of glaucoma. These results also suggest that the predetermined spatial patterns used commonly for sectoral thickness measurements are not ideally suited for all individual retinal layers or combined layer parameters. The corollary is that either continuous, pixel-based measurements could be used (for any layer) or that the spatial sector pattern should be tailored to the desired layer.

Our first main finding implies that the thickness of the individual macular inner retinal layers NFL and GCL undergo thinning earlier and more frequently than the IPL as currently detected by the Spectralis OCT device we employed. However, while ganglion cell dendritic atrophy is known to occur at very early stages of experimental glaucoma and has even been shown to be a rescuable stage of retinal ganglion cell injury,27 our results suggest that this pathophysiological phenomenon, to the extent that it may occur in clinical glaucoma,28,29 generally does not manifest as early thinning of the IPL, although it is possible that analysis of IPL sublaminae may reveal subtler changes.30 Atrophy of retinal ganglion cell dendrites may also be masked by changes in other constituents of the IPL, such that textural analysis of OCT reflectance may reveal more than thickness measurements have done.31

Another finding of our study that is important to clinical diagnosis of glaucoma is that the best spatial locations for revealing glaucomatous loss within the macula depend on the specific layer being measured. The most salient locations for a given parameter do not necessarily conform to the most common schemes of sectoral analysis in current use by commercial instrumentation. Although a “dartboard” like pattern of sectors centered on the fovea arrangement should work well for GCL (or GC-IPL) thickness, it would not be well suited for best detection of NFL thinning across the macula. Similarly, our results confirm a spatial pattern of earlier and more prevalent loss within the inferior macula compared to the superior macula,6,32 further supporting a benefit of comparing across the midline such as done in visual field analysis by the Glaucoma Hemifield Test and for asymmetry analysis of OCT posterior pole scans.32–34 Our results also lend further support for use continuous spatial maps and continuous probabilities (z-scores), much like the extensive work by Hood and colleagues has shown,35,36 rather than relying on color-coded broad sectors and/or discrete probability (or thickness) maps for glaucoma diagnosis based on OCT scans of the posterior pole.4,12,37–40 Our results showed slightly better performance of cumulative z-score compared to total abnormal pixel counts across all individual and combined layer definitions, which is consistent with our hypothesis that the z-score approach is more capable of detecting deepening localized loss. However, this improvement did not achieve statistical significance despite the relatively large size of our cohorts and was found only for GL eyes, not GL suspect eyes. Thus the benefit of a z-score approach may be only marginal for population-based diagnosis and best suited to longitudinal follow-up within individual eyes (to better detect deepening of localized loss in addition to lateral spread of progressive damage).

Another result of our study demonstrated that there was a modest benefit to glaucoma diagnosis of analyzing individual inner retinal layer thickness across the posterior pole. Individual layer thickness was only a small enhancement over what is probably the most common parameter currently used, the GCC thickness, which combines the NFL, GCL and IPL together. Our finding that the IPL had substantially worse diagnostic performance than either NFL or GCL as individual single layers is the most likely explanation for why the GCC had slightly lesser performance than the Boolean combination NFL or GCL. However, this difference was small, not statistically significant and subject to the additional requirement of image segmentation for separating individual layers. More accurate segmentations may alleviate this burden in the future and enable larger databases to determine the potential diagnostic advantages with greater precision.

In our research, we are examining both individuals with diagnosed glaucoma and those who are considered potential glaucoma suspects. It is recognized that only a fraction of the eyes categorized as “glaucoma suspects”—between 30% and 60% are likely to develop POAG over the next 20 years and only about one fourth would be likely to develop detectable visual field damage over that time (given that the vast majority of these eyes had a similar profile to the OHTS inclusion criteria at study entry).41 Therefore a reliable diagnostic metric should demonstrate significantly improved performance in differentiating between “suspect” and “healthy” categories compared to random chance (e.g., an AUC well above 50%). However, this performance should be lower than the accuracy achieved in distinguishing between “glaucoma” and “healthy” eyes. Assessment of this group of eyes categorized as “suspect” provides an additional opportunity to compare the OCT parameters head-to-head for their relative diagnostic efficacy and as a test of the robustness of the findings from the eyes classified as having glaucoma. As seen in Table 3, all metrics were able to distinguish “glaucoma suspect” from “healthy” eyes, with AUC significantly greater than 50%. These AUCs were also in all cases lower than when distinguishing “glaucoma” from “healthy” eyes, and the pattern across OCT parameters was quite similar for the two study groups. These findings strengthen our conclusions, because the “glaucoma suspect” group likely contains a mix of truly glaucomatous and non-glaucomatous eyes, and the results suggest that the same OCT parameters may be helpful in detecting early structural damage and/or for stratifying risk in eyes categorized as being “glaucoma suspects.”

Among the limitations of this study, one important to consider is that only one eye of each participant was included in the analysis; so we did not perform intereye asymmetry analysis, for example, which may otherwise lead to different results. Further, it is likely that some of the GL suspect eyes have higher risk than others of having or developing glaucoma given a glaucoma diagnosis of their contralateral eye. While it is broadly true that such risk varies across any cohort of GL suspects based on a wide array of factors, the focus of this study was on comparison between diagnostic criteria rather than on comparison between GL and GL suspect eyes, or between these cohorts and those forming the basis for other reports. That is, the key results are not the absolute values observed for AUC or sensitivity, but rather how those values compared across criteria. In the same sense, it remains to be determined whether our results will generalize to other cohorts, particularly those comprised of more diverse ethnic backgrounds.

Our research has other limitations. Because of the complex nature of health profiles in senior individuals, we have not adjusted for most risk factors or medication usage. These risk factors could include systemic hypertension, migraine, diet-controlled diabetes, peripheral vasospasm, and family history of glaucoma. Adjusting for medication use, including IOP-lowering and diabetic medications, was also not feasible due to the wide variety of medications and dosages commonly used by the elderly population.

An additional point of consideration about our study is the somewhat arbitrary cutoff used to populate the severity sub-groups into Early vs Moderate-Advanced GL. While this resulted in similar sample sizes for these two sub-groups, and confirmed the overall pattern of results found for the entire group, suggesting robustness, it is possible that other cutoffs may have revealed slightly different results. We defined early glaucoma in eyes with VF MD > −4.0 dB which represents less functional loss than is typically used to define a cutoff for “early” glaucoma (MD > −6.0 dB).42–44 Our aim of this subanalysis was to find the best performing parameters in an early stage of glaucoma; therefore we used a more stringent definition than −6 dB cutoff. Nevertheless, to be able to compare to other studies, we repeated the same severity sub-group analysis using the MD cutoff of −6 dB (instead of −4 dB) and found the same pattern of results entirely, suggesting they are robust.

One other potential limitation is that we did not separate the retinal vasculature (neither larger vessels nor microvasculature) in the thickness analyses. It is possible that such an approach would yield enhanced diagnostic performance, for example, in cases where layer segmentation boundaries (and thus thickness measurements) are strongly influenced by the presence of large vessels or given that layer boundaries based on OCT reflectance are strongly influenced by the retinal capillary plexuses. In particular context to our study, for example, the intermediate capillary plexus located at the posterior IPL boundary may exert a strong influence on boundary contrast, segmentation and thickness. We used the instrument's posterior pole horizonal scan pattern for this study, which is a structural (reflectance) OCT scan only and thus does not provide OCT angiography results that would otherwise facilitate separate analysis in which vascular voxels are excluded. Future studies may help determine if such an approach would be diagnostically beneficial. Moreover, future studies may also determine the benefit of combining metrics from the posterior pole scan area with measurements of the peripapillary RNFL thickness, ONH rim, and lamina cribrosa, particularly using a topographically correspondent approach.45–47

One difference in the approach we applied in this study from clinical practice is that we adjusted each thickness value at each pixel according to the linear functions relating thickness to age and to FoBMO distance in HC eyes, then determined diagnostic performance for each layer at each pixel using the adjusted values in all eyes. The adjustments for age were small for individual inner retinal layers (see Methods), but this differs from common clinical practice for which unadjusted values are compared against the commercial instrument's internal normative range, itself either age-specific or age-adjusted. Although this may affect comparisons to other studies, the main point of our study was to compare diagnostic performance of layers and approaches to each other; thus it was important to apply the same approach across these conditions. Finally, we did not adjust transverse scaling according to axial length differences in this study. However, we excluded eyes with significant refractive error, so our results may not generalize to eyes with higher axial myopia. Moreover, we did adjust for the fovea-to-BMO centroid distance, itself a strong surrogate of the effects on lateral magnification of increased axial length.48

In summary, in this study we compared the ability to discriminate GL and GLS eyes from healthy eyes on the basis of pixelwise OCT thickness measurements for three individual inner retinal layers, combined layers (such as GCC and total neural retina), as well as for Boolean combinations of individual layers. We used manually corrected segmentations of OCT scans in large groups of well-characterized HC eyes, GLS eyes and GL eyes. We found much better diagnostic performance for NFL and GCL than for IPL, suggesting that thinning of the NFL and GCL occurs earlier and more frequently than the IPL in glaucomatous neurodegeneration. However, diagnostic performance of NFL and GCL was only modestly improved (not statistically significant) over the commonly used combined GCC layer, suggesting that inclusion of the IPL in this parameter does not dilute its efficacy much. Our results demonstrate the importance of using continuous maps instead of the current common predetermined spatial patterns of sectors or grids. This is due to the fact that the most diagnostically useful pixels across the posterior pole do not necessarily conform to any of these patterns, depending on which layer is being assessed. Thus continuous maps of abnormality or cumulative Z-scores of individual layer thicknesses (NFL or GCL) or the combined layers (GCC parameter) may be best for clinical practice.
