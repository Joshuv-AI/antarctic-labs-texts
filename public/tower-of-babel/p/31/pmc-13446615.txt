Improving nutritional assessment in pediatric anorexia nervosa: Clinical value of key serum biomarkers
Authors: Ante Vidović, Nora Nikolac Gabaj, Barbara Perše, Marina Galešić, Magdalena Kršić, Sara Mašković, Matej Mustapić, Rea Takač, Orjena Žaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesReliable biochemical markers of starvation are needed to improve detection and monitoring of starvation in pediatric anorexia nervosa (AN), thus we explored new and revisited old potential serum biochemical markers.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study we included 65 pediatric patients with AN. Clinical data and serum at diagnosis were collected. Measured analytes included calcium, inorganic phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, transferrin, ceruloplasmin, prealbumin, C3 and C4 complement, ferritin, and vitamin D. Groups were compared by phenotype (classical AN vs. atypical anorexia nervosa [AAN]) and by severity of starvation (body mass index z‐score: severe Z ≤ −2.0 vs. mild–moderate Z > −2.0) using the Mann–Whitney test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis assessed discriminatory performance. Moreover, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated.ResultsMedian age was 15 years, 63 participants were female. Median symptom duration was 6 months and median weight loss 19.8% (z‐score −2.03 ± 1.40). Compared with classical AN, AAN patients had higher transferrin and C3 (p = 0.019 and p = 0.009). Severe malnutrition (Z ≤ −2.0) was associated with higher ferritin (p = 0.013) and lower transferrin, ceruloplasmin, and C3 (p = 0.004, p = 0.004, and p < 0.001). ROC area under the curves were: C3 0.75 (95% confidence interval 0.63–0.87), transferrin 0.71, ceruloplasmin 0.70, and ferritin 0.68. Specificity was high (71%–97%) and negative predictive values excellent (98%–99%).ConclusionTransferrin, ceruloplasmin, complement C3 and ferritin reflect complementary aspects of starvation in pediatric AN. Complement C3 showed the best discriminatory performance for severe malnutrition. Prealbumin did not correlate with phenotype or severity. These markers could help in early identification and monitoring of severe malnutrition.

INTRODUCTION

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a complex eating disorder characterized primarily by restricted oral intake leading to significant weight loss. 1 AN is the third most common chronic illness among adolescent girls, with prevalence in teenage girls ranging from 1% to 4% in Europe. 2 , 3

According to the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM‐5), AN is classified into several subtypes. In this study, we focused exclusively on the classical (C‐AN, often called just AN) and atypical (AAN) forms. While AAN shares many characteristics with C‐AN, such as restricted oral intake, body image disturbance and fear of gaining weight, the key difference is that patients with AAN are not underweight at the time of diagnosis. 4 , 5 AN induces a pattern of starvation that is physiologically distinct from classic childhood malnutrition. The energy deficit is self‐imposed and major neuroendocrine reprogramming is triggered: hypothalamic amenorrhea (due to low gonadotropins and sex hormones), growth‐hormone resistance, hypercortisolemia, and a sick‐euthyroid pattern. AN patients have significantly suppressed basal metabolic rate. Energy is preferentially derived from fat oxidation and ketosis, whereas protein catabolism is “proportionate” across organs and muscle. 6 , 7 On the other hand, marasmus, starvation due to general calorie deficiency, and kwashiorkor, a primarily protein deficiency, produce different patterns. Marasmus causes severe fat and muscle wasting with preservation of plasma proteins. Oncotic pressure remains normal and usually no edema develops. 8 Kwashiorkor′s hallmark is profound visceral protein loss, which leads to hypoalbuminemia, fatty liver, and pitting edema. 8 Moreover, AN appears paradoxically pro‐inflammatory and AN patients have cell‐mediated immunity relatively preserved and generally do not suffer the typical severe infections of malnourished children. 9

Malnutrition is considered the hallmark of AN. Early recognition and timely intervention are critical to prevent irreversible organ and tissue damage. 4 Clinicians face distinctive challenges in treating AN due to often inaccurate patient histories influenced by distorted self‐image and negativity, as well as frustration from patients' apparent lackof desire to recover amidst busy inpatient services. 10

To predict and prevent further progression of the disease, as well as to assess the current status of patients accurately, clinicians require more reliable biomarkers of malnutrition and starvation. The biomarkers currently available in clinical practice lack sensitivity and specificity. 11 Various parameters, such as leukocytes, albumin levels, electrolytes, prealbumin, transferrin and complement components, have been used, but none of them had appropriate diagnostic value or the data is limited and contradictory. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15

To date, children diagnosed with AN lack a useful biomarker of starvation. The aim of this study is to determine the association between serum biomarker concentration and clinical indicators in pediatric patients with AN, to assess their potential role as a biomarker of starvation and disease severity.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of University Hospital Center Sestre Milosrdnice. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians of all participants.

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study included 65 pediatric patients diagnosed with AN, according to the DSM‐5 classification, at an inpatient tertiary care center, consecutively enrolled from September 2022 to October 2024.

Inclusion criteria were: age < 18 years, new diagnosis of AN, and availability of baseline blood sample and clinical data. Exclusion criteria were: chronic systemic disease causing secondary malnutrition (e.g., cystic fibrosis, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, chronic liver or kidney disease, or malignancy), acute infection or systemic inflammation at sampling, recent blood transfusion or recent iron therapy, medication use (including use of systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies) within the prior 4 weeks or pregnancy.

Serum samples and clinical parameters were collected at the time of diagnosis. Clinical parameters included age, gender, symptom duration, anorexia phenotype, percentage and rate of weight loss, and body mass index (BMI) z‐score. Patients' BMI z‐scores were calculated using Ped(z) calculator 16 and categorized based on World Health Organization growth reference data. 17 For measurement of biochemical parameters blood was sampled in tubes with clot activator without anticoagulant (Vacuette, Greiner, Bio‐One GmBH, Kremsmünster, Austria). Serum concentrations of calcium, inorganic phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, transferrin, ceruloplasmin, prealbumin, complement components C3 and C4, ferritin, and vitamin D were measured on Alinity ci analyzers (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA) using the original manufacturers' reagents and applications.

The primary outcome was the association between baseline serum biomarkers and severity of malnutrition (BMI z‐score categories). Secondary outcomes included biomarker differences by anorexia phenotype (AN vs. AAN), diagnostic accuracy (receiver operating characteristic/area under the curve [ROC/AUC]) for distinguishing severe versus mild–moderate malnutrition, and correlations with continuous nutritional measures.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were tested for normality and presented as median and interquartile range or arithmetic mean and standard deviation. Qualitative data are presented as counts and ratios. Differences in biochemical parameters according to anorexia phenotype and anorexia severity (BMI z‐score: severe, Z ≤ −2.0 vs. mild–moderate, Z > −2.0) were investigated using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney test. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of individual biomarkers. The AUC with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated. An AUC of 0.5 represents no diagnostic discrimination, whereas AUC values ≥ 0.7 were considered to indicate clinically relevant diagnostic performance.

Sample size considerations for ROC analysis were based on detecting an AUC significantly different from the null hypothesis value of 0.5 with a significance level of α = 0.05 and statistical power of 80%. A sample of approximately 60 subjects is sufficient to detect an AUC of approximately 0.70 or higher. Additionally, measures of diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value [PPV] and negative predictive value [NPV]) with corresponding 95% CIs were calculated. p‐values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was done in MedCalc® Statistical Software version 20.015 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).

DISCUSSION

Malnutrition in pediatric AN can be clinically subtle yet metabolically profound, and sensitive laboratory markers that reflect the degree of starvation would help clinicians detect and monitor at‐risk children and adolescents earlier. A recent World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry consensus statement on candidate biomarkers for AN emphasized that multiple biological markers have pathophysiological relevance, but most of the available evidence still comes from group comparisons and robust data on sensitivity, specificity, and prognostic value are lacking. In this context, ferritin has received some attention as a laboratory risk marker, whereas transferrin, ceruloplasmin, and complement C3 remain underexplored in AN. 18 To address this, we evaluated a panel of serum biomarkers in a well‐characterized pediatric cohort and compared their performance across anorexia phenotypes and by severity of malnutrition. Several biomarkers evaluated in this study, including ferritin, ceruloplasmin and complement C3, may be influenced by inflammatory processes. To minimize this potential confounding, inflammatory markers were assessed in all participants. Both CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate were within the normal range in all patients, indicating the absence of clinically relevant systemic inflammation. Therefore, the observed biomarker alterations in our cohort are more likely to reflect metabolic adaptations to starvation rather than an acute‐phase inflammatory response.

Transferrin is a liver‐derived glycoprotein that binds two ferric ions and shuttles iron to cells. It has an intermediate serum half‐life, around 8 days, making it more responsive than albumin, but slower than prealbumin. It is relatively unaffected by hydration or renal function, but is limited by iron homeostasis. 11 , 19 Transferrin synthesis requires adequate protein supply, its concentration falls in chronic starvation. Moreover, it is also a negative acute‐phase reactant, so inflammation further suppresses its concentration. Low transferrin has long been suggested as a marker of malnutrition in both children and adults, due to drops of its production in protein‐calorie deficiency, whereas general iron‐deficiency tends to raise transferrin concentration to scavenge more iron. 19 , 20 , 21 We observed markedly reduced transferrin concentration in severe AN, which is consistent with profound protein depletion. Therefore, transferrin reflects long‐term protein status, and since its half‐life is several days, it is not ideal for day to day monitoring, but low values reliably signal chronic protein‐calorie deficit. In our cohort, transferrin showed good specificity for severe AN (98%–99% NPV) and was significantly lower in those with worse malnutrition, further supporting its utility as a nutritional biomarker.

Ceruloplasmin is a copper‐binding ferroxidase that carries > 95% of serum copper. It oxidizes Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺, facilitating iron loading onto transferrin. Hepatocytes secrete ceruloplasmin into plasma and copper loading of the apo‐protein produces the holo‐ceruloplasmin with a long half‐life (several days), whereas apo‐ceruloplasmin (without copper) is unstable and is rapidly degraded with half‐life around 5–6 h. Ceruloplasmin is a positive acute‐phase reactant; therefore, it rises with inflammation or estrogen (e.g., in pregnancy or use of estrogen‐containing oral contraceptives). 22 Starvation leads to decrease in serum ceruloplasmin concentration because of protein and copper deficiency. An old study found ceruloplasmin slightly, though not significantly reduced in AN. 23 In our cohort, most malnourished patients had significantly lower ceruloplasmin, suggesting that chronic malnutrition can overcome its positive‐reactant drive. We demonstrated a clear correlation with the degree of starvation. This decrease is likely due to limited copper intake and impaired hepatic synthesis. Clinically, ceruloplasmin is less sensitive than prealbumin (due to its longer half‐life) but like transferrin, it can signal severe protein‐calorie malnutrition, and it is confounded by factors that affect copper metabolism. 22 , 24 All things considered, low ceruloplasmin in AN likely marks severe starvation and may contribute to disrupted iron metabolism when paired with low transferrin.

Complement C3 is the central component of the complement cascade that opsonizes pathogens and drives inflammation. It is predominantly synthesized by the liver with a minor contribution from adipose tissue. C3 is also an acute‐phase protein, but in AN its behavior appears dominated by nutritional effects. Studies have shown that malnutrition causes global immunologic deficits, including reduced complement levels. 9 , 23 , 25 Our data shows that most severely malnourished AN group had significantly lower C3 than in milder cases. Moreover, C3 had the highest specificity and AUC among the markers we tested. The literature supports these findings: Ozten et al. reported that in starving underweight patients C3 levels are significantly suppressed. They also observed that as patients gained weight, C3 rose in parallel. 23 Wyatt et al. found that starved AN patients have low serum C3, along with other alternative‐pathway factors. 25 It is important to note that C3 rebounds quickly with refeeding. In the Sakarya AN trial, C3 levels increased significantly over 6 weeks of nutritional rehabilitation, tracking BMI improvement. 23 Our results reinforce the idea that complement C3 could serve as a practical biomarker in a non‐inflammatory setting, it is suppressed in protein‐calorie deficit and normalizes with refeeding, allowing clinicians to monitor the metabolic impact of AN early in treatment.

Ferritin is the universal intracellular iron‐storage protein that sequesters iron as nontoxic ferric ions. It is a standard marker of total body iron. Ferritin expression is regulated by cellular iron and also by cytokines. It rises in infection, inflammation, liver injury, or malignancy. 26 , 27 In malnutrition and eating disorder, ferritin often behaves paradoxically. Rather than falling with nutritional deprivation, serum ferritin is commonly elevated in AN and severe malnutrition, and falls as patients gain weight. These elevations occurred with normal serum iron and without raised C‐reactive protein or other inflammatory markers, suggesting no true iron overload. Furthermore, higher ferritin in AN correlated with elevated hepcidin, an iron‐regulatory hormone, and decreased markedly after nutritional rehabilitation. 28 , 29 Pediatric data parallel these findings. A cohort of undernourished children and adolescents with restrictive eating disorders had serum ferritin inversely associated with BMI Z‐score. 30 This inappropriately high ferritin for their nutritional state might reflect hepatic stress and altered iron metabolism during starvation. Our study followed aforementioned data and showed significantly higher concentration of ferritin in severe AN. Even though positive predictive value was low, NPV was excellent making normal ferritin concentrations reliable for excluding more severe type of disease. These features make ferritin a useful, but complex parameter in assessing nutritional status, especially when interpreted alongside other markers.

There are several reasons why prealbumin, also known as transthyretin, could have been successfully implemented, along with other parameters, as a biomarker for compromised nutritional status in clinical settings. Its short half‐life of 48 h and its independence from hydration status and renal and liver function hypothesize prealbumin as a superior marker compared to albumin, which has a half‐life of 20 days. Prealbumin is a carrier protein for thyroxine and retinol in serum and cerebrospinal fluid. The name “prealbumin” does not reflect a functional similarity to albumin, but rather the fact that it migrates faster than albumin in electrophoresis. Biochemically, the two proteins are unrelated. 11 Sex‐related differences in prealbumin concentration persist into adulthood, simplifying the interpretation of laboratory results between female and male patients. 31 Additionally, prealbumin levels reportedly do not differ among ethnic groups, nor are they affected by genetic deficiencies, as is the case with albumin. 32 However, it is important to note that prealbumin is influenced by inflammation, as it functions as a negative acute‐phase reactant, while factors such as prednisone use and alcohol consumption can increase its concentration. 33 A study on malnourished children showed elevated prealbumin concentration shortly after protein supplementation was initiated, which might indicate that reduction of circulating prealbumin is due to protein‐calorie deficiency, rather than total calorie deficit. 34 Although prior studies offer conflicting views on the role of prealbumin in AN, we found no statistically significant association between prealbumin concentration and anorexia phenotype or malnutrition severity in our cohort. This finding suggests that prealbumin has limited utility as a standalone biomarker of starvation in pediatric AN, which is predominantly marasmic.

CONCLUSION

All things considered, transferrin, ceruloplasmin, complement C3 and ferritin reflect complementary aspects of hepatic synthetic function and iron/copper handling and together respond predictably to prolonged starvation. In our cohort, severe AN was associated with lower transferrin, lower ceruloplasmin and lower C3, while ferritin was paradoxically higher in the most severely malnourished patients. Complement C3 demonstrated the best discriminatory performance for severe malnutrition among the markers we studied. These patterns are consistent with altered iron metabolism and hepatic stress in starvation and support the value of an iron/copper‐focused panel for detecting clinically important malnutrition. Prealbumin deserves explicit comment because its utility is heavily debated in the literature: some studies argue it is a sensitive, short‐lived indicator of protein–calorie status, while others show that its levels are confounded by inflammation, steroids, and other factors. In our study prealbumin did not differ between AN phenotype or severity of malnutrition, a finding that argues against relying on prealbumin as a standalone biomarker of starvation in pediatric AN. Taken together with existing contradictory evidence, our results suggest that prealbumin, if used at all, should be interpreted cautiously, ideally alongside other biochemical and clinical data, and preferably tracked longitudinally rather than relying on single measurements. Several routine biochemical measures in our panel did not differ by phenotype or severity in this sample: calcium, inorganic phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, C4 complement and vitamin D. Overall, our findings indicate that a targeted panel emphasizing hepatic iron/copper‐related proteins and complement (transferrin, ceruloplasmin, C3, and ferritin), may be more informative for identifying and monitoring severe malnutrition in growing patients than routine single analytes alone. Our findings emphasize the value of sensitive biomarkers in assessing disease and malutrition severity, particularly when clinical history is uninformative, a common challenge when caring for patients with AN.

Prospective, larger and longitudinal pediatric studies are needed to validate cutoff values, quantify the impact of inflammatory and renal confounders, and determine whether serial measurement of these markers improves early detection and management of severe AN.
