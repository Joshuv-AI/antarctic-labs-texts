New RCTs Advance Incremental Dialysis With Clinically-Guided HD and HDF-Based Prescriptions
Authors: Mariana Murea, Andrea K Viecelli
Published: 2025

See Clinical Research on Pages 3796 and 4188

Across nations and dialysis programs, thrice-weekly is the mainstream and default treatment initiation frequency with extracorporeal dialysis, whether hemodialysis (HD) or hemodiafiltration (HDF), irrespective of residual kidney function (RKF), comorbidities, or patient preference. This conventional frequency is rooted in tradition and logistical convenience rather than scientific evidence. By contrast, incremental dialysis individualizes treatment by incorporating RKF into the prescription, typically starting with lower frequency (once- or twice-weekly sessions) and escalating to thrice-weekly as clinical status or RKF decline dictates. Up to one-quarter of patients initiating therapy may be suitable for < 3 sessions weekly, depending on RKF, metabolic needs, and comorbidity profile.1

The standard method to quantify RKF is timed urine collection. When performed accurately, 24-, 48-, and 72-hour collections provide comparable results; longer collections may improve precision at very low urine volumes but increase patient burden and risk of error.2 A pragmatic approach is a ∼ 24-hour collection before routine laboratory testing, with RKF expressed as urea clearance (KrU), creatinine clearance, or their average, adjusted or not for body surface area. Eligibility thresholds for incremental dialysis generally include KrU in the range 2.0 to 3.5 ml/min (corresponding to urine volume of ≥ 500 ml/d), though patient-specific factors such as age, diet, and comorbidity modify these thresholds.1

The rationale for incremental dialysis is physiologically sound. Even modest RKF supports solute and fluid clearance, phosphorus regulation, and middle-molecule removal, while preserving endocrine function and dampening inflammation.3,4 Patients who have RKF on dialysis have lower mortality, fewer cardiovascular events, and better quality of life.5,6 Observational studies, pilot randomized trials, and meta-analyses suggest that initiating with reduced frequency is feasible, safe, and may slow RKF decline without increasing hospitalization or mortality.7

Despite these data and its sound rationale, incremental dialysis has remained a niche practice, with broader adoption hindered, in part, by the absence of phase 3 randomized controlled trial (RCT) data. Against this backdrop, 2 new studies—1 pilot trial8 and 1 phase 3 RCT9—add critical evidence that initiation of dialysis according to clinical manifestations and RKF is both feasible and safe.

The D-LITE Pilot Trial

The D-LITE trial, conducted in Canada by White et al.,8 is the first randomized study to specifically evaluate twice-weekly HD and focus on elderly incident patients. The trial enrolled 40 patients aged ≥ 70 years who completed a 5-week run-in on thrice-weekly HD, after which they were randomized either to continue thrice-weekly treatment or to reduce to twice-weekly schedule for 9 months. Randomization did not require a minimum RKF; eligibility instead depended on stable clinical status at the end of the run-in (ultrafiltration rate < 8.7 ml/kg/h and postdialysis weight within 1.0 kg of prescribed dry weight). This reflected a pragmatic, symptom-driven approach. In the twice-weekly arm, escalation to thrice-weekly HD was enacted for complications such as hyperkalemia or volume overload, not for routine RKF decline or urea-based adequacy.

Adherence exceeded 99%, and 78% of participants completed the 9-month follow-up. Hospitalizations were similar between groups (8 in each arm), though hospitalization days per patient-day were lower with twice-weekly HD (0.006 vs. 0.027). Urgent unscheduled dialysis occurred once per group (hyperkalemia in the twice-weekly arm; volume overload in the thrice-weekly arm). Mortality was comparable, with 1 death in each arm. RKF was assessed at baseline and at study completion using the mean of KrU and creatinine clearance from 48-hour urine collections in all participants, except for those in the twice-weekly group who at study end provided a 72-hour collection instead of a 48-hour urine collection. RKF was preserved in both groups; average clearance increased slightly in the twice-weekly group (6.7 to 7.2 ml/min) but decreased in the thrice-weekly group (7.0 to 6.2 ml/min). Urine volume increased in the twice-weekly group (1010 to 1120 ml) and remained essentially unchanged in the thrice-weekly group (1080 to 1050 ml). None of these changes reached statistical significance, though trends favored the twice-weekly group. Patient-reported outcomes trended in favor of incremental group, with more participants reporting reduced symptom burden, but the study was underpowered for definitive quality-of-life comparisons.

The D-LITE pilot trial offers several notable strengths, including its exclusive focus on older adults—a rapidly growing segment of patients on incident dialysis who are often underrepresented in RCT—and its clinically driven approach to HD prescription. Its salient feature is the departure from urea-based dosing: HD dose and frequency were not adjusted based on spKt/V or stdKt/V, and no target Kt/V was specified for either study arm. Instead, escalation from twice- to thrice-weekly HD was guided by clinical events such as hyperkalemia or volume overload, rather than RKF or KrU thresholds. The protocol explicitly discouraged reflex dose escalation based solely on RKF or Kt/V, and the investigators are commended for prioritizing symptom-guided care over urea metrics.

As a pilot, the study was constrained by its single-center design, small sample size, and short follow-up. Its predominantly White population limits generalizability. Methodologic limitations also warrant attention; patients who escalated to thrice-weekly HD were censored at the time of transition, so subsequent outcomes were excluded from the main analyses. This approach may underestimate the risks of twice-weekly initiation; for a future phase 3 RCT, investigators should retain participants after escalation to more frequent dialysis to avoid survivorship bias. Finally, comparability of RKF assessment would be improved by using the same urine collection duration across all groups and time points; the longer (72-hour) collection at study end in the twice-weekly group may have contributed to an apparent advantage in RKF preservation independent of the intervention.

The Incremental HDF Phase 3 RCT

The multicenter RCT by Fernández et al.9 in Spain is the first to test incremental initiation with online HDF. The trial enrolled 150 patients with stage 5 chronic kidney disease with residual KrU ≥ 2.5 ml/min, and randomized them to incremental HDF (i-HDF) or conventional thrice-weekly HDF (c-HDF). Patients in the i-HDF group began with twice-weekly sessions, escalating to thrice-weekly if KrU fell below threshold or if clinical need arose. RKF was assessed as the mean of KrU and creatinine clearance, adjusted for body surface area, from 24-hour urine collections at baseline and every 2 months.

After 12 months, outcomes were remarkably similar between groups. The rate of RKF decline did not differ significantly (between-group slope difference −0.09 ml/min per 1.73 m2/mo, P = 0.23). Anuria developed in 5 patients in the i-HDF group (6.5%) and 4 patients in the c-HDF group (5.5%, P = 0.40). Hospitalizations were comparable—31 admissions (40%) with i-HDF and 29 (39.7%) with c-HDF—primarily from cardiovascular and infectious causes. Mortality was modestly lower with i-HDF (5.2% vs. 9.6%), but not statistically significant (absolute risk difference −4.4%, 95% confidence interval: −13% to 4%). No serious hyperkalemia events occurred, and overall rates of hyperkalemia or fluid overload were similar at 6 and 12 months. Patients in the i-HDF group received an average of 69 sessions/yr compared with 123 in the c-HDF group, representing a 44% reduction. Fifteen patients on i-HDF (19.5%) transitioned to thrice-weekly therapy after a mean of 193 days, and all accepted escalation. Quality-of-life scores (KDQOL-36) improved modestly in the i-HDF group at 6 months, particularly in the “effects of chronic kidney disease” subscale (mean difference +10.6 points, 95% confidence interval: 1.1–20.2), though this difference diminished by 12 months.

The i-HDF trial used a fixed target model for dialysis adequacy based on the Casino and López nomogram10 and protocolized transition criteria from twice- to thrice-weekly HDF based on KrU < 2.5 ml/min on 2 measurements obtained within 2 weeks, onset of uncontrolled hypertension, heart failure, or other clinical events; while attaining standardized adequacy targets: spKt/V >1.6 for i-HDF and > 1.2 for c-HDF. Consequently, the weekly dialysis dose in terms of ‘urea adequacy’ was similar between groups, which may have minimized differences in clinical outcomes attributable solely to treatment frequency. This design is not a weakness: for patients, the main benefit of incremental regimens is the reduction in treatment days, potentially improving quality of life, and reducing treatment burden, even if individual sessions are longer.

Although protocolized adequacy targets enhance reproducibility in the RCT setting, it has been increasingly recognized that a urea-based approach does not fully capture the complexity of dialysis adequacy.11 Furthermore, the concept of fixed target model for total urea adequacy per week is mathematically equivalent to stdKt/V, which was derived from data of RCTs conducted in prevalent patients on thrice-weekly HD without appreciable RKF.12 Unlike the variable target model, the equivalence of dialytic and kidney KrU assumed in the fixed target model does not fully account for the greater clinical impact of RKF compared with dialysis. Moreover, whether same “target” total urea adequacy per week is necessary in patients with RKF treated with HDF is not known.

Complementary Lessons

The D-LITE pilot trial by White et al.8 and the i-HDF phase 3 RCT by Fernández et al.9 bring new and complementary knowledge. The D-LITE trial operationalized incremental high-flux HD through session frequency and symptom-guided prescription, without relying on RKF thresholds or urea-based targets—making it a pragmatic model especially suited for elderly or frail individuals.8 The i-HDF trial employed an approach based on session frequency, RKF, urea-based adequacy targets, and clinical status, demonstrating that incremental dialysis can be safely applied as a structured program and to HDF.9

Despite different designs and populations, both trials affirm that thrice-weekly initiation in extracorporeal dialysis need not be universal and that this principle transcends modality—applying not only to high-flux HD but also to HDF. Advances in dialyzer technology, including medium cut-off membranes, which enhance middle-molecule and inflammatory mediator depuration beyond that of high-flux membranes should further strengthen the clinical rationale for individualized dialysis prescriptions. With each new piece of evidence from RCTs, incremental dialysis is poised to evolve from a niche approach to a mainstream, evidence-based practice. Because it aligns with patient-minded care.
