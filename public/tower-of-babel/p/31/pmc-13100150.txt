Spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of chronic respiratory disease in charcoal-producing areas, northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Mulugeta Tesfa Gebeyehu, Achenef Motbainor Begosew, Mintesnot Teni, Muluken Azage Yenesew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of chronic respiratory disease (CRD) is significant for the early prevention of the disease and efficient allocation of scarce healthcare resources. However, this is not well known in the Ethiopian context, particularly in charcoal-producing areas. Thus, this study aimed to assess the spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of CRD in northwest Ethiopia from 2012/13 to 2018/19. This study was conducted in three zones (Awi, East Gojjam, and West Gojjam), Amhara Region, Ethiopia. These zones are known for charcoal production. Data on CRD among those aged 15 years and above were obtained from 44 districts for seven years from the three zonal health departments’ annual records. Scan statistics with a discrete Poisson model was used to identify statistically significant clusters using SaTScan version 10.1. Global Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi statistics were employed to assess the spatial heterogeneity and hot spot areas of CRD respectively. To display maps of cluster locations, ArcGIS version 10.8 was used. The annual morbidity rate of CRD was remarkably varied from 305 to 731per 100,000 population in 2012/13 and 2018/19 respectively. The highest annual morbidity rate, 6508/100,000 population, was observed in the Awi zone. The global spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.2772, Z-score = 3.6597, and P-value < 0.001) shows a strong clustered distribution. The most likely purely spatial and spatiotemporal clusters were detected in eleven districts, and eight of these were in the Awi zone. The cluster center/radius was 10.967700 N, 36.925500 E/ 48.37 km, and RR = 4.37, p-value < 0.001. People living in the highest risk areas had a 4.37 times greater chance of developing CRD than those in the lowest risk areas. The Getis-Ord Gi hot spot analysis detected that hot spot clusters were primarily located in the northwest and southwest parts of the study area. The spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of CRD were non-random and clustered in high charcoal production areas. The identified high-risk clusters are the priority areas for target intervention. Further research is recommended to identify risk factors for higher clusters of CRD morbidity in these areas.Keywords: CRD, Hot spots, Spatiotemporal, Charcoal production, SaTScan, Amhara, EthiopiaSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Health occupations

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRD) are chronic diseases of the airways and the other structures of the lungs. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma are the most prevalent CRDs1,2. COPD is characterized by airflow obstruction that can progress to pulmonary heart disease and respiratory failure3,4. Asthma occurs due to inflammation and narrowing of the small airways in the lungs and may cause a cough, recurrent wheezing, shortness of breath, and /or chest tightness as presenting symptoms5,6.

CRD is among the most common NCDs7 and continues to be a considerable public health problem in the world8,9. In 2019 alone, 7.4% of the world’s population was threatened with CRD in 201710. Africa has a huge burden of CRD11; nonetheless, as compared to other NCDs, it is underestimated and neglected12. One-third of Ethiopians have NCDs, and CRDs are among the most common diseases with increasing public health challenges13–16. Addressing chronic diseases is integral to the 2030 Agenda for sustainable development17. Ethiopia is working by following the World Health Organization’s (WHO) global action plan to prevent and control NCDs by the year 202518. Environmental exposure to tobacco smoke, indoor air pollution, and occupational clouds of dust, smoke, and chemicals are important risk factors for COPD19–22. Other studies have been investigating the impact of frequent long hours of exposure to smoke released from the incomplete combustion of wood leads to lung function impairment23. A prior study reported that major clusters of high COPD hospitalization were observed in an area near high emissions of pollutants24.

Previous epidemiological studies of CRD morbidity in Ethiopia and similar settings have mainly examined the prevalence of the disease25–27. Investigation of possible disease clustering is fundamental to epidemiology, to detect the high-risk population28, and examining diseases over time provides essential insight into overall disease burden29,30. However, spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of CRD have not been well explored in charcoal production areas, constraining the ability of policymakers to implement targeted and efficient interventions. Thus, this study was performed to assess the spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of CRD among adults in the charcoal-producing areas of northwest Ethiopia from 2012/13 to 2018/2019.

Methods

Study design and settings

This spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal study was conducted in three zones (Awi, East Gojjam, and West Gojjam) of the Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The region has a total of 13 zones and is located between 9° 20’ and 14°20’ North latitude and 36° 20’ and 40° 20’ East longitude. The three study zones have a total of 47 districts and lower administrative areas under the zones. The three zones were selected because they are known for high charcoal production. A cancer study conducted in the Amhara region revealed that more than 10 million quintals of charcoal are sent from the study area to the big cities in Ethiopia31. Moreover, about 164,648.2 tons of charcoal was produced between 2014 and 2018 by households from one of the 47 districts in the study area32.

Data sources and extraction procedures

We acknowledge that the present study is based on routinely collected surveillance data from the three zones of the Amhara region through the National Health Management Information System (HMIS) reported between 01 July 2012 and 30 June 2019 that have been used previously in a separate publication focusing on the trend, seasonality, and forecasting analyses34. In contrast, the current study applies spatial and spatiotemporal analytical methods to examine the geographic distribution and clustering of the disease, addressing distinct research questions. These analyses provide complementary insights by identifying geographic heterogeneity and localized clustering that cannot be inferred from trend, seasonality and forecasting of CRD analysis alone. The current manuscript does not reuse any analyses from that publication but instead conducts an independent spatial, temporal and spatiotemporal investigation to assess geographic distribution and clustering. Chronic respiratory disease cases of the three districts established since 2016/17 were included in their mother district (make ready this sentence for deleting). We considered bronchial asthma and COPD cases in our analysis since these are the most prevalent types of CRDs27,35,36.

There is an integrated health information system from the bottom up through the HMIS. Health-related data based on the recommended WHO guideline ICD-1037 are compiled at the district level and reported quarterly to the Zone Health Department offices, then the Regional Health Bureau, and finally to the Federal Ministry of Health38. The annual morbidity report covers the period from July 2012/13 to 30 June 2018/19. Data were collected by health management information system officers from October to December 2022.

Data quality control

A checklist was prepared based on the HMIS registry format to extract the data from the zonal health department office records. We tried to reduce misclassification by including ICD-10 codes and carefully checking the data for obvious mistakes. Despite these efforts, some underreporting or misclassification may still exist, and we acknowledge this as a limitation. The data collectors were informed about the project’s objectives and trained in the data collection procedure. Every day, after extraction, the data were checked for completeness and consistency by the principal investigators of the study. In addition, cross-checking activities with the quarterly and annual reports of the districts were carried out. Data were carefully entered into Microsoft Excel for further cleaning and analysis. The number of CRD cases together with their district was carefully geocoded based on address information from registers and using X and Y-coordinates. All district attributes were properly recorded and cross-checked before analysis.

Data analysis

Ethiopian administrative regions’ shapefile, district-level polygon map, and geographic coordinates were obtained from the publicly accessible DIVA-GIS database (https://diva-gis.org/data.html), and spatial data layers were created in ArcGIS 10.8. Spatial, temporal, and spatio-temporal analysis was employed using the Poisson model. The data represent all CRD cases registered from 44 districts of the three zones between 01 July 2012 and 30 June 2019 and archived in the Zonal Health Department offices. We included bronchial asthma and COPD cases in our analysis since these are the most prevalent types of CRDs1,2. An attribute table for districts was prepared by including location ID, name of districts, number of CRD cases, sex, and population of the district, and all the CRD records were geocoded based on their X and Y-coordinates of each district where CRD cases were residing. The SaTScan software V10.1 was used to detect the randomness of CRD distribution over space and time among adults aged 15 years and above, and cluster maps with CRD cases were plotted using ArcGIS V10.8.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation (global Moran’s I) statistic was employed to assess whether the spatial pattern expressed by CRD cases is clustered, dispersed, or random in the study area29. The significance of Moran’s I is identified based on the Monte Carlo method, which simulates the distribution according to random data. Moran’s I value lies between + 1 and -1, and when its value is greater than zero and p-value < 0.05 shows clustering, while, Moran’s I value of zero shows no spatial autocorrelation (the pattern is random) and less than zero shows the pattern is dispersed. The local Moran’ I and Getis-Ord Gi analyses were also done to locate the local cluster after detecting the global Moran’ I. The local Moran’ I describes spatial clustering of observations in high or low rates and outliers, and the Getis-Ord Gi statistic identifies local spatial clustering patterns, either “hot spots” (spatial clusters of high values) or “cold spots” (spatial clusters of low values). The statistical significance for local Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi was determined via hypothesis testing using standardized z-scores calculated for each observation and significant p-values. A spatial weight matrix was used to specify the spatial relationships between neighboring districts. Neighbors were defined using the first-order queen’s polygon contiguity, and districts sharing common borders and vertex were considered neighbors39–41. ArcGIS was used to explore the distribution of CRD cases in terms of their geographic relations42,43.

Spatial clusters

We used Kulldorff’s SaTScan software version 10.1 to employ the spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal scan statistics to depict the purely spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal clusters as described previously44,45 using the attribute table prepared for districts. Kulldorff’s scan statistics also help to calculate the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) and relative risk (RR), and provide a p-value using Monte Carlo Simulations. In this SaTScan statistics, the Poisson model was employed, where the number of cases is compared to the background population data and the expected number of cases in each unit is proportional to the size of the population at risk46. The observed CRD cases were compared with the expected cases inside and outside of each window and the risk ratios were estimated based on the Poisson distribution. The null hypothesis of the spatial scan statistic states that CRD is randomly distributed throughout the districts and that the expected event count is proportional to the population at risk. The purely spatial clusters were selected based on the likelihood ratio and p-value < 0.05. The LLR was calculated to measure the RR of CRD occurrence within the cluster when compared to the risk outside using Monte Carlo simulations. The maximum number of permutations for the Monte Carlo simulation was set to 999 to assess the sensitivity of significant locations to the number of permutations (p < 0.0539. The scanning window was a circle in the purely spatial pattern, and the upper limit for the maximum purely spatial was set to be 50% of the population at risk, which is important to detect small and large clusters. For each circle, the rejection of the null hypothesis is based on a likelihood ratio statistic. The p-value was calculated through Monte Carlo hypothesis testing, by comparing the rank of the maximum likelihood from the real data set with the maximum likelihoods from random data sets. To evaluate the statistical significance of the most likely cluster, the 999 random replications of the data set are generated under the null hypothesis47. For each location and size of the scanning window, the alternative hypothesis is that there is a higher risk within the window as compared to outside.

SaTScan also identifies secondary clusters in the data set in addition to the most likely cluster and orders them according to their likelihood ratio test statistic. Secondary clusters are computed, based on the degree of overlap allowed in the cluster circles, and include the option of no geographical overlap28,40.

Temporal clusters

A purely temporal cluster analysis scanning was performed to detect the temporal clusters of CRD cases with high rates, representing the whole geographic area, but with a one-year time aggregation length. The temporal scan statistic uses a scanning window, a time interval that moves in one dimension. The upper limit for the maximum was specified to be 50% within the study period for purely temporal cluster analysis. To identify clusters, a likelihood function was maximized across all locations and times. The maximum likelihood indicates the cluster is least likely to have occurred by chance (primary cluster). The p-value is obtained through Monte Carlo hypothesis testing. For purely temporal analyses, only the most likely cluster is reported49.

Space–time clusters

The space–time scan statistic was employed to detect clusters in both space and time. The time of the space–time cluster analysis was set to the time frame from 2013 to 2019. The space–time scan statistic was defined by a cylindrical window with a circular (or elliptic) geographic base and with a height corresponding to time. The base is defined exactly as for the purely spatial scan statistic, while the height reflects the time of potential clusters. The cylindrical window is then moved in space and time so that for each possible geographical location and size, it also visits each possible time interval, where each cylinder reflects a possible cluster46. Thus, this method enables simultaneous detection of the location and time of a cluster occurrence. A likelihood ratio was calculated for each space–time window to indicate to what extent the rate of cases inside the area is higher than expected. Monte Carlo hypothesis testing is then used to indicate the significance level of specific space–time windows. For each space–time window, the likelihood ratio was calculated to determine whether the rate of observed cases inside the area was higher than expected cases. By considering the number of observed and expected cases inside the window, the software identifies and assesses the statistical significance of space–time clusters. The Monte Carlo simulation technique is used to estimate the p-value. The maximum number of permutations for the Monte Carlo simulation was set to 999 to assess the sensitivity of the significant locations to the number of permutations39. This means that SaTScan will generate 999 random permutations of the data to compare with the real data50. The cluster with the maximum LLR was defined as the most likely cluster (a cluster with the highest LLR test means a cluster least likely to have occurred by chance). Likewise, for purely spatial analysis, in space–time analyses, SaTScan also identifies secondary clusters in the data set in addition to the most likely cluster and orders them according to their LLR test statistic. A standard of no geographical overlap and hierarchical cluster type was selected to report secondary clusters (separation of clusters). Statistical significance for the presence of clusters was reported when the p-value was < 0.05. A discrete Poisson-based model was used to analyze annually reported CRD cases following the Poisson distribution48.

Results

Distribution patterns of CRD cases over time

During the seven years (2012/13 to 2018/19), CRD cases were reported from 44 districts in three zones of the Amhara region, and the overall morbidity rate was 4,641 (95%CI: 3,903–5379) cases per 100,000 population at risk. CRD shows no substantial sex differences, with the rate among males slightly higher and accounting for 51%. The CRD morbidity rate was 6,508(95%CI: 2,933–10,082), 4,208(95%CI:1,714–6702), and 3,202 (95%CI:1852–4552) per 100,000 population in Awi, West Gojjam, and East Gojjam zones, respectively.

Purely temporal cluster analysis detected a significantly higher rate of purely temporal CRD clusters. Only one peak period of an identified primary cluster occurred in all districts (with 86,410 observed and 55,822.41 expected CRD cases, LLR = 10,866.30, P < 0.001) from 2018 to 2019.

The distribution patterns of adult CRD morbidity rate for each year and district varied and the highest morbidity rate (7314/100,000 population) was reported in Dangila town in 2018/19, followed by Finote Selam town (4275/100,000 population) in 2018/19, and Mota town (3626/100,000 population) in 2017/18, while the lowest observed in Yilmana Densa district (62/100,000 population at risk) in 2015/16 (Table 1).

Spatial clustering of CRD cases

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatially, the distribution of CRD cases was non-random (global Moran’s I = 0.277148, Z-score = 3.6597, p < 0.001), indicating that the distribution pattern of CRD morbidity was clustered (Fig. 3). Since the global spatial autocorrelation showed clustering, we employed further analysis to identify spatial cluster features with high or low values (cluster and outlier analysis) and to identify hot spot areas using Anselin’s local Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi* methods respectively. The result from Anselin’s local Moran’s I analysis revealed that the high-high cluster was located in the districts of the Awi zone. The low-low clusters and outliers were also detected in most districts of East Gojjam and Awi zones (Fig. 4). The hot spot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi) demonstrated that hot spot areas were located primarily in the northwest and southwest of the study area (Fig. 5).

Purely spatial clusters of adult CRD cases

Ten statistically significant purely spatial clusters, one most likely cluster, and nine secondary clusters were detected in the study period. The most likely cluster which comprises 11 districts was located in two zones (8 in the Awi Zone and 3 in the West Gojjam Zone). The cluster window was centered at 10.967700N, 36.925500 E, and the radius of the window was 48.37 km. There were 92,597 observed and 33,716 expected cases (LLR = 47,274, p-value < 0.001), with a relative risk (RR) of 4.37 in this cluster window (Table 2). Secondary clusters are computed based on the degree of overlap allowed in the cluster circles, and include the option of no geographical overlap criteria for reporting secondary clusters. Among the nine secondary clusters observed, five, one, and three clusters were located in the East Gojjam zone, Awi zone, and West Gojjam zone respectively, during the study period 2012/2013 to 2018/2019. In this spatial distribution, most of the clusters were shown in the northwest and southwest parts of the study area (Fig. 5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to apply GIS and SaTScan analyses to depict the spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal variations of CRDs in the Amhara region. The study revealed that the spatial, temporal, and space–time distribution patterns of CRD morbidity rate in the study districts were non-random. The possible spatial variations might be explained by the difference in exposure to risk factors for CRD. The population within these districts has been exposed to massive charcoal production and indoor biomass fuel use. Investigating risk factors of CRD in the surrounding districts is an area of research. This is in agreement with a spatial analysis that revealed COPD case clustering observed in areas with higher firewood exposure, greater smoking exposure, and in households with closer proximity to local roads51. Congruently, other studies also showed respiratory health outcomes with charcoal production and utilization52,53

NCD deaths accounted for 43% in the Amhara region, and CRDs were among Ethiopia’s leading causes of death in 201954. Our study revealed that the spatial distribution pattern of CRD cases in districts is not random. CRD morbidity rate persistently increased in three zones across the study period. The purely spatial and spatiotemporal model identified that most of the districts included in the most likely cluster were from the Awi zone, and the secondary clusters were located in districts of the East Gojjam zone and the West Gojjam zone. Moreover, hot spot clusters were detected in the northwest and southwest of the study area. This clustering of CRD cases coincides with areas of high charcoal production31,32,55,56 in these areas, in addition to the other contributing factors. This is in agreement with a study that showed morbidity rate attributed to CRD was commonly observed in areas with higher firewood exposure51,57.

Based on the analysis of records from 2012/13 to 2018/19, the annual morbidity rate of CRD showed an increase of 139.7%. Increasing numbers of health facilities and improving the diagnostic capacity which leads to early detection and prevention of progression of CRDs, could be the reason for increasing CRD morbidity rates. In our study area, the number of hospitals increased from five to twenty since 2015/16 onwards due to the initiatives for the establishment of primary hospitals in districts in the country58. This increase is in agreement with the results from a global data 1990–2017 and systematic review1,59. Hence, we acknowledge that diagnostic capacity for chronic respiratory diseases may have improved during the study period due to expanded access to health services, increased training of health workers, and better availability of diagnostic tools across districts. Due to these reasons, some degree of misclassification or temporal variation in diagnostic accuracy may persist, and this is acknowledged as an inherent limitation of routine surveillance data. Despite the increment in the annual morbidity rate of CRD, it might be still underestimated because the average readiness of health facilities for CRD service was 45% in 201660. A finding from the spatial analysis showed inappropriate medication users lived at a distance from their preferred health facilities compared to appropriate users61.

A study on NCDs reported that 60.4% of CRD mortality is attributed to modifiable risk factors including indoor and outdoor air pollution, the existence of exposure to biomass smoke, and road traffic vehicle exposure51,54. In our study population, wood charcoal production which is known to emit high levels of CO2 appears to be the main factor associated with CRD morbidity and mortality32. This is supported by studies revealing a higher proportion of CRD cases reported in areas exposed to biomass smoke35,57

Our study found that CRD morbidity rates vary from 62 to 7314 per 100,000 population among districts. The reason for this variation is not clear and needs further study, but the potential effect of the volume of charcoal production and meteorological variables may be important. The geographic variations in CRD morbidity have been observed in other countries62–64. Nevertheless, the magnitude of these geographical inequalities also highlights the importance of geographically manifesting factors in understanding the CRD burden in the study area. In contrast, even if the number of CRD cases is still increasing, CRD mortality declined among Global Alliance Respiratory Disease member countries from 2000 to 201530,65. This might be due to strong advocacy for the reduction of cigarette smoking interventions, utilization of clean fuel sources, and early detection and treatment. Our study revealed that CRD distribution among males and females was similar. This is in agreement with a study reported in China30. On the contrary, some studies have shown that CRD morbidity is higher in men than women26,66. This might be explained by the fact that the former studies were conducted among factory workers, and males were more involved than females in this occupation. Congruently, the mortality rate of CRD in males was higher than in females.

The possible spatial variations might be explained by the difference in exposure to risk factors for CRD. The population within these districts has been exposed to massive charcoal production and indoor biomass fuel use. Investigating risk factors of CRD in the surrounding districts is an area of research. This is in agreement with a spatial analysis that revealed COPD case clustering observed in areas with higher firewood exposure, greater smoking exposure, and in households with closer proximity to local roads51. Congruently, other studies also showed respiratory health outcomes concerning charcoal production and utilization52,53.

We employed different spatial statistical analyses to identify the true cluster of districts with CRD. Identifying clusters and hot spot areas in CRD has a paramount effect on receiving priority attention from public health planners for the allocation of scarce resources and to devise interventions.

This study, however, is not without limitations. Because we used routinely collected DHIS2 data, the CRD morbidity rate in the study area may be underestimated, as some people in the community may not have visited health facilities. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study, we lacked information on important environmental and lifestyle factors, such as smoking, household air pollution (PM₂.₅ data), or access to healthcare.

In addition, our analysis was based on the presence of asthma and/ or COPD as a component of CRD. These might underestimate the magnitude of CRD cases and affect their spatial–temporal clustering in the zone. Despite these limitations, we believe the findings still provide useful insights into disease patterns in these areas.

Conclusions

The spatial distribution of CRD cases varied across districts rather than occurring at random. CRD morbidity also varied over time. CRD morbidity rates increased consistently in three zones, and the Awi zone reported the highest rates throughout the study period. This indicated that there is a need to have targeted interventions in the identified cluster areas with CRD morbidity. Therefore, increased efforts should be placed in the high-risk areas in the future for the allocation of scarce resources. Moreover, the clustering of CRD cases can occur for a variety of reasons. Since this study used aggregated data, future research should collect individual-level information to better understand personal exposures and other risk factors for CRD.
