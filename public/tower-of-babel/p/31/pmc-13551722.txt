The Detroit Public Schools Health Corps: a student-led model for delivering sideline and preventive sports medicine in underserved communities
Authors: David Abdelnour, Alqasim Elnaggar, Julian D Johnson, Rumyah Rafique, Marley Sternberg, Sara Naessig, Audrey Millar, Faith Scott, Ashley Frei, Jack Mao, Detroit Public Schools Health Corps, Andrew Gupta, Diane Levine, Rahul Vaidya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResource-limited school districts often lack athletic trainers and real-time medical support during sports events, placing student-athletes at risk for improper care. The Detroit Public School Health Corps (DPSHC), a medical student-led initiative, was developed to fill this need through supervised sideline coverage by trained medical students. Background

In 2023, a high school basketball player in Detroit lost his life after he collapsed during a game and suffered a cardiac event despite bystanders' attempts at delivering aid and subsequent Emergency Management Services (EMS) intervention [1]. While onsite EMS may have not saved this young man’s life, this tragedy underscored a critical gap in real-time medical support at athletic events across the Detroit Public Schools Community District (DPSCD), where systemic funding barriers have limited access to athletic trainers and sports medicine services [2–4].

Unlike well-resourced districts that employ full-time athletic trainers, many schools in Detroit lack trained personnel due to financial constraints and administrative hurdles [2, 3]. Schools in higher-income zip codes are up to three times more likely to have athletic trainers than those in lower-income areas [2, 5–7]. These disparities increase the likelihood that concussions, orthopedic injuries, and even life-threatening conditions, which may go unrecognized or improperly managed without appropriate medical support [6, 8, 9].

To address gaps in sideline medical care for underserved student-athletes, the Detroit Public School Health Corps (DPSHC) developed a structured system of trained medical student volunteers known as Health Leaders (HLs). Supported by Wayne Pediatrics, Wayne State University/Detroit Medical Center (WSU/DMC) Department of Orthopedics, and the Wayne State University School of Medicine Alumni Foundation, HLs undergo extensive training in emergency triage, musculoskeletal injury management, concussion protocols, and structured communication methods such as Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation (SBAR) [10–13]. The program was implemented across Detroit public high schools during the 2024–2025 academic year to provide structured sideline support and formalized injury documentation. In addition to addressing care disparities, DPSHC was designed as an immersive, service-oriented clinical learning opportunity for medical students [8, 14].

This study reports the outcomes of DPSHC’s first full academic year of sideline coverage, focusing on the type and severity of injuries encountered, management protocols, and system-level implications of student-led medical deployment in a high-need public school district. We present this program as a replicable model for medical schools and institutions aiming to enhance youth sports health and safety in underserved communities through student-driven initiatives.

Methods

Ethical approval

This project was approved by the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board (IRB-24–11–7337) and conducted in collaboration with officials from the DPSCD under a formal memorandum of understanding supporting sideline medical coverage and data collection.

Program design and rationale

The Detroit Public Schools Health Corps (DPSHC) is a student-led initiative at Wayne State University School of Medicine (WSUSOM) that provides sideline medical coverage and preparticipation physical evaluations (PPEs) for students in the DPSCD, which is the largest school district in Michigan and is designated a Health Professional Shortage Area by the Michigan Department of Health. Prior to the launch of this initiative, DPSCD lacked access to on-site athletic training or sports medicine services. A memorandum of understanding between DPSCD and Wayne State University formally established the partnership, authorizing medical students to provide supervised care at athletic events.

Setting and participants

Of the 47 total medical students to undergo the training, a total of 31 students opted to volunteer and were deployed across 16 high schools in DPSCD during the 2024–2025 academic year. HLs staffed 280 athletic events, including football, basketball, baseball, softball, and track/field, providing sideline assessment and management of acute injuries sustained during play.

Volunteer screening and training

All student volunteers complied with the DPSCD-mandated fingerprinting and criminal background checks and were issued photo identification badges by the DPSCD Police Department. Each HL completed a 12-h training curriculum divided into 3 sessions across the year. The training focused on sideline triage, musculoskeletal examination, concussion protocol, emergency communication using SBAR, and basic first aid. Instruction was delivered through a combination of didactic lectures, simulation-based workshops, and hands-on clinical skill sessions all guided by attending physicians and resident physicians in pediatrics, sports medicine, and orthopedic surgery. Competency was assessed through standardized mock physical examination scenarios after each session, during which students demonstrated triage skills, described necessary diagnostic tests, and delivered SBAR communication. These demonstrations were directly observed by attending physicians and residents in orthopedics and family medicine who provided students with structured feedback. In addition to direct feedback, surveys were administered immediately before and after each individual training session to assess changes in knowledge and confidence. A knowledge quiz was administered to each participant at the end of each training session, and a benchmark of 70% correct was established as a threshold to indicate a volunteer HL’s readiness for field work. If a volunteer HL were to score below 70% on the knowledge quiz, we prepared to provide them with supplementary materials and additional instruction. However, no student failed to meet the 70% benchmark. While this process facilitated a standardized approach across all HLs, it did not constitute any formal certification, and long-term retention skills have not yet been evaluated. The DPSHC volunteer training process is detailed in a 2025 article by Elnaggar et al. [9]. Once trained, HLs served as the primary on-site medical personnel during sporting games, with constant support from several orthopedics/sports medicine trained on-call physicians who were consulted to assist and advise on each case. The HLs assessed injuries, administered first aid, and triaged student-athletes. This included communicating with student parents for follow-up as needed, as well as referring cases to follow-up in clinic or escalating to EMS as needed. All care was provided under the supervision of physicians who were available in real time by phone for consultation, consistent with Michigan state law governing medical student clinical activity (Michigan Legislature).

Injury documentation and data collection

This IRB-approved retrospective study evaluated injury encounters during the program’s first year of implementation (2024–2025). HLs filled out a secure and standardized web-based injury report following each injury event. The documentation included: school affiliation, athlete demographics (age, gender, and grade), the sport during which the injury occurred, the anatomical region and type of injury, the mechanism of injury (contact or non-contact), reinjury status, and timing of injury, such as quarter or half. Diagnostic techniques including neurologic screening and orthopedic maneuvers used during injury assessment were also recorded along with clinical disposition. Disposition categories included return to play, withheld from further participation, referral for outpatient follow-up, emergency department referral, or transported via EMS.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize injury data by sport, anatomical region, injury type, and clinical disposition. Frequencies and proportions were calculated using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA). No inferential statistical testing was performed.

Results

During the 2024–2025 academic year, 31 medical student volunteers (17 first-year, 8 s-year, 4 third-year, 2 fourth-year) from WSUSOM participated in the initiative. Each volunteer completed a total of 12 h of training, delivered through a combination of didactic instruction, simulation-based scenarios, and hands-on sessions tailored to the seasonal sports they covered. Training was administered in 2-h sport-specific sessions throughout the year: football and soccer training occurred in the fall; basketball training in the winter; and baseball, softball, and track & field/cross country training in the spring. By the start of each season, volunteers had completed the corresponding sport-specific session(s), in addition to BLS and First Aid certification, which all medical students are required to have regardless of DPSHC participation. Training continued across the year, culminating in 12 total hours per volunteer. Collectively, HLs delivered 598 h of sideline medical coverage (174 football, 234 basketball, 125 baseball/softball, 40 track and field, 25 soccer hours) across 280 athletic events at 16 public high schools in Detroit. This amounted to 58 of 64 home football games (minimum 2 HLs per game), 156 of 250 home basketball games (minimum 1 HL per game), 50 of 185 home baseball/softball games (minimum 1 HL per game), 10 of 14 home soccer games (minimum 2 HLs per game), and 10 of 10 track meets (minimum 4 HLs per meet) held by the DPSCD. HLs averaged 12.3 (range 3–22) events this year when accounting for the minimum number of health leaders present at each event.

Over the course of the year, HLs documented 89 injury encounters. The majority involved the lower extremities, accounting for 50 cases (56.2%). Head and neck injuries, including concussions and suspected cervical spine instability, comprised 21 cases (23.6%), while upper extremity injuries represented 10 cases (11.2%). Torso and trunk injuries, along with miscellaneous cases such as lacerations, made up 8 cases (8.9%). Cardiorespiratory events were the least common, accounting for only 3 cases (3.4%).

All injuries, regardless of severity, were assessed on-site by a HL in real time with immediate consultation from an on-call physician via video call/phone call. This standardized approach ensured that every clinical decision, ranging from return-to-play clearance to emergency escalation, was made collaboratively with an attending and in conjunction with staff at the children’s hospital emergency department. The injury triage algorithm is highlighted in Fig. 1. Minor injuries, including sprains, contusions, and muscle cramps, were managed on-site using first aid protocols such as rest, ice, compression, and elevation, along with stabilization through taping or splinting when appropriate. In cases of suspected concussion, cervical spine injury, or severe musculoskeletal trauma (e.g., fractures, non-reducible dislocations, or ligamentous injuries), HLs contacted an on call physician and worked closely with them to determine the appropriate next step, which included next-day clinic referrals to orthopedic or neurological specialists, or activation of emergency medical services when indicated. This consultative model aimed to maintain that all sideline care was physician-directed while preserving the educational and service roles of the medical student HLs.

Of the 89 total injuries, 50 cases (56.2%) were managed fully on-site without the need for further intervention, and athletes returned to play after a period of observation. Twenty cases (22.4%) were referred for follow-up care with a physician, coordinated through orthopedic or primary care sports medicine partners offering rapid clinic access, often as soon as the next day. Ten cases (11.2%) were sent to the local children’s hospital emergency department on the same day due to concerns for serious injury, including suspected fractures, concussions, or dislocations. Of these, five were transported by EMS, while the remaining five were taken by a family member. An additional nine cases (10.2%) were triaged and removed from play out of an abundance of caution. These primarily involved head impacts where athletes were transiently symptomatic and demonstrated normal neurologic and functional status. Therefore, they did not require immediate clinic or ED referral but were instructed to visit their physician if symptoms returned or worsened. Head impacts with a concerning mechanism, but lack of symptoms as well as an unremarkable neurological exam and SCAT-6 assessment were allowed to return to play after a 15-min period of monitoring. Discussion

Program overview and context

Our findings demonstrate that well-trained medical student volunteers, when supported by institutional infrastructure and physician oversight, can serve as effective first-line responders in resource-limited athletic settings. The DPSHC model demonstrates the feasibility of implementing a promising and innovative approach to addressing long-standing disparities in school-based sports medicine coverage. In a city where nearly 30% of student-athletes attend schools without certified athletic trainers [2, 7], the consistent presence of HLs at athletic events provided access to immediate care, triage, and referral pathways [9]. More than half of the injuries documented were managed fully on-site, while the remaining cases were appropriately escalated using structured communication protocols (Table 2).

Contextualizing our findings

Our results demonstrate the viability of a student-led program aimed at identifying injuries and providing sideline assessment and management for underserved student-athletes. DPSHC offers a cost-effective, scalable model rooted in community engagement. While our results present the rates of certain injury patterns and student-athlete dispositions, our dataset does not include pre-intervention injury identification rates or comparative decision-making outcomes. As such, we cannot independently verify an increased association between injury identification and the presence of HLs within our cohort. However, the DPSHC model contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating the feasibility of implementing a community-based, low-cost health liaison program in high school athletics. Future work should aim to include baseline and comparative data to directly measure impact. Unlike short-term grant-based programs or paid staffing models, DPSHC leverages medical student altruism and institutional partnerships to maintain a consistent sideline presence, without placing financial strain on schools or families within the community [3, 11, 12].

Educational impact

HLs engaged in over 12 h of structured training and hands-on simulation, with competencies aligned to real-world sports coverage. As documented in a recent evaluation of our training program [9], simulation-based instruction produced significantly greater gains in student confidence than lecture-based training, with participants reporting improved preparedness for injury triage, communication, and emergency management. This dual impact, enhancing care delivery while enriching student learning, positions DPSHC as a unique intersection of service and education [7, 8, 14–19].

Coverage impact

The deployment of HLs increased access to sideline medical care within a school district that historically has limited sports medicine resources. Over the course of 280 athletic events, the consistent presence of trained medical student volunteers enabled timely assessment and management of a wide range of acute injuries and medical conditions, facilitating informed return-to-play decisions. This consistent coverage contributed to early recognition and timely emergency responses, a critical step in preventing morbid outcomes.

Collectively, these efforts represent a multi-faceted approach to improving athlete safety and care in an underserved community, with potential implications for similar programs in comparable settings.

Future directions and scalability

Building on these outcomes, DPSHC is pursuing several initiatives to hone its current standing and broaden its impact. We are working on doubling the size of our current volunteer base to eventually offer volunteers to every single athletic event at a high school in Detroit. Furthermore, planned initiatives include funding for enhanced hydration monitoring resources at events, implementation of health literacy education programs tailored to student-athletes and families, and integration of mental health support for high school athletes, a population often overlooked in traditional sports medicine. These programs aim to provide more holistic care and reinforce the long-term well-being of student-athletes beyond acute injury management. Finally, the scalability of this model is being actively explored at other urban medical schools, with interest from institutions facing similar resource gaps in youth sports [20, 21].

Limitations

The program’s retrospective data collection introduces several limitations. Injury reporting relies on voluntary documentation by HLs in real time, which may have led to underreporting or inconsistencies. Additionally, we were unable to assess recovery care, including return-to-play intervals or ongoing symptoms. Furthermore, due to the novelty of our program, we lacked a comparator group necessary to conduct any inferential analysis. Lastly, the organization has a limited number of attending physician specialties that are on call (3 orthopaedic surgeons and 1 pediatric sports medicine physician), as well as a lack of follow up for the 59 athletes that weren’t referred. These gaps underscore the need for formalized follow-up processes and prospective data capture in future iterations [9, 22].

Conclusion

The DPSHC offers a replicable and sustainable model for enhancing athletic safety and cultivating the clinical skills of future physicians. By embedding medical students within underserved athletic communities, the initiative bridges a critical gap in real-time medical care and emergency response where resources are traditionally limited. The hands-on experience gained by medical students through this program enriches their education, helping them develop skills in emergency triage, communication, and patient-centered care that are essential for their future careers. DPSHC represents an innovative integration of service, education, and public health that may be adapted and scaled to similar urban settings nationwide, ultimately contributing to improvements in youth athletic health care and health equity.
