Quantification of Lower Extremity Dressing Movements in Patients With Stroke
Authors: Yuki Kuroda, Naoyuki Motojima, Sano Kouhei, Sumiko Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Lower garment-raising movements in stroke/hemiplegia differed by assistance level.•The supervision group showed decreased efficiency during early manipulation.•The assistance group was inefficient during downward reach and final manipulations.

For patients who have experienced a stroke and hemiplegia, the need for assistance with toileting has been associated with depression, reduced social participation, decreased quality of life.1, 2, 3 Furthermore, the need for assistance with toileting not only places economic, mental, and physical strain on individuals but also on their families and society.4 Moreover, the level of independence in toileting influences the length of hospital stays.5 Therefore, many hemiplegic individuals desire to reduce the amount of assistance they require.6

Toileting is categorized into multiple movements, such as raising and lowering lower garments and cleansing. Raising the lower garment is the most difficult task to perform independently.7 In patient with stroke and hemiplegia, garment-raising begins with a “downward reach,” followed by non–paretic- and paretic-side manipulations.

Patients with stroke and hemiplegia who require supervision exhibit longer manipulation times and lower paretic limb loading ratios than independent patients.8, 9, 10, 11 These differences are attributed to trunk and lower limb impairments. Based on these reports, training of the trunk and lower limb function, as well as repetitive task practice to promote motor learning, is recommended for acquiring lower garment-raising movements. The Royal College of Physicians Stroke Guideline 2023 emphasizes the importance of task-oriented practice. However, specific guidelines on which manipulations to prioritize remains lacking.12 This gap partially reflects prior studies’ emphasis on overall manipulation time and kinematic characteristics rather than phase-specific movement analysis. However, few studies have analyzed individual manipulations, such as the downward reach, non–paretic-side manipulation, and paretic-side manipulation.10,13 The second issue concerns the bias in the study population. Previous studies have primarily focused on independent or supervised patients; research on patients with stroke and hemiplegia who require assistance is limited. Consequently, as patients progress across assistance, supervision, and independence levels, therapists lack clear guidelines on which manipulations to prioritize. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the characteristics of lower extremity garment-raising movements among the independent, supervised, and assistive groups.

Further, previous studies have suggested that the downward reach requires trunk movement in the sagittal plane, whereas non–paretic- and paretic-side manipulations involve trunk movement in the frontal plane.¹¹ Recent reports have also indicated differences in pelvic rotation angles between the independent and supervision groups during paretic side manipulation.⁹ Accordingly, we hypothesized that trunk and pelvic flexion angles during the downward reach and trunk and pelvic rotation angles during non–paretic- and paretic-side manipulations would differ between the independent and supervision groups, as well as between the supervision and assistance groups.

Methods

Participants

We selected participants from data collected between February 2021 and January 2024, among patients hospitalized in the convalescent rehabilitation ward of the author’s institution. Group classification was determined based on the Functional Independence Measure (FIM), which has high interrater reliability.14 The independent group was defined as those who scored 6 points or more on the toileting items of the FIM. The supervision group included those who scored 5 points and required supervision during lower garment raising to prevent falls. The assistance group included those who scored 4 points and required physical assistance during lower garment raising because of the risk of falling. The FIM was scored by a nurse-in-charge, multiple physical therapists, and an occupational therapist. Data from participants whose level of independence was expected to change at the time of measurement, or those with vestibular disorders, visual field deficits, or orthopedic conditions, were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Convalescent Rehabilitation Ward (20240327-b). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurement equipment

Ⅿeasurements were performed using a 3-dimensional motion analysis systema comprising 8 camerasb and 2 force platesc and video camera.d Infrared reflex markers were used for the trunk (the cervical vertebral tuberosities, second thoracic spinous process [T2] and seventh thoracic spinous process [T7]), xiphoid process, upper extremity segment (acromia, humeral external epicondyles, ulnar styloid processes, and radial styloid process), pelvis segment (anterior superior iliac spines and posterior superior iliac spines), thigh segment (greater trochanters, lateral femurs, and medial and lateral epicondyle of the femur), shank segment (lateral shanks and medial and lateral malleoli), and foot segment (calcaneus, second metatarsal bones, and fifth metatarsal bones), and were affixed to each site for definition. Additionally, considering that the markers on the knee joints and anterior superior iliac spine may not be visible during trunk flexion, 3-point markers were affixed to the sacral region and both thighs.15 Marker trajectories and ground reaction force data were synchronized and measured at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. Video recordings obtained from a camera positioned in front of the participants were also synchronized with the motion capture system.

Lower garments used during measurements

Participants wore leggings extending to the proximal thighs, along with shoes, during the measurements. Marker-fixing pants were worn over the leggings to prevent the markers from detaching during measurement (fig 1a). While wearing these garments, the participants raised the lower garment from the knee joint to the anterior superior iliac spine, with one foot placed on each of the 2 force plates.9,11,16 A thin lower garment was selected for manipulation during the task to ensure that the cameras could clearly capture the markers (fig 1b). Data processing

Marker trajectories and ground reaction force data were low-pass-filtered using a second-order Butterworth filter with cutoffs of 6 Hz and 18 Hz, respectively.17 Virtual markers were created using Vicon Body Builder version 3.6.4.e

The trunk segment coordinate system was established with its origin at T2 (fig 2a). The Z-axis was defined as the vector from T7 to T2. The X-axis was calculated as the cross product of the vector from T2 to the clavicle marker and the Z-axis, whereas the Y-axis was obtained as the cross product of the Z- and X-axes. For the pelvic segment, the origin of the pelvic coordinate system was set at the midpoint between the right and left anterior superior iliac spines (fig 2b). The Y-axis was defined as the vector from the midpoint of the right and left posterior superior iliac spines to the origin of the pelvic coordinate system. The Z-axis was determined as the cross product of the vector from the left anterior superior iliac spine to the right anterior superior iliac spine and the Y-axis, and the X-axis was defined as the cross product of the Y- and Z-axes.Fig. 2Trunk coordinate system and pelvic coordinate system.a) Trunk coordinate system.b) Pelvic coordinate system.The trunk coordinate origin was defined at T2, and the pelvic origin (CA) at the midpoint between RASIS and LASIS.Abbreviations: LASIS, left anterior superior iliac spine; RASIS, right anterior superior iliac spine.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Trunk and pelvic angles were calculated as Euler angles of the local coordinate systems relative to the laboratory coordinate system. Flexion/extension was defined as rotation about the X-axis, with positive values indicating flexion. Lateral flexion was calculated as rotation about the Y-axis, and axial rotation as rotation about the Z-axis. The Euler rotation sequence was XYZ for flexion/extension and ZYX for lateral flexion and axial rotation. Angles were sign-adjusted such that movements toward the nonparetic side during nonparetic manipulations were defined as positive, as were movements toward the paretic side during paretic manipulations. For each phase, the maximum angle was extracted. Ground reaction force data were also expressed relative to the laboratory coordinate system, with positive directions defined as rightward, forward, and upward.

Measurement periods

In this study, 5 phases were defined as the analysis intervals (fig 3). The measurement periods were determined based on the similarity of hand movement patterns among the participants for each manipulation side, using the radial styloid process marker of the manipulating hand as a reference. The beginning of the movement was defined as the time point when the center of pressure (COP) showed a change of ±2 standard deviations or more from the mean value.18 The downward reach period is defined as the duration from the beginning of the movement until the Z-axis coordinate of the radial styloid process marker reaches its lowest point. The first manipulation on the nonparetic or paretic side was subsequently defined as the duration from the lowest to the highest Z-axis coordinates of the radial styloid process marker. The subsequent manipulations were performed in the same manner.Fig. 3Analysis phases of lower garment-raising movement.Fig 3 dummy alt text

Extracted outcomes

The following outcomes, previously reported in earlier studies,9,11,19 were extracted for each measurement period: the primary outcomes were trunk (°) and pelvic (°) angles. The secondary outcomes included the paretic limb loading ratio (%), manipulation time (s), range of the COP in the mediolateral direction (m/height), and range of the COP in the anteroposterior direction (m/height).

To further analyze the manipulation efficiency, the vertical displacement of the lower garment (cm/height) during each manipulation was extracted. The displacement was calculated from still images obtained from video recordings using ImageJ ver. 1.53f by measuring the distance between the plantar surface and the waist portion of the lower garment.20

The paretic limb loading ratio (%) was calculated from the vertical component of the ground reaction forces of both feet, with a value of 50% indicating perfect symmetry.21 The range of the COP along each axis was defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum positions, with larger values indicating greater postural instability.22,23 For each outcome, the mean of 3 trials was used for participants in the independent and supervised groups. For the assistance group, the mean of 2 successful trials was used when 3 trials could not be completed.

In addition, the Fugl–Meyer Assessment and Trunk Control Test, which have been shown in previous studies to be associated with the amount of assistance required for lower garment raising, were calculated from medical records.8, 9, 10

Statistical approach

The normality of all outcome measures was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences among the independent, supervision, and assistance groups were analyzed using 1-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Considering that the typical recovery process progresses sequentially from assistance to supervision to independence, post hoc comparisons were performed between the independent and supervision groups, and between the supervision and assistance groups, using an unpaired t test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. The significance level was set at P<.025, based on the Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed using JASP version 0.19.3.g

Comparison between the independent and supervision groups

Compared to the independent group, the supervision group exhibited greater pelvic rotation toward the paretic side during the first paretic-side manipulation (P=.009). In addition, across all manipulations, the supervision group had a significantly lower paretic limb loading ratio. The manipulation time was significantly longer in the supervision group during the downward reach (P=.005), first paretic-side manipulation (P=.013), and total manipulation period (P=.003). Furthermore, the vertical displacement of the lower garment was significantly smaller in the supervision group during the first non–paretic-side manipulation (P=.013).

Comparison between the supervision and assistance groups

During downward reach, the assistance group exhibited significantly lower pelvic flexion than the supervision group (P=.002). Furthermore, during the final non–paretic-side manipulation, the assistance group showed significantly smaller trunk rotation toward the nonparetic side and smaller pelvic rotation toward the nonparetic side than the supervision group (P=.002 and P=.013, respectively). During the final paretic-side manipulation, the manipulation time was longer (P=.015) and pelvic rotation toward the paretic side was greater in the assistance group (P=.009). Across all the manipulations, the assistance group demonstrated a significantly lower paretic limb loading ratio than the supervision group.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to analyze the motor characteristics of the independent, supervision, and assistance groups during lower garment raising in patients with stroke and hemiplegia. The results revealed phase-specific differences in trunk and pelvic angles between the independent and supervision groups, as well as between the supervision and assistance groups, partially supporting our hypothesis. The present findings offer clinically relevant insights to guide therapists in prioritizing manipulations when supporting patients aiming to achieve independence in lower garment raising.

Kinematic data showed that, compared with the independent group, the supervision group demonstrated significantly greater pelvic rotation toward the paretic side during the first paretic-side manipulation. The supervision group showed longer manipulation times during the downward reach, the first paretic-side manipulation, and overall. In addition, the vertical displacement of the lower garment was smaller during the first non–paretic-side manipulation. Previous studies reported similar kinematic characteristics and prolonged manipulation times in the supervision group.9,11 Detailed phase-specific analysis revealed that the inefficiency of the supervision group was particularly evident during early manipulations contributing to the prolonged manipulation time across all manipulations. In contrast, previous studies have reported that the inefficiency in patients with stroke and hemiplegia tended to manifest during the final paretic-side manipulation.13 Because this study focused on the supervision group, and it is possible that reduced lower limb function led to decreased efficiency during early downward manipulations. These findings highlight the importance of targeting early manipulation phases to support progression toward independence in supervised patients.

The assistance group showed multiple kinematic differences compared with the supervision group. During downward reach, the assistance group exhibited a smaller pelvic flexion angle and lower paretic limb loading ratio overall. Patients with stroke and hemiplegia often suppress trunk and pelvic movements during various tasks to minimize postural sway.24,25 Similarly, our results suggest that the assistance group employed a compensatory strategy to control the pelvic angle and maintain postural stability. During the final non–paretic-side manipulation, the assistance group performed the task with their trunk and pelvis oriented toward the paretic side. Furthermore, compared with the supervision group, the assistance group exhibited a greater pelvic rotation angle toward the paretic side and a longer manipulation time during the final paretic-side manipulation. These findings suggest that the assistance group performed manipulations in postures that limited hand access to the lower garment during the downward reach, the final non–paretic-side manipulation, and the final paretic-side manipulation, reflecting an inefficient movement strategy. Previous research on assistance groups has primarily focused on body function, with few studies analyzing movement characteristics.10,26,27 Our study revealed distinct kinematic features during the downward reach and final manipulations on the nonparetic and paretic sides.

Many participants in the assistance group failed multiple attempts during the measurements, indicating that the identified features likely require improvement.

Clinical applications

First, to facilitate the transition from the supervision group to the independent group, it is important to improve not only lower limb function but also the efficiency of early manipulations. Specifically, reducing manipulation time during the early phases and correcting greater pelvic rotation during paretic-side manipulations may contribute to enhanced movement efficiency.

To facilitate progression from the assistance to the supervision group, improving trunk function and implementing repetitive task practice focusing on trunk and pelvic movements are necessary. Specifically, therapists should evaluate whether the trunk and pelvis are sufficiently mobilized during the downward reach and the final non–paretic-side manipulation, and whether greater pelvic rotation occurs during the final paretic-side manipulation. Based on these assessments, targeted repetitive task practice should be implemented.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the independent and supervision groups were analyzed using the mean of 3 trials, whereas the assistance group was analyzed using only 2 trials. This inconsistency may have reduced data comparability and underestimated movement variability in the assistance group. Second, some participants performed each manipulation 3 or more times, indicating that certain movement characteristics may not have been reflected in this analysis interval. Third, the sample size was relatively small, limiting generalizability. Fourth, the structure of lower garments differs between men and women and varies widely by type. These differences may influence the kinematic characteristics of garment-raising movements. Fifth, because of the task demands, the hand occasionally came into contact with the thigh markers and displaced them, potentially introducing measurement error. Therefore, detailed lower limb kinematic analysis was challenging. Although markerless motion capture may address this limitation, challenges have been noted regarding measurements in patients with hemiplegia and the accuracy of trunk and pelvic angles.28,29 In this study, the markers used to calculate trunk and pelvic angles were secured by marker‑fixing pants and supplemented with virtual markers to prevent displacement or detachment during measurement. Accordingly, marker stability was maintained, and the results obtained in this study remain valuable.

Conclusions

Movements involved in raising lower garments in patients with stroke or hemiplegia vary based on the level of assistance required. In the supervision group, reduced efficiency was observed during the downward reach and initial nonparetic and paretic-side manipulations. The assistance group demonstrated smaller trunk and pelvic movements during the downward reach and the final non–paretic‑side manipulation, and exhibited greater pelvic rotation toward the paretic side during the final paretic‑side manipulation than the supervision group. These findings indicate that the assistance group performed manipulations in postures that limited hand access to the lower garment, reflecting an inefficient movement strategy. These findings guide therapists in prioritizing specific phases of lower garment-raising when working with patients with stroke and hemiplegia.
