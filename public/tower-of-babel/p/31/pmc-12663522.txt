The impact of educational attainment on the risk of three types of atherosclerosis: A two-sample mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Cong Ding, Haifeng Chen, Chunlan Li, Tianyuan Fang, Guancheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This study suggests a potential causal association between higher educational attainment and reduced atherosclerosis risk using Mendelian randomization.•College graduation is associated with a lower risk of atherosclerosis (OR=0.43, 95 % CI: 0.32–0.57, P = 1.79E-08) and coronary artery disease (OR=0.57, 95 % CI: 0.46–0.71, P = 6.62E-07).•No significant association was found between educational attainment and cerebral atherosclerosis (P > 0.05).•The study highlights the potential of integrating educational policies into cardiovascular disease prevention strategies.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is the primary pathological basis of cardiovascular diseases and one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 17 million people globally die from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases each year, accounting for over 32 % of all deaths, with a large proportion attributable to atherosclerosis. The pathogenesis of atherosclerosis involves complex inflammatory responses, lipid metabolism disorders, and endothelial dysfunction, ultimately leading to arterial lumen narrowing or blockage, which may result in severe outcomes such as myocardial infarction and stroke [2,3]. With lifestyle changes and the aging population, the incidence of atherosclerosis continues to rise, presenting a major public health challenge globally.

Given its high prevalence, atherosclerosis is a critical factor in the development of cardiovascular events. Therefore, early intervention and prevention are crucial [4]. Research indicates that atherosclerosis is influenced by a variety of factors, including genetic susceptibility and significant socioeconomic factors, with educational attainment being one such factor that has garnered considerable attention [5]. Observational studies have found that lower educational attainment and poor cardiovascular health are both associated with a higher risk of dementia [6]. However, these studies are often confounded by various factors, making it difficult to establish a clear causal relationship between education and atherosclerosis.

Early prevention of atherosclerosis is essential to reduce the burden of cardiovascular disease, especially as the early onset and rapid progression of atherosclerosis-related conditions contribute to substantial socioeconomic costs worldwide. Thus, exploring modifiable risk factors, such as educational attainment, is of great practical significance in the prevention of atherosclerosis. Mendelian randomization (MR) provides a framework for causal inference by using genetic variants as instrumental variables. While the core MR approach is sensitive to certain biases, the use of multiple complementary methods—such as MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO—can help improve robustness under different assumptions [7].

Individuals with higher educational attainment can benefit from improved cardiovascular health in multiple ways. Higher educational attainment enhances health-related cognition and socioeconomic resources, which facilitate both social-level and individual-level health behaviors. At the social level, better education is associated with improved employment opportunities, economic stability, and more effective utilization of medical services. These, in turn, support individual-level behaviors such as balanced diet, regular physical activity, and adequate sleep. Together, these factors mediate the relationship between education and reduced cardiovascular risk, as shown in Fig. 1. This research employs a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach to investigate the causal relationship between educational attainment and the risk of atherosclerosis. By utilizing large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data, this study examines the association between educational milestones (such as university graduation and years of education) and atherosclerosis risk, aiming to provide a scientific foundation for developing more targeted cardiovascular disease prevention strategies.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the influence of education level on arteriosclerosis.Fig 1

Materials and methods

Study design

As illustrated in Fig. 2, this study employs a Mendelian Randomization (MR) design to systematically investigate the association between educational attainment and the risk of atherosclerosis. Since this study is a re-analysis of publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, no additional ethical approval was required.Fig. 2Mendelian randomized analysis process of education and risk of arterial atherosclerosis.Fig 2

Data sources

In this study, a comprehensive Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis was conducted to explore the causal relationship between educational attainment and atherosclerosis. All patients included met the diagnostic criteria defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD).

The exposure variables used in this study were:Educational attainment (college completion) with GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90029012, and Educational attainment (years of education) with GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90029013. These data were sourced from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) dataset [8], with a sample size of 470,941 and 11,972,619 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) analyzed. All GWAS summary statistics used in this study were derived from participants of European ancestry. This population homogeneity minimizes the risk of bias due to population stratification, a critical consideration in Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses. The consistency in ancestry between exposure and outcome datasets helps fulfill the core MR assumption that genetic variants are not confounded by population structure.

The outcome variables in this study included the following three atherosclerosis phenotypes: Atherosclerosis (excluding cerebral, coronary, and peripheral artery disease), Coronary atherosclerosis, and Cerebral atherosclerosis.

The specific data sources are as follows:

Atherosclerosis: Data from the 2024 Finnish dataset (finngen_R11_I9_ATHSCLE), including 17,832 cases and 420,324 controls, with 20,093,918 SNPs analyzed.

Coronary atherosclerosis: Data from the 2024 Finnish dataset (finngen_R11_I9_CORATHER), including 56,685 cases and 378,019 controls, with 20,093,889 SNPs analyzed.

Cerebral atherosclerosis: Data from the 2024 Finnish dataset (finngen_R11_I9_CEREBATHER), including 379 cases and 453,354 controls, with 20,094,120 SNPs analyzed. All GWAS summary statistics used in this study were obtained from publicly available and well-documented sources. For the exposure variables, the educational attainment GWAS datasets (ebi-a-GCST90029012 and GCST90029013) were derived from the meta-analysis conducted by the Social Science Genetic Association Consortium (SSGAC), including over 1.1 million individuals of European ancestry. These datasets include approximately 11.9 million SNPs after quality control, based on imputed genotype data primarily from the UK Biobank and 23andMe, harmonized using the 1000 Genomes reference panel. All SNPs included passed standard filters applied by the SSGAC, including minor allele frequency, imputation quality, and call rate.

For the outcome variables, we used summary-level data from FinnGen Release R11 (2024), which includes >20 million SNPs per phenotype. The FinnGen project applies a standardized and rigorous QC pipeline, including genotype-level filtering, imputation using the SISu v3 reference panel, and variant-level exclusions based on imputation info score and call rate. The high number of SNPs reflects the dense imputation panel and extensive coverage of rare and low-frequency variants. The summary statistics we used are those released post-QC by FinnGen and did not require additional filtering or beta cut-offs in our study. We verified that no inflation or signal distortion was observed in the instrumental SNPs used in our MR analysis.

All SNPs used in the MR analysis passed genome-wide significance thresholds (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), linkage disequilibrium pruning, and F-statistic strength checks, and were further screened using the PhenoScanner database to minimize potential confounding.

Selection of instrumental variables

Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis is based on three core assumptions: (1) the genetic variants must be strongly associated with the exposure factor; (2) the genetic variants must be independent of confounding factors; and (3) the genetic variants should not be influenced by the outcome factors [7,9,10]. An initial genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10^−8 was set to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with educational characteristics [11]. To ensure the independence of these SNPs, we set a linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold of r^2 = 0.001 and required a physical distance of over 10,000 KB to avoid LD between the selected SNPs [12].

Additionally, the F-statistic was calculated for each instrumental variable (IV) to assess the potential for weak instrument bias. The F-value was calculated using the formula F = Beta^2/Se^2, where Beta represents the effect size of the allele, and Se is the standard error of Beta. Only IVs with an F-value greater than 10 were retained for further analysis, thereby minimizing the risk of bias from weak instruments [13].

Finally, to further ensure the robustness of our analysis, all selected SNPs were cross-checked against the Phenoscanner database (www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk) on June 10, 2024, to exclude any SNPs that may be associated with confounding phenotypes [14]. To minimize potential violations of the exchangeability assumption, all selected SNPs were screened using the PhenoScanner V2 database. We excluded SNPs with genome-wide significant associations (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) with known confounding phenotypes, including socioeconomic indicators (e.g., income, occupational status), behavioral traits (e.g., smoking, alcohol use, physical activity), and metabolic traits (e.g., BMI, type 2 diabetes, blood pressure). This process helped reduce the risk of horizontal pleiotropy or confounding through shared pathways. These steps ensured the validity and reliability of our MR study.

Mendelian randomization analysis

In this study, we employed the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method as the primary approach for estimating causal effects. IVW is considered a robust tool for detecting causal relationships in two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted supplementary analyses, including MR-Egger regression, the weighted median method, and the weighted mode method [15].

Furthermore, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses to validate the results. First, Cochran's Q test was applied to assess heterogeneity, which might influence the causal estimates. A p-value greater than 0.05 indicates that heterogeneity can be ignored, and a fixed-effect model is used. Conversely, if the p-value is less than or equal to 0.05, a random-effect model is applied to account for heterogeneity [16].

To detect horizontal pleiotropy, which may bias the MR results, we performed the MR-Egger intercept test. Additionally, we used the MR-PRESSO outlier test to identify and correct for outliers in the SNPs, thus addressing any potential horizontal pleiotropy [17]. Residual sensitivity analyses were also conducted to further assess the robustness of the findings. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1), with the "TwoSampleMR" and "MRPRESSO" packages facilitating MR analysis, and the "forest plot" package used for visualization. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Instrumental variables

In this study, to satisfy the three core assumptions, 198, 198, and 199 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified as instrumental variables for the analysis of Educational attainment (college completion) with Atherosclerosis, Coronary atherosclerosis, and Cerebral atherosclerosis, respectively. Similarly, 206, 205, and 206 SNPs were identified as instrumental variables for the analysis of Educational attainment (years of education) with Atherosclerosis, Coronary atherosclerosis, and Cerebral atherosclerosis, respectively. For university completion, the beta coefficients (representing the per-allele increase in log-odds of completing university) ranged from –0.188 to 0.238, with a median of 0.0053 across the selected SNPs. All instruments had F-statistics greater than 10, and the median F-statistic was 37.5, indicating overall strong instrument strength. Two-Sample Mendelian randomization analysis

This study employed four methods (Inverse Variance Weighted, MR Egger, Weighted Median, and Weighted Mode) to evaluate the causal relationship between educational attainment and atherosclerosis. As shown in Fig. 3, the results demonstrated that Educational attainment (college completion) was negatively correlated with the occurrence of Atherosclerosis and Coronary atherosclerosis, showing a significant protective effect. Specifically, the odds ratio (OR) for the association between Educational attainment (college completion) and Atherosclerosis was 0.43 (95 % CI: 0.32–0.57, p = 1.79E-08), and the OR for Coronary atherosclerosis was 0.57 (95 % CI: 0.46–0.71, p = 6.62E-07). No significant causal effect was found between Educational attainment (college completion) and Cerebral atherosclerosis (p > 0.05).Fig. 3Forest chart of causal relationship between education level and atherosclerosis of diseased arteries.Fig 3

Discussion

In this study, Mendelian randomization analysis revealed a significant inverse relationship between educational attainment and the risk of atherosclerosis. Specifically, higher educational attainment, such as college graduation, was significantly associated with a reduced risk of atherosclerosis, particularly showing a more pronounced protective effect against coronary atherosclerosis. Using the IVW method, higher educational attainment, such as college graduation, was significantly associated with a reduced risk of atherosclerosis (OR = 0.43, 95 % CI: 0.32–0.57, p = 1.79E-08) and coronary atherosclerosis (OR = 0.57, 95 % CI: 0.46–0.71, p = 6.62E-07). Years of education were also inversely associated with the risk of coronary atherosclerosis (OR = 0.95, 95 % CI: 0.93–0.97, p = 2.85E-06). No significant association was observed with cerebral atherosclerosis (p > 0.05). Although these associations did not reach statistical significance in MR-Egger or weighted mode analyses, the odds ratios from these methods consistently remained below 1, indicating protective trends and supporting the robustness of the findings.

Comparison with existing studies

The relationship between educational attainment and atherosclerosis risk is supported by several existing studies. A study published in JAMA Cardiology reported that educational attainment is associated with lifetime cardiovascular risk, with higher levels of education being linked to healthier and longer lives [18]. Moreover, another study found that socioeconomic status, including education, is related to the risk of subclinical atherosclerosis, with lower educational levels being associated with a higher risk of atherosclerosis [19]. Additionally, research has shown that educational attainment is significantly associated with cardiovascular disease incidence and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where lower educational levels are a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease and death, accounting for approximately 12.5 % of the population attributable fraction (PAF) [20]. This suggests that education, as part of socioeconomic and psychosocial factors, may influence cardiovascular disease risk by affecting individual health behaviors and lifestyle choices. Furthermore, individuals with lower educational attainment in low- and middle-income countries appear to be more significantly affected by cardiovascular disease compared to those in high-income countries. These findings are consistent with the conclusions drawn from our study.

Biological mechanisms explanation

This study indicates that educational attainment influences atherosclerosis through multiple mechanisms. First, higher educational levels are typically associated with healthier lifestyles, such as a balanced diet, avoiding smoking, and regular physical activity. These health behaviors have been extensively demonstrated to significantly reduce cardiovascular disease risk. For example, studies have shown that after 12 weeks of exercise training, pregnant women exhibited notable improvements in body mass index, augmentation index, systolic blood pressure, and pulse pressure, particularly with a significant correlation between augmentation index and the second and third trimesters. Additionally, exercise interventions led to a reduction in pulse wave velocity (PWV), suggesting that exercise can help slow the progression of arterial stiffness [21].

An increasing body of interdisciplinary research suggests that educational attainment plays a multifaceted role in shaping health outcomes through both direct and indirect pathways. Education exerts lasting effects on brain development, cognitive function, health literacy, and behavioral regulation. These effects are not solely knowledge-based but also neurobiologically grounded, as higher education is associated with enhanced executive function, emotional regulation, and long-term planning—capacities that are essential for making informed health-related decisions[22].

Moreover, individuals with higher educational attainment are more likely to achieve a higher socioeconomic status (SES), which is linked to greater access to healthcare resources, healthier living environments, and more frequent engagement in health-promoting behaviors[23]. By enhancing SES, these individuals are better positioned to access preventive health services and information, thereby indirectly reducing their risk of atherosclerosis. Additionally, education is well known to improve cognitive skills and health literacy[24], which in turn facilitate better understanding and management of major cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia[25]. Together, these mechanisms promote more active participation in preventive healthcare and healthier lifestyle choices.

Conversely, individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds may face structural barriers to healthcare access, limited exposure to health-related knowledge, and insufficient resources to maintain good health. These disadvantages may increase their vulnerability to cardiovascular risk factors and hinder engagement in preventive behaviors, thereby elevating their risk of developing atherosclerosis[26].Importantly, while our current study revealed a significant inverse association between genetically predicted higher educational attainment and the risk of coronary atherosclerosis and atherosclerosis in general, no such association was observed for cerebral atherosclerosis. This discrepancy may be explained by disease-specific pathophysiological processes. Individuals with higher educational attainment may engage in prolonged cognitive activities that increase cerebral metabolic demand and chronic vascular load, potentially offsetting the protective effects observed in peripheral arteries [27].

In summary, educational attainment influences atherosclerosis through multiple layers, including lifestyle factors, health literacy, and socioeconomic status. Future public health strategies and cardiovascular disease prevention measures should incorporate educational factors to comprehensively reduce the incidence of cardiovascular diseases.

Limitations

Although this study provides Mendelian randomization (MR)-based evidence supporting a causal relationship between educational attainment and atherosclerosis-related outcomes, several important limitations should be noted.

First, while genetic instruments were derived from large-scale GWAS datasets, the selected SNPs may not comprehensively capture the total genetic architecture of educational attainment, and some instruments may have small effect sizes, raising concerns about potential weak instrument bias. In particular, although all SNPs had F-statistics greater than 10, the median effect size for the instrumental variables was modest (e.g., for years of education, the median β corresponds to an odds ratio of exp(0.0053) = 1.005). Such a weak per-allele effect is common in education-related GWAS but nonetheless limits the strength of causal inference, and this should be carefully considered when interpreting our findings.

Second, our analyses were restricted to populations of predominantly European ancestry, particularly individuals from Finnish and UK Biobank cohorts. While this genetic homogeneity reduces confounding due to population stratification and improves internal validity, it limits the generalizability of the findings to other ancestral groups. Future MR studies should replicate our analyses in more diverse populations to examine the consistency and external validity of the observed associations.

Third, although we performed PhenoScanner screening and used sensitivity methods such as MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO to address horizontal pleiotropy, the exchangeability (exclusion restriction) assumption may still be violated. This is particularly relevant for socially complex exposures like education, which is known to exhibit strong assortative mating. Such mating patterns may induce correlations between the instruments and unmeasured familial or socioeconomic confounders, potentially biasing causal estimates.

Fourth, we recognize that our causal diagram (DAG) may not fully reflect the multifactorial and socially mediated pathways through which education influences cardiovascular outcomes. For example, dynastic effects—where parental genotypes shape offspring environments—could confound the association between genetic proxies for education and health outcomes. Moreover, education may influence outcomes indirectly through intermediate factors such as income, occupation, and health behavior, rather than exerting a direct biological effect.

Finally, MR estimates reflect the lifelong effect of genetic liability to education, rather than the impact of specific educational policies or interventions. Thus, the results should be interpreted as indicating indirect causal pathways rather than policy-actionable causal effects. Caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to real-world educational interventions.

Conclusion

This Mendelian randomization study provides genetic evidence supporting an indirect causal association between higher genetically predicted educational attainment and a lower risk of coronary atherosclerosis and overall atherosclerosis. These findings suggest that education may influence cardiovascular health through complex and multifactorial pathways, including modifiable intermediates such as health-related behaviors, socioeconomic factors, and health literacy.

While no significant association was found for cerebral atherosclerosis, the observed protective effects on coronary and general atherosclerosis highlight the importance of upstream social determinants in shaping long-term cardiovascular outcomes. However, as educational attainment is a socially embedded and polygenic trait, caution is warranted in interpreting these results as directly translatable to educational interventions.

Future research should explore the specific mediating mechanisms—such as physical activity, smoking, income, and cognitive health—through which education may exert its effects on cardiovascular disease. Longitudinal studies and MR analyses focusing on these proximal risk factors will further elucidate actionable targets for prevention strategies.
