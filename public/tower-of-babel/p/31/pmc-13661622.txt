Glucose enrichment accelerates C. elegans reproductive aging via non-autonomous DAF-2/insulin-like receptor signaling in somatic tissues
Authors: Faria Athar, Emma J. Houston, Emily Jewett, Chelsey K. May, Manuel Centeno Duque, Nicole M. Templeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reproductive systems are highly sensitive to diet, yet the long-term reproductive consequences of overnutrition are poorly defined. Glucose supplementation shortens Caenorhabditis elegans lifespan, and here we find that it also hastens age-related reproductive decline, evidenced by a greater oocyte deterioration and lower fertility with age. 20-mM glucose enrichment still shortens the lifespan of daf-2(e1370) mutants with reduced insulin-like signaling, but unexpectedly, we do not detect detrimental impacts on their reproductive aging phenotypes. Using auxin-induced tissue-selective degradation, we show that DAF-2/insulin-like receptor signaling in C. elegans intestine and body wall musculature is required for glucose enrichment to impair reproductive function of aged worms, and DAF-2 degradation in either tissue protects against glucose-induced reproductive aging. We also observe that disrupting lipid homeostasis via RNAi against the lipl-4 lipase can impair daf-2(e1370) reproductive function under glucose enrichment. Therefore, insulin-like signaling in metabolically active somatic tissues could represent a key link between overnutrition and reproductive aging.

Introduction

Modern diets high in energy-dense foods have led to widespread overnutrition, with physiological implications that extend beyond metabolic health. Since reproductive systems are very responsive to nutrient and metabolic cues, overnutrition has significant ramifications for reproductive health1—and potentially, for the age-related decline in female reproductive function. High-sugar, calorie-dense foods are associated with reduced ovarian function and subfertility, as well as greater intensity of menopausal symptoms1–4. It seems probable that chronic overnutrition would have cumulative impacts on reproductive aging, and metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes can be linked to both subfertility and earlier menopause in humans5–9. However, it is challenging to untangle specific mechanisms underlying these responses, because nutrient excess and metabolic dysfunction induce such breadth in changes across multiple organ systems.

The female reproductive system is one of the earliest to show age-related physiological deterioration in humans, with reductions in oocyte quality and fertility that can manifest around 30 years of age10,11. Caenorhabditis elegans is a powerful model for studying reproductive aging, with a well-defined reproductive system that undergoes a relatively early decline, driven by deteriorating oocyte quality12. The C. elegans reproductive system is also attuned to metabolic cues, akin to mammals. Evolutionarily conserved signaling systems such as insulin-like signaling respond dynamically to nutrient levels and promote energy-intensive processes like reproduction and growth, favored over preservation-type processes like stress resistance13,14. Suppressing insulin-like signaling by reducing levels or function of DAF-2, the only C. elegans insulin-like receptor, extends somatic lifespan, preserves reproductive tissue integrity with age, and significantly prolongs the fertile window15–17. Conversely, supplementation with excess glucose generates metabolically stressful conditions for C. elegans that lead to shortened lifespan18–26 and perturbed fertility25,27–29. Interestingly, reduction-of-function daf-2(e1370) mutants are still susceptible to lifespan-shortening effects of glucose exposure19,23, which suggests that an assortment of metabolic responses and signaling pathways are involved in mediating glucose-induced somatic aging.

In this study, we used C. elegans to investigate the long-term repercussions of chronic overnutrition on reproductive aging. We found that glucose enrichment worsens oocyte quality maintenance and reduces the capacity of reproductively aged worms to produce viable progeny. Intriguingly, however, we discovered that suppressing DAF-2/insulin-like receptor signaling allows reproductive function to be preserved under glucose-enriched conditions—even if this suppression is restricted to the somatic tissues of intestine or muscle. This suggests that dysregulated insulin signaling in metabolically active somatic tissues may have important implications for the reproductive system and the progression of age-related reproductive decline.

Results and discussion

Excess glucose accelerates somatic and reproductive aging

A mild dose of glucose (20 mM) was used to induce metabolically unhealthy, obesogenic-like conditions for C. elegans without causing excess-glucose toxicity18. We observed that exposure to 20-mM glucose during adulthood reduced wild-type lifespan by 15% (Fig. 1a), similar to the lifespan-shortening effects reported in other studies18–20. In addition to augmenting somatic aging, we found that glucose enrichment (GE) also advanced age-associated reproductive decline. When exposed to GE during adulthood (from the L4 larval stage onward), hermaphrodites were less likely to produce viable progeny if they were mated at day 5 of adulthood (Fig. 1b) or day 7 of adulthood (Fig. 1c), two time points of age-related reproductive deterioration12. Glucose has sex-specific and age-specific effects on lifespan and motility21,22 and can also indirectly exert impacts on worms through altering the metabolism of the OP50 E. coli that C. elegans consume30,31. In addition, glucose supplementation elevates osmolarity, introducing the possibility of hyperosmolarity-induced effects on worm physiology32. To test for this, we compared the effects of 20-mM glucose to 20-mM sorbitol, a non-metabolizable sugar that serves as an osmotic control without causing nutritional responses32. Compared to NGM, 20-mM sorbitol did slightly reduce late-mating capacity of day 7 adult hermaphrodites, but the late-mating capacity of glucose-exposed worms was markedly lower: a third of that of sorbitol-exposed populations (Fig. 1c). This indicates that osmotic effects only play a minor role. To disentangle direct effects of glucose on worm physiology from any potential impacts of bacteria-produced glucose metabolites or other bacterial changes30,31, we also tested the reproductive success of day 5 adult hermaphrodites fed heat-killed bacteria throughout adulthood, and found that the detrimental effects of GE persisted without the presence of live bacteria (precluding bacteria-mediated effects; Fig. 1d). Next, we confirmed that adulthood exposure to glucose still had a significant and lasting impact when reproductively aged hermaphrodites were shifted to NGM-only plates at day 5 for the mating assay, thus ensuring that the male worms were never exposed to glucose enrichment themselves (Fig. 1e). This helped us separate our observations from any potential effects of GE on males, male sperm, or mating proclivity. Although not statistically significant, trends suggested the possibility of lower egg and unfertilized oocyte yield by unmated day 3 adults if they had been on GE (Fig. 1f), and mated glucose-exposed hermaphrodites produced significantly fewer progeny in total (Fig. 1g, h), consistent with other published observations25,26. However, longitudinal mated brood size assays may underrepresent the output of highly fertile worms that die due to internal hatching (matricidal bagging), and do not fully differentiate oocyte-level changes from any GE-induced impacts on the stored sperm. Therefore, building on our observations that glucose exposure suppresses reproductive output and worsens reproductive function of aging C. elegans hermaphrodites, we next focused on identifying responses specific to germline and oocyte cells.

Glucose exposure induces germline- and oocyte-level changes in worms

Many stressors—including starvation, pathogens or toxins, and DNA replication stress—can cause an elevation in mitotic germ cell arrest and/or meiotic germ cell apoptosis33. This germline plasticity, which can be associated with germline shrinkage, protects against damage and limits its inheritance33. We found that glucose exposure significantly reduced the number of germ cell nuclei in the gonad of mated day 4 adult hermaphrodites (Fig. 2a, b), likely contributing to their reduced progeny production (Fig. 1). In addition to distal germline characteristics, oocyte quality is a key determinant of age-related reproductive decline12,14,34. We found that glucose exposure significantly impaired the maintenance of oocyte quality, based on altered oocyte morphology and mitochondrial dynamics. By day 5 of adulthood, glucose-exposed worms were more likely to have maturing oocytes with an accumulation of morphological features that correspond to deteriorated quality, including oocytes that were abnormally small, misshapen, and/or interspersed with cavities (Fig. 2d, e). Small oocyte size in worms or mammals is associated with poor developmental capability of the oocyte, potentially due to maturation abnormalities or insufficient resource allocation for fertilization and embryogenesis34–37. The presence of cavities between neighboring oocytes or between oocytes and their surrounding somatic tissue has also been observed in older worms with poor-quality oocytes15,38; these morphological changes might disrupt the cellular contact required for signal transduction or molecule transfer39.

Oocytes have an abundance of mitochondria, which are involved in ATP production, calcium signaling, redox balance, and metabolism40. High glucose induces mitochondrial dysfunction in C. elegans body wall muscle24, thus we examined the morphology of oocyte mitochondrial networks. Cellular functions ranging from energy production to mitochondrial quality control are affected by a coordinated balance of mitochondrial fission and fusion41,42, with elongated mitochondria associated with increased fusion43 and an increased presence of more punctate mitochondria suggesting a shift towards more fission. Using day 5 adult mex-50:tomm-20::gfp hermaphrodites with germline-specific labeling of mitochondria44, we found that glucose-exposed worms had a higher likelihood of -1 mature oocytes with punctate or mixed mitochondrial morphology, unlike the predominantly elongated mitochondria in oocytes of age-matched NGM-exposed controls (Fig. 2f, g). Imbalances in mitochondrial dynamics can impair cellular homeostasis through means such as increasing oxidative stress, lowering mitochondrial membrane potential and respiratory capacity, and altering intracellular distribution and signaling effects41,45. Although glucose enrichment has been shown to promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in whole worms18,46, we did not detect changed ROS levels in the oocytes of 20-mM glucose-exposed worms (Fig. 2h, i). In mammals, oocytes maintain lower levels of ROS than somatic cells by limiting ROS generation in mitochondrial metabolism47,48; similar mechanisms might protect C. elegans oocytes against glucose-induced ROS accumulation.

Proper oocyte functioning is also critically dependent on normal lipid metabolism49,50, and GE is known to increase whole-body lipid deposition in C. elegans51–54 (see Fig. 2j for representative examples). To gauge lipid dynamics within oocytes, we quantified lipid droplets in the three most-mature oocytes of dissected gonads, but we did not observe increased lipid content in maturing oocytes of GE-exposed, day 2 adult worms (compared to the oocytes of NGM-only controls; Fig. 2k, l). Altogether, we observed that glucose enrichment compromises the maintenance of healthy oocyte morphology and mitochondrial dynamics in addition to reducing germ cell numbers. These germline- and oocyte-level changes likely contribute towards the acceleration of reproductive aging.

Reduction-of-function daf-2(e1370) mutation: protective against glucose-induced reproductive decline, but not against a shortened lifespan

Nutrient levels and energy balance are interpreted by nutrient-sensing signaling pathways that direct physiological responses. Among them, insulin-like signaling is particularly linked to glucose19,55. We used a C. elegans strain with GFP-labeled DAF-256 to show that 20 mM of glucose stimulates an elevation of DAF-2/insulin-like receptor levels (Fig. 3a), consistent with previous observations using a higher glucose concentration55. Mutants with genetic reduction of function of the insulin-like receptor are long-lived and have delayed reproductive aging15–17,38, but they are still susceptible to lifespan-shortening effects of glucose exposure19,23. Our results were consistent in showing that 20 mM GE significantly reduced the lifespan of daf-2(e1370) mutants (Fig. 3b). Surprisingly, however, daf-2(e1370) worms were completely protected against a glucose-induced reduction of reproductive capacity, even exhibiting resistance to a higher, 40-mM dose of glucose. Since daf-2(-) mutants have longer baseline reproductive spans than wild-type worms, we performed late-mating assays on day 8 and day 10 of adulthood; we found that daf-2(e1370) worms had similar degrees of late-mating reproductive success regardless of whether their adulthoods were spent on NGM, glucose, or 20 mM of the non-metabolizable sugar sorbitol (Fig. 3c, d). Interestingly, when testing another reduction-of-function mutant, daf-2(e1368), we found that this class I mutant was susceptible to glucose and showed similar glucose-induced reductions in late-mating capacity as wild-type worms (Fig. 3e). The daf-2(e1368) allele contains a missense mutation in the ligand-binding domain of the DAF-2 insulin-like receptor and is associated with weaker longevity and stress resistance phenotypes than daf-2(e1370), which harbors a mutation in the tyrosine kinase domain57. These two daf-2 alleles may induce differential effects on transcription factor regulation, as nuclear localization of the DAF-16d/f isoform is enriched in daf-2(e1370) but not daf-2(e1368) mutants58. Interestingly, while daf-2(e1370) worms have significantly higher levels of lipogenesis and triglyceride reserves compared to wild type, this does not appear to be the case for daf-2(e1368) mutants59; we speculate that strain differences in lipid homeostasis and metabolic regulation could potentially contribute towards their divergent reproductive responses to GE. Consistent with their protection against late-mating detriments of glucose, daf-2(e1370) mutants did not respond to glucose enrichment with significantly altered egg or unfertilized oocyte yield at day 3 of adulthood, nor was there a GE-induced loss of their oocyte quality maintenance or overall brood size (despite differences in progeny numbers at the day 2 peak; Fig. 3f–j). Previous studies documented reproductive decline at higher glucose concentrations (250 mM)28, so a higher-dose toxicity may reduce reproductive output. Thus, while insulin-like signaling is important for both somatic aging and reproductive aging, it seems that this pathway might play a greater role in regulating glucose-induced reproductive system effects. This dichotomy aligns with previous observations that somatic and reproductive aging can be uncoupled through genetic manipulations. For instance, temporally restricted daf-2 downregulation after early adulthood extends somatic lifespan without prolonging reproductive span15,60. Lowering daf-2 leads to distinct transcriptional changes in aged oocytes compared to somatic tissues, which further reinforces the concept that insulin-like signaling has diverse regulatory effects for reproductive and somatic tissues15,38. We show here that this compartmentalization persists under metabolically stressful conditions such as glucose enrichment, which disproportionately promotes daf-2(e1370) somatic aging.

DAF-2 signaling in the intestine and body wall promotes glucose-induced reproductive decline

To further investigate the interaction between insulin-like signaling and reproductive function under glucose exposure, we used the auxin-induced degradation (AID) system for precise spatial control of DAF-2 protein levels56. These experiments were performed at day 8 of adulthood so that we could make direct comparisons across the spectrum of reproductive aging rates for groups with and without glucose enrichment and/or DAF-2, while allowing for the possibility of a delay in glucose-induced reproductive damage in worms with reduced insulin-like signaling. Selective DAF-2 degradation in body wall muscle, intestine, germline, or hypodermis could improve the reproductive success of day 8 adult worms under control dietary conditions (i.e., without GE, comparing each strain’s DAF-2 AID group to EtOH-treated controls without DAF-2 degradation; Fig. 4a). However, we found that glucose enrichment lowered reproductive success for most strains (i.e., comparing GE to without-GE for auxin-treated daf-2::degron animals; Fig. 4a). Therefore, worms with germline-specific or hypodermis-specific DAF-2 degradation were still susceptible to glucose’s detrimental impacts on reproductive aging. Notably, though, glucose enrichment did not significantly worsen late-mating capacity if DAF-2 was degraded in either the intestine or the body wall musculature (Fig. 4a). In other words, worm strains with selective DAF-2 degradation in body wall muscle or intestine were protected against glucose-induced reproductive decline. This suggests that the presence of insulin-like signaling in the intestine and muscle allows glucose enrichment to accelerate reproductive aging. We observed that selectively degrading DAF-2 in either of these somatic tissues—but not in the germline itself—was sufficient to improve the late-life fertility of GE-exposed animals (i.e., comparing DAF-2 AID to EtOH-treated controls under glucose-enriched conditions; Fig. 4b). Importantly, targeted DAF-2 degradation in the intestine or body wall musculature also led to improved oocyte quality under glucose enrichment, as indicated by lower frequencies of small, irregularly shaped, or cavity-interspersed oocytes (Fig. 4c–f). Together, our data reveal that DAF-2/insulin-like receptor signaling in the intestine and body wall musculature, two metabolically and energetically important somatic tissues, promotes an acceleration of C. elegans reproductive aging under glucose-enriched conditions. This indicates that suppressing insulin-like signaling in these somatic tissues during glucose exposure can protect the germline against reproductive dysfunction. DAF-16/FOXO, a transcription factor effector in the insulin-like pathway, also acts in the intestine and muscle to extend the reproductive span of daf-2(e1370) mutants on NGM15, so these are important sites for insulin-like signaling to regulate reproductive aging across a range of metabolic conditions. The intestine has emerged as a critical regulator of reproduction in several species. For instance, in Drosophila, intestinal remodeling and metabolic rewiring directly improve fecundity and egg production61, and in mammals, intestinal microbiota influence hormone balance, ovarian follicular atresia, and fertility62. Our findings show that the intestine can also modulate reproductive outcomes during metabolic stress in C. elegans, demonstrating a fundamental consequence of inter-tissue communication in response to nutritional state.

Role of lipid homeostasis in mediating reproductive function under glucose enrichment

Somatic tissues like the intestine can exert impacts on reproductive tissues via numerous mechanisms. This could involve shifting nutrient dynamics, for instance, or altering the production, secretion, and/or degradation of proteins (including histones), nucleic acids (including microRNAs), yolk, metabolites, and signaling molecules that induce responsive changes in the germline and germ cells63–67. To investigate downstream regulators of glucose-induced reproductive decline, we first evaluated transcription factors of the insulin-like signaling pathway with roles in the C. elegans intestine. As expected, a null mutation of daf-16 reduced the late-mating capacity of day 7 adult daf-2(e1370) worms on NGM (Fig. 5a), highlighting the critical function of the DAF-16/FOXO transcription factor in prolonging reproduction in daf-2(e1370) worms under control dietary conditions15–17. However, daf-16(mu86);daf-2(e1370) double mutants did not show a statistically significant reduction in late-mating capacity in response to glucose enrichment (Fig. 5a), suggesting that if DAF-16/FOXO is involved in protecting the reproductive system against glucose, it may only be a partial player. The transcription factor PQM-1 complements DAF-16/FOXO, affects reproductive aging, and regulates metabolic processes in the intestine38,68, but pqm-1 loss of function did not increase daf-2(e1370) susceptibility to GE-induced reproductive damage (Fig. 5b). SKN-1/Nrf2 is transcription factor that is inhibited in response to the DAF-2 signaling pathway; it regulates stress resistance and metabolic pathways through its actions in the intestine64,69. We found that RNAi-mediated reduction of skn-1 significantly lowered the capacity of day 7 adult daf-2(e1370) worms to produce viable progeny under both NGM and GE conditions (Fig. 5c). Late-mating success was too low with skn-1 RNAi to properly assess impacts of NGM versus GE, so we instead tested whether elevated SKN-1 actions might protect against glucose. However, gain of function of a constitutively active skn-1 allele did not preserve late-mating capacity with GE (Fig. 5d). We next considered the possibility that reproductive system responses to nutrient stress might relate to the intestine’s integral role governing metabolic balance and nutrient flux. Vitellogenins are intestine-produced yolk proteins that transport nutrients such as lipids to the germline to support embryonic development70. While we did not detect altered intestinal VIT-2 levels in unmated day 2 adults in response to 20-mM GE during adulthood (Fig. 5e), lifelong exposure to 100 mM glucose has been shown to elevate VIT-2 in intestine and oocytes63. We hypothesized that reducing vitellogenin levels might suppress delivery of lipid-rich particles to the oocytes, thus altering metabolic flux under GE conditions and potentially impacting reproductive aging. While vit-1/2 RNAi or vit-5 RNAi did not significantly alter the late-mating capacity of wild-type worms on NGM, lowering vitellogenin levels exacerbated the detrimental impacts of glucose on reproductive function, since GE-exposed worms had significantly reduced late-mating success with RNAi compared to the L4440 vector (Fig. 5f, g). Glucose-exposed daf-2(e1370) worms, however, did not show an overt response to vit-1/2 RNAi (Fig. 5h). Unexpectedly, we noticed that daf-2(e1370) hermaphrodites reared on HT115 (the E. coli strain typically used in RNAi experiments) showed a marked enhancement of late-mating capacity with GE (Fig. 5c, h, i), an exacerbation of an otherwise subtle trend and a pattern that we did not detect in N2 worms on HT115 (Fig. 5e, g). Although we did not directly compare effects of E. coli strains in our experiments, HT115 has higher levels of carbohydrates and vitamin B12 than the standard laboratory food source of OP50, and it may facilitate relatively greater stress tolerance and lower triacylglycerol stores71,72. Under these conditions, daf-2(e1370) worms appear to benefit from glucose enrichment, perhaps due to their distinctive regulation of metabolism and lipid homeostasis. Notably, we found that disrupting lipolysis by lowering lipl-4 levels eliminated the GE-induced benefits on daf-2(e1370) reproductive function. The lysosomal lipase lipl-4 is an intestinally expressed gene with roles in regulating lipid mobilization and longevity; it is elevated in daf-2(e1370) mutants73 and was previously shown to be highly upregulated in response to glucose enrichment in N2 hermaphrodites, part of a suite of lipid and carbohydrate metabolism genes74. Although lipl-4 RNAi had no obvious effects under NGM conditions, we found that an RNAi-mediated reduction of lipl-4 significantly reduced the late-mating capacity of glucose-exposed daf-2(e1370) worms, compared to the GE group on the L4440 empty vector (Fig. 5i). GE did not reduce the late-mating success of lipl-4(RNAi);daf-2(e1370) worms, but with lipl-4(RNAi) the daf-2(e1370) worms did not show the glucose-induced rise in reproductive function that we observed on E. coli HT115 with L4440 (Fig. 5i). Therefore, the presence of the lipase LIPL-4 helps enhance daf-2(e1370) reproductive function under glucose enrichment. Based on these collective results, we speculate that inter-tissue nutrient dynamics and lipid homeostasis may contribute towards mediating reproductive system responses to glucose enrichment.

In closing, for this study C. elegans were exposed to glucose enrichment to explore the impacts of nutrient excess on reproductive aging. We found that glucose exposure during adulthood accelerates not only somatic aging but also reproductive aging. We also discovered that the insulin-like signaling pathway plays a crucial role in driving the glucose-induced age-related reproductive decline, through its non-autonomous actions in energetically dynamic C. elegans somatic tissues. An impaired capacity to mobilize nutrients such as lipids from these somatic tissues may be particularly detrimental for reproductive function under conditions of glucose enrichment. While lowering DAF-2/insulin-like receptor signaling prevents the glucose-induced acceleration of reproductive aging, it does so without protecting against lifespan-shortening effects, which demonstrates a regulatory dichotomy in the mechanisms by which GE worsens reproductive system and somatic tissue functions. These findings suggest that metabolically unhealthy states associated with chronic overnutrition and upregulated somatic-tissue insulin signaling may exacerbate the age-related decline of the female reproductive system.

Auxin-induced degradation (AID)

Auxin-containing plates were prepared to a final concentration of 1 mM as previously described56,76. In brief, a 400 mM stock solution was prepared by dissolving auxin (indole-3-acetic acid, Sigma #I3750) in ethanol and stored at 4 °C. Auxin was added to NGM agar cooled down to 60 °C before pouring plates. Control plates for the auxin experiments had the same amount of ethanol (0.25%). Auxin plates were stored covered in aluminum foil to prevent light exposure.

Lifespan assays

Hypochlorite-synchronized worms were first transferred onto glucose-enriched plates (or NGM control plates) at the L4 larval stage, and maintained in groups throughout their lifespans. Days of adulthood were considered to start with L4 stage as t = 0 and the next day as day 1. The worms were transferred to new plates every two days during their reproductive period. If gentle prodding produced no response in an animal, it was assessed as dead. Worms were censored if they died of non-ageing related deaths such as contamination, internal hatching (bagging), or desiccation from climbing the wall of the petri plate.

Late-mating assays

Late-mating assays were conducted as previously described77. Groups of hypochlorite-synchronized worms were first transferred onto glucose-enriched plates (or NGM control plates) at the L4 larval stage and then transferred between fresh plates until they were aged to the late-mating time point, when individual hermaphrodites were distributed onto separate 35 mm plates along with three day1 adult fog-2(q71) males per plate. Presence or absence of progeny were scored 2–5 days post-mating; plates with dead or missing hermaphrodites, absent males, or contamination were censored. Plates with internal hatching (bagging) were not censored in these assays; bagged worms were scored as reproductively capable. Independent replicates of these late-mating assays (conducted on different days) showed some variation in terms of the precise percentage value of population fertility on a given day of adulthood; importantly, however, relative differences between experimental groups remained robust for each replicate. Therefore, it is important to limit interpretations of these data to between-group comparisons within each experiment.

Brood size and egg lay assays

For mated brood size counts, hypochlorite-synchronized L4 larvae were individually placed on 35 mm glucose-enriched or NGM plates, and young males were added in a 1:3 ratio. Mating the worms with young males increases the likelihood of a continuous supply of sperm, allowing reproductive output to better reflect oocyte quality rather than sperm availability; however, it should be noted that the possibility of glucose-induced effects on spermatheca-stored sperm still remains. The mated hermaphrodites were separated from males and transferred to fresh plates every 24 h for the entirety of their reproductive span, and successful mating was confirmed through the continuous production of male progeny (i.e., cross-progeny) throughout the assay. Plates with dead or missing hermaphrodites or contamination were censored. After hermaphrodite removal, plates were kept at 20 °C and progeny was counted the next day.

For egg lay assays, 10–12 unmated N2 and daf-2(e1370) hermaphrodites raised on either NGM or glucose-supplemented plates from L4 onward were individually transferred to 35 mm plates and allowed to lay eggs undisturbed for 3 h at 20 °C. After the egg laying period, the hermaphrodite was removed and the total number of eggs and unfertilized oocytes laid per worm were counted.

Germ cell numbers and apoptotic cell corpses

To quantify the number of germ cells, L4-staged CED-1::GFP hermaphrodites on either NGM or 20 mM glucose were mated with young males at a 1:3 ratio for 24 h. On day 4 of adulthood, worms were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) as previously described78. Briefly, worms were fixed in ice-cold methanol for 5 min and incubated in 500 ng/mL DAPI solution in the dark for 30 min. Worms were then washed with M9 and mounted on slides for imaging on a Nikon Ti2 microscope. DAPI-stained worms were imaged as Z-stacks at 40x magnification. Z-stack images were pre-processed in FIJI by applying local contrast enhancement to the distal gonad, then cell segmentation was performed using Cellpose v3 (cyto3 model) in Cell-ACDC79,80 with consistent parameters applied across all images. Segmented images were blinded, manually reviewed, and assigned a quality score; the germ cell counts for worms with poor or unreliable segmentation were excluded from analysis. To quantify apoptotic germ cells, z-stacks were blinded, and the number of visible apoptotic rings marked by CED-1::GFP in the pachytene region was manually counted as previously described81.

Oocyte morphology – imaging and analyses

Oocyte quality measurements were performed as previously described15,38. Hypochlorite-synchronized worms were exposed to either glucose enrichment or NGM starting at the L4 stage. L4 larvae were plated at a 1:3 ratio with day 1 males for 48 h of mating, and then successfully mated hermaphrodites (indicated by the subsequent presence of male progeny on their individual plates) were transferred between fresh plates until imaging on day 5 or day 8 of adulthood. On imaging day, worms were anesthetized with levamisole on 2% agar pads. 7–8 worms were imaged per slide on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2-E microscope using differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy. Images were blinded and randomized prior to scoring criteria related to the phenotypes of oocyte shape, size, and connectivity. In brief, oocytes were scored as normally shaped if they were all uniformly cuboidal; mildly misshapen if there was a lack of this cuboidal shape; and severely misshapen if oocytes were visibly damaged or very irregular in shape. For the small oocyte phenotype, normal indicated that all oocyte cells filled the gonad arm and contacted the body wall; mild indicated oocytes that did not fill the gonad arm or contact the body wall; and severe indicated the presence of some miniscule oocytes. For the cavity phenotype, normal indicated no discernible cavities between oocytes; mild indicated some loss of contact between oocytes; and severe indicated the presence of distinct spaces between oocytes.

Mitochondrial morphology assay

For the mitochondrial morphology evaluations, hypochlorite-synchronized mex-5p:tomm-20::gfp worms were exposed to either glucose enrichment or NGM starting at the L4 stage, when they were plated at a 1:3 ratio with day 1 fog-2(q71) males for 24 h of mating. Hermaphrodites were then transferred between fresh plates every other day to prevent progeny accumulation. Upon reaching day 5 of adulthood, mex-5p:tomm-20::gfp worms were transferred onto 2% agar pads and fixed for 10 min in 3% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by rinsing three times with phosphate buffered saline. Oocytes were imaged using confocal microscopy with a Nikon Eclipse C2 confocal microscope. Single images and Z-stacks were taken at 60x magnification, and 2 µm slices were taken for each Z stack.

For mitochondrial analyses, images were first blinded and randomized, then the -1 oocyte of each image was scored as elongated, mixed, or punctate for mitochondrial morphology. The elongated category was indicated by oocytes with greater than half of their mitochondria being elongated, characterized by network-like structure and extensive connections with surrounding mitochondria. The mixed category was indicated by oocytes with approximately half of their mitochondria being elongated and half being punctate. The punctate category was indicated by oocytes with more than half of their mitochondria being punctate, characterized by circular morphology and a high degree of separation between mitochondria.

ROS assay

Hypochlorite-synchronized N2 worms were placed on NGM or 20 mM glucose plates as L4 larvae and mated with day 1 fog-2(q71) males for 24 h. On day 5 of adulthood, worms were anesthetized with 0.4% levamisole and gonads were dissected using two 29-gauge needles attached to 1 mL syringes. Gonads were dissected by cutting the head off the worm in a scissor-like motion just below the pharynx, which resulted in complete or partial expulsion of the gonad. Gonads were incubated in 50 nM 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) diluted in M9 for 10 min, followed by fixation for five minutes in 1% PFA, and rinsing three times with M9 buffer. Gonads were mounted onto 2% agar pads and imaged using fluorescence and DIC microscopy on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2-E at 40x magnification. Approximately 30 worms were imaged per experimental condition per independent replicate. Fluorescence intensity of the -1 oocytes (with background fluorescence subtracted) was analyzed using NIS elements software.

Fluorescence imaging of DAF-2 and VIT-2 levels

MQD2428 hermaphrodites with a degron and mNeonGreen tag at the 3’ end of the endogenous daf-2 gene locus56 and BCN9071 hermaphrodites with a GFP tag on the C terminus of the vit-2 gene82 were anesthetized and placed on 2% agar pad after 2 days of adulthood spent on either 20 mM glucose-NGM or NGM alone. Fluorescence imaging was conducted on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2-E microscope at 20x magnification, and a minimum of 20–30 worms were imaged per strain and condition. Z stacks were acquired with a slice separation of 0.6 μm, and maximal intensity projection images were analyzed using NIS elements software by subtracting mean fluorescence intensities from background fluorescence. For VIT-2::GFP analyses, an additional non-fluorescent image that included the spermatheca was taken without changing worm position. The region of interest (ROI) for each worm was defined on this image as the anterior portion of the worm until the spermatheca; the ROI was copied to the maximal intensity projection images for fluorescence quantification.

Oil-Red-O staining

Mated hermaphrodites were stained with Oil-Red-O to quantify lipid presence and distribution according to the protocol by O’Rourke et al.83. Briefly, hypochlorite-synchronized N2 worms were placed on NGM or 20 mM glucose plates as L4 larvae and mated with day 1 fog-2(q71) males for 48 h. Worms were collected in M9, washed in 60% freshly prepared isopropanol, and stained with filtered Oil-Red-O solution for 18 h. Stained worms were then washed with 0.01% Triton X-100 in S buffer and imaged using color imaging on the Nikon Eclipse Ti2-E microscope. For gonad staining, hermaphrodite gonads were dissected from day 2 adult worms as outlined in the ROS assay protocol, and were fixed in PFA before being stained with Oil-Red-O and washed with 0.01% Triton X-100 as previously described84. ImageJ was used to quantify the spread of the red stain over the area of the first 3 proximal oocytes.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed in at least three independent populations that were raised from separate starting populations, typically on different days. Lifespan assays were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves (log-rank tests). Oocyte quality was assessed using Fischer’s exact tests to identify whether experimental populations differed with respect to the proportions of oocytes scored as having severe/mild/normal phenotypes (categorical variables) for each gross morphological trait, which were scored in blinded and randomized images according to predetermined criteria, as described above. Differences in late-mating success and mitochondrial morphology across all replicate populations were determined using Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel tests with Benjamini-Hochberg corrections applied when adjusting for multiple comparisons. Progeny counts and germline cell numbers were evaluated with independent two-tailed t-tests. Egg and oocyte yield, CED-1::GFP apoptotic rings, fluorescence intensities, and Oil-Red-O coverage were analysed using two-tailed Mann-Whitney U tests. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10 and R Studio with R Version 4.5.0.
