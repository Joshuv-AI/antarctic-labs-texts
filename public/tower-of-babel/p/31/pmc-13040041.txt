Laser fluence dependent antibacterial activity of cadmium sulfide quantum dots prepared by one step laser ablation in liquid
Authors: Karrar M Hassan, Ali A Taha, Raid A Ismail, Mayyadah H Mohsin, M H Eisa, O Aldaghri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCadmium sulfide (CdS) quantum dots (QDs) were synthesized using pulsed laser ablation in liquid (PLAL) at different laser fluences. The effect of laser fluence on the optical, structural, morphological, and electrical properties of CdS QDs was investigated by using X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX), zeta potential, photoluminescence (PL), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), UV–Visible absorption spectroscopy, and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). XRD analysis show that the synthesized CdS QDs have a hexagonal wurzite structure and the crystallite size was in the range of 6–11 nm. They also exhibit negative surface charge about − 2.7, − 18.2, and − 19.0 mV, all that for CdS QDs, which were prepared at 1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm², laser fluences, respectively. The antibacterial activity of the CdS QDs was evaluated against both Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa) and Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae). The results showed varying inhibition zones depending on the concentration, surface deformation, and particle size of the CdS QDs, in which the smallest size QDs, which were prepared by using 7.64 J/cm² laser fluence, show higher inhibition zones than other particles. In addition, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined for each bacterial strain.Keywords: CdS, PLAL, QDs, Laser fluence, Antibacterial activity, MICSubject terms: Materials science, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The drug resistance of the bacterial is rapidly evolving and has develop into a major global public health threat, representation many infections increasingly difficult to treat with conventional antibiotics1. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is now considered the main cause of severe infections, morbidity, and mortality worldwide2. Bacteria can evolve complex defensive mechanisms by using a variety of mechanisms to withstand the effects of antibiotics, such as the production of target protection proteins and morphological alterations. Higher dosages of antibiotics are therefore frequently needed, which can have serious adverse effects and ultimately make treatment useless. Alternative therapeutic strategies are therefore desperately needed3.

A promising area of biomedicine, nanotechnology offers novel treatments for a range of physiological, metabolic, and biochemical conditions4. Due to quantum confinement effects, semiconductor QDs have special physicochemical characteristics among nanomaterials, especially when their size gets close to the Bohr exciton radius. Both photoluminescence and absorption spectra show a blue shift as a result of this phenomenon, which results in tunable optical properties5. One of the II–VI semiconductor group, CdS has a direct energy gap of 2.42 eV at ambient temperature6. Its size-dependent characteristics and capacity to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) when exposed to light have drawn a lot of attention. These ROS, which include superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals, can harm bacterial lipids, proteins, and DNA, resulting in cell death7,8, and9. Cadmium ions (Cd²), which break cell membranes, interfere with enzyme function, and damage DNA, can also be released by CdS QDs and increase antibacterial activity10. Because of this, CdS QDs are efficient against both Gram-positiveand Gram-negative bacteria, including those that are resistant to multiple drugs.

The preparation of CdS quantum dots using different chemical or environmentally friendly preparation methods and their antibacterial activity has been documented in a number of earlier studies. For instance, biosynthesized utilizing green tea extract CdS QDs with a size ≈ of 2–4 nm show antibacterial activity against both Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecalis; however, this preparation method consumes about 36 h, and their results particle does not exhibit high purity. The particles may be coated with residual phenolic compounds, proteins, and amino acids11. Another study showed that under visible-light photocatalytic conditions, CdS QDs with size < 10 nm supported on mesoporous silicates achieved 100% bacterial growth suppression against E. coli and S. aureus at concentrations between 63 and 125 µg/mL. In addition, this preparation method exhibits some disadvantages, such as their uses of many chemicals, such as CTAB, TEOS, and Pluronic. Moreover, it is associated with a high preparationcost12. Green synthesis of CdS QDs using Aspergillus niger prepared a particle size range of 2–10 nm, with a concentration of about 100 µg/mL, which exhibits antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Proteus vulgaris. However, this method has limitations to uses due to its higher time consuming about 9 − 7 days13.

The antibacterial effectiveness of CdS quantum dots made chemically or environmentally has been documented in a number of earlier research. For instance, Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecalis were significantly inhibited by biosynthesized CdS QDs (≈ 2–4 nm, utilizing green tea extract)14. Another work showed that under visible-light photocatalytic conditions, CdS QDs (< 10 nm) supported on mesoporous silicates achieved 100% bacterial growth suppression against E. coli and S. aureus at concentrations between 63 and 125 µg/mL15. These studies demonstrate how light activation and smaller particle sizes (< 10 nm) significantly increase antibacterial activity. Our pulsed laser ablation in liquid (PLAL) method produces surfactant-free CdS QDs with regulated size and phase, providing a more straightforward and environmentally friendly option for biomedical applications. However, such methods sometimes include stabilizing supports or more complicated synthesis procedures. Wet chemical procedures, solvothermal processes, microwave irradiation, sol–gel methods, block copolymer templating, chemical growth, and laser ablation in liquids are just a few of the synthetic strategies used to create CdS QDs. Among these, pulsed laser ablation in liquid (PLAL) has drawn interest because of its ease of use16, lack of surfactants, and capacity to regulate particle size and shape17,18. The properties of the synthesized nanoparticles are found to be depended on several laser parameters, for instance, fluence, wavelength, pulse duration, pulse repetition frequency. Furthermore, the type of liquid medium plays also an essential role on the morphology of the product. The antibacterial activity of all synthesized CdS QDs was assessed and compared, and the MIC was determined for each formulation.

Despite the promising antimicrobial potential of CdS QDs, most reported synthesis approaches rely on complex chemical methods that often require surfactants, stabilizers, or toxic reagents, which can limit their biomedical applicability. Moreover, limited studies have evaluated the antibacterial activity of CdS QDs synthesized via green, surfactant-free techniques such as PLAL, particularly against clinically isolated multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial strains. Here we report on proposed green and low-cost method for synthesizing CdS QDs using pulsed laser ablation in liquid under different laser fluences. This approach enables the production of high-purity, surfactant-free QDs with tunable sizes and optical properties. The synthesized QDs were thoroughly characterized and tested for their antibacterial efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, including the determination of MIC values. This work provides a sustainable nanomaterial-based strategy to combat antibiotic resistance using laser-synthesized quantum dots. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first comprehensive study of how laser fluence determines the antibacterial effectiveness of CdS QDs that are entirely surfactant-free. We remove the confounding effects of surface-masking chemicals that are frequently observed in earlier studies by using PLAL. This creates a pure, “green” standard for next-generation nanomedicine by enabling an unprecedented, direct link between laser-induced size tuning and the MIC against high-priority MDR clinical isolates.

Materials and methodology

Materials

The materials and chemicals used in this study were commercially available and used without further purification. CdS powder with 99.9% purity was obtained from MERCK, and resazurin dye was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Muller-Hinton agar was obtained from Accumix, and nutrient broth was supplied by a Spanish company. Deionized water used in the experiments was provided by the Baghdad Company, Baghdad, Iraq.

Preparation of CdS QDs

An Nd: YAG laser (PicoSure – picosecond laser system, model PL755) was used to ablate CdS in deionized water at a repetition rate of 2 Hz. The laser beam (1 mm) was focused onto CdS powder (> 99% purity) to generate CdS QDs. Ten milliliters of deionized water were placed in a beaker with the powder. The beaker was covered with a lid with an opening equal to the diameter of the laser beam in order to reduce water leakage and safeguard the convex lens. A colloidal CdS QD solution was created by using a magnetic stirrer to maintain uniform dispersion during ablation. For the synthesis of CdS QDs, laser pulses with different fluences (1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm²) at a wavelength of 1064 nm with 150 pulses for each sample and a pulse duration of 7 ns were used. The schematic diagram of pulsed laser ablation in liquid is shown in Fig. 1.

Experimental Setup for the synthesis of CdS QDs and photographs of the prepared solutions before and after laser irradiation.

Characterization of CdS QDs

UV-Visible spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer, Lambda 365, USA) was used to measure the prepared samples’ maximum absorption wavelength (λmax). A PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer (USA) was used to determine the samples’ elemental makeup. The Brookhaven Zeta PALS equipment (HORIBA, SZ100, Japan) was used to detect zeta potential in order to evaluate colloidal stability. The crystal structure of the CdS QDs was examined using X-ray diffractometer (PHILIPS PW1730). FESEM (MIRA 3 TESCAN microscope) equipped with EDX system (EDAX, Mahwah, NJ, USA) were employed to investigate the surface morphology and chemical composition of the nanoparticles. TEM was performed with a PHILIPS CM120 microscope to investigate the particle size and morphology. The photoluminescence (PL) of the samples was measured using a FluoroMate FS-2 spectrofluorometer (Scinco, USA), which confirmed their quantum dot behavior through emission under excitation19.

Bacterial isolates

Both Gram-negative bacteria isolates Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), and Gram-positive isolates Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Streptococcus agalactiae (S. agalactiae) were collected from patients attending Al-Kindi Teaching Hospital in Baghdad, Iraq. Clinical samples were directly streaked onto selective and differential media, including MacConkey agar, mannitol salt agar, blood agar, and nutrient agar. The cultured plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacterial identification was performed using the automated VITEK system (Biomérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France).

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee at the Department of Applied Sciences, University of Technology, and Al-Kindi Teaching Hospital, Baghdad, Iraq (Approval No. BCSR13, dated April 21, 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study procedures followed the guidelines outlined by the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1975).

Antibacterial activity of CdS QDs

The antibacterial activity of the synthesized CdS QDs was evaluated using the well-diffusion method against four clinical bacterial strains: Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Staphylococcus aureus. The clinical isolates were first inoculated into nutrient broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. Subsequently, the bacterial suspensions were uniformly swabbed onto the surface of Muller-Hinton agar plates using sterile cotton swabs. Wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically punched into the agar. 50 µL of each of the three concentrations (360, 400, and 500 µg/mL) of CdS QDs, which were produced using three distinct laser fluences (1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm²) was added to each well. After that, the plates were incubated for a full day at 37 °C. Following incubation, the antibacterial efficiency was evaluated by measuring the zones of inhibition in millimeters using a sterile ruler.

Evaluation of the MICs CdS QDs

Gram-positive (S. aureus, S. agalactiae) and Gram-negative (E. coli, P. aeruginosa) bacterial strains were used to assess the MIC of CdS QDs.

To carry out consistent inoculum density, bacterial cultures were cultivated in nutrient broth at 37 °C for 24 h before being diluted to meet the turbidity of a 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5*108 CFU/ml). 100 µL of sterile nutrient broth (free of microorganisms) and 100 µL of serially diluted CdS QDs solutions were applied to each well of a sterile 96-well microtiter plate. Each well was then filled with 10 µL of the prepared bacterial suspension, with the exception of the negative control, which simply contained nutritional broth. For a whole day, the plates were incubated at 37 °C. Following incubation, 20 µL of resazurin dye solution was added to each well to assess bacterial viability. Plates were incubated for an additional 4 h under the same conditions. A color change from blue to pink indicated bacterial growth, while the lowest concentration that maintained the blue color was recorded as the MIC20.

Results and discussion

Figure 2 shows the UV–Vis absorption spectra of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences (1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm²). The spectra exhibit broad absorption in the range of 250–800 nm, with distinct peaks observed at approximately 214 nm, 212 nm, and 210 nm for samples prepared at 1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm², respectively. These absorption peaks are indicative of size-dependent optical properties and are attributed to the quantum confinement effect. The color of the prepared CdS QDs colloidal suspension was found to vary with the applied laser fluence. At low laser fluence, the suspension appeared light yellow, while increasing the fluence led to a darker yellow coloration, as illustrated in the inset of Fig. 2. This color change is likely attributed to variations in particle size and concentration of CdS QDs induced by the laser ablation energy21. This result demonstrated a blue shift, when compared to the absorption peak of 520 nm for CdS bulk material. This phenomenon is attributed to quantum confinement effect at the CdS QDs22,23, and24.

Figure 4 illustrates the photoluminescence (PL) spectra of CdS quantum dots (QDs) synthesized via pulsed laser ablation in deionized water at three laser fluences: 1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm². A noticeable enhancement in PL intensity is observed with increasing laser energy, with the 7.64 J/cm² sample exhibiting the most intense and broadest emission. This behavior is attributed to improved crystallinity and higher defect density at elevated ablation energies, which provide more radiative recombination centers.

A PL spectra of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences, B photograph image of sample synthesis by 1.27 J/cm2 irradiation under visible light and long UV light.

The emission bands in the region of 350–450 nm can be attributed to the radiative recombination, which confirms that the quantum confinement results in a blue-shifted CdS QDs energy gap compared to that of the bulk CdS, which agrees with optical absorption results. This strong peak indicates the high crystallinity of the product. The band observed in the range of 480–600 nm belongs to structural defects accompanying the ablation process, which come from the dangling bonds, sulfur vacancies, and Cd interstitials. While the band observed in the spectral region of 650–840 nm can be related to the deep traps and incomplete stoichiometric CdS. The sample synthesized at 7.64 J/cm2 shows a red shift and significant PL broadening, suggesting an increase in particle size and reduced quantum confinement effects due to the formation of a high density of surface states26,27. These results confirm that laser energy fluence is a crucial parameter in tailoring the optical properties of CdS QDs, with higher energies favoring enhanced luminescence, making such QDs suitable for photonic and optoelectronic applications. The broad PL spectrum observed (Fig. 4) of CdS QDs is typically attributed to the superposition of band-edge emission and radiative recombination from a distribution of surface and intrinsic trap states28,29. The dramatic increase in intensity with higher laser fluence is consistent with the phenomenon of photo-induced passivation reported for similar systems30. In this process, the supplied optical energy likely promotes the chemical restructuring or oxidation of the NPs’ surface, effectively reducing the density of non-radiative trap states of sulfur vacancies or unsaturated bonds28,30. Consequently, a greater proportion of photo-excited charge carriers recombine radiatively, leading to the observed enhancement in integrated PL intensity without a substantial blue-shift, indicating that the quantum-confined core remains intact while the surface is modified29,30.

Figure 5 presents the FT-IR of CdS QDs synthesized by laser ablation in deionized water under different laser fluences. The spectra exhibit distinct absorption bands at 3271.62 cm⁻¹, 1634.87 cm⁻¹, 530.21 cm⁻¹, and 514.94 cm⁻¹. The broad peak at 3271.62 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the O–H stretching vibration, indicating the presence of hydroxyl groups or adsorbed water molecules on the particle surface. The band at 1634.87 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the bending vibrations of water molecules or Cd–O–H interactions. The production of CdS QDs is confirmed by the absorption peaks at 530.21 cm⁻¹ and 514.94 cm⁻¹, which are ascribed to the stretching vibrations of Cd–S bonds31. The FTIR spectra provide critical indirect evidence for CdS QD formation. The distinct absorption band at 400–500 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the Cd-S stretching vibration within the lattice, confirming the primary chemical bond formation. Furthermore, the presence of broad O-H stretching (~ 3400 cm⁻¹) and O-H bending (~ 1630 cm⁻¹) modes, along with organic functional groups (C-O, C-H), indicates the surface-bound capping agents and hydroxyl groups that stabilize the nanoparticles against aggregation. This surface coordination is essential for maintaining the quantum-confined state by preventing particle growth and Ostwald ripening, as reported in recent studies on colloidal QD synthesis32,33.

FTIR spectra of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences (1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm²), showing characteristic peaks corresponding to O–H and Cd–O–H surface groups, as well as Cd–S bond vibrations, confirming the successful formation of CdS QDs.

The effect of laser fluence on the XRD patterns of the prepared CdS QDs is depicted in Fig. 6. The XRD pattern of CdS powder exhibits crystalline hexagonal CdS with peaks of high intensity. The XRD of CdS synthesized at various laser fluences show all the diffraction peaks belong to the crystalline hexagonal wurzite structure of CdS according to JCPDS No: 41–1049. All patterns exhibit distinct diffraction peaks located at 2θ corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (112), (201), (203), (212), and (204) planes, respectively26. The broadening in XRD peaks of the synthesized CdS, compared to CdS powder, is mainly attributed to quantum confinement effect and the high surface-to-volume ratio of the nanocrystals. Particularly, an intense peak centered on 2θ = 26–28° is observed in all samples, which corresponded to the (002) plane (c-axis plane) of hexagonal CdS34. On the other hand, the presence of peak at 2θ = 32° corresponding to (101) plane for all synthesized CdS confirming the formation of CdS QDs. Actually, increasing the laser fluence results in rapid melting followed by ultrafast quenching, creating strong non-equilibrium growth conditions due to the higher plasma temperature and shockwave intensity. Figure 7 presents FESEM images of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences, highlighting the strong dependence of particle size and morphology on laser fluence. At a low fluence of 1.27 J/cm² (Fig. 7a), the CdS QDs appeared formation of small irregular and non-dispersed nanoparticles. Increases the laser fluence to 3.82 J/cm² (Fig. 7b), results in growth of the nanoparticles and formed larger particles due to the ablation of larger volume16,35–37. As shown, some agglomerated and elongated nanoparticles were observed due to increasing the cooling rate (quenching)38. Actually, the high surface energy of the nanoparticles and van der Waal are the main reasons cause the particle agglomeration.

The TEM images of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences are shown in Fig. 7 (d, e, and f). At 1.27 J/cm² (Fig. 7d), the TEM image confirms the presence of nanoparticles that maintained a mostly rounded morphology with improved edge definition and moderate dispersion, although some aggregation remained. The nanoparticles appear predominantly spherical with evident aggregation in some regions. Increasing the laser fluence exhibited more uniform nanoparticles and minimal agglomeration. The average particle sizes determined from the TEM images were approximately 2, 2.86, and 2.73 nm, for the samples synthesized with 1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm², respectively. These values are slightly differ than those obtained from XRD analysis and to that calculated from the Brus equation. The reason beyond this finding can be attributed to the fundamental differences between the two techniques: XRD estimates crystallite size based on coherent diffraction domains free of defects, while TEM directly visualizes the overall particle dimensions despite of crystallinity or structural defects39, 40.

The particle size distribution (PSD) of CdS quantum dots synthesized at different laser fluences was estimated from TEM images with aid of image J software exhibits a narrow size spread for all samples, with the fitted curves closely following Gaussian distributions. The average particle sizes for samples prepared at laser fluences of 1.27, 3.82, and 7.64 J/cm² were 2.5 nm, 1.7 nm, and 2 nm, respectively. As shown in Fig. 7g, h, and I, the average particle size obtained by from SEM with aid of image J software was larger than those obtained from TEM due to particle agglomeration due to the effect of agglomeration of the nanoparticles.

The EDX analysis of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences, as shown in Fig. 8, reveals distinct and strong peaks corresponding to cadmium (Cd) and sulfur (S) elements, confirming the successful formation of CdS as the dominant compound in all samples as shown in Fig. 8a-c. Additionally, the presence of an oxygen (O) signal in the spectra is likely attributed to surface-adsorbed oxygen or water molecules from the aqueous medium used during the laser ablation process40.

Zeta potential measurements offer crucial information about the stability and surface charge properties of the produced CdS QDs, as illustrated in Fig. 9. A negative zeta potential of − 2.7 mV was found at a laser fluence of 1.27 J/cm² (Fig. 9a), showing limited colloidal stability and a significant tendency for agglomeration. A considerably negative zeta potential of − 18.2 mV was obtained by increasing the laser fluence to 3.82 J/cm² (Fig. 9b), which was linked to smaller and more homogeneous nanoparticles, as depicted in Fig. 7b. Under physiological conditions, aggregation is achievable due to the mild surface charge, which suggests limited electrostatic repulsion. The zeta potential moved marginally to − 19.0 mV at the maximum laser fluence of 7.64 J/cm² (Fig. 9c), which is consistent with their bigger particle size as seen in Fig. 7c.

Zeta potential of CdS QDs synthesized at different laser fluences: a–c 1.27, 3.82, and 7.64/cm2, respectively.

These findings show that increased laser fluences increase the negative surface charge and stabilize the colloidal CdS QDs. Despite, the average particle size increases as laser fluence increases, the stability of CdS colloid was improved as a result to the increase in the magnitude and the narrowing of the zeta potential distribution, as shown in Fig. 9 (a–c). The electrostatic repulsion can govern the stability of the colloid rather than particle size alone; therefore, higher absolute zeta potential values improve repulsive interactions between CdS nanoparticles and suppress agglomeration and aggregation of the nanoparticles, in accordance with DLVO theory. Additionally, the measured particle size represents the hydrodynamic diameter, which may increase due to surface adsorption layers, solvation effects, or changes in surface chemistry rather than intrinsic particle growth. Consequently, improved stability can be achieved even at larger hydrodynamic sizes when sufficiently high surface charge density is present41,42.

Particle size, concentration, surface activity, surface deformation, and their capacity to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) can all be linked to variations in the antibacterial efficiency of QDs.The smaller particle size of QDs allows them to interact with the negative charge of the cell wall cadmium ruptures the cell wall. Moreover, the bacterial-growth-signaling system is impacted by QDs, which would impair the viability of the cell44,45. The positive charge of CdS QDs forms electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged proteins on the surface of bacterial cells as they travel through the membrane, altering the internal structure of bacterial cells46. When the thiol groups in proteins interact with the released ions from the QDs, ROS are created in excessive amounts, such as hydrogen peroxide, superoxide anion, hydroxyl radical, and singlet oxygen, which can damage biomolecules and organelle structures because of their high oxidation potential47. This damage includes protein oxidative carbonylation, lipid peroxidation, DNA/RNA breakage, enzyme inhibition48, and causes damage to cell walls and membranes by breaking the carbonyl group of peptide bonds49, all of which can result in necrosis, apoptosis, or even mutagenesis. Upon binding CdS QDs to the protein layer, inhibition of active transport, dehydrogenase, and enzymatic activity occurs in the periplasm zone, thus inhibiting the synthesis of DNA, RNA, and proteins, followed by cell lysis40,50–52. Finally, we should mention the environmental and toxicological concerns that arise from using the CdS quantum dot, which will be determined in a future study.

In comparison to those prepared at 1.27 and 3.82 J/cm², the MIC results shown in Table 4; Fig. 11 demonstrate the increased antibacterial efficacy of CdS QDs synthesized using a 7.64 J/cm² laser fluence against both Gram-positive (S. aureus, S. agalactiae) and Gram-negative (E. coli, P. aeruginosa) bacterial strains. The CdS QDs synthesized with 7.64 J/cm² exhibited the lowest MIC values, indicating higher antibacterial activity, with MICs 7.8, 15.6, 31.25, and 62.5 µg/mL for S. agalactiae, S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, respectively. This can be ascribed to the better stability of the colloids compared to those prepared at lower laser fluences53,54.

Conclusion

Pulsed laser ablation in liquid technique was successfully used to synthesize high purity CdS QDs without using a catalyst. The effect of laser fluence on structural, optical, and antibacterial activity of the CdS QDs was studied. XRD analysis confirmed the formation of a hexagonal wurzite crystalline phase, with crystallite sizes in the range of 6–11 nm. TEM analysis showed the formation of spherical nanoparticles with a particle size in the range of 2–2.8 nm. EDX analysis confirmed the presence of cadmium and sulfur as the main constituents of the CdS QDs. The optical characterization showed a notable decrease in the optical gap energy as the laser fluence increased. Agglomeration of the nanoparticles was observed when ablation occurred at high laser fluence. The antibacterial activity of the CdS QDs was investigated and was found to be improved with increasing of the laser fluence. The largest inhibition zone was found for QDs synthesized at 7.64 J/cm²/pulse, particularly against S. agalactiae, while P. aeruginosa. MIC evaluation further supported these findings, showing the lowest MIC values for higher concentration and surface deformation CdS QDs. According to the obtained data, laser fluence is a crucial factor in modifying the structural, optical, and biological characteristics of CdS QDs, allowing for their possible use in nanoscale antimicrobial agents. These discoveries will make it possible to incorporate CdS QDs into multipurpose platforms for numerous significant uses, including photocatalytic disinfection systems, targeted medication delivery, and biosensing.
