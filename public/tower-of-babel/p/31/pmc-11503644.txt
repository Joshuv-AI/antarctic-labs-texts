Constructing the dynamic transcriptional regulatory networks to identify phenotype-specific transcription regulators
Authors: Yang Guo, Zhiqiang Xiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transcriptional regulatory network (TRN) is a graph framework that helps understand the complex transcriptional regulation mechanisms in the transcription process. Identifying the phenotype-specific transcription regulators is vital to reveal the functional roles of transcription elements in associating the specific phenotypes. Although many methods have been developed towards detecting the phenotype-specific transcription elements based on the static TRN in the past decade, most of them are not satisfactory for elucidating the phenotype-related functional roles of transcription regulators in multiple levels, as the dynamic characteristics of transcription regulators are usually ignored in static models. In this study, we introduce a novel framework called DTGN to identify the phenotype-specific transcription factors (TFs) and pathways by constructing dynamic TRNs. We first design a graph autoencoder model to integrate the phenotype-oriented time-series gene expression data and static TRN to learn the temporal representations of genes. Then, based on the learned temporal representations of genes, we develop a statistical method to construct a series of dynamic TRNs associated with the development of specific phenotypes. Finally, we identify the phenotype-specific TFs and pathways from the constructed dynamic TRNs. Results from multiple phenotypic datasets show that the proposed DTGN framework outperforms most existing methods in identifying phenotype-specific TFs and pathways. Our framework offers a new approach to exploring the functional roles of transcription regulators that associate with specific phenotypes in a dynamic model.

Introduction

Transcriptional regulation is a common and crucial process of genetic regulation in biological systems [1, 2]. Uncovering the phenotype-oriented functional roles of the transcription factors (TFs) or elements is one of the key points to understanding the complex mechanisms of transcription regulation in biological processes, such as cell differentiation and disease progression [3, 4], etc. The complex regulations between the TFs and their targets present a challenge to studying the functional roles of the transcription regulators in influencing specific phenotypes. To study the regulatory dependencies of transcription regulators, the transcriptional regulatory network (TRN) is usually used to depict the regulation relationships between TFs and their target genes (TGs) [5, 6] and to build the global map of transcription regulation. Over the past decade, the rapid advance in high-throughput sequencing has provided unparalleled opportunities to study the regulations between TFs and their targets to construct TRNs [7–9]. By bridging the linkages of the transcription regulators in TRNs to the concerned phenotype, we can reveal the phenotype-relevant functional roles of the transcription regulators according to mining the TRNs. Therefore, it is important to develop effective methods to construct accurate TRNs and conduct deep analyses of TRNs to identify the phenotype-specific transcription regulators and elucidate their functional roles in transcription regulation.

Many methods have been developed over the last few decades to construct the TRNs for specific phenotypes using various types of omics data [4, 10–12]. Early methods mainly used bulk transcriptomic data for specific phenotypes to construct static TRN based on correlation analysis between TFs and their targets [13–15]. However, these methods usually faced the challenges such as indirect effects in correlation analysis and the requirement of sufficient transcriptomic samples. Although the problem of indirect effects can be partially solved by incorporating other sequencing data (such as TF-binding data, etc.) [16, 17], the considerable number of samples required and the inherent static regulation states limit their practical applications. As the processes of transcription regulation are dynamic in triggering the phenotype changes, using the static TRN model to study the functions of transcription regulators ignores their dynamic characteristics. Dynamic TRNs address the limitations of static TRN in the functional analysis of transcription regulators, and various methods have been developed to construct dynamic TRNs using the time-series gene expression data in the past decade [3, 4, 11, 18]. For example, LEPA [19] uses a pseudo-time ordering correlation method to infer the co-expression relationships of gene pairs in time-series gene expression data to construct the dynamic TRNs. dynGENIE3 [20] extends the GENIE3 [15] method to time-series analysis and utilizes the ordinary differential equation and random forest regression to infer regulations between genes in dynamic TRNs. SSN [21] is a general statistic correlation test method to construct sample-specific networks using gene expression data and can also be used to infer the time-specific dynamic TRNs in time-series data. DREM [22, 23] is a probabilistic graph model that integrates the time-series gene expression data and static TRN to predict the dynamic regulation map and events. Based on the DREM model, many extended models have been proposed to integrate multi-omics data, such as mirDREM [24], SDREM [25], etc. Besides, the advancement of single-cell sequencing in recent years has generated extensive time-series single-cell data, many methods have been proposed to consider the challenges in single-cell data analysis and reconstruct the dynamic TRNs at the level of cell types, such as SCENIC [26], CellOracle [27], Monocle3 [28, 29], CSHMM [30], CICT [31], GNNLink [9], Dictys [18], etc. Although these methods have shown promising results to some extent, most of them require a substantial number of samples in time-series data. Furthermore, they do not adequately incorporate prior knowledge of transcription regulation in dynamic TRNs inference.

To comprehensively identify the phenotype-specific transcription regulators based on TRN, it is necessary to consider the dynamic characteristics of regulators in transcription regulation processes. By constructing the dynamic TRNs specific to a phenotype, we can use various network mining methods to identify more comprehensive phenotype-specific transcription regulators or elements. DREM [22, 23] is a widely used model that infers the dynamic TRNs and identifies the significant regulators by identifying bifurcation points of the co-expression genes that diverge in time series using the tree-structure model. As DREM identifies the significant regulators based on the sub-paths in the tree-structure model, it is hard to accurately determine the timing of regulations. Another method, TSMiner [4] integrates time-series gene expression, static TRN, and pathway gene sets to construct time-specific TRNs and pathways. Although these methods have been effective in identifying time-specific transcription regulators in various datasets, they are not comprehensive for the task of mining phenotype-specific transcription regulators. Currently, there are still not enough alternative methods for studying the phenotype-specific transcription regulators using dynamic TRNs.

In this study, we propose a new framework named DTGN, which identifies the phenotype-specific transcription regulators according to constructing the dynamic transcription factor (TF) to gene networks (DTGN). DTGN first integrates the phenotype-oriented time-series gene expression data and prior static TRN to learn the new temporal representations of genes using the graph autoencoder model (GAE). Then, based on the learned temporal representations of genes, it uses a statistic correlation test model to detect the significant regulatory relationships in TRN to reconstruct the dynamic TRN at each specific time point. Finally, it identifies the phenotype-specific TFs and pathways by integrating the constructed dynamic TRNs across all time points. Experiments on multiple datasets demonstrate that DTGN outperforms most existing methods in predicting phenotype-specific TFs and pathways. To sum up, the main contributions of this study are summarized as follows: (1) We propose a GAE-based model to learn the temporal representations of genes by integrating time-series gene expression data and static TRN. The temporal representations of genes inherently include dynamic gene expression features and prior transcription regulation knowledge. (2) We extend the statistic correlation method in SSN [21] to handle time-series data with multiple replicates at a time point. It allows us to test the significant TF-gene regulations in TRN at specific time points and reconstruct the dynamic TRNs efficiently. (3) We propose new robust methods to identify the phenotype-specific TFs and pathways using dynamic TRNs. In summary, DTGN is a comprehensive framework for identifying phenotype-specific transcription regulators by incorporating dynamic characteristics.

Methods

In this section, we introduce the main framework of DTGN, which includes four main steps: (i) preprocessing time-series gene expression data; (ii) learning temporal representations of genes; (iii) testing time-specific significant regulations and constructing dynamic TRNs; (iv) identifying phenotype-specific transcription regulators based on dynamic TRNs. The overall diagram of the DTGN framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The diagram illustration of the DTGN framework. (A) The static TRN and phenotype-related time-series gene expression data are used to construct attributed TRN. The raw gene expression features are encoded using a one-hot encoding approach. (B) A GAE neural network model is designed to learn the temporal representations of genes using the same model at each time point in a time series. The representations at each time point are then concatenated according to the time series. (C) Dynamic TRNs are constructed at all time points based on the learned temporal representations of genes using a correlation statistic test model. (D) Phenotype-specific TFs and pathways are identified based on the constructed dynamic TRNs over time.

Preprocessing of time-series gene expression data

To robustly study dynamic changes of gene expression over time, we start by preprocessing the raw time-series gene expression data before conducting representation learning. For the raw time-series gene expression data having multiple replicates at each time point, we use the average value of all replicates for each gene to determine its expression level at that specific time. This approach ensures there are no time replicates and helps to reflect the dynamic changes of gene expression over time. After that, to each gene in the time-series data, we encode the expression value at each time as a K-dim feature vector using the one-hot encoding method by considering the global distribution of its expression across all time points. To do this, we first divide the expression range of each gene at all time points into K equal intervals, and each interval serves as a bin that maps to a specific position in the K-dim feature vector. Learning the temporal representations of time-series gene expression

The expression of genes is influenced by their transcription regulators, so it’s important to consider the prior regulation knowledge between TFs and genes during dynamic expression representation learning. The static TRN describes the regulatory relationships between TFs and their TGs. To consider the comprehensive relationships between TFs and genes, we integrate the processed time-series gene expression features and undirected static TRN to learn new temporal representations of gene expression features using the GAE model [32]. In general, GAE is a class of self-supervised learning graph neural network models that accept a graph and a feature matrix of nodes to embed the relationships and features of nodes into new representations. A typical GAE model usually consists of an encoder and decoder modules. In the encoder, the primary goal is to learn the representations of nodes by interactively aggregating the features of their neighboring nodes in the attributed graph. In our study, we use a two-layer graph convolutional neural network (GCN) [33] to perform the aggregation operations of neighboring nodes’ features. Identification of phenotype-specific TFs

We identify the phenotype-specific TFs using the constructed dynamic TRNs. As the predicted TRN at each time point represents a snapshot network of temporary transcriptional regulations in the process of phenotype development, all the considerable TFs that have significant actions in each snapshot TRN on the time series are active in playing regulatory roles and tend to be the phenotype-specific TFs. Therefore, we identify all the active TFs in each snapshot TRN in the time series of phenotype development as the corresponding phenotype-specific TFs in this study. Identification of phenotype-specific pathways

By considering the phenotype-specific TFs and their TGs in dynamic TRNs, we further investigate the phenotype-specific pathways in biological regulation during phenotype development. To identify the phenotype-specific pathways, we utilize the same method as in study [4] to detect significant pathways related to the phenotype-specific TFs and then use a permutation test to identify the phenotype-specific pathways activated by the identified phenotype-specific TFs in dynamic TRNs. For each identified TF in a snapshot TRN, triple-gene blocks are first built based on its targets and given pathways; then, the mutual interaction scores and interaction probabilities between the TF and given pathways are calculated. Finally, a permutation test method is used to verify if the genes in each selected pathway show significant up- or down-regulation at the time of TRN compared to the start time (more details in study [4]). We identify all significant pathways correlated with the phenotype-specific TFs in each TRN and use the minimum permutation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document}-value as the significance of each pathway if a pathway appears in multiple TRNs. All the identified significant pathways estimated from dynamic TRNs are deemed as phenotype-specific pathways, which link to the regulation process of phenotype formation.

Results

Data collection and preprocessing

Time-series gene expression data and static TRN

We downloaded three time-series gene expression data linked to specific phenotypes in mouse or human tissues from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The three time-series gene expression data include the following: (1) The time-series RNA-seq data of mouse liver regeneration (LR) post-PH at 12 time points (GSE95135 [34]). Similar to the study [4], we used the time-series data at 10 time points (0 h, 1 h, 4 h, 10 h, 20 h, 36 h, 48 h, 72 h, 168 h, and 4 week) by excluding the adjacent time points with small intervals. (2) The time-series RNA-seq data of myocardial infarction (MI) in a mouse model at six time points (0 h, 10 min, 1 h, 6 h, 24 h, and 72 h; GSE206281 [35]). (3) The time-series microarray gene expression data of Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) post-infection on human Huh7 cells at five time points (6 h, 12 h, 18 h, 24 h, and 48 h; GSE20948 [36]). For each of the three data, the mean expression of genes on different replicates at each time point was used as the global expression level of the corresponding time. In order to ease the effect of data noise, we filtered out low-expressed and small variation genes over the time points (LR: average expression>1.0 and variance>0; MI: average expression>0 and variance>0.5; HCV: average expression>1.0 and variance>1.0) and used the logarithm transformed values in downstream analyses. For each gene expression data, we determine the appropriate one-hot feature dimension parameters (16-, 32-, and 24-dim in LP, MI, and HCV, respectively). The static TRNs for mice and humans were downloaded from CellNet [37] and GRNdb [38], respectively. For the human TRN from GRNdb [38], we selected the sub-network with high confidence edges as the trusted static TRN. In each data analysis, we used the common genes of processed gene expression and static TRN in experimental studies. We end up retained 6,139 genes of expression data and TRN with 31,851 edges in LR; 5,042 genes of expression data and TRN with 26,693 edges in MI; 10,733 genes of expression data and TRN with 46,308 edges in HVC.

The validation data of phenotype-related TFs and pathways

We collected the gold-standard validation TFs and pathways related to each type of phenotype from reference literatures or phenotype-related annotation databases. In LR data, the whole LR process can be further divided into two phases related to sub-phenotypes of cell proliferation (10–36 h) and growth suppression (72–168 h). Kurinna et al. [39] reported 27 and 10 positive TFs related to the cell proliferation and growth suppression phenotypes, respectively. We selected the TFs from LR processed gene data and finally got 21 and 8 positive TFs related to cell proliferation (LR-CP) and growth suppression (LR-GS), respectively. Meanwhile, we set the positive TFs of LR-GS as the negative TFs for LR-CP and let the positive TFs of LR-CP as the negative TFs of LR-GS. The LR-related positive and negative pathways were collected from the study [4], including 58 positive pathways and 78 negative pathways. In MI and HCV data, we collected the phenotype-related positive TFs from DisGeNet [40] using the search keywords “myocardial infarction” and “Hepatitis”, respectively. We manually filtered the returned lists and defined 34 positive TFs in MI and 26 positive TFs in HCV. After that, we also collected 22 negative TFs in MI and 28 negative TFs in HCV by excluding all TFs that were directly or indirectly related to a positive TF in pathways. We used the same keywords to search MI-related and HCV-related positive pathways in the KEGG [41] database and defined 52 positive pathways in MI and 24 positive pathways in HCV, respectively. Meanwhile, 92 and 36 negative pathways were defined in MI and HCV respectively by excluding all pathways that may overlap with a positive pathway for respective data. The details of collected standard data are included in Supplementary Table S1.

SSN [21] is a sample-specific network construction method using time-series gene expression data and can be employed to construct dynamic TRNs. We used the same method in this study to identify phenotype-specific TFs based on the constructed dynamic TRNs using SSN [21] and denoted this comparison method as SSN-Based. We predicted the significance of TFs in each data and ranked the TFs overlapped with validation data according to their FSR scores. Performance evaluation to predict phenotype-specific pathways

We further investigated the performance of DTGN to predict the phenotype-specific pathways by comparing it with other widely used methods, including TSMiner [4], DREM2 [23], and the traditional clustering methods. Among them, TSMiner can directly predict phenotype-specific pathways by using a dynamic way. DREM2 can identify the phenotype-specific TFs and we then used the same permutation test approach in study [4] to identify the significant pathways enriched in the phenotype-specific TFs and their targets. The clustering methods assign the genes into different clusters based on time-series gene expression data and then identify the significantly enriched pathways of these clusters to define phenotype-specific pathways. We used the hierarchical clustering (scikit-learn package for agglomerative clustering), k-means (scikit-learn package for k-means clustering), and WGCNA [42] methods to cluster genes and identify significantly enriched phenotype-related pathways. The number of clusters was set to 40 (average number of sub-paths from different parameters [4]) in both hierarchical clustering and k-means algorithms, and other parameters were used the default values. We also used the default parameters in WGCNA [42]. For each method of clustering, the most significant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document}-value of each candidate pathway estimated from enrichment analysis in all clusters is used in our experiments. Figure 3(A–C) shows the ROC performance of each method to predict the phenotype-specific pathways in three data. We can see that DTGN consistently outperforms other methods in both LR and MI data (Fig. 3(A–B)). In the HCV data, although TSMiner shows slightly better performance than DTGN, their overall performance is very similar, and the AUC is still over 0.9. Furthermore, we investigated the precision, recall, and F1 score measures for the top phenotype-specific pathways in the three data. We find that DTGN achieves better performance compared to other existing methods in both LR and MI data (Fig. 3(D–E, G-H)). These results suggest that the proposed DTGN method is highly effective in identifying the phenotype-related pathways. However, it is inferior to the TSMiner in HCV data, although still better than other methods (Fig. 3(F, I)). This may be due to the small samples and the specific characteristics of HCV data, as DTGN is a correlation-based method that requires an adequate number of samples. Meanwhile, we also observed that both the DTGN and TSMiner outperform the clustering methods in all three data. This further highlights the superiority of the dynamic network models in identifying phenotype-related regulators. In conclusion, DTGN can identify more accurate phenotype-specific pathways via constructing dynamic TRNs.

As DTGN constructed snapshot TRNs at whole time, we used the union set of targets in all snapshot TRNs for each selected TF in analysis. Discussion

Identifying the phenotype-related transcriptional regulators is crucial to studying the functional roles of regulators and elucidating the mechanisms of transcriptional regulation affecting phenotype development. It is important to consider the natural dynamics of transcriptional regulations when identifying phenotypic factors. Currently, the time-series gene expression data is widely used to study temporally dynamic regulation characteristics, and several methods have been proposed to construct dynamic TRNs [3, 7, 15, 22]. Although these methods can help identify phenotypic regulators through analyzing dynamic TRNs, most of them don’t take into account prior regulation knowledge and cannot directly predict phenotypic regulators. The recent DREM [22, 23] and TSMiner [4] are widely used methods to mine the dynamic regulatory events and factors based on time-series gene expression data. Although they are able to identify the phenotypic regulators, they are not comprehensive methods to identify phenotypic regulators and the complexity of tree-based model limits their practical applications. In this study, the proposed DTGN provides a new framework to construct dynamic TRNs and identify phenotypic regulators according to the dynamic regulation analysis. DTGN uses the GAE [32] model to learn new temporal representations of genes by integrating the time-series gene expression data and static TRN. The discretized expression at each time point is used in GAE to improve the robustness of the learning model. The finally learned temporal representations of genes naturally encompass the temporal expression information and prior regulation knowledge of genes, which is crucial for reducing false positives from indirect regulations. In addition, an extended statistical method based on the SSN [21] model was developed to test significant regulations at specific time points, enabling the construction of dynamic TRNs over the time series. The statistical method is simple and efficient for large-scale data. Moreover, by considering the dynamic regulatory relationships in all snapshot TRNs, DTGN provides robust methods to identify the phenotypic TFs and pathways in a data-oriented manner. As far as we know, it is the first comprehensive framework towards the mission of identifying phenotype-related regulators via integrating dynamic characteristics.

However, a few limitations should be further considered in the future. On the one hand, as we used a correlation statistical model to predict the dynamic TRNs, enough temporal samples in time series are usually required to ensure a promising prediction. This phenomenon has been observed in the HCV data in our experiments, such as in the prediction effect of phenotype-specific pathways. It is challenging to apply the model to small sample data. On the other hand, we used a one-hot encoding method to obtain the discretized gene expression features. It may have the risk of ignoring the accurate expression features during the temporal representation learning. Multiple encoding features can be used to assimilate the expression patterns from different perspectives. We used the chi-square statistical model to construct the dynamic TRNs, in which the degrees of freedom in the model is decided by the dimension of the learned features at each time point. Although the dimension of the final latent features is a hyperparameter in the GAE model, we have noticed that using relatively low feature dimensions can produce more dependable results. This might be affected by the nature of the chi-square model, and additional investigation is needed for statistical models using high-dimensional features. Finally, the rapid advance of single-cell sequencing has generated large-scale time course data. It provides excellent opportunities to study the phenotypically relevant transcriptional regulators for specific cell types or tissues. While, as the characteristics of single-cell sequencing data, such as high noise and sparsity, etc., more robust dynamic models should be developed to help predict the phenotypic regulators at the cellular or tissue level. The DTGN currently works on the bulk-seq data and can be further extended to satisfy the analysis of single-cell time course data by considering the characteristics of single-cell data. Besides, different species may have various intrinsic data characteristics and can affect the integrative capability of computational methods. In this study, we investigated the integrative capability of DTGN on mouse and human data. Systematic cross-analysis of the data from other species is anticipated to reveal more generalized regulators in transcription. It should be valuable to consider these improvements in the future work.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new framework, DTGN, to identify the phenotypic TFs and pathways by constructing the dynamic TRNs over time series. We use a GAE neural network model to integrate the time-series gene expression data and static TRN to learn temporal representations of genes, which include comprehensive knowledge based on expression and regulation fusion. Then, a correlation statistical model is proposed to test significant gene regulations at a specific time, thus constructing the dynamic TRNs over a time series. Finally, based on the dynamic TRNs, we develop relevant methods to identify phenotype-related TFs and pathways that coordinate the phenotypic time-series data. Comprehensive experiments on multiple data demonstrate that DTGN outperforms most existing methods and can identify more accurate phenotype-related TFs and pathways. Importantly, the identified phenotypic TFs and pathways are supported by disease-phenotype enrichment analysis and known phenotypic biomarkers. In conclusion, our DTGN framework provides an alternative to identify phenotype-related regulators dynamically. We hope it could be a useful tool to help elucidate the transcriptional regulation mechanisms towards specific phenotypes.
