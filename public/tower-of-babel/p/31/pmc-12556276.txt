VDAC1 inhibitor DIDS uncouples respiration in isolated tissue mitochondria and induces mitochondrial hyperfusion in mammalian cells
Authors: Surajit Das, Arpita Dutta, Minakshi Bedi, Arumay Pal, Shubhra Majumder, Alok Ghosh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•VDAC1 inhibitor DIDS uncouples respiration in isolated tissue mitochondria and human osteosarcoma U2OS and mouse fibroblasts NIH 3T3 cells.•Short-term 2 h treatment with DIDS significantly reduces VDAC1 expression and depolarizes mitochondrial membrane potential in U2OS cells.•DIDS alters mitochondrial dynamics by inducing hyperfusion in mammalian cells.•In silico modelling showed DIDS binds to the inner pocket of VDAC1 beta barrel.

Introduction

Voltage Dependent Anion Channel (VDAC) proteins are the most abundant outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) proteins. VDACs form ion-selective channels, and thereby control the transport of ions such as Ca2+, Na+, K+, Cl-, or OH –, nucleotides such as ATP, ADP, and other water-soluble mitochondrial metabolites such as pyruvate, succinate, etc. between cytosol and mitochondria [1]. The three VDAC family proteins in mammalian cells, namely VDAC1, VDAC2 and VDAC3, share >70 % sequence similarity, and may complement each other, at least partially, in performing mitochondrial channel functions and bioenergetics. However, each member has specific function(s) that is important for various cellular processes. Among the VDAC family proteins, VDAC1 is the most abundant protein in diverse cells and tissue types. Also, the structural and functional aspects of VDAC1 is well-studied as compared to the two other VDACs [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. VDACs share a common cylindrical transmembrane β-barrel structure consisting of 19 antiparallel β-strands. However, the N-terminal extension (25–36 residues long) in three VDACs are less conserved, both in composition and number of amino acids, and therefore accounts for the variation in molecular weight of these three proteins. The crystal structure of VDAC1 revealed that its 25 residue-long N-terminal region lies inside the pore generated by β-strands, which likely move out to the open space and translocate inside to regulate channel gating as ‘closed’ and ‘open’ states respectively [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Other two VDACs are thought to form similar structure with varying channel conductance owing to their divergent N-terminal region.

VDACs may oligomerize at the OMM to form larger pore that facilitates transport of bigger molecules such as- cytochrome c, from mitochondria to cytoplasm [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. In fact, VDAC1 remains in dynamic equilibrium between the monomeric and oligomeric forms, which critically regulate mitochondrial metabolism and bioenergetics, and cellular apoptosis. VDAC1 can interact with apoptosis-regulatory proteins such as Bcl 2-Associated X protein (Bax), Bcl 2 Antagonist/Killer (Bak), adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT), and various kinases such as Hexokinase (HK), glycerol kinase, mitochondrial creatinine kinase (MtCK), which play a crucial role in transition point of cell death, cell survival and metabolisms [[16], [17], [18]].

VDAC1 is over-expressed in various tumors [19,20] pointing to its significance in high-energy-demanding cancer cells. Cells in solid tumors typically are maintained in hypoxic environment, which may lead to truncation of the C-terminal end of VDAC1 that is recently implicated in both the up-regulation of glycolysis, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and resistance to chemotherapy [21]. VDAC1 depletion in glioblastoma, breast, lung and pancreatic cancer cells reduces cellular ATP levels and thus inhibits tumor growth [2,22,23]. It is considered that overexpression of VDAC1 in cancer cells provides increased metabolic activity, while its oligomerization is prevented by the binding of anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl 2. The detachment of anti-apoptotic proteins from VDAC1 leads to VDAC1 oligomerization and triggers apoptosis [24,25].

During the last few decades, VDAC1 is considered as potential target to modulate apoptosis as a therapeutic intervention, particularly in cancer. A recent study identified a small molecule SC18 to interact with the NADH binding pocket of VDAC1, which rewires the cellular metabolism in cancer cells [26]. Importantly, another small molecule 4,4′-diisothiocyanatostilbene-2,2′-disulfonic acid (DIDS), a chloride channel inhibitor, was found to bind with VDAC1 (Kd value = 498 nM) [25,27], reduces the conductance of VDAC1-mediated flux and blocks apoptosis-induced VDAC1 oligomerization [28]. DIDS protects cisplatin-treated various human cancer-derived cells from apoptosis by mitigating cisplatin-induced MFN1-dependent mitochondrial fusion and oxidative damage [29]. Recently, VDAC1 knockout in haplodiploid HAP1 cells was shown to reduce overall mitochondrial oxygen flux, however, the complex I linked respiration was increased by drawing resources from respiratory reserves. Also, the upregulation of VDAC1 rescued complex I mediated mitochondrial respiration in transgenic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis mice [30,31]. However, the exact role of VDAC1 in OXPHOS is still unclear. On these premises, here we investigated the impact of VDAC1 inhibitor, DIDS, on OXPHOS by treating isolated mitochondria from multiple mice tissues as well as in mouse fibroblasts NIH 3T3 and in human U2OS osteosarcoma cells. We found drastic loss of mitochondrial membrane potential upon treating the cells with DIDS, which also significantly affected mitochondrial dynamics in the U2OS cells. Furthermore, in silico study of the interaction between VDAC1 and DIDS suggested that DIDS may alter the function of VDAC1 monomer, thereby supporting our experimental observations. Overall, this study offers insight into how VDAC1 inhibitor DIDS affects mitochondrial physiology and function in cellular model and isolated mitochondria.

Materials and methods

Cells, culture conditions, and treatment

Human osteosarcoma cell line U2OS (ATCC, USA) and mouse fibroblast cell line NIH 3T3 (Cell Repository, BRIC—NCCS, Govt. of India) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Himedia, India), 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified chamber in the presence of 5 % CO2. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of DIDS or mitochondrial uncoupler CCCP (both from Sigma-Aldrich, MERCK, USA) for 2 h or 24 h as mentioned.

Cell viability assay

To measure the cell viability, 5000 cells (U2OS or NIH 3T3 cells) were seeded in 96-well transparent cell culture plates and grown for 24 h. Cells were treated with DIDS or CCCP solubilized in DMSO or the solvent (DMSO) for the mentioned duration, followed by the addition of MTT reagent at 0.5 mg/ml final concentration, and were incubated for 1.5 h in the cell culture incubator at 37 °C. Next, the media was removed, and formazan precipitate was dissolved in equal volume of DMSO. Optical density (OD) of the formazan solution was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek Synergy microplate reader (Agilent, USA). Viability was calculated as percentage of OD570 of the test wells with respect to that of the solvent control.

Measurements of respiratory capacity in the cell line

Mitochondrial respirometry alteration was measured by calculating an oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in an Oxytherm fitted with a Clark-type electrode at 37 °C (Hansatech, UK). 1 × 106 growing cells were permeabilized with 3 μM digitonin and mitochondrial stress test was performed. Subsequently, combined 5 mM pyruvate, 2 mM malate, and 10 mM glutamate were added as substrates to initiate respiration. 1 mM adenosine di-phosphate (ADP) was added to boost state-3 oxidative phosphorylation. Next, 5 μM oligomycin A was added to block the complex V and after two minutes of oxygen consumption measurements, DIDS (final concentration 500 μM) or uncoupler carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP, final concentration 50 μM) was added. Ultimately, oxygen consumption was inhibited by 1 mM sodium azide, a complex IV blocker. OCR was calculated as nmol of O2 consumed/min/106 cells.

Immunoblotting

Cell lysate was prepared in a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 % NP-40, and 1x protease inhibitor cocktail. Concentrations of the lysates were determined using BCA reagent (Thermo, USA). 30–50 μg cellular proteins were separated on 10 % SDS-PAGE and transferred onto the PVDF membrane. The primary antibodies for immunoblotting were: mouse anti-VDAC1 (ab14734, 1: 5000; Abcam), mouse anti-complex I subunit protein NDUFB8 (ab110242, 1: 5000; Abcam), rabbit anti-Complex V subunit protein ATP5B (GTX132925, 1: 5000; GENetex), rabbit anti-DRP1 (1:750, Proteintech), rabbit anti-MFN1 (1:500, Abclonal), rabbit anti-β Tubulin (2128T, 1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse anti-β-Actin (8226, 1:1000; Abcam). Secondary antibodies were either anti-mouse or anti-rabbit peroxidase-conjugated IgG (both at 1:5000 dilution) or Alexafluor 680-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (1:1000) antibody for chemiluminescence or fluorescence detection methods respectively. Images were acquired with a ChemiDoc Imaging system (Biorad, USA), followed by densitometric analyses of the bands using ImageJ software, or using Odyssey-DLX infrared imaging system (Li-Cor), where the background-corrected band intensities were determined using ImageStudio (Li-cor). Dylight800 conjugated anti-human β-Actin (1:5000, BioRad) was used for loading control in case of fluorescence detection method.

Cytology

Cells were fixed in either 4 % paraformaldehyde (EMS) and 0.2 % TritonX-100 for 10 mins at room temperature or chilled methanol at −20 °C for 10 mins as described earlier [2]. For indirect immunofluorescence the antibodies used were: rabbit anti-TOM20 (1:500, Abcam), rabbit anti-DRP1 (1:750, Proteintech) and mouse anti-COXIV (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology). Secondary antibodies were- donkey anti-rabbit or donkey anti-mouse tagged with either Alexaflour488 or Alexafluor568 (1:1000; Invitrogen). Hoechst33358 (1 μg/ml; Sigma) was used to stain nuclear DNA. To measure mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were incubated with MitoTracker Red CMX Rox (Invitrogen) at 100 nM final concentration for 45 min before aldehyde fixation [2]. For live imaging of mitochondria, MitoTracker Green (Invitrogen) was used at 100 nM final concentration for 45 min. Images of stained cells were acquired using a Leica Confocal laser scanning microscope fitted with a 63X Plan Apo oil immersion objective (NA 1.4). Total cellular fluorescence of MitoTracker Red-stained cells was determined by measuring background-corrected fluorescence intensities from images acquired using identical optical parameters (more than hundred cells per sample) using the LasX software package of the Leica confocal microscope. To determine various parameters of mitochondrial shape and size, 2D images acquired using confocal fluorescence microscope were analysed using Mitochondrial Analyser plugin associated with FIJI-ImageJ software [32,33] and then plotted using GraphPad Prism.

Flow cytometry by Tetramethyl-Rhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) staining

Briefly, U2OS cells were treated with DMSO (control), 100- 500 μM of DIDS or 20 μM CCCP for 2 hr and 24 hr. Cultured cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated with trypsin to obtain a single-cell suspension in PBS. After that, cells were stained with 50 nM TMRE (Cayman chemicals, cat. no. 21,426) in the dark at 37 °C for 10 min, and analyzed by flow cytometry (FACS Verse, BD Biosciences). Ten thousand events were recorded for each experimental sample. Histograms were generated by plotting the cell count against fluorescence in the respective PE-A channel, at least from three biological replicates. The data were analyzed by specific population gating depending on the subset for each experiment and were kept constant for a particular assay. The gating and statistical analysis were done using BD FACS Verse software.

Preparation of mitochondria

Animal experiments were carried out according to the institutional ethics committee (IEC) approval number 797/GO/Re/S/O3/CPCSEA, on 05/09/2019. Mitochondria from different tissues of mice were isolated at 4 °C. Briefly, skeletal muscle (limbs) and liver tissues were taken from freshly sacrificed normal Swiss albino female mice. Fibrosarcoma tumor muscle tissues were taken from the hind leg of 3-methylcholanthrene (3-MC) treated mice as described previously in our laboratory [34]. Mitochondria were isolated as described previously [34]. Briefly, mitochondria were prepared by differential centrifugation in a sucrose buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, and pH 7.4.

Oxygen consumption rate measurement of isolated mitochondria

The oxygen consumption rate was measured using freshly isolated mitochondria in an Oxytherm device at 30 °C from different tissues following the protocol standardized earlier [35]. Briefly, the respiratory medium in a total volume of 2 ml contained 125 mM KCl, 10 mM Tris/MOPS (pH 7.4), 1 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM EGTA. For the mitochondrial respirometry assay combined 5 mM pyruvate, 2 mM malate, and 10 mM glutamate was added to start respiration. Following that, 1 mM ADP and 500 μM DIDS were added sequentially and oxygen consumption was measured for 2 min after each addition. In another assay, 1 mM ADP, 5 µM oligomycin A, and 500 μM DIDS were added sequentially in 2 min intervals. Finally, 1 mM sodium azide was added at the end of each individual assay to stop the respiration. Each assay was performed using 1∼2 mg of mitochondrial proteins. Oxygen consumption rates (OCR) were calculated by measuring nmol of O2 consumed/min/mg of mitochondrial protein (n = 3).

ROS estimation

To measure the cellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), 5000 U2OS cells were seeded in 96-well black cell culture plate and grown for 24 h in a suitable cell culture medium without Phenol red. Then the cells were either kept untreated or treated with 1 mM N-acetylcystine (NAC, MedChemExpress) for 1 h followed by treatment with DIDS or control solvent (DMSO) for 2 h. For the last 30 min of the treatment, cells were added with 20 µM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA, Sigma). The cells were then washed with warm PBS (Gibco) and immediately DCF fluorescence (excitation- 485 nm; emission- 535 nm) was recorded using BioTek Synergy microplate reader (Agilent).

ATP release assay

CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability assay (Promega) reagent was used to measure the ATP release in cells, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5000 U2OS cells were seeded in each well of a 96-well black cell culture plate and grown for 24 h. Cells were treated with DIDS or CCCP or the control solvent (DMSO) for 2 h or 24 h, followed by the addition of CellTiter-Glo reagent. After 15 min incubation at room temperature, the luminescence signal was recorded using a BioTek Synergy microplate reader (Agilent).

Molecular docking

AlphaFold3 [36] was run locally using the VDAC1 protein sequence (UniProt database Accession number P21796) and the SMILES string of DIDS. Each run modelled 25 poses. Autodock Vina v1.2.0 [37] was used to dock the DIDS structure on the VDAC1 structure using the default parameters. The VDAC1 monomeric conformation from the crystal structure obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 5XDO) was used as the target. The DIDS conformations were obtained from the PDB structures 8Y85.pdb and 8GV8.pdb. A total of 25 poses were modelled per run.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed at least three times with 2–3 technical replicates wherever applicable. Either One-way Anova or unpaired Student T-test was performed to determine the significance level. The *p-value <0.05, **p-value <0.01, and ***p-value <0.001 were considered as statistically significant. n.s. indicates non-significant.

Results

The effect of DIDS on the cell survivability of U2OS cells and NIH 3T3 cells

The role of VDAC1 is crucial for cell metabolism and cell death processes. Small molecule inhibitor DIDS is known to suppress apoptotic stimuli-induced VDAC1 oligomerization, and thereby suppress cell death [38]. However, how DIDS affects bioenergetics, and mitochondrial physiology is not well-studied. To address these issues, we choose human osteosarcoma U2OS cells and mouse NIH 3T3 fibroblast cells, and determined viability of these cells treated with varying concentrations of DIDS (Fig. 1A). We determined IC50, the half maximal effective concentration of DIDS in these cell types as approximately 508 μM and 580 μM after 24 h of treatment in U2OS cells and NIH 3T3 cells, respectively (Fig. 1A). DIDS causes uncoupling of mitochondrial OXPHOS and attenuates ATP synthesis in mammalian cells

To assay the physiological consequences of DIDS treatment in whole cell respiration we measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using an Oxytherm. We performed a mitochondrial stress test in U2OS and in NIH 3T3 cells. The representative graph shows the sequential additions of substrates/inhibitors with times (Fig. 1B). The ADP stimulated OCR for U2OS and NIH 3T3 cells were 0.75 nmol/min/106 cells and 2.66 nmol/min/106 cells, respectively. When oligomycin A was added to inhibit mitochondrial complex V, we found OCR decreased to 0.213 nmol/min/106 cells and 1.33 nmol/min/106 cells. After that CCCP followed by azide were added to uncouple respiration and inhibit oxygen consumption through complex IV (Fig. 1C-D). Surprisingly, similar to CCCP (50 µM), when DIDS was added at a final concentration of 500 µM, OCRs were increased to ∼3 nmol/min/106 cells in both the cell lines, and azide was able to inhibit this uncoupled respiration in usual manner (Fig. 1C-D). When we estimated cellular ATP concentration after 2 h of treatments, only 5 % reduction in ATP level was observed in U2OS cells treated with 500 µM DIDS, which was increased to roughly 30 % after 24 h treatment. In comparison, treating cells with 20 µM CCCP led to roughly 18 % and almost 75 % decrease in ATP level after 2 h and 24 h respectively (Fig. 1E). The above finding thus suggests that DIDS treatment uncouples mitochondrial respiration and significantly inhibits ATP synthesis in mammalian cells.

DIDS does not alter OXPHOS subunits expression

A previous study reported that overexpression of VDAC1 in the transgenic mice causes upregulation of OXPHOS complex I subunits [31]. Therefore, we wanted to examine whether DIDS treatment alters expression of OXPHOS subunits and VDAC1 in cells. We performed western blot analysis to check the levels of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (NDUFB8), and complex V (ATP5B) subunits, along with VDAC1, upon treating the cells with DIDS or CCCP for 2 h or 24 h. For this purpose, we chose a 200 µM concentration of DIDS at which >85 % cells remained viable, as compared to the solvent control treatment. Surprisingly, we observed a roughly ∼35 % reduction in VDAC1 protein level after 2 h treatment of DIDS, without altering NDUFB8 and ATP5B (Fig. 2A-B). However, a prolonged 24 h treatment did not show any alteration in the protein level of VDAC1, NDUFB8, or ATP5B (Fig. 2C, Supplementary 1B). Neither of the short- and long-term treatments of CCCP, the conventional uncoupler, affects VDAC1 protein level (Fig. 2). Next, we examined VDAC1 level in U2OS and NIH 3T3 cells treated with varying concentrations of DIDS for 2 h. We observed a dose-dependent decrease in VDAC1 level in both the cell types, where the reduction in VDAC1 level is comparable in cells treated with either 200 or 500 µM of DIDS for 2 h (Fig. 2D-E). Based on this, we choose to use 200 µM DIDS to treat U2OS cells in subsequent experiments for inhibiting VDAC1 and to examine cellular or mitochondrial physiology. DIDS depolarizes mitochondrial membrane potential without significantly altering cellular ROS levels

VDAC1 plays a major role in regulating permeability of ATP/ADP [39], and the regulation of calcium flux at ER-mitochondria contact sites [40]. It is previously demonstrated in several literatures that DIDS interacts with purified VDAC1 reconstituted into liposomes and reduces channel conductance [28]. We questioned whether DIDS treatment results in any change in mitochondrial normal membrane potential. Therefore, we tested the mitochondrial membrane potential of U2OS cells as judged by the incorporation of MitoTracker Red upon brief DIDS treatment (2 h). Interestingly, we observed a gradual decrease in MitoTracker Red incorporation in U2OS cells with increasing concentration of DIDS, and only poor incorporation at 200 µM concentration of DIDS, when compared to DMSO-treated cells (Fig. 3A-B). Such drastic loss of MitoTracker Red fluorescence intensities in 200 µM DIDS-treated cells is comparable to 20 µM CCCP treatment for 2 h, suggesting that DIDS treatment may significantly depolarize mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 3A-B). Notably, depletion of VDAC family proteins significantly reduced MitoTracker Red incorporation [41], similar to what we observed when VDAC1 is inhibited by DIDS. We also examined the incorporation of TMRE dye using flow cytometry analyses to further validate the mitochondrial membrane depolarization. We found that mitochondrial membrane potential of U2OS was significantly depolarized after 2 h and 24 h of DIDS treatment. We observed that about 8 %, 36 %, and 57 % U2OS cells become depolarized after the 100 µM, 200 µM, and 500 µM DIDS treatments for 2 h as compared to control (DMSO) cells. While the depolarization has increased after 24 h treatment under similar doses. The known uncoupler CCCP (20 µM) depolarizes roughly 50 % of cells when treated for either 2 h or 24 h (Fig. 3C-E, Supplementary 2–3). In order to determine if briefly treating cells with DIDS upregulates cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), we further estimated total cellular ROS by measuring DCFDA fluorescence intensity. Interestingly, DIDS treatment does not significantly escalate the cellular ROS after 2 h of DIDS treatment in U2OS cells. Whereas, conventional uncoupler CCCP significantly enhances cellular ROS by about 1.5-fold, compared to the control treatment, which was drastically diminished when the cells were pre-treated with N- acetyl cysteine (NAC), a well-known ROS scavenger (Fig. 3F). 3.5 DIDS uncouples OXPHOS of isolated mouse liver, muscle, and sarcoma tumor mitochondria.

To determine the direct effect of DIDS on isolated mitochondrial bioenergetic, we purified mitochondria from liver, skeletal muscle, and sarcoma tumor tissues from Swiss albino mice. Like the whole cell respiration, isolated mitochondrial respiration was measured in an Oxytherm fitted with a Clark type O2 electrode. The representative graphs show the sequential additions of substrates/inhibitors with times (Fig. 4A). Combined pyruvate, malate, glutamate followed by ADP were added to measure the state −3 respiration. The ADP stimulated state-3 OCR were 43 nmol/min/ mg of protein, 12.33 nmol/min/ mg of protein and 23.25 nmol/min/ mg of protein for liver, muscle, and sarcoma mitochondria, respectively. Addition of 500 µM DIDS increased OCR by 37 %, 33 %, and 90 % in the liver, muscle, and sarcoma mitochondria, respectively (Fig. 4B-D, left panel). However, azide was able to inhibit respiration in the usual manner, suggesting that DIDS increases the OCR of mitochondria. Further, when we added DIDS after the oligomycin A treatment a significant increase of OCR was noticed (Fig. 4B-D, right panel). It is intriguing to mention that DIDS was found to restore the OCR with varying capacities, such as in sarcoma mitochondria it brings the OCR to the ADP stimulated rate but in the case of normal liver and muscle mitochondria, OCR is partially restored by DIDS. DIDS treatment increases the population of hyperfused mitochondria in growing cells

Mitochondrial dynamics that are maintained by a fine balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion are critical for proper cellular activity and to mitigate stress [42]. Under stressful conditions, such as during nutrient scarcity, the mitochondrial branches fuse to increase the efficiency of energy production, whereas, during apoptotic stimuli, mitochondria get fragmented and selectively undergo autophagy as a quality control strategy [28]. Commonly, any asynchronously growing cell population contains a certain population of cells with hyperfused, fragmented or dispersed, and an intermediate phenotype of mitochondrial network. Treating cells with mitochondrial uncoupler CCCP leads to mitochondrial fission [43]. We wondered if DIDS treatment would affect mitochondrial dynamics. Therefore, we first examined the distribution of the U2OS cell population with those three mitochondrial morphologies after treating with DIDS or CCCP for 2 h, by immunostaining for COXIV, which primarily localizes to the mitochondrial inner membrane. Roughly 30 % and 10 % cells contain hyperfused and fragmented mitochondrial phenotypes, respectively, in control (DMSO) treatment, while the remaining 60 % show intermediate phenotype (Supplementary 4A-B). Expectedly, CCCP treatment drastically increases the ‘fragmented’ subpopulation to roughly 60 %. Importantly, upon DIDS treatment, the subpopulation of cells with ‘hyperfused’ phenotype increased significantly, in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary 4A-B). Based on this notion, we sought to examine the mitochondrial morphology more closely. However, when we tried to examine mitochondrial morphology in live cells using MitoTracker Green, the signal almost disappeared upon 200 µM DIDS treatment for 2 h, and was only moderately visible in cells treated with CCCP, as compared to a bright signal in control cells (Supplementary 4C). While such a decrease in MitoTracker Green incorporation in DIDS or CCCP-treated cells corroborated well with the fact that such treatments depolarize mitochondria (Fig. 3A-B), this live cell imaging study failed to suggest any change in mitochondrial morphology and dynamics upon DIDS treatment. Therefore, we stained the treated U2OS cells for TOM20, an outer mitochondrial protein, which, in comparison to COXIV, may suggest a better change in mitochondrial morphology. Indeed, we observed that DIDS treatment (200 µM) for 2 h led to elongated mitochondrial branches in a large population of cells, as compared to the control treatment, while CCCP treatment led to majorly fragmented mitochondria. We analyzed TOM20-stained U2OS cells using Mitochondria Analyzer tool that is well-received [32] in determining various mitochondrial parameters to ultimately suggest mitochondrial shape, size and morphology from 2D images acquired using confocal fluorescence microscope. Our analyses clearly show that DIDS treatment significantly increases mitochondrial size, area, and branch length, as compared to DMSO treatment, which implicates mitochondrial hyperfusion in those cells (Fig. 5A-B; Supplementary 4D). On the contrary, CCCP treatment mostly decreased these parameters. Thus, our data suggest that DIDS and CCCP affect mitochondrial morphology in an opposing manner, and possibly via altering mitochondrial fission-fusion dynamics. Such dynamics is operated continuously in healthy cells in a regulated manner. Recruitment of Dynamin-related protein DRP1 to the mitochondrial outer membrane, and its activation upon phosphorylation, is the key to initiating mitochondrial fission, while MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 promote mitochondrial fusion [40]. When such homeostasis is perturbed, the balance of the dynamics may shift to either side, which often happens due to various cellular and mitochondrial stresses. However, we did not observe any significant alteration in the level of DRP1 or MFN1 upon treating U2OS cells with DIDS or CCCP (Fig. 5C). Next, we sought to examine recruitment of DRP1 onto mitochondria in these treated cells [42]. DRP1 is usually a cytoplasmic protein, and often colocalized with mitochondria that were marked by COXIV in control cells (Fig. 5D). However, DIDS treatment markedly reduced such colocalization of DRP1 with COXIV, which is visible from the representative image and also evident from the reduction in Pearson Coefficient (Fig. 5D) [44,45]. In contrary, CCCP-treated cells show prominent colocalization of DRP1 with COXIV, which is likely the reason for observed mitochondrial fragmentation in these cells, as also shown by earlier studies. Based on our data, we speculate that VDAC1 inhibition by DIDS may shift the dynamics towards mitochondrial fusion, leading to an increase in various mitochondrial parameters indicating mitochondrial branch length elongation and hyperfusion. DIDS interacts with the N-terminal helical domain of VDAC1

The utility of Molecular Docking in protein-small molecule binding prediction and analysis has been well-established. The structural studies of VDAC1 indicated two distinct structural elements, the barrel structure and the N-terminal flexible region [6–8]. Since DIDS, which was known to inhibit VDAC1 oligomerization, showed uncoupling of OXPHOS and regulation of mitochondrial dynamics in the present study. We wondered if in silico analyses of the interaction between VDAC1 and DIDS may provide structural insight to explain the observation. Here, we docked DIDS to VDAC1 using two state-of-the-art methods which work on different types of algorithms. One of them is AlphaFold3 (AF3), a recently developed machine-learning method, which uses a deep learning model to predict the 3D complex structure of the protein and the small molecule [36]. Though AF3 outperforms classical docking tools like Autodock Vina, we note its limitations in predicting the correct chirality and conformational change of small molecules upon binding. To complement that, we used the other algorithm Autodock Vina (Vina), a top-ranking choice for protein-small molecule docking, which uses a physics-based efficient scoring function for the docking prediction [37]. Agreement on docking poses from these two algorithms should provide correct docked conformations of the VDAC1-DIDS complex with higher confidence.

Indeed, DIDS binds to the same binding site of VDAC1 as obtained from AF3 and Vina in the top-ranked poses (Fig . 6: Ai,ii and Bi,ii, respectively), where compared to AF3, Vina placed DIDS at a slightly lower region on the beta-barrel. In both cases, DIDS goes inside the beta-barrel structure and interacts with residues from the N-terminal helical region and the inner surface of the barrel. To analyse their interactions, we selected all VDAC1 residues within 6 Å of DIDS in both models. A positively charged side-chain Arg15 from the N-terminal helix interacts with the negatively charged sulphate moiety of DIDS. Hydrophobic interactions between the aromatic side chains from the N-terminal helix (Phe18, Tyr22, and Phe24 in AF3; Phe18 in Vina) and the ring moiety of DIDS are also found to be consistent. Another polar side chain, Thr19, is found in both models. These structural data indicate that the N-terminal helix of VDAC1 plays a major role in binding DIDS. Added to that, residues from the beta-barrel also contribute to DIDS binding. A positively charged Arg218 and a polar side chain Asn238 were found interacting with DIDS in both models, along with other aliphatic and polar residues in individual cases.

Since small molecules have a greater variety of interaction modes with their target protein, we also analysed the top 25 binding poses from both algorithms (Fig. 6: Aiii,iv and Biii,iv). The top-ranked DIDS poses are mostly placed within the orange cluster in both cases. Another common cluster (green) was also provided by both the algorithms, where DIDS is located at the opposite side of the N-terminal helix (Fig. 6: Aiii,iv and Biii,iv). We point out that Vina provides two additional clusters (blue and yellow), out of which DIDS binds on the outer surface of VDAC1 in the yellow cluster. The binding affinity of different poses of DIDS in each cluster (Fig. 6B, iii, iv) was listed in Supplementary 5, which showed thermodynamically favourable binding (lowest free energy change, −6.685 kcal/mol). While this observation is consistent with the fact that DIDS is known to disrupt VDAC1 oligomer formation [46], further analysis is needed to confirm this aspect. When docked to the VDAC1 hexamer using AF3, we found DIDS binding each monomer to the same pocket on the N-terminal helix as observed in the case of the VDAC1 monomer (Supplementary 6A and Fig. 6: Ai,ii and Bi,ii). Notably, docking with other isoforms of mammalian VDACs, VDAC2 and VDAC3, also showed potential of DIDS binding within the inner pocket of their beta barrel structure (Supplementary 6B). However, suggesting the specificity of DIDS binding with the VDAC isoforms requires extensive examination of the strength of such bindings followed by the validation using experimental studies, which is beyond the scope of the present study but will be addressed in the near future. Discussion

Intracellular metabolic processes are under the direct control of mitochondrial VDAC1 activity. VDAC1 interacts with hexokinase to regulate the glycolysis. However, how VDAC1 regulates the OXPHOS pathway is still under investigation. VDAC1 has been a major target to combat several diseases including cancer, neuronal diseases, etc [[47], [48], [49]]. A variety of pharmacological ligands can interact with VDAC1. Such as, small molecule VBIT-4, DIDS, etc. can prevent apoptosis by inhibiting VDAC1 oligomerization. Conversely, VDAC1 closers cause an excessive buildup of Ca2+ in the mitochondrial matrix, which opens the mitochondrial pore and causes cell death [50,51].

DIDS is a known inhibitor of VDAC1 monomeric as well as oligomeric structure, which halts the pro-apoptotic functions mediated by VDAC1. Also, it has been suggested that DIDS can interact with the monomeric form of VDAC1 and hinder the flux activity. However, the effects of VDAC1 functional inhibition cause differential effects on mitochondrial respiration in tissue and substrate specific manner [52]. Recently VDAC1 inhibitor VBIT-4 showed increased OCR at low 5 μM concentration and inhibition at the high 30 μM condition by inhibiting complex I. Also, VBIT-4 inhibited mitochondrial membrane potential in the isolated mitochondria and cell lines. Here, we also found that a graded concentration of DIDS inhibited mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 3), like the conventional uncoupler CCCP. Also, we found that DIDS, even at 500 μM concentration increases OCR in the pyruvate, malate, and glutamate stimulated respiration that is inhibited by complex V inhibitor oligomycin A, just like the conventional mitochondrial uncoupler CCCP (Fig. 1, Fig. 4). This data corroborated with the recent finding that after the knockout of VDAC1 in the haploid HAP1 leukemic cells increases complex I linked respiration despite a decrease of overall OCR. This is not surprising, as mitochondrial uncoupler causes an increase in OCR, but at higher concentrations, OCR declines [53]. This is intriguing to notice that VDAC1 inhibitors module mitochondrial coupling, this is not astonishing as VDAC1 is already hypothesized to form a mitochondrial interactosome complex with the OXPHOS complex V, adenine nucleotide translocase, inorganic phosphate transporter, etc [54].

Further, we discovered that a brief 2 h treatment with DIDS not only depolarizes the membrane potential of mitochondria but also significantly increases mitochondrial branch length leading to an increased in cell population with hyperfused mitochondria. Possibly, depolarization of membrane potential and mitochondrial uncoupling by DIDS, decrease mitochondrial ATP turnover (Fig. 1E) and to compensate this mitochondrial energy deprivation, the mitochondrial dynamics shifts towards fusion, likely by inhibiting mitochondrial fission. Whereas, CCCP, the conventional mitochondrial uncoupler, induces mitochondrial fragmentation as also shown by other studies. So, we may assume that the uncoupling of mitochondrial OXPHOS by DIDS does not solely follow the same mechanism as the CCCP. Rather, we hypothesize that DIDS uncoupled OXPHOS by binding to VDAC1 inner pocket (Fig. 6) and may modulate the ATP synthase function by some yet to discover mechanism (Fig. 7). Our study suggests that VDAC1 directly controls mitochondrial bioenergetics and dynamics, and inhibiting VDAC1 by DIDS may uncouple mitochondrial electron transport and ATP synthase coupling, and alter the mitochondrial dynamics to promote mitochondrial hyperfusion. Thus, this study explores a potential new avenue of VDAC1 inhibition using DIDS that may be used in future to control cell growth.Fig. 7Schematic model of DIDS-mediated mitochondrial uncoupling.
