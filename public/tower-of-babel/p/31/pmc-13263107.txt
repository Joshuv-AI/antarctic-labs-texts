Feasibility and clinical utility of home-based fecal immunochemical testing for self-monitoring of disease activity in ulcerative colitis
Authors: Yuki Aoyama, Toshihiro Inokuchi, Sakiko Hiraoka, Mikako Ishiguro, Shoko Igawa, Keiko Takeuchi, Masahiro Takahara, Junki Toyosawa, Yasushi Yamasaki, Hideaki Kinugasa, Motoyuki Otsuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHome-based quantitative fecal calprotectin testing has been used to improve the prognosis of ulcerative colitis (UC) patients. However, its cost and the need for specific measurement devices limit accessibility. This study explored the potential of the home-based fecal immunochemical test (FIT) for self-monitoring in patients with UC.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, we recruited patients in clinical remission who were scheduled for colonoscopy between October 2020 and October 2021. We evaluated the correlation between home-based qualitative FIT results obtained using a commercially available kit and hospital-based quantitative fecal markers, endoscopic activity, and treatment intensification. We also assessed the feasibility of continued home-based FIT.ResultsAmong the 54 enrolled patients, 38 tested negative and 16 tested positive using the home-based FIT. The FIT-negative patients had significantly lower hospital-based FIT and fecal calprotectin levels than the FIT-positive patients (P < .0001 and P = .0029, respectively). The accuracy of home-based FIT for predicting endoscopic remission was 0.83, which was comparable to that of hospital-based FIT (0.85) and fecal calprotectin (0.79). None of the 38 FIT-negative patients required intensification of treatment. Among the 20 patients eligible for continued home-based FIT, the adherence rate over one year was 97.0% (82.1%-100%). Notably, none of the 8 FIT-negative patients experienced clinical relapse, whereas those with at least one FIT-positive showed a higher tendency for clinical relapse (P = .055).ConclusionsHome-based FIT is a feasible, accurate, and well-accepted tool for monitoring remission in UC and is comparable to hospital-based fecal markers (UMIN000040572).Keywords: ulcerative colitis, self-monitoring, fecal immunochemical test, fecal calprotectin

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, incurable disease in which the early detection of relapses and timely therapeutic intervention are crucial for maintaining a stable life within society. Achieving endoscopic remission has been shown to reduce relapse rates, hospitalizations, and the need for surgery in UC management. Consequently, both endoscopic and clinical remission have become key therapeutic targets.1,2 Although colonoscopy remains the gold standard for assessing endoscopic activity, frequent procedures should be avoided, prompting interest in biomarkers as noninvasive alternatives.

Fecal calprotectin is closely correlated with clinical, endoscopic, and histological activity in UC and is widely used to predict inflammation3–8 and disease relapse.9,10 In asymptomatic UC patients, routine monitoring every 6-12 months using fecal calprotectin is recommended, with levels below 150-250 μg/g, to rule out active disease.7,10 Recently, home-based fecal calprotectin testing to improve the disease prognosis has been explored in Europe and the United States, and has shown promising utility.8,11–13 However, fecal calprotectin levels exhibit both interindividual and intraday variability, making it difficult to establish a definitive cutoff value. Additionally, quantitative measurements are required, which adds complexity to home-based monitoring.

The fecal immunochemical test (FIT), which detects hemoglobin in stool using an antibody against human hemoglobin, is widely used for colorectal cancer screening. Several studies have shown that FIT levels are strongly correlated with endoscopic disease activity in UC, supporting its role as a simple and objective biomarker.14,15 Moreover, other studies have reported that FIT can predict endoscopic remission in UC with greater predictive accuracy than fecal calprotectin.16,17 In addition, a transition from negative to positive FIT results in remission has been associated with an increased risk of relapse, requiring treatment intensification.18 FIT also offers practical advantages, including lower cost, easier interpretation, and a well-established cutoff value based on colorectal cancer screening guidelines.19,20 Despite these advantages, the use of FIT for home monitoring of UC activity has not been studied, and no data are available regarding its feasibility, acceptability, or comparative accuracy with hospital-based FIT or fecal calprotectin.

To address this gap, we conducted a study to assess the feasibility and acceptability of home-based FIT in patients with UC. With the long-term goal of determining whether home-based FIT monitoring could facilitate early relapse detection and improve the prognosis, we examined whether patients could reliably perform home-based FIT and adhere to continued testing.

Methods

Patients and the study design

This prospective observational study was conducted at our hospital between October 2020 and October 2021. UC patients of 18-80 years of age who were scheduled to undergo colonoscopy and achieved clinical remission were eligible for inclusion. Patients with dementia or other conditions impairing their ability to comprehend the study protocol, or those unable to independently perform home-based FIT, were excluded.

Procedures and sample collection

Participants performed a home-based qualitative FIT using a commercially available kit (OC-Hemocatch® S1.0; Eiken Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) 1-3 days before colonoscopy. The handling of the kit is illustrated in Figure 1. Before starting the test, participants were provided with a written, illustrated instruction sheet (Figure 1). The attending physician only demonstrated the test kit to each participant and conducted an in-person tutorial on its proper use. Participants were then instructed to perform the test independently at home.

Stool sampling method and the handling of a commercially available kit. (A) Proper technique for stool sample collection: rub the entire surface of the stool evenly rather than sampling in situ. Pinpointing only 2-3 spots in the stool is not recommended. (B) Detailed instructions for using the kit and interpreting test results.

Simultaneously, two additional stool samples were collected: one for hospital-based quantitative FIT and one for a hospital-based quantitative fecal calprotectin analysis. Hospital-based FIT samples were processed and analyzed at our institution using an OC-SENSOR PLEDIA analyzer (Eiken Chemical, Tokyo, Japan). FIT values below 50 ng/mL were treated as 50 ng/mL in all analyses because the reliability of the results under this threshold was not guaranteed by the manufacturer. Samples for fecal calprotectin measurement were stored at −30 °C until shipment to a centralized laboratory (BML Inc., Tokyo, Japan), where fecal calprotectin was measured using a Phadia EliA Calprotectin 2 assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden).

For the home-based FIT, patients independently judged the result as either “positive” or “negative.” They also recorded their impression of the kit’s usability (categorized as “Easy,” “Acceptable,” “Difficult,” or “Troublesome”).

Assessment of disease activity

Clinical disease activity was assessed using a two-item patient-reported outcome (PRO-2), which consists of the rectal bleeding sub-score (RBS) and stool frequency sub-score (SFS),21 extracted from the Mayo score.22 Patients recorded stool frequency and rectal bleeding on the day of FIT collection. Clinical remission was defined as a PRO-2 score ≤1, rectal bleeding subscore of 0, and stool frequency subscore of ≤1.

Endoscopic disease activity was evaluated using the Mayo Endoscopic Subscore (MES), which assesses the most severely inflamed area during colonoscopy on a scale of 0-3.23 Endoscopic remission was defined as an MES of 0 or 1 throughout the colorectum, whereas complete endoscopic remission was defined as an MES of 0. All patients underwent a total colonoscopy with standard bowel preparation.

Continued home-based FIT monitoring

A subgroup of patients who satisfied the following criteria was enrolled in a continued follow-up cohort: clinical remission, negative home-based FIT, and hospital-based FIT <50 ng/mL. These patients underwent home-based FIT every 2 weeks for at least one year and stool frequency, rectal bleeding, and home-based FIT results were recorded at each time point. In the event of suspected symptom recurrence, additional home-based FIT measurements can be performed at the patient’s discretion. Patients were instructed to report their symptoms and home-based FIT results to their attending physicians at each outpatient visit. Colonoscopy was performed at the end of the follow-up period whenever feasible.

If the home-based FIT changed from negative to positive, patients with relapsed symptoms were recommended to visit the hospital. Patients who were asymptomatic or unable to visit the hospital continued to undergo a home-based FIT. Patients were informed that if severe symptoms appeared, regardless of the results of the home-based FIT, they would visit the hospital as soon as possible, as in routine medical care. In this study, FIT-positive conversion was defined as the occurrence of at least one positive home-based FIT result during the follow-up period. Clinical relapse was defined as a RBS of ≥1 or a PRO-2 score of >1, accompanied by the need for treatment escalation for UC.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the associations between home-based qualitative FIT results and hospital-based quantitative FIT values, fecal calprotectin levels, endoscopic findings, and clinical outcomes. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Logistic regression analyses with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of fecal biomarkers in predicting endoscopic remission and treatment escalation. Performance metrics included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy with 95% confidence interval (CI). Cutoff values were set at 50 ng/mL for quantitative FIT, based on the minimum detectable limit of the assay, and 250 μg/g for fecal calprotectin, based on previous studies.7,10 Statistical analyses were performed using JMP (ver. 17; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value of <.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and examination findings

Fifty-four patients with UC (23 males) were included in the study. Clinical characteristics, biomarker results, and endoscopic findings are summarized in Table 1. The median (range) age at colonoscopy was 47.0 (22.0-80.3) years, and the median (range) disease duration was 16.2 (0.08-40.6) years. The disease location was classified as pancolitis in 38 patients, left-sided colitis in 15 patients, and proctitis in 1 patient. On the day of colonoscopy, 47 patients (87.0%) were in clinical remission, while 7 (13.0%) had clinically active disease. The median (range) PRO-2 score for clinical activity was 0 (range, 0-5).

For predicting MES 0-1, the AUCs of home-based FIT, hospital-based FIT, and hospital-based fecal calprotectin were 0.90, 0.92, and 0.88, respectively. There were no significant differences among these markers (home-based FIT vs. hospital-based FIT, P = .071; home-based FIT vs. hospital-based fecal calprotectin, P = .69). For predicting MES 0, the AUCs of home-based FIT, hospital-based FIT, and hospital-based fecal calprotectin were 0.66, 0.68, and 0.73, respectively. The AUC of home-based FIT was comparable to that of hospital-based FIT (P = .64) but significantly lower than that of hospital-based fecal calprotectin (P = .0025).

All 38 (100%) patients with negative home-based FIT results had endoscopic remission (MES 0-1), and 22 (57.8%) also achieved complete endoscopic remission (MES 0). The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of home-based FIT for predicting endoscopic remission were 0.81, 1.00, 1.00, and 0.44, respectively (Table 2). The corresponding values for complete endoscopic remission were 0.88, 0.45, 0.58, and 0.81, respectively.

Feasibility of continued home-based FIT monitoring

Twenty patients were eligible for home-based FIT monitoring. Their characteristics are summarized in Table S2. The observation period was 1.4 (1.1-2.0) years.

Patients underwent a median of 34 (24-54) home-based FIT measurements, with a median of 1 (0-5) missed tests. The adherence rate to biweekly home-based FIT monitoring was 97.0% (82.1-100%) (Table S2). The distribution of adherence rates was as follows: 100% (n = 12; 60.0%), 90%-99% (n = 5; 25.0%), and 80%-89% in (n = 3; 15.0%), indicating high feasibility for long-term self-monitoring (Figure 4B).

Among the 20 patients, 12 (60.0%) experienced at least one FIT-positive conversion. Of these, 5 (41.7%) developed clinical relapse (Table 3). Clinical relapse was more frequent among patients with FIT-positive conversion than among those who remained FIT-negative (P = .055). Notably, in 3 of the 5 symptomatic patients, home-based FIT-positive conversion preceded the onset of clinical symptoms.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that home-based qualitative FIT has high predictive accuracy for endoscopic remission, comparable to hospital-based quantitative FIT and fecal calprotectin measured in a clinical setting. Moreover, home-based FIT was well accepted by the patients and showed high adherence rates. Notably, home-based FIT positivity demonstrated high sensitivity and a high NPV for detecting endoscopic activity, suggesting its potential as a reliable predictor of treatment intensification. In the continued home-based FIT monitoring cohort, none of the patients who remained FIT-negative experienced relapse or required treatment intensification, further underscoring the utility of FIT-negative results in ruling out disease activity. These findings suggest that home-based FIT is a promising tool for early detection of mild relapses, particularly when the interval between hospital visits is prolonged.

Regarding feasibility, adherence to continued home-based FIT was exceptionally high, at 97.0% (82.1%-100%). The simplicity and ease of handling of the FIT kit likely contributed to its acceptability and consistent implementation in routine clinical practice. Previous studies on home-based fecal calprotectin testing have also reported high adherence rates.11,12 However, factors such as reluctance to handle stool samples, difficulty in sample collection, forgetfulness, and lack of perceived benefits have been identified as barriers to adherence.24–27 Additionally, younger patients have been reported to experience greater discomfort in handling stool samples than older patients,27,28 potentially affecting adherence. The predominance of middle-aged participants in this study may have contributed to the high adherence observed. Therefore, strategies tailored to improve adherence in younger patients should be considered.

Notably, this monitoring tool demonstrated an NPV of 100%, enabling accurate exclusion of relapse and making it an ideal screening modality for early detection. In the continued home-based FIT monitoring cohort, none of the patients who remained FIT-negative throughout the follow-up period experienced clinical relapse or required treatment intensification. In contrast, 7 of the 12 (58.3%) patients who experienced FIT-positive conversion underwent medication escalation. This finding aligns with our previous report,17 in which none of the patients with sustained FIT negativity required treatment, whereas 25 of the 40 (63%) FIT-positive conversion cases relapsed. Although the PPV of home-based FIT remains relatively low, some patients exhibit FIT-positive conversion prior to the onset of clinical symptoms. Therefore, rather than warranting immediate action, positive conversion may serve as an early warning signal, prompting further assessment via endoscopy or clinical evaluation. Although confirmatory tests may be needed in FIT-positive cases, the low cost and excellent NPV of FIT make it a cost-effective screening tool suitable for longitudinal outpatient monitoring, especially in patients undergoing medication tapering or discontinuation.

A key advantage of home-based FIT is its lower cost relative to fecal calprotectin testing. In this study, the commercially available home-based FIT kit cost approximately $19 per test. In contrast, home-based fecal calprotectin testing kits cost approximately $80 per test.29 Home-based fecal calprotectin testing typically requires initial training by healthcare staff, digital result submission, and a centralized hospital-based analysis. In contrast, home-based FIT is easy to use, requires minimal time, and allows patients to interpret their results independently. As demonstrated by the high acceptability observed in this study, home-based FIT may be better suited for routine monitoring than home-based fecal calprotectin testing.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Second, adherence to fecal testing may have been higher than that observed in other institutions, as FIT-based monitoring was routinely implemented at our hospital. The relatively short duration of monitoring and older age of the study population may have contributed to the high adherence rates. Further studies with extended follow-up periods and diverse age groups are warranted to assess the long-term sustainability of adherence in real-world clinical settings. Third, home-based FIT results were not immediately transmitted electronically, but were recorded on paper and reported at clinic visits, introducing the possibility of recall bias or data entry errors. Fourth, potential confounding factors that could result in false-positive FIT or fecal calprotectin findings could not be completely eliminated in this study.30,31 Nevertheless, patients with identifiable risk factors, such as NSAID use or clinically evident infection, were excluded to partially mitigate their potential impact on biomarker results.

In conclusion, the home-based FIT is a feasible, cost-effective, and reliable tool for disease monitoring in patients with UC. It strongly correlates with fecal calprotectin levels and accurately assesses clinical and endoscopic remission. Importantly, its exceptionally high NPV enables reliable exclusion of relapse, making it ideally suited as a screening tool during remission. The high adherence rates suggest that it is well suited for long-term, self-managed monitoring. Future studies should explore whether incorporating home-based FIT into self-management strategies can contribute to better disease control and improve the quality of life of UC patients.
