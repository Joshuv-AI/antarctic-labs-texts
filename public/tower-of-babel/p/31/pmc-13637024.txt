Valorization of Silkworm Cocoons into Sustainable Nanomaterials: Applications and Challenges
Authors: Caroliny M. Santos, Thiago F. Santos, Shuchi Tiwari, Arnóbio Antônio da Silva Júnior, J. H. O. Nascimento
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silk fibroin (SFb) derived from silkworm cocoons (Bombyx mori) is increasingly recognized as an innovative biodegradable protein for sustainable applications. We discuss its wide biomedical and industrial applications based on the review of 1791 articles from 2001 to 2025 (Scopus). There has been a large increase in research, with 66.44% of publications and 74.84% of citations during 2019–2025. SFb-based nanomaterials have excellent performance in tissue engineering (18.53%), drug delivery (60.59%), and in novel areas, including flexible electronics and sustainable construction, owing to their high strength, controllable structure, and optical transparency. Crucially, exploiting ∼50,000 tonnes of annual silk industry cocoon valorization for aerogels, hydrogels, and NPs promotes the circular economy and minimizes environmental impact using green technologies (lower kg CO2/kg fibroin). These developments make a direct, but by no means exclusive, contribution to the UN Sustainable Development Goals, in particular health (SDG 3), innovation (SDG 9), sustainable production (SDG 12), and climate action (SDG 13). Still, scaling, costs, and the long-term environmental consequences are hurdles. However, such concerns will need to be addressed through sustainable biomanufacturing and life-cycle assessments if SFb can be used to its full potential for a greener future.

Introduction

The silk industry, one of the oldest economic activities of humanity, generates approximately 50,000 tons of waste annually (400,000 tons/year in dried cocoons), including sericin and discarded cocoons, which represent both an environmental challenge and an opportunity for sustainable valorization. , Silkworm cocoon waste refers to discarded cocoons, defective fibers, and sericin-rich residues generated during silk processing. These byproducts represent an underutilized source of silk fibroin for sustainable nanomaterials. These residues, rich in silk fibroin, a biocompatible and biodegradable protein extracted from the cocoons of the silkworm (Bombyx mori), possess unique properties, including high mechanical strength, flexibility, and optical transparency, making them promising for the development of sustainable nanomaterials. , The valorization of these byproducts aligns with the principles of the circular economy, reducing environmental impact and promoting sustainability, especially in the context of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). − The study of silk fibroin nanoparticles is justified by the need for alternatives to synthetic materials, often associated with high environmental impacts and dependence on nonrenewable resources. − Due to its remarkable biocompatibility and biodegradability, fibroin has gained prominence in several cutting-edge applications, as shown in Figure . In the biomedical field, its main uses include drug delivery, which represents the largest application area (60.59%). This trend is driven by its ability to form stable nanoparticle systems for controlled release. And tissue engineering (scaffolds, 18.53%), where it serves as a matrix for cell regeneration. In addition, it is used in wound dressings (healing, 10.6%) and sensors (biodevices, 1.41%) thanks to its mechanical properties and ability to interact with biological tissues. In industrial contexts, fibroin has also been applied in storage (energy, 5.51%), building materials (2.35%), and electronics (1.45%), highlighting its versatility as a functional and sustainable material in emerging technologies.

The objective of this review is to critically evaluate the valorization of the silkworm cocoon as a sustainable source of silk fibroin for nanomaterial development, highlighting scientific advances, challenges, and future perspectives. The approach adopted is bibliometric-systematic, using the Scopus database to analyze 1791 articles published between 2001 and 2025, after refining criteria such as document type and language. This methodology allows identifying trends, gaps, and future perspectives in research, contributing to directing efforts in sustainable innovation and valorization for the silk industry.

Methodological Approach

The methodology used was bibliometric analysis, a widely accepted scientific method that applies mathematical and statistical techniques to evaluate scientific production. , The study followed PRISMA guidelines, including identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion steps, as shown in Figure . The research was developed based on Scopus, due to the greater variety of publications compared to the Web of Science. Data collection was carried out in March 2026, covering all scientific publications since the beginning of the historical period. The bibliometric methodology of this article follows a structured process for the retrieval and analysis of scientific data similar to methodologies in the current literature. − It begins with the definition of the research framework and keywords, and strategy, performing a preliminary search in the Scopus database with the string (“silk fibroin OR fibroin OR silk”) AND (“cocoon waste OR silk waste OR discarded cocoon”) OR (“nanoparticle* OR nanofiber* OR hydrogel* OR drug delivery”) in the TITLE-ABS-KEY fields. After the initial search, the screening process was conducted in two stages: (i) title and abstract screening followed by (ii) full-text assessment. The inclusion criteria used were document type, language, and publication period (2001–2025). Performance Analysis evaluates aspects such as total publications, citations, countries, and keywords, while science mapping uses VOSviewer (version 1.6.19) with the following parameters: minimum occurrence of keywords = 20 (21 meet), normalization method = occurrences, clustering = 6 to identify thematic clusters. Content analysis examines the keywords and organizes the themes into clusters. Finally, the challenges and future perspectives stage suggests directions for future research.

Composition and Structure

The silkworm, Bombyx mori, spins silk protein fibers naturally, and the fibroin fiber is produced via a secretory pathway, whereas sericin coats and encases the fiber. This protein is produced in the silkworms’ silk and makes up the silk fiber. It starts with the silkworm secreting the fibroin and sericin proteins through the spinning spindle from its salivary glands. When the fiber is extruded, fibroin microfibrils are assembled into an ordered material, providing the fiber with its tensile and other properties. In fact, this fiber at the molecular level, repeating units of glycine (Gly), sericin (Ser), and alanine (Ala), forms (Gly-Ala-Gly-Ala-Gly-Ser) n , as shown in Figure for its chemical structure.

Sustainability and Challenges

The growing demand for sustainable approaches in materials processing has intensified in response to the urgent need to preserve natural resources and mitigate the environmental impact of industrial production. In the field of biomaterials, this trend manifests itself in the effort to develop materials that combine biological performance with ecological responsibility, eliminating toxic substances, reducing energy consumption, and promoting more sustainable life-cycle processes. However, the relationship between “biomaterial” and “sustainability” is not always straightforward or guaranteed. Although biomaterials are defined by their biological applications, it is a misconception to assume that they are inherently environmentally friendly. Silk, for instance, is well-known for its fibroin, a biocompatible, biodegradable, and highly resistant protein, which illustrates this contradiction. While fibroin is highly valued for regenerative and medical applications, its extraction and transformation often involve environmentally harmful processes, such as the use of harsh solvents and significant energy consumption. Figure , which depicts the processing cycle of silk fibroin, highlights stages, such as synthesis and dissolution, during which the natural raw material is converted into moldable forms for various purposes. This cycle, however, also exposes a critical issue: the gap between the material’s “green” potential and the industrial practices required to realize it. Dissolved silk can be used in promising fields such as drug delivery, cosmetics, aerogels, hydrogels, and tissue engineering, but the true sustainability of these applications depends on re-evaluating the extraction, purification, and processing methods involved. − While silk cocoons are the primary source, they can also be molded for a variety of purposes. The optimized silk processing cycle can reduce environmental impact while aligning with sustainability principles.

The carbon footprint of silk processing varies considerably depending on energy demand, the type of chemical reagents used, and the overall resource intensity. The values reported in the literature vary widely. The carbon footprint of raw silk ranged from 2.41 to 28.56 kg of CO2e/kg (11.8×), with 89.1 to 96.5% of emissions linked to energy; allocation reduced the carbon footprint by 9.66–12.45%. Hotspots were drying (48.1%; 49.3%) and spinning (up to 69%). Emissions were dominated by Scope 2 (A) or Scope 1 (B–E), with CH4 and N2O being relevant. Electric heating is advantageous when the emission factor is <0.435 kg CO2e/(kW·h). Carbon emissions in silk production derive mainly from the use of electricity and heat in stages such as spinning, knitting, weaving, drying, boiling, dyeing, and finishing, often dependent on coal, gas, or wood, in addition to indirect emissions from auxiliary inputs. Shuyi et al. reported 18,563 kg CO2eq per 100 kg of silk products (cocoon to fabric); the UK organization, Waste & Resources Action Program (WRAP) estimated 21,820 kg CO2eq/tonne from fiber to garment; and Faragò et al. calculated 1820.97, 1,912.59 and 1,846.98 kg CO2eq per 100 kg of yarn-dyed, printed and open-width dyed silk fabrics, respectively. Considering the complete production inventories presented in Table , it can be observed that the climatic impact associated with silk, which includes stages such as cultivation, breeding and processing, among them degumming with sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) and boiling, , typically ranges between 52.5 and 80.9 kg CO2e per kilogram of fiber, indicating that degumming contributes only a fraction of this total. Dissolution using lithium bromide (LiBr), due to the high energy cost of salt production and heating to around 60 °C, increases the footprint in kg CO2/kg fibroin. , Purification processes, such as dialysis, filtration, and centrifugation, can further raise the impact of kg of CO2/kg of fibroin, depending on scale and efficiency. In contrast, water-based methods, which avoid the use of toxic solvents, tend to show lower emissions (kg CO2/kg fibroin), mainly due to reduced chemical use and more moderate energy consumption. Although these values provide a perspective, the literature often presents them in isolation, emphasizing the optimization of specific stages without adequately considering sustainability from a systemic standpoint. Thus, several studies focus on unit operations, neglecting the interactions among stages, such as degumming, dissolution, and purification. Furthermore, few analyses incorporate the social or economic dimensions of sustainability, such as occupational safety, solvent recovery costs, or industrial-scale feasibility.

The widespread dependence on halide-based solvents such as LiBr illustrates this gap: despite well-known issues of toxicity and waste disposal, many studies continue to prioritize short-term efficiency over long-term environmental resilience. The literature also tends to treat “green” water-based processes as inherently sustainable, although these methods face significant challenges related to reproducibility, process control, and material quality at larger scales. Consequently, the notion of sustainability becomes relative, depending not only on CO2 metrics but also on broader environmental trade-offs associated with purity, durability, and biocompatibility. In the absence of standardized assessment frameworks, claims of “low-impact silk processing” risk being fragmented or inconsistent across studies. Future research should go beyond simple carbon accounting and aim for deeper integration among processing stages, redesigning degumming, dissolution, and purification as parts of a closed-loop, low-energy system rather than as isolated, energy-intensive steps. It is also necessary to invest in solvent innovation, exploring biobased or recyclable alternatives that can replace LiBr and other hazardous chemicals without compromising fibroin quality. Finally, the creation of standardized life-cycle assessment metrics and models is essential for consistently evaluating processing methods in both laboratory and industrial contexts, enabling fair comparisons and transparent sustainability measurements. Adopting this critical and holistic perspective brings silk biomaterial research closer to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly those related to responsible production (SDG 12), innovation (SDG 9), and health (SDG 3). − Ultimately, achieving true sustainability in silk processing requires not only technical optimization but also a systemic re-evaluation of how materials, energy, and ethics intersect in biomaterial development. In this way, the methods of processing silk, described in Table , present different advantages and challenges with varying impacts on CO2. The widely used degumming process is effective in producing pure fibroin, but has a moderate impact due to energy use and chemical waste disposal. , Dissolution, which offers high purity, uses toxic chemicals, which increases the carbon footprint. Purification improves material quality but is expensive and energy-intensive. Water processing, although more environmentally friendly, has scalability limitations and still requires energy. These processes highlight the need to seek more efficient and low-impact approaches. Adopting sustainable methods in the processing of silk biomaterials aligns with the SDGs, especially about health, innovation, and sustainability. Green processing techniques, which use renewable sources and low-energy technologies, help to create a greener industrial infrastructure and reduce carbon emissions. This not only contributes to human health by eliminating hazardous materials but also promotes more responsible production and consumption patterns, aligning with global efforts against climate change and favoring a more innovative and accountable industry.

Conclusions

Research on nanomaterials based on SFb extracted from silkworm cocoons (Bombyx mori) has revealed a rapidly expanding field, with publications (66.44% between 2019 and 2025) and citations (74.84% in the same period), consolidating its scientific and technological relevance. Using a bibliometric-systematic approach with 1,791 articles analyzed in the Scopus database (2001–2025), highlights the versatility of fibroin in applications ranging from biomedicine, with advances in tissue engineering, controlled drug delivery, and wound healing, to industrial innovations, such as flexible electronics, sustainable building materials, sensors, and energy storage devices. These applications, driven by fibroin’s unique biocompatibility, biodegradability, and tunability properties, position it as a promising alternative to synthetic materials, directly aligning with SDG 3, SDG 9, SDG 12, and SDG 13. The valorization of silk industry waste, such as sericin and discarded cocoons, exemplifies a circular economy model, transforming approximately 50,000 tons of biowaste annually into high-value products, such as nanoparticles, aerogels, and hydrogels, with reduced CO2 impact in the SFb of the water-based processes. However, challenges such as scalability, production cost, and quality standardization persist, requiring innovations in sustainable biomanufacturing, such as the SILK.IT project, and advances in recombinant DNA technologies. Furthermore, assessing the long-term environmental impact through LCA is essential to maximizing the sustainability of these materials. This work highlights the transformative potential of SFb as a pillar for sustainable development, promoting innovative solutions that integrate health, technology, and environmental preservation. Continued research, focusing on ecological processes and global interdisciplinary collaboration, will be crucial to overcoming current barriers and consolidating fibroin as a key material in building a greener and more equitable future, in line with global sustainability goals.
