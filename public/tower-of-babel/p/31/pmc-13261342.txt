Repeat cesarean delivery with hysterectomy: a sheep model to examine uterine scars
Authors: Alexander Paping, Elise Elsenmüller, Rebecca Mirbach, Julia Sommer, Alma Franziska Mackert, Loreen Ehrlich, Kerstin Melchior, Thomas Ziska, Lilli Bittner-Schwerda, Alexander Starke, Karin Heinichen, Wolfgang Henrich, Thorsten Braun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical Abstract AbstractCesarean delivery (CD) is one of the most common surgical procedures worldwide, with rising rates leading to an increasing impact of complications, such as uterine rupture during a trial of labor in a subsequent pregnancy. To date, only little information on uterine wound healing is available. The current challenge is to establish a suitable model and to define quantifiable outcome parameters of uterine scar tissue with the aim of comparing the effect of different surgical techniques on uterine scarring. This study presents a sheep model to examine the effects of different hysterotomy closure techniques. A total of 48 Merino ewes underwent planned CD. After one year, 40 ewes were mated again following estrus synchronization and received planned repeat CD with peripartum supracervical hysterectomy and sampling of uterine scar tissue without any major complications. Survival rates at repeat CD were 95.0 and 91.4% for ewes and lambs, respectively. Due to placement of non-absorbable sutures around the hysterotomy closure during the first CD, all scarred uterine wall segments could be identified. Of 80 uterine scars, 16 (20%) showed dehiscences with a macroscopic thinning of the uterine wall. A full-thickness uterine rupture was not found. Gomori trichrome staining was used to assess the collagen content histomorphometrically. It revealed a markedly scarred uterine wall. This translational model can be used to evaluate intraoperative approaches to optimize uterine wound healing, such as suturing techniques, pharmacological agents, or biomaterials, with the aim of reducing the risk of uterine rupture in humans and improving maternal outcomes in pregnancies after previous CD.Lay summaryCD is one of the most common operations worldwide. After a cesarean, a scar remains on the uterus. This scar may not heal properly, leading to the risk of serious complications in a subsequent pregnancy, such as uterine rupture. Despite this risk, very little is known about how the uterine wall heals after surgery, because suitable research models are scarce. This study shows that sheep can be used as a model to study uterine scar healing. A planned CD was performed in sheep. Sheep were mated again, and at the next planned cesarean, the uterus was removed and tissue from the scar was collected. The scars could be clearly identified, and some showed thinning similar to what is seen in humans. Microscopic analysis confirmed scar formation. This model will help researchers test new ways to improve uterine healing and make pregnancies after CD safer.

Introduction

Uterine rupture during a trial of labor after cesarean delivery (CD) is a serious condition associated with high neonatal morbidity and mortality (Al-Zirqi et al. 2018). The underlying cause is impaired wound healing, leading to the formation of a uterine scar that is less stable than intact myometrium. Unfortunately, uterine wound healing has been investigated only to a limited extent, and available data remain scarce. As of today, no drugs or surgical techniques have been introduced into the clinic that significantly enhance uterine wound healing after CD to the extent that they completely eliminate the risk of uterine rupture in a subsequent pregnancy (Tinelli et al. 2020, Debras et al. 2024). The development of such treatment strategies requires a solid foundation of basic research. However, in the emergency of uterine rupture, obtaining samples of the scarred uterine wall for fundamental research is impractical and challenging to standardize. Moreover, it is not feasible to excise large amounts of human uterine tissue from scarred and unscarred regions of the uterus, besides the difficulty of properly identifying scarred areas during cesarean sections (Paping et al. 2022). A current review on the topic has therefore concluded that ‘to better understand […] the biology of myometrial healing after [cesarean delivery] and myomectomy, since it is not possible to study the human being, it would be advisable to investigate the uterine healing in animals’ (Tinelli et al. 2020). A few studies have evaluated uterine wound healing in rodent models (Buhimschi et al. 2010, Li et al. 2011, Lin et al. 2012, Xu et al. 2017). However, the anatomy of rodents and humans differs quite strongly, which makes it difficult to translate all results from one to the other species. In addition, analyses were performed after euthanizing animals few days to weeks after CD to analyze effects of interventions on the early wound healing phases. However, pregnancy is accompanied by profound uterine remodeling, and the mechanical stress caused by fetal growth leads to progressive stretching of the scar tissue. To better understand the reasons for uterine rupture during labor, it therefore appears more adequate to evaluate biomechanical and histological aspects as well as gene expression of factors involved in uterine scar remodeling during a pregnancy after CD. This manuscript presents a sheep model for the study of uterine scar tissue in a subsequent pregnancy after a previous CD with a focus on feasibility, scar localization, and sampling strategy. More detailed quantitative histological and biomechanical comparisons are planned for subsequent analyses using this model. To enable systematic sampling of uterine tissues at repeat CD, peripartum hysterectomy was performed. Due to the limited literature on ovine pregnancy rates and outcomes following CD, the primary research question in evaluating this repeat CD sheep model was whether Merino ewes can become pregnant after CD and whether repeat CD with peripartum hysterectomy and comprehensive uterine scar sampling is feasible (Waage & Wangensteen 2013). As Merino ewes are usually mated in autumn, this study additionally evaluated which seasons allow successful estrus synchronization and mating. Intraoperatively, adhesions were classified using a previously published score for humans (Tulandi & Lyell 2013). Furthermore, in contrast to rodent studies, the ovine uterus is much larger and it is not possible to evaluate the cross section of one uterine horn in one histological section that always contains the scar. Therefore, the macroscopic retrievability of uterine scars during repeat CD was analyzed. Finally, to evaluate ovine uterine scars under similar conditions compared with human CD scars, it appears essential that the tissue is exposed to mechanical stress due to distension of the uterus caused by growth of the fetus.

Materials and methods

Study design and experimental animals

As published earlier, 48 Merino ewes received a first CD at 142–144 days of gestation (dG) (Paping et al. 2023a). Two hysterotomies were performed per ewe, regardless of the number of fetuses: one in each of the two uterine horns. Six non-absorbable sutures (Prolene® 2-0 SH, Ethicon, Germany) were placed around the closed hysterotomies to facilitate localization of the scarred uterine wall segment during repeat CD (Fig. 1A). After an interval of at least one year, a further pregnancy was brought about by estrus synchronization with intravaginal application of 20 mg flugestone acetate (Chronogest CR, MSD, USA), followed by 500 IU intramuscular PMSG (Pregmagon, Ceva Santé Animal, France) and scheduled mating with a breeding ram. When unsuccessful, the synchronization attempt was repeated. CD with peripartum hysterectomy was performed at 142 dG or later (Fig. 1B).

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the country directorate of Saxony (Landesdirektion Sachsen, TVV 03/22).

Preoperative preparation, anesthesia, and perioperative medication

Protocols were implemented as published earlier (Paping et al. 2023a). In short, all ewes received an intramuscular injection of dexamethasone 10 mg on the day before CD. Sacrococcygeal epidural anesthesia with xylazine and intravenous ketamine was combined with incision line infiltration as multimodal pain management under preoperative NSAID administration. After delivery of the lambs, anesthesia was deepened with intravenous xylazine. As published earlier, all ewes received amoxicillin, meloxicam, and anti-tetanus serum (Paping et al. 2023a).

Operation protocol

Repeat CD was performed similar to first CD (Paping et al. 2023a). In short, ewes were placed supine on an operating table with the upper body elevated by 30° and a paramedian abdominal incision of approximately 15 cm was performed in the region of the previous CD scar. Intra-abdominal adhesions due to the previous CD were classified according to Tulandi and Lyell (Table 1) (Tulandi & Lyell 2013).

Uterine horns were incised in the large curvature outside the former hysterotomy area, followed by gentle delivery of the lambs. Subsequently, a supracervical hysterectomy with the removal of both adnexa was performed. As hysterectomy is not a standard procedure in sheep, the technique of abdominal supracervical hysterectomy was adapted to the ovine anatomy. First, the whole uterus was exteriorized (Fig. 2A).

(B) Devascularization of the uterus with Rochester–Ochsner hemostatic forceps. Uterine arteries are highlighted. (C) Cervical remnant after supracervical hysterectomy without hematoma. (D) Cervical remnant after supracervical hysterectomy with a 3cm hematoma in the area of the left uterine artery (marked).

Four Rochester-Ochsner hemostatic forceps with long jaws and no spikes were placed on the lower uterine segment (Fig. 2B). These clamps were chosen instead of Wertheim hysterectomy clamps as they allow grasping more tissue at once and cause less damage to the fragile ovine uterine wall tissue, thus minimizing the risk of strong bleeding and hematomas. Following clamp placement, uterine devascularization was performed from lateral to medial with multiple sutures in the clamped area, followed by stepwise extirpation of the uterus using surgical scissors. For effective devascularization, sutures with a large needle (Vicryl 1 CTX & Vicryl 2 TP-1, Ethicon) proved to be useful. After hemostasis was achieved, the cervical remnant was closed with a continuous suture. The remaining two forceps on the cervical remnant were opened slowly while the cervical remnant was held cranially by two corner sutures to prevent it from sinking back into the pelvis and thus to ensure adequate access to possible uterine artery hematomas occurring following clamp removal (Fig. 2C and D). At the same time, a suture with a large needle (Vicryl 2 TP-1, Ethicon) was kept ready for quick reaction in case of hematoma formation. All further steps, including the administration of oxytocin and closure of the abdomen, were performed as published earlier (Paping et al. 2023a). In particular, closure of the peritoneum, abdominal wall musculature, and fascia was performed with U-stitches (Starke & Kehler 2004, Paping et al. 2023a).

Sampling

Sampling of the uterus was performed immediately after hysterectomy in a laboratory next to the operating room. Multiple samples were taken from unscarred uterine wall, scarred uterine wall of both hysterotomies, amniotic membrane, umbilical cord, umbilical artery blood, umbilical vein blood, and ovary, following a standardized sampling scheme using a template.

Gomori trichrome staining

Gomori trichrome staining was performed to identify scarred tissue as previously described (Schwickert et al. 2022, Paping et al. 2023b). Paraffin sections 5 μm thick were cut and dewaxed in xylene (2 × 5 min; J T Baker, USA). Rehydration was carried out in ethanol (2 min each in 96, 70, 50%; Carl Roth, Germany), followed by incubation for 30 min at 56°C in Bouin solution. The sections were rinsed under running water for 5 min and incubated in Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (Merck, Germany) for 10 min, followed by another rinsing under running water for 10 min. Afterward, samples were incubated in trichrome solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 25 min. Differentiation was done in 0.5% acetic acid (Merck, Germany) for 2 and 1 min, followed by dehydration in ethanol (1 min 96%, 2 × 2 min 100%) and xylene (2 × 5 min). The sections were then covered with Entellan® Neu (Merck, Germany) and a cover glass. Due to staining, cytoplasm and erythrocytes are red, fibrin and muscle pink, nuclei blue to black, and collagen fibers green.

Animal care

Housing and husbandry, postoperative care and medication of the ewes, and neonatal care and feeding of the lambs were carried out as published earlier (Paping et al. 2023a).

Statistical analysis

Due to the impracticality of systematic extensive sampling of human uterine scar tissue during repeat cesarean deliveries, very little fundamental research data are available on human uterine scarring, especially with respect to the effect of the hysterotomy suturing technique on uterine wound healing (Paping et al. 2022, 2023b). As published earlier, sample size calculation was performed with the software nQuery + nTerim 3.0 based on a publication that compares the ultrasonographic aspect of uterine scars in humans (O’Brien & Muller 1993, Sevket et al. 2014, Paping et al. 2023a). In the study, 36 women were randomized into two groups and underwent closure of the hysterotomy by single-layer or double-layer suture during elective CD (Sevket et al. 2014). The primary outcome measure is the thickness of the lower uterine segment in mm on transvaginal ultrasound (hydrosonography) 6 months after CD. Analysis of variance showed that to detect differences between four groups, at least 11 samples per group would be needed (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, effect size = 0.28). Therefore, 12 animals had originally been included per group (4 × 12 = 48). Continuous data are reported as median and interquartile range, and categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentage. Comparisons between groups were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test, chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test. The data analysis was performed using R within the RStudio environment and the tidyverse suite of packages for data manipulation and visualization (Wickham et al. 2019, R Core Team 2021, Posit Team 2023). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As Merino ewes usually conceive singletons or twins, it is possible that a horn does not carry a fetus in the second pregnancy (Paping et al. 2023a). To assess uterine wound healing post-CD, it appears reasonable to include only the scars on horns that have contained a fetus in both or at least in the second pregnancy in analyses, as only in these cases, the uterine scar is stretched by the growth of the fetus. To allow for appropriate sample size calculation in future studies, we examined the number of uterine horns carrying a fetus in either both pregnancies or at least in the second pregnancy.

Results

Synchronization outcomes after previous CD

Outcome parameters are presented in Tables 2 and 3. During the planned interval of at least 365 days between the first CD and the second pregnancy, three out of 48 ewes that had received the first CD died of reasons not associated with the study and one ewe gave birth vaginally due to an undetected pregnancy. A total of 44 ewes were mated again, out of which 40 ewes became pregnant and underwent repeat CD and hysterectomy with sampling (90.9%). Four ewes did not become pregnant again despite four to six synchronization attempts. The median time between the first CD and the second pregnancy was less than 15 months (388 (382–475) days). Most pregnancies occurred after the first synchronization cycle (67.5%) with only three ewes needing four cycles (7.5%). Most pregnancies were achieved in the months April to June (45.0%). The number of lambs per ewe was significantly lower due to more singletons in the second pregnancy (mean number of lambs per ewe: 1.5 vs 1.9, P < 0.01).

Surgery

Repeat CD with hysterectomy and tissue sampling could be carried out as planned at 142–144 days of gestation in all pregnant ewes (n = 40). There were no pregnancy complications or spontaneous preterm deliveries. Entry into the abdominal cavity via the paramedian laparotomy scar from the first CD was always possible (Fig. 3A). In most of the ewes, adhesions were found, with adhesions between omentum and uterus or abdominal fascia being the most common (Table 3) (Fig. 3B, C, D). All adhesions were removed easily with bipolar scissors.

Ovine laparotomy scar and intra-abdominal adhesions. (A) Paramedian laparotomy scar. (B) Dense adhesions between omentum and abdominal fascia. (C) Filmy adhesions between uterus and omentum. (D) Dense adhesions between uterus and omentum (the dashed arrow points to the non-absorbable suture).

Only 43/80 uterine horns carried a fetus in both pregnancies (53.8%). 76.8% of horns with at least one fetus in the second pregnancy had also previously carried a fetus (43/56). A total of 24 uterine horns (30.0%) did not carry a fetus in the second pregnancy. Supracervical hysterectomy proved to be challenging, since due to the location of the uterus in the pelvis behind the udder, paramedian laparotomy could not be extended caudally as much as in humans. Uterine extirpation was thus performed as caudally as possible but as cranially as needed, to ensure adequate surgical access to the cervical remnant. The ovaries were located close to the lower uterine segment – much more caudal compared with the human anatomy – and thus at the level of supracervical extirpation of the uterus. It therefore appeared not practical to leave the ovaries in situ while removing the uterus so that supracervical hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy was performed. Uterine arteries were very prominent so that meticulous devascularization had to be performed to prevent hemorrhage from these vessels into the tissue, which can cause rapid occurrence of cervical hematomas that are difficult to control surgically due to the restricted access to the lower uterine segment. The median duration of the operation was 114 min and thus significantly longer than the first CD (76 min) (P < 0.001). There were no surgical complications, and all ewes passed the postoperative period uneventful. Two ewes died perioperatively of cardiovascular failure of unknown cause after sampling and before laparotomy closure were completed.

Uterine dehiscence and scarring of the uterine wall

All uterine scars could be identified due to the non-absorbable sutures that had been placed around the hysterotomy during the first CD (two uterine scars per ewe, resulting in a total of 80 scars in 40 ewes). Of 80 uterine scars, 16 (20%) showed dehiscences, defined as a macroscopic thinning of the uterine wall (Fig. 4A, B, C). No uterus showed a full uterine rupture with a hole in the uterine wall and a defect in the serosa. All samples could be taken from the uterus as planned. Gomori trichrome staining revealed a markedly scarred uterine wall (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

This translational study advances the current understanding of uterine wound healing by presenting the first large-scale sheep model specifically designed to evaluate CD scars using a standardized and comprehensive sampling protocol. This model enables systematic investigation of intraoperative strategies to improve uterine repair and provides mechanistic insights that cannot be obtained in human studies. Furthermore, the model allows the assessment of intra-abdominal adhesions and of approaches to reduce adhesion formation. Our results show that Merino ewes tolerate CD extremely well: 9 of 10 animals conceived again after their initial CD, confirming previous suggestions that fertility is largely preserved. Although Merino sheep are considered aseasonal breeders, our synchronization results revealed seasonal variation, with conception rates highest in spring, consistent with earlier reports (Kleemann et al. 2006, Edmondson et al. 2012). No ewe experienced pregnancy-related complications in either gestation, including pregnancy toxemia or spontaneous preterm birth (Paping et al. 2023a). Repeat CD combined with supracervical hysterectomy was feasible using a modified technique similar to human obstetrics. As expected, operative times increased compared with CD alone due to extensive intra-abdominal adhesions, reflected by a median adhesion score comparable to human data (Nuamah et al. 2017).

Precise retrieval of uterine scars proved challenging. Only 20% of scars were macroscopically detectable through partial wall dehiscence, highlighting the necessity of marking the hysterotomy with non-absorbable sutures. This technique was essential for accurate localization during repeat CD. Ultimately, 56 of 80 horns (70%) were analyzable due to the fact that these horns contained a fetus in the second pregnancy. The possibility of including up to two horns per ewe helps to ensure robust sample numbers for downstream tissue analyses.

The literature on ovine fertility and outcomes after CD remains limited. A Norwegian cohort of 162 ewes undergoing CD around gestational day 147 reported an 89% conception rate after previous CD and fewer live-born lambs compared with vaginal birth due to smaller litters (Waage & Wangensteen 2013). Our observations mirror these findings, with a 90.9% conception rate after CD and reduced litter size in the subsequent pregnancy (mean lambs/ewe: 1.5 vs 1.9, P < 0.01). A recent study of breeding records following vaginal delivery (n = 233) and elective caesarean section (n = 122) also confirmed a significant reduction in litter size in the first pregnancy immediately following a CD in comparison with animals that had previously delivered vaginally (P = 0.001) (Voigt et al. 2024). It therefore appears plausible that uterine scarring due to CD influences ovine fertility, although dedicated longitudinal data on ewes with consecutive vaginal births only would be required to fully exclude age-related variation as a contributing factor.

In humans, fear of uterine rupture has led to extensive epidemiological research and a growing preference for planned repeat CD (Deshmukh et al. 2024). For women attempting a trial of labor after cesarean (TOLAC), ultrasonographic measurement of lower uterine segment thickness may help identify patients at increased rupture risk (Swift et al. 2019). However, no proven technique or pharmaceutical intervention currently exists to enhance uterine healing to the extent of lowering rupture rates (Debras et al. 2024). A prospective human study evaluating scar tissue in the next pregnancy is not feasible: long-term follow-up is unreliable, many women will deliver vaginally after a first CD, and others will deliver in different hospitals. Furthermore, marking the uterine scar with non-absorbable materials would be ethically unacceptable, as this may cause foreign body granulomas (Sayari et al. 2014). These constraints fundamentally limit the quality of human tissue-based investigations. A recent review therefore concluded that to better understand the biology of myometrial healing after CD or myomectomy, it is advisable to study uterine healing in animals (Tinelli et al. 2020).

To date, available mechanistic insights stem predominantly from rodent models (Li et al. 2011, Lin et al. 2012, Xu et al. 2017, Debras et al. 2025), which differ substantially in uterine size and physiology. The very small dimensions of rat uteri limit their translational value for evaluating surgical approaches such as hysterotomy closure techniques (Vasilenko et al. 1981). One small study in eight ewes examined histological and biomechanical differences in scars after single- versus double-layer suturing but was likely underpowered and investigated scars in the non-gravid uterus, which is less relevant for rupture risk (Lapointe-Milot et al. 2014). In contrast, our model evaluates mature scars under conditions of uterine distension and contractility during pregnancy, aligning more closely with human physiology.

From a clinical perspective, improved strategies to prevent pathological uterine scarring are urgently needed. Symptomatic uterine rupture during TOLAC occurs in 0.2–0.7% of cases (Landon et al. 2004, Al-Zirqi et al. 2017) and is associated with high neonatal morbidity and a mortality rate of up to 26.2% (Al-Zirqi et al. 2018). Fear of rupture influences decision-making, contributing to the rising rates of planned repeat CD. Yet, repeat CD itself carries increased risks, including adhesions, iatrogenic injuries, and placenta accreta spectrum disorders (Silver et al. 2006). Large-animal models such as ours therefore represent a crucial intermediate step for safely translating novel interventions from rodent studies into human obstetric practice (Paping et al. 2023a).

Future work will use this model to systematically test surgical, biomaterial-based, and pharmacologic strategies to enhance uterine healing. Importantly, the model enables assessment not only of mature scars but also of healing dynamics during pregnancy – highly relevant for conditions such as open fetal surgery, where multiple hysterotomies and short inter-surgical intervals elevate rupture risk (Zepf et al. 2022).

This study also provides practical insights for planning future experiments. It demonstrates that pregnancies after CD and repeat CD near term are both feasible. Synchronization success appears strongest in spring and autumn. Approximately 70% of uterine horns are typically analyzable due to the presence of a fetus, which should guide sample size calculations. The data from this study seem not to imply that implantation of ovine pregnancies occurs preferentially in a specific horn: 67.2% (43/64) of pregnant horns from the first study pregnancy as opposed to 81.3% (13/16) of non-pregnant horns carried a fetus in the second pregnancy. Attrition over multi-year study periods – including age-related losses, as seen in two older ewes in this study – supports including reserve animals in each experimental group.

Strengths of this model include its standardized design, large cohort of 40 Merino ewes, low perioperative mortality, and sampling of gravid uterine tissue, which is particularly valuable for biomechanical assessments (Buhimschi et al. 2010, Lin et al. 2012, Lapointe-Milot et al. 2014, Xu et al. 2017). The use of non-absorbable markers ensured precise scar localization, and the anatomy and type of pregnancy in sheep closely resemble the human condition. Accordingly, the presence of a macroscopic thinning of the uterine wall – and therefore a macroscopically identifiable scar – in a reasonable amount of uterine horns (20%) suggests that hysterotomy and its closure affect uterine wall integrity in a similar way in human beings and sheep, which underlines the translational potential of this model. Meanwhile, macroscopic thinning likely represents the visually apparent end of a continuous spectrum of scar remodeling and weakening. Beyond the aspect of visible scar thinning, the model has been designed to capture a broader range of histological and biomechanical alterations, including scars that appear macroscopically intact. In future studies using this model, the application of standardized histological and biomechanical parameters can help to study impaired uterine wound healing even in scars without overt dehiscence.

Limitations include the long interval between pregnancies, the requirement for substantial facilities and personnel, and the inability to analyze very early phases of wound healing. As this manuscript focuses on feasibility, scar localization, and sampling strategy, detailed quantitative histological and biomechanical comparisons lie outside the scope of this work but are planned for subsequent analyses using this model, which is specifically intended to enable histological stratification of weak versus robust scars through biomechanical testing. In future, studies might include ex vivo morphometric measurements on histological sections (e.g. minimal myometrial thickness at the scar versus adjacent intact myometrium) and establish a quantitative cutoff (e.g. a certain percentage reduction in myometrial thickness) to define dehiscence or ‘rupture-like’ thinning in a reproducible manner. Furthermore, we acknowledge that applying the effect size derived from a human clinical ultrasound study (Sevket et al. 2014) to estimate the sample size for this ovine model represents a methodological limitation, as interspecies differences in uterine wall structure and wound healing kinetics may influence variability in uterine scarring. To mitigate this, the study was designed as an exploratory investigation to generate baseline data for future power calculations specific to the sheep model.

Conclusion

Uterine rupture during TOLAC remains a rare but severe complication with significant maternal and neonatal consequences. Despite its importance, no intraoperative techniques or pharmacologic agents have yet been shown to reliably enhance uterine healing after CD. Because tissue sampling during a subsequent human pregnancy is not feasible, translational animal models are essential. We developed and validated a robust sheep model that permits comprehensive evaluation of uterine scars during the next pregnancy. This model offers a valuable platform for testing suture techniques, biomaterials, and pharmacologic interventions with the long-term goal of improving uterine healing and reducing rupture risk in humans.
