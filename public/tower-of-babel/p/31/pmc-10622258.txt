Is Sliding Compression Necessary for Intramedullary Nailing Fixation of AO/OTA Type A3.3 Intertrochanteric Fracture?
Authors: Cong Zhang, Zhangxin Chen, Mengyuan Wang, Huiyu Chen, Lingqi Zhu, Wenqing Yang, Zhenqi Ding, Guofeng Huang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe intramedullary nail is considered the gold standard for treating AO/OTA type A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures. However, it still faces a significant rate of failure, mainly due to the critical factor of comminuted lateral wall defects leading to inadequate proximal sliding compression. The primary objective of this study is to investigate the requirement of sliding compression in the treatment of unstable AO/OTA type A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures. To achieve this, we conduct a comparative analysis between two approaches: InterTAN alone and proximal femoral anti‐rotation blade nailing (PFNA) combined with lateral wall reconstruction for treating AO/OTA type A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures with lateral wall damage.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of patients who underwent intramedullary nailing fixation for AO/OTA type A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures at our hospital from January 2012 to January 2022. Patient characteristics as well as treatment details, including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, weight‐bearing time, fracture healing time, tip apex distance (TAD) loss, Harris hip scores (HHS), Parker–Palmer mobility score (PPMS), and postoperative complications, were collected and analyzed. Continuous variables were analyzed using independent sample t‐tests, while categorical variables were examined using the chi‐square test. For group comparisons, variance analysis was applied, and pairwise comparisons were conducted using the LSD‐t test.ResultsThese patients were divided into PFNA combined with lateral wall reconstruction group (sliding compression group) and InterTAN fixation group (static fixation group) based on surgical methods. The operation time, intraoperative bleeding loss, HHS at 12 months and PPMS at 12 months in the sliding compression group were significantly higher than those in the static fixation group, and time to weight‐bearing and fracture healing time were significantly lower than those in the static fixation group (p < 0.05). There were no significant differences between two groups in terms of the TAD at 2 days, 2, and 12 months postoperatively, the incidence of complications (p > 0.05). At 6 months postoperatively, femoral neck length was shortened compared to 2 days postoperatively in both groups, and the sliding compression group had a significantly greater degree of femoral neck shortening than the static fixation group (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe use of PFNA with lateral wall reconstruction for A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures demonstrated superior mobility, efficiency, and reduced internal fixation failure rates compared to InterTAN. These findings suggest that sliding compression may be required for intramedullary nailing treatment.Keywords: InterTAN, Lateral wall reconstruction, PFNA, Type A3.3 intertrochanteric fracture

Introduction

Intramedullary and extramedullary fixation are surgical options for intertrochanteric fractures. Intramedullary fixation options include proximal femoral anti‐rotation nail (PFNA) and InterTAN nails. According to the AO/OTA classification, Fracture types A2.2 and higher are considered as unstable fractures. 1 Both intramedullary and extramedullary fixation can achieve good results for stable intertrochanteric fractures. 2 However, extramedullary fixation is associated with a high failure rate of internal fixation, excessive postoperative internal rotation of the femoral shaft, and Coxa vara for unstable fractures due to its insufficient biomechanical stability. 3 Thus, intramedullary nailing, especially for AO/OTA A3.3 fractures that involve lateral wall injury and the inability to reconstruct the medial wall, is considered the gold standard for the treatment of unstable intertrochanteric fractures. 4

InterTAN functions effectively to distribute stresses on the lateral cortex and function as a replacement for the lateral wall. 5 However, for unstable intertrochanteric fractures with an incomplete lateral wall, InterTAN's locking fixation pattern is inadequate in providing dynamic sliding compression to the fracture end, and this can prolong the duration of the healing process. PFNA, on the other hand, utilizes its helical blade and main nail to achieve enhanced axial compression and rotational stability, resulting in dynamic compression of the fracture end. 6 Previous studies have demonstrated that both sliding compression and static fixation are effective treatments for stable intertrochanteric fractures. 7 In a relevant meta‐analysis, 8 the authors indicated that PFNA outperforms InterTAN in terms of intraoperative blood loss (MD 27.02, p = 0.04) and fluoroscopy usage (MD 1.3, p = 0.02). However, it is inferior to Intertan in terms of revision rates (OR 0.27, p = 0.0003) and hip pain (OR 0.53, p = 0.00005). Nonetheless, for unstable intertrochanteric fractures, intramedullary nails alone cannot achieve effective sliding compression. Specifically, incomplete lateral wall support fails to stabilize the bone fragments at the femoral head and neck, leading to displacement of the proximal fragment and retraction of the lag screw, ultimately resulting in the failure of PFNA sliding compression. 9 Recent research has shown that the addition of a plate to reconstruct the lateral wall can assist PFNA in achieving sliding compression for unstable fractures. 10 In patients with AO type A3 intertrochanteric fractures, the group treated with PFNA and an additional plate demonstrated shorter fracture healing time (11.8 ± 3.6% vs. 14.2 ± 2.8 weeks, p = 0.03) and a lower rate of internal fixation failure and complications (6.3% vs. 31.6%, p = 0.06) compared to the group treated with PFNA alone. 11

There is a dearth of literature exploring the use of sliding compression techniques for proximal fractures beyond the type A3.3 classification. The objectives of this study were: (i) to explore the necessity of utilizing sliding compression in the treatment of A3.3 unstable intertrochanteric fractures; (ii) to assess whether the implementation of sliding compression could potentially raise the occurrence of internal fixation failure. To achieve this goal, we conducted a retrospective analysis of the efficacy and safety of two treatment approaches: the sliding compression group (PFNA fixation combined with lateral wall reconstruction) and the static fixation group (InterTAN) in patients with AO/Orthopedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) A3.3 Intertrochanteric fracture who were admitted to our hospital between January 2012 and January 2022.

Methods

Patient's Selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Dongnan Hospital of Xiamen University and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration (No. 2022122901). The only interventions administered were PFNA with lateral wall reconstruction or IT for A3.3 Intertrochanteric fractures.

Inclusion criteria for this retrospective study included: (1) patients diagnosed with AO/OTA type A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures with incomplete lateral wall; (2) patients aged between 60 and 85 years; (3) patients with fractures resulting from trauma; (4) patients treated with either PFNA accompanied by lateral wall reconstruction or only InterTan for intramedullary nailing at our center. The PFNA and InterTan fixations were provided by the Synthes GmbH (Waldenburg, Switzerland); and (5) patients with high postoperative compliance and who were followed up for at least 12 months.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who were unable to ambulate and required prolonged bed rest before surgery; (2) patients with pathological fractures attributed to tumor; (3) patients who presented with other associated injuries upon preoperative examination; (4) patients with open fractures, multiple fractures, or subtrochanteric fractures; and (5) patients who exhibited poor reduction of the fracture during postoperative reexamination, which was defined as significant coxa vara with a femoral neck‐shaft angle of less than 120° or lateral wall block greater than 2.5 cm. 12

Patients with unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures with incomplete lateral walls were identified through preoperative radiographs and CT scans. Conventional anteroposterior (AP) and lateral pelvis X‐ray examinations were conducted, and the AO/OTA classification was determined. As this was a retrospective study, the need for written informed consent was waived by the committee.

Surgical Techniques

All patients were categorized into two groups based on their intramedullary nail fixation methods: dynamic compression group and static fixation group. The former underwent PFNA fixation combined with lateral wall reconstruction while the latter received only InterTAN fixation. All surgical procedures were conducted by an orthopedic surgical team, consisting of two senior doctors with equivalent surgical proficiency.

Postoperative Management

All patients adhered to a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol until their hospital discharge. Postoperative antibiotic therapy was administered for 24 h to prevent infection, and low‐molecular‐weight heparin was initiated on the second postoperative day to prevent deep vein thrombosis in the lower extremities. Passive joint functional activities and exercises were initiated on the day following the operation, and the intensity of functional exercises were gradually increased. Partial weight‐bearing functional training was initiated under the guidance of a physician after 2 weeks.

Clinical Observations

Baseline demographic data were collected, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), the affected side of the injury, etiology, comorbidities, and the quality of reduction. Therapeutic evaluative indices such as operation time, intra‐operative blood loss, weight‐bearing time, fracture union time, Harris hip score (HHS), Parker–Palmer mobility score (PPMS), tip‐apex distance on postoperative day 2, tip‐apex distance variables at 2 and 12 months, as well as complications such as cut out, screw migration, loss of reduction, nonunion, and infections were recorded. Weight‐bearing time was defined as the duration from postoperative period until the patient's first attempt to bear weight on the affected limb using crutches or a walking aid on the floor. 13 During this time, the affected limb is allowed to make contact with the floor, but without actively exerting force. Subsequently, under the guidance of a physician, the patient is required to undergo a series of functional exercises in order to gradually increase the weight‐bearing capacity of the affected lower limb and transition to full weight‐bearing. Our orthopedic center at the hospital follows a standardized rehabilitation protocol. Nonunion was defined as incomplete fracture healing 9 months after the injury, with no signs of progress in the preceding 3 months as observed on radiographs. 14 Severe osteoporosis reduces mechanical strength, leading to a higher likelihood of fracture in individuals with decreased bone density, with severe osteoporosis defined as T‐score ≤ −2.5. 15

Postoperative evaluations were carried out at 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months following the surgical procedure. At each follow‐up, femoral X‐ray examination was conducted in both the anteroposterior and lateral views. The duration of surgery was measured as the time between the start of repositioning and wound closure. The functional states and mobility levels was evaluated based on the HHS and the PPMS. 16

The calculation of the Tip‐Apex Distance (TAD) has been extensively documented. 17 It involves measuring the sum of the distances from the tip of the traction screw to the tip of the femoral head on both anteroposterior and lateral X‐ray images. We adjusted the X‐ray magnification using the known diameter of the hip joint screw. All images were exported to Surgimap software (Nemaris Inc., New York, USA; version 2.3.2.1) for measuring the TAD on each X‐ray. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, it was not possible to ensure that all X‐ray anteroposterior and lateral images were standardized. TAD measurements were independently performed by two experienced orthopedic surgeons and a skilled radiologist. By averaging the TAD values, we calculated the overall TAD. Each measurement for each set of X‐rays was repeated in a blinded manner after 1 month, and the two sets of measurements were compared using the same method. Cohen's kappa test was employed to assess intra‐rater reliability.

All hip fractures reductions were carried out before the operation commenced, using a specialized fracture table and assessed with the aid of fluoroscopy. Postoperative reduction quality was evaluated as good, acceptable, or poor, based on two factors: alignment and displacement of the main fragments. 18 , 19 Alignment criteria consisted of normal cervicodiaphyseal angle or slight valgus (anteroposterior view), and less than 20° of angulation (lateral view). Displacement criteria included displacement less than 1 cortical thickness on the medial cortex (anteroposterior view) and less than 1 cortical thickness on the anterior cortex (lateral view). Good reduction quality was defined as satisfying all four criteria of ABCD simultaneously. Acceptable reduction quality was defined as satisfying both AB and only one of the CD criteria. Poor reduction quality was defined as satisfying only one of the ABCD criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The t‐test was utilized to identify significant differences. The 2‐tailed, unpaired t‐test was used to compare the differences between two groups, and the 2‐tailed, paired t‐test was utilized to detect changes in preoperative and postoperative outcome scores. ANOVA was conducted both before and after the operation within the group, with the median and interquartile range (IQR) being used. For group‐wise comparisons, variance analysis was used, while pairwise comparisons were made using the LSD‐t test. Continuous data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent sample t‐tests were performed for continuous variables, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test. The level of significance for all statistical analyses was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics between the Two Groups

The sliding compression group consisted of 16 males and 18 females, with an age range of 60–84 years and a mean age of 73.18 ± 7.15 years. The static fixation group included 20 males and 22 females, with an age range of 61–78 years and a mean age of 70.95 ± 5.31 years. In both groups, traffic accidents were the main cause of injury (58.8% vs. 54.7%). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 22.5 ± 3.1 kg/m2 in the sliding compression group and 22.8 ± 3.0 kg/m2 in the static fixation group. Left intertrochanteric fracture was present in 55.8% (19/34) patients in the sliding compression group and 47.6% (20/42) patients in the static fixation group. In the sliding compression group, one patient had hypertension, two had coronary heart disease, three had diabetes, one had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and one had an old cerebral infarction. In the static fixation group, five patients had hypertension, one had coronary heart disease, two had diabetes, one had an old cerebral infarction, and four had severe osteoporosis. Good quality of reduction was observed in 67.6% (23/34) patients in the sliding compression group and 52.3% (22/42) patients in the static fixation group. The mean follow‐up period was 15.12 ± 3.88 months (range: 9–21 months). Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). Typical cases are shown in Figure 1 and 2.

Comparison of Intra‐operative Data and Postoperative Recovery

The sliding compression group and static fixation group did not demonstrate statistically significant differences in TAD variances at 2 days, 6, and 12 months post‐operation (21.11 ± 2.26, 0.1 ± 2.52, and −0.39 ± 2.36 mm for the sliding compression group, and 20.51 ± 2.38, −0.2 ± 2.40, and −0.14 ± 2.33 mm for the static fixation group, respectively, Table 2). There was no statistical difference in the femoral neck length between the two groups at 2 days post‐operation (p > 0.05, Table 2). However, both groups experienced a significant decrease in femoral neck length at 2 months post‐operation compared to 2 days post‐operation (p < 0.05), with the sliding compression group showing a more significant decrease than the static fixation group (p < 0.05, Table 2). Furthermore, both groups experienced a significant decrease in femoral neck length at 1 year post‐operation compared to 2 months post‐operation (p < 0.05, Table 2), with the sliding compression group showing a more significant decrease than the static fixation group (p < 0.05). Regarding functional outcome, the HHSs of the sliding compression group and static fixation group were 91.71 ± 7.37 and 84.33 ± 8.63, respectively, at 12 months post‐operation, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.01). Similar results were observed for PPMS (Table 3).

The sliding compression group was subjected to a follow‐up period of 9–21 months, with an average duration of 15.12 ± 3.88 months. The static fixation group was followed up for 9–20 months, with an average duration of 13.69 ± 2.64 months. Throughout the follow‐up period, we observed that the static fixation group had a complication incidence of 14.2%, which included one case of internal fixation breakage, two cases of cut‐out, one case of screw migration, one case of reduction loss, and one case of nonunion. In contrast, the sliding compression group had a lower complication incidence of 5.8%, with only two cases of screw migration. The difference in complication incidence between the two groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.365, Table 3). Patients with internal fixation failure (internal fixation breakage, cut‐out, and screw migration) and nonunion underwent secondary surgeries based on the indications for revision. 20 Among these patients, five underwent artificial hip joint replacement, and two received replacement of the intramedullary nail with bone grafting at the fracture site. The patients who underwent secondary surgeries achieved favorable fracture healing without any complications, such as prosthetic loosening or internal fixation failure. As for the patients with reduction loss, they did not exhibit severe coxa vara deformity, and we provided them with regular follow‐up.

Discussion

In this study, the PFNA combined with plate group showed shorter postoperative Weight‐bearing time and fracture healing time, higher HHS and PPMS scores, and a lower incidence of complications compared to the InterTAN group. These findings suggest that: (i) sliding compression may be necessary for intramedullary treatment of A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures; and (ii) sliding compression does not pose a higher risk of internal fixation failure.

Sliding Compression and Reconstruction of the Lateral Wall

The AO‐A3.3 type fracture is characterized by lateral wall rupture combined with an irreparable medial column injury, which results in severe comminution and instability. Intramedullary nailing is the primary treatment for unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures. Previous literature has suggested that posterior medial fracture displacement does not have a significant effect on the treatment of intertrochanteric fractures with intramedullary nailing. 21 Since intramedullary nailing is a central fixation, the medial wall is biomechanically reconstructed to a certain extent. 22 Therefore, we did not include posterior medial wall bone displacement in our observations. However, incomplete lateral wall reconstruction hinders the functionality of PFNA sliding compression and leads to high postoperative complication and internal fixation failure rates. 9 , 23

Sliding Compression and Clinical Treatment Outcomes

In this study, sliding compression and static fixation were used to treat A3.3 fractures with lateral wall injury, and the operative time, intraoperative blood loss, fracture healing time and postoperative functional recovery of the hip joint were compared between the two groups. The results showed that both internal fixation methods were effective for A3.3 fractures with lateral wall injury. However, sliding compression was superior to static locking in terms of fracture healing time, weight‐bearing time, incidence of complications and postoperative functional recovery. Compared with InterTAN, PFNA combined with a reconstruction plate had a shorter fracture healing time (p = 0.003), earlier weight bearing (p = 0.001) and a lower incidence of complications (5.8% vs. 14.3%, p = 0.367). However, the static locking group also had the advantages of shorter operative time and less total intraoperative blood loss compared to the gliding group (p = 0.001; p = 0.002). It is not difficult to understand that the sliding compression group requires an additional incision outside the rotor interval for anatomical reduction of the lateral wall, and the operative time may be prolonged and intraoperative bleeding may increase due to the surgeon's familiarity with the static fixation device.

In our study found that femoral neck length was reduced at 2 months and 1 year after surgery in the sliding compression group compared to the static fixation group. However, there was no significant difference in TAD between the two groups. Adequate sliding compression led to better alignment of the fracture ends and increased stress stimulation, which facilitated fracture healing and resulted in a shorter healing time compared to static fixation. Sliding compression also provides better bone anchorage in unstable fractures and osteoporotic patients, allowing for earlier weight‐bearing training, 24 which promotes functional recovery of the hip joint. In terms of functional hip outcomes (HSS and PPMS), the sliding compression group outperformed the static fixation group in this study. Additionally, screw withdrawal may cause painful pulling of the lateral thigh muscles, which can affect hip function and reduce hip scores. Femoral neck inversion and shortening were also found to be negatively correlated with Harris scores. 25

Sliding Compression and the Incidence of Internal Fixation Failure

The quality of reduction is crucial for restoring fracture stability. Poor reduction quality is one of the risk factors for internal fixation failure in AO/OTA‐A3.3 intertrochanteric femoral fractures. 26 In patients with insufficient lateral wall support, caution should be taken while employing the proximal sliding compression technique in order to prevent failure of the internal fixation. The use of a plate for the lateral wall reconstruction, without compromising the blood supply, enables proper reduction and decreases the inversion stress on the internal fixation due to the lack of efficient support in the medial wall. 27 PFNA combined with lateral femoral wall microplates reduces the fracture gap and improves strong compression and fixation of the fracture end. 28 Using finite element analysis, Zheng et al. 9 proposed that reconstruction of the lateral wall could improve the intrinsic stiffness and damage load of internal fixation. In our study, we found no significant statistical difference in the incidence of postoperative complications between the two groups. This suggests that PFNA in combination with a reconstructed lateral wall plate can achieve similar reduction effects to InterTAN, even in the absence of medial wall support.

The fixation failure rate is associated with the quality of internal fixation using intramedullary nails when the lateral wall is reconstructed, and proper fracture reduction and blood supply preservation are ensured. 29 In a retrospective study of 70 elderly patients with A3 fractures, the postoperative complication rate of PFNA for intertrochanteric fractures was as high as 14.3% (two cases of cut‐out, two cases of screw migration, and one case of nonunion). However, in the PFNA group with reconstructed lateral walls, only one case of screw migration occurred. 30 Another study showed that 6% of patients treated with intramedullary nail and reconstruction plate had fixation failure complications, while 31% of patients treated with intramedullary nails alone had fixation failure complications. 11 Consistent with previous literature, our study also found a lower incidence of internal fixation failure in the sliding compression group compared to the static fixation group (5.8% vs. 14.2%). This suggests that sliding compression may possess a higher quality of internal fixation.

Prospects of Clinical Application

In the management of A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures with intramedullary nails, sliding compression remains therapeutically significant. Therefore, for such patients, we can initially focus on reconstructing the lateral wall using plates or cables. Following the reconstruction of the lateral wall, the implementation of a dynamic compression intramedullary nail may yield favorable treatment outcomes.

Limitations

There are still some limitations to this study. First, there was no uniformity in the type of intramedullary nail used, which could have resulted in bias. When dealing with comminuted lateral wall A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures, the most common clinical approach is to perform lateral wall reconstruction followed by PFNA fixation, which maintains sliding compression, or to use InterTAN static locking, which prevents dynamic sliding compression and avoids internal fixation failure due to incomplete lateral wall. 7 , 8 Our study aimed to compare the advantages and disadvantages of these two fixation methods, focusing on the healing results and complications associated with sliding compression. However, the differences caused by lateral wall reconstruction or lack thereof, and by the variety of intramedullary nail itself, were not taken into consideration.

Conclusion

The use of PFNA with lateral wall reconstruction for A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures demonstrated superior mobility, efficiency, and reduced internal fixation failure rates compared to InterTAN alone. These findings suggest that sliding compression may be required for intramedullary nailing treatment.
