Artificial intelligence-powered electrocardiogram is superior to NT-proBNP in predicting left ventricular systolic dysfunction in atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response

Despite its prevalence, risk stratification for tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy remains poorly understood. A deep learning model developed at Mayo Clinic uses standard 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) to predict reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (≤35%) in the general population with high accuracy (area under the curve, 0.93).1 The performance of artificial intelligence (AI)–powered ECG (AI-ECG) in predicting systolic dysfunction among patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) with rapid ventricular response (AF RVR) has not been previously explored.

We conducted a Declaration of Helsinki–adherent retrospective study (institutional review board approval #25-000904) of adult patients (age ≥18 years) evaluated across the Mayo Clinic enterprise with AF RVR (heart rate >100 beats/min), including AF occurring in-hospital and AF at presentation, between January 2017 and May 2025. Patients were included if they had a 12-lead ECG documenting AF RVR (heart rate >100 beats/min) processed through the AI-ECG dashboard accessible through the electronic medical record system and an echocardiogram done within 7 days of the ECG. We excluded patients with a known history of heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction (EF) (<50%). We also collected data on N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels when available within 1 week of the ECG and echocardiogram. Guided by previous research, we tested cutoffs of 300, 1200, and 2000 pg/mL in addition to the mean level among our sample.2,3 The study was deemed minimal risk by the institutional review board, and a written patient consent was not required, considering the retrospective nature, use of deidentified patient data, and the impracticality of acquiring a written consent from all patients.

A total of 11,801 patients were included, with a mean age of 73.2 ± 12.6 years, 48.6% female, and 94.8% white. The prevalence of reduced LVEF in our cohort was 20.9% for an LVEF of <50% and 3.2% for an LVEF of <35%.

A combination of AI-ECG low EF probability cutoff of 75% had a specificity of 96% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 98% in ruling out an LVEF of <35%. AI-ECG low EF probability cutoffs of 50% (specificity 91%, NPV 98%) and 26% (previously determined Youden index for AI-ECG low EF probability model, specificity 84%, NPV 98%) also performed reasonably well in ruling out an LVEF of <35%. A combination of AI-ECG low EF probability cutoff of 17% and an LVEF of <35% provided the best balance of sensitivity and specificity, 66% and 78%, respectively, with an accuracy of 78% (Table 1).

NT-proBNP data were available on 5279 patients (48%). The mean NT-proBNP level was 4090 pg/mL. Among the tested cutoffs, ≥4090 pg/mL performed best in ruling out reduced LVEF, with 1767 pg/mL being the Youden index. However, NT-proBNP classified a much higher proportion of cases as positive than AI-ECG, translating into higher sensitivity but lower positive predictive value. As a result, AI-ECG performed better with greater overall accuracy than NT-proBNP (Table 2).

Based on receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, AI-ECG was superior to NT-proBNP in predicting an LVEF of <35% (area under the curve, 0.72 vs 0.59), with accuracy of 77% vs 47% at Youden cut points of AI-ECG probability of 17% and NT-proBNP level of 1767 pg/mL, respectively (Figure 1).

Our study demonstrates the utility of AI-ECG in predicting reduced LVEF among patients with AF RVR. AI-ECG demonstrated excellent specificity and NPV in ruling out reduced LVEF, serving as a powerful clinical screening tool to assist with the pharmacologic choice of rate control and timing of echocardiographic evaluation. The ability to rule out systolic dysfunction in patients with AF RVR using an inexpensive, accessible ECG tool can have important implications for acute management, influencing point-of-care clinical decisions. AI-ECG use in this context may also support cost-effectiveness and optimize resource utilization. A secondary analysis of the pragmatic randomized Electrocardiogram Artificial Intelligence-Guided Screening for Low Ejection Fraction trial previously demonstrated its cost-effectiveness in early detection of low LVEF particularly in outpatient routine clinical practice.4

Although elevated NT-proBNP levels often raise concern for systolic dysfunction and have been shown to predict future HF events in patients with AF without HF, they are commonly elevated in patients with AF RVR and may not reliably predict reduced LVEF.3,5 At our institution, the AI-ECG dashboard is readily available through the electronic medical record with AI analysis available for review as soon as an ECG is digitally uploaded into the record and processed. Thus, AI-ECG is a quick, point-of-care, noninvasive tool that can help guide timely clinical care.

The limitations of our study include the retrospective nature of data and the possibility of misclassification owing to the use of diagnostic codes. Statistical performance analysis of AI-ECG and NT-proBNP especially of their positive predictive value may be affected by the lower incidence of reduced LVEF in our cohort. Although we only included patients who had an echocardiogram within 1 week of AF RVR to limit variability, the exact timing of echocardiography could influence LVEF classification and thus AI-ECG performance analysis. Our patient cohort was predominantly white, and thus, racial generalizability may be limited despite the large cohort. The AI-ECG model used was not developed exclusively on a cohort with AF; however, our results from an extrapolated use of the model suggest even greater predictive power of an AI-ECG model calibrated specifically for use in AF RVR.

Our study offers novel insights paving the way for future investigations to help fine-tune and further delineate the role of AI-ECG in risk stratifying patients with suspected tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, guiding therapeutic and diagnostic clinical decisions.
