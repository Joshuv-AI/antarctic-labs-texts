Mentorship Over Infrastructure: What Drives Dermatology Residents to Pursue Pediatric Dermatology Fellowship
Authors: Jennifer J. Schoch, Han Li, Israel D. Andrews, Nanette Silverberg, Marla Jahnke, Teresa Wright, Maria Gnarra Buethe, Kelly Cordoro, Kelly Cordoro, Reesa Monir, Nicholas V. Nguyen, Brea Prindaville, Adam Rubin, Kerrie Satcher, Meredith Steuer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe pediatric dermatology workforce shortage threatens equitable access to specialized care for children. We conducted a national survey of dermatology residents (n = 202) to examine the relative influence of mentorship, educational exposure, training satisfaction, and financial considerations on fellowship decision‐making. In multivariable analysis, mentorship emerged as the primary predictor of fellowship pursuit (OR: 6.034, 95% CI: 2.315–19.648, p = 0.013), while structural program features such as in‐house fellowships and faculty availability improved training satisfaction but did not independently drive fellowship intentions. These findings support workforce strategies that prioritize structured mentorship pathways alongside compensation models that sustain the pediatric dermatology faculty base.

Introduction

The pediatric dermatology workforce shortage represents a growing threat to timely access and quality care for children with skin disease in the United States. Despite increasing demand, the density of pediatric dermatologists remains well below recommended benchmarks, with marked geographic maldistribution and persistent access gaps, particularly in underserved regions [1]. Prior workforce analyses have highlighted stagnant growth of the pediatric dermatology pipeline relative to population needs, underscoring the importance of understanding factors that influence career choice during dermatology residency.

While prior retrospective surveys of practicing pediatric dermatologists have consistently identified mentorship as a critical driver of subspecialty choice [2, 3], prospective data examining residents during training remain limited. Lee et al. [4] recently demonstrated that approximately half of dermatology residents initially interested in pediatric dermatology reported declining interest during residency, citing insufficient mentorship and perceived income limitations as major deterrents. The present study evaluated factors associated with residents' decisions to pursue pediatric dermatology fellowship, with particular focus on mentorship, educational exposure, training satisfaction, and debt burden.

Methods

An anonymous cross‐sectional online survey developed by consensus among pediatric dermatology faculty members of the Society for Pediatric Dermatology Workforce Committee was distributed via the Association of Professors of Dermatology listserv.

Multiple logistic regression evaluated associations with fellowship pursuit. Ordinal regression was used to assess correlates of training satisfaction. Statistical significance was p < 0.05 with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Analyses were performed using R version 2023.03.0+386. The survey instrument is provided as Supporting Information.

Results

Two hundred two residents completed the survey (response rate 13.5%). Respondents represented all training years and geographic regions, and a range of anticipated career paths (Table 1). Of 201 respondents who answered the question, 40 (19.9%) reported that they were pursuing a pediatric dermatology fellowship. In multivariable analysis, mentorship emerged as the main predictor of fellowship pursuit (OR: 6.034, 95% CI: 2.315–19.648, p = 0.013, Table 2), with pediatric dermatology mentors conferring the highest likelihood. In‐house fellowships, the number of pediatric dermatology faculty, the amount of pediatric dermatology exposure by weeks, and debt burden showed no significant association (presence of debt, p = 0.364; by increasing debt level, p = 0.15, 0.14, and 0.29, respectively). Similarly, measures of self‐reported competence and confidence in pediatric dermatology scenarios were not predictive of fellowship intention. Training satisfaction, on the other hand, correlated with in‐house fellowships (p = 0.002) and access to board‐certified pediatric dermatologists (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This national survey identifies mentorship as the primary predictor of pursuing a pediatric dermatology fellowship, reinforcing prior retrospective studies in which practicing pediatric dermatologists identified mentorship as the dominant influence on career choice [3], and extending this literature by prospectively evaluating residents during training.

While educational infrastructure and faculty availability improved resident satisfaction, in line with prior findings [5], they did not independently drive fellowship pursuit. Notably, this training satisfaction is valuable in its own right: the majority of residents will ultimately pursue careers outside pediatric dermatology, and a strong pediatric dermatology presence during residency enhances the overall quality of training for all graduates, regardless of subspecialty intent. However, exposure alone appears insufficient to sustain interest without meaningful, individualized mentorship relationships. Debt burden showed no association with fellowship decisions in this study, consistent with prior findings [6].

Lee et al. [4] identified low‐income potential—along with insufficient mentorship and the additional year of trainee salary—as key deterrents among residents whose interest declined during residency. Our findings complement rather than contradict these results: our survey was intentionally designed to evaluate mentorship and training experiences, and low‐income potential was not included as a response option.

These data support a unified workforce message in which compensation and mentorship are tightly linked rather than competing explanations. Compensation disparities between academic pediatric dermatology and other practice settings may constrain recruitment and retention of board‐certified pediatric dermatologists, thereby shrinking the mentor pool and limiting residents' access to the individualized guidance most associated with fellowship pursuit [7, 8]. Inadequate compensation can thus indirectly erode mentorship capacity, creating a self‐reinforcing cycle that worsens the workforce shortage. Our findings, therefore, reinforce the need to valorize our specialty through both structured mentorship pathways and financial models that sustain a robust pediatric dermatology faculty base.

Limitations include potential career changes among first‐year respondents. The response rate was modest, as is common in surveys of non‐captive, voluntary populations. Nonresponse bias may limit generalizability; nevertheless, the consistency of these findings with prior findings.

In conclusion, workforce solutions should be two‐pronged: strengthen structured mentorship pathways and implement compensation models that sustain the very mentors required to rebuild the pipeline. Without mentors, the future pediatric dermatology workforce remains at risk.
