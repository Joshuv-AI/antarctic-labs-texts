Post-Pneumonectomy Syndrome Causing Reversible Right Ventricular Dilation and Tricuspid Regurgitation
Authors: Cecilia Villa Etchegoyen, Juan M Farina, Dana M Ellis, Kevin Chen, Reza Arsanjani, Kristen A Sell-Dottin, Staci E Beamer, Samine Ravanbakhsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-pneumonectomy syndrome is a rare complication caused by excessive mediastinal shift and cardiac rotation, leading to nonspecific cardiopulmonary symptoms.Case SummariesWe report the cases of 2 patients who developed cardiopulmonary symptoms after having left pneumonectomy for lung cancer. In both cases, multimodality imaging demonstrated an early mediastinal displacement with cardiac rotation. Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography revealed right ventricular enlargement and new significant functional tricuspid regurgitation. Computed tomography confirmed the anatomic distortion and excluded other pathology. Symptoms were attributed to cardiac displacement, and both patients underwent surgical intervention for mediastinal repositioning with placement of an intrathoracic tissue expander. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed immediate improvement in right ventricular size and tricuspid regurgitation following expander placement, with sustained clinical and imaging improvement during follow-up.ConclusionsPost-pneumonectomy syndrome should be considered in patients with new right-heart abnormalities after surgery. Left Pneumonectomy Results in Marked Mediastinal Shift With Cardiac Displacement and Rotation, Leading to Right Ventricular Dilation and Functional Tricuspid Regurgitation

Surgical mediastinal repositioning with intrathoracic tissue expander restores cardiac position, improving right ventricular geometry and reducing tricuspid regurgitation.

Case 1

History of presentation

A 45-year-old woman presented with an incidental finding of a central left pulmonary carcinoid tumor. Her medical history included hyperadrenergic postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, mild asthma, and sleep apnea. Preoperative cardiopulmonary assessment was normal, and the patient underwent robotic-assisted thoracoscopy and left pneumonectomy without complications. She had an uneventful initial postoperative recovery; however, over the next few months, she developed palpitations, peripheral edema, upper chest tightness, and dyspnea.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed normal left-heart anatomy and function but an enlarged right ventricular (RV) chamber size with normal systolic function. The estimated RV systolic pressure was 28 mm Hg, and new moderate tricuspid valve regurgitation (TR) due to annulus dilatation was noted (Figures 1A and 1B, Videos 1 and 2). Post-pneumonectomy chest computed tomography (CT) with intravenous contrast showed pronounced mediastinal shift with no evidence of pulmonary embolism (Figures 1C and 1D). No air in the left pneumonectomy space and no pericardial fluid or thickening was observed.

Post–Left Pneumonectomy Evaluation (Case 1)

(A and B) Transthoracic echocardiography obtained through a suboptimal acoustic window after left pneumonectomy, demonstrating right ventricle enlargement, tricuspid annulus dilatation and significant tricuspid regurgitation in the setting of pronounced mediastinal shift and cardiac displacement. (C and D) Chest computed tomography, sagittal and axial views, showing marked cardiac displacement toward the left hemithorax.

Management and outcomes

Due to secondary to heart rotation and displacement, the surgical correction of the cardiac distortion was decided. A silicone tissue expander was placed in the left hemithorax via minithoracotomy. Intraoperative assessment with transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated immediate improvement in RV dilation and in the TR after tissue expander placement (Figure 2, Videos 3 to 6).

Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography, Mid-Esophageal 4-Chamber View (Case 1)

(A and B) Preoperative transesophageal echocardiography showing tricuspid annular dilatation with severe functional tricuspid regurgitation. (C and D) After tissue expander placement, transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated improved tricuspid leaflet coaptation with marked reduction in tricuspid regurgitation and normalization in right ventricle dimensions.

Follow-up

Surveillance follow-up at 6 months and 1 year after surgical intervention revealed an asymptomatic patient with normal clinical examination and normal imaging, including chest radiograph and CT-confirmed stable implant configuration and TTE showed no evidence of cardiac anomalies (Figure 3).

Follow-Up Evaluation After Surgical Mediastinal Repositioning (Case 1)

(A) Chest radiograph demonstrating appropriate positioning of the intrathoracic tissue expander (red arrow) with restoration of the cardiac silhouette toward the midline. (B and C) Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography in the apical 4-chamber view showing normalized right ventricular size and only mild residual tricuspid regurgitation.

Case 2

History of presentation

A 55-year-old woman presented with dyspnea, chest pain, and presyncope following a left pneumonectomy performed for lung cancer. During an evaluation for dyspnea and cough, a left hilar mass was identified, and biopsy confirmed adenoid cystic carcinoma of the salivary gland. Preoperative cardiovascular assessment was normal. The patient subsequently underwent a robotic-assisted left pneumonectomy.

Investigations

Following pneumonectomy, the electrocardiogram showed new negative T waves in the inferior leads and right axis deviation (Figure 4A). TTE demonstrated normal left-heart anatomy and function, but a moderately enlarged RV with preserved systolic function. A new severe TR was identified, with a large central regurgitant jet secondary to a leaflet coaptation gap consistent with tricuspid annular dilatation (Figures 4B and 4C, Videos 7 and 8). Chest CT with intravenous contrast showed leftward mediastinal shift and a small amount of fluid in the pneumonectomy bed, with no evidence of acute or chronic pulmonary embolism (Figures 4D to 4E).

Post–Left Pneumonectomy Evaluation (Case 2)

(A) Twelve-lead electrocardiogram showing sinus rhythm, new negative T waves in the inferior leads and right axis deviation. (B and C) Transthoracic echocardiography, 4-chamber view, obtained through a suboptimal acoustic window demonstrating right ventricular enlargement and significant functional tricuspid regurgitation in the setting of pronounced mediastinal shift and cardiac displacement. (D and E) Chest computed tomography, coronal and axial views, demonstrating marked leftward mediastinal shift with cardiac displacement into the left hemithorax following left pneumonectomy.

Management and outcomes

In addition to the patient’s multiple comorbidities, it was considered that the significant cardiac rotation and displacement were contributing to her limiting symptoms, thus a multidisciplinary team decided to proceed with surgical correction. A silicone tissue expander was placed in the left hemithorax via mini-thoracotomy. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated severe functional tricuspid regurgitation with annular dilatation (tricuspid annular diameter: 33 mm) and leaflet malcoaptation (coaptation gap: 2.93 mm) before surgical correction, with a marked reduction to trivial–mild regurgitation after tissue expander placement, accompanied by improved leaflet coaptation and reduction in tricuspid annular dimensions (tricuspid annular diameter: 22 mm) (Figure 5, Videos 9 and 10).

Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography, Mid-Esophageal 4-Chamber View

(A to C) Pre-repositioning images demonstrating tricuspid annular dilatation, leaflet malcoaptation, and severe central tricuspid regurgitation. (D to F) Post-repositioning images after intrathoracic tissue expander placement showing reduction in annular dimensions, improved leaflet coaptation, and marked decrease in tricuspid regurgitation.

Follow-up

At follow-up, chest radiography confirmed stable positioning of the intrathoracic tissue expander with restoration of the cardiac silhouette toward the midline (Figure 6A). Surveillance TTE at 6 months and 1 year after surgical correction showed normal left and right cardiac anatomy and function, with only trivial mitral regurgitation and mild TR (Figures 6B and 6C), and follow-up electrocardiography showed resolution of prior axis deviation and conduction abnormalities (Figure 6D).

Follow-Up Evaluation After Surgical Mediastinal Repositioning (Case 2)

(A) Chest radiograph demonstrating appropriate positioning of the intrathoracic tissue expander (red arrow) with restoration of the cardiac silhouette toward the midline. (B and C) Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography in the apical 4-chamber view showing normalization of right ventricular size and only mild residual tricuspid regurgitation. (D) Twelve-lead electrocardiogram showing sinus rhythm without axis deviation or conduction abnormalities.

Discussion

Post-pneumonectomy syndrome is a rare but well-described complication following pneumonectomy, resulting from excessive mediastinal shift and rotation of the heart and great vessels into the post-pneumonectomy space.1 Although it is more frequently reported after right pneumonectomy, left pneumonectomy can also lead to clinically relevant anatomic distortion, particularly when the mediastinal shift is marked.2 The syndrome was initially described as a cause of airway compression, but subsequent reports have emphasized that cardiac rotation and deformation may be the dominant mechanism in selected patients, leading to cardiopulmonary symptoms even in the absence of bronchial obstruction.3, 4, 5

In the present cases, symptoms after left pneumonectomy were the result of progressive mediastinal displacement and cardiac rotation, documented by noncontrast CT, which allowed visualization of mediastinal shift and cardiac displacement but did not provide sufficient resolution for detailed assessment of the tricuspid valve. Echocardiography demonstrated RV enlargement and new significant TR, consistent with a functional mechanism driven by annular dilatation and altered right-heart geometry rather than intrinsic valve disease. These findings are in line with prior surgical and imaging series showing that distortion of the heart after pneumonectomy may lead to secondary valvular regurgitation and right-heart dysfunction due to changes in spatial orientation and loading conditions.

Importantly, these cases highlight that patients after pneumonectomy often present with multifactorial and nonspecific symptoms.6 Conditions such as asthma, dysautonomia, deconditioning, or pulmonary vascular disease may coexist and act as confounding factors during clinical evaluation. Pulmonary embolism and pulmonary hypertension should be considered in the differential diagnosis in patients presenting with exertional symptoms and new right-heart structural or functional changes after pneumonectomy. In addition, RV dysfunction or failure also has been described as a potential complication following pneumonectomy. However, the identification of marked and early cardiac displacement with new functional TR strongly suggests a mechanical component contributing to symptoms, reinforcing the importance of comprehensive multimodality assessment rather than attributing findings to a single diagnosis.

From a management perspective, there are no formal guideline-recommended algorithms for pneumonectomy syndrome, and treatment decisions rely on expert consensus and multidisciplinary discussion.2,7, 8, 9 Surgical mediastinal repositioning using intrathoracic tissue expanders or prostheses is currently considered the preferred approach in symptomatic patients with significant anatomic distortion, both adults and pediatric patients.10,11 Furthermore, prophylactic placement of an intrathoracic expander has been proposed in high-risk populations, particularly in children and young women, to prevent the development of post-pneumonectomy syndrome.8

In our cases, intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated immediate improvement in RV size and TR after expander placement, providing direct physiological confirmation of the mechanism. Durable clinical and imaging improvement at mid- and long-term follow-up further supports that correction of cardiac displacement can effectively reverse functional right-heart abnormalities in carefully selected patients.

Conclusions

Pneumonectomy syndrome is an uncommon but clinically relevant cause of cardiopulmonary symptoms after pneumonectomy, particularly when marked mediastinal shift results in cardiac rotation and distortion. These cases illustrate that symptoms are often multifactorial but may be driven predominantly by mechanical cardiac displacement. Multimodality imaging is essential for diagnosis, with echocardiography demonstrating RV enlargement and new or worsening functional TR, and CT defining the anatomic substrate. Surgical mediastinal repositioning with intrathoracic tissue expansion can lead to rapid and durable improvement in RV size and TR severity. Early recognition of this potentially reversible condition is key to guiding appropriate multidisciplinary management.
