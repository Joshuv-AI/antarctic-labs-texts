Pregnancy outcomes in an international cohort of autoimmune hepatitis
Authors: Yooyun Chung, Meera Garriga, Ilkay Ergenc, Michele Tana, Roxanna A. Irani, Monika Sarkar, Michael A. Heneghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsAutoimmune hepatitis (AIH) poses challenges in pregnancy, including immunosuppression (IMS) management and risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Granular AIH pregnancy data are limited.MethodsThis two-center retrospective study included pregnancies in women with AIH at King’s College Hospital and the University of California, San Francisco between 1970 and 2024. Clinical characteristics, IMS regimens and pregnancy outcomes were obtained from chart review. Liver biochemical markers and prognostic scores were assessed as predictive tools for pregnancy outcomes.ResultsThere were 197 AIH pregnancies in 112 women. AIH pregnancies increased over time (p <0.001). Median age at conception was 29 years. Cirrhosis was present in 43% of pregnancies. IMS was used in 84% of pregnancies. Preconception counseling was provided in 52% of pregnancies and was associated with higher IMS use (61% vs. 31%, p = 0.008). Live birth occurred in 88% of pregnancies, with preterm delivery in 24%. Gestational complications included intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (9%) and preeclampsia (8%). Preterm birth was more common in pregnancies complicated by cirrhosis (33% vs. 16%, p = 0.04), although live birth rates were similar. Loss of biochemical response occurred more frequently postpartum than during pregnancy (25% vs. 6%, p <0.001). The UKELD score was the strongest predictor of live birth (AUROC 0.75), followed by MELD-Na (AUROC 0.73), while preconception AST <33 IU/L predicted maintenance of biochemical response (AUROC 0.65).ConclusionsAIH in pregnancy carries meaningful risks, including a 9% risk of intrahepatic cholestasis and increased preterm birth in women with cirrhosis. Preconception counseling and immunosuppression adherence are associated with favorable outcomes. Preconception UKELD and AST levels may guide individualized risk stratification for live birth and biochemical response.Impact and implicationsThis large two-center study provides one of the most comprehensive characterizations of autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) in pregnancy, demonstrating high live birth rates but clinically meaningful risks, particularly in women with cirrhosis. It highlights intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy and preterm delivery as key complications and shows that disease control and immunosuppression adherence are associated with improved outcomes. Importantly, the study identifies preconception counseling as a modifiable factor linked to better treatment continuation, and introduces UKELD and AST as potential tools for pre-pregnancy risk stratification. These findings support structured preconception care and suggest that validated liver disease scores may help individualise counseling and management in women with AIH considering pregnancy.

Introduction

Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory liver disease that predominantly affects women of reproductive age and occurs in approximately 1–7 in 100,000 pregnancies.1,2 AIH in pregnancy has been associated with increased maternal and fetal risks, including loss of biochemical response (LOBR), gestational diabetes, hypertensive diseases of pregnancy, preterm birth, and low birth weight.1,[3], [4], [5] However, existing data are heterogeneous, with studies reporting conflicting findings, includingsome studies reporting an increased risk of stillbirth and neonatal mortality, while others show no significant differences compared with the general population.[4], [5], [6] These inconsistencies may reflect differences in study cohorts (population-based datasets vs. case series), severity of liver disease and degree of AIH control, as disease remission prior to conception has been associated with improved outcomes.7

Historically, there was concern about the safety of immunosuppression (IMS), the cornerstone of AIH treatment, in pregnancy. Current guidelines now emphasize the importance of maintaining IMS throughout pregnancy to lower the risk of AIH flares.[7], [8], [9], [10] However, published data to guide treatment of patients with AIH in pregnancy are limited to case series,3,7,11 which may be underpowered to inform the efficacy of treatment regimens and dosing. In contrast, epidemiologic databases lack detailed clinical phenotyping, including data on disease activity and IMS exposure.1,12

To address existing knowledge gaps, we present an international cohort of AIH in pregnancy including granular, chart-based validation of medication exposures and AIH control on liver-related and pregnancy outcomes. These data can inform preconception counseling in women with AIH and help to optimize liver-related and obstetric care throughout pregnancy.

Patients and methods

Study population

This was an international collaboration between King’s College Hospital (KCH) and the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF). KCH cases were identified from a prospective database of self-reported pregnancies from 1970-2024, and missing data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. UCSF cases included all pregnancies in women with AIH between 2010 and 2025, identified using diagnosis codes from any encounter, hospital billing record, medical history, or problem list in the electronic medical records. All cases were subsequently validated by chart review, including confirmation of AIH diagnosis by serologic and liver biopsy data.

Cohort characteristics

Data obtained included demographics (age at pregnancy, ethnicity), pre-existing autoimmune conditions (e.g. thyroid disease, systemic lupus erythematosus), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), polycystic ovary syndrome, and pregnancy-related characteristics (e.g. multiple gestation, parity, gravidity). AIH-related data included dosing and duration of IMS medications including adjustments throughout pregnancy, serial liver test and immunoglobulin levels from preconception through postpartum, fibrosis stage by biopsy at diagnoses, non-invasive measures at diagnosis and at preconception, and endoscopy data where relevant. Preconception was defined as within 6 months prior to conception and postpartum was up to 6 months following delivery. Pre-pregnancy counseling included a documented discussion regarding pregnancy conducted either during a routine clinic visit or within a dedicated joint liver-obstetric clinic. Variceal screening endoscopy performed within 12 months of conception or during pregnancy (typically in the second trimester in accordance with local policy and guidelines) were included.10,13 IMS adherence was assessed by documentation during clinic visits. Drug levels including tacrolimus levels and thioguanine metabolites were not routinely performed and primarily measured for dose adjustments during pregnancy. Loss of biochemical response (LOBR) was defined as elevated liver transaminases above the upper limit of normal (aspartate aminotransferase [AST] >35 IU/ml), with or without elevated IgG. Liver biochemistry and prognostic scores including model for end-stage liver disease sodium (MELD-Na), model for end-stage liver disease 3.0 (MELD 3.0), United Kingdom model for end-stage liver disease (UKELD), albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score, AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) were used to evaluate their predictive performance for pregnancy-related outcomes.[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]

Study outcomes

Obstetric outcomes included the development of intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), hypertensive diseases of pregnancy (including gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, HELLP [hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets] syndrome, or eclampsia), cesarean delivery, postpartum hemorrhage, and death. Perinatal and fetal outcomes comprised of miscarriage, elective terminations, live birth, pre-term birth (<37 weeks gestation), birthweight, special care baby unit or neonatal intensive care unit admission, congenital abnormalities, and fetal death.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as medians with IQR, total number, or percentages. For continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test and Student's t test were used to compare non-parametric and parametric data, respectively. Categorical data were compared by χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. All p values were two tailed and a value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic curve analyses were performed using liver biochemistry parameters and prognostic scores (MELD-Na, MELD 3.0, UKELD, ALBI, APRI) to evaluate their predictive ability for live birth and preterm birth. A multiple logistic regression model was constructed to assess the independent association of cirrhosis with preterm birth, while adjusting for known maternal risk factors (maternal age, T2DM, hypertension, and other autoimmune conditions).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declarations of Helsinki and the Liver Research Governance at each institution. It was concluded that individual patient consent and formal Research Ethics Approval were not required. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 28 (Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Immunosuppression

After excluding pregnancies with a postpartum diagnosis of AIH (n = 6), elective termination (n = 15), or missing data (n = 11), 138 pregnancies (84%) involved gestational IMS use. The most common IMS used was thiopurines (64%) and prednis(ol)one (60%), followed by budesonide (7%), tacrolimus (6%), and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)(3%) (Table 2). Thiopurines were used in 106 pregnancies (64%); among these, 74 (45%) also received prednis(ol)one and 7 (4%) received budesonide. Tacrolimus was used in 10 pregnancies (6%); among these, 9 (5%) also received prednis(ol)one, and 3 (2%) received concomitant thiopurines and prednis(ol)one. There was a trend towards IMS initiation with prednis(ol)one and/or azathioprine, or dose escalation, in the postpartum period (Table S1A,B, Fig. 2). In AIH pregnancies requiring dose adjustments, prednis(ol)one doses increased from a median of 4 mg/day (IQR 1–5) preconception to 10 mg/day (IQR 5–20) postpartum (p = 0.01). Azathioprine doses also tended to increase from a median of 0 mg (IQR 0–88) preconception to 50 mg (IQR 0–100) postpartum, although this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.39). The incidence of immunosuppression use preconception, intrapartum and postpartum.

A total of 62 IMS changes were made in 37 pregnancies (20% of all pregnancies) which occurred from preconception to postpartum. These changes included initiation of new agents or dose escalation (32%), cessation or reduction (9%) or incomplete adherence to medications (4%) (Table 2 and Table S1). Of the 37 pregnancies requiring IMS changes, 9 (24%) had more than one type of IMS change and 14 (38%) pregnancies involved changes in more than one IMS.

Only 3 out of 181 (2%) pregnancies involved preconception medication adjustments. This included cessation of azathioprine and budesonide (n = 1), dose reduction of prednisolone (n = 2) and dose escalation of budesonide (n = 1). Prednisolone reductions occurred with stable disease as a joint decision with the patient and clinician. One patient was weaning prednisolone while increasing budesonide dosing. One patient discontinued azathioprine and budesonide without clinician advice due to concerns about fetal risk. These agents were subsequently restarted during pregnancy following counseling.

There were 42 gestational IMS changes occurring in 29 pregnancies (16%), which included IMS initiation (n = 13, 7%), dose escalation (n = 6, 3%), dose reduction (n = 7, 4%) and cessation (n = 9, 5%) (Table S1). Of the 9 pregnancies with IMS cessation, 8 involved thiopurine and prednis(ol)one without clinician consultation. One patient continued MMF until late in pregnancy and presented at the time of delivery (see below).

There were 10/181 (6%) pregnancies that involved postpartum IMS adjustment. Eight changes were either initiation or dose escalation, while one patient was switched from azathioprine to prednisolone as advised by her doctors during breastfeeding. One patient was transitioning from prednisolone to budesonide.

Seven (4%) pregnancies occurred on MMF between 2008 and 2023. Two pregnancies transitioned to thiopurines at gestational weeks 4 and <10. Both pregnancies were unplanned in a patient with variable medical adherence. Two patients continued MMF throughout pregnancy without hepatology follow-up. One miscarriage occurred at 12 weeks of gestation in an unplanned pregnancy, and the remaining two pregnancies were terminated per patient preference. Of the 4 MMF-exposed pregnancies that progressed to delivery, 1 infant had talipes, failure to thrive and seizures, data were missing for 1 infant and the remaining 2 infants did not have congenital malformations.

Pregnancy outcomes

After excluding elective terminations (n = 15), there were 160 live births (88%), with data on gestational age at delivery in 152. Of these, 37/152 (24%) were preterm and 115/152 (76%) were term deliveries (Table 3). Of the preterm births, 76% (n = 28) occurred at 32–37 weeks, followed by very preterm births (28–32 weeks, n = 8), and one extreme preterm birth at <28 weeks. The extreme preterm birth occurred in a patient with AIH cirrhosis and immune thrombocytopenia, who had a history of preeclampsia and aspirin intolerance secondary to immune thrombocytopenia. This patient developed pneumonia and subsequent multiorgan failure. Both the patient and baby recovered following emergency cesarean. Liver-related outcomes

LOBR occurred more frequently postpartum (25%) than during pregnancy (5%) or preconception (2%) (p <0.001) (Table 3). Six out of 15 patients (40%) with a reduction or cessation of IMS at preconception or intrapartum experienced LOBR. Preconception transaminase levels were higher in those who developed gestational and/or postpartum LOBR compared to those who remained in biological response (median AST 37 U/ml vs. 28 U/ml, p = 0.01 and median alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 42 U/ml vs. 25 U/ml, p = 0.02) (Table S5).

There were 85 (43%) pregnancies in patients with AIH cirrhosis. Patients with cirrhosis were younger at conception (28 vs. 30 years, p = 0.03), more likely to have preterm births (16% vs. 33%, p = 0.04), and less likely to receive aspirin (Table 4). Live birth rate and cesarean delivery were similar by cirrhosis status. In pregnancies with cirrhosis, 48% (n = 39) received variceal screening, which was more common in those who received PPC. More than half of patients who underwent screening endoscopy during pregnancy or within 1 year prior to conception had varices (n = 20, 51%) of whom 20% (n = 4) required endoscopic therapy. Hepatic decompensation occurred in 10 pregnancies (6% of AIH pregnancies excluding termination, 12% of pregnancies in those with cirrhosis) though all had live births. Decompensation events included variceal bleed (n = 3, 2% of AIH pregnancies), intra- or postpartum ascites (n = 3, 2%), jaundice (n = 2, 1%), hepatic encephalopathy (n = 1, 1%), and postpartum liver failure in AIH cirrhosis requiring liver transplantation (n = 1, 1%). Of the 10 pregnancies with decompensation, only 1 had had pre-pregnancy decompensation. There was no significant difference in preconception liver prognostic scores and decompensation in pregnancy among patients with cirrhosis (Table S3).

Characteristics of pregnancies resulting in term and preterm births were compared, showing that the preterm cohort had a higher prevalence of cirrhosis and lower platelet count (Table S4). Liver prognostic scores were not associated with preterm births. In univariate logistic regression analyses, cirrhosis at conception was the only significant predictor of preterm birth (B = −0.825, SE = 0.386, p = 0.03), whereas other risk factors – including age at conception, presence of T2DM, hypertension, other autoimmune conditions, and preconception APRI – were not significant. In multivariate logistic regression including cirrhosis, age and preconception APRI, cirrhosis remained the only significant predictor (B = -1.354, SE = 0.539, p = 0.01).

Preconception AST was associated with LOBR during pregnancy and postpartum (Table S5b). Patients were stratified into quartiles based on preconception AST values (AST ≤10 IU/L, 10–≤20 IU/L, 20–≤30 IU/L, 30–≤40 IU/L) with AST >40 IU/L indicating lack of BR. Increasing AST quartiles correlated with higher LOBR rates (p = 0.02). ROC analysis yielded an AUROC of 0.65 (p = 0.01) with an AST value of 33 IU/L demonstrating 69% sensitivity and 59% specificity for predicting LOBR during pregnancy or postpartum (Fig. 3B,C).

Discussion

This international study of nearly 200 pregnancies with AIH demonstrates generally favorable outcomes, although preterm birth was more frequent in women with AIH cirrhosis. This is the largest cohort of granular, patient-level data on immunosuppression regimen, liver biochemistry, and pregnancy outcomes. We also found preconception UKELD and AST levels to be potential predictors of live birth and LOBR, respectively, though their predictive performance was limited. Lastly, these data highlight gaps in clinical practice and preconception counseling, which notably influence variceal screening and aspirin initiation in our patient populations, two established interventions for mitigating morbidity and mortality.10,13,19

Pregnancy outcomes in AIH have thus far been derived from large population data or small case series.1,3,5,6,20 Recent population data from the United States, Denmark, and Sweden have also reported conflicting results on relevant patient outcomes, such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and cesarean rates.1,3,5,6,20 The rate of preterm births and ICP in our study was higher than that in the general population, and consistent with previous AIH registry data. Notably, our data did not demonstrate an increased risk of ICP associated with thiopurine use as recently highlighted by the US FDA, though this may reflect limited power and type II error. However, consistent with our findings, the global consensus statement on IBD and pregnancy supports the continuation of thiopurines during pregnancy as the benefit of maintaining stable disease outweighs the potential risks related to uncontrolled disease activity.21 The rate of miscarriage, gestational diabetes, cesarean delivery, stillbirth, and congenital malformations in our data are comparable to those in the general population and largely consistent with previous AIH pregnancy data. The cesarean rates in the general population in Sweden and Denmark are lower than in the US and UK (<20% vs. 30%, respectively). Consequently, studies from Sweden and Denmark report a higher rate of cesarean in AIH pregnancies. One US registry study found an increased rate of gestational diabetes in AIH pregnancies whereas more recent US data did not confirm this.1,20 Differences between registry data likely relate to variability in sample size, time period, and clinical practice between countries. Miscarriage rates in AIH have been reported to be >20% by some,12,22 and the lower rate in our study may reflect underreporting of early miscarriages.

In the current study, patients with AIH, and particularly cirrhosis, had a higher rate of preterm births (14% in non-cirrhosis and 33% in cirrhosis) vs. <10% in the general population.23 Preeclampsia and ICP are known risk factors for preterm birth, which was also evident in our study. We also noted ICP in 10% of our cohort, which is higher than in the general population, and consistent with prior AIH studies.3,24 Importantly, patients with AIH cirrhosis were less likely to receive aspirin for preeclampsia prophylaxis which likely reflects concerns about bleeding risk in this population. However, even in patients with AIH without cirrhosis, only 20% received aspirin prophylaxis, highlighting an opportunity for improved management.19,25

Immunosuppression is the mainstay of AIH treatment, with all agents other than MMF are deemed safe for use in pregnancy and lactation.10,13 Steroids and thiopurines were the most commonly used agents in our study, consistent with their role as first-line immunosuppression for AIH. There were minor regional variations in prescribing practices, with budesonide more commonly prescribed in the UCSF cohort than in the KCH cohort. While MMF exposure in pregnancy was more common in the KCH cohort, this reflects an earlier inclusion period, with pregnancies dating back to the 1990s, when less was known about MMF-associated risks in pregnancy. In our data, budesonide was initiated during pregnancy in most patients due to de novo diagnosis, following careful assessment of disease severity, exclusion of cirrhosis, and consideration of patient preferences. Immunosuppression use (84%) was not associated with adverse outcomes such as preterm birth or fetal death. Lack of IMS use (16%) during pregnancy was not associated with increased rates of LOBR which may be due to the limited sample size. The continuation of IMS in pregnancy should be emphasized, except for MMF, which should ideally be switched 12 weeks before conception.

Pre-pregnancy counseling is the ideal timepoint to address and reduce risks associated with adverse outcomes. Pregnancy can affect AIH in terms of gestational and postpartum LOBR, and worsening portal hypertension in cirrhosis. AIH also increases obstetric risks of preterm births and hypertensive complications of pregnancy. PPC includes transition to safer medication for pregnancy, patient education surrounding treatment adherence to maintain AIH control, initiation of aspirin prophylaxis in early pregnancy, as well as close monitoring of liver tests to allow for adjustment of IMS. Variceal screening in those with cirrhosis can also be pursued prior to pregnancy for those who receive adequate PPC.10,13 In the current study, PPC was associated with increased uptake of variceal screening, although surveillance was performed in <50% of pregnancies with cirrhosis, indicating an area for improved clinical care. Such findings are particularly relevant as half of all screened patients had varices, with 20% requiring endoscopic therapy.

Preconception liver biochemical parameters were evaluated as potential predictors of pregnancy-related outcomes. Preconception ALBI score has previously been shown to be a better predictor of live births in chronic liver disease compared to the APRI and MELD score.26 In our dataset of AIH pregnancies, we found the UKELD score to be the best predictor of live birth, followed by the MELD and ALBI scores. These differences may relate to differences in disease etiology and severity, as no prior data have evaluated these measures in a dedicated cohort of AIH. However, the sensitivity and specificity were below the commonly accepted threshold of 80%, likely due to the small sample size and potential multifactorial nature of pregnancy outcomes. There remains a need to identify robust prognostic markers to allow for personalized risk stratification and management of AIH in pregnancy, though current data support the clinical application of UKELD in pregnancy counseling. Baseline autoantibody status, including anti-SLA/LP and anti-Ro/SSA, has been associated with maternal complications including postpartum flares and represents a further potential avenue for preconception risk stratification that warrants additional study.12,27

Regarding predictors of AIH control, we found that a low preconception AST level of 33 IU/L was a predictor of achieving biochemical response during pregnancy and postpartum. Risk stratifying patients based on the depth of biochemical response builds on previous data in AIH.27 A prior Dutch multicenter study also found that lack of preconception biochemical response (defined as normal aminotransferase and IgG levels) was associated with gestational and postpartum relapse, as well as preeclampsia and ICP.7 We did not observe this association in our data which may be related to the relatively small sample size. AST was assessed as it is more routinely measured than ALT at KCH and further validation, particularly using ALT as a potentially more specific marker, is required. Taken together these data support the importance of preconception counseling regarding IMS adherence to optimize AIH control and the associated clinical consequences of LOBR. Informing expectant mothers of the implications of LOBR for infant health may help to enhance motivation surrounding use of IMS throughout pregnancy and diminish concerns regarding infant risks.

This study does have some notable strengths and limitations. The relatively small sample size limited statistical power, although the granularity of data allowed for detailed assessment of medication adjustments from preconception to postpartum, with broad patient representation across countries. The data spans across five decades during which there have been significant changes in both hepatology and obstetric management of AIH pregnancies. Sensitivity and specificity of predictive markers may have been limited by type II error. All patients are derived from tertiary centers, which may bias towards more severe liver and/or obstetric-related outcomes, although these cases do represent real-world clinical scenarios that highlight the need for enhanced clinical care.

In conclusion, pregnancies in women with AIH are associated with overall favorable outcomes under multidisciplinary care; however, preterm birth is more common, particularly in those with cirrhosis. While our data suggest cirrhosis, preconception UKELD and AST may influence outcomes, these findings require further validation. Pre-pregnancy counseling is key for optimizing IMS regimens for pregnancy, completing necessary pre-pregnancy screening, and guiding patients on the importance of medication adherence and laboratory monitoring to enhance obstetric, hepatic, and infant outcomes.
