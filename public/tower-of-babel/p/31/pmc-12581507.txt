Increased lateral, but not medial, posterior tibial slope is associated with early graft failure following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Mehmet Ozbey Buyukkuscu, Ahmet Kulduk, Kerem Sencan, Ulas Yavuz, Kutalmis Albayrak, Bulent Karslioglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and study aimExcessive posterior tibial slope (PTS) has been identified as a biomechanical factor contributing to the risk of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries and graft failures. Notably, medial and lateral PTS (MPTS and LPTS) may have distinct influences on these outcomes. This study aims to investigate the impact of MPTS and LPTS on the likelihood of ACL graft failure.Patients and methodsThis retrospective study included 43 patients who underwent revision ACL reconstruction between 2011 and 2018 due to graft failure within two years of primary surgery. These patients were matched with a control group of 43 individuals who underwent ACL reconstruction without graft failure. Medial and lateral posterior tibial slope (MPTS and LPTS) were measured using the Hudek method on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and compared between the two groups. Clinical evaluation included the preoperative Tegner activity score, pivot-shift test, Lachman test, and Lysholm score, as well as the postoperative pivot-shift test, Lachman test, and Lysholm score assessed at the two-year follow-up.ResultsThe mean LPTS was significantly higher in the ACL revision reconstruction (RR) group compared to the primary reconstruction (PR) group (6.8 ± 3.6 vs. 3.2 ± 5.7, p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed in MPTS between the groups (3.0 ± 3.5 in the RR group vs. 2.4 ± 4.5 in the PR group, p = 0.56). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis determined an LPTS cut-off value of 6.0°. The Lachman test was positive in all patients preoperatively and became negative postoperatively in both groups. There was a significant improvement in pivot-shift grades from preoperative to postoperative evaluations in both groups. Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in Lysholm scores from preoperative to postoperative assessments, with no significant differences between the groups in either preoperative or postoperative scores.ConclusionAn increased LPTS (> 6.0°) is a significant risk factor for early ACL graft failure. Therefore, routine preoperative measurement of LPTS is recommended to identify patients at higher risk.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, Graft failure, Tibial slope

Introduction

It is well known that an increased posterior tibial slope (PTS) may predispose individuals to anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries [1, 2]. Along with other risk factors such as an increased Q angle, decreased notch width, subtalar pronation, and knee recurvatum, an increased PTS plays a significant role in the mechanism underlying primary ACL injury [3, 4]. It has also been demonstrated that increased PTS is associated with graft failure following ACL reconstruction surgery [5, 6].

The slope of the medial and lateral tibial plateau varies among patients and can be individually measured on conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using established techniques described in the literature [6–8]. Numerous studies suggest that lateral PTS (LPTS) may play a more significant role in ACL injuries [9–11]. However, most research has focused on primary ACL ruptures, with less attention given to its impact on graft failure. A few studies have correlated increased LPTS with early graft failure following ACL reconstruction [12, 13]. Additionally, one study identified both LPTS and medial PTS (MPTS) as independent risk factors for graft failure [14].

The purpose of this study is to independently examine the effect of MPTS and LPTS on the likelihood of ACL graft failure. We hypothesized that increased LPTS and MPTS may be associated with early graft failure following ACL reconstruction.

Materials and methods

Patient groups

Hospital records were retrospectively reviewed to identify patients who underwent revision ACL reconstruction for graft failure between January 2011 and January 2018. During this period, 1,215 patients underwent primary ACL reconstruction.

The 2022 consensus statement of the European Society of Sports Traumatology, Knee Surgery and Arthroscopy (ESSKA) defines graft failure as “abnormal knee function associated with a previous primary reconstruction” [15]. In the present study, ACL graft failure was defined as a clinically established diagnosis confirmed by MRI, with graft rupture demonstrated on imaging. MRI was performed during follow-up visits if patients reported symptoms suggestive of instability and/or if clinical examination revealed findings indicative of graft insufficiency (positive anterior drawer test, Lachman test, and/or pivot shift test). No additional imaging beyond MRI was required to confirm the diagnosis.

Inclusion criteria were patients who underwent revision ACL reconstruction due to graft failure occurring within two years after the primary surgery, with the failure unrelated to major trauma or contact injuries. Patients with a history of minor trauma or non-contact injury were not excluded from the study, as such cases were regarded as manifestations of graft failure predominantly attributable to chronic biomechanical stress. Seventy-four patients met these criteria. Thirty-one patients were excluded based on the following criteria: concomitant ligamentous injuries; posterior tibial slope >12° (in which slope correction may be performed either simultaneously or in a staged manner during primary or revision surgery); tunnel malposition; postoperative infection; skeletal immaturity; or absence of a preoperative MRI scan. All femoral and tibial tunnel positions were assessed on CT scans using a previously validated radiographic measurement method, and only patients with both femoral and tibial tunnel positions classified as anatomic were included in the study [16, 17].

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 43 patients who underwent single-stage revision ACL reconstruction were included in the study and were designated as the revision reconstruction (RR) group. Of these, 38 (88.3%) were male and 5 (11.7%) were female.

For the primary reconstruction (PR) group, 43 age- and sex-matched patients without graft failure were randomly selected from the remaining 1,141 patients who had undergone primary ACL reconstruction. The mean follow-up duration in the control group was 36 ± 4.5 months.

Surgical procedure

In all primary and revision cases, hamstring tendons (semitendinosus and gracilis) were harvested, and a quadruple-stranded graft was prepared. For revision procedures, the graft was harvested from the contralateral limb due to the unavailability of allografts. In primary reconstructions, an ipsilateral hamstring autograft was used.

All primary ACL reconstructions were performed using the anatomic single-bundle technique. The femoral tunnel was created through the anteromedial portal, followed by tibial tunnel preparation using a drill guide. An impingement test was routinely performed after tunnel placement.

The prepared hamstring graft was passed through the tunnels using an elevator system and fixed to the femur with a button-based cortical suspension device (Rigidloop™ Cortical Fixation System, DePuy Synthes, Livingston, UK). Tibial fixation in all cases, and additional femoral fixation in 10 revision cases, was achieved using a bioabsorbable interference screw (Biosure™ HA, Smith & Nephew) sized 1 mm larger than the tunnel diameter, along with a staple (Smith & Nephew).

In our revision cohort, meticulous attention was given to tunnel placement, graft tensioning, and fixation technique to ensure optimal biomechanical reconstruction. While no additional surgical modifications such as bone–patellar tendon–bone (BTB) grafts or slope-reducing tibial osteotomies were performed, a lateral extra-articular tenodesis procedure was selectively added in cases presenting with grade 3 pivot shift to enhance rotational stability.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Partial weightbearing was allowed beginning at two weeks postoperatively, progressing to full weightbearing at four weeks in both primary and revision ACL reconstruction cases. In patients who underwent concurrent ACL reconstruction and meniscal repair, as well as in revision ACL cases, only toe-touch weightbearing was permitted until the sixth postoperative week. All revision cases were managed with a knee immobilizer locked in full extension for six weeks.

Isometric quadriceps exercises were initiated on postoperative day one. Range of motion exercises also began on the first postoperative day using a continuous passive motion (CPM) machine, with 10° of flexion added daily. Patients were discharged once they could perform a straight leg raise and achieve 90° of knee flexion.

Jogging was initiated at three to four months, and full return to previous sports activities was permitted between nine and twelve months [18–20].

Clinical assessment

In both groups, follow-up examinations were conducted at weeks 2, 4, and 6, and subsequently at months 3, 6, and 12, with annual follow-ups thereafter. Preoperative clinical assessments included the Tegner activity score, pivot-shift test, Lachman test, and Lysholm score. All but the Tegner score were reassessed at the two-year follow-up. Preoperative waiting time for surgery was also documented.

LPTS and MPTS were measured using MRI, based on the method described by Hudek et al. [7] (Fig. 1). Measurements were performed by two orthopedic surgeons, each with five years of experience, who were blinded to the study groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software version 22.0. The mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum values and frequency were presented as descriptive statistics. The distribution of the variables was evaluated with Shapiro-Wilk test. For continuous variables, an independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for data that did not follow a normal distribution. Chi-square test was used to analyze categorical data. To determine the optimal threshold for lateral posterior tibial slope (LPTS), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess both intraobserver and interobserver reliability of the measurements. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in this study. A priori sample size estimation was performed using effect size data obtained from the literature [21]. Assuming an α error of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 6 patients per group (total n = 12) would be required to detect a significant difference between groups. A post-hoc power analysis was performed (n = 43 in each group). The effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.76) was calculated using the mean and standard deviation values of the LTPS measurements. With an alpha level of 0.05, the post-hoc analysis yielded a statistical power of 0.97, indicating that the study was adequately powered.

Results

No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, gender, side of the operation, body mass index, preoperative Tegner activity scores or preoperative waiting times (Table 1). All ACL deficiencies were chronic ruptures (> 6 weeks). Graft failure in the revision reconstruction group occurred at a mean of 14.4 ± 7.1 months postoperatively (range, 8–22 months). A total of 23 patients had medial meniscal tears and 11 had lateral meniscal tears—10 medial and 5 lateral in the PR group, and 13 medial and 6 lateral in the RR group. Among the medial meniscal tears, ramp lesions were the most common subtype (n = 6), followed by longitudinal tears (n = 6), transverse tears (n = 5), bucket-handle tears (n = 4), and radial tears (n = 2). For lateral meniscal tears, 6 were longitudinal and 5 were radial. Among the patients with medial meniscal tears, 15 underwent meniscal repair (8 in the PR group and 7 in the RR group), while 8 underwent partial meniscectomy (4 in each group). For lateral meniscal tears, 7 patients underwent meniscal repair (4 in the PR group and 3 in the RR group), and 4 underwent partial meniscectomy (2 in each group).

Excellent interobserver reliability was observed for both MPTS and LPTS measurements. The ICC for MPTS was 0.827 (95% CI: 0.74–0.89; p < 0.05), and for LPTS was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.95; p < 0.05).

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that increased LPTS—but not MPTS—is significantly associated with early ACL graft failure. In this study, we evaluated LPTS and MPTS to identify factors associated with early graft failure in ACL-reconstructed knees, as morphologic factors have been previously associated with sports injuries [4, 22, 23].

The influence of PTS on both native ACL and graft injuries has been well-documented in the literature. Ni et al. identified steep PTS as a significant risk factor for primary ACL graft failure in a multivariate analysis [24]. Similarly, Webb et al., in a series of 181 patients, demonstrated that those with a PTS ≥ 12° had five times greater odds of sustaining a subsequent ACL injury (ipsilateral or contralateral) after reconstruction [5]. Another study found significantly greater PTS in patients with non-contact ACL graft failure compared to those without [6]. Grassi et al. also concluded that steep PTS contributes to graft failure and recommended assessing proximal tibial morphology in patients undergoing ACL revision to identify those at high risk [21]. Notably, previous studies evaluated PTS as a single parameter, typically on plain radiographs, without distinguishing between LPTS and MPTS.

In contrast, our study separately measured LPTS and MPTS using MRI, which we consider a more accurate method for assessing tibial slope. Our results show that LPTS, specifically, is a significant predictor of early graft failure, whereas MPTS is not. This finding aligns with Christensen et al., who reported that increased LPTS measured on MRI is a risk factor for ACL graft failure, particularly in female patients [12]. They demonstrated that each 2°, 4°, and 6° increase in LPTS corresponded to an odds ratio of 1.6, 2.4, and 3.8, respectively, for graft failure. While our study similarly found a significant relationship between increased LPTS and early graft failure, the limited number of female patients in our cohort prevented us from evaluating gender-based differences.

We did not observe a significant association between MPTS and graft failure in our study. Li et al. reported that both MPTS and LPTS were significantly higher in patients with failed ACL reconstructions [25]. They identified slopes greater than 5° on MRI as a risk factor for graft failure. A recent study supported these findings, suggesting that MPTS was an independent risk factor for graft failure in male patients only [14]. Some literature indicates LPTS may be more strongly linked to ACL injury than MPTS [26, 27]. Jacker et al. found that both contributed equally to graft failure in males and suggested LPTS may be a more valuable predictor independent of gender [14]. However, their study did not differentiate early versus late graft failures and used a different method (Hashemi’s method) to assess PTS on MRI, which may explain differences in findings [26].

Given the established role of increased PTS in graft failure, slope-reducing osteotomy may be a viable option for selected high-risk revision patients. Sonnery-Cottet et al. reported favorable outcomes in five patients with a mean PTS of 13.6° who underwent closing-wedge tibial slope-reducing osteotomy at the time of re-revision ACL reconstruction, achieving a postoperative slope of 9.2° [28]. Another study showed satisfactory results with slope correction in re-revision ACL cases, recommending slope correction when the tibial slope exceeds 12° [29]. However, these reports involve small sample sizes and short follow-up durations, highlighting the need for further research to clarify the role of slope correction in ACL revision surgery. In our study, none of the patients in the revision group had a tibial slope exceeding 12°, and therefore, an indication for slope-reducing osteotomy was not present. We believe that clinical success could be achieved in these revisions—despite using the same surgical technique and graft source (contralateral hamstring autograft)—through meticulous tunnel positioning, secure graft fixation, and an appropriate rehabilitation protocol.

The biomechanical basis for slope-reducing osteotomy is supported by cadaveric studies [1, 30]. Imhoff et al. demonstrated that reducing PTS decreased forces on the ACL graft under axial load, thereby protecting it from excessive stress [31]. However, these studies evaluated overall PTS and did not specifically address LPTS or MPTS. Since our findings specifically identify LPTS as a predictor of early graft failure, future studies should explore the biomechanical basis of the isolated effect of LPTS and its correction on both the native ACL and ACL grafts.

The findings of our study emphasize the clinical significance of lateral posterior tibial slope (LPTS) as a modifiable risk factor in the context of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. In our cohort, an LPTS threshold of 6.0° was identified as the most predictive value for graft failure. Accordingly, patients exceeding this threshold require a carefully individualized surgical strategy. In patients with an LPTS greater than 6.0°, optimal outcomes during primary ACL reconstruction may be achieved not through routine slope-reducing osteotomy, but rather through precise anatomic tunnel placement, strict adherence to a conservative rehabilitation protocol, and, in the presence of a grade 3 pivot shift, the addition of lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) to enhance rotational stability. However, in the setting of first-time revision ACL reconstruction, slope-reducing osteotomy may be considered in patients with an LPTS greater than 6.0° as an adjunct to these strategies in order to achieve successful results.

It is also important to recognize that factors unrelated to tibial morphology may contribute to early graft failure. Differences in graft type (e.g., allograft vs. autograft) have been shown to affect failure rates in previous studies [32, 33]. In our study, all ACL reconstructions—both primary and revision—were performed using hamstring autografts and with anatomic single-bundle tunnel placement. This methodological consistency is a strength of our study, as it minimizes confounding variables and enhances the reliability of our findings.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature is a limitation. Second, although power analyses confirmed that the study was adequately powered for the primary outcome, the overall sample size was relatively small, which restricted the feasibility of subgroup analyses, such as the female subgroup, which was underpowered to detect statistically significant differences. Moreover, because the cohort was predominantly male, the generalizability of our findings to female patients is limited, and future gender-stratified studies are warranted. Multicentric collaborations with larger cohorts would help overcome these limitations and validate our findings. Third, other factors known to influence graft failure—such as femoral notch width, and other morphological parameters—were not evaluated in this study. In addition, variables such as graft tensioning and postoperative activity level, which are recognized risk factors for graft failure, could not be systematically controlled. Finally, only univariate analyses were performed, as the limited sample size did not allow for a reliable multivariate analysis. As a result, the influence of these potential confounders could not be fully assessed. Future studies with larger sample sizes, prospective designs, and multivariate analyses are needed to better control for potential confounders and more comprehensively assess the impact of lateral and medial tibial posterior slope on graft failure risk. Due to the limited sample size, a sensitivity analysis could not be performed, which constitutes an additional limitation of our study. Future investigations with larger cohorts and subgroup analyses will be valuable in more clearly delineating the impact of minor trauma and non-contact reinjuries, thereby providing supportive evidence for our findings.

In conclusion, increased LPTS was found to be associated with early ACL graft failure. We recommend that orthopedic surgeons consider measuring LPTS as part of preoperative planning in patients undergoing ACL reconstruction.
