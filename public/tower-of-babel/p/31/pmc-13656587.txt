The impact of fresh versus cryopreserved testicular sperm on in-vitro fertilization outcomes in couples with elevated sperm DNA fragmentation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Dana R Siegel, Laura K Kaizer, Mary D Sammel, Cassandra Roeca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElevated sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) is associated with male factor infertility, recurrent pregnancy loss, and poor reproductive outcomes. An option for management includes the utilization of testicular sperm; however, the comparative reproductive efficacy of fresh versus cryopreserved testicular sperm men with elevated SDF remains unclear. Therefore, the objective of this study was to identify differences in reproductive outcomes between fresh and cryopreserved testicular sperm in patients with elevated SDF who are undergoing intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) after testicular sperm extraction (TESE).MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of a multi-site fertility network of patients with elevated SDF who underwent TESE and subsequent ICSI using either fresh or cryopreserved testicular sperm.ResultsA total of 60 men with elevated SDF underwent TESE and subsequent IVF with ICSI. 66.7% (n = 40) used fresh testicular sperm while the remaining 33.3% (n = 20) used cryopreserved testicular sperm following TESE. There were no significant differences in patient demographics or SDF (median 46.5 (IQR: 34.0, 59.0) vs. 48.0 (IQR: 38.0, 64.0), p = 0.525) between those that used fresh or cryopreserved testicular sperm. Among all initiated treatment cycles, there was no statistically significant difference in cumulative live birth (42.5% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.656), clinical pregnancy (55.0% vs. 35.0% p = 0.144) or miscarriage rates (10% vs. 5.0%, p = 0.185) between fresh and cryopreserved testicular sperm. Similarly, there were no significant differences in fertilization (median 48.8 (IQR 33.5, 61.3) vs. 43.7 (IQR:32.8, 63.1), p = 0.937) or blastocyst conversion (median 16.7 (IQR: 0.0, 40.0) vs. 26.1 (IQR: 16.7, 52.8), p = 0.106).ConclusionLive birth, clinical pregnancy, miscarriage, fertilization and blastocyst yield were not significantly different when using fresh or cryopreserved testicular sperm in couples undergoing TESE for elevated SDF. Background

The sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI) is an indicator of sperm DNA integrity by measuring the percentage of damaged sperm chromatin present in an ejaculate. There is increasing evidence that elevated sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) is more prevalent in those with male factor infertility and couples with recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) and has a negative impact on reproductive success [1–4]. Although DNA fragmentation analysis is not recommended in the initial evaluation of the infertile couple, the American Urological Association (AUA) and the American Society of Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) recommend evaluation of SDF in couples with failed assisted reproductive technology (ART) cycles and/or RPL [5]. Numerous factors are thought to induce SDF, which may occur due to underlying defects throughout the male reproductive tract, factors leading to delays in sperm transit, or may accumulate due to post-testicular damaging mechanisms including but not limited to increased scrotal temperature, obesity, infection, environmental toxins, diet, and hormonal imbalances [6, 7].

In couples with infertility or RPL found to have elevated DFI, there are several management options to discuss, including both medical and surgical interventions. While there are mixed results on the benefits of antioxidant therapy on SDF in infertile men [8–12], other conservative interventions to consider include treatment of sexually transmitted infections (STI) [13], weight loss [14], short-term recurrent ejaculation protocols [15], repair of varicoceles if present [16–18] and/or microfluidic sperm sorting devices [19]. Importantly, there has also been increasing evidence that use of testicular spermatozoa via testicular sperm extraction (TESE) in couples undergoing ART with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) have lower levels of SDF and better reproductive outcomes compared to ejaculated sperm [20–22]. Additionally, Xie et al. highlighted that mean SDF levels obtained from testicular sperm are lower than other surgically retrieved reproductive tract origins including the vas deferens and epididymis [23]. Clinically, using testicular sperm with ICSI as compared to ejaculated sperm in infertile couples with high DFI has shown to result in decreased rates of miscarriage and improved rates of clinical pregnancy and live birth [20, 21, 23–26].

After a surgical sperm retrieval, the AUA and ASRM state that the extracted sperm can then be used fresh for ICSI or cryopreserved for later ICSI. While utilizing cryopreserved sperm lowers the rate of cycle cancellations by ensuring viable sperm on the day of oocyte retrieval, allows more flexibility in timing for laboratory procedures, and decreases the need for repeated surgical retrievals, there have been some studies that have shown a detrimental impact on live sperm numbers and implantation rates after using frozen-thawed sperm in comparison to fresh sperm [27–29]. Nonetheless, the effects on live birth, clinical pregnancy, miscarriage or fertilization rates after ICSI using fresh or cryopreserved testicular sperm in couples utilizing TESE specifically in those with elevated SDF remains unknown. Therefore, the present study aims to compare the IVF and reproductive outcomes between fresh and cryopreserved TESE sperm in men with elevated DFI utilizing ICSI fertilization.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from a single fertility network, US Fertility, with study sites including Shady Grove Fertility, RMA of New York and IVF Florida. The electronic medical records were investigated to identify men who underwent a TESE procedure and subsequent ICSI due to elevated SDF using either fresh or cryopreserved testicular sperm between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2022.

Data collection

Once the cycle data from patients who underwent a TESE procedure between the queried dates was obtained, patient charts were reviewed to determine the primary reason for the TESE and only those who underwent the procedure for high DFI planning IVF were investigated further. All DNA fragmentation results were obtained from the same clinical laboratory using the HaloSperm G2® based on the Sperm Chromatin Dispersion (SCD) technique where anything < 20% is considered a normal result and thus these patients were excluded from analysis. We additionally excluded couples using donor gametes, female partner age > 42 years old, those who underwent TESE prior to DNA fragmentation assessment, and those receiving other assisted conception treatments including in vitro fertilization (IVF) with conventional insemination, oocyte cryopreservation, or intrauterine insemination (IUI). We obtained data on the characteristics of the female partners’ stimulation cycle in which the testicular sperm was used to perform ICSI, whether the sperm had been used as fresh or frozen thawed in the IVF with ICSI cycle immediately following TESE and the outcomes of all subsequent frozen embryo transfer(s) (FET) created from that particular cycle.

Study outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome of this study was cumulative live birth, defined as delivery of a viable infant at or beyond 24 weeks gestation. Secondary outcomes included fertilization, blastocyst conversion, clinical pregnancy and miscarriage rates. The fertilization rate was defined as the percentage of two pronuclei zygotes (2PN) per number of mature oocytes injected, the blastocyst conversion rate was the number of day 5 to day 7 blastocysts per number of 2PN, clinical pregnancy rate was defined as the presence of at least a gestational sac diagnosed by ultrasound and miscarriage rate was defined as a pregnancy loss after a confirmed clinical pregnancy. A positive clinical pregnancy was also identified in any cycle with evidence of a birth if the data on the gestational sac was missing. The exposure was fresh or cryopreserved testicular sperm after undergoing TESE due to elevated SDF.

Clinical protocols

Sperm DNA fragmentation testing. The DNA fragmentation assay was ordered by the treating physician, and all were analyzed at the same andrology laboratory using the Halosperm G2 Kit®. A fresh semen sample was collected in a sterile receptacle at the patient’s clinic location and shipped to Rockville, MD after frozen in liquid nitrogen (if applicable). The DNA fragmentation assay was then performed immediately after the sample was obtained or thawed. The sperm sample was diluted in a human sperm extender and placed into a melted agarose screw tube, which was then processed, solidified and incubated for 27 min. The sample was then washed with distilled water, dehydrated with ethanol and stained with Eosin Y where it was then visualized under bright field microscopy to determine the percentage of sperm with fragmented DNA.

Testicular sperm extraction

After sperm DNA fragmentation testing, the decision to undergo TESE due to IVF failure and high DFI was made through shared decision making between the treating physician and patient. The affected couples were referred to urology where the risks, benefits and alternatives of TESE were discussed in detail in addition to undergoing a comprehensive medical and physical examination. Once in agreement to move forward with TESE, the patients and treatment team discussed and predetermined if the testicular sperm would be used fresh in coordination with the female partner’s oocyte retrieval or cryopreserved and used at a later date. Prior failed IVF cycles with fresh testicular sperm were not a prerequisite for inclusion in the cryopreserved sperm group. The TESE procedures were performed in standard fashion where the scrotum was incised, dartos fascia was divided and the hemiscrotal compartment was entered where a small piece of seminiferous tubule was extracted and provided to the embryology team for further dissection and processing. First, 3–5 µL of the sample was observed under a conventional microscope while in the operating room to confirm the presence of sperm. The specimen was then transported to the embryology laboratory at room temperature with sperm wash media. In the laboratory, the tubules were mechanically teased apart under a dissecting microscope to release intratubular contents. Tissue fragments were then subjected to enzymatic digestion using collagenase and DNase I to break down interstitial tissue and basement membrane in order to liberate individual spermatozoa. The resulting suspension was washed and centrifuged, and the pellet examined under high-power microscopy to identify and isolate motile sperm for use in ICSI. If undergoing cryopreservation, the digested tissue was then mixed with equal volume of sperm freeze media using Quinn’s Advantage™ Sperm Freezing Medium (CooperSurgical, Trumbull, CT, USA), a HEPES-buffered medium containing glycerol and sucrose as cryoprotectants and human serum albumin, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The samples were aliquoted into cryovials, subjected to a controlled slow-cooling program with stepwise exposure to liquid nitrogen vapors, and then plunged into liquid nitrogen for long-term storage. Post-thaw, quality control was performed by assessing sperm motility and viability.

Controlled ovarian stimulation and insemination

Ovarian stimulation protocols and administration of the ovulatory trigger were performed per the standard protocols at the clinical sites. These protocols at the studied institutions typically include recombinant follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) and human menopausal gonadotropin as well as a gonadotrophin releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist for pituitary down-regulation. The ovulatory trigger was performed using either human chorionic gonadotrophic (HCG) alone (Novarel, Ferring Pharmaceuticals, USA; or Pregnyl, Merck, USA), GnRH agonist (leuprolide acetate) alone or as a dual trigger with both HCG and GnRH agonist. Oocyte retrieval was performed transvaginally under ultrasound guidance approximately 35–36 h after the ovulatory trigger had been administered. The utilization of fresh or frozen-thawed testicular sperm was predetermined and individualized by the treating physician and shared decision making with the patient. If using cryopreserved testicular sperm, the sample was first thawed in a water bath then centrifuged and washed. ICSI was performed by mechanically denuding the oocytes to assess nuclear maturity, followed by the injection of mature oocytes with a single sperm. The decision to perform PGT-A testing was predetermined by the treating physician and patient. If undergoing PGT-A analysis, the embryos that reached the blastocyst stage were biopsied by aspirating 3–7 cells of the trophectoderm using a biopsy pipette after the zona pellucida was perforated with laser pulsation.

Embryo transfer

All studied patients underwent a frozen embryo transfer (FET). Embryo cryopreservation was performed by vitrification at the blastocyst stage and warmed on the day of the FET. The decision to transfer one or more embryos was predetermined by the treating physician through shared decision making with the patient. Additionally, the endometrial preparation protocol for the FET was also predetermined by the treating physician but was performed per the standard protocols at the clinical sites, which typically includes programmed/medicated cycles. This programmed cycle often utilizes oral micronized estradiol (E2) (2 mg three times daily), or transdermal E2 (0.1 mg/24 hours) after a baseline hormone assessment and transvaginal ultrasound to ensure a thin endometrium without ovarian cysts. Once an adequate endometrium was achieved (≥ 7 mm thick and trilaminar in appearance), intramuscular progesterone in oil (PIO) alone (50 mg daily) or in combination every 3 days with intravaginal progesterone (200 mg twice daily) (Endometrin; Ferring Pharmaceuticals Inc, Parsippany, NJ) was initiated. The FET occurred under transabdominal ultrasound guidance 123 ± 3 h after progesterone initiation.

Pregnancy monitoring

Patients returned 9 days after the ET for serum hCG. If the hCG level measured > 5 mIU/mL, the hCG level was measured again 48 h later. Patients with a normally increasing hCG level underwent a viability ultrasound in the sixth week of gestation. Patients with an ongoing viable pregnancy transferred care with an obstetrician between 8 and 10 weeks of gestation.

Statistical analysis

Each participant’s first ICSI cycle following TESE and the corresponding embryos created from that cycle were included in the analysis after verification through chart review. Mean (SD), Median (25th percentile, 75th percentile), or n(percent) are presented.

Comparisons between couples utilizing fresh vs. cryopreserved testicular sperm after undergoing TESE due to elevated SDF including demographics, health history, and ICSI cycle characteristics were conducted using two-sample t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, negative binomial regression models, chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Cumulative outcomes (live birth, clinical pregnancy, and miscarriage) from embryos created from the first oocyte retrieval following TESE between fresh and cryopreserved testicular sperm were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s Exact tests. Two analyses were conducted–the first was among all initiated IVF cycles, which included couples who underwent IVF but did not go on to have a FET. The second analysis restricted to include only those who underwent a FET. In an effort to create an adjusted model among all initiated IVF cycles, a log-binomial regression model was used to assess whether livebirth differed by fresh vs. cryopreserved testicular sperm. Covariates to be considered were decided a priori (total motile count, PGT-A testing, female age, female BMI, semen morphology, DNA fragmentation percent, and number of embryos transferred). These were modeled one at a time in a model with fresh vs. cryopreserved testicular sperm. The percent change in the estimated log(risk) was calculated in each model. Variables with a 20% or greater change were included in the final model. This threshold was determined a priori due to the anticipated small cell sizes. Risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals are presented.

The fertilization and blastocyst conversion rates in patients who initially used fresh testicular sperm but later required additional ICSI cycles using frozen-thawed testicular sperm were also evaluated and are included in the supplementary analysis. We compared their outcomes when utilizing fresh vs. cryopreserved sperm using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which takes into account the paired nature of the data. Median (interquartile range) are presented for these analyses.

Results

The multi-center electronic medical record (EMR) database query first identified 68 patients who underwent TESE due to elevated SDF and subsequent ICSI during the study timeframe. Three patients were excluded as the TESE procedure was performed prior to the DNA fragmentation date of test, one patient was excluded as their partner did not proceed with IVF, two patients were excluded due to the use of donor gametes, and two were excluded due to a DFI of < 20% prior to their TESE procedure leaving a total of 60 patients included in analysis. Two of these patients went on to have additional FETs after their first live birth; however, only the FETs leading up to the first live birth were included in the analyses. Most participants (n = 40, 66.7%) utilized fresh TESE sperm during their first ICSI cycle, while 20 (33.3%) utilized a cryopreserved TESE sample. Both fresh and cryopreserved TESE groups were comparable in respect to male and female partner age, female BMI, % DNA fragmentation (46.5 (34.0, 59.0) vs. 48.0 (38.0,6 4.0, P = 0.525), baseline follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), antral follicle count (AFC), semen concentration and percent normal morphology at the time of the first ICSI cycle (Table 1). A total of 7 men had more than one DNA fragmentation test; however, their testicular sperm type (cryopreserved vs. fresh) was consistent. Therefore, the results from their first test date were used.

Analysis of the reproductive outcomes was performed for all 60 couples who initiated IVF treatment as well as for the subset of couples who underwent one or more FETs (i.e. excluding the 22 couples who did not undergo a FET) (Table 3). Among all initiated treatment cycles, there were no significant differences in cumulative clinical pregnancy, miscarriage or live birth (p = 0.144, p = 0.185 and p = 0.656 respectively). For the primary outcome of live birth, the unadjusted risk ratio was 1.62 when using fresh vs. cryopreserved testicular sperm (95%CI: 0.70, 3.76). No covariate assessed changed the log(risk) estimate coefficient by 20% or more. A sensitivity analysis was performed by adjusting for PGT and maternal age > = 35. The adjusted risk ratio was 1.71 (95% CI: 0.82, 3.57).

Discussion

In our study of 60 patients who underwent TESE due to elevated DFI, we found the cumulative live birth to be 42.50% when using fresh testicular sperm and 25.0% with cryopreserved testicular sperm though these results were not statistically significant. Similarly, we found no significant differences in fertilization, blastocyst conversion or miscarriage rates between fresh and cryopreserved testicular sperm in patients with elevated SDF. Furthermore, the 10 couples who had no embryo to transfer after using fresh testicular sperm and therefore subsequently used frozen testicular sperm for an additional ICSI cycle had no significant differences in fertilization or blastocyst conversion though the sample included in this analysis was very small. Though the observed differences in live birth between fresh and cryopreserved testicular sperm may indicate a clinically meaningful difference, this observational study was not sufficiently powered to demonstrate a statistically significant difference. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and support the need for larger prospective studies to better define the impact of sperm cryopreservation on reproductive outcomes in this population. Nonetheless, this study represents the largest cohort to date examining outcomes between fresh versus frozen testicular sperm in couples with high sperm DNA fragmentation and provides information for clinicians and patients planning TESE due to elevated SDF.

While incidence rates of clinically significant elevated DNA fragmentation are not fully defined in reproductive medicine literature, it is now well-recognized that high levels of DNA fragmentation may play a role in adverse fertility outcomes even with a seemingly “normal” semen analysis (SA) [4, 30–32]. Therefore, leading fertility organizations have developed guidelines to try to address these challenges. This is the reason why our study chose to focus specifically on patients with elevated sperm DNA fragmentation [5, 33]. One such validated recommendation includes using testicular spermatozoa rather than ejaculated spermatozoa along with ICSI after the male partner has been evaluated for all possible correctable measures to improve his reproductive health. With testicular sperm extraction, the immature spermatozoa are surgically retrieved from the seminiferous tubules where the earliest stages of spermatogenesis occur. In comparison to other surgically retrieved male reproductive tract origins, testicular spermatozoa tend to have lower DNA fragmentation indexes compared to spermatozoa from the epididymis or vas deferens likely owing to the decreased risk of exposure to oxidative stress and other damaging factors that can occur during the transit through the reproductive system [23, 34, 35].

After a surgical sperm retrieval, the extracted sperm can then be used before or after cryopreservation to inject mature oocytes. While there are many practical advantages to utilizing cryopreserved sperm in fertility treatment, there continues to be hesitation in recommending this approach universally given the potential detrimental impacts it may have on sperm quality and viability. At the molecular level, several studies have shown that various cryopreservation techniques, including both slow freezing and vitrification, lead to significantly increased levels of sperm DNA fragmentation and negative consequences on other semen parameters including progressive motility and morphology [36–38]. Several theories for these negative consequences have been proposed and include a breach in spermatozoa membranes leading to organelle dysfunction as a result of intra- and extracellular ice crystal formation, a reduction in antioxidant activity of spermatozoa making them more susceptible to reactive oxygen species (ROS), as well as cellular dehydration and osmotic pressure alterations as water effluxes from the internal to external environment during the thawing process [39–41].

Nonetheless, the clinical impact of cryopreservation specifically in the setting of patients with elevated SDF is overall sparse. Existing literature by Raad et al. highlighted that the DFI in fertile men was unaffected after cryopreservation using various different commercially available cryomedia compared to fresh samples; however, men with infertility had significantly increased DFI post-freezing/thawing and importantly showed a positive correlation to pre-freezing DFI levels [42]. These patients, however, had an unspecified infertility diagnosis and DNA fragmentation analysis was performed on ejaculated sperm without any reported clinical outcomes. In terms of surgically retrieved sperm, the large retrospective cohort study conducted by Lewin et al. in couples with male factor subfertility highlighted that there was no significant difference in live birth rates when using fresh or cryopreserved sperm; nevertheless, the lack of case level data and inability to differentiate between etiology of male subfertility makes the findings from this study not applicable to those specifically with elevated SDF [43]. Importantly, therefore, the results from our study looking only at patients undergoing TESE due to elevated SDF address this gap in the literature. Although the cumulative live birth rate was higher in the fresh vs. cryopreserved group (42.5% vs. 25.0%), this difference did not reach statistical significance and a larger sample size would be necessary to adequately assess whether the observed 17.5% absolute difference in live birth rate reflects a true effect. Our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and may inform the design of larger, adequately powered prospective studies in men with elevated SDF.

The findings from our study can be used in counseling couples who may choose to utilize cryopreserved testicular sperm during ART. One advantage to utilizing cryopreserved testicular sperm over freshly collected testicular sperm includes the ability to store sperm for future use thereby avoiding the need for repeated surgical sperm procedures in subsequent ART cycles. Furthermore, cryopreservation allows for improved logistical flexibility by separating the timing of sperm and oocyte retrieval thus reducing the stress of coordinating procedures and allowing both partners to be available to support each other post-operatively [44]. Given these advantages, our study suggests that live birth rates may not be significantly impacted when using cryopreserved testicular sperm in men undergoing TESE for elevated SDF. Additionally, we found that couples who utilized frozen testicular sperm after a previous failed fresh TESE cycle have comparable fertilization and blastocyst conversion rates as observed during the fresh TESE cycle.

Though this was a novel study highlighting the reproductive outcomes between fresh and cryopreserved testicular sperm in couples undergoing TESE specifically due to elevated SDF, there certainly were several limitations. Although our sample represents three large fertility clinical sites over the span of 6 years, the small sample size significantly reduced the statistical power of the study, limiting our ability to detect statistically significant results from clinically meaningful differences between groups. Power calculations demonstrated that more than 4 times as many participants would be needed to have 80% power for a 41% reduction in live birth after utilizing cryopreserved testicular sperm. The small number of observed live births in this sample also limited our ability to estimate a model that was adjusted for potential confounders and precision variables. Therefore, we report only unadjusted associations. To confidently establish that cryopreserved testicular sperm is non-inferior to fresh testicular sperm in this specific patient population, a substantially larger, adequately powered study would be required. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of the study inherently causes a lack of complete standardized data collection limiting the generalizability despite all ART cycles performed by the same parent fertility company with similar standards and protocols. Several male-factor variables potentially associated with high SDF including tobacco exposure, recent fever/illness, BMI, medications, or genitourinary infections and any corrective interventions undertaken between SDF testing and TESE were not consistently captured in the medical record; therefore, to avoid bias from selective or incomplete recording, these variables were not included in the primary analyses. Fortunately, however, identical DNA fragmentation assays (SCD) and reporting were performed by the same andrology laboratory, which has been cited as a limitation to other DNA fragmentation studies using differing platforms across results. Similarly, the TESE procedures were all performed by only three reproductive urologists employed by the same reproductive urology network, therefore limiting surgical technique confounders. An additional limitation of this retrospective study is that the specific clinical indication for sperm DNA fragmentation testing was not available. In clinical practice, DNA fragmentation testing may be obtained for a variety of reasons; however, this information was not available for this studied cohort. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate whether outcomes differed according to the underlying indication for testing or TESE utilization. Future prospective studies incorporating these clinical factors would help to address this concern.

Nonetheless, given the low prevalence of managing elevated DFI with TESE, this multi-center database allowed us to study the largest cohort of this population to date and supports the need for individualized counseling and shared decision-making with couples considering fresh versus frozen testicular sperm when undergoing TESE for elevated SDF. Despite these limitations, the findings from our study support the opinions of governing bodies of reproductive medicine and urology indicating that utilization of fresh or cryopreserved surgically collected sperm is acceptable for ICSI and can importantly be applied to patients undergoing TESE specifically for elevated SDF.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with elevated SDF undergoing TESE, fresh testicular sperm was associated with numerically higher cumulative live birth rates as compared to cryopreserved testicular sperm, though these results were not statistically significant likely due to the small sample size and therefore underpowered study. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and a larger prospective, non-inferior and potentially pragmatic study would help to strengthen these findings.
