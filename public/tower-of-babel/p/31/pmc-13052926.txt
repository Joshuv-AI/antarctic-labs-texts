Psychological determinants and predictors of outcomes in spinal cord stimulation: a systematic review
Authors: Caro Edelbroek, J.W. Kallewaard, Frank Huygen, Jitske Tiemensma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.A comprehensive synthesis of the findings from 29 included studies was meticulously presented. The results could serve as a valuable contribution in the spinal cord stimulation patient selection process.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain poses a significant global health burden, affecting approximately 30% of adults worldwide.17 Despite improved understanding pain pathophysiology, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience inadequate pain relief. This negatively affects their quality of life, daily functioning, and mental health.43

The treatment of chronic pain is inherently complex and varies depending on the individual needs of the patient. Common treatment approaches include pharmacological treatments, physical therapy, cognitive behavioral therapies, and/or minimally invasive pain treatments such as injection therapies. When these treatments fail to provide sufficient pain relief, more invasive neuromodulation options such as spinal cord stimulation (SCS) may be considered. Spinal cord stimulation is an invasive but reversible treatment in which 1 or more electrodes are implanted in the epidural space to modulate pain transmission in the nervous system. The treatment typically involves a trial period followed by permanent implantation of the Implantable Pulse Generator (IPG) if pain reduction exceeds 50%. Numerous high-quality studies have demonstrated that SCS can have positive effects on pain and holistic outcomes in specific chronic pain syndromes; persistent spinal pain syndrome type 2 (PSPS),9,15,21,23,28,36 complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS),24,34,35 diabetic polyneuropathy,1,11,50,57 and ischemic pain.13 Negative short- and long-term outcomes and explant rates of SCS are determined by clinical and technical aspects as well as pre-existing psychological factors.

Various psychological factors have been proposed as potential predictors of treatment outcomes, including lack of engagement, dysfunctional coping, unrealistic expectations, inadequate daily activity level, problematic social support, secondary gain, psychological distress, unwillingness to reduce high-dose opioids, trauma in childhood (history), and somatization.8,30,47In current practice, there is no consensus on the predictive outcome of the psychological factors for treatment outcome. This systematic review aimed to provide a structured overview of the existing literature and take a first step toward summarizing and synthesizing the existing evidence to determine the extent to which psychological factors influence both the short- and long-term SCS outcomes, thereby supporting clinical decision-making and future research.

2.1. Information sources

Studies were identified from major databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, MEDLINE, and Web of Science, using a predefined search strategy with specific keywords. For detailed information about the search strategy see Addendum 1, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A396.

2.2. Study selection process

The titles and abstracts of potentially relevant articles were independently screened by 2 authors (J.W.K. and C.E.) using Rayyan online software (Rayyan System, Cambridge, MA).40 Similarly, all potentially eligible citations had their full-text versions independently reviewed by 2 reviewers for final inclusion (J.W.K. and C.E.). The final selection of studies was performed schematically according to PRISMA (Fig. 1). Any discrepancies were resolved after discussion. There were no requests sent to authors for additional data. References of included articles were searched for possible additional articles missed through the initial literature search.

2.3. Method of extracting data

A textual narrative synthesis19,31 was conducted to summarize findings, this decision is consistent with PRISMA guidelines, which recommend narrative synthesis within a review when heterogeneity and design diversity preclude valid quantitative pooling. Owing to substantial methodological and clinical heterogeneity among the included studies, a meta-analysis was not performed. The studies varied in design, comprising both single-arm pre–post evaluations and nonrandomized comparative cohorts, and used different outcome measures and follow-up periods. These differences precluded the calculation of a common effect size or a meaningful pooled estimate. Furthermore, uncontrolled pre–post studies and nonrandomized comparisons estimate fundamentally different parameters—absolute change vs relative group differences—making quantitative synthesis inappropriate. Therefore, we opted for a narrative synthesis, presenting study characteristics and outcomes descriptively to allow for contextual interpretation.

2.4. Methods for assessing risk of bias

Selection bias was minimized by selecting the articles with 2 independent reviewers.

For the nonrandomized controlled studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa (NOS) scale was used. The NOS tool assesses the risk of bias in 3 domains including the selection of the study group(s), the comparability of the group(s), and the ascertainment of the outcome of interest.10 The various domains encompass a range of inquiries that can be addressed with a binary response, designated as “*.” The greater the number of stars awarded to an article, the higher the quality of the study is perceived to be, and consequently, the risk of bias is reduced. The study's quality is determined by the number of stars it is awarded, with a maximum of 9 stars indicating a high level of quality. Specifically, a study is considered to be of fair quality if it has received between 4 and 6 stars, while a study is deemed to be of poor quality if it has received between zero and 3 stars.7

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

The search yielded 2893 studies. Duplicates (n = 1733) were removed. After title/abstract screening, n = 226 studies remained. During the full-text availability check, all studies could be retrieved. Of the 226 studies that underwent a full-text screening, n = 193 were excluded because they did not meet the selection and inclusion criteria. Agreement between reviewers before discussion was 100% for title/abstract screening, 82% for full-text eligibility, and 100% after a discussion session. When multiple studies were based on the same research trial, the most recently published study was included. A total of n = 29 studies were included, comprising results from more than 17,000 patients for further analysis. A complete overview of the included studies is summarized in Table 1.

3.2. Study characteristics

The identified studies included single-center (n = 20) and multicenter (n = 9) approaches across 9 different countries. Twenty of the 29 studies were conducted in the United States and 10 studies took place in Europe.

Twenty studies were conducted in a single center. Nine studies took place in multicenters. Fourteen studies were prospective of which one was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) and 15 studies used retrospective data.

Seven studies reported no conflicts of interest, while 5 studies lacked a report on conflicts of interest. Four studies were funded by device manufacturers. Other declared conflicts were mainly due to authors providing consultancy to device manufacturers (n = 17), being employed by medical companies, or having received grants from medical companies.

4. Domain fear/anxiety

A number of studies have investigated the relationship between fear/anxiety and SCS outcome, demonstrating both predictive and nonpredictive directions.

A weak correlation was identified between pain and anxiety at baseline and postimplantation, suggesting that heightened anxiety levels might be associated with increased pain intensity.47 Moreover, a study found that there was a small association between anxiety, childhood trauma, and treatment effectiveness.32 A number of studies have indicated that anxiety may have a bearing on SCS outcomes in certain circumstances. For instance, patients diagnosed with anxiety disorders exhibited higher rates of SCS removal within a two-year period when compared with those not diagnosed with mental disorders. However, subsequent surgeries following this procedure did not demonstrate a significant impact, thereby leading researchers to conclude that anxiety does not exert a substantial influence on the postremoval surgical rates.55 Patients with a documented history of anxiety were found to have a higher probability of undergoing SCS system removal within the first year on implantation.42 Patients who successfully completed an SCS trial showed significantly lower anxiety levels compared with patients who did not successfully completed the trial, suggesting that fear and anxiety may contribute to the success of a seven-day trial period.14 In addition, this study demonstrated the correlation between elevated levels of kinesiophobia (fear of movement) in the preoperative period and the nonresponsiveness to 10-kHz SCS therapy in nonresponders.56

However, the extant literature suggests that fear and anxiety do not play a significant role in predicting the outcomes of SCS. For instance, it was reported that although patients with lower anxiety scores on the Derogatis Affects Balance Scale experienced more successful trial stimulations, anxiety was not a predictor of long-term SCS success.38 Similarly, no association was found between anxiety levels and trial success rates.39 No significant differences were identified in preoperative Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale scores between patients who failed the trial phase and those who proceeded to IPG implantation, indicating limited predictive value for long-term outcomes.61 Furthermore, no evidence was found to support the hypothesis that fear and anxiety were associated with suboptimal SCS results.3 The extant literature did not support a statistically significant relationship between anxiety and SCS outcomes.4 It was further noted that fear and anxiety did not precede chronic pain onset nor did they influence SCS outcomes.46 A similar conclusion was reached regarding the determination that fear and anxiety were not predictive factors in the analysis in question.58 Elevated baseline anxiety levels had no significant effect on treatment outcomes.49 After comprehensive analysis, it was determined that fear and anxiety were not associated with SCS failure or removal.20

In total, data from more than 3,400 patients across 8 studies demonstrated that fear and anxiety were nonpredictive factors for SCS outcomes. These studies encompassed both short- and long-term follow-up periods. By contrast, 6 predictive studies, involving a combined sample of 11,861 patients, suggested that fear and anxiety may be associated with short-term outcomes and the likelihood of removal within 2 years of follow-up.

The methodological quality of both the predictive and nonpredictive studies ranged from fair to good on the NOS, indicating a low to moderate risk of bias. There was no significant difference in quality between the predictive and nonpredictive studies when the results were weighted. Overall, the results of the various studies show a certain degree of heterogeneity: although most of the evidence points to a lack of predictive value, several larger studies indicate that fear and anxiety may influence short-term outcomes and the likelihood of implant removal. This mixed pattern of findings justifies the classification of the domain of fear–anxiety as partially predictive of SCS outcomes. An overview of the classification within the fear/anxiety domain is summarized in Table 4.

5. Domain depressive feelings

A number of studies have indicated that depressive feelings may act as a predictor of SCS outcomes. It has been established that depression has a detrimental effect on pain, with depressive feelings proving to be predictive in 88% of the study population.6 An analysis of explanted SCS patients revealed that depressive feelings were a negative predictor, correlating with higher explant rates.20 It has also been reported that patients with higher Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI) depression subscale scores were more likely to experience inadequate pain relief during SCS trials. The MMPI depression score has been shown to predict outcomes after 3 months of SCS therapy.39 Depression has been identified as a factor influencing pain intensity and disability scores, affecting SCS treatment effectiveness.52 Depressive feelings have been identified as a risk factor for SCS therapy failure.3 Research has been conducted into depression and childhood trauma in relation to SCS therapy, finding a moderate association between depressive feelings, trauma, and SCS outcomes.32 The PETSCSC tool was used to assess depressive feelings, concluding that this domain is predictive for SCS outcomes.45 It was also found that patients with a history of depression were more likely to have their SCS system removed within 1 year of implantation, although depression was not associated with pain outcomes or SCS efficacy.42 It has been hypothesized that patients who demonstrate effective management of their overall health, including depressive symptoms, are more likely to respond positively to SCS therapy.58 Other findings indicate that depression independently reduces the likelihood of an SCS responder.22 Research has revealed that patients who have undergone successful SCS trials have reported lower levels of depression before the trial and a decrease in depression levels after the trial.14

Conversely, the findings of certain studies have suggested an absence of substantial predictive value. Long-term outcomes in patients exhibiting severe psychological disturbances, including depressive symptoms, have been observed to be comparable with those observed in other patients.59 This study found that SCS outcomes could not be predicted based on tested psychological factors, including depressive feelings.61 It was confirmed that depression did not precede chronic pain onset and was not predictive for SCS outcomes.46 Depressive feelings were included under the broader category of mental disorders but no predictive value for removal rates and additional surgery were found.55 In a cohort study, no psychological predictors were found, including depressive feelings.49 Also found not predictive was depression; this factor did not seem to influence increased explant rate specific in women.51

Within the domain of depression, 17 studies were analyzed. In 11 of these studies (including 3,600 patients), depressive symptoms were identified as a negative predictor for both short- and long-term SCS outcomes. Patients with depressive symptoms were more likely to have suboptimal outcomes and were at higher risk of insufficient therapeutic effectiveness. By contrast, 6 studies (encompassing more than 11,000 patients) found no predictive association between depressive symptoms and SCS outcomes.

In summary, the evidence suggests that depression can be considered as partially predictive of SCS outcomes, contributing to an increased risk of nonresponsiveness, implant removal, and reduced therapeutic benefit during both short- and long-term follow-up.

The total number of patients included in the nonpredictive studies was greater than the number included in the predictive studies. The subsequent follow-up period for both predictive and nonpredictive studies reviewed was comparable. A comparative analysis of the quality of the studies showed that the predictive studies were generally of higher quality than the nonpredictive studies. An overview of the classification within the depressive feelings domain is summarized in Table 5.

6. Domain catastrophizing

In recent decades, there has been a growing interest in the role of pain catastrophizing in SCS outcome. One study showed patients with lower catastrophizing levels experienced less pain, greater pain relief, higher satisfaction with SCS therapy, and improved quality of life. By contrast, those with higher catastrophizing levels reported increased pain intensity and poorer treatment outcomes.47 The impact of autonomous coping on pain reduction after SCS therapy was highlighted in another study. It was noted that coping strategies influence sensory factors such as pain intensity and disability, ultimately affecting SCS effectiveness.52 It was also found that greater reductions in the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) scores were associated with remission, suggesting that depressive feelings and pain catastrophizing play a role in predicting SCS outcome.25 Most patients who failed SCS exhibited a dysfunctional coping profile, reinforcing the idea that pain catastrophizing influences treatment success.41 Catastrophizing was found to be a positive predictor of SCS outcomes.45 A significant difference in baseline pain catastrophizing levels between SCS responders and nonresponders were identified, concluding that catastrophizing is a key predictor of treatment outcomes.56

Other studies suggest no predictive value of pain catastrophizing in SCS; no evidence was found that pain catastrophizing predicted SCS outcomes.29 In addition, no correlation was reported between PCS scores and SCS success, nor any relationship between PCS scores and the time to device removal.44 Catastrophizing was not associated with SCS explant rates and had no predictive value.51 Another study highlighted the importance of considering psychological factors, including coping, in SCS treatment planning and follow-up. However, the study did not establish pain catastrophizing as a definitive predictor of SCS success.60

The present domain has been delineated in the context of 10 studies. In 6 of 10 studies, this domain was found to be a useful predictor. Patients who engage in catastrophizing are more likely to have worse outcomes with SCS. The total number of patients in these predictive studies was 799. Conversely, 4 studies encompassing 418 patients collectively demonstrated an absence of a predictive factor within this domain.

The quality of both predictive and nonpredictive studies was rated as good, with the risk of bias considered low based on the NOS quality assessment. Based on the larger number of participants in the predictive studies, the fact that all results from these studies were long-term results and the good quality of the studies, pain catastrophizing was classified as partially predictive for the outcome of SCS at long-term follow-up. This domain was classified as partially predictive because the nonpredictive studies were comparable in quality and also showed long-term results (in 3 of the 4 studies).

An overview of the classification within the catastrophizing domain is summarized in Table 6.

7. Domain sleep

A number of studies have identified an association between sleep disturbance and outcomes of SCS; As emphasized, the significance of sleep interference was a key finding. It was observed that sleep interference, as measured by a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score greater than 7 of 10, emerged as an independent predictor of a less favorable outcome in the context of the multiple logistic regression analysis. This finding suggests the potential of sleep interference as a predictor of short-term SCS outcomes.3 In addition, patients who underwent a successful SCS trial exhibited considerably diminished levels of sleep disturbance at the initial baseline assessment. It was determined that sleep disturbance is a predictive factor for SCS outcomes.14

Conversely, other studies have not identified a relation between sleep disturbances and the outcomes of SCS; there is an absence of evidence to support the hypothesis that sleep disturbance is a predictive factor for SCS outcomes.4 Trends of improvement in sleep disturbance were identified across groups (remitter and nonresponder group), particularly with the Insomnia Severity Index. However, no significant relationship was identified between remitter status and sleep disturbance, suggesting that sleep disturbance does not serve as a predictor of SCS outcomes.25 One study revealed no statistically significant difference in the PROMIS sleep variable at baseline between the 2 groups (responders/nonresponders). Indicating that sleep disturbance does not serve as a predictor of SCS outcomes.56

In summary, 2 studies identified this domain as a predictor of SCS outcome (141 patients), while 3 studies did not (357 patients).

The quality of the studies was good based on the NOS, meaning that the risk of bias was low. The subsequent follow-up periods and the quality of the studies in both predictive and nonpredictive analyses were virtually equivalent. The number of participants in nonpredictive studies was higher, but not high enough to influence the conclusions. Therefore, this domain was classified as inconclusive. An overview of the classification within the sleep domain is summarized in Table 7.

8. Domain social functioning

Within the domain social functioning, only 2 studies were analyzed, showing contradictive results. One explored the impact of loneliness, a component of the social functioning domain, on SCS outcomes. It was found that loneliness was associated with increased pain and worse postoperative outcomes. Based on these findings, the study concluded that social functioning was a predictive factor for SCS outcomes.26

By contrast, the other study found no statistically significant difference in PROMIS social role scores at baseline between the 2 groups (responders and nonresponders). This result, along with the lack of a statistical difference related to the history of psychiatric illness in the sample, led the authors to conclude that the social functioning domain was not predictive of SCS outcomes in their study.56

The nonpredictive study was a retrospective study of 237 patients, with the focus being on the trial period only. The study was evaluated as being of good quality and was deemed to have a low risk of bias. In the predictive study, data were prospectively analyzed for 69 patients, with a follow-up period of 1 year. The study was evaluated as “good-poor” on the NOS, indicating a reasonable probability of bias. Specifically, this means that while the different variables were mentioned, the comparability was not controlled for confounders.

In summary, the existing results were inconclusive regarding the predictive value of this domain for SCS outcome. This is primarily due to factors such as the length of follow-up periods, the number of participants, and the quality of the studies.

Furthermore, it should be noted that only 2 studies demonstrated results in this domain.

An overview of the classification within the sleep domain is summarized in Table 8.

9. Domain pain

Within the domain pain, several studies investigated the role of preprocedure pain duration and pain intensity and SCS outcomes. One study suggests that the duration of pain may be a relevant factor in predicting the success of SCS therapy.58 Pain intensity at baseline was also found predictive of SCS outcomes. Statistically significant differences were observed, with nonresponders demonstrating higher pain intensity at baseline in comparison with the responders.56

By contrast, other findings suggested that neither the duration nor the severity of pain were indicative of SCS outcomes.3,29 In addition, no difference in preoperative NRS pain scores between patients with unsuccessful trials and those who underwent IPG implantation were found. Consequently, the study concluded that the pain domain was not a predictive factor for SCS outcomes.61

The 2 predictive studies were found to be of good to fair quality, with a low risk of bias or with some concerns identified. The total number of patients in these 2 studies was n = 282. The nonpredictive studies included a total of 152 patients and were classified as good, fair, or poor. This classification indicates a low or high risk of bias, with concerns for bias.

The 2 predictive studies (including 282 patients) were found to be of good to fair quality, with a low risk of bias or with some concerns identified. The 3 nonpredictive studies included a total of 152 patients and were rated as good, fair, or poor on the NOS. This classification indicates a low or high risk of bias, with concerns about bias. Despite the larger number of participants and the slightly higher quality rating in the predictive studies, no conclusion can be drawn as to whether pain can be considered a reliable predictor of SCS outcome. The difference is not significant enough to consider this domain predictive. The classification inconclusive is also based on the lack of studies in this area. 10. Domain psychiatric factors

10.1. Several studies have identified psychiatric factors as predictive of SCS outcomes

Poorer outcomes and lower patient satisfaction were associated with higher preimplant MMPI-2-RF scores, concluded that several psychiatric factors could negatively affect cognition, motivation, compliance, and pain perception, thus influencing SCS outcomes. The psychiatric domain was therefore considered predictive.5 Other results revealed that patients who failed SCS therapy often had a higher presence of psychiatric disorders, which significantly influenced their experience and perception of pain. This study concluded that the psychiatric domain is predictive of SCS outcomes.41 Furthermore, patients with a history of post-traumatic stress symptoms were more likely to have their SCS system removed within 1 year of implantation. The psychiatric domain was identified as predictive for SCS outcomes.42 In addition, psychiatric comorbidities were associated with higher removal rates of SCS systems. The psychiatric group had higher removal rates than the nonpsychiatric group, with each additional psychiatric comorbidity increasing the odds of reoperation. The study concluded that the psychiatric domain was predictive of SCS outcomes.2

10.2. Several studies did not find psychiatric factors to be predictive of spinal cord stimulation outcomes

No evidence to support the psychiatric domain as a predictor of SCS outcomes.59 Patients who proceeded to permanent implant had elevated MMPI hypochondriasis scale scores. While associations were found between successful trials and MMPI scales, none of the factors were statistically significant predictors of long-term SCS outcomes.38 Patients with successful trial phases had higher energy levels, as measured by the MMPI mania subscale. However, their data did not indicate a predictive role for stress-related disorders, concluding that the psychiatric domain was not predictive of SCS outcomes.39 Results also showed that patients with high levels of childhood trauma did not experience pain relief from neuropathic pain 12 months after SCS implantation, in contrast to those with low childhood trauma. However, those with more childhood trauma did report similar pain and disability relief postsurgery compared with the low trauma group. The psychiatric domain was not considered a convincing predictor in this study.32 Psychiatric factors, specifically psychoses, were not predictive of SCS outcomes.20 No statistically significant difference in PROMIS scores at baseline between 2 groups, which also indicated no difference related to psychiatric illness history. As a result, the psychiatric domain was not considered predictive for SCS outcomes in this study.56

The psychiatric domain was found to have been studied in 10 articles. Six studies showed that there was no predictive value of SCS outcome, while 4 studies did indicate a predictive value for this domain. The quality of both predictive- and nonpredictive studies varied within the NOS, with studies falling into all 3 categories: good, fair, and poor. This variation in quality resulted in a spectrum of risk of bias, ranging from low to high or moderate. The number of patients examined in the predictive studies was more than 13,000. In the nonpredictive studies, that number was 3,946. All predictive studies presented long-term follow-up results, while the nonpredictive studies showed both short- and long-term follow-up results. In summary, it can be stated that the role of psychiatric factors in predicting SCS outcomes is inconsistent in the literature reviewed and cannot be considered predictive. This classification was based on patient numbers, the quality of the studies, and the follow-up terms. The only specific conclusion that can be drawn is that patients with 5 or more existing psychiatric disorders may be at increased risk of further surgery in the context of SCS during long-term follow-up. 11. Discussion

This systematic review provides an overview of potential psychological factors that may predict outcomes for SCS as a treatment for various chronic pain conditions. A plethora of additional factors were identified in the reviewed articles. For instance, quality of life, physical functioning, sex, age and patients' comorbidities were used as outcome measures in numerous studies. However, these factors were excluded from the results due to their nonspecific psychological nature and utilization as outcome factors in SCS studies rather than predictive factors.

Numerous publications addressed the selected psychological factors and this review aimed to synthesize the findings, offering guidance to healthcare professionals working with SCS patients. It also seeks to contribute to decision-making processes and offer insights into future research directions.

Overall, most research suggested that the domain fear and anxiety was partially predictive for SCS. While some studies indicate a weak or situation-dependent influence, most show no strong correlation. More research is required to determine whether fear and anxiety affect short-term trial success, and whether addressing fear and anxiety could improve treatment outcomes. One older review found that fear and anxiety were predictive of poor SCS outcomes, stating that psychological factors such as anxiety play a significant role.8 However, this review is outdated and should be reassessed in light of more recent findings. A more recent systematic review found a positive correlation between pain and anxiety in SCS patients, emphasizing the interaction between personal factors and bodily functions/structures in SCS treatments.16 This review supports a cautious, domain-specific approach rather than exclusionary decision-making. In daily practice, instrument choice and cut-offs in fear/anxiety questionnaires should be more consistent to add to more consistent findings. Practical guidance involves pain education; reassurance and expectation management should be provided to patients. Consider prehabilitation to reduce fear-driven nonadherence during the trial and long-term follow-up.

Some studies suggested that depressive feelings may negatively affect SCS therapy outcomes, while others found no significant predictive relationship. Research showed that depression may correlate with higher explant rates, reduced pain relief, and a lower likelihood of being an SCS responder. However, other studies argued that depression did not independently predict SCS success. A comparison of the results of this review with those of earlier studies and reviews reveals a divergence of opinion regarding the suitability of SCS for patients with depressive feelings. While suggested that such patients may benefit more from SCS,12 another study found that those with low depression levels are more suitable candidates for implantable therapies.48 However, the findings must be considered with a degree of caution due to the relatively small sample size of the follow-up study. A study conducted on 915 patients who had received SCS or spine surgery, concluded that emotional/internalizing dysfunction can predict poorer outcomes. This finding suggests the possibility that depression may be a predictive factor for SCS outcomes.33 Further research is required with larger data sets and comparable questionnaires to confirm these results. This review aligns with a case-by-case approach, avoiding categorical exclusion. In daily practice, personalized preimplant mood optimization, enlarging overall motivation and setting realistic expectations is advised.

The role of pain catastrophizing in SCS outcomes remains unclear. Some studies suggest that higher levels of catastrophizing are associated with increased pain, reduced pain relief, and higher treatment failure rates, while others find no significant relationship. The domain catastrophizing was therefore considered partially predictive for SCS outcomes.

Comparing this conclusion with other reviews and previous literature, it is stated that catastrophizing is not a proven predictive value for SCS outcomes; in a review from 2009 to 2016, the findings stated that pretreatment for psychological variables as catastrophizing did not predict treatment outcom.12

In another systematic review,16 the association between catastrophizing and pain is not statistically significantly proven. From a qualitative approach, catastrophizing was recognized by patients who were interviewed in this study53 and the data from this study showed patients were using passive coping strategies which let to negative thoughts and passing responsiveness to the healthcare provider. This study advised considering this topic to be part of pre-SCS education and actively guiding patients to achieve active coping strategies may affect treatment outcomes. Self-efficacy should also be reinforced. During the trial and the early postimplant period, structured feedback and graded activity could be provided to build confidence and motivate patients to apply active coping strategies.

The domains sleep, social functioning and pain were found to be inconclusive for SCS outcomes. The domain sleep was examined in 5 studies. Two studies found a predictive link to SCS outcomes, while 3 did not. Overall, the evidence is inconclusive regarding sleep's impact on SCS success. The role of social functioning in predicting SCS outcomes remains unclear. Only 2 studies within this review assessed this domain, with one study concluding to be predictive and one study was nonpredictive. Consequently, no conclusion can be drawn about the reliability of social functioning as a predictor of SCS outcomes. Pain, as a predictor for SCS outcomes, was studied in 5 articles. Three of these studies found no predictive value for pain. Although some studies identified pain-related factors (such as duration and intensity) as predictors of SCS success, a previous literature review concluded that pain is not a predictor of SCS outcomes.8 In addition, no statistical significance between pain and SCS outcomes were found.12 Therefore, additional research is needed to clarify the predictive potential of the domains sleep, social functioning, and pain on SCS outcomes, which will require the use of big data sources, or studies with enough power to show an significant effect size. Practical guidance consists of addressing severe insomnia before implantation. Intervene pragmatically: Sleep hygiene and medication review. For social functioning address modifiable barriers: Link patients to rehabilitation and social support pathways to facilitate activity goals. For the domain pain, specific phenotypes (eg, neuropathic vs nociceptive components) may matter for indication suitability, but are not consistently predictive of response magnitude. Implications for practice advice to not overweigh baseline intensity: Prioritize modifiable psychological domains and functional goals over numeric pain scores.

Psychiatric disorders and symptoms showed mixed results in the reviewed studies. An earlier review concluded that patients without psychiatric disorders had better outcomes in disability and pain catastrophizing at the one-year follow-up, although these findings were not statistically significant.12 A more recent review found that psychiatric disorders did not predict SCS outcomes, suggesting that patients with psychiatric comorbidities should not be automatically excluded from SCS therapy.18 Another older review, noted inconsistent findings across studies, making conclusions challenging.54 This review is subject to several limitations. The included studies varied widely in design, follow-up duration, and patient populations—spanning conditions such as PSPS type 2, CRPS, peripheral neuropathic pain, and ischemic pain—making direct comparisons challenging. In some domains, smaller studies report predictive effects while larger data sets report null findings. This imbalance should be acknowledged to avoid overstating weak or inconsistent results. Differences in SCS waveforms, implant materials, psychological definitions, and measurement tools further limit the generalizability of findings. Most studies were nonrandomized, reducing the overall level of evidence. In view of the limitations, it proved impossible to conduct a meta-analysis. A quantitative meta-analysis was not conducted, which may limit the ability to provide a pooled estimate of effect. However, this decision was methodologically justified given the substantial heterogeneity in study designs (single-arm pre–post and nonrandomized comparative cohorts) and outcome measures, as well as the conceptual differences in estimates. In line with PRISMA recommendations, a narrative synthesis was deemed the most appropriate approach to ensure transparency and validity of the review findings. In addition, the review was restricted to English-language publications, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant data. Concerning mixed findings within certain domains; the inconsistencies observed across studies within each domain may partly reflect differences in the psychological measures used. Constructs such as anxiety and coping are often operationalized using diverse instruments, each with unique psychometric properties, scoring thresholds, and sensitivity to change. These methodological variations can influence effect sizes and comparability, thereby contributing to the mixed findings rather than indicating true variability in underlying phenomena. Acknowledging this measurement heterogeneity is essential for interpreting the observed patterns. Nonetheless, the inclusion of a sufficient number of studies and the use of independent reviewers for selection and quality assessment support the reliability of the findings.

To strengthen the evidence base, future research should focus on large-scale, high-quality RCTs and real-world (big) data analyses.27

It is clear that this review cannot provide definitive conclusions or make statements with certainty. This is mainly due to the use of an abundance of assessment tools and the considerable variability in definitions in the literature reviewed. Therefore, it is crucial to reach consensus on standardized psychological assessment tools and definitions to improve comparability between studies. Moreover, future research should explore tailored psychological interventions before SCS implantation, aiming to optimize patient selection and treatment outcomes.

12. Conclusions

This review, using narrative synthesis alongside systematic guideline application and rigorous quality assessment, determined that the evidence supporting psychological factors as predictors of SCS outcomes is markedly inconsistent and exhibits substantial variability.

This review demonstrates that fear/anxiety, depressive feelings, and catastrophizing exhibit partial predictive value for specific SCS outcomes under certain conditions, whereas the domains sleep, social functioning, pain, and psychiatric factors do not seem to be decisive and/or predictive. These findings underscore that psychological factors should not serve as sole determinants for SCS eligibility. When a factor demonstrates limited predictive influence, exclusion from therapy is unwarranted; rather, it should be addressed through targeted interventions in collaboration with the patient. Conversely, when a factor is predictive, structured prehabilitation should be implemented to optimize outcomes and enhance care quality. Only in cases where such interventions fail to mitigate the predictive factor should SCS be reconsidered as the preferred treatment option.
