Wild boar as a sentinel species for ecosystem health surveillance: A review
Authors: P. Barroso, C. Gortázar, A. Balseiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractUnlabelled Image

Introduction

Ecosystem health refers to the capacity of an ecosystem to maintain its structure, functions and processes over time while sustaining biodiversity, resilience, and the provision of ecosystem services such as food or freshwater [1], [2]. Within a One Health framework, this concept also encompasses the dynamic interactions among environmental quality, wildlife populations and potential risks to animal and human health, including the circulation of pathogens, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and environmental pollutants [3]. In this context, wild animal populations have been widely used as early warning systems for public health hazards, for example, by providing evidence of pathogen circulation and the emergence of infectious diseases [4], [5]. Current integrated wildlife monitoring (IWM) systems, often focused on pathogens but increasingly extended to environmental health, aim to assess and monitor ecosystem health and support the implementation of effective risk management actions based on the data generated [6], [7]. However, IWM implementation is limited by the cost and complexity of monitoring a wide range of species and hazards in ecological communities [8]. Among the strategies proposed to address these constraints is the identification of suitable indicator or sentinel species [9], [10].

Sentinel surveillance focuses on specific species or populations to improve the detection of environmental and infectious hazards while reducing the costs associated with monitoring [11], [12]. Its main objective is to provide timely and affordable information, rather than precise estimates of risk for the entire population [10]. For these purposes, not all species are equally suitable as sentinels. Obtaining representative sample sizes can be particularly challenging for certain taxa, especially small or elusive species such as rodents or carnivores, which often require considerable time and effort to sample [13], [14], [15]. Ideally, sentinel species should be widely distributed, abundant, and well connected within ecological networks (i.e., host communities), as well as easy to sample [8], [10]. In addition, they should be exposed to a broad range of hazards and capable of developing detectable immune responses to them, thereby maximizing the information obtained while minimizing sampling effort, in line with current principles aimed at reducing wildlife use in research [10], [16], [17]. Anyway, the criteria used to assess the suitability of sentinel species depend on the aims and context of each surveillance system.

The Eurasian wild boar and its relative, the feral pig (Sus scrofa), meet many of these conditions. Its wide distribution, increasing population density and frequent interaction with human-modified environments, together with its importance as a game species, facilitate sample collection in many regions [8], [18], [19]. In addition, its ability to mount immune responses to multiple pathogens, often without developing clinical disease, makes it a valuable sentinel species for pathogen surveillance [20], [21]. This review assesses the use of wild boar as a sentinel species for monitoring ecosystem health hazards by analyzing the available literature and studying how this concept has been explicitly applied. Specifically, we characterized the main hazards monitored, including pathogens, AMR, biological toxins and environmental pollutants; examined the rationale for its selection; and identified the main limitations reported by the authors. We also synthetized authors' perceptions regarding its suitability as a sentinel and their recommendations to enhance its use in IWM systems, where animal sentinels remain underused despite their potential to inform risk assessment for both human and animal health [10], [17], [22].

Methods

In March 2026, we conducted a systematic literature review following the methodological guidelines of Haddaway et al. [23] and the PRISMA protocol [24] (Supplementary material 1 - Fig.S1). For this purpose, we used the Scopus and Web of Science platforms.

We searched for studies that mentioned the term “wild boar” together with either “sentinel”, “indicator” or “bioindicator” in their titles, abstracts, and/or keywords, using the Boolean search string: (“wild boar”) AND (“sentinel” OR “indicator” OR “bioindicator”). The same Boolean search strategy was applied in both databases. We selected original research articles published in English up to the search date that explicitly described or discussed the use of wild boar as a sentinel, indicator or bioindicator species for monitoring ecosystem health hazards (e.g., pathogens, environmental contaminants, biological toxins or AMR). Studies on captive or experimentally infected animals, reviews, perspectives, conference abstracts, and studies that did not explicitly consider wild boar as a sentinel or indicator species were excluded. The initial search yielded a total of 571 articles. After removing duplicates (206 articles), we manually verified the remaining 365 articles. We screened the full text of these articles to assess their eligibility, excluding those that did not meet the established criteria (279 articles) (Supplementary material 1 – Fig. S1). Finally, 86 studies were reviewed for data extraction and analysis. The variables extracted from each study are compiled in Table 1. Specifically, the perceived suitability of wild boar as a sentinel species was extracted from the authors' discussion and conclusions and classified into four qualitative categories according to the terminology used (see Table 1). When the wording was ambiguous, the full context of the discussion and conclusions was considered to assign the category that best reflected the authors' overall interpretation. Results

General results

A total of 86 articles employing wild boar as a sentinel or indicator species were identified. The earliest publication dates to 1985, followed by a gap until 2004. Health hazards, sample types and analytical methods

The animals sampled were mainly obtained from hunting activities or population control programs (90.7% of the reviewed studies). This was followed by live-captured animals that were subsequently released (5.8%), animals captured and euthanized for study purposes (2.3%), road-killed individuals (2.3%), and, to a lesser extent, individuals found dead by game keepers (1.2%) or non-invasive sampling approaches such as environmental faecal collection (1.2%) (Fig. 2). Each study could fit into more than one of the proposed categories; therefore, percentages may sum to more than 100% throughout this section.

Regarding the ecosystem health hazards monitored, most studies used wild boar as sentinels for pathogen circulation (57%), followed by environmental contaminants (31.4%), while fewer focused on antimicrobial resistance (AMR; 12.8%) or biological toxins (3.5%) (Fig. 1b).

Among those studies focused on pathogens, a total of 30 different pathogens were assessed (Fig. 2 & Supplementary material 1 - Table S1). Viruses and bacteria were the most frequently studied groups (46.9% and 38.8%, respectively, of pathogen-related studies), followed by parasites (24.5%) and fungi (2%) (Fig. 1b). The most investigated pathogens were Toxoplasma gondii (20.4%), tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) (18.4%), West Nile virus (WNV) and related flaviviruses (16.3%), and the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC; 12.2%). A complete list of the specific pathogens and their frequencies of occurrence in the reviewed studies is provided in Supplementary material 1 - Table S1.

Regarding studies addressing environmental contamination, the majority focused on metals and metalloids (51.9%), including trace elements and heavy metals. Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) were investigated in 33.3% of studies, followed by organophosphate pesticides (OPs; 7.4%), and other contaminants such as asbestos (7.4%). Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and pyrethroids were each assessed in a single study (3.7%, respectively). A complete list of the specific elements and their frequencies of occurrence in the reviewed studies is provided in Supplementary Material 1 - Table S2.

Among AMR, the most frequently reported resistances were extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)- and AmpC β-lactamase-producing bacteria (72.7%), followed by resistance to tetracyclines (63.6%), sulphonamides, and quinolones (54.5% each).

Regarding the samples collected, blood was the most frequently collected one (41.9%), followed by liver (24.4%), muscle tissue (17.4%), kidney (16.3%), and faeces (11.6%; Supplementary material 1- Table S3). Lung and lymph nodes were also analyzed in 7% of the studies, with submandibular, bronchial, mediastinal and mesenteric lymph nodes being most frequently targeted.

Regarding the methodology employed for detecting different hazards and for risk assessment, immunological methods such as serology were the most frequent (44.2%), followed by chromatographic and spectrophotometric analyses (29,1%), microbiological techniques such as culture (18.6%), and molecular methods (11.6%). Necropsy, gross lesion assessment and histopathology were less frequently employed (5.8%).

Justification for wild boar as a sentinel species

A total of 86% (n = 74) of the reviewed studies mentioned the reasons for selecting wild boar as a sentinel for hazards relevant to ecosystem health. These reasons were grouped into five categories: (i) evidence of previous use as sentinels, (ii) epidemiological role, (iii) biological and physiological traits, (iv) distribution and ecological interface, and (v) exposure and detectability of health hazards. Each study provided one or more justifications for using wild boar as a sentinel species. Table 2 displays these categories, the underlying reasons, the opportunities identified for ecosystem health surveillance, and the frequency with which each reason was reported in the 74 studies. Their omnivorous and opportunistic diet (56.8%), previous use as a sentinel species (51.4%), their wide geographic distribution and ecological plasticity (47.3%), and their presence at the wildlife-livestock-human interface (32.4%) were the most frequently mentioned reasons. Limitations of wild boar as a sentinel species

A total of 12.8% (n = 11) of the reviewed studies reported limitations associated with the use of wild boar as a sentinel species for monitoring ecosystem health hazards (Table 3). The main constraints discussed in these studies included: (i) a potential sampling bias in the detection of ecosystem health threats due to the concentration of most or all samples during the hunting season (45.5%), (ii) limitations related to sample quality, particularly in those obtained from hunted animals or carcasses found in the field, as well as challenges associated with methodological complexity and the need for specialized equipment (45.5%), and (iii) the potential influence of ecological factors (e.g., home range size, feeding behaviour) and individual traits (e.g., sex, age, body condition, immune status) on exposure probability, pathogen detection, and the accuracy of risk estimation at the ecosystem level (54.5%). Perceived suitability and performance of wild boar as a sentinel species

Sixty-four out of 86 studies (74.4%) explicitly supported in their conclusions the use of wild boar as sentinels contributing to ecosystem health monitoring. Among them, one provided a dual assessment depending on the pathogen considered, supporting wild boar as sentinels for MTC but rejecting their suitability for the Mycobacterium avium complex [20].

Of these 64 studies, 53 classified their potential using qualitative terms. Nearly half of the studies (46.2%) considered wild boar a “functional/useful” sentinel. When combining the upper levels, 32.7% described it as “optimal” or “highly valuable/effective”, whereas 21.2% adopted a more cautious view, classifying it just as a “potential” sentinel. Regarding specific health hazards, the wild boar was consistently considered a reliable indicator for monitoring pathogens and environmental pollutants, with 81.3% and 80% of studies, respectively, placing them between “functional/useful” and “optimal” levels, and around 30% rating them as “highly valuable/highly effective” (Fig. 2c). In contrast, their perceived quality as a sentinel for AMR was more variable, with 33.3% of studies classifying their suitability at the lowest level of certainty (i.e., “potential”) (Fig. 2c).

The remaining 11 studies (17.2%) did not explicitly address the suitability of the wild boar as a sentinel species in their conclusions. Within this group, one study suggested that red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) could be better sentinels for Francisella tularensis [25].

Notably, none of the three studies on biological toxins used qualitative terms to describe the suitability of wild boar as sentinels for these hazards. However, one study highlighted their usefulness for identifying areas particularly vulnerable to ochratoxin A contamination [26].

Proposed improvements for wild boar sentinel surveillance

Almost half of the reviewed studies (40.7%) provided recommendations to enhance the use of wild boar as sentinels for ecosystem health surveillance. The most frequent proposals focused on methodological optimization, including systematic sampling (e.g., year-round surveys), regular active surveillance, and the establishment of serum banks from hunter-harvested animals to enable longitudinal and retrospective analyses (28.6%). Most studies also proposed large-scale and long-term surveys (17.1%). Several studies suggested multi-species surveillance approaches, combining wild boar data with data from other species to improve representativeness and reduce bias (14.3%). Similarly, 14.3% of studies underscored the necessity of a One Health multidisciplinary approach and interdisciplinary research. Recommendations also referred to incorporating wild boar into national action plans, surveillance programmes, and disease control strategies (14.3%).

Some studies highlighted the importance of accounting for sources of uncertainty in analyses and interpretations, including ecological (e.g., diet or spatial range; 8.6%), population-related (e.g., geographical location or population density, 8.6%), and individual factors (e.g., age; 2.9%), as well as sample quality (5.7%).

Targeted sampling strategies, such as promoting collaboration between hunters and veterinarians (5.7%) or prioritizing juvenile individuals to capture recent viral circulation (2.9%) were less frequently reported.

As individual studies frequently proposed more than one recommendation, these categories were not mutually exclusive, and percentages may therefore exceed 100%.

Discussion

Despite possessing traits that make it accessible and potentially effective for ecosystem health monitoring, the explicit framing of wild boar as a sentinel species remains limited, with only 86 studies identified in this review. Although this number is likely an underestimation due to the search approach, this review should be interpreted as a synthesis of studies explicitly framing wild boar as a sentinel species rather than as a comprehensive review of all wild boar surveillance research. Notably, these studies covered 30 different pathogens, 44 environmental contaminants, two biological toxins, and 13 AMR indicators, highlighting the wide range of health threats that can be monitored through wild boar surveillance. Importantly, most studies addressed specific health hazards within a One Health framework rather than undertaking integrated ecosystem health assessments.

General results

The recognition of wild boar as a sentinel species has remained low, showing only a slight increase between 2010 and 2022, which does not concur with the exponential growth in publications on wild boar in veterinary and related disciplines. This suggests that, although the species is increasingly studied from ecological and epidemiological perspectives, its role is not always explicitly framed as “sentinel use” in the literature, even when its data could be directly applicable to monitoring ecosystem health hazards.

The geographical distribution of studies was strongly biased towards Europe, which is likely driven by high wild boar population densities, long-standing ecological presence, and hunting frameworks that facilitate sampling [18]. Thus, these studies reflect regional sampling availability rather than a global assessment of the wild boar's full sentinel potential. Although also native to Asia and northern Africa and widely introduced in other continents, its potential as a sentinel species remains comparatively underexplored in these regions [27].

Health hazards, sample types and analytical methods

The results suggest that the use of the wild boar as a sentinel species was driven by opportunistic sampling and active surveillance, with almost 91% of samples coming from hunting or population control activities, which reflects the dependence of current sentinel surveillance on existing hunting frameworks rather than on surveillance systems specifically designed for this purpose. This provides high logistical efficiency and low sampling costs, allowing access to larger sample sizes than those obtained from non-game wildlife [21], [28], [29]. However, it may also introduce some biases, as discussed in the limitations section. The low proportion of live-captured, road-killed and found-dead individuals may be associated with the higher cost and logistical complexity of capturing live individuals (e.g., personnel, anaesthesia, equipment, etc), as well as the limited implementation of passive surveillance in some wildlife health monitoring systems [6], [14]. In addition, the limited use of non-invasive methods suggests an opportunity to expand spatiotemporal coverage of research and reduce hunting-related biases [30].

Regarding health hazards, the focus on pathogens may be related to the established role of wild boar as a reservoir or amplifying host for many of them [19], [31]. The higher attention given to T. gondii, vector-borne viruses (TBEV and WNV), and the MTC may be associated with their importance to human and animal health and their suitability for detection in this species [20], [21], [32]. In this regard, intrinsic traits of wild boar (e.g., omnivorous diet, scavenging behaviour, and frequent use of peri-urban and agricultural landscapes) increase its probability of exposure to these pathogens (e.g., the frequent use of contaminated environments relevant for MTC transmission (e.g., waterholes) [33], [34], [35]). In addition, the wild boar tends to develop detectable antibody responses against these pathogens that persist over time [20], [21], [36].

Despite covering a broad diversity of pathogens, the reviewed studies did not include some major diseases affecting wild boar, such as African swine fever virus (ASFV) and Trichinella spp. [37]. Although studies on both diseases were retrieved during the initial search, none met the inclusion criteria because they did not explicitly describe or evaluate wild boar as a sentinel or indicator species. Instead, most ASFV studies focused on experimental infections [38], epidemiological modelling [39], or control. On the other hand, research on Trichinella focused on estimating prevalence or food safety risks or included other wildlife species as sentinel hosts (e.g., hunting dogs or wolves) [40], [41]. This suggests that the potential contribution of wild boar as a sentinel species for these important health hazards remains largely unexplored and deserves further investigation.

The use of wild boar as a sentinel has been dominated by the monitoring of zoonotic pathogens (97.9%) rather than by an integrated assessment of ecosystem health, which encompasses the interconnected health of humans, animals, and the environment [42], [43]. This is evidenced by the lower frequency of studies addressing environmental contaminants or AMR, despite the well-known potential of wild boar as sentinels for these hazards [44], [45], [46]. Nevertheless, AMR represents a recent and emerging concern, which may explain its limited (12.8%) but increasing incorporation into wild boar surveillance programs.

Regarding the analytical methods, blood (or blood serum) was the most frequently used sample, likely because it is easy to collect and does not necessarily require opening the animal, reducing time and equipment needs [47]. Accordingly, serological methods were the most applied ones, as most studies focused on pathogens. This suggests a tendency to assess exposure rather than active infection, since the primary objective of sentinel surveillance is often to evaluate pathogen circulation in the host community, i.e., the risk of exposure for other species, including domestic animals and humans, rather than to characterise pathogen dynamics in detail [10]. This is further supported by the limited use of tissues for bacteriological, molecular or pathological methods in the studies reviewed.

Justification for defining the wild boar as a sentinel species

The most frequently reported reasons for selecting wild boar as a sentinel species, beyond prior evidence, were their wide geographic distribution and ecological plasticity, their presence at the wildlife-livestock-human interface, their opportunistic omnivorous and scavenging diet, and their status as a game species.

Wild boar occupy multiple habitat types, from natural ecosystems to anthropogenic areas such as agricultural, urban, and peri-urban environments. This increases their opportunities for interaction with other wild and domestic species, as well as humans, both directly through spatial overlap and indirectly via vectors and the environment, including garbage or sewage [45], [48], [49]. This also increases their likelihood of exposure to multiple health hazards. Their feeding behaviour also enhances exposure, as they consume a wide variety of food sources, including plants, invertebrates, vertebrates, eggs, and carrion, and may also feed on waste in anthropogenic settings [50]. This facilitates contact with multiple pathogens, including bacteria and viruses that can be transmitted through scavenging (e.g. WNV, MTC, or Salmonella spp.) [51], [52], [53], as well as parasites such as T. gondii through ingestion of oocysts shed by felids or present in infected tissues [54]. In addition, their foraging behaviour and physiology favour the accumulation of environmental contaminants in tissues such as fat, liver and kidney [55], [56].

Finally, it is a species often hunted recreationally or removed as part of management programs. This allows access to large sample numbers and volumes, as well as to a wide variety of sample types at relatively low cost [13], [28]. In contrast, smaller species such as rodents and some carnivores typically yield limited serum volumes, and the collection of certain tissues (e.g., lymph nodes in rodents) can be technically challenging. Similarly, more labour-intensive sampling methods, such as the live capture of other wildlife species (e.g., wild birds for Flavivirus surveillance), further constrain large-scale sampling. Thus, wild boar represents a logistically efficient option for large-scale surveillance.

Synthesis of wild boar sentinel performance and future perspectives

The use of the wild boar as a sentinel involves some limitations. First, wild boar sampling relies largely on hunting activities, so sample availability depends on hunting seasons, strategies, and local regulations, as well as the characteristics of harvested animals (e.g., age, sex, and health status). This may lead to biased or incomplete assessments of ecosystem health hazards [57], [58].

Although hunting seasons have increasingly been extended in many regions as a management tool for population and disease control (e.g., ASFV), sampling effort remains strongly concentrated in autumn and winter due to hunting traditions, particularly in southern Europe [59]. Seasonal concentration of sampling in autumn and winter can overlook temporal variation in exposure to certain health hazards, such as vector-borne viruses (e.g., WNV, TBEV, Usutu virus), whose circulation may be underestimated when sampling occurs after peak transmission periods [60]. Furthermore, the occurrence of ESBL/AmpC E. coli isolates can be influenced by climatic factors such as temperature and precipitation [61]. Seasonal changes in body condition (due to food availability) and population structure may also affect pathogen detection and the bioaccumulation of certain contaminants, such as persistent organic pollutants and heavy metals [58], [62]. To address this issue, some studies recommended year-round surveys and regular active surveillance [56], [60], [63]. In addition, hunting strategies may influence sample distribution, as autumn-winter drive hunts typically target entire groups, whereas summer hunts tend to focus on individual animals [61]. Sampling bias may also arise from differences in sex or age representation, e.g., live-capture studies may overrepresent females, while hunting often targets males, potentially affecting exposure estimates [64], [65].

Second, field conditions (e.g., higher temperatures) and post-mortem delays can lead to sample degradation in hunted or found-dead animals. For example, post-mortem blood collection often leads to haemolysis, which may interfere with diagnostic tests and increase the likelihood of false positives, as reported for WNV [57], [60], whereas for other pathogens such as Aujeszky's disease virus, little to no effect has been observed [66]. Similar issues occur in road-killed or found-dead animals, where carcass condition and the time elapsed between death and sampling may limit sample availability and lead to degradation [67]. In this regard, the reviewed studies recommended (i) accounting for sample quality factors (e.g., haemolysis or post-mortem delay) in data analysis to improve interpretation [60], and (ii) strengthening collaboration between veterinarians and hunters to support disease surveillance and monitoring, facilitating carcass detection in the field and reducing the time between death and sampling [68].

In addition, ecological and individual factors, including diet, trophic level, sex, age, and home range size, can influence exposure to health hazards and affect the estimated risks [69], [70], [71]. For example, as an omnivorous species with a relatively high trophic level, wild boar may be less representative for bioaccumulation than herbivores (e.g., PCBs or OPs) [70]. In addition, sex-related differences in ecology, such as larger home ranges in males, may lead to differential exposure to certain hazards, as reported for T. gondii and persistent organic pollutants [58], [64]. To reduce these effects, some studies proposed (i) multi-species surveillance approaches, combining wild boar data with that from other species [64], [70], [72], [73], (ii) prioritizing specific groups (e.g., juveniles) to assess the recent circulation of certain pathogens, such as WNV [60], and (iii) incorporating individual, ecological and population-related variables into analyses [61], [62], [69].

Finally, studies highlighted the need to adopt multidisciplinary One Health strategies and incorporate wild boar into monitoring programs, which can improve both the interpretation and the applicability of findings from wild boar sentinel surveillance [56], [74], [75]. This highlights that ecosystem health should be understood as the result of interconnected human, animal, and environmental components rather than isolated domains [42], [43].

Overall, the wild boar is widely considered an effective sentinel, and there is consensus on its practical value, as 78.9% of studies perceived its potential as between useful and optimal. However, its performance appears to depend on the specific hazard monitored, suggesting a context-dependent application. Its usefulness was particularly highlighted for monitoring pathogens and environmental contaminants, with most studies rating its performance as “functional/useful” to “optimal” (81.3% and 80%, respectively). However, its evaluation for AMR was more variable and often lower, suggesting that its suitability in this field is less established, possibly due to its more recent incorporation into wildlife surveillance and the need for complementary data [61].

Conclusions

Despite the extensive literature on wild boar in a One Health context, its role as a sentinel species is not always explicitly recognized. Thus, future research should more clearly frame its value for monitoring ecosystem health hazards. Among the studies that addressed this role, wild boar sentinel surveillance is considered logistically efficient. However, sometimes, its representativeness may be limited, as it usually relies on human activities (i.e., hunting), which may influence data interpretation. Moreover, the current evidence is largely based on native European populations and may reflect not only the suitability of wild boar as a sentinel species, but also the availability of well-established hunting-based sampling programs in Europe.

Although the usefulness of wild boar as a sentinel species depends on the health hazard, ecological context, and surveillance framework, most studies included in this review considered it a valuable sentinel species. Accordingly, it should be regarded as a valuable component of broader, context-dependent One Health surveillance systems, particularly when integrated with information from other sentinel species. As ecological context, population management, and epidemiological dynamics may differ between native and introduced or feral populations, further studies are needed before these findings can be extrapolated to other settings. To improve its value as a sentinel, future approaches should incorporate alternative sampling sources beyond hunted animals (including live-captured, found-dead, road-killed, and non-invasive), develop systematic year-round sampling strategies, account for individual, ecological, and sample quality factors in data analyses, and incorporate data from other species, when necessary, to reduce bias and enhance the accuracy of ecosystem health risk assessments.
