Medial prefrontal cortex activity precedes dorsomedial striatum in need for change during history-based flexible behavior
Authors: Áron Kőszeghy, Wei Xu, Mingshan Liu, Peiheng Lu, Long Wan, Tong Deng, Sungmin Kang, Peggy Seriès, Jian Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBeing cognitively flexible is vital for survival, and its impairment is central to many psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders. Both the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and dorsomedial striatum (DMS) are essential for cognitive flexibility, but their specific roles—particularly which region first detects and encodes environmental changes—remain debated. Using head-restrained mice in a choice task based on action–outcome history, we show that many mPFC and DMS neurons represent the difference in reward history between options, a decision variable guiding advantageous choices. Crucially, mPFC encodes this variable earlier and more rapidly than DMS. Temporally targeted optogenetic interference with mPFC representation impairs decision-making. Moreover, functional connectivity between mPFC and DMS strengthens as the overall reward proportion declines. Our results support a framework in which the prefrontal cortex acts as the initial detector of inferred environmental changes, enhancing communication with the striatum to promote adaptive and flexible behavior.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Plant biology

Introduction

The ability to adapt one’s actions and choices to mitigate risks and optimize rewards in response to changes in the environment plays a crucial role in survival. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is a critical brain region for flexible decision making1,2,3,4,5 by monitoring behavioral performance and executing top-down control.6,7,8,9 However, PFC does not operate in isolation. Notably, the striatum, which serves as the input nucleus of the basal ganglia (BG), plays a crucial role in enabling all forms of flexible goal-directed behavior.10,11 More specifically, the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and dorsomedial striatum (DMS) are both critical in action-outcome based flexible behaviors, where the choice of action is based on the evaluation of outcomes from previous actions on a trial-by-trial basis.2,10,11,12,13,14

In the context of cognitive flexibility, mPFC and DMS have been typically studied in isolation for their respective roles, using distinct experimental preparations and behavioral tasks. In recent studies, for instance, the mPFC persistently stores value-based decision variables that are key to predicting the animal’s choice in an action value-based decision task,15 and specialized neurons in the mPFC are shown to evaluate choice outcomes during a gambling task.16 In comparison, DMS is shown to represent action sequence and value in a freely moving outcome-history based decision-making task,17 and DMS inactivation impairs action selection during multiple forms of risk/reward decision making.18 Technically, typical approaches used for in vivo electrophysiological recordings, e.g., tetrode and microdrive, have limited the interrogation to a single brain region in a behavioral design.

Although evidence for the respective roles of mPFC and DMS is emerging, how mPFC and DMS coordinate at the level of neural dynamics to enable flexible behavior during context switches remains poorly understood. Two competing hypotheses propose that PFC and striatum detect and represent changes in environmental context differentially, leading to opposing predictions about their temporal dynamics during behavioral flexibility. The first hypothesis suggests that the striatum—particularly the dorsomedial striatum (DMS)—acts as a “context detector,” playing a central role in learning about environmental changes. This idea is grounded in the striatum’s anatomical position as a hub connecting neocortical and subcortical structures, and in the high convergence of inputs onto medium spiny neurons, which are well-suited for reinforcement learning-related plasticity.19,20 According to this model, striatal activity should precede PFC activity during context switches, implying that the PFC is “informed” by the striatum. In contrast, the second hypothesis posits that the mPFC is responsible for detecting changes in the environment, while the striatum selects among multiple learned responses.21,22 This view predicts that mPFC activity should precede striatal activity during flexible behavior.

Several limitations in earlier studies may account for this ongoing debate: (1) the PFC and striatum were often studied in isolation; (2) behavioral paradigms rarely allowed for the monitoring of neural dynamics across multiple context switches within a single session, limiting the examination of real-time synaptic plasticity; and (3) non-human primate studies lacked the temporal precision and projection-specific interventions needed to probe circuit interactions causally.

Recent evidence, however, suggests a more delicate interaction. Based on non-human primate results,23 the striatum may represent associative information early in learning, while the PFC becomes more dominant when cognitive demands increase, such as during rule learning, categorization, or the formation of internal models.24 Building on this idea, we hypothesized that the mPFC would track context changes earlier than DMS during a probabilistic history-based decision-making task. Furthermore, we examined whether there were functional signatures indicating a directional influence from the mPFC to DMS.

Results

Dynamic foraging in head-restrained mice enables the study of prefronto-striatal correlates of action-outcome history-based flexible behavior

To overcome previous experimental limitations, we first developed a steering wheel choice device-based25 probabilistic dynamic foraging paradigm15 in head-restrained mice (see also STAR Methods). This task is based on an action-outcome history. No sensory cue instructs responses for the favorable option. In this task, mice were given two alternative options (moving the wheel left or right) to obtain a drop of liquid reward (Figure 1A). The temporal structure of a trial is illustrated in Figure 1B (Top). In a single trial, the mean wheel angular speed, which reflects the animal turning the wheel, and the distribution of the timing of reward delivery for a trial are plotted in Figure 1B (Bottom, calculated from all sessions). The time of reward delivery peaked around 0.36 s after trial initiation (at t = 0). The probabilities of reward for turning the wheel to the left or right were not equal, with one side always having a much higher reward probability than the other. The mean number of trials per behavioral session was 396.2 ± 90.3 trials, and the mean response time was 0.92 ± 0.02 s (median: 0.39 s). A representative session is shown in Figure 1C. The two sides’ reward probabilities stayed constant for a variable number of trials, which we will refer to as a block of trials (mean block duration = 116.2 ± 52.3 trials, 5 animals, 29 sessions). After the end of each block, the high and low probability sides were reversed, unannounced to the animal. This reversal occurs several times in a session (mean number of blocks per session = 3.8 ± 1.1, 5 animals, 29 sessions).

To measure the reward history, and therefore a surrogate of the animal’s perception of current reward probability based on past events, we calculated for each side the proportion of rewards received in the last ten trials for responses to that side (perceived/prior reward probability, PRP, see STAR Methods). The PRP values from the same example session are plotted (Figure 1C, bottom upper). PRP may reflect the animal’s ongoing estimate of the likelihood that a specific choice will lead to reward, based on recent outcomes. As a measure of the perceived reward probability difference (PRPD) between the two sides, which might capture the animal’s relative preference and expected value computation, and therefore a gauge of the animal’s internal drive toward the advantageous side, we subtracted the PRP of the ipsilateral side from the contralateral side (relative to the hemisphere being recorded, see STAR Methods) – example plotted in Figure 1C (bottom lower, black trace), showing this metric to modulate similarly with response side choice (Figure 1C, middle, blue trace). We also quantitatively described mice’s foraging behavior in our task using a generative reinforcement learning model (Q-learning) (Table S6, details in STAR Methods),26,27 which has been used in similar dynamic foraging paradigms from earlier studies.15,16 Consistent with these studies, we find that the animal’s choices in our task strongly overlap with the prediction from this Q-learning model (Figure 1C, bottom, magenta trace). In addition, the calculated relative values (ΔQ, the difference between the left and right action values, Qleft − Qright), which have been shown to bias the animal’s choice previously,15 overlap tightly with the PRPD values in our task (see an example session in Figure 1C, bottom panel), suggesting PRPD is a simplified yet reliable quantitative measure of reward history of the animal and can be used as a proxy of relative value (ΔQ) (Figure S4).

In our task, on average, animals chose the high-probability side significantly more often than the low-probability side (Figure 1E, left, p < 0.001, F(1,28) = 34.36, linear mixed-effects model (LMM), on the effect of low/high probability side). It took several trials after a probability switch (block transition) for the animal to discover the new advantageous side and adapt their strategy to favor the other direction; therefore, the proportion of responses to a given side lags the changes in set probability for that side, as shown by their averaged cross-correlogram (Figure 1E, right). In order to chart how quickly an animal adapts to new reward rules after a switch, we looked at task performance before and after block transitions. The overall proportion of rewarded trials and advantageous responses is aligned by switch points (from all trials in all sessions, averaged over trials for each trial) (Figure 1D). Both parameters drop sharply after the switch and only start to increase when the animal starts to adapt to the new advantageous strategy. On average, animals learned the change with newly adapted behavior plateaus around 30 trials post-switch. The time constants for the proportion of rewarded trials and the proportion of advantageous responses (therefore, the speed of behavioral adaptation to the changed reward probabilities) were 19.7 and 17.2 trials, respectively.

By using two 128-channel silicon probes, we recorded single units from 5 mice for a total of 29 sessions from mPFC and the ipsilateral DMS simultaneously during the dynamic foraging task. The median coordinates of all 29 sessions were visualized in 3D (Figures S1A–S1C) and projected in 2D (Figures S1E and S1H). Post-hoc histology analysis confirmed the locations of the recording electrodes (Figures S1D and S1G). In total, following clustering, 1668 neurons from mPFC and 974 neurons from DMS were recorded during the performance of the task. A summary of behavioral information for each animal is provided in Table S1.

Medial prefrontal cortex and dorsomedial striatum neurons encode the perceived reward probability difference, which explains animal’s choice

To explore the possibility that principal cells and interneurons in the mPFC and DMS are differentially modulated by the reward history, as measured by PRPD in our probabilistic dynamic foraging task, we first classified single units into subpopulations using established methods28,29,30 (Figure S2). From a total of 1668 single units in the mPFC, we identified 986 putative pyramidal neurons and 382 putative interneurons, leaving 74 unclassified. 226 mPFC units were not used for classification (see STAR Methods). From a total of 974 single units in the DMS, we identified 692 putative medium spiny neurons, 21 putative fast-spiking interneurons, 66 putative unidentified interneurons, and 43 putative tonically active neurons, leaving 40 units unclassified. 112 DMS units were not used for classification (see STAR Methods). A summary of single units and cell-type classification across animals is provided in Table S2.

Neural activities at different epochs of a task trial might play distinct roles in flexible decision-making. For instance, neural activities during the intertrial interval (ITI) period are generally more sustained and may involve anticipation and action planning,31 strategy maintenance and updating,15 as well as the integration of recent feedback.32 In contrast, neural activities during the response time window (RTW) period are often phasic and event-locked, and could represent, e.g., current choice and behavioral response that guide action selection,32 as well as outcome value.16 To explore potential planning and decision updating signals that are more likely observable across multiple trials in our dynamic foraging task and to avoid confounding internally driven representations with motor execution signals, we analyzed neural activities during ITIs and RTWs separately across trials.

Previous work has shown that a large number of mPFC neurons modulate with the relative value (Q) during both ITI and RTW periods.15 In agreement with this finding, Figure 2A shows raster and firing rate plots of a putative pyramidal neuron in the mPFC, aligned by the trial-initiation signal. When reward probability was high on the left and low on the right, and the PRPD was positive, its firing rate in both RTW and ITI was high (and vice versa). Surprisingly, putative interneurons in the mPFC were similarly modulated by the PRPD as pyramidal cells. Figure 2B shows an example interneuron recorded in the same session, whose firing rate in both RTW and ITI was high when PRPD was positive (and vice versa). We also encountered pyramidal neurons and interneurons in the mPFC that showed an inhibitory response during each trial. The change in their firing rates can also be modulated with PRPD. Overall, in the mPFC, both pyramidal neurons and interneurons during each trial could be positively or negatively correlated with PRPD (Table S4). Approximately half of each subpopulation exhibited modulation related to PRPD, either during ITIs, RTWs, or both (Figure 2C). There was no significant difference between the proportions of neurons that were PRPD-modulated or non-modulated across subtypes (Figure 2C; Tables S3 and S7, proportions calculated for each session, LMM on the effect of cell subtypes, all p > 0.05).

Next, we examined neural activities in the DMS. Interestingly, we found that the PRPD was also represented in the DMS. In addition, DMS interneurons (including fast-spiking interneurons and unidentified interneurons) were similarly modulated by the PRPD as medium spiny cells. Figures 2D and 2E show a raster and firing rate plots of a putative medium spiny neuron and a putative fast-spiking interneuron, respectively, in the same session. Their firing rates during ITIs and RTWs were negatively modulated by PRPD (and vice versa). Again, we found that in the DMS, the spike response magnitude of both medium spiny neurons and interneurons during each trial can be positively or negatively correlated with PRPD (Table S4). Slightly more than half of each subpopulation exhibited modulation related to PRPD, either during ITIs, RTWs, or both (Figure 2F). There was no significant difference between the proportions of neurons that were PRPD-modulated or non-modulated across neuronal subtypes (Figure 2F; Tables S3 and S7, proportions calculated for each session, LMM on the effect of cell subtypes, all p > 0.05).

Medial prefrontal cortex spike modulation leads dorsomedial striatum spike modulation in response to changing reward probability difference during a dynamic foraging task

Considering PRPD is represented in both mPFC and DMS, given the strong anatomical connections between these two structures, we asked how mPFC neurons communicate with their DMS counterparts. To this end, we examined the temporal relationships between pairs of simultaneously recorded mPFC and DMS neurons over the course of the entire task session to gauge the inter-brain region dynamics of spike modulation during the foraging behavior. We first low-pass filtered the trial-wise intertrial firing rates (background firing) and the firing rate in the RTW (response magnitude) over the entire session (below 20 times the fundamental frequency of the session - see STAR Methods) and examined the phase difference between mPFC and DMS trial-wise spiking modulation. Figures 3A and 3B show an example session with four switches and the trial-wise response magnitudes for an example mPFC-DMS cell pair over the entire session. While the response magnitudes fluctuate from trial to trial, there is a clear, slow, and large fluctuation in both cells’ responses that mirrors the PRPD plotted in panel A. The response magnitude traces were low-pass filtered, and their phases were calculated via a Hilbert transform (Figure 3B, middle and bottom panels), showing a non-synchronous phase between the mPFC and DMS neurons. The circular-mean phase differences between each mPFC-DMS neuron pair were calculated for all sessions, and their distribution was plotted in Figure 3C (top) for both response magnitudes and inter-trial firings.

(B) Top: Example mPFC and DMS spiking response magnitude over the course of the same session. Middle: Low-pass filtered and demeaned spiking response magnitude. Bottom: Trial-wise phases of the low-pass filtered spiking magnitude.

A mPFC neuron and a DMS neuron that are both positively or both negatively correlated with PRPD will have a phase difference close to 0, whereas if one is positively correlated and the other is negatively correlated, then they will have a phase difference close to ±π. Since we have shown that roughly equal numbers of neurons are positively and negatively correlated with PRPD (Figure 2), one would assume that pairwise mPFC-DMS phase differences would fall into a bimodal and symmetrical distribution with modes around 0 and ±π (i.e., roughly equal numbers of phase difference values between -π and 0 radians and between 0 and π radians). In reality, there are significantly more pairs with phase differences between 0 and π radians than between -π and 0 radians (p = 0.0059, t(28.0) = 2.98 for response magnitude and p = 0.012, t(28.0) = 2.70 for intertrial firing rate, LMM on the effect of phase type; numbers of pairs calculated for each session), giving an asymmetrical appearance to the phase-difference distribution (Figure 3C). This signifies an excess of cell pairs with a prefrontal lead, suggesting that overall mPFC neurons change their firing rates in response to the change in PRPD earlier than DMS neurons.

To test whether the degree of mPFC lead in PRPD-modulated firing rate change is correlated with animals’ performance in the task, we first gauged how well the animal followed the rules of the task for each session by calculating how well the response proportion to a given side followed the set probability for that side using a cross-correlation (normalized to give a unitary autocorrelation). The cross-correlation for left and right is averaged for each session, and the peak value is used as a measure of the animal’s performance. Sessions were then separated according to whether their values were above or below average (mean: 0.81±0.01, see Table S6). The histograms of the mPFC-DMS phase differences are plotted in Figure 3D for above- and below-average sessions (blue and orange). Above average sessions (good performance) have clear peaks around 0 radians (signifying prefrontal-striatal synchrony) as well as significantly more mPFC-DMS cell pairs with prefrontal lead (positive values in its phase difference), giving an asymmetrical phase distribution (significantly more phase difference values between 0 and π radians than between -π and 0 radians, p = 0.0089, t(16) = 2.98, for response magnitude and p = 0.0090, t(16) = 2.97, for intertrial firing rate, LMM on the effect of phase type; numbers of pairs calculated for each session). In contrast, below-average sessions (bad performance) have a largely symmetrical phase distribution without significantly more or fewer pairs showing mPFC lead or lag (p = 0.34, t(11) = 1.00, for response magnitude and p = 0.65, t(11) = 0.47, for intertrial firing rate).

We also take the mPFC and DMS neurons whose trial-wise spiking phases are significantly correlated with the phases of the PRPD (after similar filtering and Hilbert transform and calculate circular-to-circular correlation coefficients). For response magnitudes: 1346/1668 mPFC and 810/974 DMS neurons are significantly correlated; for intertrial firing: 1340/1668 mPFC and 802/974 DMS neurons significantly correlated. We examined a possible phase difference between the PRPD and trial-wise spiking phases of these neurons in the mPFC and DMS, respectively. The distributions of their phase differences are shown in Figure 3E. The mPFC neurons’ phases are not significantly different from the PRPD phases (symmetric distribution of phase difference values with peak at 0), but DMS neurons show an overall lag (more positive values of phase difference, p < 0.05, circular m-test of PRPD-neuron phase differences) – indicating that the majority of DMS neurons lag behind PRPD changes in their firing rate changes. Again, this agrees with an mPFC led over DMS in firing rate modulation in the task.

Putative monosynaptic pairs between the medial prefrontal cortex and dorso-medial striatum convey perceived reward probability difference information

Given strong unidirectional excitatory connections between mPFC and DMS,33,34,35 and the fact that a large proportion of neurons in the mPFC and DMS represent PRPD during dynamic foraging (Figure 2), we examined the possibility of locating these representations at the level of monosynaptic mPFC-DMS pairs. To this end, putative monosynaptic excitatory (PMSE) pairs were sought from all possible mPFC and DMS pairs (Figure 4A). PMSE pairs were identified based on well-established methods with minor modifications.36,37 The criterion is based on a strongly increased likelihood of spiking of the postsynaptic pair member with minimal jitter within a narrow time window (monosynaptic delay) after the presynaptic member’s spikes (see STAR Methods for details). Out of all 62774 possible mPFC-DMS pairs, 590 PMSE pairs were identified. In roughly one-third of the PMSE pairs, at least one member was related to PRPD, whereas in roughly one-fifth of them, both members encoded this decision variable (Figure 4B). These results agree with the notion of mPFC-to-DMS transfer of PRPD information.

Magenta and green lines on the cross-correlograms indicate the expected number of spikes and detection threshold (expected+3SD), respectively, based on shuffling postsynaptic spike times (see STAR Methods for more details).

Lack of reward strengthens communication between the medial prefrontal cortex and dorso-medial striatum during foraging

After elucidating the temporal relationship between the mPFC and DMS neural dynamics during foraging, we examined whether the strength of inter-brain region information transfer, e.g., to convey the decision signal PRPD, is modulated when the animal encounters a constant change of reward experience in the task. To test this, we calculated pairwise spike-to-spike cross-correlations between mPFC and DMS neurons in the inter-trial periods (in order to avoid the effects of task-related co-modulations), normalized to negate the effects of changes in firing frequencies.38 We use the absolute maximal deviation of spike-to-spike cross-correlation from zero to measure the strength of the interconnectivity between a given mPFC-DMS cell pair. One example of this measure across an entire session (for inter-trial interval spikes in a moving centered 20-trial long window) is plotted along with prior reward proportion, PRP, concomitantly in Figure 5A. This example shows a significant negative correlation between interconnectivity strength and PRP (Figure 5B). Overall, a strong significant number of pairs have a negative correlation between interconnectivity strength and PRP (Figure 5E, p < 0.001, F(1,28) = 67.159, LMM on the effect of cell pair correlation type), and the averaged cross-correlation across all pairs and all sessions was negative (Figure 5F) – indicating that periods of low reward experience lead to a strengthening of spike-to-spike transmission. Consistent with this interpretation, the cell-to-cell interconnectivity is significantly higher in non-rewarded trials than in rewarded trials across all pairs and all sessions (Figure 5G, p < 0.001, t(23840000) = −156.56, LMM on the effect of reward type). In addition, cell-to-cell interconnectivity is also significantly increased in trials during block transitions than in trials during block plateaus across all pairs and all sessions (Figure 5H, p < 0.001, t(7150000) = 82.38, LMM on the effect of trial type).

We also sought to examine the strength of any directly connected mPFC-DMS neuronal pairs.34 To this end, we applied the above analysis to previously identified PMSE pairs between the mPFC and DMS, as shown in Figure 4. An example from such PMSE pairs is plotted in Figures 5C and 5D. In agreement with findings from all possible pairs, the spike-to-spike transmission fidelity between mPFC and DMS at the monosynaptic level was also enhanced when the animal experienced overall low reward proportions (Figures 5I–5K: p = 0.0014, F(1,28) = 12.652, LMM on the effect of cell pair correlation type; Figure 5K: p < 0.001, t(225610.1) = −26.76, LMM on the effect of reward type; Figure 5L: p < 0.001, t(68206.5) = 9.63, LMM on the effect of trial type.) Since PRP is a sensible measure of the need for behavioral change to an alternative advantageous strategy in our paradigm, these findings indicate that mPFC exerts enhanced influence over DMS when top-down control is beneficial.

mPFC and DMS spiking differentially represent overall reward history information

As the history of prior rewards is a key parameter in this behavioral paradigm that influences the animals' choice toward an optimal strategy, it is crucial to examine where and how this information is encoded in the PFC-DMS system. To this end, trials were first separated into 3 categories: S - those with trial initiation signals only (i.e., no subsequent response from the animal, no supra-threshold wheel movement), SM - those with signal and wheel movement but no reward, and SMR - those with signal, wheel movement, and reward. We examined how the spiking profiles in trials were modulated by the three task modalities, namely trial initiation signal, wheel movement, and reward, respectively (Figures 6A and 6B). These three task modality-associated firing modulations were then averaged across all neurons for each brain region, scaled to have amplitudes of one, and used as regressors in a multiple linear regression fit to assess the spiking modulation of each neuron (also scaled to give unitary amplitudes) (Figures S3A and S3B). The values of the fitted regression coefficients for each regressor were used to gauge the salience of representation for each task modality in the spiking modulation for a particular neuron and under a particular context. We further verified the validity of this approach (Figures S3C and S3D, details in STAR Methods).

Using this approach, first, for each rewarded trial (i.e., SMR trial), we calculated the proportion of rewarded trials in the prior 10 trials (regardless of the chosen side) and separated them into prior reward proportion (PRP) above 0.5 (High PRP) and PRP below 0.5 (Low PRP). Second, we gauged whether the modality-specific regression coefficients were influenced by PRP, therefore reward history information. For mPFC neurons, the signal-related regression coefficients between high and low PRPs were similar (Figure 6C, left, p = 0.43, t(757.4) = −0.79, LMM on the effect of PRP). The wheel movement-related coefficients were also unchanged (Figure 6C, Right, p = 0.51, t(758.5) = −0.66, LMM on the effect of PRP). For DMS neurons, both signal and wheel movement-related coefficients were unchanged (Figure 6D, left, p = 0.62, t(470.7) = 0.49, LMM on the effect of PRP; right, p = 0.41, t(464.4) = 0.83, LMM on the effect of PRP). However, when the fitted reward-related coefficient was examined in the mPFC and DMS, LMM showed a significant effect of PRP and brain region (PRP: p < 0.001, F(1,1260.4) = 28.04; brain region: p < 0.001, F(1,1267.8) = 29.33; the interaction between PRP and brain region: p = 0.35, F(1,1260.4) = 0.89). Furthermore, the reward-related regression coefficient was profoundly smaller for low prior reward trials in the mPFC (Figure 6E, filled columns, p < 0.001, t(1266) = 4.99, Post hoc comparisons with Tukey’s adjustment). Interestingly, the reward-related regression coefficient was just modestly smaller for low prior reward trials in the DMS (Figure 6E, blank columns, p = 0.028, t(1266) = 2.79, post hoc comparisons with Tukey’s adjustment). These results suggest that although both mPFC and DMS spiking profiles represent rewards delivered in the current trial as well as rewards delivered in previous trials, a “working memory” of reward history that the animal has experienced, mPFC spiking seems much more sensitive to prior reward experience.

Temporally targeted optogenetic interference with perceived reward probability difference-modulated firings in the medial prefrontal cortex impairs behavioral adaptation in foraging

Finally, given the decision variable, PRPD is represented in both mPFC and DMS, and the “mPFC lead, DMS lag” temporal structure of neural activities in representing PRPD is observed. We set out to test the necessity of the mPFC-to-DMS projection and this temporal structure in transmitting the information of advantageous choices across brain regions in dynamic foraging. To this end, we selectively expressed channelrhodopsin 2 (ChR2) in DMS-projecting mPFC neurons to enable temporally controlled projection-specific perturbation (Figure 7A, see also Table S5). We reason that since we have shown that neurons are both positively and negatively correlated with PRPD (Figure 2; Table S4), for a trial with a positive PRPD value, only those mPFC cells that are positively modulated by PRPD will fire strongly, and vice versa for a trial with a negative PRPD value. Therefore, in each trial, only a “selective population” of PRPD-modulated mPFC cells will fire to inform the advantageous choice. If this is the case, the interference with this population selectivity, specifically with its timing of firing in mPFC in each trial, may impair the animal’s choice behavior.

To test this hypothesis, we stimulated DMS-projecting mPFC neurons transiently only during ITIs or RTWs, respectively, by blue light (473 nm) from the 2nd block transition when a well-trained animal had successfully made one successful switch in a foraging session (Figure 7B). The light was delivered only to those trials within 2 min following the block transition, during which a well-trained animal could confidently adapt. In contrast, the delivery of yellow light (589 nm), which does not activate ChR2, as a negative control, to the mPFC during ITIs (3.96 ± 0.13 s) did not affect behavior in the same animals in different foraging sessions, measured by response time (Figure 7C left, yellow, 0.66 ± 0.09 s to 1.01 ± 0.15 s, 21 sessions, 3 animals, p = 0.0841, t(20.0) = 1.82, LMM on the effect of optogenetic manipulation), the percentage of unresponded trials (Figure 7C middle, yellow, 0.63 ± 0.63% to 0.76 ± 0.53%, 21 sessions, 3 animals, p = 0.6009, F(1,20) = 0.2825, LMM on the effect of optogenetic manipulation). Finally, as shown in Figure 7D, the animal’s overall adaptation after block transition was prevented by blue light stimulation during both ITIs (20 sessions from 3 animals) and RTWs (22 sessions from 3 animals), but preserved by yellow light during ITIs (n = 21 sessions from 3 animals). These results from temporally targeted “optogenetic interference” experiments suggest that the maintenance and transfer of instructing information for choice from the mPFC to DMS, in the form of the firing of a selective population of PRPD-modulated DMS-projecting mPFC neurons in each trial, is essential for implementing dynamic foraging.

Discussion

In this study, we examined neural activities in the mPFC and DMS in head-restrained mice performing a non-perceptual but action-outcome association-based dynamic foraging task, which requires internal tracking and updating of reward history to enable optimal performance in flexible decision-making. In summary, our results show: 1) A large proportion of neurons in mPFC and DMS represent PRPD, and a quarter of putative monosynaptic mPFC-DMS pairs encode PRPD. 2) mPFC leads DMS in spiking modulation in response to PRPD changes, and this temporal relationship is required for good performance. 3) Lack of reward strengthens the functional connectivity between mPFC and DMS. 4) Neural population activity in mPFC but not DMS represents overall reward history information. 5) Optogenetic interference with selective PRPD-modulated firing in mPFC impairs behavioral adaptation.

The prefronto-striatal circuits are essential in flexible behavior, but the nature of their interaction is still not well understood. Two hypotheses were proposed, arguing for the leading role of the striatum19,20 or prefrontal cortex21,40 in detecting and representing circumstance changes in flexible behavior. Our results are in agreement with the second hypothesis, where mPFC functions as a “context change detector” and sends top-down control information to the striatum. However, these two theories may not be mutually exclusive. More recently, an updated, reconciling hypothesis from non-human primate studies postulates that DMS representation will appear early in flexible behavior involving only stimulus-response learning,41 whereas when cognitive demand is higher, e.g., in tasks requiring the learning of abstract or inferred information, such as rules and categories, the prefrontal cortex will encode such changes earlier.23,42 Our experiments, involving the learning of history, one set of abstract and inferred information, provide direct evidence to support this theory and extend it to rodents. The fact that mPFC precedes DMS and that mPFC represents overall reward history information dominantly suggests that in a response-outcome association where the subjects have to rely not on learning perceptual information (visual or auditory stimuli) per se but self-inferred concepts such as reward history, the mPFC may play a leading role in this circuitry. In addition, our data suggest that the establishment of this mPFC-leads-DMS relationship could be essential for successfully executing this form of flexible behavior, as evidenced by the fact that when this temporal coordination is lost, task performance is impaired and vice versa. This interpretation is corroborated by the profound impairment of behavioral adaptation following temporally targeted optogenetic interference with trial-selective corticostriatal activities in the mPFC. In particular, the robust impairment in decision-making upon targeted interference within ITIs may suggest the necessity of mPFC corticostriatal neurons in holding and updating the decision variable, PRPD, that indicates a preferred choice, likely in the form of persistent firing between decisions.15 Finally, our results show that striatal activities not only faithfully reflect sensorimotor activities in corresponding cortical regions29 but also inherit the representations of more abstract and cognitively inferred information from high-order associative cortices such as the mPFC. The notion that DMS can make independent contributions to goal-directed behavior and strategy implementation is well documented. Extensive work has shown that the DMS is critical for encoding action-outcome associations and supporting flexible, outcome-based decision-making.10,43 Rather than passively reflecting cortical activity, the DMS actively contributes to strategy selection, particularly when behavioral contingencies change, as evidenced by impairments in reversal learning following DMS inactivation.44 Furthermore, single-unit recordings have demonstrated that DMS neurons encode action-specific reward values and decision-related signals, implicating the DMS in value computation and action selection.45,46 These findings underscore the DMS’s role in integrating cognitive inputs with sensorimotor information to guide adaptive behavior.47 Thus, our results support the view that while the DMS participates in shaping behavioral strategies through its own integrative and computational capacities, it is also strongly influenced top-down by associative cortical regions, such as mPFC.

The dynamics of functional connectivity between mPFC and DMS in flexible behavior is poorly understood, likely due to limited studies involving simultaneous prefrontal and striatal recordings in task-performing animals that switch responses or behavioral strategies back and forth several times in a single session, involving periods of learning, learned, and new learning (adaption vs. exploitation) several times in one experimental episode. Our experimental design provides such an opportunity. By employing simultaneous double-silicon probe recordings in two separate brain regions during behavior, we have identified putative monosynaptic mPFC-DMS pairs that are jointly modulated by decision variables of the task. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to delineate neural dynamics in the prefronto-striatal connectivity at the level of real-time, cross-region spike transmission. Our results provide initial functional evidence at the cellular level with defined anatomical specificity on how mPFC and DMS cooperate in real-time in implementing flexible behavior. Our results show that the strength of information transmission between mPFC and DMS during flexible behavior is not static but dynamic, even at the level of monosynaptic pairs. It is negatively modulated by the overall prior reward proportion, a behavioral variable likely associated with an animal’s overall drive and vigor.15 How is this modulation achieved in the corticostriatal circuit? The reward prediction error theory postulates that a teaching signal conveyed by dopamine is critical for multiple forms of associative learning by modulating corticostriatal synaptic plasticity in striatal medium spiny neurons.48 Previously, reward prediction errors were commonly observed in behavioral paradigms of explicit stimulus-outcome and stimulus-response associations.49,50 The fact that a lack of reward enhances functional connectivity between mPFC and DMS in our experiments may be explained by a recent finding of action-outcome (response-outcome) prediction error signals in goal-directed behavior, conveyed by nigrostriatal dopamine projections.51 In such a scenario, dopamine release is selectively suppressed in the reinforced actions associated with the expected outcome (advantageous choices in trials in the plateau period in our experiment) to minimize new learning in “learned periods” for predicted outcomes (expected rewards), permitting performance to stabilize. During the block transitions, this suppression of dopamine in previously reinforced actions may facilitate the standing out of unexpected reward signals from unreinforced actions and/or error signals following reward omission,16 which promotes new learning in pursuing alternative choices. Nevertheless, uncovering anatomically defined circuit mechanisms responsible for this response-outcome reward prediction error, which may underlie the observed strengthening of corticostriatal transmission efficacy by lack of reward during flexible behavior, remains an important question for future studies.

In conclusion, our results are consistent with a framework in which the mPFC may track action-outcome history more rapidly and earlier than the DMS during flexible behavior. Within this possible model, a decision-related signal could be transmitted from the mPFC to the DMS via corticostriatal projection neurons, potentially biasing behavioral engagement toward more advantageous strategies. The DMS, or more broadly the basal ganglia, might function as a behavioral-output filter that reflects the currently engaged strategy and is modulated by inputs from the mPFC when adaptation is required. The mPFC may detect the need for such changes through circuit mechanisms that could involve dopamine-mediated reward prediction error signals and feedback monitoring.32 Under conditions of enhanced mPFC-to-DMS functional connectivity, higher-order cortical influence might facilitate a shift in basal ganglia engagement away from a current strategy and toward a more advantageous one, thereby supporting behavioral flexibility in dynamic environments.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of the present study is the use of a head-fixed behavioral paradigm. Although our head-fixed paradigm offers well-controlled and quantitative analysis of behavioral performance, it does not recapitulate animals’ naturalistic behavior during foraging, which may affect the ecological validity of the findings. Though animals have been well-habituated and trained before recording, head restraints could still induce stress responses, such as the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, potentially influencing mPFC-DMS interactions and decision-making processes. Additionally, our task structure features high-frequency reversals of reward contingencies, which is very demanding for the animals. It does not fully capture the more gradual and continuous changes in reward landscapes that animals typically encounter in natural environments. Future studies leveraging freely moving paradigms and more naturalistic, temporally extended decision-making tasks will be essential for assessing the generalizability of these findings to ethologically relevant scenarios. The influence of gender on the results cannot be assessed, as only male mice were used in this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

All animal procedures received prior written approval from the UK Home Office and the University of Edinburgh Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body (AWERB) under the project license PP8564759. 3–5 months old male C57BL/6J mice (Charles River, UK) were housed in a 12-h reversed light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The housing environment was maintained at 23°C–25°C with 50–55% relative humidity. Following headplate implantation or optogenetic surgery, animals were singly housed and allowed to recover for at least one week before the start of behavior training. After recovery, mice were handled daily and gradually habituated to a water restriction schedule in preparation for dynamic foraging behavior training. During water restriction, animal health and body weight were monitored daily to ensure welfare.

Method details

Surgical preparation

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction, 1–2% maintenance) and mounted into a stereotaxic frame. The skull was surgically exposed, cleaned, and covered with a layer of cyanoacrylate superglue (Loctite, Germany). The sites of craniotomies were marked. Two steel screws (1.3 mm diameter) were placed in small boreholes drilled above the cerebellum and olfactory bulb. A custom-made head-ring for later head-restraint training and recording was attached to the skull and the screws and tightly fixed using bone cement (Refobacin, Biomet). After the surgery, mice were injected with Carprofen (Rimadyl, 120-130 μL S.C., 20 mg/kg) and received a liquid supplement with 0.9% NaCl, 500 μL S.C. Mice were given at least 7 days to recover, with their health monitored. Mice were then handled and habituated to be head-restrained on a custom-modified wheel-turning apparatus25 adapted into a virtual reality (VR) system (Phenosys, Germany). After habituation, mice were water-restricted (approximately 1.2 mL of water/day) and trained to perform the foraging task by turning the wheel for liquid rewards. Daily training lasted about 30–40 min. When the animals were deemed to have learned the dynamic foraging task (successful 4–5 block transitions in one training session on two consecutive days) after 3–6 weeks, they were anesthetized, and two small craniotomies were drilled (Right hemisphere: 4 mice; Left hemisphere: 1 mouse). One above the medial prefrontal cortex and one above the ipsilateral dorsomedial striatum (see below for coordinates). The craniotomy positions were adjusted to the applied silicon probe angles, for the DMS probe, on average 14.5° antero-posterior angle, and for the PFC probe, on average 15.3° medio-lateral angle was used. Dura was removed. Craniotomies were sealed and protected by silicone (Kwik-Cast, World Precision Instruments, USA). Animals were left for recovery for at least 8 h before electrophysiological recordings started.

Action-outcome history-based dynamic foraging task

Animals were head-fixed within a 180-degree virtual-reality screen (PhenoSys, Germany). Their front paws were placed on a wheel which they were able to move counter- and clockwise (left or right, see Figure 1A). Turning the wheel left or right moves an on-screen cursor left or right. Fluid rewards were delivered in volumes of 4 μL per rewarded trial via a tube placed in front of the animal’s mouth. Each trial starts with the appearance of the cursor in the center of the screen and a trial initiation signal (auditory tone of 8000Hz lasting 0.03s). After the animal rotates the wheel, the reward is delivered in a probabilistic fashion. One side will have a high reward probability (discrete values between 0.7 and 0.9), and the other side will have a low reward probability (discrete values between 0.05 and 0.2). After a variable number of trials, the high and low reward probabilities switch sides, so that the previously advantageous side becomes disadvantageous. Block transition was based on multiple conditioning. When the trial number reaches a randomly selected discrete value between 50 and 110, the animal’s performance is checked. If it is over the 75% advantageous choice ratio, a block transition is triggered. If it does not, a randomly selected number of extra trials between 15 and 30 are added to the current block, at the end of which performance is rechecked. After the animal executed wheel-turning (and a reward is either delivered or not delivered), there was a pause of variable duration (inter-trial interval, ranging from 1s to 1.6s after responded trials and 1.9s–2.3s after non-responded trials). The variable duration is to make it more difficult for the animals to anticipate when the next trial starts and thus discourage rhythmic premature responding and encourage attentive responding after the trial initiation signal. After the end of the variable pause period, there is a period lasting 1s when the animal is not permitted to make any suprathreshold movements of the wheel. If such movements occur, the time window resets to the start of the 1s. At the end of this period, the next trial commences. If the animal makes no suprathreshold movement for 7s after the trial initiation signal, a new trial is started. The times of the trial initiation signals and rewards were recorded as digital pulses simultaneously with neuronal signals via the signal-acquisition device’s digital inputs. The wheel’s instantaneous angular velocity was calculated from the raw outputs of its 1024-tick quadrature rotary encoder, also simultaneously recorded.

The proportion of rewarded trials and the proportion of responses toward the high probability side (advantageous responses) were calculated using a centered moving 20-trial long window. Perceived reward probability was gauged by calculating, for each side, the proportion of rewarded trials in the previous 10 trials of responses made to that side.

In vivo electrophysiological recordings

Electrodes consisting of 128-contacts on 4 parallel shanks (A4X32-Poly2-5mm-23s-200-177, NeuroNexus, USA) were inserted into the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) (Median coordinates from the tip of the medial most shank: AP: 2.10 ± 0.16 mm; ML: 0.23 ± 0.09 mm; DV: 1.90 ± 0.12 mm), and dorso-medial striatum (DMS) (Median coordinates from the tip of the medial most shank: AP: 0.85 ± 0.26 mm; ML: 1.00 ± 0.10 mm; DV: 3.40 ± 0.60 mm). Each shank contained 2 columns of contacts with horizontal and vertical inter-contact distances of 30 and 46 μm. Contact impedance values ranged from 0.5 to 3MΩ. Signals were recorded using an Intan 128-channel head-stage from each electrode and an Intan RHD recording controller. Signals were sampled at 20 KHz and digitized as 16-bit signed integers. Spikes were discriminated using the software Kilosort version 2.0.52 Automatically clustered waveforms were post hoc manually curated using the software Phy (https://phy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/). All filtering in the study was carried out using a 4-pole Butterworth filter, filtering in both directions. Signal processing, data analysis, and graphing were carried out in MATLAB (Mathworks, USA), Python, and GraphPad Prism10. Figure 1A was visualized with Blender using a 3D synthetic animated mouse.53

Multiple linear regression analysis

Cell firing rate profiles are aligned by trial initiation signals (−0.5 to 1.5s) using 20 ms-wide bins and then low-pass filtered at 20Hz. In order to gauge the degree of representation of different task events in spiking profile we first separated trials into: 1) those that had only the trial initiation signal but no subsequent suprathreshold movements or rewards (S trials); 2) those with trial initiation signal and movement but no reward (SM trials); 3) those with signal, movement and reward (SMR trials). In order to derive spike firing modulations that represent the trial initiation signal we simply took the average firing rate profile for S trials (Figures S2C and S2D). To derive movement-related firing modulations we subtracted spiking profiles of S trials from SM trials and averaged across all neurons in each area. In order to derive the reward-associated spiking modulation we subtracted the spiking profiles of SM trials from SMR trials. N.B. The later decreasing part (‘tail’) of the reward-related firing profile is likely associated with the prolonged wheel-turning motoric behavior when an expected reward is not delivered. Therefore, we do not claim that this signal is purely reward modulated but simply reward-associated. We used these three modality-related modulation patterns (S,M and R, representing signal, movement, and reward, scaled to have magnitudes of 1) as basis functions with which to fit individual neuronal firing profiles via a multiple linear regression:

where y is the modulation of an individual cell’s firing rate in SMR trials (i.e., where all three task modalities are present). S, M and R are the modality-associated basis functions for signal, movement and reward; a, b and c are their least-squares fitted coefficients; Ɛ represents the overall error of the regression fit. The values of a, b, c, and K are derived with the aim of producing the smallest value of Ɛ.

In order to guard against over-fitting, we carried out a 10-fold cross-validation. We randomly sampled 10% of neurons in each area as the training set and used their firing profiles in SMR trials to generate the modality-associated basis functions and their respective coefficients. Using these values, we generated a fitted firing profile and compared it with the actual firing profiles of the remaining 90% of neurons via a Pearson’s correlation analysis. This was repeated 10 times, each time with a distinct set of neurons in the training set. The correlation coefficients for all iterations are all significantly positive (P < 0.001, one-sample t test).

Phase calculation of trial-wise neuronal firing modulations

In order to examine broad and slow changes in the trial-wise spiking responses of neurons throughout the entire recording session we calculated for each trial the intertrial firing rate and response magnitude. Intertrial firing rate was defined as firing frequency for times between -1s and −0.5s relative to trial initiation signal. Response magnitude was defined as the mean absolute difference between firing rate in the 0 to 1.5s window and mean intertrial firing rate. We then low-pass filtered these signals using a cut-off frequency that is 10 times the fundamental frequency of the session (the fundamental trial frequency of each session is simply 1 divided by the number of trials), using a Butterworth filter per session. This allowed us to capture any oscillatory spiking process with frequencies up to 10 cycles per session. To calculate the trial-wise phase of the filtered signal, we removed its mean using the detrend function and performed a Hilbert transform to obtain the instantaneous phase. The circular-mean phase differences between each mPFC-DMS neuron pair were calculated for all sessions and for both response magnitudes and inter-trial firings.

Because the spike-pair correlations can be both positive and negative, the degree of interconnectivity between a given pair of neurons is defined as its maximal absolute deviation from zero. To adjust for the sudden variations in the firing rates, the interconnectivity strength for each trial is calculated using a centered 20-trial-long window. In order to remove the effects of task-driven co-modulation on spike cross correlation we only used spikes in the inter-trial interval.

Cell-type classification

Single units were excluded if their autocorrelograms had a peak spike count below 10. All remaining units were manually inspected to ensure waveform quality, with unclassified units marked as such due to potential artifacts, including waveform instability or overlapping multi-unit activity.

mPFC

To classify neurons recorded in the mPFC, we extracted average spike waveforms from each unit and computed two key features: (1) spike width, defined as the time between the waveform trough and subsequent peak, and (2) spike half-width, defined as the trough width at half-amplitude. Units were grouped into two clusters using an unsupervised k-means clustering algorithm (MATLAB function kmeans), based on these waveform features. This approach reliably separated neurons into two putative classes: putative pyramidal neurons (pPNs) and putative interneurons (pINs), in line with previously established waveform-based classification methods.28

DMS

Neurons recorded in the DMS were classified into four putative cell types: medium spiny neurons (MSNs), fast-spiking interneurons (FSIs), tonically active neurons (TANs), and unidentified interneurons (UINs), following criteria adapted from previous studies.29,30 Briefly, units with narrow spike waveforms (trough-to-peak duration ≤400 μs) were categorized as interneurons (either FSIs or UINs). To further separate these, we calculated the long-ISI ratio, defined as the total time spent in inter spike intervals (ISIs) > 2 s divided by the total recording time. Units with a ratio >10% were classified as UINs; the remainder were labeled as FSIs. The remaining wide-waveform units were presumed to be either MSNs or TANs and were distinguished using post-spike suppression duration. Post-spike suppression duration was calculated by examining the autocorrelogram and counting the number of 1-ms bins following the spike until the firing rate reaching or exceeding the mean rate (computed from the 600–900 ms range). Units with post-spike suppression >40 ms were classified as TANs, while those with shorter suppression were classified as MSNs.

Putative monosynaptic pair detection

Putative monosynaptic excitatory (PMSE) pairs were identified based on an increased likelihood of spiking of the postsynaptic pair member in a short latency, narrow time window after the presynaptic member’s spikes in a cross-correlogram-based based analysis modified from previously published methods.36,37 Cross-correlograms were constructed as postsynaptic-member spike count histograms, from −10 to +20 ms time windows around every presynaptic spike, in 0.5 ms bins. All pairs that fulfilled the following 4 conditions were identified as putative PMSE pairs. I.) The postsynaptic member spike count was 3-SD above the expected spike count threshold in a 0.5 – 7 ms time window after the presynaptic spikes. For the cortico-striatal pairs, a longer delay time window was selected compared to what is usually used in local circuits, where the synaptic delay is short, with EPSP latencies in the range of 0.5–3.5 ms.54,55 This is also reflected in previous studies with a maximum delay for identifying PMSE pairs in cross-correlograms (up to 5 ms) in the hippocampus.36,37,56 In cross-region cortico-striatal projections, actional potential conduction velocity is slower than the pyramidal tract and other faster pathways,57 resulting in a peak probability of postsynaptic spiking at 5-7 ms delay but frequently up to 11 ms.58 Therefore, our 0.5-7 ms delay time window for identifying PMSE pairs was chosen accordingly. The expected spike count for every bin of each pair was calculated based on shuffling postsynaptic spike times with a ±5 ms jitter 500 times. II.) There were at least 10 spikes above the expected line in the peak. III.) The ratio of spikes above the expected count and the expected count itself is above 1.5. IV.) The peak width was not longer than 1 ms, as low jitter is an indication of a monosynaptic interaction to guard against polysynaptic interactions.

Optogenetics

Viral vectors

Retrograde recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) containing Cre recombinase (pAAV-Ef1ɑ-mCherry-IRES-Cre, a gift from Karl Deisseroth, Addgene plasmid # 55632; http://n2t.net/addgene:55632; RRID: Addgene_55632), was obtained from Addgene (Addgene viral prep # 55632-AAVrg).59 AAV serotype 1 containing ChR2 (H134R) (pAAV-Ef1ɑ-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-EYFP-WPRE-HGHpA, a gift from Karl Deisseroth, Addgene plasmid # 20298; http://n2t.net/addgene:20298; RRID: Addgene_20298), was obtained from Addgene (Addgene viral prep # 20298-AAV1).

Virus injection and fiber implantation

To selectively express ChR2 in dorsomedial striatum (DMS)-projecting neurons of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), AAVrg-Cre was bilaterally injected into the dorsomedial striatum (DMS) bilaterally, in two locations in each hemisphere (Location 1: AP: 0.2 mm; ML: ±2.0 mm; DV:3.0 mm; Location 2: AP: 1.4 mm; ML: ±1.5 mm; DV:3.0 mm; 400-500 nL for each site). AAV1 carrying Cre-dependent ChR2(H134R)-EYFP was bilaterally injected into the mPFC bilaterally (AP: 1.9 mm; ML: ±0.6 mm; DV: 1.9 mm; 400-500 nL for each site). Fiber optic cannulae (200 μm diameter core, 0.39NA) with ceramic ferrule (1.25 mm diameter) from Thorlabs (www.thorlabs.com) or RWD Life Sciences (www.rwdstco.com) were implanted bilaterally to mPFC with a 10° angel (AP:1.9 mm; ML: ±0.7 mm; DV:1.7 mm) following virus injections during the same surgery.

Optogenetic manipulation

Virus-injected and cannulae-implanted mice were left for recovery and then trained for the dynamic foraging task. After 5–7 weeks, mice were used for optogenetic manipulation during behavior. An optical fiber (diameter 200 μm; Thor labs) with bifurcating ends was connected with pre-implanted cannulae bilaterally. Constant light pulses were applied using a double-wavelength diode-pumped solid-state laser (473 nm and 589 nm, CNI Laser), either for the duration of the whole ITI or the whole RWT periods. Laser intensity was set to reach 50% of the maximal power, giving a mean power of 25.3 mW and 33.2 mW, respectively, at the tip of the optical fiber. To examine localization and specificity of viral infection, AAV-injected mice were transcardially perfused, using 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by neutral buffered 10% formalin (Sigma Aldrich). Figures 7A and 7B were prepared using Biorender (https://www.biorender.com).

Reinforcement learning modeling

In addition to the intuitive PRPD, reinforcement learning modeling was also utilized to quantitatively describe the animals’ foraging behavior. The Rescorla-Wagner (RW) model, commonly used in non-stationary two-arm bandit tasks, was chosen to be a suitable candidate.

The model (sometimes referred to as the Q-learning model in other publications15,16)’s decision is guided by two decision variables, associated with two alternatives given to the animals. In our case, Ql corresponds to leftward choices, while Qr corresponds to the right. Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R or GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, shown in boxplots with overlaid scatter points, pie charts, or line graphs, as appropriate. For analyses involving in vivo electrophysiological recordings at the single-neuron or neuron-pair level, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used to account for repeated measures across sessions from the same animal and to capture inter-subject variability where appropriate. LMMs were also used in optogenetic experiments to account for multiple task sessions per animal when justified. To assess the significance of fixed effects in LMMs, Satterthwaite’s degrees of freedom approximation was used by default. In cases where model structure or sample size led to unstable or inaccurate estimates under the Satterthwaite method, Kenward–Roger’s approximation was applied instead to obtain more reliable degrees of freedom and test statistics. In cases where LMMs were not appropriate, the circular M test was used for Figure 3E. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test with p-value adjustment (Figures 6E, S2C, and S2D). Friedman ANOVA test with Post hoc Dunn’s multiple comparison analysis was used for Figure 7D. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance set at p < 0.05. Detailed descriptions of the statistical tests used for each figure are provided in the respective figure legends. Additional session-level information, including behavioral performance and cell-level profiles per animal, is available in Tables S1 and S2 to highlight inter-subject variability and session distribution.
