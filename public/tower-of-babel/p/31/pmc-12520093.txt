Immunogenicity of single-chain antibodies: germlining of a VHH lowers T-cell activation from epitopes in FR2 and CDR regions
Authors: Remi Giraudet, Adrien Laroche, Benjamin Chalopin, Steven Dubois, Evelyne Correia, Isabelle Staropoli, Olivier Schwartz, Bernard Maillère, Hervé Nozach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSingle-chain antibodies (scAbs), derived from camelid antibodies, have gained attention as therapeutic candidates due to their small size and perceived low immunogenicity, but recent studies have reported immune responses to several scAbs. To better understand their immunogenicity, we investigated the T-cell responses induced by VHH76, a VHH-Fc engineered to target the SARS-CoV-2 RBD, along with its humanized and germlined variants. The humanized variant contains six human substitutions, while the germlined variant was obtained by screening of a combinatorial library of the VHH76 sequences, comprising human and wild-type substitutions at 12 different positions. The germlined variant finally contains 16 human substitutions. All VHH76 variants triggered CD4 T-cell responses from healthy donors, with the germlined VHH76 showing significantly reduced T-cell stimulation. Two epitope regions were identified: one overlapping CDR3 and another spreading from CDR1 to CDR2. Additional human substitutions at the VHH-conserved positions in FR2 compromised the biological properties of the germlined VHH76 and did not seem to reduce clearly the risk of T-cell response. In conclusion, using a sensitive T-cell assay, we showed that T cells specific for VHH76 variants were detected in the blood of healthy donors and that the frequency of responding T cells diminished with germlining. While epitopes in CDR3 are linked to VHH76 specificity, modifying the conserved FR2 region presents challenges for reducing VHH76 immunogenicity. This study contributes to the understanding of VHH76 immunogenicity and offers insights into strategies to mitigate immune responses.KEYWORDS: Germlining, humanization, immunogenicity, single-chain antibody, T-cell assay, T-cell epitopes

Introduction

Originally derived from heavy-chain only antibodies naturally found in camelids such as camels, llamas, and alpacas, the variable domains (VHH) consist of a single monomeric variable domain that retains full antigen-binding capacity1. This unique structure not only simplifies their production but may also enhance their tissue penetration, while their long CDR3 has been proposed to facilitate recognition of epitopes that are inaccessible to conventional antibodies2. Additionally, VHHs are modular proteins, allowing for easy combination with other VHHs or with different proteins, such as Fc regions or full-length antibodies, to create multivalent or multispecific proteins with either short or long half-lives3. Thanks to their modularity, these VHH-derived proteins (single-chain antibodies or nanobodies) enable a wide range of domain combinations, and therefore of innovative therapeutic candidates. While several nanobodies have been approved for clinical use in the treatment of inflammatory diseases4 and rare blood disorders5, many others are being actively explored in a wide range of therapeutic areas, including oncology, autoimmune, and infectious diseases6,7. For example, nanobodies have been developed as neutralizing agents against pathogens such as HIV, influenza, and respiratory syncytial virus8. During the COVID-19 pandemic, nanobodies targeting the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein were rapidly developed and have shown potential in neutralizing the virus, highlighting their versatility and utility in combating emerging infectious diseases9–11.

Another appealing aspect of nanobodies in therapeutic applications is their perceived low immunogenicity, notably because the first approved VHH-derived molecule caplacizumab (Cablivi®) elicits few anti- drug antibody (ADA) responses5,12. Low immunogenicity is crucial for the development of safe and effective biologic therapies, as the formation of ADAs against therapeutic proteins can lead to reduced efficacy, adverse effects, and treatment failure. The putatively low immunogenicity of nanobodies has been attributed to their small size, high sequence homology with human immunoglobulins and the absence of Fc regions13,14. Nevertheless, several studies have detected ADAs directed against nanobodies in the blood of treated patients4,15–17. ADAs can be present at baseline, i.e., preexisting antibodies detectable before any exposure to nanobodies, but the origin of these remains uncertain. For example, baseline anti-nanobody antibodies have been reported for TAS266, a tetravalent DR5-targeting VHH construct15, and for the humanized VHH MC6.118. To mitigate such reactivity, engineering strategies have been proposed to abrogate binding by natural antibodies18,19. Beyond baseline seroreactivity, de novo neutralizing or non-neutralizing ADA onset has also been documented during treatment. Up to 36% of patients developed ADAs against envafolimab (ENWEIDA, 恩維達®), a VHH-Fc fusion protein20, while and ozoralizumab (Nanozora®), a trivalent anti-TNFα nanobody, showed ADA development in over 40% of patients in clinical trials4,17. Vobarilizumab, a bispecific nanobody that binds the IL-6 receptor and serum albumin that is no longer in development, induced ADAs in up to 31% of patients14. Despite being humanized, nanobodies can therefore elicit ADA responses, thus underscoring the need for more advanced strategies to enhance the safety of these therapeutic products.

Several factors contribute to the immunogenicity of biopharmaceuticals, including intrinsic factors related to the pharmaceutical products themselves and external factors arising from the patient’s genetic makeup, disease state and immune profile, and the mode of drug administration21. A key driver of immunogenicity is the presence of CD4 T-cell epitopes within the sequences of biopharmaceuticals22. CD4 T-cell epitopes are specific peptide fragments that result from the proteolytic degradation of the biopharmaceuticals and are displayed by HLA class II molecules on the surface of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) for recognition by T cells22. The interaction of these peptide-HLA complexes with CD4 T cells can initiate an immune response against the therapeutic protein, leading to the production of ADAs. The polymorphism of HLA molecules is significant in the global population and greatly affects their peptide-binding site23. As a result, CD4 T-cell epitopes are differently immunogenic and vary from one patient to another, depending on their HLA type22. In conventional therapeutic antibodies, CD4 T-cell epitopes often lie within regions that are mutated compared to the germline sequence, and therefore often overlap with the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)24–26. Since the CDRs are crucial for antigen recognition, modifying these regions to reduce immunogenicity without compromising the antibody’s efficacy is challenging27. Although VHHs are smaller than conventional antibodies, their non-human components can still harbor T-cell epitopes that trigger unwanted immune reactions. T-cell reactivity of VHHs has been scarcely investigated14, but identifying CD4 T-cell epitopes could help reduce ADA formation and improve the overall safety profile of VHH-based therapeutics.

In this study, we investigated the T-cell response to a VHH-Fc (VHH76) we have recently engineered28. VHH76 was originally derived from VHH72, a VHH specific to the SARS-CoV-1 receptor-binding domain (RBD)29. Through deep mutational engineering, VHH76 was adapted to improve its binding affinity to both SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 RBDs, allowing it to effectively neutralize multiple viral variants28. Several forms of VHH76 were generated to humanize its sequences and subsequently tested for their capacity to prime the T-cell response from cells collected from healthy donors. Generation of VHH76-specific T-cell lines allowed us to reveal different levels of T-cell response across the llama and humanized VHH76 variants and to identify CD4 T-cell epitopes.

Materials and methods

Production and biotinylation of RBD SARS-CoV-2

The gene encoding the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 delta strain of the virus was cloned in the pCAGGS RBD-SARS-CoV-2 plasmid, a kind gift from Florian Krammer (Mount Sinai, NY). HEK293 FreestyleTM (Thermo) were transiently transfected at a density of 2.5 106 cells/mL in 100 mL Freestyle medium (Thermo-Fisher) by addition of 150 µg plasmid and 1.8 mL of linear polyethylenimine (PEI, 0.5 mg/mL) (Polysciences). After 24 h, 100 mL of fresh medium was added. After 7 d at 37°C, 120 rpm, 8% CO2, supernatant was purified using HisTrap Excel for RBD proteins, following the manufacturer’s instructions (GE Healthcare). Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) was performed (HiPrep Sephacryl-S-200 HR or S100HR) with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Biotinylation of SARS-CoV2 RBD was performed with EZ-Link sulfo-NHS-LC-biotin following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A solution containing 23 µM SARS-CoV2 RBD was incubated with a 20-fold molar excess of sulfosuccinimidyl-6-[biotin-amido]hexanoate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in PBS for 30 min at 4°C. Unreacted biotin was removed using a desalting column (Zeba™ Spin desalting column, Thermo Fisher Scientific)

Yeast surface display of VHH

Preparation of competent yeast cells EBY100 (ATCC® MYA-4941) and library transformation were performed according to Benatuil et al.30 Gap repair transformations were made in plasmid pSWG VHH76 between restriction sites NheI and NotI with 1 µg of digested vector and a molar ratio of 12:1 (library/digested vector). After transformation, cells were cultivated in 250 mL of SD-CAA medium31. After a passage to an OD600 of 0.25, cells were grown at 30°C until OD600 0.6–0.8 and re-suspended in 50 mL of SG-CAA for induction.

Screening of VHH specific for RBD SARS-CoV-2 by cytometry

For library sorting, 107 to 2.108 induced cells were washed in PBSF (PBS, 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) and resuspended in PBSF containing the appropriate RBD SARS-CoV-2 concentration using an appropriate volume to avoid ligand depletion as performed in Hunter et al32. After 2 h of incubation at 20°C with agitation, cells were washed with ice-cold PBSF to avoid dissociation. Cells were incubated on ice for 15 min with anti-HA antibody (Invitrogen HA Tag Mouse anti-Tag, DyLight® 650 conjugate, Clone: 2–2.2.14; 1:100 dilution) and streptavidin-PE (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog number S866; 1:100 dilution). Cells were subsequently washed with ice-cold PBSF and sorted with a BD FACS Aria™ III cytometer using BD FACSdiva™ software.

Design of DMS or combinatorial libraries for YSD screening

Two deep mutational scanning (DMS) libraries of VHH76 variants with single amino acid mutations were generated by splicing by overlap extension PCR (SOE-PCR) using degenerate NNK primers. A library was constructed for the two targeted regions (positions 1 to 59 and 61 to 125), encompassing a diversity of 1888 and 2112 DNA variants, respectively. A combinatorial library of germlined mutants was generated by oligo gene synthesis using primers designed by Mutation Maker33 (https://github.com/Merck/Mutation_Maker). Various oligonucleotides, which permitted the introduction of either the human residue or the residue of the llama parental sequence at the positions of interest, were used in the synthesis PCR, resulting in the generation of a library comprising 1024 DNA variants. All the gene libraries were assembled in pSWG plasmids by homologous recombination in yeast, thus enabling them to be screened in yeast surface display.

Deep sequencing and analysis of NGS data

Plasmid DNA of each yeast population was extracted using Zymoprep Yeast Plasmid Miniprep II and prepared for sequencing as described in Medina-Cucurella and Whitehead34. Two-step PCR was performed to amplify the region of interest to add Illumina adapters and barcodes for multiplexing. Deep sequencing was performed with an Illumina ISeq 100 device (2x150 bp, 300 cycles) with at least 300,000 reads per population. Reads were demultiplexed, and each sample was processed separately using the Galaxy platform (https://usegalaxy.org/). First, paired reads were joined (Fastq Joiner). A trim was then performed (Fastq Trimmer) on reads to keep just the region of interest in the correct frame. A quality filter (Filter FASTQ) was applied to eliminate reads with a minimum quality score under 30. Next, DNA sequences were translated in protein sequences, and identical sequences were grouped. Sequences not repeated at least two times were filtered out. Using the software RStudio, single mutants were selected to allow calculation of enrichment ratios for each single mutation.

Production of VHH-Fc proteins

Selected VHH76-Fc expression plasmids were constructed by PCR-base accurate gene synthesis and cloned into the mammalian expression vector pCDNA 3.4. VHH-Fc constructs were obtained by transient transfection of Expi293F TM (Thermo). Cells were incubated at 37°C, 8% CO2, 125 rpm, and sub-passaged every 3–4 d in their respective expression media, as described in the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher). Cell densities were maintained between 0.3 and 4 × 106 cells/mL. One day prior to transfection, cells were seeded into a new flask at a final cell density of 1.6 × 106 cells/mL. DNA: Expifectamine complexes were formed at room temperature for 10 min, using 1.2 µg plasmid in total per mL of culture. Supernatant was purified using HiTrap Protein A (GE Healthcare) following the manufacturer’s instructions. SEC was performed (HiPrep Sephacryl-S-200 HR or S100HR) with PBS.

Affinity measurement by biolayer interferometry

Binding kinetics were determined using an Octet RED96 instrument (ForteBio). Anti-hIgG Fc Capture (AHC) Biosensors (Fortebio) were loaded with VHH-Fc molecules (50 nM) for 60 s. After baseline using kinetic buffer (PBS, BSA 0.5% (w/v) and Tween 20 0.05% (v/v)), association of RBD SARS-CoV-2 delta was measured at different concentrations (50 nM to 0.78 nM) for 300 s before dissociation in kinetic buffer for 1500 s. Data of the control without antigen were subtracted from all binding curves, and binding kinetics were fitted using a global 1:1 Langmuir-binding model.

S-Fuse neutralization assay

The neutralizing activity of the VHHs was assessed using the S-Fuse neutralization assay as previously described35. This assay leverages the fact that U2OS-ACE2 GFP1–10 or GFP11 cells, also known as S-Fuse cells, emit a green fluorescent protein (GFP) signal when they are productively infected with SARS-CoV-2, including the Delta variant36. Briefly, cells were mixed (at a 1:1 ratio) and plated at 8 × 103 cells per well in a μClear 96-well plate (Greiner, Bio-One). The SARS-CoV-2 Delta strain was incubated with VHHs at the indicated concentrations for 15 min at room temperature and added to S-Fuse cells. Then, 18 h later, cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde, washed and stained with Hoechst (1:1,000 dilution; Invitrogen). Images were acquired with an Opera Phenix high-content confocal microscope (PerkinElmer). The GFP area and the number of nuclei were quantified using the Harmony software (PerkinElmer). The percentage of neutralization and ED50 (concentration corresponding to 50% neutralization) values were calculated based on duplicate measurements.

Thermal stability

Thermal stability and aggregation were assessed with a Promotheus NT48 instrument from Nanotemper using 1 mg/mL VHH-Fc constructs loaded in high-sensitivity capillaries. A temperature ramp was performed, increasing from 20°C to 95°C at a rate of 1°C per minute. The data was analyzed using the manufacturer’s software to determine Tm and aggregation temperature values. This was achieved by calculating the derivative of the fluorescence signal ratio (350 nm/330 nm) and measuring the scattering signal, respectively. Associated onset values were also calculated.

Generation of specific T-cell lines

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were purified from the whole blood of anonymous healthy donors, who gave informed consent (Etablissement Français du Sang), using Ficoll-Paque®. Monocyte-derived immature dendritic cells (iDCs) were produced from cells isolated using Percoll gradient by 5 d of culture in AIM-V medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 1,000 U/mL recombinant human (rh) IL-4 (130–093-924, Miltenyi Biotec) and rhGM-CSF (130–093-867, Miltenyi Biotec). Immature DCs (20,000 per well) were loaded overnight at 37°C with either 1 or 3 µM of VHH or 1 µM of hemocyanin from keyhole limpet (KLH) (H8283, Sigma-Aldrich) in AIM-V medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide and 10 µg/mL R848 in multiple 96-well U-bottom plates and incubated overnight at 37°C. DCs were then washed twice with 100 µL AIM-V medium and with 100 µL IMDM (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% human AB serum (Sigma), 0.24 mM glutamine, 0.55 mM asparagine, 1.5 mM arginine (all amino acids from Sigma-Aldrich), 50 U/mL penicillin, and 50 μg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen) (complete IMDM). CD4 T cells were isolated from autologous PBMCs by positive selection using labeling with anti-CD4 mAbs conjugated to magnetic microbeads, followed by magnetic cell sorting (Miltenyi Biotec). Autologous CD4 T cells (200,000 per well) were added to the round bottom wells of culture plates in 200 µL complete IMDM containing 1000 U/mL IL-6 (130–096-933, Miltenyi Biotec) and 10 ng/mL IL-12 (130–096-705, Miltenyi Biotec). About 24 wells were seeded for each antibody and 8 wells only for KLH. CD4 T lymphocytes were re-stimulated at days 7 and 14 with 20 000 autologous DCs freshly loaded with the appropriate protein and were grown in complete IMDM medium supplemented with 10 U/mL IL-2 (130–095-769, Miltenyi Biotec) and 5 ng/mL IL-7 (130–095-362, Miltenyi Biotec). The specificity of the CD4 T-cell lines (T cells contained in each independent well) was assessed at day 21 for the evaluation of the frequency of specific T cells and at day 28 for the T-cell epitope mapping after a supplementary step of stimulation with VHH-loaded DCs at day 21.

IFN-γ ELISPOT assay

Multiscreen hemagglutinin (HA) 96-well plates (Merck Millipore) were coated overnight at 4°C with 2.5 µg/mL anti-human IFN-γ Mab (1-D1K, 3420–3-1000, Mabtech Biotec) in PBS (Invitrogen). Wells were saturated for 2 h at 37°C with complete IMDM and washed with PBS. VHHs were loaded at a concentration of 3 µM onto autologous iDCs in AIM-V supplemented with 1.7 U/mL rhIL-7 for 4 h at 37°C. KLH was used at a concentration of 2 µM, only. Overlapping peptides were from ProteoGenix and were directly added as peptide pools or individual peptides to MultiScreen plates at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL. PBMCs (50,000/well) or protein-loaded iDCs (5,000/well) were used as APCs and co-cultured in the plates with approximately 10,000 to 30,000 CD4 T cells in AIM-V supplemented with 0.5 ng/mL rh-IL-7. The T-cell lines and APCs were plated at equal number for both stimulated and unstimulated control conditions. After overnight incubation at 37°C and washing, plates were subsequently treated with 0.25 µg/mL biotinylated anti-human IFN-γ mAb (7-B6-1; 3420–6-250, Mabtech biotec) in PBS/BSA 1%, extravidin-phosphatase (dilution 1:3000 in PBS/Tween 20 0.05%/BSA 1%; Sigma-Aldrich) and NBT/BCIP (Sigma-Aldrich). Spot number was determined by the AID ELISPOT Reader System (AID GmbH). CD4 T-cell lines were considered as specific when a spot count was 2-fold higher in the presence of the protein or peptide than in its absence, with a minimal difference of 25 spots. CD4 T-cell precursor frequencies were estimated using the Poisson distribution according to the following formula: Frequency = -Ln ((Number of nonspecific CD4 T-cell lines/Total number of CD4 T-cell lines seeded))/(Number of CD4 T cells/well).

Results

Design of germlined VHH76 combinatorial library by using DMS

To germline the VHH76 sequence and therefore to introduce human substitutions in both FR and CDR regions, we first introduced the human substitutions that had previously been incorporated into the parental VHH7229,37 to create the humanized 76 H nanobody. We then aimed to identify permissive positions within the 76 H sequence that could tolerate human substitutions without compromising its binding to the SARS-CoV-2 RBD. To achieve this, we used a DMS approach (Figure 1(A)). Due to sequencing length limitations, two comprehensive DMS libraries were constructed, each covering all 20 possible single amino acid substitutions across the 125 positions of the 76 H sequence. The libraries were divided into two parts: one covering amino acids 1–66 and the other covering amino acids 67–128. VHH variants were displayed on the surface of yeast cells via the Aga1p/Aga2p system, with expression monitored using an HA-Tag. Binding to the RBD-Delta antigen was assessed by incubating the yeast cells with biotinylated RBD-Delta, followed by flow cytometry. To accurately compare the RBD binding of mutated clones to the parental 76 H, we co-expressed a small proportion (typically 5–10%) of cells containing the parental clone and intracellular enhanced GFP (EGFP) using an additional plasmid (pSWG 76 H EGFP). This approach allowed us to distinguish the parental clones during fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis based on their EGFP fluorescence signal (in black), enabling precise definition of the sorting gates (Figure 1(C)). As shown in Figure 1(B), a large array of variants with reduced binding to the RBD-Delta antigen maintained high levels of HA-tag expression, indicating proper display on the yeast surface. Low binding variants were sequenced to determine specific mutations associated with the loss of binding affinity, which are reported as heatmaps in Figure 1(C). Many mutations close to CDR2 or within CDR2 and CDR3 were associated with a significant binding loss, but other substitutions were well tolerated. To further increase the humanness of the 76 H sequence, we compared it with the closest human germline genes using the IMGT database38 and identified IGHV3-23 × 01 and IGHJ4 × 01 as the most similar. Based on these germlines, 12 positions with amino acid discrepancies were selected. We then constructed a focused combinatorial library varying only at these positions, allowing either the human germline or the parental residues, thereby constraining library diversity while enabling the possibility of backmutation of potentially deleterious human substitutions. We decided to omit at this stage of the study the hallmark positions in the FR2 that are conserved in VHH (42F, 49E, 50 R and 52F) and two substitutions in the CDR2. A single combinatorial library was generated using splicing by overlap extension PCR (SOE-PCR).

Screening of germlined variants and selection of VHH 76G3

The combinatorial library was screened via cytometry to identify high-affinity variants after expression in yeast cells. One round of selection was conducted at a low concentration of RBD, allowing for the isolation of the best RBD binders (Figure 2(A)). While many clones exhibited reduced binding affinity as detected by cytometry, several showed comparable levels of apparent affinity to the 76 H nanobody and were sequenced using next-generation sequencing (NGS) across two distinct regions owing to the sequencing limitations imposed by read length. The enrichment of sequencing reads containing each human/llama substitution was evaluated before and after sorting (Figure 2(B)) to assess the impact of these substitutions on RBD binding. Notably, between residues 5 and 68, the human T68A substitution was significantly enriched, indicating enhanced RBD binding affinity. In contrast, other substitutions either failed to improve binding or showed varying degrees of decreased affinity. As a result, less favorable substitutions (I67Y, Y115F) were removed from each region to design the germlined 76G3.

The three VHH variants (76, 76 H, and 76G3) were expressed as VHH-Fc constructs. Following transfection, the VHH-Fc molecules were purified, characterized using analytical SEC and SDS-PAGE, and their affinity for the RBD-Delta antigen was measured through biolayer interferometry. All variants exhibited strong sub-nanomolar affinities for SARS-CoV-2 RBD, with similar association constants (Figure 2(D)). The affinity constants for 76 and 76 H were 345 pM and 21 pM, respectively, with 76 H benefiting from a slower dissociation rate compared to 76. The 76G3 variant showed an activity lower than 76 H, but similar to 76. All three variants showed a strong neutralizing activity against the SARS-CoV-2 Delta virus35 with very similar ED50 values below 0.02 µg/mL (Figure 2(E)). As anticipated, the humanness of the sequences, calculated using OASis percentiles, was significantly higher for 76G3 compared to VHH 76, 76 H, and caplacizumab, with 76G3 containing fewer non-human 9-mer peptides (Figure 2(E)). Germlining also modestly reduced the number of binding cores to HLA class II molecules, particularly for the percentiles corresponding to moderate (10%) and low (20%) binding affinity for HLA class II molecules (Figure 2(F)). Finally, thermal stability testing of the three VHH variants, conducted via NanoDSF technology, revealed a clear advantage for 76G3 (Figure 2(G)). The 76G3 variant had a denaturation temperature (Tm) of 65°C, compared to 52°C and 58°C for 76 and 76 H, respectively. All variants exhibited aggregation at approximately 80°C. In summary, 76G3 combines sub-nanomolar affinity, potent neutralizing activity against SARS-CoV-2, superior stability, improved humanness, and reduced T-cell epitope prediction. This raises the possibility that 76G3 carries a lower risk of immunogenicity and thus may be less likely to prime T cells than the original VHH76 and the partially humanized 76 H.

Evaluation of the T-cell response to VHH76

Because animal models are poorly predictive of immune responses in humans, we assessed the risk of immunogenicity of the VHH-Fc by using a human T-cell assay relying on the detection of specific T cells in the blood of normal human donors39–41. The rationale of this approach is that the T-cell repertoire of normal donors mimics the T-cell repertoire of patients before being infused with the VHH. Because of the low frequency of antigen-specific T cells in healthy donors, we attempted to detect VHH-specific T cells by generating T-cell lines39. CD4 T cells collected from healthy donors were stimulated in 24 culture wells by 3 rounds of stimulation with DCs loaded with VHHs or KLH, KLH being introduced as a positive control to exclude cell preparations unable to mount in vitro a primary T-cell response. Antigen specificity was assessed by IFN-γ ELISPOT, the T cells being skewed to a Th1 phenotype by addition of IL-12 at the beginning of the cell culture. As shown in Figure 3(A), 10, 6 and 3 of the 24 seeded culture wells with the T cells of the same donor contained T cells specific for 76, 76 H and G3, respectively. The number of VHH-specific T-cell lines is highly variable across different donors, reflecting the impact of HLA polymorphism and diversity of the naïve T-cell repertoire. We then estimated, for each VHH and for KLH, the mean frequency of antigen-specific CD4+ T cells, assuming that at assay initiation their distribution follows a Poisson distribution39. KLH generated T-cell lines for each donor and exhibited a mean frequency of 5.6 specific CD4 T-cells per million. The T-cell responses to 76 and 76 H were very similar, with a T-cell frequency of 1 and 0.93 cell per million, respectively. The mean frequency of 76G3 is significantly lower with a frequency of 0.4 cell per million. As a result, while humanization of VHH76 did not reduce the risk of immunogenicity, the sequence germlining diminishes this risk and therefore appears beneficial.

Identification of CD4 T-cell epitope of VHH76

We then aimed to identify CD4 T-cell epitopes within the VHH76 sequence to have a complete view of their location. CD4 T-cell lines were generated from 12 HLA-typed donors by culturing the purified CD4 T cells with autologous DCs loaded with VHH76 as done previously. T-cell lines were submitted to a first ELISPOT assay using peptide pools, and their specificity was confirmed in a second independent ELISPOT with individual peptides25,42 (Figure 4). As examples, T-cell lines reacted with the different peptide pools and then with one or two individual peptides (Figure 4(A)). A total of 14 T-cell lines were found responding to individual peptides and pinpointed a total of 11 T-cell epitopes. As shown in Figure 4(B), the CD4 T-cell epitopes clustered in two different regions: the CDR3, which resulted from the recombination of V, D, and J genes, and a region spreading from the CDR1 to CDR2. The last region therefore encompasses the FR2 region, which contains amino acids conserved across the nanobodies that are not human.

Humanization of the FR2 region

We then focused on the FR2 region of 76G3, as this region contains many T-cell epitopes and includes hallmark residues that are conserved across VHHs, but are distinct from corresponding human germline sequences. This region might therefore be involved in the onset of the ADA response of different VHHs. In contrast, the CDR3 region is unique to each VHH. We therefore introduced human substitutions in the conserved residues across the VHH, in the FR2, namely 42, 49, 50 and 52. Two of these substitutions increased the humanness of the sequence as evaluated by the OASis index43 (Figure 5(B)). However, the content in non-human 9-mer peptides was little modified by these human substitutions (Figure 5(B)) and the number of predicted of HLA binding cores44 varies little as compared to 76G3 (Figure 5(C)). The 3 VHH were produced as VHH-Fc, purified and characterized for their affinity (Figure 5(D)) and stability (Figure 5(E)). The human substitutions 44 and 45 did not change the binding parameters of the VHH, slightly increased its denaturation temperature, but altered the aggregation profile. Other substitutions are more deleterious. Substitution 37 clearly made the VHH unstable and less active, while substitution 47 led to a not very active molecule. As a result, humanization of the FR2 positions did not reduce the risk of immunogenicity, while maintaining full binding activity.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the T-cell response to VHH76, a nanobody engineered to target the SARS-CoV-2 RBD28, to anticipate its immunogenicity in humans. Our results showed that germlining of VHH76 reduced T-cell priming and revealed two regions that hosted VHH76-specific T-cell epitopes.

From a drug-development perspective for VHH7628, we aimed to humanize its sequence as extensively as possible. We first introduced human substitutions previously identified as well tolerated in the parental antibody VHH7210,37, generating the humanized 76 H variant. We then further extended humanization by incorporating 16 germline substitutions to generate the 76G3 variant. Given the limitations of animal models in predicting immunogenicity in humans, we assessed the T-cell response specific to these three VHH76 variants (76, 76 H, and 76G3) using a human-based T-cell assay39, as recommended by US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency guidelines (https://www.ema.europa.eu.). This assay relies on the generation of VHH-specific T-cell lines using cells collected from healthy donors39. Cells of healthy donors reflect the T-cell repertoire status of patients prior to the first drug administration. The assay format is designed to quantify rare naïve drug-specific T cells since the donors have never been exposed to the drug and therefore do not have preexisting memory T cells. Furthermore, the amplitude of the memory T-cell response is shaped by the size of the naïve precursor pool45–47. We applied this T-cell assay to various therapeutic proteins39–41,48 and found very good concordance with their immunogenicity in humans39–41. Using this approach, all three VHH variants elicited an in vitro T-cell response in multiple donors. The frequency of specific T cells was comparable for 76 and 76 H, but was significantly reduced for the germlined form, 76G3. Notably, the responses observed for all VHH variants were within a range similar to known immunogenic antibodies such as adalimumab and ixekizumab,39,40 indicating a potential risk of immunogenicity, although the germlined 76G3 showed a lower risk. This result contrasts with findings from the only other study investigating the in vitro T-cell response to VHHs14. Several differences may account for the discrepancy, including T-cell assay methodologies, VHH sequences, and molecular formats. The VHHs examined differed at least in their CDR3, which, in our study, contained CD4 T-cell epitopes unique to VHH76. Our constructs are VHH-Fc fusions rather than single-domain VHH molecules. Although the previous study evaluated uptake of their VHH constructs by DCs14, the absence of an Fc region may reduce their capture. By contrast, the Fc region in our VHH-Fc constructs likely enhances DC uptake via Fcγ receptors (FcγR)49 or the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)50, thereby improving epitope presentation to T cells.

Based on our experiments, VHH76 variants appear to be at risk of immunogenicity, including 76G3, which has been germlined. As recently reported, VHH humanization strategies for some nanobodies may be insufficient to prevent de novo ADA onset14,17,20,51. Envafolimab is an Fc-containing construct similar to VHH 76, 76 H and 76G3 and was immunogenic in up to 36% of patients20. Envafolimab binds to PD-L1, which is expressed on DCs, potentially facilitating presentation of envafolimab peptides to T cells. Multivalent nanobodies lacking Fcs, such as vobarilizumab14, sonelokimab51, and ozoralizumab17, have also shown significant ADA responses. In contrast, caplacizumab and gefurulimab appear weakly immunogenic12,52. These observations underscore early heterogeneity across the limited VHHs investigated and, as clinical experience grows, their immunogenicity may ultimately be as variable as that of full-length antibodies. Immunogenicity results from multiple factors depending on the patients (genetics, disease, concomitant drugs), dosing and administration and the product itself. Because of this multifactorial nature, T-cell assays alone cannot fully predict the onset of ADA responses in clinical settings. However, they are valuable tools for evaluating the risk of immunogenicity39–41,48 by ranking therapeutic candidates, provided they are sensitive enough to detect low-frequency naïve T cells39,46,53.

In this study, we also identified multiple T-cell epitopes within VHH76 sequences. Responding donors generated one to three distinct T-cell lines, with two peptides (P33-47 and P41-55) shared by two donors. T-cell epitopes were localized in two regions spanning CDR1–CDR2 and within CDR3. All these regions correspond to non-human sequences, consistent with findings for other therapeutic antibodies, including chimeric antibodies such as infliximab and rituximab42, and human(ized) antibodies such as adalimumab25, natalizumab24,25, and ixekizumab26. This localization helps explain differences in T-cell responses among germlined 76G3, humanized 76 H, and wild-type 76. Germlining introduced human sequences into the CDR1 and into regions flanking the CDR3, thereby reducing the presence of non-human 9-mers that could trigger T-cell responses. The presence of T-cell epitopes spanning the CDR3 supports the notion that V, D, and J recombination in VHHs can generate neoepitopes, as observed in human(ized) antibodies25,26. It also suggests that the longer CDR3 in VHHs54, compared with conventional antibodies25,26, may increase the risk of neoepitope generation. T-cell epitopes overlapping the CDRs are associated with antigen specificity and thus are unique to each nanobody, potentially accounting for substantial differences in clinical immunogenicity across nanobodies.

A notable finding is the presence of multiple T-cell epitopes within the FR2 region. VHHs have evolved to contain more hydrophilic amino acids in this region, such as E49 and R50, than conventional VH domains55. This adaptation enhances solubility and prevents unwanted pairing with VL domains55. However, humanizing these two positions altered binding activity and drastically compromised thermal stability, in accord with previous studies55. These modifications could favor aggregate formation, which is known to increase T-cell responses56 and immunogenicity57. Moreover, the substitutions did not significantly reduce the OASis scores, non-human peptide content, or HLA-binding scores, indicating a limited impact on reducing VHH immunogenicity. Substitutions at other conserved positions, 42 and 52, were deleterious to binding and therefore cannot be used in the design of VHH therapeutics. Alternative strategies beyond simple humanization are necessary to mitigate T-cell responses driven by epitopes present in the FR2 region. Removing these T-cell epitopes by disrupting HLA class II binding appears challenging. For example, the conserved position F52 in VHH is predicted to serve as a critical anchor residue for the P1 pocket of several HLA molecules44, making it difficult to modify. However, other positions and HLA-binding registers may be adjustable to prevent HLA binding and reduce immunogenicity.

Initially, VHH nanobodies were thought to exhibit low immunogenicity; however, increasing reports highlight immune responses to these molecules. To better understand VHH immunogenicity, we investigated the T-cell responses elicited by VHH76 and its variants. All tested variants triggered a T-cell response, with epitopes identified in the FR2 and CDR regions. Investigation of VHH immunogenicity is in its early stages, necessitating further research to fully characterize and mitigate the immunogenicity risks associated with the therapeutic use of VHH nanobodies.
