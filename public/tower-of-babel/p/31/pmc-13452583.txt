Experimental evidence for early bacterial inheritance in the giant kelp, Macrocystis pyrifera
Authors: Sara Usandizaga, Jessica Beltrán, Gustavo Rodríguez-Valdecantos, Génesis Parada-Pozo, Marie-Laure Guillemin, Sylvain Faugeron, Nicole Trefault, Carolina Camus, Julie Olson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteria associated with macroalgae influence the development, health, and ecological interactions of their hosts. Although vertical transmission of bacterial symbionts has been proposed in some algal species, evidence remains limited. In this study, we combined 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to assess bacterial presence and localization throughout early developmental stages of the brown macroalga Macrocystis pyrifera. Amplicon sequencing detected bacterial taxa in reproductive sori, released spores, and gametophytes, even under sterile conditions and using pasteurized, filtered seawater. Certain bacterial taxa, including Balneola alkaliphila and Marinoscillum luteum, were recurrently detected across multiple developmental stages. FISH imaging showed bacterial signal associated with host tissues across life stages, distinguishable from algal autofluorescence, while TEM observations revealed structures consistent with intracellular bacterial cells within the cytoplasm of gametophyte cells and embedded in sporangial cuticles. Together, these observations suggest that bacteria are associated with both internal and external reproductive tissues, pointing to multiple potential transmission routes. Altogether, our findings provide evidence consistent with early-stage bacterial inheritance in M. pyrifera, contributing to the growing evidence that macroalgal holobionts are assembled, at least in part, through vertical transmission of symbionts.Keywords: vertical bacterial transmission, early life stages, kelp holobiont, microbiome assembly, intracellular bacteria, reproductive tissues

Introduction

The functioning, development, and ecological performance of macroalgae are tightly linked to their associated microbial communities (Egan et al. 2013, Singh and Reddy 2014). These intimate and persistent associations form a complex ecological unit known as the holobiont, in which the algal host and its microbiota interact through chemical signaling and metabolic exchange (Egan et al. 2013, Saha and Weinberger 2019, Saha et al. 2024, Marzinelli et al. 2024). In macroalgae, bacterial associates are not passive colonizers; they contribute to host morphogenesis (Matsuo et al. 2005, Wichard et al. 2015, Tapia et al. 2016), nutrient acquisition (Croft et al. 2005, Singh and Reddy 2014), stress tolerance (Minich et al. 2018, Li et al. 2023, Vadillo-González et al. 2024), and defense (Saha et al. 2011, Weinberger et al. 2011), and therefore are increasingly recognized as integral components of algal biology. Macroalgal-associated bacterial communities are taxonomically diverse yet non-randomly assembled. Numerous studies have demonstrated that bacterial communities associated with different species of macroalgae differ markedly from surrounding seawater and are often highly host-specific (Lachnit et al. 2009, Lemay et al. 2018, Weigel and Pfister 2019, Morrissey et al. 2019). Although spatial and temporal turnover has been documented across regions, depths, and seasons, microbial assemblages often show relative stability within host individuals, implying an important role of host-mediated filtering processes (Phelps et al. 2021, Burgunter-Delamare et al. 2023, Esaian et al. 2024).

Several mechanisms can contribute to this host-associated microbial structure. At the physical level, thallus morphology, tissue differentiation, and the properties of the external surface (e.g. mucilage layers, microtopography) strongly influence microbial attachment and biofilm development, promoting spatially structured microbiomes across the algal body (Wahl et al. 2012, Lemay et al. 2021). At the chemical level, macroalgae release suites of metabolites that act as infochemicals, mediating microbial recruitment, and biofilm succession, and thereby shaping microbial community composition and function (Egan et al. 2013, Saha and Weinberger 2019, Saha et al. 2021). Within these biofilms, microbial interactions are also regulated by chemical communication networks, including quorum sensing (QS) and quorum quenching (QQ), which modulate biofilm formation, competitive interactions, and community stability, and may contribute to maintaining host–microbial homeostasis in marine holobionts (Wahl et al. 2012, Modolon et al. 2020, Adouane et al. 2024, Coolahan and Whalen 2025). For example, in the kelp Saccharina latissima, crosstalk among bacterial and fungal members of the epimicrobiome has been shown to structure microbial consortia and potentially influence host health and resilience (Adouane et al. 2024).

While the composition of microbiomes associated with adult macroalgal thallus has been well characterized, how these microbial communities are established during early stages of the host remains poorly understood. Early developmental stages are critical because they mark the onset of holobiont assembly, potentially influencing later performance, resilience, and susceptibility to stress. Experimental work in the green macroalga Ulva spp. has shown that bacteria induce blade cell division, promote rhizoid differentiation from basal cells, and support cell wall formation (Wichard 2023). Similarly, in the brown alga Ectocarpus spp., bacterial presence is required for the development of its characteristic branched morphology and reproductive structures (Tapia et al. 2016). Given this intimate interdependency during morphogenesis and early development, it is plausible to hypothesize that at least a subset of the bacteria is transmitted from parents to offspring via vertical transmission. Alternatively, spores may acquire bacteria from the surrounding environment after release (i.e. horizontal transmission), but this process would depend on the local availability of the specific bacterial taxa required for development.

In terrestrial systems, vertical microbial transmission is well documented in angiosperms, where endophytes and seed-associated microorganisms are vertically transmitted and can contribute to seedling vigor, stress tolerance, and disease resistance (Truyens et al. 2015, Shade et al. 2017). Likewise, vertical inheritance of microbiota has been observed in numerous animal systems, such as bumblebees and flies, where gut-associated microbiota is passed from queen to workers across generations (Su et al. 2021, Schmidt and Engel 2021). In marine systems, vertically inherited bacteria have been reported in sponges and corals, where they are transmitted via brooded larvae or gametes and contribute to host immunity and nutrient cycling (Leite et al. 2017, van Oppen and Blackall 2019, Carrier et al. 2022). In macroalgae, recent evidence suggests that different species employ diverse strategies of symbiont transmission, ranging from purely horizontal acquisition to clear vertical transmission. For example, in a recent study on Ecklonia radiata, gametophytes exhibited bacterial communities more similar to parental sporophytes than to those of surrounding seawater, which the authors interpret as supporting a possible vertical transmission pathway (Veenhof et al. 2025). Furthermore, Syukur et al. (2023) demonstrated vertical transmission in the brown macroalgae Homosira banksii and Phyllospora comosa, as well as in the red macroalga Delisea pulchra, but not in the green macroalga Ulva australis. In the mentioned brown macroalgae, bacteria were observed in association with the mucilage layer of the eggs, although they did not appear to penetrate the inner cell layer of the gametes (Syukur et al. 2023). During the life cycle of both H. banksii and P. comosa, eggs are released from the oogonia enveloped in mucilage (Levring 1949). It is thus possible that bacteria from the parental thallus adhere to this mucilage upon gamete release (Fletcher and Callow 1992), or that they are already present in the gamete prior to their extrusion. In fact, ultrastructural studies have revealed that Golgi bodies positioned near the nucleus in brown algal spores contribute to the production of residual mucilage, which may facilitate initial adhesion and possibly mediate microbial transfer (Baker and Evans 1973, Fletcher and Callow 1992). Together, these studies suggest that different macroalgal lineages may employ distinct mechanisms for symbiont transfer from adults to their progeny, potentially influenced by development and structural traits specific to each algal group.

Macrocystis pyrifera is a brown macroalga commonly known as giant kelp and has a heteromorphic life history with an alternation of macroscopic diploid sporophyte and microscopic haploid gametophyte generations (Schiel and Foster 2015). In the sporophyte, meiosis occurs on the reproductive blades, the sporophylls, where haploid spores are released from sporangia. Spores developed into microscopic free-living male and female gametophytes. Sperm is released from male antheridia and swims to the female’s egg, which remains attached to the gametophyte. After fertilization, diploid zygotes germinate to form early sporophytes (Schiel and Foster 2015). Bacteria have been shown to colonize both the external surfaces and internal thallus tissues of adult sporophytes (Ramírez-Puebla et al. 2022). Recent work has further shown that early developmental stages of M. pyrifera host microbial communities distinct from surrounding seawater, suggesting stage-specific microbial colonization (Icaza et al. 2025). However, little is known about how these microorganisms are acquired during early development or how they reach reproductive structures and tissues. Clarifying the timing and mechanisms of bacterial acquisition during early stages is essential for understanding microbiome assembly and functional integration in this ecologically foundational holobiont.

Here, we test the hypothesis that specific bacterial taxa are vertically transmitted in M. pyrifera and persist across early developmental stages. To address this, we combined transmission electron microscopy (TEM), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and 16S rRNA gene sequencing to examine the presence, localization, and taxonomic identity of bacteria from reproductive tissue (i.e. sori) to gametophytes. By integrating these taxonomic and imaging methods across sori, released spores, settled spores, and gametophytes, we aim to determine whether early-stage microbiomes in M. pyrifera are consistent with partial vertical inheritance rather than solely horizontal acquisition from seawater.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and experimental design

Forty-five sporophylls with mature sori (i.e. clusters of sporangia) were collected from different individuals of M. pyrifera in March 2024 at a coastal site, Pichilemu (34° 38′ S, 72° 01′ W), located on the Southeastern Pacific coast. In parallel, 5 l of seawater were collected in a clean and rinsed plastic container in close proximity to the sporophylls. Both sporophylls and seawater were transported to the laboratory in insulated cooler boxes containing ice packs and processed immediately upon arrival (within 4 h after sampling). Short transport times under cooled conditions are standard practice in macroalgal microbiome studies and have been used for kelps and other seaweeds in order to minimize bacterial community shifts during transport, thereby preserving in situ community structure (Ling et al. 2022, Schenk 2025, Veenhof et al. 2025).

To characterize the seawater’s bacterial community from the sampling site, the 5 l seawater sample was filtered through a Sartorius filtration unit equipped with 0.22 µm nominal pore size membrane filters (47 mm diameter; GSWP MF-Millipore, USA), generating three replicate filters for molecular analyses.

Upon arrival at the laboratory, five sori were randomly selected and left unmanipulated (hereafter “untreated sori”) to serve as baseline characterization of bacterial associations with reproductive tissue (Fig. 1). All remaining sori were subjected to a surface-cleaning protocol designed to reduce surface-associated bacteria (see below for more details), hereafter “treated sori”. Tissue samples of 1 × 1 cm the five treated sori and the five untreated sori were processed to generate independent replicates for each downstream analysis: i) frozen at −20°C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing; ii) fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis (FISH), and iii) stored in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation (Fig. 1).

The remaining treated sori (n = 35) were pooled to generate a spore suspension with sufficient concentration for subsequent steps. From this pooled suspension, three early developmental stages were obtained under controlled laboratory conditions: recently released spores, settled spores, and 7-day gametophytes (Fig. 1). For each developmental stage, five replicated subsamples were generated. Three subsamples were used for microscopy analyses (FISH and TEM), whereas two subsamples were combined for 16S rRNA gene sequencing, to ensure sufficient DNA yield, resulting in a single sequencing library per sample type.

Surface-cleaning protocol of reproductive tissue (or sori)

The surface-cleaning protocol was designed to reduce surface-associated and epiphytic bacteria. Sori were subjected to 10 s of sonication in an ultrasonic bath (50 kHz, 575T-Crest), followed by brushing with 0.2 µm-filtered seawater using a sterile soft brush. Samples were then immersed in a 0.25% povidone-iodine solution for 5 min and subsequently rinsed with distilled water. This procedure was followed by two additional rounds of sonication and brushing. Sori were then immersed for 30 min in an antibiotic cocktail at 4% (0.16 mg·ml−1 penicillin, 0.4 mg·ml−1 streptomycin and 0.4 mg·ml−1 neomycin), and finally rinsed with 0.2 µm-filtered seawater. The combination of mechanical disruption (sonication and brushing), povidone-ione disinfection and broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail was chosen to target distinct fractions of the microbiota (firmly attached epiphytes and surface biofilms), achieving greater reduction than any single treatment alone. We acknowledge that axenity is rarely achievable in macroalgal tissue, even with combined chemical and antibiotic treatments (Baweja et al. 2009, Wichard 2015 et al. 2015). The effectiveness of povidone-iodine for reducing surface-associated contaminants on macroalgal tissues has been demonstrated in Kappaphycus micropropagation protocols, where 1% solutions applied briefly allow removal of epiphytes without compromising tissue integrity (Ali et al. 2020). Similarly, the antibiotic cocktail lowers bacterial loads while maintaining tissue viability in other kelp species (Vadillo-González et al. 2024). In our study, tissue integrity was evaluated indirectly through TEM imaging of treated sori, which showed intact cell walls, membranes, and organelles, indicating that the cleaning protocol did not cause detectable structural damage (Fig. 6A–F).

Seawater preparation and M. pyrifera culture conditions

All cultures were established using filtered and pasteurized seawater from the collection site to minimize environmental bacterial inputs. No UV treatment was used. Seawater was filtered through sterile 0.2 µm nominal pore-size membrane filters (47 mm diameter, GSWP MF-Millipore, USA) and heated at 80°C for 30 min, cooled to 10°C, and re-filtered through 0.2 µm membrane filters before use. This approach keeps the natural properties of the seawater, particularly by avoiding salt precipitation during more stringent conditions such as autoclaving at 120°C (Minich et al. 2018, Vadillo-González et al. 2024). All culture vessels, filtration units and materials were sterilized, seawater was handled aseptically and batches were not reused across developmental stages. This processed seawater was used throughout the study.

The treated sori were air-dried overnight in closed 500 ml Erlenmeyer flasks under sterile laboratory conditions, to prevent external bacterial contamination. The next day, 200 ml of 0.2 µm-filtered and pasteurized seawater was added to induce sporulation, which occurred within 1 h. A portion of the resulting spore suspension was concentrated and quantified using a Neubauer chamber under a Nikon Optiphot-2 microscope at 20× magnification. For 16S rRNA gene sequencing analyses, released spores were collected on a 0.22 µm cellulose membrane (GSWP MF-Millipore, USA) using a 47 mm Boeco filtration unit. To obtain settled spores and 7-day gametophytes for subsequent analyses, 40 000 spores·ml−1 were seeded into sterile 750 ml-polypropylene flasks. In parallel, spores were seeded into 6-well multiplates containing poly-L-lysine coated coverslips (# 1.5H, 170 µm thickness) for subsequent TEM and FISH analyses.

For the M. pyrifera cultures, a concentration of 5000 spores was seeded in 750 ml cell culture flasks. Cultures were maintained at 12°C, 40 μmol photons m⁻2 s⁻1 irradiance under a 12:12 h light/dark cycle. After 24 h, settled spores were observed, and gametophytes were identified after 7 days of cultivation. For both settled spores and gametophytes, the supernatant was removed and replaced with 50 ml of TBE buffer to preserve bacterial cell integrity and prevent DNA degradation. Samples were sonicated for three cycles of 10 min each at 35 kHz, with an ice pack in the sonicator to maintain a lower temperature and then filtered through a 47 mm polysulfone unit with a 0.22 µm cellulose membrane. Filters containing settled spores and gametophytes were immediately stored at −80°C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and data analysis

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from treated and untreated sori, spores, settled spores, gametophytes, and seawater filters, according to the following protocols. For sori tissue (untreated and treated), 0,5 cm2 of algal material was transferred into a 2 ml PowerBead Pro Tube from the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (Qiagen), and 800 μl of Solution CD1 was added. After a brief vortexing, the tubes were spun in a FastPrep-24 5 G (MPbio) for 40 s at 6 m/s. DNA was then extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocol with a modified initial mechanical lysis step, which was extended to 20 min of vortexing. DNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresis in 1% agarose gel using 1X TAE buffer and stored at −20°C until further analysis.

For the surrounding seawater and developmental phases (spores, settled spores and gametophytes), DNA was extracted from thawed 0.22 µm filters (GSWP MF-Millipore, USA). Half-filters were cut into small pieces under sterile conditions and incubated in a lysis buffer (1X TE, NaCl 0.15 M, 10% SDS, and 20 mg ml−1 of proteinase K) at 37°C for 1 h to facilitate DNA extraction. DNA was extracted at 65°C for 10 min using CTAB buffer (10% CTAB and 0.7% NaCl), and 5 M NaCl, followed by phase separation with phenol-chloroform. DNA was precipitated with isopropanol at −20°C for 1 h, washed twice with 100% ethanol, and resuspended in 30 μl of nuclease-free water. DNA integrity was checked on 1% agarose gel and stored at −20°C until further analysis.

16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing

The 16S gene sequencing approach was used to characterize bacterial community composition and to assess the persistence of specific amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) across developmental stages. The V6-V8 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers B969F and BA1406R (Comeau et al. 2023). PCR reactions (25 µl) were prepared in triplicate and contained: 1X Taq buffer, 2 mM of MgCl2, 0.5 mM dNTPs, 0.5 µM of each primer, 0.5 U GoTaq Flexi DNA Polymerase (Promega), and 1 ng of template DNA. Amplification conditions were 94°C for 3 min; 28 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 57°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 90 s, followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were visualized on 2% agarose gel (TAE 1X buffer) and pooled. PCR products were purified using Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery kit (Zymo Research) and visualized on 2% agarose gel (TAE 1X). Sequencing was performed at Integrated Microbiome Resource (www.imr.bio, Canada) on an Illumina Miseq platform (600 bp paired-end V3 chemistry). Details of amplicon normalization and library pooling are described in Comeau and Kwawukume (2023).

16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis

Raw sequence reads were processed using the open-source package Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm (DADA2) (Callahan et al. 2016) in R Statistical software v 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2008). Demultiplexed fastq files were quality-filtered and trimmed. Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASV) (Callahan et al. 2017) were inferred using the dada function, utilizing error rates learned from the data. Forward and reverse reads were merged, chimeras, mitochondrial, and chloroplast sequences were removed. Taxonomic assignments were conducted using the Silva v138.1 database (Quast et al. 2012), with species-level assignment only for exact matches (Edgar 2018). Sequences of the twelve most abundant unassigned ASVs at the genus level were aligned using the BLAST platform, with blastn tool against the nr/nt database (NCBI 2023). The first cultured bacteria alignment with ≥97% identity was considered for assignment at the genus level.

Rarefaction curves were constructed by random subsampling without replacement across a range of sequencing depths (1 to total reads per sample, 100 iterations per depth). Given the highly unequal sequencing depth across samples -most notably the pronounced rarefaction of the treated sorus fraction to 45 reads, consistent with the effective decontamination-absolute abundances were retained without rarefaction or additional normalization, to avoid discarding ecologically meaningful information. Relative abundances were calculated from these counts and used for visualization and diversity analyses using the Phyloseq package (McMurdie and Holmes 2013) in R software. To explore the exclusive and shared bacterial ASVs across the early developmental stages of M. pyrifera, we used the UpSetR package (Conway et al. 2017). To analyze presence/absence patterns and changes in relative ASV abundances of the most representative ASV across stages, the dataset was filtered. ASVs with ≥ 1% relative abundance in at least one sample were selected, and changes in relative abundances were visualized in a bubble plot. The most abundant ASVs, selected according to these criteria, collectively represented 97.6%, 100%, 99.8%, and 99.4% of the total bacterial community relative abundance in untreated sori, treated sori, released spores, and gametophyte samples, respectively. Visualizations were created using the ggplot2 package (Wickham et al. 2007) in R software.

A source-tracking approach was implemented to assess whether the bacterial assemblage associated with released spores was representative of treated sori or of a differentiated subset potentially shaped by selective processes. The relative contribution of spore-associated bacteria to the gametophyte microbiome was compared to potential inputs from parental sporophytic tissue (sori), or by a fraction not explained by the defined sources, which may represent de novo emergence or uncharacterized sources during germination. Bacterial source tracking was performed in Python using the Fast Expectation-Maximization microbial Source Tracking (FEAST) algorithm (Shenhav et al. 2019), implemented with the NumPy (Harris et al. 2020), Pandas (McKinney 2011), SciPy (Virtanen et al. 2020) and Matplotlib (Hunter 2007) libraries. FEAST models the taxonomic composition of a sink community as a linear mixture of K source communities plus an unknown source, estimating mixing proportions α = (α1,…, α2, αUnknown) via Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm (Shenhav et al. 2019). Raw, non-rarefied integer counts were used as input, consistent with recommendations for datasets with highly unequal sequencing depths (Shenhav et al. 2019). Three experimental designs were evaluated, with the gametophyte community as the invariant sink: (i) three sources: untreated sorus + treated sorus + spores-released, (ii) two sources: untreated sorus + spores-released. To avoid convergence on local optima, each model was run 100 times with random Dirichlet-distributed starting values, and the solution with the highest log-likelihood was selected. The stability of the solution was evaluated by examining the variance of the mixing proportions α across all 100 initializations. Because sequencing was based on a single library per sample type, confidence intervals via bootstrap resampling cannot be computed, and the FEAST estimates should be interpreted as point estimates. All source tracking analyses and visualizations were implemented in Python using a custom EM algorithm validated against the original FEAST outputs.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FISH analyses were performed to assess the spatial localization of bacterial cells in the different phases of the life cycle and reproductive tissues following Ramírez-Puebla et al. (2022) with modifications. Fixed samples from all life stages (sporangium of sori, released spores, settled spores and gametophytes) were dehydrated in 50%, 70%, and 100% ethanol for 10 min each. Briefly, whole-mount agarose embedding was performed by adding 50 µl of standard agarose (melting point 87.7°C) and cooling for 10 min. Hybridization was performed in buffer [900 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 0.01% SDS, 20% (vol/vol) formamide] with 2 µM Eub338-I probe and incubated at 46°C for 2 h in a chamber humidified with 20% (vol/vol) formamide. The Eub338-I probe binds to a conserved region of the 16S rRNA and hybridizes with the majority of known bacterial lineages, while not targeting Archaea or Eukarya (Daims et al. 1999). Negative controls were prepared without adding the probe. After incubation, samples were washed five times with 100 µl of washing buffer (215 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 5 mM EDTA) followed by three washes with 500 µl of the same buffer at 48°C for 5 min each. Samples were left overnight in the dark at room temperature before observations.

All life cycle phases were processed separately and were visualized using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880) with Airyscan Detection and a Plan-Apochromat 63x NA 1.40 Oil immersion objective. An excitation range of 405–514 nm was used to capture images. Pixel dwell (0.66 µs), laser power (4%–65%), and digital gain (1.00) were maintained as fixed acquisition parameters to achieve the optimal fluorophore signal. Z-stack depth (i.e. sample depth) ranged from 7.5 to 19.6 µm, depending on the sample. To obtain a representative dataset, a minimum of five images per phase were processed and analyzed using ZEN Blue Software. To rule out non-specific binding of the Alexa Fluor 532-labeled probe (EUB338) to host organelles such as mitochondria or chloroplasts, we performed BLASTn searches of the probe against the M. pyrifera genome and mitochondrial sequences in GenBank. For this, the probe’s sequence was converted to its reverse complement and uploaded as the query sequence in Blastn. Alignments were evaluated based on the percentage of identity. The highest similarity detected was less than a 50% match, indicating a negligible risk of cross-hybridization with host mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences.

Centroid-based spatial analysis was performed using FIJI/ImageJ to evaluate the spatial distribution of bacterial signals relative to algal autofluorescence and phase. Confocal images (.czi) were opened as hyperstacks with channel separation and autoscaling. The autofluorescence channel (red) was thresholded and converted to a binary mask to generate an autofluorescence mask. The bacterial channel (green) was thresholded to retain clearly distinguishable pixels while minimizing background signal and converted to a binary image. Bacterial objects were detected using the 3D Objects counter plugin with a fixed threshold of 128. A minimum size filter was applied to exclude small noise-derived objects, and the selected value (10 voxels) was chosen based on visual comparison between detected objects and the original bacterial signal. To define the algal structure, regions of interest were manually delineated slice-by-slice on merged channel images using the Z-stack as a visual reference. These regions were used to generate a binary structure mask. Spatial analysis was performed by comparing bacterial centroid positions with the structure mask and autofluorescence mask. Spatial overlap was calculated as the percentage of bacterial centroids located within the structure mask while excluding those overlapping with autofluorescent regions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was used to validate the intracellular or tissue-embedded localization of bacterial cells at ultrastructural resolution. Fixed samples were exposed to 1% aqueous osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 1 h, washed three times in double-distilled water (10 min each), and stained with 2% aqueous uranyl for 1 h. Samples were dehydrated in an acetone series (50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%: 10 min each), followed by 24–48 h infiltration in 1 : 1 mixture of acetone and Spurr resin. After evaporation of residual acetone (minimum 8 h), the samples were embedded, positioned on the label, and filled with air-dried Spurr resin. The samples were left in the oven to polymerize for a minimum of 24 h. Ultrathin sections (90 nm) were obtained with an ultramicrotome (LEICA, ULTRACUT R). TEM images were acquired using a Talos F200C G2 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) operating at 200 kV. A minimum of 5 representative images per sample were acquired.

Results

16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis

Reliable sequencing data were obtained for all samples except for the settled spore stage, which was excluded from downstream analysis due to poor sequencing quality (fewer than 1000 reads per sample and low-quality forward and reverse reads prior to DADA2 processing). Total read counts ranged from 24 704 (seawater) and 27 992 (untreated sori) to 227 000 and 290 000 reads for the remaining samples (see Supplementary Table 1). Rarefaction curves indicated that samples with typical sequencing depth (untreated sorus, released spores, and gametophytes) reached saturation, while the treated sorus, with only 45 reads, also approached saturation, consistent with a low observed diversity (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1)

Bacterial community composition varied markedly across sample types (Fig. 2). Seawater at the sampling site was dominated by members of the classes Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and Bacteroidia. In contrast, untreated sori harbored a community dominated by Alphaproteobacteria, Bacteroidia, and Verrucomicrobiae (Fig. 2). Treated sori displayed a pronounced reduction in bacterial richness, with only ASVs belonging to Alphaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia detected (Fig. 2). In released spores, Rhodothermia appeared for the first time and co-occurred with high abundances of Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia (Fig. 2). In gametophytes, the pattern persists but with variation in the relative abundance of dominant classes (Fig. 2).

Stages include untreated sori, treated sori, released spores, and 7-day gametophytes. Only bacterial classes with ≥1% average relative abundance are shown; low-abundance ASVs (<1%) are grouped.

Comparative analysis of ASV richness and exclusivity across developmental stages of M. pyrifera revealed distinct community profiles (Fig. 3). This compositional variation was reflected in the number of ASVs detected; seawater contained the highest ASV richness (326 ASVs), followed by untreated sori (191 ASVs). The treated sori displayed a drastically reduced ASV richness, with only four ASVs. Surprisingly, released spores had 45 identified ASVs, while 30 ASVs were detected in gametophytes (Fig. 3).

Upset plot showing exclusive and shared bacterial ASVs across M. pyrifera early developmental stages. The lower panel displays the total number of ASVs per stage. Vertical bars represent the number of ASVs exclusive to each stage or shared between combinations of stages. Bar values indicate the total number of ASVs exclusive or shared between the combinations.

Most ASVs detected in untreated sori (174 of 191 ASVs) were exclusive to this stage. Of the four ASVs found in treated sori, three were shared with the untreated sori and belong to Dokdonia (ASV0017, which was also present in the released spores) and Granulosicoccus (ASV0114 and ASV0205), a common macroalgal symbiont (Singh and Reddy 2014, Brunet et al. 2025). The remaining ASV (ASV0310), also belonging to the Granulosicoccus genus, was exclusive to the treated sori (Fig. 4).

Bubble plot showing the relative abundance of the most abundant bacterial ASV (defined as ASVs with ≥1% relative abundance in at least one sample) across the different developmental stages of M. pyrifera. Together, these ASVs account for more than 97% of the total bacterial community relative abundance within each developmental stage. Both released spores and gametophytes exhibited substantial numbers of unique ASVs (15 and 13, respectively). They also shared 14 ASVs, suggesting that ASV turnover and nestedness contributed equally to the changes in community composition between stages. In other words, approximately half of the predominant ASVs present in the gametophyte stage were persistent (inherited from earlier stages), while the other half were undetected in previous stages. Additionally, the untreated sori shared 11 ASVs with the released spores, while only one ASV (ASV0290) was shared between untreated sori and gametophytes. Two ASVs, Balneola alkaliphila (ASV0004) and Marinoscillum luteum (ASV0009), were common to all three early developmental stages, except in treated sori (Fig. 3).

Analysis of dominant ASVs revealed that a few taxa accounted for the majority of community composition at each developmental stage (Fig. 4). In untreated sori, dominant ASVs included Litorimonas (Alphaproteobacteria, ASV0007: 22% and ASV0018: 4.5%), Rubritalea ASVs (Verrucomicrobiae, ASV0012: 10% and ASV0015: 5.1%), Granulosicoccus (Gammaproteobacteria, ASV0016: 5%), and Dokdonia (Bacteroidia, ASV0017: 4.7%), which together represented over 50% of the relative abundance. In treated sori, the community was represented by three Granulosicoccus ASVs (ASV0114: 20%, ASV0205: 42%, and ASV0310: 31%), and one Dokdonia ASV0017 present at 6.7% relative abundance. Released spores were dominated by ASVs from Marinomonas (Gammaproteobacteria, ASV0003: 43% and ASV0013: 7%), Bacterioplanoides (Gammaproteobacteria, ASV0005: 24%), Balneola alkaliphila (Rhodothermia, ASV0004: 11%), and Marinoscillum (Bacteroidia, ASV0009: 9%), which together represented over 93% of the relative abundance. In gametophytes, a similar taxonomic composition was observed. Balneola alkaliphila (ASV0004) was the most abundant (59%), followed by Marinoscillum (ASV0009: 9%), Marinomonas (ASV0003: 9%), and Bacterioplanoides (ASV0005: 4%, ASV0028: 3%, and ASV0029: 3%), which together accounted for ∼87% of the gametophyte phase bacterial community (Fig. 4).

Overall, these results show that dominant bacterial genera shifted throughout the early development, from Litorimonas (Alphaproteobacteria) and Rubritalea (Verrucomicrobiae) in untreated sori, to Granulosicoccus (Gammaproteobacteria) in treated sori, Marinomonas (Gammaproteobacteria) and Bacterioplanoides (Gammaproteobacteria) in spores, and Balneola (Rhodothermia) in gametophytes (Fig. 4).

FEAST source tracking indicated that the bacterial community associated with released spores accounted for 91.4% of the estimated gametophyte bacteriome, with the remaining 8.6% attributed to an unknown source (Fig. 5A). Of the 30 ASVs detected in the gametophyte, 16 (53%) were also present in the released spore fraction, collectively accounting for 92.7% of total gametophyte reads (Fig. 5B). The remaining 14 ASVs (47%), representing 7.3% of gametophyte reads, were absent from spores and likely represent de novo assembled taxa (Fig. 5B). The soral fractions, both untreated and treated, contributed negligible proportions to the spore’s bacterial community (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the EM algorithm converged to identical solutions across all 100 independent initializations (standard deviation of α = 0.00% for all sources), indicating that the likelihood of surface has a single global optimum for this dataset, and that the result is not a consequence of initialization bias. Among the shared ASVs, a single ASV belonging to the genus Balneola (ASV0004) showed a markedly disproportionate relative abundance in the gametophyte (∼60%) compared to its occurrence in released spores (∼10%), suggesting selective enrichment of this taxon during vertical transmission and early gametophyte development. In contrast, three single ASVs belonging to Marinomonas (ASV0003), Marinoscillum (ASV0009), and an unknown member of the family Saccharospirillaceae (Bacterioplanoides ASV0005) displayed comparable relative abundances in both the spore and gametophyte fractions, consistent with faithful, proportional vertical transmission (Fig. 5B). These taxon-specific patterns corroborate the compositional shifts documented in the ASV overlap (Fig. 3) and abundance analyses (Fig. 4), supporting a scenario in which the gametophyte microbiome is vertically inherited from spores but is subsequently shaped by stage-specific selective processes.

FISH—imaging analysis

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) confirmed the presence of bacterial signals across all examined life stages of M. pyrifera (Fig. 6). Green, fluorescent pixels corresponding to bacterial signals were clearly distinguishable from the diffuse red autofluorescence associated with secondary algal pigments such as fucoxanthin, a carotenoid accessory pigment in brown algae with absorption between 400–540 nm (Zailanie and Purnomo 2017).

The proportion of bacterial centroids located within the algal structure and not overlapping with autofluorescent regions was 65.4% in untreated sori, 33.3% in released spores, and 8.0% in gametophytes. To support the effect of the cleaning protocol, untreated sori showing clear surface-associated bacterial signals are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2, indicating a reduction of detectable bacterial after treatment.

TEM ultrastructural analysis

To confirm the intracellular bacterial localization observed via FISH, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on early developmental stages of M. pyrifera. This analysis provided high-resolution insights into the ultrastructure and bacterial associations across different tissues (Figs. 7 and 8).

Soral tissue exhibited sporangia densely packed with meiospores, each containing multiple chloroplasts and a centrally located nucleus (Fig. 7A, B). Organelles such as the nucleus (N), Golgi bodies (G), and chloroplasts (C) appeared structurally intact and well-organized, with normal ultrastructure (Fig. 7B–H). Intercellular connections between individual cells, special areas containing plasmodesmata (i.e. channels that pass through the cell wall (CW) and connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells), were observed between neighboring cells, suggesting active cytoplasmic communication (Fig. 7C). In gametophyte cells, organelles remained clearly distinguishable, with nuclei, Golgi bodies, mitochondria (M), and chloroplasts (C) maintaining typical morphology (Fig. 7F–H). Chloroplasts in gametophytes were often positioned peripherally within the cells, a spatial arrangement consistent with early developmental stages.

TEM also revealed the presence of bacteria, both intracellularly and extracellularly at various life stages of M. pyrifera (Fig. 8). Extracellular bacteria were embedded within the mucilaginous layer covering the cuticle of vegetative lamina tissue (Fig. 8A) and within the cuticle of the sporangia (Fig. 8B). Intracellular bacteria, i.e. bacteria located within the compartment delimited by the cell wall when the cell membrane was not evident, were observed in the gametophyte (Fig. 8C–F). In each of them, bacterial cells were typically spherical, enclosed by a clearly defined double membrane, and exhibited a finely granular internal texture.

Surrounding the intracellular bacteria, several host-derived membrane-bound vesicles (V) were observed (Fig. 8E, F). These vesicles displayed heterogeneous electron densities and irregular sizes and were frequently located in close proximity to the bacterial cells. Notably, the host cell walls remained intact, indicating that bacteria did not compromise cellular integrity.

Discussion

The vertical transmission of bacterial associates across generations can be seen as a form of inheritance, whereby the host passes on part of its microbiota to its progeny. This process can occur in different ways, including the presence of symbionts within host cells (intracellular) or embedded within extracellular matrices such as the cell wall or cuticle, as well as being attached to the cell surface. When released from the sporangium, kelp spores can eventually be in contact with bacteria from the sporophyll’s surface and therefore settle and develop as a gametophyte bearing a sample of bacteria obtained from its parental sporophyte. In the present study, most of the bacteria from sporophyll’s surface were removed, as evidenced by the drastic reduction in the number of ASVs associated with the treated sori. However, many ASVs were detected in association with released spores, including ASVs that were not detected in the untreated thalli. Notably, Balneola alkaliphila (Rhodothermia, ASV0004) and Marinoscillum luteum (Bacteroidia, ASV0009), initially present at low abundance in untreated sori and absent from treated sori, became dominant during later stages. Considering that amplicon sequencing methods preferentially recover the most abundant organisms and can underrepresent rare taxa (Qin et al. 2023), these results imply that kelp spores can be associated with a broad spectrum of bacterial taxa, including those present at very low abundance (i.e. below amplicon sequencing detection limits) in their parental tissue. Amplicon sequencing detection limits are on the order of ∼0.02%–0.1% of total community biomass in aquatic assemblages (Hatzenbuhler et al. 2017), which roughly corresponds to the presence of tens of bacterial cells (∼10–100 cells per ml of sample), depending on sequencing depth and data processing (Brandt and Albertsen 2018).

Given the relative dimensions of kelp spores (∼5–8 µm in diameter) and typical marine bacteria (0.2–2 µm), each spore can bear only a few bacterial cells on its surface. Our results, therefore, suggest that a large number of released spores may be required to capture the large bacterial diversity identified at this stage. Some bacteria may have been released into seawater during spore discharge and subsequently retained on filters alongside spores. If so, we would expect a bacterial profile resembling that of treated sori, and partially reflecting the untreated sori community, as evidenced in Phyllospora comosa and Delisea pulchra (Syukur et al. 2023) and in early sporelings of Saccharina japonica (Zhuang et al. 2024). While these studies did not directly assess vertical transmission, the observed taxonomic continuity suggests that core bacteria persist across life stages. In our study, however, three of the four dominant ASVs on treated sori prior to release were absent from the spore-associated fraction. These compositional shifts point to selective bacterial recruitment by spores, not passive co-capture, as documented in Hormosira banksii (Syukur et al. 2023). Consequently, spores seem to be a key component in promoting the transgenerational persistence of kelp’s associated bacterial communities. The persistence of at least a subset of parental host-associated bacteria aligns with established models of vertical transmission observed in diverse host-microorganism systems (Truyens et al. 2015, Leite et al. 2017, van Oppen and Blackall 2019), but appears rare in marine organisms (Russell 2019). Evidence of vertical transmission in macroalgae remains scarce. The only known case of likely vertical transmission in green seaweeds to date revealed the presence of intracellular bacteria in the gametes of Bryopsis hypnoides (Burr and West 1970). Here we provide initial evidence supporting vertical bacterial inheritance from spores to gametophyte transition in a macroalga. In other host systems, the seed or birth-associated inoculum dominates early-colonizer communities, with 30%–60% vertical transfer rates, depending on host species and methodology (Ferretti et al. 2018, Bergna et al. 2018, Shahzad et al. 2022). The substantially higher vertical contribution estimated here (91.4%) may reflect the controlled laboratory conditions under which gametophytes were cultured, effectively minimizing horizontal environmental inputs, and/07 the selective pressure exerted by the spore surface on bacterial recruitment during meiospore formation.

Although our observations do not identify specific endosymbiotic bacteria, the persistence of bacterial cells through reproductive structures shares features with mechanisms described in fungal models, where vertically transmitted endobacteria have been observed within spores of Endogone spp. (Desirò et al. 2015), Gigaspora spp. (Bianciotto et al. 2004), and Rhizopus spp. (Partida-Martinez et al. 2007), and in the cytoplasm of related taxa (Ghignone et al. 2012). In contrast, mixed modes of transmission (i.e. combining vertical inheritance with environmental acquisition) are common in marine invertebrates such as sponges, bivalves, tubeworms, and gutless annelids (Bright and Bulgheresi 2010, Webster and Taylor 2012, Carrier et al. 2022). Such parallels suggest that M. pyrifera may follow a similar pattern, in which bacterial inheritance is partial and environmentally reinforced rather than strictly vertical. In this context, vertical-like retention through reproductive structures may ensure early colonization of released spores, whereas horizontal inputs during or shortly after spore release could replenish or diversify bacterial associations in developing gametophytes.

The combined application of FISH and TEM confirms bacterial localization across M. pyrifera developmental stages. FISH provided evidence of internal bacterial signals across life stages, while TEM validated their intracellular localization at ultrastructural resolution. Strong, punctate FISH signals were consistently observed in spores and gametophytes, supporting the presence of intracellular bacteria. Importantly, no bacterial signal was detected from the surface of treated sori, suggesting a reduction in detectable bacterial signal following the cleaning protocol. TEM further revealed intracellular bacteria within gametophyte cytoplasm, characterized by a distinct double membrane and granular cytoplasm, with no signs of host membrane disruption, suggesting that these bacterial cells are not associated with cell death. Instead, their membrane integrity and close proximity to host organelles are consistent with the interpretation that they may represent viable symbionts. In addition, extracellular bacteria were observed embedded within the cuticle of reproductive sori. However, while FISH images suggested bacterial signals close to plastids (based on spatial overlap with regions of red autofluorescence), this association was not supported by TEM observations. This discrepancy may reflect methodological limitations: TEM examines ultrathin sections that provide very high ultrastructural resolution but lack broader dimensional spatial context, whereas confocal microscopy used in FISH captures distribution of fluorescent signals but is limited by optical resolution and diffraction, which can produce apparent colocalizations when dense organelles (e.g. plastids) dominate the cytoplasm (e.g. comparative microscopy studies) (Yemc 2020, Elliott 2020, Butola et al. 2025).

The presence of both intracellular bacteria within gametophyte cells and extracellular bacteria embedded in the sporangial cuticle suggests that M. pyrifera may employ multiple mechanisms of early bacterial inheritance, potentially involving both intracellular retention and extracellular association with reproductive structures such as the sporangial cuticle. Similar dual strategies have been documented in insects, where even intracellular symbionts can temporarily transition to an extracellular state during transmission (Caspi-Fluger et al. 2012). It could also explain the apparent specificity of spore-bacteria associations discussed above. Such bacterial inheritance may be functionally significant for host development, as vertically transmitted microbiota in other macroalgal systems contribute to host nutrition, defense, and morphogenesis (Vandenkoornhuyse et al. 2015, Syukur et al. 2023).

Notably, bacterial composition remains relatively stable during the spore-to-gametophyte transition, with only minor changes among low-abundance taxa. Fourteen ASVs were detected exclusively in spores and gametophytes, possibly reflecting bacterial reactivation or community re-assembly during development (Burke et al. 2011, Debray et al. 2021). A more granular picture emerges from an ASV-level analysis of gametophyte community composition relative to its spore source. Of the 30 ASVs detected in the gametophyte, 16 (53.3%) were also present in the spore fraction; however, these shared ASVs accounted for 92.7% of total gametophyte reads. Notably, not all vertically transmitted ASVs maintained constant relative abundances across the transition. Balneola alkaliphila (Bacteroidota), the dominant gametophyte taxon (59.0% of reads), was present in spores at only 11.0% relative abundance, an approximately five-fold enrichment during germination. Conversely, Marinomonas spp. (Proteobacteria), which dominated the spore fraction (43.0%), declined to 9.3% in the gametophyte. This selective amplification of specific Nacteroidota lineages during gametophyte development parallels observations in the kelp Ecklonia radiata, where the gametophyte microbiota was dominated by Bacteroidota and showed higher taxonomic similarity to the adult sporophyte than to the ambient seawater (Veenhof et al. 2025). Such processes have been described in other host-associated systems, where bacterial communities undergo restructuring in response to host ontogeny, tissue differentiation, or environmental cues (Longo et al. 2015, Fieth et al. 2016, Engelen et al. 2018, Prest et al. 2018). For instance, in marine invertebrates and plants, dormant or low-abundance symbionts can become active during specific developmental stages, contributing to functional shifts in the holobiont microbiome (Egan et al. 2013, Lemoine et al. 2020, Dumbrell et al. 2020).

Finally, our results highlight that the cleaning protocol applied to reproductive sori, while effective at reducing bacterial load, did not fully eliminate all the bacteria. This finding is particularly relevant for understanding vertical transmission, as it implies that even under conditions designed to suppress microbiota, some symbionts still reach the next generation. Additionally, the antibiotic cocktail used targets mainly bacteria, and does not remove fungal associates, which may continue interacting with the residual microbiome and potentially influence subsequent colonization dynamics (Koch and Durako 1991). To support the interpretation of intracellular bacterial signals observed in this study, it is important to note that comparable confocal FISH approaches have successfully revealed intracellular symbionts in other marine holobionts, such as corals (Caspi-Fluger et al. 2012), reinforcing the suitability of this method for spatial localization of host-associated microbiota. Nevertheless, conventional FISH is widely regarded as a qualitative or, at best, semi-quantitative method, because signal strength and the number of detectable puncta depend on cellular rRNA content, probe accessibility and permeabilization efficiency rather than directly reflecting cell numbers (Daims et al. 1999, Pernthaler et al. 2002, Amann and Fuchs 2008). Accordingly, in this study the overlap metric is used only to describe spatial relationships between bacterial signals and plastid-rich regions, not to estimate bacterial load.

Conclusion

By combining microscopy and 16S rRNA sequencing approaches under controlled laboratory conditions, we observed that specific bacterial taxa persist across early developmental stages of M. pyrifera. These results suggest that environmental acquisition alone may not fully explain the observed bacterial assemblages and instead support an early bacterial inheritance, via either intracellular retention or extracellular association with reproductive tissues. Microbial source tracking confirmed that the vast majority of the gametophyte microbiome can be attributed to spore-associated bacteria. While not all sources of contamination can be fully excluded, the recurrence detection of specific ASVs under sterile conditions, together with direct visualization of intracellular bacteria, reinforces the hypothesis that certain symbionts are transferred across generations. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of algal holobiont dynamics and provide a foundation for future research into the ecological and functional significance of inherited bacteria in macroalgal development.

Nevertheless, unequivocal demonstrations of vertical transmission in macroalgae remain methodologically challenging. The limited resolution of fluorescence in situ hybridization techniques, the minute size of spores, and potential artifacts introduced during surface cleaning or bacterial load reduction constrain the inference of true parental continuity. Advances in molecular tracers and correlative microscopy will be essential to confirm whether these associations are genuinely inherited. Future studies integrating genomic and functional assays will be required to determine whether these inherited bacteria contribute to host development, stress tolerance, or defense, thereby clarifying their ecological role within the Macrocystis holobiont.
