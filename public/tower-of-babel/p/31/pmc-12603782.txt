Interactive effects of blood–brain barrier breakdown and Alzheimer's disease biomarker status on cognitive decline in older adults without dementia
Authors: Lauren Edwards, Amanda I. Gonzalez, Kelsey R. Thomas, Denis S. Smirnov, Einat K. Brenner, Daniel A. Nation, Fini Chang, Kaj Blennow, David P. Salmon, Douglas Galasko, Katherine J. Bangen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSome research suggests that blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity is altered in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Few studies have examined markers of BBB integrity and their interactions with AD risk factors on multi‐domain cognition.METHODS83 older adults without dementia underwent lumbar puncture, apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping, and neuropsychological testing with up to 5 years of follow‐up. Soluble platelet‐derived growth factor receptor beta (sPDGFRβ) was measured in cerebrospinal fluid.RESULTSHigher baseline sPDGFRβ was associated with better cross‐sectional attention, visuospatial, and executive functioning, but with steeper longitudinal decline in executive function. INTRODUCTION

There is growing recognition of vascular contributions to Alzheimer's disease (AD). Recent criteria proposed by the Alzheimer's Association Workgroup 1 suggest biomarkers of vascular pathologies such as white matter hyperintensities on neuroimaging be considered in the diagnostic framework that focuses on amyloid and tau, allowing for a multimodal biomarker profile that acknowledges co‐pathologies. It is possible that even subtler cerebrovascular changes, such as reduced blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, may be a sensitive marker of AD that occurs early in the disease or may contribute to decline in individuals harboring AD pathology.

The neurovascular unit (NVU) is a complex structure of neurons, vascular cells, and glial cells 2 reciprocally linked to each other functionally and anatomically to form an efficient system for regulating cerebral blood flow. 3 , 4 Structures of the NVU play a role in maintenance of the BBB 5 and the connection between the cerebral nervous system and the peripheral blood circulation. 6 The BBB acts as a selective permeability barrier protecting the brain from external pathogens, serving as a clearance mechanism, and maintaining central nervous system homeostasis. 5 , 6 BBB disruption is associated with impaired clearance and accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ). 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Animal studies have shown that BBB dysfunction occurs early in models of AD, before onset of Aβ deposition and cognitive impairment, 11 , 13 suggesting that BBB markers may have utility as a vascular biomarker in early AD.

Pericytes are NVU vascular cells involved in regulating capillary blood flow, clearing amyloid from the brain, and maintaining the integrity of the BBB. 6 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Soluble platelet‐derived growth factor receptor beta (sPDGFRβ) is released by pericytes in response to harmful stimuli and may act as a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker of pericyte degeneration or damage and as an indicator of altered BBB integrity. 18 Indeed, studies have reported that sPDGFRβ is correlated with hippocampal advanced dynamic contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE‐MRI), 19 , 20 a measure used to assess regional BBB permeability. 21 , 22 , 23 Similarly, studies have reported positive associations between CSF sPDGFRβ and CSF/plasma albumin ratio (QAlb), another biomarker of BBB breakdown. 5 , 6 , 20 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 However, findings vary by clinical subgroups, 26 , 27 and other studies reported no association. 28 , 29 Overall, results suggest that CSF sPDGFRβ may be a relatively accessible and novel marker relating to BBB function; however, additional research is needed to further elucidate its role in AD.

CSF sPDGFRβ levels are increased in patients with AD, 28 but findings are conflicting in terms of its associations with AD biomarkers and risk factors. While some studies found no association between CSF levels of sPDGFRβ and amyloid as measured via positron emission tomography (PET), 5 CSF Aβ42, 28 or both, 30 others showed mixed results depending on the specific biomarker or population studied. 6 , 26 , 27 , 29 , 31 , 32 Several studies have reported that sPDGFRβ is positively associated with total tau and phosphorylated tau in CSF among at least some samples or subgroups examined 6 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 and with cortical tau PET standardized uptake value ratio. 33 However, another study showed no association between sPDGFRβ and tau PET. 5 Similarly, one study found that CSF sPDGFRβ levels were higher in apolipoprotein E (APOE) ɛ4 carriers relative to APOE ɛ3 homozygotes among participants with normal cognition or early cognitive symptoms, 24 although findings vary 5 , 27 dependent upon clinical characteristics. Differences in participant characteristics and/or assay methods may contribute to mixed findings.

Few studies have investigated associations between sPDGFRβ and cognition. Nation and colleagues showed an association between higher sPDGFRβ levels and early cognitive dysfunction as defined by the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) or impairment on one or more cognitive domains on neuropsychological assessment. 20 Findings were independent of CSF Aβ1‐42 and/or p‐tau biomarker status, suggesting BBB breakdown may be an independent and early biomarker of cognitive dysfunction. 20 However, few studies have examined the effect of sPDGFRβ on multi‐domain cognition or interactions with AD biomarker positivity and APOE ɛ4 status, which is important given associations may differ across risk groups.

Given that the relationship between sPDGFRβ, AD risk, and cognition remains unclear, this study aimed to examine whether sPDGFRβ a) was associated with cognition cross‐sectionally and/or longitudinally and b) interacted with AD biomarker or APOE ɛ4 status to impact cognition in a well‐characterized sample of older adults without dementia.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were 83 older adults enrolled in the ongoing longitudinal study of the University of California, San Diego (UCSD) Shiley‐Marcos Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC). Participants were identified as cognitively unimpaired (CU) or diagnosed as having mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by consensus of a multidisciplinary team comprised of two neurologists and a neuropsychologist based on results of the ADRC evaluation at the time of CSF collection used for sPDGFRβ analysis (considered baseline for the present analyses). CSF biomarker results were not used in making the ADRC diagnosis. Individuals diagnosed with dementia at the time of CSF collection were not included in the present study. Additional inclusion criteria for the ADRC include stable health status, availability of a study partner, and no history of major stroke, neurologic disorders, severe psychiatric illness, substance abuse, or learning disability. For the present analyses, availability of the sPDGFRβ marker and of at least 1 neuropsychological assessment was further required for inclusion in analyses. The current sample contained 62 CU and 21 MCI participants.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Existing literature was reviewed using PubMed and Google Scholar. Previous research found that blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption increases risk of dementia, but few studies examined the impact of BBB integrity and its interactions with Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk factors and biomarkers on multi‐domain cognition. Interpretation: Cross‐sectional findings showed that elevated soluble platelet‐derived growth factor receptor beta (sPDGFRβ) was related to better baseline cognition, though longitudinal results showed that elevated sPDGFRβ was associated with steeper cognitive decline, particularly among individuals who are AD biomarker positive or apolipoprotein E (APOE) ɛ4 carriers. Markers of BBB disruption may be useful in detecting risk of cognitive decline and potential therapeutic targets to prevent or delay decline. Future directions: Future studies need to replicate the observed associations in a more representative sample of older adults. Ongoing research focused on sPDGFRβ and other vascular biomarkers could facilitate early identification of individuals at risk of AD and inform personalized therapies.

Biomarker acquisition and analysis

Lumbar punctures were performed after overnight fasting to obtain CSF samples (15–25 mL) as previously described. 34 , 35 Samples were aliquoted into 500 µL fractions in polypropylene microtubes, snap‐frozen, stored at −80 °C until assayed, and analyzed for AD biomarkers on an automated Lumipulse platform using immunoassays developed with specific monoclonal antibodies (Fujirebio Inc.). Amyloid status was determined by Lumipulse‐derived Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, which has been cleared by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to aid in the diagnosis of AD. 36 Amyloid positivity was defined using a cutoff of <0.056 derived from mixture model analysis that included CSF samples from 525 CU, MCI, and AD participants using previously described methods. 37 The current sample contained 26 biomarker‐positive and 57 biomarker‐negative participants.

Sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to measure CSF sPDGFRβ levels in the Neurochemistry lab at the University of Gothenburg (Invitrogen Cat. no. EHPDGFRB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, UK) as previously described. 28 Undiluted CSF samples were loaded. Absorbance was read at 450 nM in a FLUOstar OPTIMA plate reader (BMG Labtech, Aylesbury, UK). For each case, PDGFRβ concentration was calculated by interpolation against the standard curve derived from serial dilutions of recombinant PDGFRβ (18,000‐24 pg/mL).

Blood was collected by venipuncture in the early morning in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacutainer tube and centrifuged within 30 min or less at 4°C and 3500 × g for 10 min, then plasma is aliquoted into 500 uL fractions into polypropylene Lo‐Bind tubes, snap‐frozen, and stored at −80°C until analyzed.

APOE genotyping was performed using a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐based method. 38 A buffy coat was pipetted, snap‐frozen, and stored at −80°C until analyzed. The present study used APOE data provided by NCRAD, using the following method (from their website): NCRAD extracts DNA and generates a 96 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genetic fingerprint (StandardBiotools Juno 96.96 IFC/BioMarkHD; more information on our validation and SOPs is available by request) for each participant banked in the repository. This SNP fingerprint is generated immediately following extraction and when samples are distributed. As part of this fingerprint, APOE haplotypes are generated from the APOE SNPs, rs7412 and rs429358, which are validated by running the same primers in duplicate. APOE ɛ4 status was characterized by the absence or presence of at least 1 ɛ4 allele, and the current sample contained 33 ɛ4‐positive and 50 ɛ4‐negative participants.

Demographic and Vascular Health Assessment

Pulse pressure was calculated by subtracting diastolic from systolic blood pressure. Self‐reported history of diabetes, atrial fibrillation, transient ischemic attack, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and current smoking (last 30 days) was collected from the Uniform Data Set (UDS) Subject Health History form that was completed closest to baseline (Mdate interval = 0.125 years, Rangedate interval: 0–3.18 years) in order to characterize our sample in terms of vascular health. Age was calculated as the number of days between the lumbar puncture collection date and the 15th of the reported birth month and year, divided by 365.25.

Neuropsychological Assessment and Cognitive Status

Participants completed annual clinical, neurologic, and neuropsychological evaluations as part of the UCSD ADRC longitudinal study. Participants completed neuropsychological testing in their preferred language of English or Spanish. Neuropsychological testing was administered to participants by a trained examiner using standardized protocols. Longitudinal analyses included testing that occurred up to 5 years following baseline.

Cognitive composite scores were calculated after converting raw neuropsychological test scores to z‐scores based on means and standard deviations from a reference sample of 139 robust normal controls, defined previously (mean age 74.08 years; mean education 16.91 years; 55.4% female). 34 The reference group overlapped with the present analytic samples (n = 30) and was missing 4.6% of the cognitive data overall. Cognitive test z‐scores were averaged across five cognitive domains to create composite measures for memory, executive function, language, visuospatial function, and attention. Using a data‐driven approach, tests were selected to represent a given cognitive domain based on the highest loading on components identified through a previously reported principal component analysis (see Table 1). 34 Missing data were handled such that composite domain Z‐scores were only generated if scores on at least half of the tests that made up the domain were available (i.e., nonmissing).

Statistical analysis

Baseline bivariate associations between CSF sPDGFRβ concentration and participant demographic and vascular health characteristics were analyzed using Pearson correlation or χ2 tests (Fisher's exact tests if any expected cell count < 5).

Cross‐sectional analyses used linear regression models to examine the association between CSF sPDGFRβ concentration and each cognitive domain composite score. Covariates included baseline age, sex, education, and pulse pressure. Longitudinal analyses used mixed effects models to examine associations between baseline CSF sPDGFRβ concentration and longitudinal decline (up to 5 years) on each cognitive domain score (sPDGFRβ by time interaction), adjusting for baseline age, sex, education, and pulse pressure. Additional models examined whether CSF AD biomarker (±) or APOE ɛ4 status (±) moderated associations between sPDGFRβ and cognitive decline over 5 years (three‐way interaction of sPDGFRβ, time, and CSF AD biomarker or APOE ɛ4 status with all lower‐order interaction terms included), also adjusting for baseline age, sex, education, and pulse pressure. For all longitudinal analyses, time was assessed using annual visit number, which was centered to the baseline collection of the CSF sample that was utilized for sPDGFRβ analysis. Time was treated as a continuous variable. Random intercept and slope were included. Full information maximum likelihood was used. All continuous predictor variables were centered to the baseline mean within the full sample (n = 83). Models were assessed for extreme residual outliers (> 5 SD), and if identified, the analysis was repeated while excluding the outlier. SPSS Statistics Version 29.0.2.0 was used to run analyses. RStudio 2023.06.0+421 was used for visualization.

Baseline associations of sPDGFRβ with demographic and clinical characteristics

CSF concentration of sPDGFRβ was positively associated with baseline age (r = 0.229, p = 0.037). There was no significant association between sPDGFRβ concentration and education, sex, baseline diagnosis, baseline AD biomarker status, or APOE ɛ4 status (ps > 0.05).

Availability of Longitudinal Cognitive Data

The number of participants who had an available Z‐score for each domain at each annual evaluation is reported in Table S1. Participants with (n = 39) or without (n = 44) cognitive composite data at visit 5 did not differ significantly in age, years of education, or distributions of sex, diagnosis, AD biomarker status, or APOE ɛ4 status. This suggests that missing cognitive composite data at visit 5 was not systematically related to the examined demographic and clinical variables.

Cross‐sectional association between CSF sPDGFRβ and cognition

Across the entire sample, higher sPDGFRβ concentration in CSF was associated with better executive function (β = 0.002, 95% confidence interval [CI] [3.876 × 10−4, 0.004]; p = 0.017), visuospatial function (β = 0.003, 95%CI [0.001, 0.005]; p = 0.009), and attention (β = 0.003, 95%CI [4.720 × 10−4, 0.005]; p = 0.018) (Figure 1). There was no association between sPDGFRβ level and baseline memory or language function (ps > 0.05).

Moderation of cross‐sectional associations by AD biomarker or APOE ɛ4 status

There was no interaction effect between sPDGFRβ level and AD biomarker status or APOE ɛ4 status on baseline performance in any cognitive domain (p > 0.05).

Longitudinal association between CSF sPDGFRβ and cognition

Higher sPDGFRβ concentration at baseline was significantly associated with steeper decline in executive function (β = −0.001, 95%CI [−0.001, −4.766 × 10−6]; p = 0.048), but not with decline in other cognitive domains (p > 0.05). Results did not change when one extreme residual outlier (>5 SD) was identified and removed from the longitudinal executive function analyses.

Moderation of longitudinal associations by AD biomarker or APOE ɛ4 status

There was a significant interaction between AD biomarker status and CSF sPDGFRβ level on decline in memory (β = −0.001, 95% CI [−0.002, −3.357 × 10−4]; p = 0.011), executive function (β = −0.002, 95% CI [−0.003, −4.812 × 10−4]; p = 0.005), language (β = −0.001, 95% CI [−0.002, −4.506 × 10−4]; p = 0.005), visuospatial function (β = −0.002, 95% CI [−0.003, −1.665 × 10−5]; p = 0.048), and attention (β = −0.001, 95% CI [−0.002, −4.098 × 10−4]; p = 0.006). In each case, higher sPDGFRβ levels were more strongly associated with steeper cognitive decline in AD biomarker‐positive individuals than in AD biomarker‐negative individuals (Figure 2). There was also an interaction between APOE ɛ4 status and CSF sPDGFRβ level on decline in language (β = −0.001, 95%CI [−0.002, −9.463 × 10−5]; p = 0.032) such that higher sPDGFRβ levels were more strongly associated with decline in ɛ4‐positive than ɛ4‐negative individuals (Figure 3). Results of these analyses did not change when one extreme residual outlier (> 5 SD) was identified and removed from the longitudinal executive function analyses.

DISCUSSION

We found that a higher level of sPDGFRβ in CSF of older adults without dementia was cross‐sectionally associated with better performance in executive function, visuospatial function, and attention, but longitudinally associated with steeper decline in executive function over the next several years. Furthermore, the relationship between CSF sPDGFRβ and cognitive decline was moderated by AD risk. Higher sPDGFRβ was associated with steeper decline across all cognitive domains in AD biomarker‐positive than in AD biomarker‐negative individuals and with steeper decline in language in APOE ɛ4‐positive than in ɛ4‐negative individuals. These results suggest that BBB disruption marked by higher levels of sPDGFRβ in CSF may play a complex role in the cognitive integrity of older adults without dementia.

Although some previous studies found no significant association between CSF sPDGFRβ and clinical characteristics, 5 , 6 , 29 several found that higher levels of sPDGFRβ are associated with worse cognition, faster cognitive decline, or the presence of AD. 19 , 20 , 24 , 25 , 27 , 28 In contrast to these findings, our cross‐sectional analyses showed that higher CSF sPDGFRβ was associated with better cognitive performance across most domains assessed. Another study similarly reported this unexpected finding with an inverted‐U shape relationship between CSF sPDGFRβ and cross‐sectional level of dementia severity as measured by CDR. 26 Specifically, sPDGFRβ was higher in a CDR 0.5 group relative to a CDR 0 group, but sPDGFRβ was lower in a CDR 1‐2 group relative to CDR 0.5. The authors hypothesized that this may be due to a reduction in overall pericyte number in later stages of the AD continuum. Consistent with this possibility, Zeng and colleagues reported decreases over time in sPDGFRβ measured in blood among individuals who had positive tau biomarkers. 39 Although our study involved individuals at early clinical stages, it is possible that overall pericyte level at differing stages played a role for our cross‐sectional findings.

Although speculative, our unexpected cross‐sectional results may also be related to the influence of pericyte injury on vascular remodeling. Although pericyte injury is often considered detrimental, there are circumstances where it could be part of a temporary adaptive or compensatory response. Abnormal angiogenesis can result in the formation of excessive nonfunctional small blood vessels, leading to disruptions in endothelial wall integrity, pericyte coverage, and basement membrane function. 40 , 41 This process has been suggested as a contributing factor to BBB dysfunction that occurs in some neurological disorders. For example, a recent study found that CSF pTau181 concentration, but not Aβ42/40, was positively associated with several CSF angiogenesis factors. 42 The authors concluded that tau pathology influences the expression of angiogenic factors, leading to abnormal angiogenesis in individuals with AD. This study also reported an association between aberrant angiogenic factors and cerebrovascular dysfunction, including pericyte injury and BBB disruption. 42 Based on these findings, the authors suggested that pathological angiogenesis may represent a mechanism contributing to cerebrovascular dysfunction in AD, emphasizing vascular dysfunction and aberrant remodeling as potential therapeutic targets (e.g., inhibiting excessive blood vessel growth or promoting the maturation of immature blood vessels). 42 Others have found that angiogenic markers interact with amyloid to impact cognitive decline via a tau‐mediated pathway, further highlighting their possible mechanistic role and therapeutic potential in AD. 43

Studies on cerebral blood flow (CBF) in AD are in line with our pattern of results showing that higher levels of sPDGFRβ are associated with better cognition cross‐sectionally but faster longitudinal decline, and with the inverted U‐shaped relationship between cognition and sPDGFRβ reported by Lv and colleagues. 26 Although hypoperfusion is typically associated with AD dementia, several studies have shown increased CBF (i.e., hyperperfusion) in AD‐vulnerable regions in individuals with elevated MCI or AD risk (e.g., APOE e4 carriers, individuals with subtle cognitive decline) compared to individuals with lower risk. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 These results suggest an inverted U‐shaped pattern of CBF changes in AD with initial increases in CBF that may occur during preclinical/prodromal phases followed by decreases in CBF in the dementia stage. Though speculative, and replication in larger, independent samples will be important to confirm results, this nonlinear pattern may reflect early neurovascular dysregulation or compensatory mechanisms followed by decompensation, since similar nonlinear associations have been observed between cognition and markers related to angiogenesis, microglial activity, and inflammation. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 Previous studies have shown significant positive associations between sPDGFRβ and some of these biomarkers, including YKL‐40, a marker of neuroinflammation, 6 , 27 and angiopoeitan‐2, a marker of angiogenesis. 57 Future research with longitudinal sPDGFRβ data will be useful in elucidating the within‐in person trajectory of this marker across the continuum of AD stages from preclinical to prodromal to dementia, and in determining whether elevated sPDGFRβ may have an adaptive effect in some circumstances.

Our results showed that elevated CSF sPDGFRβ, a marker of pericyte injury that may relate to BBB integrity, was associated with lower executive function irrespective of AD risk burden. Decline in executive function occurs with normal aging and with increased vascular risk burden. 34 , 58 , 59 Advancing age and vascular risk factors are both thought to preferentially affect frontal‐subcortical networks, which subserve executive abilities. BBB dysfunction may also contribute to age‐related declines in executive functioning since it occurs during normal aging and has been linked to inflammation and loss of tight junctions. 60 Given that our statistical models adjusted for age, our results suggest that BBB integrity may impact executive functioning above and beyond age. This conclusion is strengthened by our finding that higher levels of a marker of BBB integrity had more robust effects on executive function decline and cognitive decline in general in the context of greater AD risk and with previous studies showing that cerebrovascular dysfunction preferentially impacts executive functioning. 34 , 61 , 62

The synergistic effects of sPDGFRβ and AD risk status (i.e., AD biomarker positivity/negativity or APOE ɛ4 carrier/noncarrier) on cognitive decline that we observed suggest that comorbid AD and cerebrovascular pathology have an especially negative impact on cognition and support that cognitive decline and dementia in old age are multifactorial, with several underlying pathologies contributing in various degrees. Our sample was predominantly cognitively normal, indicating that these effects may occur during predementia stages of AD. We observed interactive effects between sPDGFRβ and AD biomarker‐positivity for decline across multiple cognitive domains. Interactive effects between sPDGFRβ and APOE ɛ4 status were only observed for decline in language, an ability typically affected early in the course of AD. 63 , 64 More widespread cognitive effects related to AD biomarker status may reflect that biomarker‐positive individuals have, by definition, a significant degree of AD pathologic change, whereas many APOE ɛ4 carriers may not. Furthermore, interactive effects between vascular risk factors and APOE ɛ4 status on cognition are more prominent during midlife than late life. 65 , 66 If our sample had included middle‐aged adults, it is possible that interactive effects of sPDGFRβ and APOE ɛ4 on cognition would have been more widespread and affected additional cognitive domains. Nonetheless, our findings are in line with previous research showing that cognitive decline in older adults with lower vascular health may preferentially affect language abilities. 67

Our findings contribute to a body of evidence indicating that loss of cerebrovascular integrity interacts with AD pathophysiology to worsen cognitive decline in older adults. 34 , 65 , 66 Previous research has shown that the co‐occurrence of cerebrovascular disease and cerebral Aβ accumulation lowers the threshold at which a person develops cognitive impairment. 68 , 69 Studies have also shown that markers of BBB breakdown are associated with cognitive loss, irrespective of Aβ and/or tau biomarker changes. 20 , 24 Furthermore, there may be subtypes of AD that are enriched for biomarkers of BBB dysfunction. 70 These results suggest that vascular markers should be included as part of an AD diagnostic and staging framework. Furthermore, NVU‐related markers such as sPDGFRβ warrant further study as a potential biomarker of enhanced cognitive decline in AD and as a possible therapeutic target. 1 , 28 , 71

There are several limitations that should be considered. Our sample was relatively homogenous such that it was made up of mostly cognitively normal, medically healthy, non‐Hispanic White, and highly educated individuals, which reduces the generalizability of our results. In particular, relationships between sPDGFRβ and cognition may vary by race given that sPDGFRβ levels have been found to be lower in middle‐aged adults with family history of AD who are Black/African American individuals versus non‐Hispanic White individuals. 31 Furthermore, our sample reported a lower frequency of diabetes (6%) and hypertension (34.9% recent/active) compared to estimates for older adults in the United States (19.4%–20.9% and 57.6%–58.9%, respectively), 72 and associations between sPDGFRβ levels and cognition may have been stronger in a cohort with higher vascular risk burden. Future studies should elucidate associations among sPDGFRβ levels, AD biomarkers, and cognition in more heterogenous samples with greater racial and ethnic diversity and with greater vascular risk burden. An additional limitation of our study is that, although sPDGFRβ is thought to be a biomarker of BBB integrity, our understanding remains incomplete; therefore, multiple biomarkers, including protein analytes as well as neuroimaging, are needed to provide a more complete assessment of NVU and BBB integrity in aging and AD in future studies. Furthermore, our sample encompassed individuals both without cognitive impairment and with mild cognitive impairment, and only linear effects were tested in the present study; while present analyses were limited due to sample size, future studies in a larger sample with greater vascular risk burden should seek to consider more complex models such as ones that can further elucidate the impact of diagnosis and other subgroups, as well as consider nonlinear effects.

Despite these limitations, our study examined the relationship between a marker of BBB integrity and cognitive decline using a robust longitudinal design with CSF biomarkers of AD and APOE genotype data in a well‐characterized sample of older adults without dementia. Furthermore, a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment facilitated examination of the influence of sPDGFRβ levels on decline across multiple cognitive domains.
