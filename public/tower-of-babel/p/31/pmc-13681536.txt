Targeted protein degradation via chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-mediated antigen endocytosis in T cells
Authors: Youguang Wang, Zihao He, Na Yin, Min Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted protein degradation (TPD) holds significant therapeutic potential over conventional biologics. However, existing platforms are constrained by their reliance on host protein degradation machinery and limited durability, rendering them inadequate for chronic diseases requiring sustained treatment. Here, we constructed a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) incorporating the TNFR1 ectodomain as the antigen-binding domain (TNFR1T cells) and used CRISPR-mediated knockout of BCOR and ZC3H12A to generate persistent TNFR1TIF cells. TNF binding, endocytosis, and degradation were assessed in vitro by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence. Immunocompetent syngeneic, Tnf−/−, and hTNF-tg rheumatoid arthritis (RA) mice (n = 4–9) were used in vivo to evaluate engraftment, persistence, and disease severity (clinical scoring and grip strength), with adalimumab (Humira, 1 or 10 mg/kg) as a comparator. Safety was assessed by intravenous Listeria monocytogenes challenge (1 × 104 colony-forming units [CFU], n = 6) and Thy1.1-based depletion (0.25 mg, n = 3). We found that TNFR1T cells specifically bound, endocytosed, and degraded soluble TNF in vitro. Without lymphodepletion preconditioning, TNFR1TIF cells expanded and persisted for one year in immunocompetent mice. A single infusion into hTNF-tg mice reduced serum hTNF to near wild-type levels, preventing and treating all stages of RA with superior efficacy and durability than the repeated high-dose adalimumab. Antibacterial defense remained uncompromised, and anti-Thy1.1 antibody efficiently eliminated TNFR1TIF cells in vivo. This approach extends CAR-T cell targeting from cellular antigens to soluble extracellular proteins, establishing a host-machinery-independent, durable cellular-TPD platform for chronic inflammatory diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Extracellular factors, such as cytokines, are implicated in various diseases. Utilizing biologics to functionally neutralize these extracellular factors is a valid therapeutic approach in the clinic. While biologics have shown effectiveness in disease treatment, they have inherent limitations that require further improvement. Due to their short half-lives, biologics must be administered repeatedly to maintain therapeutic efficacy, leading to increased costs, reduced patient compliance, and decreased quality of life. For example, the widely used anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) antibody (adalimumab, Humira) requires injections every two weeks for indications like rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [[1], [2], [3]]. Repeated protein injections also cause side effects, including injection site reactions, anaphylaxis, etc. [3,4], and the development of antidrug antibodies that eventually diminish therapeutic efficacy [[5], [6], [7]].

Targeted protein degradation (TPD) technologies, such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), offer advantages over functional inhibition via biologics [8]. While traditional PROTACs can only target intracellular proteins for degradation, newer TPD approaches, such as lysosome-targeting chimeras (LYTACs) [9], antibody-based PROTACs (AbTACs) [10], proteolysis-targeting antibodies (ProTABs) [11], and cytokine receptor-targeting chimeras (KineTACs) [12], among others [[13], [14], [15], [16]], can target membrane and/or extracellular proteins by engaging surface E3 ubiquitin ligases and/or the endocytosis-lysosome pathway. However, few of these novel approaches have demonstrated potent and durable therapeutic effects in disease models in vivo. Furthermore, existing TPD methods only induce transient protein degradation, making them unsuitable for chronic diseases that require long-term treatment.

All existing TPD technologies rely on engaging the endogenous protein degradation machinery of host cells [17,18]. For instance, insulin growth factor 2 receptor (IGF2R, also known as CI-M6PR) has been widely used as an endocytosis receptor to trigger targeted degradation of membrane or extracellular proteins in vitro [9,19,20]. However, IGF2R-mutant mice exhibit excessive insulin growth factor 2 (IGF2) signaling via IGF1R, resulting in perinatal lethality [21]. This indicates that the physiological degradation of IGF2 by IGF2R is critical for maintaining an appropriate level of IGF2 in vivo. Forced endocytosis of IGF2R by exogenous agents in vivo could interfere with the physiological degradation of IGF2, potentially causing side effects. Similarly, other commonly used endocytosis receptors and surface E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as transferrin receptor (TfR) and RNF43/ZNFR3 [11,22], also actively endocytose and degrade their native ligands [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Redirecting these receptors for TPD likely interferes with the degradation of their native substrates. Indeed, unforeseeable side effects have been observed in clinical trials of TPD drugs [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. Thus, engaging host protein degradation machinery is an inherent flaw present in all current TPD technologies. Additionally, the effects of current TPD technologies heavily depend on the expression pattern and level of E3 ubiquitin ligases or endocytosis receptors, limiting their in vivo efficacy. All these limitations highlight the need for developments that bypass the engagement of endogenous protein degradation machinery.

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, as an exogenous entity, traditionally target surface antigens on tumor or pathogenic cells rather than soluble factors [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Unlike all existing TPD approaches, CAR-T cells do not rely on any component of the host’s endogenous protein degradation machinery. Moreover, CARs undergo receptor-mediated endocytosis upon antigen engagement and are continuously replenished by CAR-T cells [38], enabling iterative and sustained target degradation. In this study, we exploited these properties to engineer a TNFR1 ectodomain-based CAR-T platform for specific, durable degradation of soluble TNF. To overcome the critical barrier of poor CAR-T expansion in immunocompetent hosts, we further applied CRISPR-mediated Bcor/Zc3h12a double knockout to generate long-lived TNFR1TIF cells that persist in vivo without lymphodepleting preconditioning. We demonstrate that a single infusion of TNFR1TIF cells confers durable RA remission in mouse models, establishing a host-machinery–independent cellular TPD platform and a paradigm shift from chronic repeated drug administration to single-infusion intervention for inflammatory disease.

RESULTS

TNFR1 CAR-T (TNFR1T) Cells Endocytose TNF In Vitro

To target TNF, we designed a second-generation CAR with the extracellular domain (amino acids 1–212) of mouse TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) as the antigen-binding domain, followed by the CD28 transmembrane/intracellular domain and CD3ζ chain (Figure 1A). Given that mouse TNFR1 binds to both mouse and human TNF [39], we could evaluate the responses of TNFR1 CAR-T (TNFR1T) cells to both mouse and human TNF using the same CAR (Figure 1B). TNFR1 CAR expression was coupled with a Thy1.1 marker for the detection and purification of CAR-T cells (Figure 1C). Co-staining T cells transduced with a retrovirus expressing this TNFR1 CAR with anti-Thy1.1 and anti-TNFR1 antibodies revealed surface expression of TNFR1 CAR on T cells (Figure 1D), with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) CAR-T cells serving as controls. In an in vitro binding assay, a fusion protein of red fluorescent protein (RFP) and TNF demonstrated specific binding to TNFR1T cells, but not HER2 CAR-T cells (Figure 1E). Upon incubation with RFP-TNF, TNFR1T cells, but not HER2 CAR-T cells, exhibited an upregulation of CD69 (Figure 1F‒1G), an activation marker of T cells, indicating that soluble TNF activates TNFR1T cells. Consistently, recombinant native mouse TNF (not the RFP-TNF fusion protein) enhanced the expansion of TNFR1T cells, but not HER2 CAR-T cells in vitro (Figure 1H). These data demonstrate that soluble TNF specifically activates and promotes the expansion of TNFR1T cells in vitro.

Lymphotoxin-alpha (LT-α), also known as TNF-β, is a structurally related cytokine within the TNF superfamily. To evaluate the ligand specificity of the TNFR1 CAR, we compared the binding and activation capacities of RFP-TNF and RFP-LT-α on TNFR1T cells. RFP-LT-α did not exhibit detectable binding to TNFR1T cells, in contrast to the obvious binding observed with RFP-TNF (Figure S1A). Consistently, stimulation with RFP-LT-α failed to upregulate CD69 expression on TNFR1T cells, whereas RFP-TNF induced significant CD69 upregulation (Figure S1B–S1C). Neither RFP-LT-α nor RFP-TNF bound to or activated HER2 CAR-T cells (Figure S1). These results demonstrate that the TNFR1 CAR confers specific responsiveness to TNF, but not to LT-α, confirming the ligand selectivity of the TNFR1 CAR we used.

CAR is known to undergo endocytosis in T cells [38]. To test whether CAR endocytosis can be leveraged to internalize its antigen, we utilized an in vitro endocytosis assay (Figure 1I). In this assay, TNFR1T cells or control HER2 CAR-T cells were first labeled with anti-TNFR1 antibody or RFP-TNF at 4 °C. After washing out unbound antibody or RFP-TNF, CAR-T cells were resuspended in T cell medium and split into two groups: one group kept at 4 °C and the other incubated at 37 °C for endocytosis (Figure 1I). After 24 h, the signals of anti-TNFR1 antibody and RFP-TNF were reduced in TNFR1T cells kept at 37 °C compared with those kept at 4 °C (Figure 1J–1M), suggesting that anti-TNFR1 antibody and RFP-TNF were either endocytosed and degraded in TNFR1T cells or dissociated from TNFR1T cells. Immunofluorescent staining showed that RFP-TNF could be detected within TNFR1T cells after incubation at 37 °C (Figure 1N‒1O), indicating that RFP-TNF is endocytosed by TNFR1T cells. Together, these data demonstrate that TNFR1T cells respond to TNF and endocytose TNF in vitro.

TNFR1T Cells Lack Therapeutic Efficacy for RA in hTNF-tg Mice due to Poor In Vivo Expansion

Since TNFR1T cells are capable of endocytosing TNF in vitro, we investigated their potential therapeutic efficacy in RA, a disease that responds to anti-TNF antibodies [3,7]. We administered TNFR1T cells to human TNF transgenic (hTNF-tg) mice (Figure 2A), a widely used RA model [40,41]. In comparison to Humira (adalimumab), an U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatment for RA [3], TNFR1T cells did not reduce the clinical severity of RA in hTNF-tg mice (Figure 2B) and failed to improve grip strength (Figure 2C). Consistent with the lack of therapeutic effect, TNFR1T cells were undetectable in hTNF-tg mice (Figure 2D). TNFR1TIF Cells Expand and Persist In Vivo

To further explore the expansion of TNFR1T cells in vivo, we transferred these cells into B6 mice (Figure S2A). No TNFR1T cells could be detected in the peripheral blood of recipient mice (Figure S2B–S2C), indicating a lack of expansion of TNFR1T cells in vivo in the immunocompetent B6 mice. These observations are consistent with a critical role of lymphodepleting preconditioning in the expansion of CAR-T cells in immunocompetent hosts [42,43].

To address the inability of TNFR1T cells to expand in vivo, we employed a recently reported strategy involving the simultaneous knockout of BCOR and ZC3H12A to enhance CAR-T cell expansion and persistence in immunocompetent mice without conditioning [44,45]. BCOR- and/or ZC3H12A-deficient TNFR1T cells were generated by co-delivering TNFR1 CAR and single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) into activated Cas9+CD8+ T cells (Figure S2D). Successful editing of Bcor and Zc3h12a genes was confirmed by DNA sequencing (Figure S2E–S2F). TNFR1T cells expressing sgRNAs targeting nontargeting (NT) control (sgNT), Bcor (sgBcor), or Zc3h12a (sgZc3h12a) showed no expansion in vivo (Figure 3A‒3B). In contrast, TNFR1T cells expressing sgRNAs targeting both Bcor and Zc3h12a (sgBcor/Zc3h12a) exhibited robust expansion and persistence (Figure 3A‒3B), reflecting a synergistic effect between BCOR and ZC3H12A deficiencies in TNFR1T cell expansion and persistence in vivo, consistent with previous findings in other BCOR- and ZC3H12A-deficient CAR-T cells [44,45]. Given that TNFR1T cells expressing sgNT, sgBcor, or sgZc3h12a exhibited no expansion in vivo (Figure 3A‒3B), our subsequent studies focused on TNFR1TIF cells, with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or wild-type TNFR1T cells serving as control (Figure 3A‒3B). Long-term monitoring revealed that TNFR1TIF cells persisted in vivo for a year (the end point of the experiment), with stable percentages in peripheral blood (Figure 3C‒3D). Importantly, TNFR1TIF cells could not expand in Tnf−/− mice devoid of TNF (Figure S2G–S2I), indicating that TNFR1TIF cells respond specifically to TNF in mice.

Two weeks post infusion, TNFR1TIF cells exhibited a typical effector T cell phenotype (CD44+CD62L‒) (Figure 3E‒3F). Gradually, the percentages of central memory phenotype (CD44+CD62L+) TNFR1TIF cells increased while effector memory phenotype (CD44+CD62L‒) TNFR1TIF cells diminished (Figure 3E‒3F), suggesting a trajectory resembling memory T cell differentiation. Two months post infusion, TNFR1TIF cells expressed high levels of CD44, Ly108, and TCF1, along with positivity for PD-1, LAG-3, TOX, as well as low levels of CD127 (Figure 3G). Additionally, a subset of TNFR1TIF cells expressed CD62L and TIM-3 (Figure 3G), suggesting heterogeneity within the TNFR1TIF population at this time point and supporting the notion of continued differentiation of these cells in vivo (Figure 3E‒3F). These characteristics partially align with features of other CAR TIF cells [44,45], implying that the deficiency of BCOR and ZC3H12A induces a similar program across various CAR T cells.

To further characterize the functional state of TNFR1TIF cells in vivo, we assessed intracellular cytokine production at 3 m post infusion. Compared with endogenous CD8+ T cells, TNFR1TIF cells exhibited a markedly higher frequency of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)-producing cells and a trend toward increased interleukin-2 (IL-2) production, along with significantly elevated mean fluorescence intensities (MFIs) of both IFN-γ and IL-2 (Figure S3A–S3C). In contrast, the proportion of TNF-producing cells was significantly lower in TNFR1TIF cells relative to endogenous CD8+ T cells, although the MFI of TNF per cell was comparable between the two populations (Figure S3B–S3C). The reduced frequency of TNF-producing TNFR1TIF cells may reflect continuous CAR-mediated endocytosis and degradation of TNF, consistent with the functional role of the TNFR1 CAR. The robust IFN-γ and IL-2 expression supports an activated effector/memory-like state of TNFR1TIF cells during long-term persistence in vivo. Together, these data indicate that TNFR1TIF cells are able to expand and persist in fully immunocompetent mice without any conditioning regimen, and their existence in vivo requires the presence of TNF.

Long-term Safety of TNFR1TIF Cells

A group of mice, infused with either TNFR1TIF cells or PBS, was monitored for up to a year, marking the endpoint of our safety study. Serial sampling of peripheral blood revealed that TNFR1TIF cells persisted in the blood at relatively stable percentages, as illustrated above (Figure 3C‒3D). The presence of TNFR1TIF cells did not affect the percentages of endogenous CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood (Figure S4A–S4B), nor the activation status of endogenous T cells (Figure S4C–S4D). At 12 m post infusion, no discernible difference in body weight was observed between mice infused with TNFR1TIF cells and PBS (Figure S4E). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of slices from the heart, liver, lung, and kidney did not reveal noticeable differences between mice infused with TNFR1TIF and PBS (Figure S4F). These long-term safety data indicate that the presence of TNFR1TIF cells in mice for up to a year does not obviously affect the health of mice.

Since TNFR1TIF cells target TNF through TNFR1 CAR-mediated endocytosis, a critical safety concern is whether TNFR1TIF cells compromise the host's TNF-dependent immune defense against infections. To address this, we challenged TNFR1TIF cell-infused mice with Listeria monocytogenes (LM), a bacterial pathogen whose clearance is highly dependent on TNF signaling. One month after TNFR1TIF cell infusion, mice were infected with 1 × 104 colony-forming units (CFU) of LM via the tail vein. At 72 h post infection, qualitative assessment of splenic bacterial burden by culture-plate imaging revealed comparable bacterial growth in PBS-infused and TNFR1TIF cell-infused mice (Figure S5A). Moreover, serum mouse TNF concentrations were not significantly different between TNFR1TIF cell-infused and PBS-infused mice, either at baseline or after LM infection (Figure S5B). These results indicate that TNFR1TIF cells do not impair the host's innate immune response to bacterial infection, suggesting that the acute TNF surge during infection is not significantly affected by TNFR1TIF cells.

As a living drug with long-term persistence, the ability to eliminate TNFR1TIF cells in vivo is an important safety consideration. Since the Thy1.1 marker is coexpressed with the TNFR1 CAR on TNFR1TIF cells, we tested whether administration of anti-Thy1.1 depleting antibody could clear TNFR1TIF cells. Mice bearing established TNFR1TIF cells (approximately 15% of CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood at day 28 post infusion) were intraperitoneally injected with 0.25 mg of anti-Thy1.1 antibody on day 30 and day 33 post infusion (Figure S6A). Following antibody treatment, TNFR1TIF cells were rapidly depleted, declining to approximately 3% by day 35 and becoming essentially undetectable by day 42 (Figure S6B). These data demonstrate that TNFR1TIF cells can be efficiently eliminated by anti-Thy1.1 antibody in vivo, providing a pharmacological safety switch for potential clinical applications.

TNFR1TIF cells, but not TNFR1T cells, significantly reduced serum hTNF in hTNF-tg mice to near wild-type levels (Figure 4A). Two months post infusion, TNFR1TIF cells were isolated from mice to perform the TNF endocytosis assay described earlier (Figure 1I). TNFR1TIF Cells Confer Long-term Remission of RA

Since TNFR1TIF cells degrade hTNF in hTNF-tg mice in vivo (Figure 4), we assessed the impact of TNFR1TIF cells on RA. In female hTNF-tg mice, infusion of TNFR1TIF cells, but not TNFR1T cells, before RA onset prevented RA clinical score (Figure 5A‒5B). TNFR1TIF cells also increased the body weight and grip strength of hTNF-tg mice to near wild-type levels (Figure 5C‒5D). Swelling and deformation of joints in hTNF-tg mice were largely prevented by TNFR1TIF cells, but not TNFR1T cells (Figure 5E‒5F). DISCUSSION

While traditional CAR-T cells eliminate cells, the CAR-T cells described in this study “eliminate” proteins. TNFR1TIF cells represent the first CAR-T cells targeting a soluble factor for degradation, marking significant conceptual and technological advancements. All known TPD technologies depend on the engagement of host protein degradation machinery, which causes unforeseeable side effects [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. In contrast, TNFR1TIF cells degrade TNF via exogenously introduced CAR-T cells, bypassing the host protein degradation machinery and reducing the likelihood of affecting host physiology. While emerging extracellular TPD technologies such as LYTACs [9] and KineTACs [12] represent important advances, no existing TPD approach has achieved targeted degradation of TNF, and all require repeated administration to maintain activity, in contrast to the self-sustaining degradation provided by TNFR1TIF cells from a single infusion.

All traditional drugs must be administered repeatedly to maintain efficacy. Current anti-TNF biologics, although effective as first-line therapies for RA, require long-term repeated administration (e.g., adalimumab every 2 w for years or decades), which incurs cumulative costs and reduces patient compliance. Importantly, chronic biologic therapy frequently leads to the generation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), which progressively diminish therapeutic efficacy—a well-documented cause of secondary treatment failure [46,47]. In contrast, TNFR1TIF cells, as a living drug, exhibit unprecedented durability, with a single infusion conferring long-term degradation of TNF and sustained remission of RA. In our hTNF-tg mouse model, a single infusion achieved comparable efficacy to repeated high-dose Humira (10 mg/kg, weekly) (Figure 5K‒5L), without repeated dosing or risk of ADA formation. This offers a potential functional “cure” for a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease that typically requires repeated drug administration over years or even decades, heralding a paradigm shift in chronic disease management.

Mechanistically, TNFR1TIF cells achieve TNF degradation through TNFR1 CAR-mediated endocytosis of TNF. While classical CAR activation paradigms rely on target-induced receptor clustering at the cell surface, soluble TNF naturally exists as a homotrimer [48], which may facilitate multivalent engagement and clustering of TNFR1 CARs. Prior studies showed that monomeric green fluorescent protein (GFP) failed to activate GFP-binding CAR-expressing T cells, whereas homodimeric GFP efficiently triggered CAR signaling, establishing that soluble ligands are generally required to crosslink CARs to initiate downstream activation [49]. Since CARs can undergo partial recycling back to the cell surface or be synthesized de novo by CAR-T cells [38], TNFR1TIF cells are able to continuously degrade TNF. While individual CAR molecules may undergo ubiquitination and degradation upon ligand engagement [38], the TNFR1TIF cells isolated from mice 2 m post infusion retained functional TNF endocytic capacity (Figure 4B–4E), confirming sustained CAR turnover and renewal at the cellular level. This explains why TNFR1TIF cells potently diminished serum hTNF to near wild-type levels in hTNF-tg mice, which constitutively produce hTNF from a transgene, a condition much more severe than that of most RA patients in clinical settings. Additionally, TNFR1TIF cells circumvent side effects associated with protein drugs, such as injection site reactions, and anaphylaxis.

To overcome the challenge of TNFR1T cells’ inability to expand in vivo, we implemented a recently reported double knockout of BCOR and ZC3H12A to enhance expansion and persistence in mice [44], resulting in TNFR1TIF cells. The mechanism underlying the long-term persistence of TNFR1TIF cells in vivo remains incompletely investigated in this study due to the complete absence of all control CAR-T cells, including wild-type, BCOR-deficient, and ZC3H12A-deficient TNFR1T cells (Figure 3A‒3B). These control cells are essential for comprehensive mechanistic assays such as assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq), RNA sequencing, and others. Nevertheless, TNFR1TIF cells share certain features with other BCOR- and ZC3H12A-deficient CAR-T cells, suggesting that BCOR and ZC3H12A deficiencies induce a similar program across different CAR-T cells. Future studies will be devoted to dissecting the mechanisms underlying the long-term persistence of TNFR1TIF cells, including potential roles of altered activation thresholds, exhaustion resistance, and metabolic rewiring. Despite these limitations, our data conclusively demonstrate the safety, efficacy, and mechanism of TNF degradation, laying a foundation for clinical studies.

A potential concern with chronic antigen exposure is the induction of T cell exhaustion [50]. However, several lines of evidence argue against functional exhaustion in TNFR1TIF cells. TNFR1TIF cells persist for one year with stable peripheral blood percentages (Figure 3C‒3D), and intracellular cytokine staining at 3 m post infusion revealed markedly higher frequencies of IFN-γ-producing cells and elevated MFI of IFN-γ and IL-2 compared to endogenous CD8+ T cells (Figure S3), confirming a robust effector/memory-like functional state. While TNFR1TIF cells express some exhaustion-associated markers (PD-1, LAG-3, TOX), they coexpress memory-associated markers (TCF1, Ly108, and CD62L) (Figure 3G), a profile more consistent with a progenitor-exhausted or memory-like state than terminal exhaustion [51,52]. Unlike persistent, high-density antigen in solid tumors, TNF is a soluble factor subject to dynamic regulation. Moreover, BCOR/ZC3H12A double knockout may confer resistance to exhaustion-inducing programs [45].

In theory, alternative methods capable of promoting CAR-T cell expansion and persistence in immunocompetent mice without the need for a conditioning regimen might be applicable to implant TNFR1T cells in mice. However, as of the manuscript submission, we are not aware of such alternative methods. A significant obstacle in extending CAR-T cell therapy to noncancerous diseases lies in the requirement for a pretreatment conditioning regimen, often involving intense chemotherapeutic measures such as cyclophosphamide and/or fludarabine [53,54]. In the context of noncancerous diseases, the severe side effects associated with chemotherapeutic conditioning, such as cytokine release syndrome, heightened infection risks due to immunodeficiency, and genome instability resulting from genotoxicity, may not justify the application of CAR-T cells.

Recent clinical breakthroughs with CD19 CAR-T cell therapy in autoimmune diseases provide important context for our strategy. The Erlangen group reported drug-free remission in patients with refractory systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [55]. This approach was expanded to 15 patients across SLE, myositis, and systemic sclerosis with sustained remission at a median 15 m follow-up [56]. The CASTLE (CAR-T cells in systemic B cell-mediated autoimmune disease) Phase 1/2 basket trial (NCT06347718) further confirmed these findings, with 22 of 24 patients achieving efficacy endpoints [57]. CD19 CAR-T cells have also been applied to RA specifically, with early reports of efficacy [58,59]. Our TNFR1TIF strategy differs fundamentally: CD19 CAR-T cells deplete B cells to reset autoimmunity via transient B-cell aplasia followed by naïve B cell reconstitution, whereas TNFR1TIF cells directly degrade the pathogenic effector cytokine TNF without targeting any cell population. Notably, all current CD19 CAR-T protocols for autoimmune diseases require lymphodepleting preconditioning, whereas TNFR1TIF cells do not require any conditioning regimen. These two strategies are complementary rather than competing, as they address disease pathology at different levels—cellular (autoreactive B cells) vs effector (pathogenic cytokines).

Two additional safety considerations warrant discussion. First, a potential concern with long-term TNF degradation is the impairment of TNF-dependent antimicrobial immunity. Our LM challenge experiments demonstrate that TNFR1TIF cells do not significantly affect the host's ability to control bacterial infection or mount an acute TNF response (Figure S5). Importantly, TNFR1TIF cells reduce serum hTNF to near wild-type levels (Figure 4A), rather than achieving complete elimination, thereby preserving baseline TNF-dependent immune functions. This may be attributed to the fact that the acute, infection-driven TNF surge substantially exceeds the steady-state TNF levels targeted by TNFR1TIF cells under homeostatic conditions. Second, the long-term persistence of TNFR1TIF cells necessitates a reliable mechanism for their elimination if adverse effects arise. We demonstrate that anti-Thy1.1 depleting antibody efficiently clears TNFR1TIF cells in vivo (Figure S6), establishing a proof-of-concept for a pharmacological safety switch. In clinical settings, this strategy could be adapted by coexpressing a clinically approved surface marker, such as truncated EGFR or CD20, that can be targeted by FDA-approved depleting antibodies (e.g., cetuximab or rituximab).

We acknowledge that the hTNF-tg mouse model represents an extreme, purely TNF-driven pathology and does not fully recapitulate the immunological heterogeneity of human RA, which involves additional cytokine networks (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17), autoantibody-mediated pathology, and variable TNF dependence. TNFR1TIF cells would be most applicable to patients with confirmed TNF-dependent disease, particularly those who initially respond to TNF inhibitor (TNFi) but lose efficacy due to ADA development or secondary failure. From a translational perspective, CAR-T cell therapy carries inherent risks, including manufacturing complexity, potential insertional mutagenesis, and the commitment to a permanent intervention. However, TNFR1TIF cells offer key advantages: single infusion vs lifelong repeated dosing, elimination of ADA-related failure, no lymphodepleting preconditioning, and an available safety switch (Figure S6). This approach would be most appropriate for patients with severe, refractory disease who have failed multiple biologic therapies.

Our study marks a pivotal shift in the targets of CAR-T cells, transitioning from cells to soluble factors. Beyond TNF, numerous other inflammatory cytokines implicated in many diseases, such as IL-1β, IL-4, and IL-17, stand as potential candidates for targeting by CAR-T cells using the strategies outlined here. Key criteria for amenable targets include: the availability of a natural receptor ectodomain suitable for CAR construction, sufficient target abundance to drive CAR-T cell activation and expansion, and a pathological role requiring sustained target depletion. Targets with very low abundance or rapid turnover kinetics may be less suitable. Expanding beyond soluble factors, extracellular protein aggregates, such as Aβ aggregates in Alzheimer's disease [60], could also become potential targets for CAR-T cells.

In summary, our study introduces a novel platform for redirecting CAR-T cells to target extracellular soluble factors for degradation. This advancement not only broadens the spectrum of treatable diseases for CAR-T cells but also provides a lasting therapeutic option for chronic diseases that require repeated drug administration.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

TNF knockout (Tnf−/−) mice on a C57BL/6J background were provided by Dr. Xin Lin (Tsinghua University). C57BL/6J (B6; The Jackson Laboratory, Maine, USA; Cat#JAX:000664, RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664), hTNF transgenic mice (C57BL/6N background) (Taconic Biosciences, New York, USA; Cat#1006), Tnf−/− mice, and Cas9 transgenic mice (C57BL/6N background) (The Jackson Laboratory, Maine, USA; Cat#JAX:026430, RRID: IMSR_JAX:026430) were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment at Tsinghua University's Laboratory Animal Research Center in Beijing, China. The facilities for these animals were approved by the Beijing Administration Office of Laboratory Animals. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. All animal-related procedures were sanctioned by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tsinghua University (approval number 17-PM1).

This study employed four mouse models: B6-background Cas9 transgenic mice as immunocompetent recipients for in vivo expansion, long-term safety, and safety-switch studies; Tnf−/− mice to confirm TNF-dependency of TNFR1TIF expansion; hTNF-tg mice as the RA disease model; and hTNF-tg mice receiving Humira as a pharmacological comparator. All mice were maintained under SPF conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water, and were confirmed normal health status prior to enrollment. Female Cas9 transgenic mice (6–8 w) served as CD8+ T cell donors throughout. Female or male Cas9 transgenic mice (6–8 w) were used as immunocompetent syngeneic recipients for in vivo expansion and safety studies, consistent with published BCOR/ZC3H12A double-knockout CAR-T cell work [44,45]. For the in vivo expansion, recipients were randomized into five groups: PBS (n = 6), sgNT (n = 6), sgBcor (n = 6), sgZc3h12a (n = 5), and sgBcor/Zc3h12a (TNFR1TIF; n = 6). Long-term persistence was monitored over 12 m in PBS (n = 6) and TNFR1TIF (n = 6) groups. Phenotypic characterization was performed 2 m post infusion in PBS (n = 4) and TNFR1TIF (n = 4) groups. To evaluate whether TNFR1TIF cells compromise TNF-dependent antimicrobial defense, mice in the PBS (n = 5) and TNFR1TIF (n = 5) groups were challenged intravenously with LM (1 × 104 CFU) at 1 m post infusion, compared with no-LM treatment groups (n = 6 per group); bacterial burden and serum mouse TNF were assessed at 72 h post infection. For the safety switch study, mice bearing established TNFR1TIF cells were administered anti-Thy1.1 depleting antibody intraperitoneally on day 30 and day 33 post infusion (n = 3), compared with the PBS group (n = 3). Male Tnf−/− mice (6–8 w) served as recipients to confirm TNF-dependency of TNFR1TIF cell expansion. Recipients (B6 and Tnf−/− mice) were allocated to PBS and TNFR1TIF groups (n = 6 per group in B6 background, n = 3 per group in Tnf−/− background). For RA studies, hTNF-tg mice were enrolled at 5–8 w of age (prior to RA onset, which occurs spontaneously between 8 and 12 w) or at 18 w (established late-stage RA), as specified per experiment. Both male and female hTNF-tg mice were included; given that RA disproportionately affects women in clinical settings and that therapeutic efficacy may vary by sex, both sexes were studied to evaluate the breadth of therapeutic activity. In an initial study evaluating the therapeutic potential of unmodified TNFR1T cells, male hTNF-tg mice with established RA were allocated to PBS (n = 6), TNFR1T (n = 6), or Humira 10 mg/kg weekly (n = 7) groups, with B6 wildtype mice (n = 6) serving as healthy controls. Female hTNF-tg mice were used for the preventive study (B6, n = 9; TNFR1T, n = 9; TNFR1TIF, n = 9) and for late-stage RA treatment (B6, n = 5; TNFR1T, n = 10; TNFR1TIF, n = 9). Male hTNF-tg mice were used for preventive (B6, n = 6; TNFR1T, n = 6; TNFR1TIF, n = 6) and post-onset therapeutic studies (B6, n = 4; TNFR1T, n = 4; TNFR1TIF, n = 4). For the pharmacological comparator study, male hTNF-tg mice with established RA were randomized to B6 wildtype (n = 6), TNFR1T (n = 5), Humira 1 mg/kg weekly (n = 6), Humira 10 mg/kg weekly (n = 7), or TNFR1TIF (n = 5) groups. Humira was administered intraperitoneally once weekly; TNFR1T cells or TNFR1TIF cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse, single infusion via tail vein) were administered without any lymphodepleting preconditioning.

The experimental unit was a single mouse in all in vivo experiments. The exact number of animals in each experimental group is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. The total number of mice used was 367, and no animals were excluded. Mice were allocated to groups by simple randomization. To minimize potential confounders, cages from different groups were distributed across different shelves. Sample sizes and inclusion criteria were determined based on published studies using these models [40,41,44,45]. Investigators performing RA clinical assessments (RA scoring and grip strength measurement) were blinded to treatment allocation. All mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation (30%–70% chamber volume per minute), confirmed by cessation of respiration, followed by cervical dislocation as secondary confirmation. Peripheral blood was collected by retro-orbital puncture under isoflurane anesthesia (2%–3% in O2).

Cell Lines

Phoenix-Eco cells (Cell Biolabs, California, USA; Cat#RV101) and HEK293T cells (ATCC, Virginia, USA; Cat#CRL3216, RRID: CVCL_0063) were grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, California, USA), supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM glutamine, and antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using the TransDectTM PCR mycoplasma detection kit (TransGen, Beijing, China; Cat#FM311) and were confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma.

Plasmids

The TNFR1 CAR consisted of the mouse TNFR1 ectodomain (NCBI RefSeq: NP_035739.2), the mouse CD28 transmembrane and intracellular costimulatory domain (NCBI RefSeq: NP_031668.1), and the mouse CD3ζ signaling domain (NCBI RefSeq: NP_001106864.1). The HER2 CAR and EGFR CAR were constructed using an anti-human HER2 scFv (clone 4D5) and an anti-human EGFR scFv (clone C225), respectively, each fused to the same CD28 and CD3ζ signaling modules described above. These constructs served as irrelevant-antigen specificity controls in all binding and functional assays.

All CAR constructs were built on the pMSCV-hU6-sgRNA-EFS-Thy1.1-CAR retroviral backbone, a derivative of the murine stem cell virus (MSCV)-based vector (pMSCV; Takara, California, USA; Cat#634401), by Gibson assembly (NEB Gibson Assembly Master Mix; New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#E2611S) following HpaI/SalI restriction digestion of the backbone. For dual sgRNA expression, a second hU6 promoter-sgRNA cassette was introduced upstream of the existing cassette by the same strategy. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing across all cloning junctions before retroviral packaging.

To generate fluorescently labeled ligand constructs, the sequences of mouse TNF (NCBI RefSeq: NP_038721.1), human TNF (NCBI RefSeq: NP_000585.2), and mouse LTα (NCBI RefSeq: NP_034865.1) were individually fused in-frame to the C-terminus of an RFP via a flexible glycine–serine linker (G4S)2. The resulting RFP-mTNF, RFP-hTNF, and RFP-LTα fusion sequences were cloned into the pFUSE expression backbone (InvivoGen, California, USA; Cat#pfuse-mg2afc2) by Gibson assembly and restriction enzyme digestion with BglII and NheI. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing before use.

Retrovirus Production

Retroviruses were packaged by cotransfection of Phoenix-Eco cells with the indicated plasmid and helper plasmid pCL-Eco (Addgene, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#12371) using calcium phosphate precipitate-mediated transfection. Viral supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 h post transfection, filtered through 0.45 mm filters, aliquoted, and frozen at −80 °C.

Mouse T Cell Culture, Viral Transduction, and Adoptive Transfer

CD8+ T cells were isolated by a negative-selection kit (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#480035) from spleens and lymph nodes of Cas9+ mice, then activated overnight with 1 μg/mL anti-CD3 (Bio X Cell, New Hampshire, USA; Cat#BE0001-1, RRID: AB_1107634) and 1 μg/mL anti-CD28 (Bio X Cell, New Hampshire, USA; Cat#BE0015-1, RRID: AB_1107624) in T cell medium (TCM): RPMI1640 medium (Gibco, California, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, 55 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 2 ng/mL IL-2 (PeproTech, New Jersey, USA; Cat#200-02-1000). The following day, activated T cells were spin-infected at 2000 g with the retrovirus described above at 33 °C for 2 h. Then, cells were washed and cultured in fresh TCM with IL-2. The efficiency of transduction was determined by flow cytometry 24 h after spin-infection. All adoptive transfers to indicated mice (including Cas9 transgenic mice, Tnf−/− mice, and hTNF transgenic mice) were performed at 24 h post transduction via tail vein at a dose of 1 × 106 TNFR1T, TNFR1TIF, or other cells per mouse; control mice received an equivalent volume of PBS (200 μL per mouse) via tail vein at the same time point. All transfers were performed in the absence of any conditioning regimen.

CRISPR/Cas9 Editing Efficiency Assessment

To confirm on-target editing, genomic DNA was isolated from sgRNA-transduced or control cells. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the target locus was performed using gene-specific primers flanking the sgRNA cleavage site. For Zc3h12a, the primers used were Zc3h12a-F (5′-ACCTCAGCCAGACCAGGATC-3′) and Zc3h12a-R (5′-TCGATGACCACAGGTCTCAG-3′); for Bcor, the primers used were Bcor-F (5′-CCGAAAGAAACACTATCTCC-3′) and Bcor-R (5′-TGATGGCGTGGTATCCACCG-3′). PCR products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm amplification, then subjected to Sanger sequencing. Editing efficiency was assessed by analysis of sequencing chromatograms, with overlapping peaks downstream of the predicted cleavage site used as evidence of indel formation.

Following two washes, the RFP mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was acquired by flow cytometry.

T Cell Activation Assay

TNFR1T cells and HER2 CAR-T cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/mL in 96-well plates and stimulated with supernatants containing RFP-TNF fusion protein for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Unstimulated cells served as negative controls. Following incubation, cells were stained with an anti-CD69 antibody and a viability dye (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, DAPI) for 15 min at 4 °C, and acquired by flow cytometry.

T Cell Expansion Assay

TNFR1T cells and HER2 CAR-T cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured with recombinant native mouse TNF (0.01–100 ng/mL) (Biolegend, California, USA; Cat#575202) for 2 d at 37 °C with 5% CO2. At the endpoint, viable cells were enumerated by trypan blue exclusion using an automated cell counter.

TNF Internalization Assay

TNFR1T cells or TNFR1TIF cells were first labeled with RFP-TNF fusion protein or anti-TNF antibody on ice for 20 min [63]. After washing out unbound RFP-TNF and anti-TNF antibody, CAR-T cells were resuspended in TCM and split into two parts. One part of the cells was kept at 4 °C, whereas the other was put into a 37 °C incubator to allow endocytosis for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were stained with the indicated antibodies at 4 °C for 15 min. The signals of RFP-TNF and anti-TNF antibody were examined by FACS.

To visualize the intracellular localization of RFP-TNF, cells that underwent the above treatment were loaded on a glass slide coated with poly-lysine. After fixation, the slides were mounted with antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#P36931). Images of the cells were captured using a Zeiss LSM780 microscope (Zeiss, Thuringia, Germany) and analyzed using ImageJ software (v2.15.0).

RA Model

The hTNF-tg mice spontaneously developed severe RA, similar to human RA. These mice exhibited arthritis in both fore paws and hind paws starting around 8–12 w of age, with the condition worsening as they aged. PBS, TNFR1T cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse) or TNFR1TIF cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse) were transferred into hTNF-tg mice via the tail vein at the indicated time points. Humira (1 mg/kg or 10 mg/kg as indicated) were injected into mice intraperitoneally once a week. Clinical signs of hTNF-tg mice, including RA clinical scores, body weight, and grip strength, were monitored weekly. The scoring criteria for toes were as follows: 0, no signs; 1, mild deformity; 2, severe deformity or swelling. The scoring criteria for wrists and ankles are as follows: 0, no signs; 1, mild swelling; 2, severe swelling; 3, severe swelling along with noticeable distortion; 4, severe swelling along with severe distortion. The scores from all four paws of a mouse were combined and used as the total RA score.

The grip strength of both the forelimbs and hindlimbs was evaluated using a grip strength meter (YILIDA, Guangdong, China; Cat#DS2). As the mouse grasped the bar, the peak pull force was recorded by a digital force transducer. The highest score from three consecutive trials was used to determine forelimbs and hindlimbs grip strength.

Measurement of hTNF

To measure hTNF levels in serum, a cytometric bead array (CBA) kit (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA; Cat#551811) was used. Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding from isoflurane-anesthetized mice in 1.5 mL sterile tubes, left at room temperature for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 2000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatants were carefully transferred to new tubes and centrifuged again at 2000 rpm at 4 °C for another 10 min. The final supernatants (serum) were promptly subjected to the CBA assay according to the protocol provided by the kit.

μCT

Bone samples were preserved in a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Micro-computed tomography (μCT) images of the PFA-fixed bone samples were captured using a Quantum GX μCT (PerkinElmer, Inc., Massachusetts, USA) at 60 kV and 160 μA. Images were taken with a 9 μm voxel size resolution and a 72 mm × 72 mm field of view (FOV) in high-resolution mode. The captured images were visualized and reconstructed using the built-in software (v2.0) within the Quantum GX system.

Histology

Tissues were immersion-fixed in 4% PFA for 24–48 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with Mayer's hematoxylin (5–10 min), differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, blued in running tap water, and counterstained with 0.5% eosin Y (1–2 min). Slides were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with dibutylphthalate polystyrene xylene (DPX) mountant.

LM Infection Model

One month after TNFR1TIF cell or PBS infusion, mice were infected intravenously via the tail vein with 1 × 104 CFU of LM (kindly provided by Professor Lilin Ye, Third Military Medical University, China). LM was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm to mid-logarithmic phase (optical density at 600 nm [OD600] = 0.1–0.22), washed in sterile PBS, and resuspended to the desired concentration prior to injection. At 72 h post infection, spleens were harvested, homogenized, serially diluted, and plated on agar plates for enumeration of bacterial colonies. Serum was collected at 72 h post infection for measurement of mouse TNF by CBA as described above.

Anti-Thy1.1 Antibody-mediated Clearance of TNFR1TIF Cells

To assess the clearance of TNFR1TIF cells in vivo, mice bearing established TNFR1TIF cells were intraperitoneally injected with 0.25 mg of anti-Thy1.1 depleting antibody (Bio X Cell, New Hampshire, USA; Cat#BE0214) on day 30 and day 33 post TNFR1TIF cell infusion. Peripheral blood was collected at the indicated time points, and the percentage of Thy1.1+ cells within CD8+ T cells was determined by flow cytometry as described above.

Quantifications and Statistical Analysis

The statistical information of each experiment, including the statistical methods, the P value, and sample numbers (n), was shown in the figure or figure legends. All one-way ANOVA analyses were followed by Šidák’s test, and all two-way ANOVA analyses were followed by Tukey’s test. GraphPad Prism 9 (https://www.graphpad.com) was used to plot all graphs and to perform statistical and quantitative assessments. Error bars represent the standard error of mean (SEM).
