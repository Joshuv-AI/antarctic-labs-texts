Single-cell aneuploidy and chromosomal arm imbalances define subclones with divergent transcriptomic phenotypes
Authors: Xiangqi Bai, Billy T Lau, Anuja Sathe, Susan M Grimes, Alison Almeda-Nostine, Hanlee P Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancers are characterized by genomic instability events such as aneuploidy, chromosomal arm imbalances, and segmental copy number changes. These genomic features frequently define different subclones within a tumor. Single-cell DNA sequencing (scDNA-seq) identifies these large-scale genomic alterations that define subclonal features. However, scDNA-seq does not provide biological phenotypic information on individual subclones. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers biological information but is less accurate in discovering genomic instability events. We developed a computational framework, scAlign, for integrating scRNA-seq and scDNA-seq from the same specimen and define subclonal cellular phenotypes at the resolution of individual cells. Subclones were defined by aneuploidy and chromosomal arm imbalance among primary and metastatic cancers. Using the cells in the G0/G1 phase, the extensive cellular sampling from both assays characterized the subclonal architecture of these cancers. The scDNA-seq provided a ground truth for copy number-based subclones. From the scRNA-seq data, the epithelial cells in G0/G1 were identified and assigned to specific subclones by the scAlign based on gene dosage. Afterward, we determined the differential gene expression and biological pathway activities of specific clones. Overall, integrative multi-omics analysis of single-cell datasets is more informative than any individual genomic modality, provides deep insights into intratumoral heterogeneity and reveals subclonal biology. scAlign is available at https://github.com/XQBai/scAlign.

Introduction

Cancers have complex cellular architecture composed of distinct populations of malignant cells with different genomic properties [1–3]. These populations of cells within the same tumor are referred to as subclones. Subclonal heterogeneity can be defined through specific large-scale genomic instability events that include: aneuploidy, where the copy number of specific chromosomes increases above the diploid state; chromosomal arm imbalances with gains or losses; large segmental copy number alterations [4]. Within a given tumor, these large-scale genomic changes that involve chromosomes are likely to have a dramatic effect on the subclonal phenotype. However, few studies have addressed how subclonal-specific large genomic instability events alter cellular properties.

Traditional karyotyping was used to identify chromosomal changes that define cancer subclonal cells, but it usually provides results from only a limited number of cells, typically in the tens. More recently, many studies have relied on bulk-level DNA sequencing to define subclones [2, 3, 5–8]. However, these approaches have limitations in resolving subclonal evolution and diversity, seeing that they do not resolve individual cells. For genomic DNA sequencing of tumors, the presence of other cell types, such as immune, stromal, and endothelial cell types, are intermingled with the tumor cell subpopulations. These normal tissue cells have a diploid genome; their presence can obscure genomic instability events among the intermingled tumor cells. Therefore, conventional bulk genome sequencing of tumors does not provide cell-specific features and deconvolution methods are required to identify genomic changes present in tumor cells versus intermingled normal diploid cells. Overall, these methods do not differentiate the affected subclonal cell types from other types of surrounding cells and do not determine their phenotypic properties.

Single-cell DNA sequencing (scDNA-seq) of tumors has proven to be highly informative for defining subclonal populations within a tumor [9–14]. Most scDNA-seq approaches use whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of individual cells to identify large genomic alterations, including aneuploidy, chromosomal arm imbalances, and large segmental DNA changes covering megabases [15]. These genomic events define the subclonal structure in many cancers. However, while genome-wide scDNA-seq reveals DNA alterations of subclones, it does not provide information about specific cell types or phenotypic characteristics. Conversely, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) delineates differential gene expression patterns in cancer cells that directly reflect their underlying biology but is less informative in providing genomic DNA features, such as chromosomal imbalances that define subclones [16, 17]. To address these limitations, we used scDNA-seq to identify subclonal populations from primary tumors with scRNA-seq to analyze cells from the same tumor. By overlapping the expression data from the cellular populations, we thus determined the gene expression properties of these same subclones. This integrated approach provided a comprehensive depiction of clonal genomic alterations and transcriptional phenotype signatures [9, 11]. Moreover, using these methods provided us with a high number of single cells from both assays.

Our study focused on stomach (i.e. gastric) and colorectal cancers (CRC). From primary tumor tissue samples, we generated cell suspensions which were split into two separate single-cell assays. Then, we sequenced robust single-cell characterization of both DNA- and RNA-based features from the same tumor specimen and their cell populations. We used scDNA-seq to identify subclonal populations based on aneuploidy, chromosomal imbalances, and other significant copy number variation (CNV) events. To further analyze these subclones, we developed a Single-cell Clonal Alignment method—termed scAlign—that assigned cells from scRNA-seq to prior subclones determined by scDNA-seq. This integration analysis examined only those cells in G0/G1 of the cell cycle, which provided the best results regarding CNV changes. Subsequently, we analyzed the corresponding scRNA-seq gene expression data to determine epithelial cell lineage and cell cycle phase. Then, we utilized the scAlign to assign epithelial cells from the scRNA-seq data to specific subclones defined by the CNVs identified from scDNA-seq [18–20]. As a validation step, we independently inferred CNVs from the scRNA-seq gene expression to corroborate our results. Based on this process, we identified differential gene expression and pathway activities for each subclone [21].

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. The Institutional Review Board at Stanford University School of Medicine approved the study protocols (IRB-44036). All patients provided written informed consent to participate. Tissues were obtained from the Stanford Cancer Institute Tissue bank.

Tumor samples

Primary gastric tumor samples and metastatic CRC samples were obtained from the Stanford Cancer Institute Tissue Bank. We also had matched adjacent normal tissue for a subset of the samples. All the specimens underwent histopathology review with hematoxylin and eosin-stained tissue sections. After surgical resection, the tumor samples were stored in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium on ice for less than one hour. The sample was then macro-dissected and dissociated into a cellular suspension by the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator using the human tumor dissociation kit as per manufacturer’s recommendations and the 37C_h_TDK_3 program (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). The suspension was used for scRNA-seq. scDNA-seq was performed after thawing cryopreserved dissociated cells stored in liquid nitrogen in a 90% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) 10% Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) freezing medium.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Cellranger software suite 3.0.2 was used to process the single-cell scRNA-seq. This included sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and single cell 3′ gene counting. The complementary DNA sequence from read two was aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome. The reference contained 33,694 entries, including 20,237 genes, 2337 pseudogenes, and 5560 antisense (noncoding DNA). Cellranger provided a gene-by-cell matrix containing the read count distribution of each gene for each cell.

scDNA-seq, CNV calling, and pre-processing

Single-cell DNA libraries were generated using a two-stage microfluidic procedure using a high-throughput, droplet-based reagent delivery system (10X Genomics Inc., Pleasanton, CA). The details of scDNA-seq library preparation and sequencing are described in Supplementary Methods. Sequence data and CNV calling were processed with the Beta version of the cellranger-dna pipeline (6002.16.0), which automates the preprocessing steps for sample demultiplexing, read alignment, and report generation. Paired-end FASTQ files and a reference genome (GRCh38) were used as input. The output of CNV calls is the integer-level single-cell copy number variants in each nonoverlapped 20 kb bin across the whole genome. We merged the 50 neighboring 20 kb bins into 1 Mb-level segment. Then, the median of CNV in 50 neighborhood bins is presented as the CNV number per 1 Mb segment. Finally, we generated a dimensional-reduction matrix with 3103 features for downstream analysis.

Cell cycle assignment of scDNA-seq tumor cells

To distinguish G0/G1 phase and S phase cells, we calculated the dissimilarity between tumor cells and reference diploid cells using a Euclidean distance metric. The cells in S phase were driven by ongoing DNA replication, which overwhelmed the true clonal CNV. Thereby, the dissimilarity of S phase cells tends to have higher dissimilarity compared to cells in G0/G1 phase, as the CNVs generally double during replication. To statistically distinguish these two populations, we implemented a two-component Gaussian mixture model for the dissimilarity vector using the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm. We applied the ‘normalmixEM’ function (with parameter k = 2) from the “mixtools” (version 2.0.0.1) R package. Cells were assigned to one of the two components based on posterior probabilities. Subclonal reconstruction with scDNA-seq G0/G1 tumor cells

Seurat (v.4.0.4) was utilized to construct the subclonal structure of G0/G1 tumor cells based on the single-cell CNV matrix. We identified and filtered out normal diploid cells and replicating cells before Seurat processing (Supplementary Method). To improve the clustering performance, we developed a statistical method for scDNA-seq feature selection. It included the following steps: (i) we fitted a mixture normal distribution to the variances of the inter-cell CNVs of genomic segments by EM algorithm. (ii) we selected the most informative segments with the highest variance which were located at the larger mean normal distribution, until at least 20% of the total segments were included. The selected copy number segments were used as the informative features input to Seurat.

After feature selection, we directly applied the ‘RunPCA’ function to the raw CNV matrix and chose the first 20 principal components (PCs). We implemented the Louvain algorithm to detect the optimal clusters on a shared nearest neighbor graph constructed based on the top 20 PCs. Noted that, the resolution parameter in ‘FindClusters’ function was adjusted with the number of cells in samples to ensure the optimal cluster numbers. Finally, Seurat defined the clusters of G0/G1 tumor cells. The clusters with consistent genomic instability were merged and regarded as the same major subclone. The constructed subclones were visualized on the 2D Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) space.

Subclonal phylogeny tree construction

To explore the relationships between different tumor subclones originated from scDNA-seq profile, we construct the unrooted phylogenetic tree utilizing the “ape” package (v.5.4) [22]. The CNVs of each subclone was calculated as integer average of CNVs of single cells from the same subclone. The Euclidean distances between different subclones were calculated based on the subclonal CNVs. We plotted a heatmap to uncover the single-cell clonal architecture across 22 autosomes using ComplexHeatmap (v.2.5.4) [23]. The subclones were ordered according to their placement on the phylogenetic tree.

Identifying the epithelial cells from scRNA-seq of cancers

We merged the paired normal and tumor samples for each patient. Then Seurat was used to cluster merged cells based on gene expression matrix after normalization and features selection. We utilized the module score (MS) to annotate cell types for cell clusters, especially epithelial cell. This analysis relied on well-established cell-type marker genes for specific types including epithelial cells (MUC5AC, PGC, EPCAM, MUC2, TFF1), fibroblasts (ACTA2, DCN, SPARC, THY1), endothelium (VWF, PECAM1), dendritic (CD83), macrophage (CD14, FCGR3A), immune (PTRPC), B plasma (CD19, CD79, MS4A1, IGHA, IGHC, IGLC), and T cells (CD3D, TRAC, TRAB, CD8A, NKG7, GNLY, IL2RA). We annotated the clusters as epithelial cells when the epithelial MS is >0 and nonepithelial MS is <0. The annotated epithelial cells in scRNA-seq were used to align with subclones in scDNA-seq for the same patient.

scAlign performs clonal assignments of scRNA-seq cells onto scDNA-seq subclones

We developed a computational framework, scAlign, which utilized a projection-mapping-based approach to integrate the paired scRNA-seq and scDNA-seq datasets obtained from the same tumor sample and make clonal assignments. We used the scDNA-seq results as a ground truth dataset for the subclonal structure. For clonal alignment, we evaluated each cell’s gene expression against the potential effects of gene dosage - regions of the genome exhibiting higher CNVs can lead to elevated gene expression values, and vice versa. First, we scaled and normalized the gene expression matrix using log-transformation. Second, for a given sample we selected the genes located on chromosomes with aneuploidy and other CNV features that defined specific subclones. These chromosomal segments provided “anchors” for evaluating whether a given cell’s gene expression demonstrated an effect from gene dosage. The evaluation of gene dosage provides a projection of the scRNA-seq results onto the genomic DNA segments that defined specific subclones. Third, to optimize the projection mapping of specific cells into a given subclone, we determined which factors were solved by minimizing the loss function, which was constructed based on the genomic distance of single-cell two assays.

Copy number inferred from scRNA-seq data

To confirm whether the epithelial and other cell types had CNVs, we applied inferCNV (v.1.2.2) [24]. This program determines the large-scale chromosomal copy number alternations from scRNA-seq gene expression data. This analysis was used on the samples which had been confirmed as cancer versus matched normal tissue. For the samples with paired adjacent normal tissue, the normal epithelial cells originated were used as an internal reference group in inferCNV. For the metastatic samples without matched normal tissue, the reference cell cluster parameter was set as “NULL”. The raw count matrix of scRNA-seq data was input with cell cluster labels assigned to scDNA subclones. To enable the CNVs predicted accuracy, the rest parameters were set as “cutoff = 0.1″, “denoise = T”, and “HMM = T”, respectively.

Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment analysis

We performed differential gene expression analysis among the identified subclones using “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat package, with the parameters “min.pct = 0.25” and “logfc.threshold = 0.25”. The differentiated genes are specific to subclones of epithelial cells in the G0/G1 phase of cell cycle, derived from scRNA-seq data. Additionally, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis based on cancer hallmark signatures downloaded from MSigDB (v.6.2) [21, 28]. The fgsea [29] (v.1.24.0) package was applied to identify significant pathways within subclones based on the differential genes list and setting the parameters “minSize = 10”, “maxSize = 500”, and “scoreType = pos”.

The cancers included three primary gastric adenocarcinomas (P5846, P5847, P5931) and five CRCs (P5915, P6198, P6335, P6461, P6593). There was a subset of six matched normal tissues. For the three gastric cancers, we had tissue originating from the stomach with matched normal stomach tissue per prior histopathologic review. We had metastatic CRCs from five individuals. For two CRCs (P6461, P6593), we had both the primary colon cancer and the metastatic CRC sites (Supplementary Table S1).

Evaluation of cells in G0/G1 improves the evaluation of cell genomic instability

To define the subclonal architecture of the tumors using scDNA-seq, we first assigned individual cells into diploid, G0/G1, and S phases based on whole-genome CNV profiles (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Normal diploid cells do not have focal CNVs and provide a useful control [30]. Tumor cells in the G0/G1 phase exhibited a consistent and stable CNV profile, while those in the S phase displayed dynamic and transient CNV alternation due to ongoing DNA replication [1, 31]. We applied the statistical approach to cell cycle classification, which leveraged whole-genome CNV dissimilarity, a comprehensive metric that captures ploidy-related variability of individual cells across cell cycles and reflects the true biological difference at the CNV level.

To assess the accuracy of the cell cycle classification, we quantified the concordance of subclonal CNV patterns between G0/G1 and S phase cells. Specifically, the S phase cells were assigned to subclones detected in G0/G1 phase by maximizing Pearson correlation coefficients across their whole-genome CNV profiles. The CNV pattern of each subclone was defined as the average of CNV values across all single cells assigned to that subclone. We then quantified the concordance of corresponding subclonal CNV profiles between two phases, with particular focus on whether CNV values in the S phase reflected the doubling relative to G0/G1 due to DNA replication dynamics.

In sample P5931, subclones identified in the S phase exhibited CNV patterns that were approximately double those of their corresponding G0/G1 subclones, consistent with DNA replication dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S1A). For instance, subclone C1 in the G0/G1 phase showed amplifications on chromosomes 7 and 20, each with a CNV value of 3. In the corresponding S phase subclone, these segments showed CNV values near 6. Similarly, genomic regions that were diploid (CNV = 2) in C1 of the G0/G1 phase showed CNV values around 4 in the S phase. Comparable two-fold CNV increases were observed across other subclones within this sample. Extending this analysis across the cohort, subclonal CNV profiles between G0/G1 and S phase cells demonstrated robust concordance. Across the cohort, the Pearson correlation coefficients for subclones’ segmental CNVs between the two phases were generally greater than 0.6, indicating a high degree of concordance in CNV profiles. Furthermore, the ratios of subclonal CNVs between S phase and G0/G1 were typically centered around 2, consistent with the expected doubling associated with DNA replication, while some regions exhibited ratios closer to 1.5, indicative of partial replication states (Supplementary Fig. S1B). These results confirm that S phase CNV profiles reliably reflect DNA replication-driven increases and align with their corresponding G0/G1 subclones.

We further validated the accuracy of our cell cycle classification using an independent scDNA-seq dataset generated from gastric cell lines using the same sequencing platform [1]. Application of our classification algorithm yielded G0/G1 and S phase proportions that showed strong consistency with the reported results in the original publication [1]. Notably, these results demonstrated high concordance: the proportion of G0/G1 phase cells yielded a correlation coefficient of 0.86 (P = 0.0027), while S phase proportions showed an even higher correlation coefficient of 0.91 (P = 0.00068) relative to the published results (Supplementary Fig. S1C). These findings highlight the robustness and generalizability of our cell cycle classification method in accurately distinguishing G0/G1 and S phase cells across external datasets.

Next, we examined the distribution of cell cycle phases—diploid, G0/G1, and S phases in both adjacent normal and tumor tissues based on scDNA-seq profiles. In adjacent normal samples, diploid cells constituted the majority with ranging from 90% to 97%, except in the patient P5847, where around 32% of cells were in G0/G1 and S phases of tumor cells. This deviation is likely due to tumor-normal interface between adjacent tissues. In contrast, the matched tumor samples have a significant reduction in diploid cells. For example, in the P6198 liver metastasis, only 5% of cells were detected as diploid, compared to 96% in its adjacent normal tissue (Supplementary Table S4). The proportion of S phase cells varied from 2% in the P6593 CRC liver met to 53% in the P6461 colon tumor. In adjacent normal samples, S phase cells were generally infrequent (1% to 5%), except for P5847, which showed a proportion of 12%. On average, the G0/G1 phase cells accounted for ∼25% across the tumor samples, in contrast to the ∼5% in the adjacent normal samples. Notably, G0/G1 cells had clearly identifiable CNV events that were present across a larger number of cells compared to the cells in other cell cycle phases (Fig. 1B) [1].

Furthermore, we evaluated the extent of CIN among the tumor cells in the G0/G1 and S phases compared to cells with diploidy. We calculated the CIN of each cell as the proportion of the large-scale CNV gains and losses across the entire genome [32–34]. Among the sets of cancers, we observed varying levels of CIN among different cell cycle components. The average CIN fraction in cells with diploids ranged from 10% to 15%, and this level was consistent in adjacent normal samples. The S phase cells displayed significantly higher CIN in cancer samples than adjacent normal samples. This difference was a result of the ongoing DNA replication during cell division. Notably, the CIN percentage of G0/G1 cells was smaller than S phase cells (Fig. 1C), indicating a more stable CNV pattern compared with replication (1). Based on this result, our subsequent analysis of the CNVs focused on cells in the G0/G1 phase, which exhibited subclonal CNVs without the variation introduced by cell replication (Supplementary Table S4). The number of cells ranged from less than ten up to hundreds. The liver metastases had the lowest number given that these tumors had been exposed to treatment prior to being removed surgically.

Single cell genomic instability events among gastric and CRCs

We investigated the extent of aneuploidy, chromosomal allelic imbalances (gains and losses), and large segmental CNVs with a length of 1 MB or greater among the individual cells from gastric and CRCs. We calculated the frequency of focal events of varying segment sizes for each cell. The primary gastric cancers (P5846, P5847, P5931) had CNVs that ranged from 1–5 MB, which contributed to ∼30% of all genomic aneuploidy events per sample (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

We compared chromosomal arm allelic imbalances derived from scDNA-seq of gastric and CRCs with genomic CNV data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). For gastric cancer, we used the TCGA stomach adenocarcinoma samples. Overall, we observed concordant patterns of genomic aberrations on some chromosomal arms. Between the two datasets, six overlapping chromosomal arm imbalances were among the top 10 most frequent changes. The most frequent amplification imbalances included chromosome arms 7(p,q), 8q, and 20(p,q), while deletion imbalances were common in chromosomes 21q (Supplementary Fig. S2B). For the CRC samples, we performed the same comparison with the TCGA colorectal adenocarcinoma dataset [35]. Consistent patterns of chromosomal-arm-level genomic aberrations were identified, with frequent amplification imbalances included chromosome arms 7(p,q), 8(p,q), 12(p,q), 13q, and 20q, and deletion imbalances on chromosomes 8q (Supplementary Fig. S2C). These large-scale chromosomal alterations were present in the gastrointestinal cancer analyzed with scDNA-seq and TCGA data [36].

The CRCs (P5915, P6198, P6335, P6461, P6593) had individual cells with extensive levels of aneuploidy and chromosomal alterations. The P6593 primary CRC and the P6198 metastatic CRC in the liver showed extensive aneuploidy. For these tumors, the CNV segment length generally was >10 Mb, which accounted for ∼30% of all genomic aneuploidy events. Notably, the frequency of amplification events was consistently higher than the deletions, regardless of the segment size. In total, we observed an average 70% frequency for amplification events. The two CRC metastases (P6461, P6198) to the liver had the highest proportion of amplification events among all samples, exceeding 88.0% and 89.7%, respectively.

Integration of tumor epithelial cells in scRNA-seq with subclones from scDNA-seq using scAlign

To characterize the single-cell gene expression properties of these gastrointestinal cancers’ subclones, we conducted scRNA-seq on the same cell suspensions used in the scDNA-seq analysis. The scRNA-seq datasets included both primary and metastatic tumors, with sequencing metrics summarized in Supplementary Table S3. We implemented quality control filtering for the scRNA-seq data (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). Epithelial cells were identified by their high expression of canonical epithelial markers (Supplementary Table S7). Then, the epithelial single cell data underwent independent analysis for subsequent subclonal integration analysis.

Our prior study of gastric cancer cell lines showed the significance of cell cycle effects on subclone integration [1]. In alignment with the cell cycle classification procedure used for scDNA-seq data, we distinguished epithelial cells in the scRNA-seq into different cell cycle phases, focusing specifically on G0/G1 phase cells for integration (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). To evaluate concordance between two modalities, we compared the proportions of G0/G1 and S phase cells in each sample. Cell cycle phase distribution showed a positive correlation between scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq across seven samples (Spearman’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\rho = 0.71,\ P = 0.08$\end{document}), indicating cross-modality consistency (Supplementary Fig. S5).

By identifying genes located on chromosomes with aneuploidy or arm imbalances, we established CNV anchor segments for integrating the results from the scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq. For this process, we developed a projection mapping algorithm, termed scAlign, used for clonal assignment that relies on the paired scRNA-seq and scDNA-seq data from the same tumor specimen (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). This method had two major steps. The first involves assigning epithelial cells to subclones. The second step involved validating the subclone assignment based on inferred CNVs.

The scDNA-seq’s subclonal architecture of cells in G0/G1 phase served as the reference standard for determining the clones from the scRNA-seq. We then focused on using the scRNA-seq data from epithelial cells in the G0/G1 phase. To map the subclonal populations present in both single cell data sets, we leveraged the gene expression profile of G0/G1 cells from scRNA-seq and the potential effects of a gene dosage increase or decrease. Some genes with an increased copy number will have elevated gene expression via a gene dosage effect. The same relationship holds true for decreased copy number. The optimal projection mapping involved in scAlign reflects the impact of gene dosage as denoted by a relationship between expression levels and large copy number events such as chromosomal arm imbalances, aneuploidy, and extended segments of the genome with increased copy number. We mapped those cells which had altered gene expression to subclonal-defining CNVs. This process allowed us to assign specific tumor cells to a given subclone. We used the scRNA-seq data to determine subclonal CNVs among the epithelial cells. With the InferCNV [24] program, we identified genomic segments showing copy number changes extrapolated from the single cell gene expression. As a final confirmation, these results were compared to the CNVs from the scDNA-seq results.

As an example, we show the results of scAlign, which assigned cells from scRNA-seq to the subclonal structure defined by scDNA-seq based on the P5931 gastric cancer. The scDNA-seq analysis of cells in the G0/G1 phase revealed four subclones (C1–C4) (Fig. 5A). By applying the scAlign to the scRNA-seq data, the cancer epithelial cells in the G0/G1 phase were successfully assigned to three of the four subclones determined from scDNA-seq. Subsequently, we used scRNA-seq data to detect large-scale CNVs within cells assigned to each subclone and compared these CNVs with results determined by scDNA-seq. InferCNV was applied to identify the large-scale copy number alterations from scRNA-seq; the results were generally consistent with the subclonal structure identified by scDNA-seq (Fig. 5B) [24]. Notably, the C2 subclone was characterized by an amplification on chromosome 7 which distinguished it from C1 and C3; while a deletion on chromosome 21 distinguished C1 apart from C2 and C3.

To assess the accuracy of scRNA-seq subclone assignment, we used the Pearson correlation coefficients to compare the inferred CNVs of scRNA-seq’s assigned subclones with the CNV profiles of subclones from the scDNA-seq. The correlation coefficient values ranged from 0.72 to 0.86, indicating a strong correspondence between two modalities in clonal assignment (Fig. 5C). Additionally, we utilized a permutation test to determine the RMSE for clonal assignments. The RMSE values obtained were significantly lower than those derived from random permutation, further validating the accuracy of the subclone assignments (Fig. 5D).

As a control, the scAlign was applied to other cell types. In addition to epithelial cells, the data set included fibroblasts, endothelial, macrophages, dendritic, B plasma, and T cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Among the various cell types, epithelial cells had the highest correlation with their matched scDNA-seq somatic CNV calls (Supplementary Fig. S6B). This result showed that tumor epithelial cells exhibited a consistent CNV profile matching the scDNA-seq data. This finding demonstrated that clonal assignment for scRNA-seq data should be on the epithelial cell type.

We applied the scAlign to the remaining primary gastric and CRCs and assigned epithelial cells to the distinct subclones based on CNV. The results demonstrated that the inferred CNVs of the assigned clones of single-cell gene expression were consistent with the subclonal profiles defined by their corresponding scDNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. S7). For example, in P5847 primary gastric cancer, two subclones were distinguished by an amplification event on chromosome 8, with inferred CNVs from scRNA-seq in concordance with those identified by scDNA-seq.

For these other cancers, the Pearson correlation coefficients of subclones indicated a strong alignment between scRNA-seq and scDNA-seq assignment. Additionally, the RMSE values were significantly lower compared to random permutations. This result is indicative of the accuracy of our assignment method. Specifically, when performing the permutation test for RSME, the P-values were smaller than the significant level (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha =$\end{document} 0.05) which means a significant difference between the RMSE of our clonal assignment compared with random assignment for those samples (P5847, P6335, P6593).

For the assigned subclones in scRNA-seq, we observed the significant chromosomal-level gene dosage effect in both primary gastric and metastatic colon cancers. Subclones with chromosomal amplifications showed higher MS for genes on these chromosomes, compared to subclones without the amplification, and vice versa (Supplementary Figs S8 and S9). For example, in P5847 primary gastric cancer, subclone C2 displayed a chromosome 8 amplification identified by scDNA-seq, with its MS for chromosome 8 significantly higher than that of C1. This indicates a dosage effect in scRNA-seq, where amplified regions lead to increased aggregated gene expression in specific subclones.

In general, the results of scAlign demonstrated high concordance between the subclones identified between the scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq. We did determine that the size of the subclone had an impact on the matching process. Based on the scDNA-seq analysis, smaller subclones with an abundance of <5% were not well represented scRNA-seq data. For example, P5931’s C4 subclone made up 3.6% of the total cells. This subclone was not detected in the scRNA-seq data. We cite another example. Based on the scDNA-seq analysis, P6335’s C2 subclone made up 3% (e.g. 28 cells) of the total. This subclone was not detected among the scRNA-seq data.

scAlign outperforms existing subclonal integration methods across patient samples

To evaluate the performance of scAlign, we performed a benchmarking analysis against two representative integration approaches: Clonealign [39], a probabilistic framework designed to resolve the underlying clonal structure from matched scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq data, and a correlation-based method, which assigns scRNA-seq cells to DNA-derived subclones by maximizing the Pearson correlation between gene expression and CNV patterns, reflecting a dosage effect. All three methods follow a map-to-reference strategy, which using scDNA-seq subclones as the reference for integration.

For consistency, subclones derived by scAlign from G0/G1-phase cells were used as reference input for both Clonealign and the correlation-based method. All methods were applied using their default parameters. Notably, Clonealign implemented strict quality control criteria for scRNA-seq, which led to the elimination of many cells and a high proportion of unassigned cells (Supplementary Fig. S10 and Supplementary Results). As a result, Clonealign produced reliable outputs for only five samples, and within those, only 10%–30% of cells were successfully assigned to subclones after filtering.

To quantitatively evaluate integration accuracy, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between the inferred CNVs of assigned scRNA-seq cells and the CNV profiles of their matched subclones. In sample P5931, scAlign achieved the highest correlations across subclones C1, C2, and C3 (0.72, 0.86, and 0.81, respectively), outperforming both Clonealign and the correlation-based method (Supplementary Fig. S10). Similar trends were observed in P5847 subclones C1 and C2, and in subclone C3 of P6335, further demonstrating the superior performance of scAlign across diverse patient datasets.

In addition to our own data, we evaluated scAlign using the ovarian cancer (OV) dataset previously analyzed in the Clonealign study. This dataset includes two cell lines, OV-2295(R) and TOV-2295(R), derived from the same patient, with ground truth clonal annotations provided in the original publication [39]. scAlign successfully identified the two known subclones from the scDNA-seq data. We then applied both scAlign and Clonealign to assign scRNA-seq cells to these two subclones. Both methods demonstrated clear separation of the two subclones, each characterized by distinct inferred CNV profiles based on the scRNA-seq subclones (Supplementary Fig. S11). Subclonal assignment accuracy was assessed using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), with scAlign and Clonealign achieving ARI scores of 0.85 and 0.86, respectively. These results confirm that scAlign can reliably recover clonal structures in datasets with ground truth labels.

Subclonal differential gene expression among primary gastric epithelial tumor cells

For three gastric cancers (P5846, P5847, P5931) we performed differential expression analysis and gene set enrichment analysis for each of the major subclones. These cancers were carcinomas, meaning that they originated from epithelial cells. In addition, this set of cancers had matching normal tissue for this comparison. We compared normal epithelial cells to subclones from primary gastric cancers. The top 20 differentiated genes of subclones were detailed in Supplementary Table S8. The P5846 tumor was a gastric adenocarcinoma with mucinous and signet ring features [40]. It had only one major subclone (C1) (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The P5847 tumor was a gastric adenocarcinoma with two subclones (C1 and C2) (Fig. 2D). Per the scDNA-seq data, the C1 subclone had a dominant feature of chromosome 7 amplification. Per the scRNA-seq data, we determined that the C1 subclone had increased expression of TFF3 and OLFM4, which are associated with intestinal stem cells (Supplementary Table S8). The PRSS2 gene, located at 7q34, had increased expression. PRSS2 promotes the migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells through the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process [41]. The C2 subclone was dominated by the amplification event on chromosome 8 where the MYC gene is located. In contrast, the C2 subclone had lower expression of those genes but showed higher expression of RNA translation-related genes, such as EIFs and PABPC1. This subclone also had upregulated expression of the MYC oncogene, located at 8q24, with increased both copy number and mRNA expression, indicated as one of the driver mutations in gastric tumors [42]. Additionally, the C2 subclone had enhanced activity of the MYC pathway compared to the C1 subclone (Supplementary Table S9).

The scRNA-seq of P5931 tumor was a gastric adenocarcinoma with identified three subclones (C1, C2, C3) (Fig. 5B). The normal gastric epithelial cells included chief, mucous, and parietal cells, with highly expressed genes including PGA5, PGC, PGA4, MUC6, and GIF. In contrast, the tumor subclones had decreased expression of these same genes. The C1 subclone had higher expression for the following genes: TFF1, TFF2, and TFF3 which are normally expressed in the intestine; CTSB which is associated with ECM remodeling activity; LCN2 which participates in mediating metastasis and migration. The C2 subclone showed elevated expression of a distinct set of genes including TMEM176A, TMEAM176B, LMTK3, CXCL1,and STEAP1 which are associated with gastric cancer (Supplementary Table S8). This subclone also showed gene set enrichment in the EF2 pathway. The C3 subclone had overexpression differentiated genes including CD24, CTSB, RACK1, and SOD1. Subclonal differential gene expression in colorectal tumor epithelium

For the five CRCs (P5915, P6198, P6335, P6461, P6593) and their major subclones, we conducted differential expression and gene set enrichment analysis (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). These CRCs included three metastases (P5915, P6198, P6335) to either the liver or the omentum. The two remaining sets of samples (P6461, P6593) had matched primary and metastatic cancer. Unlike the gastric cancers, we did not have a matched normal colon tissue for comparison. Therefore, for this analysis, we used a reference normal colon epithelium data set [43] to evaluate subclonal heterogeneity. We applied the Harmony on the data to reduce the impact of batch effects [44].

We evaluated the three isolated CRC metastases (P5915, P6198, P6335) (Supplementary Table S9). For the P5915 liver metastasis, there was only a single subclone (C1) which showed significant pathway enrichment for allograft rejection, MTORC1 signaling, MYC signaling, and the unfolded protein response which mitigates protein-folding stress at the endoplasmic reticulum. Likewise, for the P6198 liver metastasis, there was only one subclone (C1) which had significant pathway enrichment for E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and MYC signaling.

For the P6335 omental metastasis, there were three subclones (C1, C2, and C3) identified from scDNA-seq. This metastasis had extensive aneuploidy that affected most of the chromosomes (Fig. 3D). From the scRNA-seq analysis, we mapped the C1 and C3 subclone. The C2 subclone had too few cells to be identified from the scRNA-seq. The C1 subclone had a diploid representation of chromosome 3 while the C3 subclone had an increased copy number of this chromosome. Both subclones (C1 and C3) had common features including the upregulation of an immune pathway associated with allograft rejection as well as MYC signaling (Fig. 6A). However, the C3 subclone had distinct features which included upregulation in multiple pathways such as the apical junction expression, complement function, inflammatory response, interferon-alpha response, interferon-gamma response, myogenesis, and TNFA signaling via NFKB. These findings underscore the diverse pathway activities associated with distinct subclones and their potential roles in tumor progression and metastasis.

Matched primary and metastatic CRC subclones show differential expression

The P6593 CRC had matched primary and metastatic cancers. Thus, we made comparisons between subclones of the primary tumor and the metastasis. The P6593 primary CRC had four subclones (Fig. 4D) while the matching liver metastasis had only a single subclone. The primary CRC subclones (C1, C2, C3, and C4) were defined by different combinations of arm imbalances involving 3q, 11q, and 21q and a gain of chromosomes 13. Multiple pathways differentiated these subclones’ phenotypes (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Table S9). For example, the C2 subclone had increased pathway activities that include allograft rejection, EMT, KRAS signaling, myogenesis and TNF-alpha signaling. The C3 clone did not have elevated activity in several of these pathways. There was more overlap between the C2 and C4 subclone. The liver metastasis had significant upregulation of two interferon response pathways that were not observed among the primary CRC subclones. These finding align with the established roles of interferons response pathways implicated in cancer immune surveillance and immunoediting [45]. Specifically, tumor cells that escape initial immune-mediated elimination may persist in a state of immune-controlled equilibrium, during which they can acquire immune-evasive properties. This evolutionary adaptation can ultimately drive tumor progression and metastasis. In metastatic CRC, alterations in the interferon response signaling are frequently observed and have been associated with enhanced immune evasion and resistance to therapy.

For the P6461 primary CRC, there were a total of four subclones (C2, C3, C4, C5, and the metastasis) per the single cell gene expression data. Both the C4 subclone and the liver metastasis subclone had the same significant pathway as MYC signaling (Supplementary Table S9). Elevated MYC signaling pathway promotes stress adaptation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion to drive cancer development and therapeutic resistance through broad changes in transcriptional and translational landscapes [46]. The shared activation of MYC signaling between the C4 and metastatic subclones suggests that a pre-existing MYC-active clone in the primary tumor may have contributed to the formation of the liver metastasis following chemotherapy. Overall, these findings highlight the complex and distinct pathway activities between metastatic and primary tumor subclones.

Discussion

A key feature of cancer is its underlying cellular diversity, characterized by multiple intratumoral subclones with distinct genomic and cellular properties. Single-cell multi-omics technologies enable the identification of genomics and transcriptomic characteristics of cancer cells. ScDNA-seq technology allows for the reconstruction of tumor subclone architectures by genomic CNV alterations. However, scDNA-seq alone does not reveal the phenotypic information of subclones. In contrast, scRNA-seq can provide gene expression that indicates cellular transcriptomics biology, but it has limitations in defining subclones [8]. Therefore, the integration of matched scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq is essential to link genomic subclones to transcriptional phenotypes and better understand tumor evolution.

Notably, there is a lack of high-throughput single-cell technologies that can simultaneously measure both copy number variants and gene expression within the same cell barcode. Although a variety of single-cell multi-omics technologies, such as G&T-seq [47], DR-seq [48], and scTrio-seq [49] enable jointly profiling the genome and transcriptome from the same single cell. These techniques are low throughput, typically sequencing a limited number of cells (<100) per sample. The restricted cellular throughput significantly reduces the capacity to resolve intertumoral subclones. To address these limitations, we conducted the high-throughput scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq on cell suspensions derived from the same tumor tissue. This approach allowed us to characterize thousands of cells for each assay per sample. We extended this multi-omics approach on a set of gastric and CRCs from surgical biopsies.

To facilitate cross-modality integration, we developed a reference-map-based computational framework, scAlign, which projects transcriptomics profiles from scRNA-seq onto subclones defined by scDNA-seq. The performance of scAlign highly relies on the resolution and accuracy of subclone delineation derived from scDNA-seq. To ensure reliable integration, we first classified cell cycle phases across both scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq data. Subclonal architecture was constructed from G0/G1 phase cells in the scDNA-seq data, which was used as the reference subclones for integration. For scRNA-seq data, only epithelial cells in the G0/G1 phase were used for integration to enhance the stability and accuracy of CNV-based clonal assignment.

Distinguishing the cell populations based on their cell cycle phase significantly enhanced the quality of the integrated results. Focusing on G0/G1 phase cells improved the accuracy of subclonal reconstruction from scDNA-seq by reducing confounding noise from diploid and replicating cells. This refinement enabled more precise delineation of CNV-driven subclonal architecture. Notably, we observed that considering the cell cycle features significantly improved the integration performance (Supplementary Fig. S12). This is consistent with our prior study of gastric cancer cell lines, which showed the significance of cell cycle effects on subclonal analysis [1].

We performed a comprehensive benchmarking analysis to evaluate the performance of scAlign in comparison to existing multi-omics integration algorithms [39, 50–54]. Representative methods include Clonealign and a correlation-based method, both also utilize a reference-map-based framework to align scRNA-seq cells to DNA-based subclones. Despite their conceptual similarity, in this study, these methods demonstrated limited correspondence between paired scDNA and scRNA from primary and metastatic tumors. This issue results from the high intratumoral heterogeneity and the complex tumor microenvironment in those clinical specimens.

The application of scAlign within a high-throughput multi-omics dataset successfully identified both the genomic CNV variation, subclonal structure, and transcriptomics phenotype of each cancer subclone. Firstly, we defined subclonal architecture based on genomic instability patterns such as aneuploidy, chromosomal arm imbalances, and long segmental CNVs. The scDNA-seq results provided large-scale CNVs at single-cell resolution and characterized subclone architectures of tumors. scRNA-seq, applied to the same tumor cell population, provided gene expression information across many cells. Using scAlign to integrate these two modalities, we assigned gene expression profiles to corresponding subclones and determined their phenotypic properties from each subclone. The high number of cells from scRNA-seq made it feasible to determine the subclones’ gene expression properties and derive biological pathway activity. Overall, our results provided valuable insights into how large-scale chromosomal changes define a tumor’s subclonal architecture and variation in gene expression properties.

This study also showed that there is significant divergence in metastatic subclones from the primary tumor. From the comparison of the matched primary CRCs and their liver metastasis, we observed a prevalent and aberrant activation of several specific pathways. Notably, the upregulation of interferon response and MYC signaling pathways in CRC liver metastases plays critical roles in promoting immune evasion, tumor progression, and resistance to therapy [45, 46]. Primary colon tumors exhibited higher degrees of intracellular heterogeneity compared to the matched liver metastases. Specifically, our analysis of two of the primary CRC samples (P6461 and P6593) revealed the presence of multiple distinct subclones. In comparison, the metastases showed extensive aneuploidy. Interestingly, tumor cells were present despite prior chemotherapy treatment. Thus, the metastatic tumor cells with increased levels of aneuploidy were resistant to prior chemotherapy treatment with DNA damage agents.

In summary, this study contributes new biological insights for the molecular mechanism studies of gastric and CRC, offering potential implications for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. We applied a single-cell multi-omics integration approach to characterize the genotype-phenotype characteristics of subclones within a given tumor. The scDNA-seq provided high-resolution clonal lineage tracking with genomic CNV changes for primary cancers and metastasis. Then, we assigned the epithelial cells with G0/G1 phase, as determined through their scRNA-seq profile, to the corresponding scDNA-seq subclones. The integration from scAlign revealed the phenotypic features and their distinct biological characteristics of specific subclones. Overall, these findings can shed light on the molecular understanding of tumorigenesis mechanisms of gastrointestinal tumors, insight about the subclonal phenotypes of tumors, and will be useful in defining how subclonal populations respond to different therapies [55, 56].
