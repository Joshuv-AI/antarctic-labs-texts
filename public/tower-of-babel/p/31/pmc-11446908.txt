Unravelling the molecular regulation network of carbon metabolism and lipid metabolism during seed development in Akebia trifoliata via integrated multi-omics analysis
Authors: Huijuan Liu, Jinling Li, Cunbin Xu, Hongchang Liu, Zhi Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Akebia trifoliata is a medicinal plant with high oil content and broad pharmacological effects. To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of key metabolic pathways during seed development, we conducted an integrated multi-omics analysis, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, exploring the dynamic changes in carbon and lipid metabolism. Metabolomics analysis revealded that glucose and sucrose levels decreased, while glycolytic intermediate phosphoenolpyruvate and fatty acids increased with seed development, indicating a shift in carbon flux towards fatty acid synthesis. Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses showed that 70 days after flowering, the expression levels of genes and proteins associated with carbon and fatty acid metabolism were upregulated, suggesting an increased energy demand. Additionally, LEC2, LEC1, WRI1, FUS3, and ABI3 were identified as vital regulators of lipid synthesis. By constructing a multi-omics co-expression network, we identified hub genes such as aroE, GAPDH, KCS, TPS, and hub proteins like PGM, PDH, ENO, PFK, PK, ACCase, SAD, PLC, and OGDH that play critical regulatory roles in seed lipid synthesis. This study provides new ideas for the molecular basis of lipid synthesis in Akebia trifoliata seeds and can facilitate future research on the genetic improvement through molecular-assisted breeding.

Introduction

Akebia trifoliata (Thunb.) koidz. is a perennial deciduous woody vine of the genus Lardizabalaceae, and is mainly distributed in East Asia1. The fruits contain high amounts of fats, proteins, vitamin C, soluble sugars, organic acids, and amino acids2, and have anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antitumour, and diuretic characteristics3. The seeds can reach up to 48.8% in oil content4, with the main fatty acids being oleic acid (47.03–47.37%), palmitic acid (20.62–21.42%), and linoleic acid (27.75–28.07%)4. The seed oil content of Akebia trifoliata (48.8%)4 is comparable to that of Acer truncatum, another woody plant with an oil content of 47.6%5, and falls within the range reported for Peony seeds (24–38%)1,6. The oleic acid content is higher than that of sesame oil (37.1%)7, while the linoleic acid content is comparable to peony oil (25.9%)6. Therefore, Akebia trifoliata is a valuable medicinal and edible fruit plant with the potential to be developed as a seed oil resource.

Central carbon metabolism provides carbon skeletons and energy necessary for the biosynthesis of metabolites such as sugars, fatty acids, and amino acids8. The primary metabolic pathways related to carbon metabolism are glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. The lipids in seeds are mostly triacylglycerols (TAG), formed by the esterification of three higher fatty acids with hydroxyl groups on three carbons of a glycerol molecule. The assembly of TAG (triacylglycerol) is essential for lipid synthesis during the seed development9. Lipid synthesis involves various regulatory networks, including glycolysis, starch metabolism, the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, and fatty acid biosynthesis10. Sucrose produced through photosynthesis serves as a carbon source, transported from leaves to seeds, where it undergoes glycolysis to produce phosphoenolpyruvate. Phosphoenolpyruvate is then utilized in the synthesis of acetyl coenzyme A, which is the substrate for the initial step of TAG synthesis11. Notably, fatty acid (FA) synthesis occurs within the plastids, where essential precursors and catalysts are abundant. In contrast, TAG assembly takes place outside the plastids, likely involving close connections with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and oil bodies, ensuring efficient storage of newly formed TAGs to provide essential nutrients for future plant growth12.

Lipid synthesis in plant seeds follows a complex pathway with three main steps10. First, sucrose is converted to pyruvate (PYR) via glycolysis in immature seeds. Pyruvate then undergoes enzymatic reactions to form saturated fatty acids bound to acyl carrier protein (ACP). These fatty acids are converted into acyl-CoA by long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase (LACS). Finally, acyl-CoA is transported to the endoplasmic reticulum, where it is further processed into diacylglycerol (DAG). The enzyme diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT) facilitates the conversion of DAG to triacylglycerol (TAG). Once TAG is produced in the endoplasmic reticulum, it is released into the cytoplasm as oil bodies. Fatty acid synthesis in plastids dictates the chain length and saturation level of fatty acids in seed oil. Modifications in enzymes such as FATA, FATB, 18:0-ACP desaturase (SAD), and KASII can affect both the chain length and saturation of fatty acids in seeds13. Seed development is also regulated by transcription factors including LEC1, LEC2, ABI3, VAL, WRI1, and FUS314. For Instance, overexpression of the Arabidopsis LEAFY COTYLEDON1 (LEC1) gene leads to a broad upregulation of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis, with LEC1 activity partly dependent on the action of ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE3 (ABI3), FUSCA3, and WRINKLED1 (WRI1) in controlling fatty acid biosynthesis15.

Multi-omics integrative analysis techniques combine data from various omics levels, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. These techniques utilize high-throughput sequencing, mass spectrometry, and bioinformatics tools to construct complex regulatory networks, exploring interactions and mechanisms underlying biological processes. Multi-omics studies investigating candidate genes involved in lipid synthesis have been carried out in various plant species, including walnut16, oilseed rape17, oil tea18, peony19, and soybean20. The genome of Akebia trifoliata has already been sequenced, and initial studies on the transcriptomics and proteomics of its fruits have been performed21–23. Nevertheless, an integrated multi-omics analysis necessary to fully understand the seed development in Akebia trifoliata is still incomplete. This study aimed to investigate the regulatory networks involved in carbon metabolism and lipid synthesis during seed development in Akebia trifoliata. To improve our understanding of this process, we utilized transcriptomics, proteomics, and broad–targeted metabolomics to clarify the complex interactions between genes, proteins, and metabolites. This study not only enriches the multi-omics databases but also provides valuable insights into plant lipid biosynthesis.

Materials and methods

Plant material

From May to September 2019, field visits were conducted and samples were collected at the teaching experimental site of Guizhou University (E106°68 ′36.51 ʺ, N°26.41′ 04.35 ʺ), Huaxi District, Guiyang city, Guizhou Province. Three plants were selected, and fruit were collected 30, 50, and 70 days after flowering (30 DAF, 50 DAF, and 70 DAF). Three biological replicates were collected for each stage. The peel and pulp were removed and the remaining material was promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome analyses.

Proteomic sequencing analysis based on data-independent acquisition (DIA) technology

Sample preparation included protein denaturation, reduction, alkylation, tryptic digestion and peptide cleanup. The commercially available iST Sample Preparation kit (PreOmics, Germany) was used. Briefly, after the samples were ground with liquid nitrogen, 50 µL of Lyse buffer was added and heated at 95 °C for 10 min at 1000 rpm with agitation. After cooling the sample to room temperature, trypsin digestion buffer was added, and the sample was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h at 500 rpm with shaking. The digestion process was stopped with addition of a stop buffer. Sample clean-up and desalting was carried out using the iST cartridge and the recommended wash buffers. Peptides were eluted with elution buffer (2 × 100 µl), and then lyophilized by SpeedVac.

The peptide mixture was dissolved in buffer A (buffer A: 20 mM ammonium formate in water, pH10.0, adjusted with ammonium hydroxide), and then fractionated by high pH separation using an Ultimate 3000 system (ThermoFisher scientific, MA, USA) connected to a reverse phase column (XBridge C18 column, 4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm, (Waters Corporation, MA, USA). High pH separation was performed using a linear gradient, starting from 5% B to 45% B in 40 min (B: 20 mM ammonium formate in 80% ACN, pH 10.0, adjusted with ammonium hydroxide). The column was re-equilibrated at the initial condition for 15 min. The column flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min and the column temperature was maintained at 30℃. Ten fractions were collected, and each fraction was dried in a vacuum concentrator prior to the next step.

The peptides were dissolved in 30 μL solvent A (A: 0.1% formic acid in water) and analyzed by on-line nanospray LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos coupled to EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). To do this, 3 μL peptide sample was loaded onto the analytical column (Acclaim PepMap C18, 75 μm × 25 cm) and separated with a 120-min gradient, from 5 to 35% B (B: 0.1% formic acid in ACN). The column flow rate was maintained at 200 nL/min with column temperature of 40 °C. The electrospray voltage of 2 kV versus the inlet of the mass spectrometer was used. The mass spectrometer was run under data dependent acquisition mode, and automatically switched between MS and MS/MS mode. The parameters were: (1) MS: scan range (m/z) = 350–1200; resolution = 120,000; AGC target = 400000; maximum injection time = 50 ms; Filter Dynamic Exclusion: exclusion duration = 30 s; (2) HCD-MS/MS: resolution = 15,000; AGC target = 50000; maximum injection time = 35 ms; collision energy = 32; stepped CE = 5%. (3) DIA was performed with variable Isolation window, each window overlapped 1 m/z, and the window number was 60.

Raw data of DIA were processed and analyzed by Spectronaut X (Biognosys AG, Switzerland) with default parameters. Retention time prediction type was set to dynamic iRT. Data extraction was performed using Spectronaut X based on extensive mass calibration. Spectronaut Pulsar (Kim et al. 2018) was used to determine the ideal extraction window dynamically depending on iRT calibration and gradient stability. Qvalue (FDR) cutoff at the precursor and protein levels was applied as 1%. Decoy generation was set to mutated, which is similar to scrambled but only applies a random number of AA position swaps (min = 2, max = length/2). All selected precursors passing the filters were included for quantification. The top three filtered peptides that passed the 1% Qvalue cutoff were used to calculate the major group quantities.

Analysis of differential expressed proteins, functional annotation and subcellular localization

Differentially expressed proteins were analyzed with Student’s t Test and Benjamini and Hochberg (BH). Next, different expressed proteins were filtered with the selection criteria of fold change > 1.5 or < 0.67 and Qvalue < 0.05.

Protein function and classification were analyzed based on searches against the gene ontology (GO), euKaryotic orthologous groups (KOG)/cluster of orthologous groups of proteins (COG), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)24 databases. The Venn diagram showed the distribution of genes and database matching results.

A protein’s function is usually related to its subcellular localization, and the ability to predict subcellular localization directly from protein sequences can be useful to infer protein functions. The software WoLFPSort25 was used to predict protein subcellular location and function.

Construction and sequencing of RNA library

Akebia trifoliata seeds were extracted for total RNA using the Trizol kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA). An Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) and RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis was used to assess RNA degradation. Total RNA after subtraction of rRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using random primers and the resulting cDNA libraries were sequenced on Illumina HiSeqTM 4000 (Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Guangzhou, China). The raw data were processed using fastp26 to eliminate reads with adapters, > 10% N, and low quality (Q ≤ 20 for more than 50% of the read). HISAT2 (2.1.0) was used to map filtered data to the Akebia trifoliata reference genome27. After reconstructing transcripts with StringTie (version 1.3.4)28, RSEM was used to calculate gene expression in each sample using the fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) method. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. To do this, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq229 software to compare two different groups (and edgeR30 to compared two samples). The genes with the parameter of false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered DEGs. DEGs were then subjected to enrichment analysis of GO functions and KEGG pathways.

Widely targeted metabolome sequencing

After removal from the freezer, the cryopreserved biomaterial samples were subjected to vacuum freeze-drying before being pulverized for 1.5 min at a frequency of 30 Hz using a grinder (MM 400, Retsch). Then 100 mg powder was weighed and extracted overnight at 4 °C with 1.0 ml 70% aqueous methanol containing 0.1 mg/L lidocaine for internal standard. The samples were vortexed three times during this period to ensure thorough extraction. Following centrifugation at 10 000 g for 10 min, the supernatant were absorbed and filtrated (SCAA-104, 0.22 μm pore size; ANPEL, Shanghai, China, www.anpel.com.cn/) before LC-MS/MS analysis.

The data acquisition instrumentation system primarily comprised of an Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) instrument (Shim-pack UFLC SHIMADZU CBM30A, http://www.shimadzu.com.cn/) and a tandem mass spectrometer (MS/MS) (Applied Biosystems 6500 QTRAP, http://www.appliedbiosystems.com.cn/). The API 6500 QTRAP LC/MS/MS system was used with specific settings for its linear ion trap and triple quadrupole components. These settings include an electrospray ionization (ESI) temperature of 550 °C, a mass spectrometry voltage of 5500 V, a curtain gas (CUR) pressure of 25 psi, and a collision-activated dissociation (CD) parameter set to high. The ESI temperature was set to 550 ℃, the mass spectrometry voltage was set to 5500 V, the curtain gas (CUR) pressure was set to 25 psi, and the collision-activated dissociation (CD) parameter was set to a high value. Each ion pair in the QQQ was analyzed by scanning with optimized declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE). The acquired data were analyzed using the software Analyst 1.6.1 (AB SCIEX).

Metabolites were identified by searching internal and public databases (MassBank, KNApSAcK, HMDB31, MoTo DB, and METLIN32 and comparing the m/z values, the RT, and the fragmentation patterns with the standards.

Analysis of partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal projection to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA)

Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to compare groups using R package models (http://www.r-project.org/). Orthogonal projection to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to compare groups using R package models (http://www.r-project.org/).

We performed multivariate statistical analysis of VIP values from OPLS-DA and univariate statistical analysis of T-test (P-values) to identify metabolites that exhibited significant differences between various comparison groups33. The criteria for determining significant differences were a VIP value greater than or equal to 1 and p-value was less than 0.05.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with R package gmodels (http://www.r-project.org/). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) between samples was utilized to help exclude outlier samples.

Data processing and analysis

Using Python scripts for calculating the correlations among transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, utilizing Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) for correlation analysis. Visualizing co-expression network graphs with Cytoscape v3.9.234. Graphpad Prism 6.0 is applied for t-test analysis and visualization of metabolite content.

Validation of the transcriptomic data by qRT-PCR

Nine DEGs were randomly selected to verify the reliability of the transcriptomic data by qRT-PCR. Primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software. Total RNA was extracted using cetyltrimethylammonium bromide, using RG 5 (EF- α) as a reference gene. Sixteen microliter samples were prepared on ice using the iScript™ gDNA Clear cDNA synthesis kit and incubated at 25 °C for 5 min and 5 min at 75 °C to remove gDNA. Next, 20 µl reverse transcription systems were prepared on ice and incubated for 5 min at 25 °C, 20 min at 46 °C, and 5 min at 95 °C, then cooled on ice and immediately used for qPCR. Reactions were performed using an ABI QuantStudio 6 Flex fluorescent PCR instrument and a SYBR Premix EX Taq kit. Experiments used 10 µl of qPCR Mix, 0.5 µL of each primer (10uM), 0.5 µL of cDNA and 8.5 µL of ddH2O. The amplification procedure was set as follows: 95 °C for 300 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C 10 s and 60 °C 30 s. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔ CT method, where the level of each gene 30 DAF was used as a control. Gene names and primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S10.

Metabolomic analysis of seed development in Akebia Trifoliata

A total of 422 metabolites of 13 classes were detected in 9 samples using an LC-MS-based broad target metabolomics method (Supplementary Table S1). Primary metabolites were identified, including 55 lipids (12.8%), 49 amino acids and derivatives (11.6%), 32 nucleotides and derivatives (7.6%), and 26 organic acids (6.2%). Secondary metabolites comprised 82 flavonoids (19.5%), 57 phenolic acids (13.5%), 29 alkaloids (6.9%), and 25 terpenoids (5.9%) (Fig. 1A). The results of the metabolomic analysis indicate that all samples can be separated at PCA1 (Supplementary Figure S1A). OPLS-DA analyses for each comparison group gave R2X values of S1 vs S2, S1 vs S3, and S2 vs S3 of 0.865, 0.924, and 1, respectively; the R2Y values were 0.999, 1, and 1, respectively; and the Q2 values were 0.993, 0.998, and 0.994, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1B), values that are very close to 1, indicating that the model was stable and reliable and can be used to identify changes in metabolites. Compared to the 30 days after flowering (DAF), we noted that 31 metabolites were upregulated at 50 DAF and 26 metabolites at 70 DAF, while 41 metabolites at 50 DAF and 56 at 70 DAF were downregulated (Fig. 1B). Metabolite levels such as D-glucose 6-phosphate and glucose-1-phosphate peaked at 70 DAF, showing a surge in carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 1C). In the lipid profile, LysoPC16:0 (2n isomer) and PC (18:2) isomer increased significantly at 50 DAF (Supplementary Table S1). Analysis of carbon metabolism pathways during seed development

From 30 to 70 DAF, our study discovered varied regulatory patterns of DEGs and DEPs within metabolic pathways. We analyzed changes in 112 DEGs and 75 DEPs involved in carbon metabolism and amino acid synthesis (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table S6). Key enzymes in carbohydrate metabolism such as sucrose synthase (SUS), invertase (INV), and trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) showed upregulation at 70 DAF, with a notable increase in glycolytic activity indicated by elevated levels of hexokinase (HK), phosphofructokinase (PFK), and pyruvate kinase (PK). Amino acid biosynthesis revealed increased expression of genes and proteins in the shikimate pathway, vital for aromatic amino acid production, while the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle exhibited mixed regulation, with enzymes like succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) being upregulated, contrasting with downregulation in pathways for methionine, leucine, and other non-aromatic amino acids. LAFL regulatory network

In the LAFL regulatory network, notable changes were observed in the expression of transcription factors FUS3, ABI3, LEC1, LEC2, and WRI1, indicating significant modulation (Supplementary Figure S6A, Supplementary Table S8). Enhanced expression was noted for certain genes at key developmental stages; for instance, two FUS3 genes, one ABI3 gene, and two LEC2 genes at 70 DAF, and two LEC1 genes and one WRI1 gene at 50 DAF, with LEC2 showing a 3.7-fold increase. These changes in transcription factor expression correspond to the fatty acid profiles in Akebia trifoliata seeds, including stearic, oleic, palmitic, and linoleic acids. The correlation analysis revealed that LEC1-1, LEC1-2, WRI1, and FUS3 are strongly linked (r > 0.9) to the expression of important enzymes in fatty acid synthesis, such as PDH, ACCase, FAD2, GPAT, KAS, DLAT, and MCAAT. LEC2-2 shows significant correlations with SAD, FATB, KCS, and GPAT, emphasizing its role in specific fatty acid pathways. Our results demonstrate that the transcription factors within the LAFL network, specifically FUS3, ABI3, LEC1, LEC2, and WRI1, enhance the expression and activity of critical enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis, such as PDH, ACCase, FAD2, and GPAT. This enhancement is not merely a general increase in activity but a targeted upregulation at crucial developmental phases, leading to significant increases in specific fatty acids such as stearic, oleic, and linoleic acids (Supplementary Figure S6B, Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

Fatty acids are not only an essential component of cell membranes but also a key substance for energy storage and signal transduction35. Thus, a comprehensive investigation of fatty acid metabolism is critical to understanding the mechanisms that regulate seed development. In this study, we investigated Akebia trifoliata seeds using an integrative approach that combines metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics to uncover the regulatory mechanisms involved in fatty acid metabolism and carbon metabolism during seed development. The multi-omics research approach enhances our understanding of the metabolic regulation mechanisms associated with seed development and provides innovative research ideas and methods that can be applied to other biological processes.

During seed maturation, significant changes in fatty acid synthesis were observed, closely linked to seed morphological changes. These findings were consistent with previous studies21. Compared to other plant seeds, the seeds of Akebia trifoliata exhibited a pattern of fatty acid accumulation similar to that of walnuts36. Analysis of metabolite changes revealed a decrease in glucose, sucrose, and trehalose levels, accompanied by an increase in the levels of fatty acids including C16:0, C18:0, C18:1, and C18:2 at 70 DAF. Additionally, at 70 DAF, the levels of the key glycolysis metabolite phosphoenolpyruvate and TCA cycle intermediates, such as succinate, malate, and citrate, increased. These metabolic shifts observed during the critical phase of seed development suggest a redirection of carbon flux from simple sugars towards fatty acid synthesis, which is crucial for efficient energy storage and membrane formation in maturing seeds37. The decrease in levels of glucose and sucrose may indicate their utilization as energy sources and substrates for fatty acid biosynthesis. Moreover, the accumulation of trehalose, a non-reducing sugar known for its involvement in stress responses and serving as a carbon reservoir, further supports this metabolic transition, highlighting an augmented demand for stress resilience and energy supply during this developmental stage38. The concurrent increase in fatty acids, such as C18:1 and C18:2, signifies the seed’s preparatory measures for desiccation and germination, given their indispensable role as constituents of cellular membranes and energy reservoirs39.

In the integrative analysis of transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics data, we identified DEGs, DEPs, and DAMs that participate in key metabolic pathways during seed development based on shared KEGG pathways. Notably, significant enrichment was observed in pathways related to starch and sucrose metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis and metabolism, secondary metabolite synthesis, and amino acid synthesis during seed development from 30 to 70 DAF. These pathways not only reflect the dynamic changes in nutrient needs during different developmental stages but also demonstrate how complex signaling networks coordinate various physiological activities. Seed development involves the dynamic regulation of metabolites, hormones, and energy metabolism, which drive the differentiation of embryos and storage tissues, maintaining efficient biosynthesis and energy balance under varying environmental conditions40. Additionally, studies have shown that, numerous plant species have mutants that significantly affect carbohydrate metabolism, exhibiting defective seed development41.

Glycolysis facilitates plant respiration and supplies the necessary carbon and energy for the biosynthesis of fatty acids, amino acids, and other compounds through the TCA pathways42. HK, PFK, and PK are pivotal enzymes in the glycolytic pathway. HK helps add a phosphate group to hexose, PFK helps convert fructose 6-phosphate into 1,6-bisphosphofructose, and PK facilitates production of ATP and pyruvate from phosphoenolpyruvate. At 70 DAF, there was increased expression of two HK, five PFK, and five PK proteins. Similar to Jatropha curcas, the PFK protein level was lowest at the initial stage of seed development in Akebia trifoliata43, indicating that PFK is important in the cytoplasmic glycolysis process for the later stages of Akebia trifoliata seeds. PK is crucial for the synthesis of precursors required for fatty acid biosynthesis. Inhibiting the activity of negative regulating factors of PDHC (such as acetonate dehydrogenase enzyme, pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase, PDK) can promote acetonic acid conversion to acetyl-CoA, increasing the amount of carbon sources entering the lipid synthesis pathway. Mutations in the Arabidopsis pkp1 and pkp2 genes, which encode plastid pyruvate kinase, result in reduced seed lipid content, significant alterations in fatty acid composition, and decreased embryonic elongation44. In addition, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) is a key enzyme positively related to precursors of oil synthesis, and enhancing its activity can promote oil accumulation45. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, PDHC, catalyzes the synthesis of acetyl-CoA from pyruvate, which is both an entry substance to the TCA cycle and a precursor to FA synthesis. Inhibition of the activity of negative regulators of mitochondrial PDHC, such as pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK), can promote the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and increase the amount of carbon source in the lipid synthesis pathway46.

Acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase (ACCase) regulates the flow of carbon into the fatty acid synthesis pathway. Overexpression of heterologous GhACCase increased seed oil content in upland cotton. For example, overexpression of GhBCCP1 significantly increased seed oil content by 21.92% in upland cotton47. KAS, HAD, ENR, and KAR are crucial enzymes involved in carbon chain elongation. Following the knockout of KASIII and FATB genes in canola, the content of various medium-chain fatty acids increased in the double mutants48. GPAT and DGAT are key enzymes that influence the efficiency of triacylglycerol assembly. The expression of the GPAT gene was found to be upregulated at 70 DAF in Akebia trifoliata seeds. GPAT is involved in the synthesis of glycerol esters in plants49. Overexpression of GPAT gene could increase the oil content of transgenic plants, while interference with the expression of the AtGPAT9 gene leads to a reduction in oil content in Arabidopsis seeds50. DGAT is the rate-limiting enzyme of the Kennedy pathway, and ectopic expression of the maize tall oil DGAT1-2 allele increased oleic acid and lipid content by 41 and 107%, respectively51. It is worth noting that although no significant change in DGAT protein levels was observed, we did observe an increase in mRNA expression of the DGAT gene at 70 DAF. This same pattern was also observed in Jatropha43. In tobacco leaves, transient co-expression of AtWRI1 and DGAT1 genes results in a noticeably increased oil yield52. We identified five LAFL transcription factors: FUS3, WR1, ABI3, LEC1, and two LEC2s, which likely positively regulate lipid synthesis. WRI1 regulates the expression of PDH, ACCase, and KASI in the FA synthesis and glycolysis pathways, thus helping to direct carbon flow into the seed for FA synthesis53. LEC1 and WRI1 have been found to be significantly involved in the production of maize seed oil54.

Furthermore, in addition to transcriptional and metabolic level regulation, sugar metabolism, fatty acid synthesis, and TAG assembly processes are also subject to post-transcriptional regulation. In general, only weak correlation was observed between mRNA expression levels and protein levels. This phenomenon could be attributed to the experimental setup and data analysis, or it could reflect regulation by various factors during the transcription of mRNA and the subsequent translation into proteins. This regulation process may cause a lack of correlation between the mRNA and the corresponding proteins55. For instance, the majority of proteins involved in the glycolytic pathway exhibit significant expression at 70 DAF, but the levels of mRNA expression are not always consistent with the protein levels. This discrepancy may be because glycolytic enzymes undergo various post-translational modifications that can influence their expression56. Based on our data, it appears that many glycolytic genes, such as HK, PFK, GAPDH, PGK, PGM, and PK, could potentially be regulated at the post-transcriptional level or subjected to post-translational modifications. This pattern is similar to what has been observed in Brassica napus43. The activities of PK and PEPC isozymes can be adjusted by amino acid alteration effectors such as glutamate, aspartate, citrate, and malate57. Furthermore, OGDH, SDH, and MDH have potential sites for phosphorylation and redox regulation58. Again, the lower correlation between DEGs and DEPs suggests that the expression of these isozymes may be regulated by independent transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms59. Overall, the integration of various omics data enables the acquisition of supplementary information and a more comprehensive understanding of changes in an organism’s gene expression60.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of carbon and lipid metabolism during seed development in Akebia trifoliata, offering a theoretical foundation and application prospects for genetic improvement to enhance seed oil content. Multi-omics analysis identified important targets for genetic enhancement, including LAFL transcription factors (LEC2, LEC1, WRI1, FUS3, and ABI3), critical genes (GAPDH, KCS, TPS), and proteins (PDH, ENO, PFK, PK, ACCase, SAD), which are crucial nodes in regulating seed lipid metabolism. These findings provide essential scientific evidence for future agricultural and medicinal development of Akebia trifoliata seed oil resources.
