East Victoria long term hydrodynamic modelling: Dataset and methodology
Authors: Dougal Greer, Rhys McIntosh, Mark Case, Dianne L McLean, Eric A Treml, Ronen Galaiduk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset is the output of a long term multi-resolution calibrated hydrodynamic model of Bass Strait waters in south-eastern Australia. The model is 3 dimensional with 16 sigma layers. It is forced by tides, wind, non-tidal sea level variability as well as salinity and temperature through a nudging scheme. The model was calibrated against existing data from previous fixed location instrument deployments and hull mounted ADCP data. While the model has limitations, it performs well against measured data and provides a useful tool for describing spatially varying currents throughout East Victorian waters.Keywords: Numerical modelling, Multi-resolution, 3 dimensional, Biophysical connectivity

Background

This dataset supports an original research article [1]. The dataset was developed to provide an understanding of the connectivity of marine biota on habitat created by platforms and subsea pipelines. Connectivity between habitats is dependent on currents generated by the interaction of multiple physical oceanographic processes. Numerical modelling provides a means to describe the cumulative effects of these processes. A hydrodynamic model was developed to simulate the principle physical drivers of currents in the east Victoria region for a 10-year period. This dataset contains the hourly model output from the modeling exercise provides the means to understand connectivity between the habitats under consideration for specific biota and formed the basis of subsequent connectivity modelling.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The hydrodynamic model has been developed using the Delft-FM model Suite [2]. Delft-FM implements an implicit finite volume solver on a staggered grid. Since Delft-FM is specifically designed for the use of unstructured meshes, cells are not computed in terms of rows and columns. Instead, the continuity equation is solved implicitly for all points in a single combined system. Time integration is computed explicitly for part of the advection term, and the resulting time-step is dynamically determined by the Courant criterion. The model was run using a k-epsilon turbulence scheme.

The simulations covered a 10-year period from 1994 to 2004, not including 1996, due to missing data in the HYCOM boundary conditions during this year. This is purely a hindcast model and it does not account for expected changes to hydrodynamics due to climate change. The model was run in year-long simulations with each simulation including 1 week of spin-up period. During the first 24 h of each model run, a relaxation scheme was applied to the open boundaries for sea level, temperature and salinity. The relaxation parameter α begins at 0 and reduces linearly to 1 at the end of the relaxation period. The length of the spin-up period was established by trial of range of different periods to establish the time required to achieve model stability. The model was forced by tides, wind and atmospheric pressure, temperature and salinity with wave-induced Stokes drift added as a post-processing step. Temperature and salinity were added through a nudging scheme similar to that used by [3]. The nudging scheme allows for the assimilation of salinity and temperature data from other models, in this case Hybrid Coordinate Ocean Model (HYCOM),1 and is applied in the model with a timestep of 1-day. The model does not incorporate atmospheric heat exchange and temperature and salinity fields are largely constrained by the nudging scheme. However, the 3D salinity and temperature fields do generate density driven currents which can interact with tidal or wind driven currents. Initial conditions for sea level, salinity and temperature were applied to the model to expedite model stability during the spin-up period.

The model grid was generated using the RGFGRID tool provided with the Delft FM model suite. This was used to create areas of high resolution over areas of interest (Fig. 1). While the grid was predominantly rectilinear, the refinement zones were made up of triangular and trapezoidal cells. The model has 16 vertical (sigma) layers and 53,658 model grid nodes. In deep water (depths > 250 m) the model had a resolution of 6960 m × 9600 m and this increased to 230 m × 290 m around infrastructure and/or natural reefs. Bathymetry data were sourced from the 2009 Geoscience Australia dataset2 [4] which covers Australia in its entirety and has a horizontal resolution of 9 arcsec (0.0025° or ∼250 m at the equator). The bathymetry was interpolated onto the model grid using 2D triangulation with Gaussian smoothing applied to the boundaries.

The final model grid used in the hydrodynamic simulations. Large cells represent coarse mesh resolution e.g., 6960 m × 9600 m whereas areas around EAPL infrastructure are represented by a finer mesh e.g., 230 m × 290 m.

Spatially and temporally varying wind boundary conditions were derived from the National Centers for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) Climate Forecast System Reanalysis (CFSR) models [5,6]. Model output is provided hourly3 with wind data on a grid with a resolution of 0.312 × 0.312° (increasing to 0.205 × 0.204° from 2011 onwards) and atmospheric pressure at a resolution of 0.5 × 0.5°.

Sea level variability on open boundaries were represented by hourly timeseries at discrete segments along the boundary and included tidal and non-tidal variability. Tidal timeseries were extracted from the TPXO wave atlas4 [7] using the South Pacific sub-model which has a resolution of 1/12 × 1/12°. The tidal constituents extracted were M2, S2, N2, K2, K1, O1, P1, Q1, M4, MS4 and MN4. This model was also used to generate initial model water level over the model domain.

Non-tidal 3-hourly open boundary conditions were sourced from the HYCOM. HYCOM model output is provided as 40 z-layers of salinity, temperature, velocity, and elevation which are available for the years of 1992 to 2012 from the GLBu0.08 reanalysis model. Sea level data were extracted from the HYCOM data server,5 combined with tidal boundary conditions, and applied to the open boundaries of the model as water level timeseries. Temperature and salinity data were also sourced from the same HYCOM model output and are applied as Dirichlet boundaries.

Wave induced Stokes drift is an x and y vector quantity which manifests in the direction of wave propagation. It is greatest at the surface and decreases with depth. Stokes drift was calculated separately to the hydrodynamic modelling using the method of [8] and added directly to the modelled velocities as a post-processing step. Model velocities were output on an hourly basis to capture hydrodynamic tidal and meteorological processes. The model output was then used as the basis of the subsequent connectivity modelling. Wave data were sourced from NOAA's WAVEWATCH III® 30-year Hindcast Phase 2.6 Data were extracted from the Australian 4-minute (preferentially) and 10-minute models both available at 3-hourly timesteps.

The scope of this study did not include gathering of in-situ oceanographic data. As part of the data review in this study existing measured datasets were identified that have been used for model calibration in this study. Among these are a series of multi-month measurements of currents, sea level and temperature near the Banks Strait north of Tasmania [9].

The model calibration was undertaken using data from the CWTb1 and CW4-A2 seabed deployments located at 40.67723°S, 148.22626°E and 40.729560°S, 148.344970°E, respectively (Fig. 2). The model was run for the periods of these deployments to allow for comparison. Resolution was increased at these locations for the calibration model runs. Several datasets were identified from hull mounted ADCP campaigns that provide a useful basis for model validation. In general, surface mounted ADCP data are less reliable than ADCP data collected from seabed frames. Measured currents include the ship velocity and the record has to be corrected to remove this signal. This can lead to the introduction of error [10]. Additionally, surface mounted ADCPs can suffer from poor performance due to bubbles adjacent to the transducer face and due to pitch and roll of the instrument motion of the instrument [11]. This would be exacerbated in energetic conditions (frequently experienced in Bass Strait) or when the vessel is traveling at high speed.

The model calibration made use of data from two voyages undertaken by the CSIRO owned and operated Franklin research vessel. The voyages were FR 09/987 which was undertaken in Victorian waters and Bass Strait during 21–29 July 1998 and FR 11/978 which was undertaken in the Victorian and eastern South Australian coastal waters between 10 and 20 December 1997. In both cases, the datasets provide current velocity from a hull mounted ADCP for the duration of the voyages. Comparisons between measured and modelled currents during the periods of the FR 11/97 are shown in Fig. 4. Overall, the model reproduces the measured signal well for both the U and V components.

Limitations

•The wave and current models are not coupled. This means that the wave field does not influence the currents and the currents do not alter the wave field.

The wave and current models are not coupled. This means that the wave field does not influence the currents and the currents do not alter the wave field.

•The local wave model is extracted from a global model and does not resolve local nearshore processes such as wave breaking, wave driven circulation.

The local wave model is extracted from a global model and does not resolve local nearshore processes such as wave breaking, wave driven circulation.

•Wind fields are extracted from global models which have a limited representation of landforms due to large-scale topographic resolution of underlying data. Consequentially, wind fields very close to coast may not account for fine-scale topographical features.

Wind fields are extracted from global models which have a limited representation of landforms due to large-scale topographic resolution of underlying data. Consequentially, wind fields very close to coast may not account for fine-scale topographical features.

•Salinity and temperature fields are applied through a nudging scheme to capture broadscale density driven currents. A consequence of this scheme is that it does not represent fine-scale changes to the temperature and salinity fields due to local bathymetric features and atmospheric processes.

Salinity and temperature fields are applied through a nudging scheme to capture broadscale density driven currents. A consequence of this scheme is that it does not represent fine-scale changes to the temperature and salinity fields due to local bathymetric features and atmospheric processes.

•The modelling relied on available measured current, sea level, temperature and salinity datasets for model calibration. As such, there were limited datasets available within the area of interest for model calibration and those that were available were limited in duration.

The modelling relied on available measured current, sea level, temperature and salinity datasets for model calibration. As such, there were limited datasets available within the area of interest for model calibration and those that were available were limited in duration.

•This model is a hindcast and does not account for the implications of climate change.
