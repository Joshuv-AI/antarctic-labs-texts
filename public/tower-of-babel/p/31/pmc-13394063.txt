Artificial intelligence in preclinical epilepsy research: Current state, potential, and challenges
Authors: Jesús Servando Medel‐Matus, Cesar Santana‐Gomez, Ruby G Escalante, Dominique Duncan, Pedro F Viana, Giulia Sofia Cereda, Naoto Kuroda, Aristea S Galanopoulou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreclinical translational epilepsy research uses animal models to better understand the mechanisms underlying epilepsy and its comorbidities, as well as to analyze and develop potential treatments that may mitigate this neurological disorder and its associated conditions. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative tool across various fields, including neuroscience research. AI can assist in the acquisition and analysis of data throughout the experimental process. Currently, the integration of AI techniques, including machine learning (ML), assumes an important role in preclinical epilepsy research. For analytical purposes, the techniques described in this review are categorized into three principal domains based on their objectives. Diagnosis involves identification, characterization, and/or prediction of epileptic seizures utilizing experimental data such as EEG recordings. Identification of comorbidities associated with epilepsy using AI represents a significant advancement in preclinical research. This approach can lead to a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between epilepsy and related disorders. The treatment domain involves the utilization of ML models to conduct simulations and computational analyses to comprehend the underlying mechanisms of epilepsy, discern potential drug targets, and evaluate the efficacy of experimental medications, thereby facilitating the translation of discoveries into clinical settings. This paper aimed to present, explain, and scrutinize some of the AI techniques used in recent years within preclinical epilepsy research. Moreover, advantages, challenges, ethical considerations, reporting issues, and future perspectives will be discussed.Plain Language SummaryResearchers study epilepsy using animal models to understand its mechanisms and develop novel therapeutic strategies. Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming an important tool in this work, helping with data collection and analysis. AI enables scientists to spot patterns in brain activity, find connections between epilepsy and other conditions, and test potential medications. INTRODUCTION

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a field within computer science dedicated to the creation of computer algorithms capable of executing tasks traditionally associated with human intelligence. Machine learning (ML), a subset of AI, enables algorithms to identify patterns and insights from extensive datasets without explicit guidance. 1 The development of ML tools for data classification obtained from preclinical and clinical research unfolds in two phases. Initially, developers select a model, a computational framework tailored to identify patterns in the data and expose it to a subset of collected data, known as the training set. This phase facilitates the model in identifying statistical patterns within the data. However, the genuine evaluation of the model's effectiveness arises from its performance on a distinct dataset, referred to as the test set. This set encompasses data not used during the training phase, serving as an indicator of the model's real‐world applicability and generalizability. The segregation of test and training sets should closely resemble real‐world scenarios to prevent overestimation or underestimation of the model's true real‐world performance. 1 AI applications demonstrate considerable potential in strengthening preclinical research and clinical practice with tasks such as hypothesis generation, literature and data organization/analysis, comprehension of mechanisms, analysis of neurophysiological and neuroradiological data, and anticipation of responses to therapy and surgical outcomes. 2 , 3

Furthermore, a range of increasingly valuable AI tools is now available, with access expected to expand. For example, the Generative Pre‐trained Transformer (GPT), a natural language generation model designed to emulate human conversation, such as ChatGPT and Dougall GPT, can contribute to knowledge organization, integration, and hypothesis generation, while also enhancing scientific education and the writing process. 4 , 5

In recent decades, ML has rapidly spread across numerous research fields, using statistical techniques to find patterns in extensive datasets. The development of effective instruments relies heavily on the adoption of data analysis methodologies and the continuous refinement of ML techniques. Given the intricate composition of data, manual identification of representations is unfeasible, thus necessitating the extensive integration of ML and human validation within healthcare contexts.

In neuroscience, supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning stand out as the most prominent ML techniques used. Within supervised learning, the learning algorithm is provided with training data paired with labels, typically assigned by human experts. This facilitates the extraction of relationships between the data and labels, enabling the system to accurately classify unscored data into their respective categories. Unsupervised learning algorithms draw inspiration from how the brain assimilates new information through raw perceptions. They operate on unclassified or unlabeled data during training, finding extensive application in the identification and classification of diseases. Reinforcement learning entails the formulation of a policy aimed at the maximization of reward acquisition through the agent‐environment interactions. Fundamental components of this learning paradigm include a policy, a reward signal, a value function, and a model of the environment. 6

The integration of AI and ML methodologies within animal research presents a promising avenue for enhancing the translatability and reproducibility of findings, thereby complementing traditional approaches such as animal models. These advanced technologies serve to scrutinize complex datasets, refine experimental methodologies, and forecast outcomes. Through the ML analyses applied to data derived from animal models with analogous datasets obtained from human clinical studies, researchers can significantly enhance translational capabilities and minimize the disparity between preclinical and clinical research domains, thereby strengthening the value of animal model findings in relation to human disease pathology. Furthermore, the adoption of standardized experimental protocols and data analysis procedures is advocated, consequently fostering replicability in research outcomes. 7 , 8

AI methodologies, applied to the analysis of animal behavior and combined with datasets from animal and human studies, allow the identification of shared and divergent characteristics across species, while simultaneously advancing our understanding of animal behavioral patterns, social interactions, and responses to environmental stimuli. 7 Additionally, ML algorithms can be trained to recognize individual animals based on distinctive body features, thus facilitating investigations into social dynamics within animal groups. 9 ML also accelerates drug discovery efforts by predicting compound efficacy and potential side effects. 10

AI has played a pivotal role in toxicity prediction by identifying hazards associated with novel chemical entities and diminishing reliance on conventional animal testing methodologies. 11

Furthermore, the use of AI and animal models addresses challenges, including the aggregation of comprehensive datasets encompassing neuroimaging, genetic, and biochemical data from diverse animal models. 12 The integration of AI within animal model research holds significant potential for transformative discoveries, augmenting the understanding of preclinical phenomena and facilitating the development of efficient strategies for the prediction, diagnosis, and treatment of neurological disorders such as epilepsy.

In this review, the AI techniques are categorized into three principal domains based on their objectives. (1) Diagnosis involves the identification, characterization, and/or prediction of epileptic seizures. (2) Identification of comorbidities associated with epilepsy using AI techniques. (3) Treatment encompasses the use of ML models to comprehend the underlying mechanisms of epilepsy, discern potential drug targets, and evaluate the efficacy of experimental medications (Figure 1).

Overview of AI applications in preclinical epilepsy research. Most of the AI and ML techniques in preclinical epilepsy can be categorized into three main domains based on their objectives: Diagnosis, comorbidity identification, and treatment development. Diagnosis encompasses AI‐based methods for the identification, characterization, and prediction of epileptic activity, primarily utilizing EEG recordings. Comorbidity identification focuses on the application of AI to monitor and analyze behavioral changes, such as sleep disturbances, memory impairments, and anxiety, which are commonly associated with epilepsy. This approach often involves AI‐driven behavior monitoring and video analysis. Treatment development involves the use of ML models for simulating epileptic mechanisms and evaluating experimental therapies, contributing to a deeper understanding of the disease and potential therapeutic interventions.

Importantly, the accelerating integration of artificial intelligence across diverse aspects of preclinical epilepsy research signifies a paradigm shift with profound implications for experimental design, data analysis, and the generation of novel insights; consequently, a robust familiarity with its core concepts becomes indispensable not only for comprehending the current landscape but also for a comprehensive evaluation of existing methodologies and discerning potential future directions within this rapidly evolving field (Table 1).

DIAGNOSIS OF EPILEPSY

The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) defines epilepsy as a disorder of the brain characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures, and by the neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition. 13 A seizure is a transient occurrence of signs and/or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. 14 The accurate detection of seizures is fundamental for the diagnosis of epilepsy, as well as for advancing strategies aimed at disease prevention or modification. Electrophysiological analyses play a critical role in epilepsy research, providing tools for evaluating neural activity and identifying biomarkers associated with seizure onset and progression. EEG is the most widely used technique for detecting abnormal brain activity in both clinical and preclinical settings. However, manual analysis of EEG recordings is inherently time‐consuming and susceptible to subjectivity. To address these challenges, automated approaches for seizure detection in animal models have been developed, significantly enhancing the reliability and efficiency of seizure identification. The detection of seizures and the identification of reliable biomarkers are essential for understanding epileptogenesis and tailoring therapeutic interventions. Although dynamic models describe the electrical patterns elicited during epileptic seizures across both human and animal models, these manifestations occur at different spatiotemporal scales. This variability underscores the need for advanced analytical methods that can accurately capture and interpret the complexity of seizure activity across diverse experimental settings. Automated detection methods, combined with in‐depth electrophysiological analyses, hold promise for refining diagnostic precision and guiding the development of targeted epilepsy treatments. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Seizure patterns and brain activity differ depending on the recording equipment used due to the effects of volume conduction. Additionally, the equipment used as well as recording parameters lead to a range of artifacts and noise overlaying brain signals. Consequently, seizure detection algorithms must be adapted to specific conditions‐such as animal model and electrode configuration rather than being directly applied to existing seizure detection methods. 19

The literature on automated human epileptic seizure detection algorithms is extensive, with initial contributions dating back to the early 1980s by Jean Gotman. 20 Typically, these algorithms begin with a feature extraction phase where the EEG is delineated by specific linear or non‐linear metrics within the temporal, spectral, or time‐frequency domains, at either the EEG channel level or extracted sources. 21 , 22 , 23 Subsequent automated steps are established on the computed features, using criteria such as knowledge‐based rules, neural networks, or other classification methods. Given the substantial variability in seizure patterns across different patient cohorts, and even within individual patients, algorithms are tailored either towards adult populations, neonatal patients, or adopt a patient‐specific approach. 19 Although the utilization of automated seizure detection techniques in animal models of epilepsy is limited, methods to detect seizures in intracranial rodent recordings have been documented. 19 , 24

Automated seizure detectors have been used on animal models of seizures. For example, Casillas‐Espinosa and collaborators successfully detected both temporal lobe and generalized seizures in various rodent models of epilepsy by utilizing a time‐frequency analysis of EEG recordings from skull screws. 16 Additionally, other studies have been developed for the automated detection of seizures in mice, although these are often highly specific to the model and, in some cases, are only semi‐automated. 24 , 25

Zebrafish larvae, due to their prolific reproduction and cost‐effectiveness, have emerged as promising models for epilepsy research, particularly in drug discovery studies. Hunyadi and collaborators introduced an innovative automated algorithm designed to detect epileptic discharges within single‐channel local field potential (LFP) recordings in zebrafish. Initially, candidate seizure segments were identified based on their energy and duration characteristics. Subsequently, discriminative features were extracted from each segment. Leveraging a labeled dataset, a support vector machine (SVM) classifier was trained to optimize feature mapping, enabling the detection of seizure segments within new signals. The evaluation of this algorithm was conducted in both chemically induced and genetic epilepsy models. The results indicated that the algorithm performs comparably to visual analysis, effectively discerning a significant disparity in seizure occurrences between epileptic and control groups. 19

Identification of electrophysiological seizure patterns

The identification of seizures constitutes a significant component of electroencephalography (EEG) examination within neurocritical care, epilepsy diagnosis and treatment, and the implementation of innovative therapeutic modalities such as closed‐loop stimulation or optogenetic seizure management. Furthermore, its importance is amplified in the context of rigorous, reliable, and replicable preclinical investigations. 18 Technological advancements enable the acquisition of extensive EEG datasets, offering potential advantages alongside analytical complexities. Although EEG analysis conventionally involves visual scrutiny supplemented by quantitative signal analysis tools, this method proves impractical for large datasets due to issues of efficiency and notable variability in interobserver concordance. Consequently, there is a need for innovative methodologies. 26 , 27

ML methods have been used to examine and study EEG data in both research and clinical fields. 28 Although numerous ML techniques have proven effective in analyzing human EEG data, particularly scalp EEG recordings, 29 , 30 , 31 their application to rodent EEG studies is limited. 17 Fumeaux and collaborators developed a novel, high‐performance seizure detection method based on a generalized linear model. This approach was validated across multiple EEG datasets from rats, including those with focal and multifocal epilepsy and designed to ensure broad accessibility for diverse applications. 18 The approach involved training a generalized linear model on 141 EEG signal features to classify seizures in continuous EEG data from two datasets. The first dataset was obtained from 16 rats with focal epilepsy (1012 spontaneous seizures over 3 months of continuous recording) and the second dataset consisted of 96 rats with 2883 spontaneous multifocal seizures. The model achieved excellent performance. Notably, the method demonstrated rapid seizure detection, with latency under 5 s for over 80% of seizures and under 12 s for over 99% of seizures. This approach represents a significant advancement in seizure detection, applicable to automated EEG analysis pipelines, closed‐loop interventions, and research settings requiring standardized, high‐throughput analysis of seizures in animals. 18

Numerous ML techniques, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been successfully applied to EEG data analysis, particularly for seizure classification tasks. CNNs are especially advantageous due to their ability to automatically learn and extract relevant features from input data, minimizing the need for manual feature engineering. 28 However, interpreting the learned features from trained CNNs remains a challenge. To address the need for efficient model training, researchers have explored transfer learning, a technique that repurposes pre‐trained networks originally developed for image classification tasks. Examples of such networks include GoogLeNet, AlexNet, and SqueezeNet, which are widely used in computer vision. These models can be adapted to EEG analysis by converting EEG signals into image‐like representations, such as spectrograms or time‐frequency plots, allowing the models to distinguish between seizure and non‐seizure events. While these are prominent examples, they represent just a subset of available tools. Other architectures and ML methods such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and hybrid models are also actively explored in EEG‐based seizure detection, depending on the specific characteristics of the data and the application goals. 17

In a recent study published by Kotloski, a preconfigured deep convolutional neural network (DCNN), specifically GoogLeNet, was used to categorize images to detect seizures. 17 Training images were created by combining scalograms, kurtosis, and entropy for 2‐s EEG segments. More than 2200 h of EEG data were assessed, with the DCNN correctly identifying 95% of seizures. However, the method also produced a relatively high false positive rate of 34% (1.52 per hour) when compared to visual scoring. The DCNN outperformed a previously described algorithm based on wavelet‐derived local line lengths. While this study demonstrates the potential of using preconfigured image‐classification networks for automated seizure detection in large‐scale rodent datasets, it also highlights important limitations. Not all seizure types may be equally detectable by a single tool, especially in the context of heterogeneous electrographic patterns across models. Additionally, a high false positive rate could introduce noise into downstream analyses or therapeutic interventions. 17 Therefore, while such tools offer scalability and objectivity, their use in preclinical research should be complemented by expert validation. Caution is also warranted in interpreting results, as automated approaches may miss subtle events or misclassify artifacts, particularly in noisy or complex EEG recordings typical of animal studies.

Detection of EEG biomarkers

For clinical purposes, identifying EEG seizure‐related phenomena is often sufficient to guide treatment decisions. In research settings, however, detailed scoring of epilepsy‐related events and the identification of potential biomarkers of epileptogenesis are crucial, both for advancing drug development and for broader biomedical research efforts. 32 In recent years, substantial interest has emerged in predicting the development of epilepsy, underscoring the importance of EEG biomarkers that can signal epileptogenic activity.

High‐frequency oscillations (HFOs) have shown strong predictive value for epilepsy development in both animal models and human patients. HFOs are typically categorized into two types: “ripples” (80–200 Hz) and “fast ripples” (FRs, 200–800 Hz), representing local field potentials that arise from increased synchronous neuronal spike activity. 33 Multiple studies, particularly in animal models, have emphasized the potential of intracranially recorded HFOs as biomarkers not only for epileptogenesis but also for identifying seizure onset areas. 34 , 35 , 36

A variety of approaches and techniques have been developed to automate the identification of HFOs in EEG signals. For instance, Navarrete and collaborators introduced RIPPLELAB, a Matlab‐based script offering a broad set of tools for both manual and automatic HFO detection. However, despite reasonable success in automating HFO detection, various efforts towards harmonization have been evaluated, revealing a high susceptibility to false positives. Consequently, manual validation of detections remains necessary. 37 , 38 More recently, Zhang and colleagues (2024) developed and validated PyHFO, an end‐to‐end software platform that facilitates the application of deep learning (DL) techniques for detecting HFOs as biomarkers of epileptogenic zones in EEG recordings. PyHFO was validated using three datasets: two involving human grid/strip and penetrating electrodes, and a third utilizing cortical and penetrating electrodes from rodents. The software showed significant efficiency, with optimization techniques allowing for speeds of up to 50 times faster than conventional HFO detection applications. 39 Another potential electrographic biomarker is spike–wave discharges (SWDs), which have been proposed as the EEG hallmark of absence epilepsy. 40 , 41 These oscillatory patterns are thought to result from reverberating activity within thalamocortical networks. 41 In rodents, SWDs are typically characterized by oscillations around 7–12 Hz. 42 , 43 , 44 Detecting SWDs in EEG recordings is crucial for both clinical diagnosis of absence epilepsy and research. Pfammatter and collaborators developed an algorithm based on a SVM to automate the detection of spike‐and‐wave discharges (SWDs) in the γ2R43Q mouse model of absence epilepsy. The results show that the algorithm scores SWDs on a continuum highly correlated with human confidence levels. Although the authors report that this methodology performs well on data from the γ2R43Q mouse model of epilepsy, it has yet to be thoroughly validated across other animal models. 45

Importantly, multiple studies have shown that SWDs may also occur in sham or control animals and are often associated with normal aging processes. 46 , 47 , 48 This highlights the necessity for careful interpretation of SWD‐related findings, as their presence alone does not necessarily indicate a pathological state. In research settings, distinguishing between physiological and pathological SWDs is essential to avoid misinterpretation of experimental results.

Seizure prediction

The unpredictability of seizure occurrences represents a major source of distress among most epilepsy patients and has been identified as the primary challenge in the field of epileptology. 49 , 50 For the past century, scientists have been striving to surmount challenges associated with identifying and forecasting epilepsy. 6 Currently, considerable effort has been directed towards developing algorithms for predicting focal seizures, characterized by the gradual recruitment of neurons within a localized area during a presumed pre‐seizure state. 6 , 51 , 52 The performance of seizure prediction algorithms varies significantly, with methods incorporating multiple variables, particularly synchronization measures between brain structures typically outperforming those relying on single variables. 52

Classical ML methodology for seizure prediction involves several key steps:

Signal processing

This step entails identifying and filtering out noise and artifacts present in raw biomedical signals, such as EEG recordings. Various techniques like band‐pass, wavelet, finite impulse response, and adaptive filters are used for this purpose. Additionally, data normalization is performed to ensure comparability across patient recordings. Challenges like data dropouts and corrupted data due to electrode limitations and the presence of outliers from muscle artifacts and environmental noise are addressed, as they can significantly affect feature extraction.

Feature extraction and selection

Reliable features correlated with pre‐ictal and interictal stages are critical for prediction methods. Features can be categorized based on the number of EEG channels as univariate (measures taken on each EEG channel separately) and multivariate (measures taken on two or more EEG channels), further divided into linear or nonlinear features.

Classification

ML algorithms such as artificial neural networks (ANN), k‐means clustering, decision trees, SVM, and fuzzy logic are employed to identify pre‐ictal and interictal patterns from EEG data. Threshold‐based approaches on feature values are commonly used for making conclusions, although recent ML‐based studies have focused on optimizing feature extraction for prediction. 6

An initial study using wavelet functions as seizure predictors achieved durations of approximately 2.24 min, demonstrating their effectiveness. 53 Varatharajah and collaborators achieved the best time results in animal models, utilizing canine EEG data and employing multiple ML algorithms to predict seizures with a lead time of 1 min. 54 In terms of sensitivity, other research has produced systems surpassing 90%, although with slightly longer prediction intervals. Rajdev and collaborators developed a seizure prediction system for rat EEG recordings using an adaptive Wiener filter, achieving a sensitivity of 92%, the highest sensitivity reported in works involving animal EEG recordings. 55

Novel algorithms, leveraging ML, have emerged and demonstrated predictive capabilities above chance levels. 49 , 56 These approaches, shown to be successful in various pattern recognition tasks such as medical diagnosis, genomics, translation, robotics, as well as image and speech recognition, use feature extraction techniques. 57 , 58 , 59

In contrast, limited attention has been paid to predicting generalized seizures due to their historical perception as aleatory events. In two validated genetic rat models of absence epilepsy (WAG/Rij strain and Genetic Absence Epilepsy Rats from Strasbourg ‐ GAERS), characterized by generalized SWDs and concurrent loss of consciousness, 50 , 60 several studies have identified preictal changes in the corticothalamic system, potentially serving as predictive features for SWDs. 61 , 62 Maksimenko and collaborators offered an initial proof of concept for SWD predictability. They calculated synchronization measures indicating SWD onset by assessing wavelet energy in LFP recordings from three cortico‐thalamic locations in WAG/Rij rats. Despite achieving high sensitivity in prediction, this algorithm suffered from a high number of false alarms, significantly reducing its specificity. 63 In another study on absence epilepsy in rats, prolonged recordings of LFPs were analyzed and generalized SWDs were identified. The wavelet‐based algorithm was used to assess synchronicity across different recording sites within the cortico‐thalamic system. Comparisons of prediction sensitivity and false alarm rates were made for various combinations of recording sites. The wavelet spectra of true and false positive predictions were fed into an ML algorithm to distinguish between them. Results showed that certain combinations of recording sites, particularly in the somatosensory cortex, had superior prediction sensitivity and fewer false alarms compared to others. Additionally, diurnal fluctuations in SWD occurrence were observed in further testing, and a trained random forest (RF) algorithm achieved a 71% reduction in false alarm rates. These findings support the cortical‐focus theory of absence epilepsy and suggest the potential for developing prediction‐prevention systems for SWDs, with implications for translating these findings to human data. 50

Despite the notable advancements achieved in automated seizure prediction since the inception of initial research efforts, lingering challenges persist requiring attention. The development of precise seizure prediction methods requires a balance involving prediction latency, sensitivity, and specificity. 64

IDENTIFICATION OF EPILEPSY‐ASSOCIATED COMORBIDITIES

Advancements in preclinical research have revolutionized the study of epilepsy by leveraging AI to analyze behavioral states in animal models. This innovative approach not only detects intricate connections between concurrent neural disorders and epileptic activity but also prevents the restrictions of traditional methods. Historically, behavioral assessments relied on labor‐intensive manual scoring, which was subjective and limited in scope. However, the application of ML has allowed for automated and unbiased analysis of key behaviors for preclinical research of epilepsy and its comorbidities. This technological leap facilitates the comprehensive analysis of behaviors before, during, and after seizures, providing researchers with invaluable time‐series data. By using AI to unlock these behavioral insights, preclinical researchers are prepared to accelerate the discovery of underlying neural circuit dysfunctions and advance the development of new treatment strategies for epilepsy.

Automated behavior monitoring

In preclinical research, AI models effectively recognize behavioral states in animal models of epilepsy, enabling the detection of connections between neural activity and interictal and ictal behavior. Video holds significance for video encephalography and monitoring. This technology is appealing due to its “contactless” nature, minimal risk, and relatively modest expenses. 65 However, extensive records of animal behaviors over extended periods are scarce, particularly due to the lack of automated recording for comprehensive behavior sets, such as those occurring before, during, and after seizures. Advancements in contemporary animal event‐logging software, incorporating image recognition and ML, offer a solution to these limitations. 66 In this context, Friard and Gamba have pioneered in this field by creating BORIS, an open‐source software that facilitates automated recording of intricate animal behaviors for single or multiple animals, providing outputs in the form of time‐series data or concise summaries, including ethograms. These datasets can be used to enrich ML methods. 67

For epilepsy research, a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms of seizures, the identification of network dysfunctions linked to comorbidities, and the evaluation of potential treatments require utilization of a robust, unbiased, and high‐throughput methodology for the analysis and quantification of animal behavior. Until recently, such behavioral assessments had been reliant upon labor‐intensive, inherently subjective, manual scoring of a limited set of predetermined behavioral manifestations associated with epilepsy. However, the advancements in ML present novel strategies for the objective analysis of animal behavior with unparalleled resolution and scalability. 65

Traditionally, the detailed analysis of an animal's movement patterns, referred to as “pose dynamics,” has been accomplished through motion capture techniques that use markers or sensors to capture kinematic patterns. 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 However, these methods pose a risk of disrupting the animal's natural behavior. Recent progress in ML and deep learning has introduced methods that facilitate markerless tracking of two‐dimensional (2D) or three‐dimensional (3D) key points from standard red, green, and blue (RGB) video data or by utilizing 3D data obtained from depth sensors. 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77

AI‐based techniques have led to novel methodologies for time‐series analysis of behavioral data. These tools are designed to either discern continuous motion patterns (referred to as “continuous representation”) or segment behavior into stereotypical, recurrent behavioral states (known as “discrete representation”), or a combination of both. Notably, the “discrete representation” approach facilitates the inference of behavioral states, thereby offering a straightforward means to quantify and compare these states across diverse experimental conditions, such as drug testing scenarios. The underlying clustering and modeling methodologies used in these tools typically belong to one of three categories: supervised, utilizing predefined labels; semi‐supervised, extrapolating from a limited labeled dataset; or unsupervised, driven solely by data and not reliant on predefined labels. 78 , 79

Gschwind and colleagues demonstrated that unsupervised, 3D video analysis of behavior in animal models of epilepsy can discern subtle variances in pose dynamics that elude visual inspection. Their findings unveiled distinct behavioral phenotypes, “fingerprints,” observed during interictal periods across various epilepsy types (including acquired and genetic), at specific time intervals during epileptogenesis, and in relation to factors such as sex, drug type, and drug dosage. This method enables an automated, impartial evaluation of epilepsy without requiring prolonged recording of electrographic activity in each animal. This approach can also be used to categorize ictal behaviors. The development of such shareable, innovative tools for unbiased, high‐throughput assessment of behavior not only holds promise to expedite scalable anti‐epileptic drug testing but also offers insights into unraveling neural circuit dysfunctions underlying ictal and interictal impairments. 80

Memory alterations

Advancements in technologies aimed at describing the spatial and temporal dynamics of neural activity have significantly enriched our comprehension of brain function across varying physiological and pathological states. A fundamental field where these technological advances manifest used is the exploration of novel therapeutic avenues for neurological and psychiatric conditions. 81

A study from 2018 introduced a novel approach to in vivo drug screening, integrating high‐throughput technology to produce comprehensive brain activity maps (BAMs) alongside ML for predictive analysis. This platform allowed the assessment of compounds' mechanisms of action and potential therapeutic applications, utilizing BAMs information obtained from drug‐treated zebrafish larvae. By using BAM‐derived clusters as a functional classifier and ML predictions, potential anti‐seizure drug candidates from non‐clinical compounds were discovered and subsequently validated for their therapeutic efficacy in a zebrafish seizure model induced by pentylenetetrazole. 81

Another recent study investigated time‐dependent changes in declarative memory and associated brain regions following status epilepticus (SE) using structural imaging and ML techniques. Pilocarpine was used to induce SE in rats, and structural imaging and contextual fear conditioning were conducted at different time points post‐SE. Voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) analysis and SVM were used to identify brain regions and predict declarative memory. Animals post pilocarpine SE displayed impaired declarative memory at 2 months, with partial recovery by 4 months. 82 Furthermore, these rats exhibited larger cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) volumes and lower gray matter volumes in hippocampal subfields, somatosensory cortex, and entorhinal cortex. 82 ML analysis identified the somatosensory cortex and entorhinal cortex as the correlated brain regions for declarative memory dysfunctions post‐SE, suggesting compensatory mechanisms and implicating structural changes in memory impairment. 82

Modification of sleep patterns

Sleep constitutes a recurrent physiological phenomenon inherent to the human body, frequently linked with the cyclical renewal of physiological (muscular, immunological) and neurological processes. This state is distinguished by the prevalence of a dominant delta frequency, alongside low‐amplitude high‐frequency oscillations, and the presence of theta frequency band. 83 , 84 Demonstrated by mounting data, there is an emerging consensus indicating a robust correlation between the manifestation of epileptic episodes (spikes, seizures) and the various states of sleep and wakefulness. 85 Remarkably, these epileptic events manifest in a synchronized mode with the natural circadian rhythm. 86

Continuous monitoring data is indispensable for understanding the details of sleep–wake patterns in both normal and epileptic conditions. However, long‐term experiments involving human subjects are unfeasible. Conversely, preclinical models are commonly used for prolonged EEG studies to track epileptic events over extended periods. Traditional EEG‐centered sleep inquiries depend on manual examination of video‐EEG recordings, considered the benchmark for distinguishing sleep and wake states. Nevertheless, this task becomes exceedingly challenging when analyzing numerous hours of EEG data. Hence, there is an urgent need for a robust and efficient automated detection system capable of autonomously identifying sleep intervals based on EEG modalities. 87

There is evidence indicating that seizures may manifest during sleep, and sleep could potentially contribute to the mechanism underlying seizure and spike generation. 88 The automation of sleep–wake state detection in rodents via EEG represents a crucial advancement in the field, promising to accelerate numerous complex and long‐term studies related to sleep, particularly within epilepsy research. In another study, a brute force approach using frequency band‐power‐based thresholding was compared with ML algorithms to distinguish sleep patterns using 600 h of EEG data collected from four epileptic and two control animals. The findings indicated that animals with spikes (without seizures) exhibited an increased frequency of sleep occurrences, along with notably extended sleep durations and overall daily sleep times compared to their control counterparts. 87 This automated algorithmic framework facilitated a deeper comprehension of the intricate relationship between sleep dynamics and the epileptic condition.

SUDEP prediction

Most of the ML algorithms in seizure detection and prediction have elevated accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity over traditional methods. This improved performance provides the opportunity to adapt similar approaches for SUDEP risk prediction. Animal models provide a valuable tool with abundant resources for exploring SUDEP within controlled genetic and environmental parameters. The animal models of SUDEP replicate numerous critical aspects observed in human cases, involving autonomic dysfunction, apnea, hypoventilation, cardiac arrhythmia, and postictal EEG suppression. Furthermore, these models offer insights into potential genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying SUDEP, such as channelopathies and disruptions in serotonergic and adenosinergic systems. 89

A wide diversity of model organisms, including mice, rats, sheep, and baboons, has been used to study SUDEP. Various single‐gene manipulations, simulating monogenic causes of developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (e.g., SCN1A, SCN8A, SCN1B, and DEPDC5) and long‐QT syndromes (e.g., KCNQ1 and KCNA1) have been used in mice to model SUDEP. 90 These studies often maintain transgenic mice on a uniform genetic background, allowing for the demonstration of the isolated effects of specific genes on SUDEP susceptibility. 91

Integrating a synchronized recording system that captures behavior, brain activity, and heart‐respiratory functions concurrently in both anesthetized and freely moving animals provides a valuable method to study the intricacies of nervous, cardiac, and respiratory function alterations associated with seizure‐induced sudden death. Additionally, animal models enable direct recordings from the brainstem, the central hub for regulating cardiorespiratory functions, which is typically challenging to access in clinical environments. 89

Apart from the nervous system, animal models of SUDEP have emphasized the significance of cardiac and respiratory functions and their interaction with the brain. Studies in DBA/1J mouse models carrying Scn8a, Scn1a, Kcna1, or Cacna1a mutations have directly linked peri‐ictal and tonic‐phase central apnea, either concurrent with or preceding terminal asystole with SUDEP. 92 , 93 , 94 Additionally, studies using a rat seizure model combining electrophysiology and fast MRI scans have revealed laryngospasm‐preceded esophageal reflux during fatal seizures, suggesting a link between obstructive apnea and subsequent sudden death in rats. 95

Comparable to findings in human studies, the amygdala's involvement in respiratory regulation has been highlighted in animal models of SUDEP. Electrolytic lesions of the amygdala in DBA/1J mice significantly decreased the occurrence of seizure‐induced respiratory arrest without affecting seizures, baseline breathing, or chemoreception. 96

In addition to respiratory issues, cardiac dysfunction and failure hold significant importance in the pathology of SUDEP. Patients with SUDEP often exhibit cardiac dysfunctions, with many genetic mutations associated with SUDEP linked to cardiac arrhythmias. 97 , 98 Various transgenic mouse models of SUDEP have also demonstrated cardiac dysfunctions, including reduced heart rate variability, hyperexcitability of cardiac myocytes, increased atrioventricular conduction blocks, bradycardia, and cardiac arrhythmias. 89 , 99 , 100 , 101

Identifying surrogate biomarkers that can be quantitatively assessed and computed is pivotal for automating SUDEP prediction, facilitating the identification of at‐risk individuals and discerning fatal seizures. Beyond conventional EEG analyses, data extracted from diverse sources such as video recordings, ECG, electrodermal responses, and muscular activities can augment computer‐assisted evaluations of SUDEP risks. 27 , 102

DEVELOPMENT OF NOVEL ANTI‐SEIZURE MEDICATIONS

The drug development process is arduous, costly, and often prone to failure; however, it holds potential for optimization through the integration of AI. 103 Given the intricate nature of biological systems, contemporary drug development requires the assimilation of diverse data sources. 104 AI allows for the analysis of large datasets, uncovering latent properties of molecules and simulating varied conditions with personalized parameters, thereby facilitating advances in understanding complex biological phenomena. 105 , 106 ML represents a widely used AI technique in drug development research. 107 ML leverages statistical techniques to construct models that can extrapolate concepts from training data, enabling the application of learned insights to novel datasets. Through the evaluation of performance metrics, ML methods identify optimal models, offering methodological advantages and numerous possibilities for model selection in drug development. 108 ML techniques find utility across all stages of drug development, encompassing drug discovery, clinical trial design, product development, quality assurance, and management. 109 Incorporating these techniques into drug development research phases holds the promise of mitigating failure rates attributed to factors such as efficacy issues, adverse toxicity, inconsistent properties, and commercial challenges. 110

ML has predominantly been used in drug development for tasks such as identifying and validating targets, discovering lead compounds, synthesizing molecules, understanding protein‐ligand interactions, and discovering predictive biomarkers. 111 For instance, deep learning‐based models such as CNNs have been used to predict drug–target interactions by learning structural features from 3D molecular data, 112 while graph neural networks (GNNs) have proven effective in capturing molecular graph representations to generate novel compounds with favorable pharmacological profiles. 113 Similarly, autoencoders and variational autoencoders have been applied in the de novo molecular design. 114 , 115 In target identification, SVM classifiers have been used on transcriptomic and proteomic datasets to identify disease‐relevant genes and signaling pathways. 116 These techniques facilitate the identification of protein structures for potential drug targets and the subsequent design of new drugs based on these targets. Typically, the process begins with the extraction of ligand and protein features from online repositories to model potential active sites for specific ligands. Tested compounds are then subjected to various physicochemical conditions, and leading candidates undergo validation through in vitro and in vivo assays. 117 Another common approach is quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, which establishes connections between chemical structures and biological activities in studied compounds using mathematical models. 118 Nevertheless, the application of ML in drug development continues to face limitations. These arise not only from the diversity and complexity of available modeling strategies but, more critically, from the limited availability of high‐quality, domain‐specific datasets required to train robust and generalizable models. 109 In disorders of the nervous system, such as epilepsy, drug development faces greater challenges and limitations, resulting in higher attrition rates compared to other therapeutic areas. 119

ML has predominantly found application in epilepsy research for the categorization of seizure events, anticipation of seizure onset, and localization of seizure onset zones. 120 , 121 , 122 A limited number of studies are focused on ML approaches specifically aimed at forecasting treatment efficacy. For example, in one study, ML algorithms were trained and assessed using features extracted from intracranial electroencephalogram (iEEG) recordings of epileptiform discharges observed in a Mecp2‐deficient mouse model of Rett Syndrome. 123 There was a correlation between the presence of cross‐frequency coupling (CFC) of the delta (2–5 Hz) and fast ripple (400–600 Hz) rhythms in epileptiform discharges. Leveraging CFC as a marker for post‐treatment outcomes, SVMs and RF classifiers were compared in terms of their ability to provide likelihood scores for successful treatment outcomes. 123 The findings revealed variability in the outcomes of antiseizure medication (ASM) treatments. ML techniques exhibited the potential to rank the effectiveness of ASMs by estimating likelihood scores for favorable treatment outcomes. Notably, CFC features emerged as the most effective in identifying suitable ASM treatments in advance, with both classifiers demonstrating comparable performance. This study showed that ML can offer predictive insights into the efficacy of drug treatments, thereby potentially alleviating the burdens associated with drug trials. 123

Integrating AI with traditional experimental methods has the potential to enhance the drug discovery process. 124 By augmenting AI's predictive capabilities with the expertise and experience of human researchers, it is possible to optimize and expedite the development of new treatments. 125 A recent study aimed to identify novel phytochemicals with potential therapeutic efficacy against S100B through ML‐enabled virtual screening. 126 Epilepsy progression is linked to an overproduction of S100B, a calcium‐binding protein crucial for cell function. S100B, located in the nucleus and cytoplasm, influences the cell cycle and differentiation and is also implicated in Alzheimer's, inflammation, heart disease, arthritis, and cancer. 126 , 127 By utilizing an extensive dataset of compounds, the authors developed and validated an ML model capable of effectively recognizing potential inhibitors of the S100B protein. The study used several ML algorithms, including SVM, k‐Nearest Neighbors (kNN), Naive Bayes (NB), and RF. Among these, the RF model demonstrated superior performance, exhibiting high predictive accuracy and offering valuable insights into feature importance. The application of this model facilitated the screening of 9000 phytochemicals, leading to the identification of 584 compounds predicted to be active against S100B. Further refinement using Lipinski's Rule of Five reduced this list to 180 compounds with favorable drug‐like properties. Molecular docking studies of these 180 compounds identified five phytochemicals with exceptional binding affinity towards the S100B protein, emphasizing their potential as lead candidates for anti‐epileptic drug development. By using ML algorithms and computer‐aided drug design, this study has accelerated the traditionally laborious and time‐consuming process of drug discovery. 126

REPORTING ISSUES

Existing AI‐based approaches do not replace traditional experimental methods nor the expertise and experience of human researchers. AI can only generate predictions based on the available data, which must subsequently be validated and interpreted by human experts. 125 Despite the substantial potential of AI in preclinical epilepsy research, several challenges and limitations must be addressed. A primary challenge is the availability of appropriate data. 109 AI typically demands a large volume of information for training purposes. 128 In many instances, the data accessible may be constrained, or of insufficient quality and consistency, which can compromise the accuracy and reliability of the results. 114 Additionally, ethical considerations pose a significant challenge, 129 as AI‐driven approaches may raise issues related to fairness and bias. For instance, if the data used to train an ML algorithm is biased or unrepresentative, the resultant predictions may be erroneous or inequitable. 125

ML has revolutionized seizure prediction by replacing manual, time‐consuming methods with efficient automated analysis. Furthermore, ML addresses the challenge of correctly classifying ailments and their sub‐types using vast biomedical data, facilitating the identification of comprehensive sub‐types. Despite progress, challenges persist in terms of seizure prediction. (1) The lack of long‐term EEG datasets impedes research reproducibility and advancement. Urgent efforts are needed to share EEG databases and code openly for validation and collaboration. (2) Missing observations due to communication failures in implanted devices hinder prediction accuracy. Integrating missingness indicators in algorithms could enhance predictive capabilities. (3) Extracting features from raw signals is time‐consuming and computationally intensive, hindering real‐time prediction. ML addresses this challenge by automatically extracting features, but its effectiveness depends on abundant data availability. 6 , 130

A critical aspect of AI implementation in preclinical epilepsy research is the standardization of reporting methodologies. Researchers often face difficulties in reproducing results due to incomplete documentation of AI methodologies, hyperparameter settings, and data preprocessing steps. The field would benefit from establishing comprehensive reporting guidelines that include detailed descriptions of data acquisition protocols, preprocessing pipelines, feature extraction methods, model architectures, training procedures, and validation strategies. Furthermore, researchers should explicitly report performance metrics beyond accuracy, such as sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC), to provide a more complete assessment of model effectiveness.

Another significant reporting challenge lies in the documentation of model limitations and potential failure modes. Publications should clearly articulate the conditions under which their AI models may not perform optimally, including variations in experimental conditions, animal models, or data quality. This transparency is crucial for clinical translation and real‐world implementation. Additionally, researchers should report the computational resources required for model training and deployment, as this information is essential for assessing the practical feasibility of implementing these solutions in research or clinical settings.

The integration of version control and proper documentation of code repositories is also crucial. Many studies fail to provide accessible code repositories or adequate documentation, hampering reproducibility efforts. Establishing standardized practices for code sharing, including detailed README files, requirements documentation, and example datasets, would significantly improve the reproducibility and validation of AI‐based research findings in the preclinical epilepsy field.

DISCUSSION

During the past decade, new technology has emerged with advancements in medical knowledge by providing therapeutic interventions for previously untreatable diseases. The expanding use and diverse applications of AI have led to new biomedical discoveries including compounds with potential antiseizure activity, such as some phytochemicals. Even though the use of AI has not yet solely resulted in the discovery of any ASMs currently on the market, it is becoming an important tool in accelerating and enhancing drug development research. The convergence of AI and epilepsy research has opened new avenues for understanding seizure dynamics, identifying novel therapeutic targets, and developing personalized treatment approaches. The integration of AI technologies has particularly accelerated the analysis of complex neurological data, enabling researchers to identify patterns and relationships that might have remained undetected through traditional analytical methods.

Concerning drug development, AI, particularly through ML, is poised to transform the research and treatment of drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE). DRE, defined as the failure to achieve sustained seizure freedom after adequate trials of two tolerated and appropriately chosen antiepileptic drug regimens, affects about 30% of people with epilepsy and significantly impacts their quality of life. 131 ML algorithms offer intriguing paths for bettering the care of people with DRE by allowing a multifactorial and tailored approach to diagnosis and treatment. By integrating and analyzing complex, multidimensional datasets including clinical, behavioral, neuroimaging, and molecular data, AI models can identify predictive biomarkers and the underlying mechanisms of treatment resistance. 132 These models can also incorporate factors such as comorbidities, age, and gender, which often affect DRE progression and therapeutic response but are underrepresented in traditional studies. 132 AI is also revolutionizing therapeutic strategies. It is increasingly contributing to the optimization of neuromodulation therapies like vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, and deep brain stimulation by improving stimulation parameter selection and enabling real‐time seizure detection and intervention. 133 By connecting clinical and preclinical insights, AI can significantly improve therapeutic precision and accelerate the drug discovery pipeline, ultimately enhancing outcomes for individuals with DRE. 132 , 134

A significant advancement in addressing data sharing challenges has been the establishment of the Data Archive BRAIN Initiative (DABI), which now houses both preclinical and clinical data from the Epilepsy Bioinformatics Study for Anti‐epileptogenic Therapy (EpiBioS4Rx). This centralized repository represents an important step forward in standardizing data collection and sharing practices in epilepsy research. DABI not only facilitates data accessibility but also promotes the implementation of FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) data principles in epilepsy research. The integration of diverse datasets from both preclinical and clinical studies within a single platform enables researchers to validate findings across species and experimental conditions, potentially accelerating the translation of preclinical discoveries into clinical applications. 135 , 136

Future studies must be focused on generating strategies that can be used to overcome the current obstacles faced by AI in the context of preclinical epilepsy research. One of these strategies for enhancing the performance of ML algorithms could be the implementation of data augmentation techniques. This process involves creating synthetic data that can be used to supplement and expand existing datasets. By doing so, the overall volume and variety of data available for training ML models can be significantly increased. This augmentation can lead to improved accuracy and reliability in the results generated by these algorithms. 125 However, careful validation of synthetic data is essential to ensure it maintains biological relevance and does not introduce artifacts that could mislead model training. Cross‐validation with real experimental data and expert verification remain critical steps in this process.

Alternatively, another approach uses the adoption of methods associated with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). 137 The primary objective of XAI methods is to provide clear, interpretable, and transparent interpretations for the predictions made by ML algorithms. Such interpretability is instrumental in addressing and mitigating concerns related to bias and fairness inherent in AI‐driven methodologies. 125 Furthermore, XAI facilitates a deeper comprehension of the underlying mechanisms and assumptions that drive the predictive processes of the algorithms, thereby contributing to a better understanding of the decision‐making frameworks. 138

Looking ahead, several key areas warrant focused attention in the field of AI‐assisted preclinical epilepsy research. First, the development of multimodal AI approaches that can integrate diverse data types (neuroimaging, EEG, behavioral, genetic, and molecular) could provide more comprehensive insights into epileptogenic mechanisms. Second, the establishment of standardized frameworks for validating AI models across different experimental settings and animal models would enhance the reproducibility and reliability of findings. Third, the implementation of federated learning approaches could enable collaborative model development while maintaining data privacy and security.

The transition from preclinical success to clinical application remains a significant challenge. Future research should prioritize the development of AI models that can effectively close the gap between animal models and human patients. This includes creating algorithms that can account for species‐specific differences and identify conserved pathological mechanisms. Additionally, the integration of AI with emerging technologies such as optogenetics and high‐resolution imaging could provide unprecedented control and monitoring capabilities in preclinical studies. To enhance translational relevance, it is essential to acknowledge the necessity of a bidirectional interface between clinical and preclinical research. While AI holds considerable promise in the analysis and modeling of complex datasets derived from preclinical studies, its optimal utility is contingent upon the integration of clinically grounded knowledge. A comprehensive understanding of the clinical manifestations of epilepsy (such as seizure semiology, disease progression, and therapeutic responses) is important to guide the design of preclinical models and the development of novel AI methodologies. In this context, AI can serve not only as a tool for data interpretation within preclinical paradigms but also as a bridge that incorporates clinically derived features into the development of more predictive animal models.

Ethical considerations will continue to play a key role in shaping the future of AI applications in epilepsy research. The development of guidelines for responsible AI use, data sharing protocols, and model validation standards should be prioritized by the research community. Furthermore, ensuring equity in access to AI‐driven research tools and findings will be essential to maximize the impact of these technological advances.

CONCLUSIONS

The integration of AI into critical aspects of epilepsy research, including diagnosis, comorbidities, and the development of novel treatment strategies, implies a paradigmatic advancement in preclinical research (Table 2). The traditional reliance on manual behavioral assessment, EEG, and imaging analysis, which is susceptible to variability and subjectivity, has progressively been supplemented by automated algorithms capable of efficiently processing large datasets. These algorithms use a variety of methodologies ranging from time‐frequency analyses to ML techniques, specifically tailored to detect epileptic activity across a range of animal models.

For instance, AI‐enabled behavioral monitoring has improved the ability for researchers to understand intricate interactions between epilepsy and concurrent neural disorders, providing unbiased, high‐throughput data pivotal for understanding seizure‐related behavioral alterations. Additionally, AI has facilitated the study of alterations of brain tissue after epileptic events through advanced imaging and ML. These approaches not only unveil structural changes in important brain regions but also predict impairments, offering insights into potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, AI‐driven methods not only enhance the efficiency and objectivity of epilepsy research but also expedite the exploration of new therapeutic approaches. As these technologies continue to evolve, they hold promise for deepening our comprehension of the complexities of epilepsy.

The traditional drug development process, characterized by high costs and frequent failures, stands to benefit profoundly from AI's capacity to analyze extensive datasets, discern intricate biological interactions, and simulate scenarios with personalized parameters. ML techniques have already demonstrated efficacy across various stages of drug development, encompassing target identification, lead compound discovery, clinical trial design, and efficacy prediction.

Despite this progress, challenges persist in epilepsy research. Issues such as the scarcity of longitudinal datasets, communication failures in implanted devices, and the computational demands associated with extracting features from raw signals remain substantial. Addressing these challenges through collaborative efforts to openly share data, integrate missingness indicators into algorithms, and automate feature extraction will be pivotal in improving the accuracy and efficiency of these techniques.

Future work is needed to refine these methods, addressing nuances specific to different models, and optimizing predictive capabilities across diverse types of seizures and coexisting neurobehavioral disorders. The convergence of AI with neurophysiological insights holds immense potential not only for enhancing diagnostic precision and therapeutic interventions but also for advancing our understanding of epilepsy's pathophysiology.

In summary, while AI holds enormous promise in expediting the development of preclinical epilepsy studies, sustained investigation and innovation are needed to address challenges and fully utilize its potential in clinical applications. By leveraging AI and advancing methodologies, the field is positioned to revolutionize epilepsy management and lead to innovative therapeutic solutions.
