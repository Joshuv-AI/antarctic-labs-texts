MRSA mycotic aneurysm associated with infected pacemaker leads
Authors: Anthony Weinstock, Tung To, Hamza Rashid, Devin Sakamoto, Luke Hsei, Kashyap Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract75-year old woman with persistent methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) bacteremia was found to have a descending thoracic mycotic aneurysm, initially identified on 2-deoxy-2-[F-18]fluoroglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) and further characterized on computed tomographic angiography (CTA). Infection was related to a combination of underlying atherosclerosis and an automatic-implantable cardiac defibrillator (AICD) that required explant due to MRSA infected leads.Keywords: “MRSA, ” “Mycotic Aneurysm, ” “AICD, ” “Infective Aortitis, ” “Infectious PET.”

Illustrated in this case is the rapid growth of aneurysm formation in infective aortitis. The patient required pacemaker explant due to MRSA (Fig. 3), with no signs of aneurysm at the time of explant, as seen in Fig. 1A. The patient was discharged on a 6-week antibiotic course using daptomycin and linezolid with presumed clearance of infection. Four months following discharge, the patient developed generalized weakness, nausea, vomiting, mild confusion, and diarrhea. Due to their volume losses and weakness, the patient fell and was taken to their local hospital. Repeat blood cultures redemonstrated MRSA bacteremia. Both transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiogram could not identify vegetations or signs of infective endocarditis. FDG-PET found hypermetabolic uptake in the descending thoracic aorta after the subclavian takeoff. This was clarified by chest CTA, demonstrating the findings in Fig. 1B and Fig. 2. The patient was treated with vancomycin leading to clinical and symptomatic improvement, but was deemed too high of a surgical risk due to comorbid illnesses and extent of atherosclerosis. Palliative care was pursued with a further 6-week course of vancomycin prescribed. The patient passed following discharge due to presumed aneurysmal rupture.

Axial chest imaging including two FDG-PET scans and a CTA Chest performed 4 months apart. (A) FDG-PET of the descending aorta, without indication of increased metabolic uptake at subclavian takeoff. (B) CTA Chest performed 4 months later with arrow pointing to descending aortic outpouching with contrast extravasation and mass effect on esophagus. (C) FDG-PET performed 4 months later with increased metabolic uptake in similar distribution to extravasation seen on CTA Chest.

CTA Chest performed 4 months later, sagittal view of the aortic arch and left heart. Arrow pointing to mycotic aneurysm immediately distal to left subclavian artery.

The location of our patient’s aneurysm demonstrates the benefit of both FDG-PET and targeted CTA, which are considered the preferred imaging modalities when mycotic aneurysm is suspected [1], [2]. Characteristic imaging findings that aid in diagnosis are contrast-enhancing lobular outpouchings, indistinct aortic walls, and rapid aneurysm progression [1], [3]. Dependence on these broad imaging modalities is due in part to the non-localizing symptoms associated with mycotic aneurysms [4]. Because of this, a degree of suspicion for mycotic aneurysm should exist when there is persistent bacteremia despite treatment and when the patient has risk factors for mycotic aneurysm. Notable risk factors include chronic kidney disease, significant aortic atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes, and recent vascular procedures with a risk of intimal damage [1], [5].
