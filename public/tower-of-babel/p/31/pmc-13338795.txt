Zn metal organic framework tailored cellulose acetate/PVdF-co-HFP based proton conductive composite membranes: performance enhancement in direct methanol fuel cells
Authors: Randa. E. Khalifa, Asmaa M. Abd El-Aziz, Mohamed Samir Mohy-Eldin, Andrew N. Aziz, Mária Kováčová, Anna Vykydalová, Štefan Nagy, Zdenko Špitalský
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A low-cost proton-conductive composite membrane composed of cellulose acetate (CA)/poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) incorporating ZIF-8 (ZPHC) was fabricated via solution casting for direct methanol fuel cell applications. Structural and physicochemical characterization, including FTIR, XRD, TGA, SEM, EDX, BET, and AFM, confirms that the introduction of ZIF-8 increases porosity, amorphous character, water uptake, and hydrogen-bonding interactions, thereby reinforcing the polymer network. Proton conductivity rises sharply from 0.08 × 10−3 S cm−1 for the pristine CA to 2.73 × 10−3 S cm−1 upon ZIF-8 incorporation, while methanol permeability drops from 18 × 10−7 to 2.25 × 10−7 cm2 s−1, notably below that of Nafion® 117, as a standard (21.31 × 10−7 cm2 s−1). Mechanical strength is also enhanced, yielding more than a 2-fold improvement over neat CA. As a result, the composite exhibits markedly higher selectivity (10.31 × 103 S s cm−3) than both pristine CA (0.04 × 103 S s cm−3) and Nafion® 117 (6.43 × 103 S s cm−3). A single DMFC using the ZPHC40-2.5 membrane delivers a peak power density of ∼38 mW cm−2 and a current density of 126.5 mA cm−2 at 30 °C, indicating slightly lower maximum power than Nafion® 117 but promising fuel efficiency and durability for DMFC. The results demonstrate the feasibility of engineering an economically promising proton exchange membrane with potential applicability in DMFC systems.

Introduction

The escalating global energy problem and the greenhouse effect are exacerbated by ongoing reliance on fossil fuels.1–3 This has fueled demand for sustainable, environmentally friendly energy technologies. Fuel cells are a feasible option due to their high efficiency and low pollutant output.4 Among them, direct methanol fuel cells (DMFCs), a type of low-temperature proton exchange membrane fuel cell, have attracted considerable attention due to the high energy density and easy handling of liquid methanol fuel, making them suitable for portable and small-scale power applications. However, issues such as methanol crossover, catalyst degradation, and efficiency losses continue to limit their widespread application, highlighting the need for improved membrane materials.5,6 Nafion® is a conventional perfluorosulfonic acid membrane that exhibits excellent proton conductivity under hydrated conditions but suffers from high cost, excessive methanol permeability, and reduced performance at elevated temperatures.7–9 Therefore, there is growing interest in developing alternative proton exchange membranes with balanced proton conductivity, methanol resistance, stability, and economic feasibility.

Biopolymer-based membranes are appealing in this context owing to their renewability, chemical versatility, and hydrophilicity.10 Cellulose acetate (CA) a key derivative of cellulose synthesized via partial or complete acetylation of hydroxyl groups on anhydroglucose units. CA has attracted significant attention due to its economic feasibility, abundance, environmental sustainability, and versatile chemical tunability.11–14 The presence of polar functional groups, particularly C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> O and C–OH moieties, enhances intermolecular interactions and contributes to proton transport behavior. Nevertheless, pristine CA exhibits limited dimensional stability and relatively low proton conductivity, necessitating modification or blending with other polymers. Previous investigations have shown that CA can physically interact with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) and its copolymers, polyvinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene (PVdF-co-HFP).15,16 These interactions disrupt the crystalline domains of PVDF, thereby increasing its amorphous content. PVdF-co-HFP has emerged as a viable constituent for composite and gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs). Due to its excellent mechanical integrity, improved electrochemical stability, and advantageous ion–transport properties. Thus, incorporating PVdF-co-HFP into biopolymers or including functional additives is a prevalent strategy to enhance ionic conductivity by promoting the formation of interconnected proton-transport channels and convoluted routes that impede methanol permeation, thereby improving overall membrane performance.17

To enhance the performance of non-fluorinated membranes, reinforcing agents such as zeolite, talc, mica, carbon black, fumed silica, and clay minerals have been used in fuel cell applications over the past decades.18–21 Recently, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have attracted increasing attention because of their highly porous structures and tunable transport properties. Among them, ZIF-8 is particularly promising due to its narrow pore aperture (∼0.34 nm), high surface area, and excellent thermal and chemical stability. The porous structure of ZIF-8 can facilitate proton and water transport while restricting methanol diffusion by forming selective transport channels. Furthermore, the imidazole-containing framework may contribute to proton conduction through hydrogen-bonding interactions.22,23 Moreover, its superior thermal and chemical stability provides benefits for DMFC applications.

Herein, the electrochemical performance of PVdF-co-HFP/CA-based electrolytes was investigated by leveraging the high miscibility of CA and PVdF-co-HFP to evaluate the effect of ZIF-8 loading. Zn-based ZIF-8 was synthesized and subsequently incorporated into composite membranes via solution casting. A comprehensive set of characterization techniques, encompassing FTIR, TGA, SEM, XRD, BET, and AFM, was employed. The influence of ZIF-8 loadings on ion-exchange capacity, water and methanol uptake, dimensional stability, thermal oxidative stability, proton conductivity, selectivity, and methanol permeability was analyzed. Subsequently, the optimal membrane performance was systematically evaluated in a single DMFC.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cellulose acetate (CA, Mw ≈ 30 000) and poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVdF-co-HFP, Mw ≈ 400 000) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). 1-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 90% purity), acetone (90% purity), hydrogen peroxide, sulfuric acid, zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O), 2-methylimidazole (C4H6N2), methanol, phenolphthalein, and hydrochloric acid (30–34% purity) and analytical-grade sodium chloride (NaCl) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification, and distilled water (DW) was employed throughout the experiments.

Preparation of ZIF-8

ZIF-8 nanoparticles were prepared via a facile solution-based method conducted at room temperature.24,25 A solution of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, 3.1609 g in 150 mL methanol, was gradually added to a solution of 2-methylimidazole [Hmim], 7.0063 g in 150 mL methanol. The resulting reaction mixture was stirred magnetically at ambient temperature for 1 hour. The resultant solid was subsequently separated via centrifugation, thoroughly washed with methanol, and ultimately exposed to heat treatment at 80 °C for 24 h.

Preparation of blend membranes

Briefly, CA and PVdF-co-HFP at different ratios were individually dissolved in a 4 : 1 (v/v) NMP/acetone mixture under continuous magnetic stirring at 65 °C until clear, homogeneous solutions were obtained. The two polymer solutions were combined in a single vessel and stirred at 70 °C for 5 h to ensure uniform molecular-level blending. The resulting casting solution was left to stand for 12 h to allow complete deaeration and removal of trapped air. The degassed dope was subsequently cast onto Petri dishes and dried at 80 °C for 12 h to yield free-standing membranes. The dried membranes were thereafter immersed in an aqueous sulfuric acid solution (H2SO4/H2O, 30 : 70 v/v) at room temperature for 12 h. The fabricated membranes were abbreviated as PHCx, where x denotes the CA weight ratio. The chemical composition of the prepared blend membranes is reported in Table 1. Pristine CA and PVdF-co-HFP membranes were fabricated using the same procedure for comparison.

Preparation of ZIF-8 modified composite membranes

ZIF-8 decorated membranes (Scheme 1) were prepared by incorporating ZIF-8 at various loadings (0.5, 1.0, 2.5, and 5.0 wt%). The requisite quantity of ZIF-8 was initially dispersed in 4 mL of cosolvent utilizing an ultrasonic probe (UP400S Ultrasonic process/Hielscher, Germany) for a cumulative duration of 14 min, comprising 5 min at 60% amplitude with 5 s on/5 s off pulses, succeeded by 4 min at 40% amplitude with 5 s on/1 s off pulses. The polymer blend solution was subsequently added to the ZIF-8 dispersion and further sonicated for 4 min (5 s on/1 s off) to ensure uniform filler distribution. The resultant composite dope was cast into Petri plates and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The membranes, with thicknesses of 50 ± 5 µm, were washed with distilled water and carefully peeled off. The membranes with codes listed in Table 2 were subsequently treated by immersion in an aqueous sulfuric acid solution (H2SO4/H2O, 30 : 70 v/v) at ambient temperature, then rinsed with deionized water to remove any residual acid.

Structure, thermal, and morphological characterizations

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Shimadzu FTIR-8400S, Japan) was employed to analyze the chemical structures of all fabricated membranes as well as ZIF-8. The crystallinity and X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the synthesized ZIF-8 and the corresponding nanocomposite membranes were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer (Shimadzu XRD-6100, Japan). In addition, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was carried out to determine the specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter of ZIF-8.

The microstructural features of ZIF-8 and fabricated membranes were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL JCM-6010LV), after sputter-coating the fracture surfaces with a thin layer of gold under vacuum, and then the atomic% of elements were determined by EDX (JEOL JSM 6360LA, Japan). Further, the thermal stability was evaluated using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA Q50, USA) under nitrogen at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from room temperature to 600 °C. Surface roughness was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM; Shimadzu SPM-9700). Small membrane specimens were cut, mounted on glass substrates, and scanned over an area of 10 × 10 µm. The surface topography was quantified using the mean roughness (Sa) and the root-mean-square height (Sq) parameters.

Physicochemical and electrochemical characterization

Results and discussion

Characterization of ZIF-8

The structural identities of ZIF-8 were characterized by employing FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 1a). The distinct absorption bands at 1570, 1450, 1425, 1310, 1150, 999, 760, and 690 cm−1, corresponding respectively to CN stretching, asymmetric –CH3 bending (at 1450 and 1425 cm−1), C–H bending of the imidazole ring, C–N stretching, C–N bending, and imidazole ring puckering.29,30 The XRD pattern of ZIF-8, together with the simulated pattern and Pawley refinement, is presented in Fig. 1b&c. The characteristic diffraction peaks appear at 2θ values of 7.4, 10.4, 12.7, 14.7, 16.4, 18.0, 22.1, 24.5, 26.7, and 29.6° can be indexed to the (011), (002), (112), (220), (310), (222), (114), (233), (134), and (235) planes, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. These main reflections agree with previously reported patterns and confirm that ZIF-8 adopts the typical sodalite-type framework.31,32 Additionally, the diffraction peaks were indexed to the corresponding Miller planes using TOPAS 3.0 software (Fig. 1c), and the reflections obtained were in good agreement with the characteristic crystalline structure of ZIF-8, confirming the successful formation of the framework without detectable impurity phases. The sharp and intense diffraction peaks indicate the high crystallinity of the synthesized material. Pawley refinement was performed to obtain more accurate structural information from the diffraction data. The refined cubic lattice parameter was calculated to be a = 17.036 Å, while the average crystallite size was approximately 35 nm. The good agreement between the experimental and refined patterns confirms the structural stability and phase purity of the synthesized ZIF-8. In addition, the refinement provided a more reliable description of the peak profiles and crystallographic parameters of the material.

ZIF-8 characterization: FTIR (a), XRD (b), simulated XRD and Pawley refinement (c), BET (d), TGA (e), SEM (f), and TEM (g).

The BET analysis displayed in Fig. 1d indicates that ZIF-8 particles are highly crystalline, exhibiting a specific surface area of about 1500 m2 g−1, consistent with their high porosity.33 Although BET surface area analysis was successfully conducted for the synthesized ZIF-8 particles, reliable BET measurements for the dense composite membranes were difficult to obtain due to the limited accessible porosity and compact polymeric structure of the cast membranes. Nevertheless, the incorporation of ZIF-8 was found to significantly influence the membrane microstructure, as confirmed by SEM and AFM in the next section. The thermal stability behavior of ZIF-8, shown in Fig. 1e, reflects a sharp thermal decomposition above roughly 420–670 °C, associated with Zn–N bond cleavage, volatilization of the imidazolate ligands, and formation of a ZnO residue, with the final mass stabilized at approximately 70%. (ref. 34 and 35) On the other hand, SEM imaging depicted in Fig. 1f reveals that ZIF-8 nanoparticles generally possess a rhombic dodecahedral or slightly truncated rhombic dodecahedral shape with well-defined facets and sharp edges, consistent with their cubic sodalite topology. The particles exhibit a uniform morphology and homogeneous distribution.30 Typical TEM images in Fig. 1g show nanosized, well-defined polyhedral crystals (often truncated rhombic dodecahedra) with clear lattice fringes at high resolution, with an average particle size of 37 ± 9 nm.

Characterization of composite membranes

FTIR

The FTIR analysis of CA in Fig. 2a displays a strong, sharp band at 1740–1750 cm−1 assigned to the ester carbonyl (CO) stretch, which confirms acetate substitution. Additional characteristic bands are observed at 1230–1240 cm−1 from the C–O–C asymmetric stretching of CH3COO− groups, and at 1370 cm−1 corresponding to C–H deformation of the acetate methyl moieties.36,37 For PVdF-co-HFP, the IR spectrum shows a band at 764 cm−1 attributed to α-phase CH2 rocking. While 870 cm−1 is attributed to the β-phase of CH2, along with a broad absorption between 1170 and 1227 cm−1 associated with CF2 vibrations.38 When CA is blended with PVdF-co-HFP, a fresh carbonyl peak from CA emerges at 1740 cm−1. The intensity of the β-phase indicator band at 870 cm−1 drops in membranes with increasing CA content, suggesting an optimal CA loading that maximizes the β-phase proportion.39–43 This peak also reflects the bending vibration of the CH2-CF2 chain in PVdF-co-HFP. This indicates interactions along the PVDF copolymer chain. Bands at 1380 cm−1 and 1660 cm−1 demonstrate interactions between the PVDF copolymer segment and CA's complex chain, respectively. Additionally, the peak at 2915 cm−1 indicates robust interactions between the aliphatic chains of PVdF-co-HFP and the hydroxyl groups of CA. These interactions boost the composite's chemical stability, reduce agglomeration, and yield a more robust system.15 No additional peaks were observed in the FTIR spectra of the polymer blend membrane. Indicating that sulfuric acid primarily acts as a pore-forming agent without altering the fundamental chemical structure of the membrane, owing to the strong chemical resistance of the polymer matrix. However, the broadening of the absorption bands in the range of 876–1380 cm−1 suggests the possible occurrence of partial sulfonation of CA. This modification may enhance the membrane's electrochemical performance by introducing additional hydrophilic sites and facilitating proton transport. Fig. 2b depicts the schematic illustration of the proposed interaction domains among PVdF-co-HFP, CA, and NMP molecules, as inferred from the FTIR analysis and corresponding band assignments.

On the other hand, the FTIR spectra for the composite membranes were analyzed to confirm the presence of the incorporated ZIF-8; Fig. 2c. Typical ZIF-8 signals were confirmed, like Zn–N coordination vibrations around 420–450 cm−1, imidazolate ring-related C–N stretching in the 650–750 cm−1 range. The broad band at 3300–3500 cm−1 decreases in intensity with increasing degree of acetylation.44 The absence of any new absorption near 1400 cm−1 confirms that no new covalent bonds are formed. Thus, ZIF-8 is physically dispersed within the polymer matrix rather than chemically reacted.39 The elemental analysis results for the modified membrane are shown in Fig. 2d. The EDX spectrum confirmed the presence of C, O, F, S, and Zn in the composite membrane. The detection of sulfur provides additional evidence of the successful introduction of sulfur-containing functional groups following the pre-treatment, consistent with the FTIR results. Furthermore, the presence of Zn confirms the successful incorporation of ZIF-8 into the blend matrix.

TGA

The thermogravimetric profiles of the prepared membranes demonstrate distinct degradation patterns that reflect their relative thermal stability within the composite architecture. CA shows an initial mass loss below 200 °C, attributed to moisture or residual solvent. Followed by a main degradation step between 300 and 350 °C, associated with depolymerization and cleavage of acetyl groups, leaving approximately 20–35% char at 600 °C. PVdF-co-HFP remains highly stable up to about 400–450 °C with less than 2% weight loss. It then undergoes rapid decomposition around 450–500 °C through dehydrofluorination and chain scission, releasing species such as HF and H2CCF2. For the blend membrane, the TGA profile shows intermediate behavior between the two neat polymers. Typically showing improved stability compared with CA alone but somewhat lower than that of pure PVdF-co-HFP. Incorporation of ZIF-8 in the blend matrix further improves thermal stability, as shown in Fig. 3. The ZPHC40-5.0 sample exhibits a 42.23% increase in thermal stability (T50%) relative to pristine CA. And 4.14% higher than PVdF-co-HFP, and 3.12% compared with PHC40, confirming the stabilizing effect of ZIF-8 within the hybrid membrane structure (Table 3).

The cross-section of the pristine, unmodified membranes (Fig. 4) exhibits finger-like macrovoids. Where pure PVdF-co-HFP forms a highly asymmetric structure with large finger-like channels. In contrast, the CA membrane shows a dense, smooth nodular top layer over a sponge-like sublayer. Physico-chemical performance of the prepared membranes

Proton conductivity of blend membranes

Proton conductivity was measured for various blend ratios to identify the optimal composition (Table 5). The results indicate that blending increases the amorphous fraction, enhancing electrolyte uptake compared to the pristine membrane. Greater chain flexibility in these regions facilitates segmental motion and improves proton transport. As shown in Table 5, the pristine CA and PVdF-co-HFP membrane initially exhibits a lower proton conductivity of 0.08 × 10−3 and 0.03 × 10−3 S cm−1, respectively. This behavior is attributed to partial acetylation, a limited number of effective proton-conducting sites in CA, and the hydrophobic nature and dense structure of PVdF-co-HFP. An enhancement in conductivity was observed upon the introduction of CA into the blend-based system, reaching an optimal loading of 40 wt% (0.37 × 10−3 S cm−1). The presence of hydroxyl functionalities in CA, together with polar segments in PVdF-co-HFP, enhances water uptake within the membrane matrix. This behavior promotes the formation of narrow channels that retain water molecules15 and facilitates the formation and transport of hydrated ionic species via the vehicular mechanism. Additionally, intermolecular interactions within the hydrated polymer matrix may facilitate proton transfer via a Grotthuss-type hopping mechanism.17 These findings align with earlier studies.48 A further increase in CA content reduced the proton conductivity of the polymer blend membrane. At 50 wt% CA, the membrane exhibited a proton conductivity of only 0.31 × 10−3 S cm−1. This reduction can be attributed to excessive CA loading, which may promote agglomeration and partial pore blockage within the membrane structure. As a result, the intermolecular interactions and the formation of effective proton-conducting pathways were weakened. Accordingly, a CA content of 40 wt% was selected as the optimum composition for fabricating ZIF-8-incorporated nanocomposite membranes.

IEC, ionic conductivity, and tensile properties of ZPHC40-x composite membranes

The IEC behavior of the ZPHC40-x membranes (Fig. 7) highlights the strong influence of polymer blending and ZIF-8 loading on ion-exchange functionality. Pristine CA shows a moderate IEC due to its polar acetyl and residual hydroxyl groups. In contrast, blending CA with PVdF-co-HFP results in a marked reduction in IEC. This attribute of PVdF-co-HFP's hydrophobic and electrochemically inert nature, which dilutes ion-exchange sites and limits ionic pathways,.49 The incorporation of ZIF-8 leads to a significant, progressive increase in IEC. Even at low ZIF-8 loading (e.g., 0.5 wt%), the IEC improves markedly, owing to ZIF-8's microporous structure and the presence of nitrogen-containing imidazolate linkers, which promote Lewis acid-base interactions and facilitate proton uptake and exchange.50 The porous structure of ZIF-8 increases surface roughness, providing additional sites and free volume for water absorption, thereby increasing water uptake and enhancing ion dissociation and mobility within the membrane, thereby providing additional ion-accessible domains.51

Ion exchange capacity of nanocomposite membranes using varying ZIF-8 load.

Further increases in ZIF-8 content (up to 2.5 wt%) promote the formation of more continuous ion-conducting pathways at the polymer–filler interface. This resulted in a near-linear increase in IEC, suggesting a more interconnected ionic network. In this regime, ZIF-8 nanoparticles are sufficiently well-dispersed to form continuous ion-conducting pathways at the polymer interface. However, hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups of CA and the fluorine segments of PVDF reduces the number of accessible functional sites. Consequently, only the carboxylic groups remain available for ion exchange, thereby limiting the ion exchange capacity to values below 1 meq g−1.

PEMs' mechanical characteristics are crucial, as they must possess adequate strength to withstand manipulation and treatment during the MEA production process. The tensile strength and elongation at break of membranes with varying ZIF-8 content were evaluated and shown in Table 6. Pristine CA exhibits relatively low tensile strength due to its inherent brittleness.52 The rigid backbone and strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding restrict chain mobility, leading to early fracture under tensile loading.53–55 In contrast, PVdF-co-HFP exhibits significantly higher tensile strength (48.02 MPa) and markedly improved ductility. Which reflect the flexible semi-crystalline fluoropolymer backbone. Blending CA with PVdF-co-HFP further increased tensile strength to 51.85 MPa while slightly reducing elongation (6.30%), indicating improved interfacial compatibility and stress transfer via hydrogen-bonding interactions.56 Upon incorporating ZIF-8, the elongation at break decreased progressively from 6.39% for ZPHC40-0.5 to 1.69% for ZPHC40-5. While the tensile strength initially increased and reached a maximum value at ZPHC40-1. This behavior can be attributed to the rigid inorganic framework of ZIF-8 and the strong interfacial interactions between ZIF-8 and the polymer matrix, which restrict polymer chain mobility and reduce membrane flexibility. Despite the decrease in elongation, the membranes maintained relatively high tensile strength and structural integrity, indicating they did not undergo catastrophic mechanical failure. The optimized membrane (ZPHC40-2.5) still exhibited acceptable mechanical stability, enhanced proton conductivity, and low methanol permeability, which are critical for DMFC operation. Therefore, the results suggest a typical trade-off between membrane rigidity and flexibility upon incorporation of inorganic filler.57

Alternatively, the evolution of proton conductivity closely correlates with the observed mechanical behavior, reflecting the intrinsic balance between structural reinforcement and chain mobility in the composite membranes. The proton conductivity results demonstrate a progressive enhancement in ionic transport upon incorporation of ZIF-8 nanoparticles. The results shown in Table 6 revealed that conductivity increased significantly from 0.79 to 2.73 × 10−3 S cm−1 as ZIF-8 content increased from 0.5 to 2.5 wt%. This improvement can be attributed to increased interfacial free volume and the formation of hydration layers at the polymer-ZIF-8 interface. Besides, the porous framework of ZIF-8, which acts as a water-distribution scaffold that promotes the continuity of proton-conducting channels.23,58 Although the IEC increased with increasing ZIF-8 loading, the proton conductivity reached its maximum at ZPHC40-2.5 and slightly decreased for ZPHC40-5. This behavior suggests that proton conductivity depends not only on IEC but also on membrane morphology and the continuity of ionic transport channels. At excessive ZIF-8 loading, partial particle aggregation and increased membrane rigidity may reduce polymer chain mobility and hinder efficient proton transport despite the higher IEC value. Therefore, ZPHC40-2.5 provided the optimal balance between ionic functionality and proton-transport efficiency.

Overall, the observed enhancement in proton conductivity arises from improved microstructural organization and hydration-assisted transport rather than the introduction of additional fixed ionic groups. Although the highest tensile strength was observed at 1 wt% ZIF-8, the maximum proton conductivity was achieved at 2.5 wt%, highlighting the balance between mechanical reinforcement and hydration behavior. Increasing filler content improves proton mobility through enhanced interfacial pathways but may slightly reduce mechanical performance. Optimal performance is achieved at 2.5 wt—% ZIF-8, where conductivity enhancement and mechanical stability are effectively balanced. Liquid uptake, contact angle, and dimensional stability

The membranes' water and methanol uptake behaviors are shown in Fig. 8, which exhibits clear temperature and composition-dependent behavior. For all samples, both water and methanol uptake increase with temperature due to enhanced polymer chain mobility and expansion of free volume. Thereby facilitating solvent diffusion into the membrane matrix. Pristine CA exhibits moderate uptake for both solvents at room temperature, reflecting its polar functional groups and its strong affinity for small polar molecules.14,60 In contrast, PVdF-co-HFP demonstrates lower solvent uptake owing to its hydrophobic fluorinated backbone, which limits solvent–polymer interactions. The PHC40 blend membrane exhibits reduced water uptake relative to neat CA, primarily due to the incorporation of the hydrophobic PVDF copolymer. However, its uptake remains higher than pristine PVdF-co-HFP, attributed to the excess hydroxyl groups of CA, which capture water molecules within the narrow channels formed by the polymer blend.55 Additionally, treatment with a sulfuric acid-water solution further enhances water uptake by activating the sulfonic groups on the surface of CA.17,61

Water (a) and methanol uptake (b) of CA, PVdF-co-HFP, PHC40, and ZPHC40-2.5 membranes.

The ZIF-8-containing membrane exhibits a significant increase in water uptake. At low/moderate ZIF-8 contents, water uptake often increases because ZIF-8's porous structure and increased surface roughness provide extra sites and free volume for water absorption. This can support higher ion uptake and hydration stability without excessive swelling, as well as conductivity.62 On the other hand, its methanol uptake remains comparatively lower than that of CA and PVdF-co-HFP, particularly at lower temperatures. This divergence suggests that the higher internal surface area and small pore size of ZIF-8 restrict methanol penetration. This thereby lowers methanol solubility and diffusion through the membrane, improving solvent selectivity. In contrast, methanol transport is hindered by tortuous diffusion paths and by size-selective effects introduced by ZIF-8.22,23 Overall, these results highlight the beneficial role of ZIF-8 incorporation in enhancing membrane hydration while suppressing methanol uptake, a desirable balance for PEM and DMFC-related applications. This behavior was supported by contact angle measurements (Fig. 9). The data indicated that blending PVdF-co-HFP with CA and incorporating ZIF-8 progressively enhances surface hydrophilicity. Neat PVdF-co-HFP exhibits the highest contact angle, confirming its intrinsically hydrophobic nature, while neat CA is less hydrophobic but still yields a relatively beaded droplet. The blend membrane exhibits a lower contact angle than pure PVdF-co-HFP, indicating that CA's polar groups partially dominate the surface. Adding a small amount of ZIF-8 to this blend further reduces the contact angle to the lowest value among the samples, suggesting that ZIF-8 and CA together promote improved water affinity and wetting of the membrane surface.

FCD and thermal oxidative stability

The data presented in Table 8 highlight that the synergistic interaction between CA polar groups and ZIF-8 surfaces enhances FCD (which expresses the concentration of fixed charges within the hydrated membrane phase). Therefore, it is strongly influenced by swelling and water uptake 22). Pristine PVdF-co-HFP exhibits the lowest FCD (0.26 meq L−1), consistent with its hydrophobic and non-ionic structure. In comparison, CA shows a comparatively higher value (2.17 meq L−1) due to its polar functional groups. The PHC40 blend membrane exhibits an intermediate FCD (0.94 meq L−1), indicating a partial enhancement of charge distribution via structural modification and increased hydration. The ZPHC40-2.5 membrane exhibits a substantial increase in FCD (10.87 meq L−1), suggesting that ZIF-8 incorporation significantly promotes interfacial charge accumulation and hydrated ionic domains within the composite matrix.

In terms of oxidative stability, PVdF-co-HFP and PHC40 membranes maintain high residual weights (92% and 91%, respectively) and comparable rupture times of 70 h, demonstrating superior durability relative to pristine CA (85%). The lower stability of CA is associated with its ester and remaining hydroxyl groups, which are vulnerable to radical attack and chain scission in oxidative environments.52 In contrast, the enhanced stability of the fluorinated systems arises from the highly stable C–F bonds within the fluorinated backbone, which possess high bond dissociation energies and strong resistance to oxidative cleavage. As a result, fluorinated segments significantly suppress polymer chain degradation. Further enhancement is observed upon incorporation of ZIF-8 (e.g., ZPHC40-2.5). The presence of ZIF-8 nanoparticles improves oxidative durability by restricting chain mobility, reducing free volume, and acting as a physical barrier against oxidative species. Additionally, the strong interfacial interactions between ZIF-8 and the polymer chains enhance structural integrity under oxidative stress, indicating that hybrid organic-inorganic architectures are more resistant to oxidative degradation than the native biopolymer membrane.

Permeability and selectivity

Methanol permeability, along with water through the membrane, adversely affects the performance of the DMFC. The control of methanol permeability in the membrane depends on three factors: hydrophobicity, tortuosity, and channel size.56 It is well known that commercial Nafion® 117 has higher methanol permeability, which is considered one of its primary drawbacks as PEM for DMFCs. Further, in the context of DMFC performance, membrane selectivity constitutes a critical parameter governing overall electrochemical efficiency. A high selectivity reflects the membrane's ability to facilitate proton transport while effectively suppressing methanol crossover, thereby minimizing fuel loss and cathodic depolarization.

The methanol permeability of the modified membranes was studied at 25 °C, and the results are reported in Table 9. Based on the provided data, incorporating ZIF-8 into the PHC40 membrane markedly improves methanol-barrier performance and overall membrane selectivity compared to CA. Pristine CA exhibits relatively high methanol permeability (18 × 10−7 cm2 s−1) with very low selectivity (0.04 × 103 S s cm−3), indicating poor suitability for DMFC applications. Although PVdF-co-HFP shows lower permeability (4.04 × 10−7 cm2 s−1), its selectivity remains limited. In contrast, PHC40 membrane demonstrates moderate permeability (9.97 × 10−7 cm2 s−1) with improved selectivity (0.46 × 103 S s cm−3), suggesting partial enhancement of proton-methanol discrimination. This may be attributed to the formation of a tortuous path and the reduction in the size of the narrow channels, as well as to the strong physical interaction between PVdF-co-HFP and CA, which acts as a barrier to swelling and protects the walls, thereby allowing high methanol crossover.23,63 The ZPHC40-2.5 membrane demonstrates the lowest methanol permeability (2.25 × 10−7 cm2 s−1) and superior selectivity (10.31 × 103 S s cm−3), compared to both pristine CA and Nafion® 117. This significant improvement can be attributed to the microporous structure of ZIF-8 with a pore aperture (∼3.4 Å) comparable to the kinetic diameter of methanol.

In addition, well-dispersed ZIF-8 particles act as a molecular sieve, restricting methanol diffusion across the membrane. The rigid ZIF-8 framework increases the tortuosity of diffusion pathways and reduces the effective free volume within the polymer matrix, thereby suppressing methanol crossover.64,65 However, this reduction is strongly dependent on ZIF-8 loading. Moderate incorporation improves methanol barrier properties, whereas excessive loading may lead to particle agglomeration and the formation of non-selective voids, potentially compromising transport selectivity. Overall, the results confirm that optimized ZIF-8 loading substantially enhances membrane efficiency by simultaneously reducing methanol permeability and maximizing selectivity, while maintaining acceptable proton–conduction pathways.23

DMFC performance

The polarization and power density profiles presented in Fig. 10a and b provide a comprehensive evaluation of the DMFC performance. In Fig. 10a, the PVdF-co-HFP support membrane exhibits the lowest current density and power output, indicating its very low proton conductivity and high ohmic resistance dominate cell losses. The neat CA membrane exhibits a moderate initial voltage but then rapidly declines with increasing current density, resulting in a relatively low maximum power density. This behavior is primarily attributed to insufficient proton conductivity, pronounced methanol crossover leading to mixed potentials at the cathode and reduced fuel efficiency, and swelling-induced instability. In contrast, the PHC40 blend markedly increases both current and power densities compared with the CA membrane, shifting the entire polarization curve to higher current at a given voltage and yielding a higher peak power density. This trend is consistent with other reports showing that cellulose-modified PVDF-type membranes exhibit enhanced ionic pathways and reduced internal resistance, which directly manifest as steeper initial polarization slopes and higher maximum power densities.17,49 However, the blend membrane still exhibits faster voltage decay at higher current densities, suggesting higher ohmic and mass-transport losses, likely due to its lower conductivity and less-optimized microstructure.

DMFC polarization and power density curves operating with 1 M methanol using (a) PVDF-HFP, CA, and PHC40 membranes, and (b) Nafion® 117 and ZPHC40-2.5 membranes.

On the other hand, Fig. 10b shows that Nafion® 117 achieves the highest peak power density (58.22 mW cm−2) at a current density of 194 mA cm−2, reflecting its intrinsically high proton conductivity and well-developed hydrophilic/hydrophobic phase separation. Notably, the ZPHC40-2.5 membrane exhibits a slightly lower maximum power density (∼38 mW cm−2) and an achievable current density of 126.5 mA cm−2. Still, its voltage remains relatively higher at low and moderate current densities, indicating lower methanol crossover and a reduced mixed potential at the cathode. This improvement, compared to pristine CA, can be attributed to the synergistic integration of PVdF-co-HFP, which enhances mechanical integrity and reduces excessive swelling, and to the incorporation of ZIF-8, which introduces additional hydrogen-bonding-assisted proton transport pathways and increases tortuosity for methanol diffusion. When benchmarked against Nafion® 117, the commercial membrane still delivers superior performance. Nevertheless, the ZPHC40-2.5 membrane significantly outperforms pristine CA and approaches the polarization behavior of Nafion® 117, indicating that controlled blending and MOF incorporation effectively improve membrane selectivity and performance.

In addition, the proposed transport mechanism suggests that ZIF-8 enhances proton migration through increased free volume, amorphous regions, water retention, and intermolecular interactions, while simultaneously creating tortuous diffusion pathways that suppress methanol crossover. Consequently, it demonstrates its potential as an economically promising alternative to Nafion®117 for low-temperature DMFC applications.

Conclusion

Conclusively, to overcome the low proton conductivity of commonly used cellulose-based membranes, the CA/PVdF-co-HFP-incorporated ZIF-8 has been introduced as a new electrolyte membrane for DMFC applications. Proton transport in the developed membranes is governed by hydration-assisted conduction, facilitated by increased amorphous regions and interfacial water domains generated by CA blending and ZIF-8 incorporation. The porous ZIF-8 acts as a water-distribution scaffold, promoting percolated proton pathways, while excessive ZIF-8 loading induces structural heterogeneity and mechanical embrittlement. Optimal performance is achieved at 2.5 wt% ZIF-8, where conductivity enhancement and mechanical stability are effectively balanced. As concerns water uptake, it was reliable only for proton and methanol transport, since water molecules were believed to act as transport agents in these processes. However, in this study, the water uptake values were not directly correlated with proton conductivity or methanol permeability. Performance tests of the laboratory-fabricated membranes showed that incorporating ZIF-8 significantly improved proton conductivity by up to 34-fold and resulted in an almost eightfold suppression of methanol permeability, compared to neat CA. Although ZPHC40-2.5 possessed higher water uptake than Nafion® 117, the value of proton conductivity was smaller, even though the methanol barrier properties were outrageously lower (2.25 × 10−7 cm2 s−1) as compared to Nafion® 117 (21.31 × 10−7 cm2 s−1). Furthermore, the performance of ZPHC40-2.5 nanocomposite membranes demonstrates a maximum power density of ∼38 mW cm−2, compared to 58.22 mW cm−2 for Nafion® 117, indicating that properly engineered non-perfluorinated or hybrid systems can narrow the performance gap relative to Nafion® while potentially offering lower cost, better dimensional stability, and tunable methanol permeability. Consequently, these results position ZPHC40-2.5 as a significant upgrade over CA for low-cost DMFCs and identify it as a promising compromise between CA-type membranes and commercial Nafion® 117, especially where crossover suppression and materials sustainability are prioritized over absolute maximum power density.
