Overcoming Resistance: A Comprehensive Review and Treatment Approach for Acyclovir-Resistant Herpes Simplex Keratitis
Authors: Arash Mirzaei, Sahel Soltani Shahgoli, Raghuram Kognati, Emine Esra Karaca, Kasra Cheraqpour, Farhaneh Alahyari, Deepak Shukla, Ali R. Djalilian, Mohammad Soleimani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To comprehensively review the treatment approach for acyclovir (ACV)-resistant herpes simplex keratitis (HSK).Methods:We thoroughly searched Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus for relevant keywords and conducted this narrative review which explores the diagnosis of ACV-resistant HSK, and discusses alternative treatment strategies, reports previous cases.Results:Treatment strategies for ACV-resistant HSK include increasing ACV doses, switching to oral valacyclovir (VACV), and using topical antivirals such as foscarnet (FOS), ganciclovir (GCV), trifluridine, and cidofovir. Debridement of lesions and emerging antiviral agents are also considered. The review emphasizes the importance of diagnosing cytomegalovirus in ACV-resistant cases and explores new treatments like helicase primase inhibitors and BX795. Insights from the authors’ clinical experience contribute to understanding ACV-resistant HSK and provide valuable guidance for clinicians managing this condition.Conclusions:HSK is a leading cause of ocular complications in HSV infections, potentially causing visual impairment. ACV has been the primary treatment, but rising ACV resistance necessitates alternative strategies. Options include increased ACV doses, oral VACV, topical FOS, GCV, interferon, and lesion debridement. Clinicians should also explore emerging treatments for ACV-resistant HSK.

INTRODUCTION

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) has two subtypes, HSV-1 and HSV-2, which infect only humans. HSV-1 affects about 67% of individuals aged 0–49, while HSV-2 affects 11.3% of this population.1 HSV-1 typically affects the lips, oral cavity, and eyes, while HSV-2 usually causes genital infections. Episodic reactivation is common, with 40% of patients having 2–5 relapses and 11% experiencing 6–15 relapses in their lifetime.2 Direct contact via mucous membranes is the primary route of HSV ocular infection.3 Primary HSV infection is often subclinical but may cause unilateral blepharitis or conjunctivitis. The virus becomes latent in the trigeminal ganglion, with reactivation leading to recurrent infections.4

Herpes simplex keratitis (HSK) is the most common ocular complication of HSV infection.56. The incidence of HSK ranges from 10 to 30 per 100,000 people per year in developed countries, with a prevalence of 149 per 100,000 people.7 It is mainly caused by infection with HSV-1 and, to a lesser extent, HSV-2.2 It is also cited as a leading cause of infectious blindness in industrialized countries,5 due to corneal scarring and opacification.8 Even in eyes that retain visual acuity, infection causes corneal opacities that can lead to aberrations and irregular astigmatism that affect the overall optical quality of the eye.9 Epithelial keratitis is the most common form of HSK, accounting for 50% to 80% of ocular herpetic infection cases.710 Epithelial HSV keratitis occurs in 5–22 per 100,000 person-years, with bilateral cases being less common but more severe. These cases often lead to increased scarring and decreased visual acuity, especially when linked with atopy or immunodeficiency.11

Acyclovir (ACV), the most commonly used drug for HSK, is a prodrug activated by HSV thymidine kinase and host enzymes into its active form, ACV triphosphate.12 ACV effectively treats HSV infections by selectively interfering with viral DNA synthesis and is available in oral, intravenous (IV), and topical forms. Oral ACV has good bioavailability, though it decreases with higher doses, with IV ACV offering about eight times the bioavailability of oral ACV.13 Successful treatment of HSK shortens disease duration and prevents vision loss and recurrence. However, increasing resistance to ACV raises concerns about its effectiveness.14

The ability of HSV to become latent in the ganglion is key to recurrent HSK, as the initial infection source often reactivates. The virus that established latency is typically the one that reactivates, as subsequent strains are less likely to cause reinfection.14 Persistent HSV is recognized when an epithelial lesion in an adherent patient has not healed within 2 weeks.2 Recurrent infections resistant to ACV suggest that patients may harbor a latent resistant strain or have subclinical manifestations. This implies that inadequate HSV-specific host defenses allow different viral genotypes to infect and establish latency, particularly in immunocompromised individuals.14

Drug resistance is the decreased susceptibility to a drug in a laboratory culture system and is reported as altered IC50 or IC90, drug concentrations required to prevent viral growth by 50% and 90%, respectively.15 It is estimated that 4% to 7% of HSV isolates from immunocompromised patients are resistant to nucleoside analogs such as ACV.16 Mutated HSV strains may recur more frequently and heal more slowly,17 but usually respond to alternative treatments.181920 In 2008, a study found ACV resistance in up to 6.4% of immunocompetent patients with recurrent HSK.21 This suggests that the cornea may be a favorable site for the development of ACV resistance compared with other known sites susceptible to HSV infection.22

Immunocompetent patients generally have a stronger immune response against HSV infections, reducing the risk of recurrent infections and antiviral resistance. However, the rising resistance of HSV to ACV poses a significant challenge for immunocompromised patients, highlighting the urgent need for alternative treatments with different mechanisms of action.14

Up to now, several studies have reported the resistance of HSK to ACV. Therefore, the objective of this review is to detail the resistance of HSK to ACV and to propose alternative treatment approaches.

METHODS

The terms searched were “keratitis”, “herpes simplex virus”, “refractory HSV keratitis”, “acyclovir”, “ganciclovir”, “valacyclovir”, “foscarnet”, “antiviral agents”, and “drug resistance”.

RESULTS

Diagnosis of resistance to acyclovir in herpes simplex keratitis

If treatment is ineffective, it is crucial to confirm the diagnosis through laboratory tests due to possible differential diagnoses. Diagnosing ACV-resistant HSV keratitis via viral culture and drug sensitivity tests is often complex.23 Instead, the use of real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a highly sensitive technique for pathogen detection and quantification.2425 We typically depend on clinical manifestations for diagnosis, using PCR only in cases with differential diagnoses like cytomegalovirus (CMV) or fungal diseases.

CMV can be detected in lesions involving all layers of the cornea, including the epithelium. Real-time PCR diagnosis detected CMV in endothelial keratitis but not in epithelial or stromal keratitis of unknown origin.26 In cases of corneal edema of unknown origin, 24.1% tested positive for CMV. This finding should be considered in the differential diagnosis of idiopathic corneal endotheliitis or graft edema, particularly in bullous keratopathy.27

Previous investigations reporting herpes simplex virus drug resistance

ACV resistance in patients with HSK has been reported since the 1990s [Table 1]. There are a number of cases of epithelial keratitis due to HSV that are refractory to topical treatment with ACV.17232834 Zhang et al. reported a 70-year-old man with dendritic HSV keratitis who was resistant to ACV after long-term use of topical ACV and fluorometholone.28 Toriyama et al. described a 54-year-old man with resistant herpetic keratitis after penetrating keratoplasty. He did not respond to 3% topical ACV ointment, but switching to topical trifluridine (TFT) led to gradual pain reduction and improvement of the lesions.32

Choong et al. reported three cases of ACV-resistant HSK in elderly patients. Treatment was switched to foscarnet (FOS), which resulted in only minimal stromal scarring.30 Azar et al. reported two immunocompetent patients, aged 77 and 76 years, who developed HSK that was resistant to ACV and sensitive to FOS.31 Minnick et al. described an 89-year-old patient with ACV-resistant HSK, initially treated with ganciclovir (GCV) and ACV. Due to resistance, treatment was switched to famciclovir, which successfully cleared the infection. The study suggests using famciclovir instead of VACV in ACV-resistant cases, as VACV is a prodrug of ACV and may exhibit cross-resistance.33

Cross-resistance to ACV, GCV, and idoxuridine has been reported. A study of 173 immunocompetent HSK patients found 6.4% resistant to ACV, with 5 of these also resistant to GCV. Such cross-resistance is more frequent in patients with atopic disease or those using topical corticosteroids.23 Rousseau et al. studied 18 patients with HSK who underwent genotypic resistance testing due to multiple relapses or poor response to antiviral therapy. They found that 15 (83%) of these patients had resistant mutations, highlighting the prevalence of ACV-resistant HSK in such cases.5 Duan et al. also examined 39 corneal isolates from 15 immunocompetent patients with recurrent HSK for ACV susceptibility. They found ACV-resistant or ACV-susceptible sequential isolates in 4 and 2 patients, respectively.29

Treatment options in patients with herpes simplex keratitis-resistant to acyclovir

First of all, it is important to evaluate the resistance profile of the HSV strain when there is suspicion of antiviral resistance. Treatment options for ACV resistance in consenting patients may include oral ACV at an increased dose (800 mg 5 times/day), VACV (1 g 3 times/day for 1 week), or FOS (2.6 mg/ml drops every 2 h for 2 weeks). Because VACV has a three-to five-fold higher bioavailability than ACV,35 oral use of VACV has emerged as one of the proposed prophylactic treatment options for HSK.3637

In addition, FOS, a pyrophosphate analog that does not require phosphorylation to prevent DNA polymerase, is active against ACV-resistant HSV with TK mutations.2 Debridement of lesions may also be considered,2 as debridement or cauterization of the corneal epithelium removes or destroys the virus-infected cells on the corneal surface. An occlusive patch applied immediately after debridement increases corneal temperature, improving the epithelial healing rate.3839 A meta-analysis indicated that combining mechanical debridement with antiviral therapy may speed epithelial keratitis healing, but definitive recommendations were limited due to study heterogeneity.40

GCV 0.15% gel is another approved topical treatment for herpetic keratitis that is better tolerated compared with ACV 0.3% ointment. Topical GCV does not cause adverse systemic complications and is therapeutic at lower concentrations.41 Hlinomazová et al. found that 14% of patients with PCR-confirmed HSV-1 keratitis and keratouveitis were clinically resistant to ACV. These ACV-resistant cases were successfully treated with topical GCV gel 0.15%.18

Moreover, interferon can be used in combination with antiviral agents for resistant HSK.40 Minkovitz et al. found that topical interferon ɑ-2a was an effective treatment adjunct for refractory HSK in patients with cell-mediated immune dysfunction and a resulting deficiency of endogenous interferon.20 In addition, a combination of interferon and debridement has been suggested to prevent early recurrence.40 Interferon therapy, however, must be administered with caution due to its significant side effects.

Another potential antiviral agent is TFT, an inhibitor of thymidylate synthetase, whose 1% solution has been used to treat HSV keratitis.42 Although topical TFT has been used with some success to treat ACV-resistant mucocutaneous lesions,2328 there are no randomized clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of TFT in ACV-resistant HSV. It is worth noting that this drug frequently causes keratopathy.43

Because of the different mechanism of action of cidofovir compared with ACV, topical cidofovir 0.2%–0.5% has been reported to be helpful in ACV-resistant HSK with certain mutations.34 The use of cidofovir is limited due to its poor safety profile, and while brivudine might benefit ACV-resistant HSV strains, it is no longer manufactured.44

New treatment approaches on the horizon for herpes simplex keratitis

New treatments for ACV-resistant HSK, such as topical 6-thioguanine, have shown potential as more effective alternatives to ACV and GCV.45 Helicase primase inhibitors (pritelivir and amenamevir) are a new class of antiviral drugs that inhibit viral DNA synthesis independently of TK.4647 Their role in the treatment of HSK is currently unknown but will be a future research focus.

Harringtonine is a natural alkaloid and homologue that exhibits antitumor activities.28 More recently, its antiviral activity has been recognized,48495051 and Liu et al. demonstrated its efficacy against five HSV-1 strains, two of which were resistant to ACV.52 Other novel therapies include Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 and SC93305.53

BX795 is a promising antiviral agent effective against ACV-resistant HSV-1. It offers an alternative treatment by targeting AKT phosphorylation in host cells, distinct from mechanism of ACV. This unique action halts viral protein synthesis and may effectively manage resistant HSV-1 infections.5455 Previous studies have explored using DECON, a new muco-adhesive, carbon-based drug delivery system, to enhance the efficacy of topical ACV. However, further investigation is needed to validate this alternative to conventional dosing systems.56

DISCUSSION

In this study, we initially tried to comprehensively review the treatment approach for ACV-resistant HSK and we noticed previous investigations on this issue. We also have following significant experience in our tertiary referral center. Among the different herpetic keratitis affecting different aspects of the cornea, we found that resistance was more common in epithelial keratitis than in stromal keratitis. Most of our ACV-resistant cases occurred in immunodeficient patients, and most of them had previously received bone marrow transplantation. We have also encountered keratitis secondary to CMV in our practice. Therefore, ophthalmologists should consider CMV as a differential diagnosis for ACV-resistant HSK, especially in patients with endothelial involvement. In these cases, an anterior chamber specimen should be obtained and tested by PCR.

Moreover, we found that in treating patients with HSK, it is preferable to use oral medications rather than topical drops. TFT may be an option because of its different mechanism, but it has insignificant effects. In addition, switching from ACV to VACV is one of the easiest ways to increase efficacy because it has higher bioavailability and equivalent effect to IV ACV. In addition, we have successfully used debridement in conjunction with the application of a bandage contact lens or occlusion patch in our patients, resulting in significant improvements in clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, ACV-resistant HSK is a growing challenge, and clinicians need to be aware of alternative treatment options. While increased doses of ACV, VACV, and topical FOS are mainstays of treatment, other options like debridement, GCV, interferon, and TFT can be used. New antiviral agents like helicase-primase inhibitors, harringtonine, and BX795 are promising for the future. Ophthalmologists should also consider CMV as a possible cause, especially in patients with endothelial involvement. When treating HSK, oral medications are generally preferred over topical drops. This review provides a comprehensive overview of current treatment strategies for ACV-resistant HSK.
