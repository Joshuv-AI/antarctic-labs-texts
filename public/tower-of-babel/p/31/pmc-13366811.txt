Patient understanding of AI-simplified oncology imaging reports requires further validation
Authors: Wei Wei, Changyu Du, Jiageng Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ribeiro and colleagues offer timely evidence that large language models can make oncology imaging reports more accessible to radiologists and patient and public involvement representatives. Their findings also highlight an important distinction between a report that is easier to read and one that patients understand accurately. As the authors acknowledge, the patient-facing assessment included three representatives with previous experience in oncology imaging research and was restricted to reports that had received high factual-correctness scores from radiologists. This approach was suitable for comparing presentation preferences, although it provides limited evidence about comprehension in routine practice, particularly for lower-scoring or clinically ambiguous outputs. Further studies could examine unselected reports in patients with varied health and digital literacy, using outcomes such as comprehension, recognition of uncertainty, emotional response, and intended action. In future adequately powered studies, accounting for ratings clustered by reader and report may provide more precise estimates. Such evidence would help clarify the role of AI-generated explanations in patient-facing oncology care.

Ribeiro and colleagues address an increasingly relevant question: how should oncology imaging findings be communicated when patients may read their reports before speaking with a clinician? Their comparison of summarised and full-length GPT-4 outputs is clinically informative. The reported instances of factual error, complex terminology, added confusion, and omitted information also provide a useful account of the limitations that remain [1]. In our view, these findings invite closer consideration of the distinction between readability, preference, comprehension, and safe interpretation.

As Ribeiro et al. acknowledge, the patient-facing evaluation was undertaken by three patient and public involvement (PPI) representatives, each with previous experience of oncology imaging research. They reviewed ten reports from each tumour cohort, selected after both simplified versions had received radiologist factual-correctness scores above four. This selection allowed the authors to compare report formats while reducing the influence of obvious factual problems. Accordingly, the resulting preference estimates most directly describe a small, highly engaged group reviewing comparatively successful outputs, rather than establishing a general bias in the study. Responses in a broader patient population may differ, particularly among people with limited health literacy, less familiarity with digital health information, heightened anxiety, or a different first language.

The reports excluded from the PPI assessment may nevertheless be particularly informative when considering clinical use. Across the complete evaluation, the readers identified 28 incorrect statements, 10 instances of added confusion, and 10 instances of missing information [1]. The supplementary examples further show that semantic fidelity involves more than replacing technical terms with simpler language. Instructions to explain “potential diagnoses”, “suggested next steps”, or the “meaning” of a finding may encourage the model to move beyond translation towards clinical interpretation. An output can therefore appear clear and reassuring while subtly altering diagnostic certainty, causal attribution, or the emphasis placed on follow-up. Similar tensions between improved readability and residual errors or omissions have been reported in earlier studies [2, 3].

Future validation could therefore extend the authors’ work in several practical ways. Evaluation on unselected consecutive reports would retain difficult cases, with errors classified according to their likely clinical consequence rather than represented only by an overall rating. Patient participants could reflect a range of cancer stages, ages, educational backgrounds, primary languages, and health-literacy levels. Alongside preference, relevant outcomes would include objective comprehension, recognition of uncertainty, recall of follow-up recommendations, emotional response, and intended action. This gap deserves particular emphasis: a recent systematic review found that only three of 16 studies (19%) evaluated comprehensibility with non-medical laypersons [4], whereas Van Driel et al. specifically evaluated patient comprehension of GPT-4-simplified radiology reports [5]. The generated explanation should also be evaluated within its intended clinical pathway. Relevant safeguards may include clear labelling, access to clinician-verified information, and a route for clarification when findings are urgent or uncertain. Systems such as RadGPT similarly suggest that explanatory materials may be better assessed as part of a broader communication resource than as a direct replacement for the radiology report [6].

In future, adequately powered validation studies, statistical analysis could account for the crossed structure of ratings within readers and reports. Mixed-effects models with reader and case effects may help distinguish report-format differences from variation between individual readers, cases, and tumour cohorts. Because the present patient-facing evaluation included only three readers, such modelling should be viewed as a design recommendation for larger studies rather than an analysis that can necessarily be implemented robustly in the current dataset.

Ribeiro and colleagues provide a valuable foundation for this developing area and identify several issues that warrant further study. The next question is whether patients with different backgrounds derive the intended clinical meaning from an AI-generated explanation, recognise uncertainty, and understand any recommended next step. Evidence from unselected reports and representative patient groups would help define how these tools might be introduced responsibly into oncology imaging practice.
