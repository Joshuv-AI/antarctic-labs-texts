Compliance with post-extraction instructions and its association with alveolar osteitis (dry socket): a prospective observational study
Authors: Aniqa Saleem, Nadia Khaleeq, Tanzeel Salim Gandapur, Nazneen Khanum, Zohaib Khan, Khalid Rehman, Zeeshan Kibria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlveolar osteitis, also known as dry socket, is a common complication following tooth extraction and is characterized by pain and delayed healing. Compliance with post-extraction instructions may influence healing outcomes; however, quantitative data on compliance and its association with alveolar osteitis are limited in Pakistan. The primary outcome of this study was to assess compliance with post-extraction instructions, and the secondary outcome was to examine its association with the occurrence of alveolar osteitis.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted among 303 patients who underwent surgical or nonsurgical tooth extraction at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Khyber College of Dentistry, using a non-probability consecutive sampling technique. Ethical approval was obtained, and written informed consent was provided by all participants. Extractions were performed by operators with varying levels of clinical experience, and standardized post-extraction instructions were delivered verbally. Baseline data were collected at the time of extraction, and patients were followed up after one week to assess compliance using a validated ten-item questionnaire (score range 0–10). High compliance was defined as adherence to ≥ 80% of instructions (score ≥ 8). Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables using SPSS v. 25. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with compliance and alveolar osteitis.ResultsAmong the 303 participants, 51.2% were male, and 48.8% were female. A total of 161 patients (53.1%) demonstrated high compliance (score of 8 or above), whereas 142 patients (46.9%) showed partial compliance (score 5–7). Binary logistic regression analysis indicated that higher compliance was associated with a lower likelihood of developing dry sockets after adjusting for demographic and clinical variables (P < 0.05). Factors associated with higher compliance included higher education level, surgical extraction, impacted tooth, and posterior tooth extraction. Employment and self-employed were associated with lower odds of compliance.ConclusionHigher compliance with post-extraction instructions was independently associated with lower odds of alveolar osteitis. Both behavioural and procedural factors were associated with post-extraction outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of structured patient education and adherence monitoring in clinical practice.

Background

Tooth extraction, whether surgical or nonsurgical, is one of the most frequently performed procedures in dental practice [1]. Although generally considered safe, it may be associated with postextraction complications that can affect healing, patient comfort, and overall treatment outcomes [2]. Among these, alveolar osteitis (dry socket) is one of the most clinically significant complications following tooth extraction [3, 4]. It typically develops within 2 to 4 days due to disruption or loss of the blood clot within the extraction socket, resulting in delayed healing and localized inflammation. Clinically, it may present with halitosis, gingival redness, swelling, and exposed bone [5, 6].

The reported incidence of alveolar osteitis varies according to the extraction type and tooth involved. In routine (nonsurgical) extractions, the incidence is generally reported to be between 1% and 5%. Substantially higher rates have been documented following surgical removal of impacted mandibular third molars, with reported incidences ranging from 20% to 30%, and up to 30% to 35% in certain high-risk groups [4, 7, 8]. Increased surgical trauma, dense bone structure, and reduced vascularity relative to the maxilla have been proposed as contributing factors [9].

To minimize post-extraction complications and promote optimal healing, patients are routinely provided with post-extraction instructions [10]. These typically include guidance on oral hygiene, diet modification, avoidance of smoking, restriction of vigorous rinsing, and proper care of the extraction site [11, 12]. Each of these behaviours may influence socket healing through distinct mechanisms. For example, smoking may impair the vascular supply and increase fibrinolysis, whereas vigorous rinsing or inappropriate dietary practices may mechanically disrupt clot stability [13]. Adherence to these instructions is therefore considered important for ensuring clot integrity and supporting uneventful healing [14].

Compliance with post-extraction instructions can be conceptualized as a health-related behavioral construct reflecting the extent to which patients follow professional recommendations after a clinical procedure. It may be influenced by patient-related factors such as education level, health literacy, occupational commitments, and understanding of instructions, as well as system-related factors such as clarity of communication and mode of instruction delivery. Observational studies have reported higher rates of alveolar osteitis among patients with poor adherence, highlighting the clinical relevance of compliance behaviour [8, 15].

However, reported associations between non-compliance and alveolar osteitis remain variable across populations and clinical settings. Differences in extraction complexity, study design, and methods used to measure compliance limit direct comparability. Furthermore, quantitative compliance scoring systems and regression-based analyses evaluating independent associations are limited in the Pakistani context, particularly in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa region. Therefore, this study aimed to assess patient compliance with postextraction instructions using a structured scoring approach and to examine its association with the occurrence of alveolar osteitis following tooth extraction.

Methods

A prospective observational study was conducted among patients who underwent tooth extraction at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Khyber College of Dentistry, from September 2022 to February 2023. The sample size was calculated via OpenEpi software, based on an anticipated compliance prevalence of 73.1% [15], a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, yielding a minimum required sample size of 303 participants. A non-probability consecutive sampling technique was used, in which all eligible patients presenting for tooth extraction during the study period were invited to participate until the required sample size was reached. Ethical approval was obtained from the IPH&SS Ethics Committee, Khyber Medical University (No.KMU/IPHSS/Ethics/2022/PC/034), and the Research Review Board of Khyber College of Dentistry (No. 8152/RRB/KCD). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

The study included all adult males and females aged ≥ 18 years who underwent surgical or nonsurgical extraction of a permanent tooth. Patients who were uncooperative, mentally disabled, or unable to understand post-extraction instructions were excluded. Extractions were performed by operators with varying levels of clinical experience (consultants, residents, trained medical officers, and final year students). Operator status and extraction type were recorded and analysed as independent variables to assess their associations with compliance with postextraction instructions. At the end of each procedure, all patients received standardized verbal post-extraction instructions in the local language delivered by the operating clinician. This approach ensured consistency in instructional content across different operators, although minor variation in delivery style may have occurred. A structured ten-item guideline based on established oral and maxillofacial surgery protocols and published clinical recommendations was developed [16]. Minor variations in verbal delivery were possible; however, the standardized format ensured consistency across operators. The instructions were as follows: [1] keep pressing the folded gauze for 30 min after extraction. Do NOT spit [2]. Avoid rinsing with any liquid for the first 24 h [3]. Take a soft, semiliquid diet at a low temperature [4]. Ice wrapped in a cloth on the outside of the face where the extraction has been performed should be applied [5]. Maintain proper oral hygiene (brushing the surgical area very gently) [6]. Smoking should be avoided during the postoperative period (7 days after surgery), and do not consume alcoholic/soft drinks during the week after surgery [7]. Do not take carbonated drinks [8]. Use antibiotics according to the recommendations of the surgeon (prescribed only when clinically indicated on the basis of extraction type and postoperative condition) [9]. Analgesics, anti-inflammatories should be taken according to the recommendations of the surgeon (paying attention to the prescription of each medication and observing the times indicated) [10]. Perform rinses with 0.12% Chlorhexidine for 24 h (3 times each day / 10 days) starting the day after surgery. Antibiotics and chlorhexidine mouthwash were prescribed selectively on the basis of clinical indications (e.g., surgical extraction severity or postextraction conditions) and were not routinely indicated for all patients. Therefore, these items were clinically conditional rather than universally applicable. Delivery of standardized instructions minimized inter-operator variability.

The participants were followed up on one week after extraction. Patients were invited for in-person assessment; those unable to attend were interviewed via telephone using the same structured questionnaire. Conducting follow-up within one week minimized recall bias.

Compliance was assessed using a validated ten-item questionnaire adopted from Aloy-Prosper et al. [16]. Each item was scored as 1 for compliance and 0 for non-compliance, with a total score ranging from 0 to 10. Compliance was categorized based on the percentage of instructions adhered to: scores ≥ 8 (≥ 80%) were classified as highly compliance, scores ranging from 5 to 7 as partial compliance, and scores < 5 as poor compliance. The ≥ 80% threshold was selected in accordance with commonly applied standards in behavioural adherence research. Although antibiotic and chlorhexidine instructions were prescribed selectively, the 80% cut-off was retained to maintain comparability with the original validated instrument. Interpretation of medication related adherence was therefore undertaken with caution.

Alveolar osteitis was diagnosed clinically during in-person follow-up based on the onset of postoperative pain within 2–4 days, partial or complete loss of the blood clots and exposed bone within the extraction socket. The clinician assessing for alveolar osteitis was blinded to the compliance scores.

Data were analysed via SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were reported for all variables; means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with compliance and alveolar osteitis. Variables identified as significant in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders. Two separate regression models were constructed, with compliance and alveolar osteitis as the respective dependent variables.

The independent variables included age, sex, education level, occupation, smoking status, extraction technique (surgical and nonsurgical), operator status, impacted tooth, medical history, and type of tooth extracted. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 303 participants were included in the study. The mean age was 37.8 ± 12.8 years; 51.2% were male, and 48.8% were female. Most participants had nonformal education (39.9%), were non-smokers (98%), and reported no medical history (84.2%). The majority of extractions involved posterior teeth (86.1%), and 89.1% were surgical extractions. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean compliance score was 7.71 ± 0.8 (maximum possible score = 10). Based on predefined cut-offs, 53.1% of participants were categorized as highly compliant (score ≥ 8), whereas 46.9% were categorized as partially compliant (score 5–7). No participant scored below 5 (Table 2). Instruction-specific compliance assessment is summarized in Table 3. The highest compliance was observed for maintaining gauze pressure for 30 min, avoiding immediate rinsing, consuming a soft diet, and maintaining oral hygiene.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of high compliance (≥ 8 points). In univariate analysis, higher education level, surgical extraction, posterior tooth extraction, impacted tooth, and operator level (consultant) were significantly associated with higher compliance (p < 0.05). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, after adjustment for age, sex, education level, occupation, extraction type, tooth type, and operator level, the following associations remained statistically significant: graduate-level education or above was associated with higher odds of compliance (OR: 3.2, 95% CI: 1.5–6.7, p-value = 0.002), surgical extraction was associated with higher odds of compliance (OR: 6.2, 95% CI: 1.7–23.2, p-value = 0.007), and posterior tooth extraction was also associated with higher compliance (OR: 2.2, 95% CI: 1.007-4.7, p-value = 0.04). Employment and self-employment were associated with lower odds of compliance. Impacted tooth extraction and operator level did not remain statistically significant after multivariate adjustment (Table 5).

Discussion

This prospective observational study assessed patient compliance with post-extraction instructions and examined its association with alveolar osteitis among 303 patients. More than half of the participants demonstrated high compliance, with a mean score of 7.71 ± 0.8. In multivariate analysis, higher compliance was significantly associated with lower odds of alveolar osteitis. The overall incidence of alveolar osteitis was 5.6%, which lies within the lower range reported in previous studies involving mixed extraction types.

Compliance varied across instruction types. High adherence was observed for gauze compression, soft diet intake, avoidance of rinsing, and maintenance of oral hygiene, whereas lower adherence was observed for chlorhexidine mouthwash use, antibiotic intake, and ice application. Notably, antibiotics and ice application were prescribed based on clinical indications rather than routinely to all patients. Lower adherence to these items should therefore be interpreted in context of selective prescription rather than behavioural non-compliance. Future research should distinguish between universally applicable behavioural instructions and condition-specific medical prescriptions when assessing compliance.

Operator status was associated with compliance in univariate analysis but did not remain statistically significant after multivariate adjustment. This suggests that the observed association may have been attributable to confounding factors related to patient or procedural characteristics, highlighting the importance of adjusted analyses when interpreting provider-related variable.

Education level was independently associated with higher compliance, consistent with the previous literature suggesting that health literacy and comprehension may influence postoperative behaviour. However, education may also reflect broader socioeconomic factors, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. In contrast, age and sex were not independently associated with compliance in multivariate analysis. Therefore, interpretations regarding demographic influences should be made cautiously and restricted to adjusted findings.

Previous studies have emphasized the role of comprehension and clarity of instructions in promoting adherence. Blinder et al. highlighted the importance of reading comprehension, although no direct association with education level was observed [2]. Similarly, studies from Saudi Arabia and Ethiopia have reported associations between understanding of post-extraction instructions and postoperative complications, underscoring the importance of effective communication strategies [17, 18].

The mean compliance score observed in this study (7.71 ± 0.8) is comparable to that reported by Aloy-Prosper et al., who documented a slightly higher score (7.9 ± 2.1) when written instructions were provided [19]. Although this may suggest a potential influence of instruction delivery mode, differences in study design, populations and healthcare settings limit direct comparison. Notably, compliance with mouthwash use in the present study (28.7%) was substantially lower than the 98% reported by Gonzalez et al. [11]. Such variations may reflect differences in prescribing practices, communication methods, or measurement approaches rather than behavioural differences alone. Consistent with studies from Nigeria [20] and Malaysia [10], adherence was highest for instructions emphasized at the time of discharge and lower for adjunctive measures, suggesting that perceived importance and timing of instruction delivery may influence adherence.

Employment status was independently associated with compliance, with employed and self-employed participants demonstrating lower odds of high compliance compared with unemployed or retired individuals. This may reflect time constraints or occupational commitments; however, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and further research is required to explore the underlying behavioural and contextual factors. Surgical extraction was independently associated with both higher compliance and higher odds of alveolar osteitis. This may reflect increased procedural complexity and postoperative symptom burden, which could influence patient behaviour. However, given the observational design of present study, these relationships should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

The incidence of alveolar osteitis in present study (5.6%) was lower than that reported in some regional studies, including Karachi (19.7%) [21] and India (14.1%) [1]. However, comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, as differences in case definitions, diagnostic criteria, follow-up duration, extraction complexity, and study design may substantially influence reported incidence rates. Therefore, differences in incidence should not be attributed to contextual factors without direct comparative evidence.

In multivariate analysis, surgical extraction was independently associated with alveolar osteitis, consistent with existing literature identifying procedural complexity as a key determinant. Although smoking and medical history were examined, neither was independently associated with alveolar osteitis after adjustment. Importantly, higher compliance was associated with lower odds of alveolar osteitis after controlling for demographic and procedural variables. These findings suggest that adherence to post-extraction instructions may be associated with improved healing outcomes; however, causality cannot be established within a prospective observational framework.

While previous research has reported as association between non-compliance and post-extraction complications, the present study contributes additional insight by providing instruction-specific compliance using a structured quantitative scoring system. It further differentiates between universally applicable behavioral instructions and selectively prescribed clinical measures, offering a more nuanced understanding of adherence patterns. Additionally, the study provides region-specific evidence from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where such data are limited, thereby contributing to context-relevant understanding of postoperative care practices.

A key strength of this study is its relatively large sample size and prospective assessment of compliance using a validated questionnaire, allowing systematic evaluation of adherence behaviour. The standardized delivery of verbal instructions reflects routine clinical practice in low and middle-income settings, enhancing the contextual relevance. Multivariate regression modelling further strengthened the analysis by adjusting for potential confounders.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of nonprobability consecutive sampling within a single tertiary care centre may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Compliance data were partially collected through telephone for participants who did not attend the in-person follow-up. Although a standardized questionnaire was used, variations in communication style and patient understanding may have influenced responses and introduced reporting bias. Follow-up within one week was intended to minimize recall bias. Additionally, residual confounding from unmeasured behavioural, cultural or socioeconomic variables may have influenced the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, this study provides quantitative evidence on compliance behaviour and its association with alveolar osteitis in a regional context where data are limited. The findings highlight that both behavioural and procedural factors are associated with postoperative outcomes, underscoring the importance of structured instruction delivery and context-sensitive strategies to improve adherence. For future research and clinical practice, the provision of printed or handwritten instructions in the local language is recommended to further enhance compliance.

Conclusion

The current study concluded that higher compliance with post-extraction instructions was independently associated with lower odds of alveolar osteitis after adjustment for demographic and clinical variables. Surgical extraction was also independently associated with higher odds of alveolar osteitis, supporting the multifactorial nature of post-extraction complications. Instruction-specific analysis showed higher adherence to universally applicable behaviors such as soft diet, gauze compression, and avoidance of rinsing, whereas lower adherence was observed for prescription-dependent measures, including antibiotics, chlorohexidine mouthwash, and ice application, which were selectively prescribed based on clinical indication. Given the observational design and single-centre setting of this study, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal relationships. Further research is needed to better understand the determinants of adherence and to evaluate strategies aimed at improving post-extraction outcomes.
