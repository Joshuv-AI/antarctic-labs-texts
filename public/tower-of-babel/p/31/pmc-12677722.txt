Spontaneous Mirror‐Symmetry Breaking Destabilizes Racemates: A Route to Homochirality and Reversed Chemical Selectivity
Authors: Josep M Ribó, Jean‐Claude Micheau, Thomas Buhse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral models and experiments yielding to spontaneous mirror‐symmetry breaking (SMSB) have been revisited. It is concluded that all show first‐order autocatalytic dynamics for the emergence of an enantiomeric excesses (ee). However, first‐order autocatalysis is by itself unable to lead to SMSB. Therefore, the emergence of scalemic nonequilibrium stationary state (NESS) is due to racemate destabilization through the coupling of autocatalysis with other enantioselective reactions. In contrast with the nonlinear effects and kinetic control in asymmetric synthesis SMSB appears as the signature of a bifurcation scenario leading to scalemic NESSs. Stoichiometric network analysis points to the potential interest of these autocatalytic networks to interpret the changes in the chemical selectivity between nonenantiomeric competitive replicators in far‐from‐equilibrium conditions.Keywords: entropy production, far‐from‐equilibrium thermodynamics, spontaneous mirror‐symmetry breaking reaction networks, systems chemistry

Introduction

In chemical systems, spontaneous mirror‐symmetry breaking (SMSB) gives rise to an enantiopure or enantioenriched state from achiral or racemic starting conditions. SMSB occurs without any asymmetric influences. Such phenomena are encountered in the Soai reaction,[ 1 , 2 ] Viedma deracemizations[ 3 , 4 ] and also in few other lesser‐studied cases.[ 5 , 6 ] These systems evolve toward nonequilibrium stationary states (NESSs) that are not racemic but instead scalemic. SMSB proceeds in a stochastic distribution of final chiral signs across repeated experiments. This stochasticity arises from unavoidable initial chiral fluctuations present in any real chemical system. Moreover, cryptochiral inductions can deterministically select the final chiral sign, overriding stochastic outcomes.[ 7 , 8 , 9 ]

To identify the key processes underlying these outstanding phenomena, a major modelling effort has been undertaken. Numerical integration of the ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of reaction rate set appears to be the most appropriate to describe the evolution toward NESS. ODE modelling (e.g., refs. [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]) confirms that SMSB occurs exclusively within autocatalytic reaction networks. When the system is far enough from equilibrium, the initially stable racemic state becomes unstable to chiral fluctuations. The final NESS is not racemic and, in many cases, is indistinguishable from homochirality. SMSB emerges beyond a critical point as the hallmark of a bifurcation phenomenon[ 16 , 17 ] and appears as an energy dissipative state[ 18 ] as those of bistability and oscillatory reactions.[ 19 ] This transition is governed by the time‐irreversibility of macroscopic chemical currents, driven by reaction affinities in far‐from‐equilibrium conditions as denoted by entropy production and entropy flows from the surroundings. Assuming thermal and hydrodynamic stability, the stability of a NESS is ultimately dictated by the kinetic stability of the underlying chemical reaction network.[ 16 , 17 , 18 , 20 ]

The purpose of the present article is to unveil the reasons of SMSB and to show its similarities with the enhanced chemical selectivity occurring between nonenantiomeric autocatalysis. It is divided into three main sections. Section 2 analyzes the relationships between SMSB and autocatalytic order through several examples. The conditions for NESS stability and instability are examined in Section 3, while Section 4 shows that a reversed chemical selectivity could be expected from a competition between nonenantiomeric autocatalysis. The conclusion summarizes the various concepts and their properties, stressing the need to make molecular chemists more familiar to them.

SMSB and Autocatalytic Kinetic Order

In autocatalysis, a chemical compound catalyzes directly or indirectly its own formation, giving rise to accelerating kinetics. In many cases, the kinetic pattern of this acceleration can be expressed in a mathematical form so that an autocatalytic kinetic order can be estimated. Plasson et al.[ 21 ] proposed a mathematical framework to describe the evolution dynamics of an autocatalytic species (X i) in terms of its concentration [X i], leading to the general kinetic expression

where { X } represents the contribution of all other species in the reaction network and k and k a (both ≥0) are the rate constants (k a ≫ k). The exponent n defines the kinetic order of the autocatalysis. The system's dynamic evolution strongly depends on the value of n. The case where n = 1 (first‐order autocatalysis) can be easily recognized by plotting log [X i] versus t. This is the only case where the plot is linear. All other cases, either n < 1 or n > 1 give rise to convex or concave profiles, respectively. This result is very useful because it allows us to demonstrate that all the SMSB under scrutiny (either experimental or models) include a first‐order autocatalytic process. For instance, from the seminal Frank model,[ 22 ] where the autocatalytic rate constant is k a, it can be easily demonstrated that ([L] − [D]) scales with exp(k a t), i.e., that log is linear versus t.[ 23 ] The Plasson's approach confirms the first‐order autocatalysis. However, using this model or further Frank‐like models (see Section 2.1), it can be easily demonstrated that without the enantiomeric inhibition (D + L ⇌ P), SMSB disappears, showing that first‐order autocatalysis by itself is insufficient to induce SMSB. Without enantiomeric inhibition, a higher autocatalytic order (for instance, n = 2) has been shown to yield SMSB.[ 24 ] In fact, considering this generic autocatalytic step

Even though all the systems under scrutiny here (either experimental or models, see below) include a first‐order autocatalytic process as indicated by a linear semilog plot of ee or racemic bias versus time (n = 1), they give rise to an SMSB. This is a paradoxical result because everything is happening as if n was higher than 1.5 as it is strictly at 1. The solution lies in the involvement of enantioselective reaction couplings (mutual inhibition and other reactions) that can increase the system's autocatalytic nonlinearity. In the following sections, we analyze several examples of SMSB reaction networks to determine what kind of reaction couplings are at work.

SMSB in Frank‐Like Systems

Subsequent to Frank's pioneering work, various other comparable mathematical models, the so‐called Frank‐like models, have been proposed to be related to the origin of biological homochirality.[ 11 , 12 ] Frank‐like systems are implemented in a continuous open‐flow reactor, with the influx of achiral resources A and B and the outflux of an equal volume of the reactor solution (Figure 1 ). Starting from a reactor full of achiral A and B and for conditions leading to SMSB, the system exhibits a time dependence of ee and of the racemic bias consistent with first‐order autocatalysis (Figure 1, left column). Additionally, the production of chiral matter ([D] + [L]) (right column) follows an autocatalytic process but with distinct dynamic behavior throughout its evolution (Figure 2 ).

SMSB in Soai Reaction and in Soai Reaction Models

The alkylation of pyrimidinyl aldehydes with diisopropyl zinc is known as the Soai reaction. It represents a unique laboratory demonstration of enantioselective autocatalysis able to yield SMSB in a homogeneous reaction system. It has been shown that the produced chiral pyrimidine alkanol accelerates its own formation and promotes the prevalence of its own configuration (Scheme 1 ).[ 26 ]

Principle of the enantioselective autocatalysis of the Soai reaction.

Soai reaction likely forms a Frank‐like system[ 27 , 28 ] because it is composed of a complex network showing chiral oligomeric intermediates that play the role of autocatalysts and additional heterochiral coupling reactions between homochiral and heterochiral oligomers that act as enantiomeric inhibition. While there are very few accurate kinetic records of the ee evolution during the Soai reaction, we have considered the results of two independent research groups.[ 29 , 30 ] Although the experiments are run in closed systems, and not in open‐flow reactor conditions and even though the data are limited and rather noisy, Figure 3 (left) shows clearly several linear periods, thus confirming the presence of first‐order autocatalysis within the Soai reaction experiments. Furthermore, a chemically realistic ODE model,[ 31 ] integrating X‐ray diffraction structural data and density functional theory calculations results, also shows first‐order autocatalysis for the Soai reaction (Figure 3, right).

SMSB in a chiral hypercycle where two replicators are competing for the same achiral resource (A) (systems parameters of Figure 1 of ref. [21]). Racemic bias and ee follow a first‐order autocatalysis, while the total chiral matter shows autocatalytic stages of different order.

SMSB in the Limited Enantioselective Autocatalysis (LES) Model

Frank systems are associated with enantiospecificity of autocatalytic formation of enantiomers. Such a feature is more typical of biological rather than of chemical processes. Therefore, Avetisov and Goldanskii[ 32 ] proposed a new scheme. There, each enantiomer leads to the formation of both L and D and racemization arises from the reversibility of these catalytic stages. In other words, the Frank heterochiral inhibition step (L + D ⇌ LD) is replaced by a simple competition between nonenantioselective autocatalysis A + D (L) ⇌ D + L and homochiral autocatalysis, A + D (L) ⇌ 2 D (2 L). Such system has been coined as “limited enantioselectivity model” (LES model). However, it has been shown that the system can only lead to SMSB when both autocatalysis shows different equilibrium constant values.[ 33 , 34 ] Such a situation is only possible in a two‐compartment system at very different temperatures and with equilibrium constants showing adequate T dependence.[ 35 ] This could fit to an origin of life scenario in deep ocean hydrothermal plumes. Revisiting the results for this singular scenario of ref. [35], we also found that the autocatalytic growths of chiral matter are both of first order (not shown here).

SMSB in Hypercycle Models

Hypercycle model[ 36 ] was originally proposed to explain evolutionary trends and describe a network of cross‐catalyzing replicators. More recently, it has been demonstrated that chiral hypercycle models can also lead to SMSB.[ 37 , 38 ] This obviously suggests a connection between the possible scenarios of the emergence of self‐reproducing systems with those of biological homochirality. Nonexplicit autocatalysis as that of hypercycle SMSB is reminiscent to what is going on in living systems’ cellular metabolism where redox and energy couplings give rise to autocatalytic networks.[ 39 , 40 ]

Previously,[ 37 ] we hypothesized that the coupling between cross‐catalysis and replicators should increase the autocatalysis kinetic order of replicators, reaching the critical threshold necessary for SMSB bifurcation. However, upon re‐examining our results, we must refute this proposal. Figure 3 illustrates a simple case where two chiral replicators, competing for the same achiral resource (A), exhibit both auto‐ and mutual catalysis. It obeys to first‐order autocatalysis.

Simulations on more complex hypercycles, such as those of refs. [37, 38], lead to the same conclusion: the SMSB autocatalytic order remains strictly first order (n = 1).

SMSB in Viedma Deracemization and Viedma‐Like Models

Viedma deracemization[ 3 ] (also referred to as Viedma ripening) is a process in which racemizing conglomerate crystals[ 14 ] are subjected to vigorous mechanical grinding or to a temperature cycling or gradient. Under these conditions, the two populations of left‐handed and right‐handed crystals cannot coexist in the same suspension: one of the populations disappears to the benefit of the other. The formation of only one enantiomeric form, which is random for (+) or (−) crystals in each individual experiment, seems to be an inescapable outcome in the Viedma deracemization. The process clearly exhibits an autocatalytic character, as it is supported by the exponential evolution of the ee observed in several experiments (Figure 4 ).[ 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 ] To explain this behavior and to establish a model, it has been assumed that the Viedma deracemization of racemic conglomerate crystals occurs through the establishment of a cyclic stationary state between crystal growth and crystal attrition. This process occurs under far‐from‐equilibrium conditions, i.e., in an open system subjected to mechanical energy and/or to a thermal flux. It leads to a final NESS where external energy is specifically delivered to relatively larger crystals, changing their solubility.[ 46 , 47 ]

Both chiral mass production and enantiomeric excess (ee) also follow first‐order autocatalysis.

Ancillary Reaction Networks

Since such complementary reaction networks assist a first‐order enantioselective autocatalysis to yield SMSB, they can be called “ancillary reaction networks.” These reactions can be classified into two types: those that are only a part of the internal reaction network and those that involve matter or energy exchange with the surroundings. The boundary conditions for matter and energy exchange not only take the system far from equilibrium but can be coupled to the internal autocatalytic network. External energy exchange is considered through specific energy‐driven internal reactions. Inflows and outflows of matter and energy must be included through first‐order pseudoreactions into the ODE set. For SMSB to occur, specific couplings between external interactions and internal reactions and far enough from equilibrium conditions are required.[ 49 ]

The simplest ancillary reaction network can be found in the seminal Frank model.[ 22 ] This is the mutual inhibition step (L + D ⇌ LD). The matter loss by this elimination, is replenished through the production of the L and D enantiomers in the course of the autocatalytic steps.[ 12 , 28 , 50 ] However, the amplification of ee through mutual inhibition[ 51 ] competes with the racemization. Therefore, SMSB occurs within a specific parameter range.

The two features of the Frank system essential for the emergence of SMSB (i.e., enantioselective autocatalysis and mutual inhibition) can be also identified in the Soai reaction (see Section 2.1). In this particular case, they are provided by the involvement of oligomeric intermediates of different catalytic activity.[ 26 ]

In contrast, there is neither a clear‐cut explicit enantioselective autocatalysis nor a mutual inhibition step in Viedma deracemization models. Enantioselective autocatalysis is buried in the coupling between aggregation and polymerization where the same oligomer arises from two parallel paths: the enantioselective growth from the achiral monomer and the homochiral cluster‐to‐cluster growth, e.g., reactions (3) and (4).[ 46 , 47 , 48 ] When these processes are coupled to the irreversible grinding of the bigger clusters (or even to thermal cycling), some internal net flows are occurring at the energy dissipative NESS's dominating racemization and thus allowing SMSB.

In chiral hypercycles, two chiral replicators compete for the same achiral resource and exhibit both auto‐ and cross catalysis. Here, the role of the cross catalysis as ancillary reactions is not obvious to the naked eye, but it lays in the cooperative enantioselectivity between different replicators. However, as shown in Section 2.5, the cooperative effect does not lead to an increase of the autocatalysis order but to the destabilization of the racemic NESS.

Conditions for NESS Stability or Instability

According to the well‐established framework of nonequilibrium thermodynamics (nonlinear regime) under the local equilibrium assumption, or local detailed balance,[ 16 , 17 , 20 ] the stability of a stationary state is defined by its response to fluctuations, which are unavoidable in real systems.[ 19 , 52 , 53 ] Kinetic stability, and therefore chemical stability, can be analyzed using the Jacobian matrix (the matrix of the derivative of the rates vs. the species), which describes how small disturbances evolve over time. A NESS is stable if all eigenvalues (λω j ) of the Jacobian have negative real parts

This ensures that small perturbations decay over time, returning the system to its original state. If any λω j > 0, the system is unstable, and perturbations grow, leading to a transition to a new stable state.[ 15 ]

Mathematical packages, such as Mathematica or new versions of the open COPASI package, can perform the numerical integration of ODE beyond the computational numerical noise. The simulation of a statistical fluctuation on the racemate can be done by applying an initial ee value below than that expected for the statistical bias[ 28 ] of an ideal racemic composition (Δee (%) < 67.43 (N −0.5), being N the number of chiral molecules.[ 54 , 55 , 56 ] In the case of stability, the effect of different initial perturbations leads to the same final NESS, and in the case of instability/bifurcation, a new NESS is reached and there is a correlation between the chiral sign of the initial fluctuation and the sign of the final racemic bias.

As it can be seen in Section 3.2, a stoichiometric network analysis (SNA) can help to identify all the reactions and the irreversible pseudoreactions arising from the energy and/or matter exchange with the surroundings. These unidirectional flux modes drive the SMSB.

SNA and Elementary Fundamental Modes

SNA is a vectorial representation of reaction networks arising from the multiplication of the stoichiometric matrix of their elemental reactions by the corresponding rate vector matrix.[ 61 , 62 , 63 ] SNA provides a geometrical perspective of the reaction networks, where the linear combination of the irreducible vectors called extreme currents of elementary fundamental modes (EFMs) yields all the possible stationary states of the system. Without considering rate and equilibrium constant values, EFMs show also in which proportion any elementary reaction participates in the system network, leading or not to unidirectional flows (see Figure 8 , right).

An overview on SNA is given, e.g., in ref. [61], and the specific application for systems able to yield SMSB was first reported in ref. [25]. Using specific values for the rate constants, NESSs correspond to points in the cone, and their stability can be analyzed by the Jacobian. In summary, SNA indicates which are the fundamental flows that may compete with the racemization and contribute to the destabilization of NESS, leading to the emergence of SMSB bifurcation.

Based on SNA, the emergence of SMSB has been previously reported for Frank‐like systems,[ 49 ] chiral hypercycles,[ 38 ] high‐order enantioselective autocatalysis,[ 25 , 49 ] limited enantioselectivity autocatalysis,[ 49 ] Schlögl bistability,[ 60 ] and recently Viedma‐like systems of oligomer self‐assembly.[ 48 ] In all these systems, the general conclusion of SNA is that the relevant EFMs leading to SMSB are composed by the coupling between autocatalytic steps, ancillary reactions, and energy and/or matter exchange with the surroundings. Previously reported examples on these significant EFMs are shown in Figure 8 for a Frank‐like system in a continuous open‐flow reactor[ 49 ] (example of Section 2.1) and a Viedma‐like system in the self‐assembly of small aggregates[ 48 ] (example of Figure 7). Since the system is unstable, a tiny chiral bias is sufficient for a wing to overtake the other and to grow at its expense; hence, SMSB is occurring.

Chemical Selectivity between Nonenantiomeric Autocatalysis

In SNA, the rate and equilibrium constants and the nature of the chemical species are not specified; all is based on the structure of the kinetic network. Therefore, the reaction networks yielding SMSB would also apply independently if the chemical species are chiral or achiral, isomers or not. For instance, simulations of systems analogous to those yielding SMSB but constituted by two nonenantiomeric autocatalysis yielding isomeric compounds X and Y are expected to show similar scenarios for the same parameter ranges than in the case of SMSB. Here, in Figure 9 , we show the competition between two nonenantiomeric autocatalytic networks as an example derived from the Frank model in an open‐flow continuous reactor (Figure 1 in Section 2.1). Replicator X is slower than Y (k fX = 10−5 < k fY = 3 × 10−5), while it shows a higher equilibrium constant (K X = k fX /k bX = 1 × 10−5/1 × 10−10 = 1 × 105 > K Y = k fY /k bY = 3 × 10−5/3 × 10−8 = 1 × 103). Such parameters can be understood as a competition between the fittest X (K X > K Y ) and the faster Y (k fX < k fY ).

Concluding Remarks

Both SMSB and asymmetric induction give rise to an ee evolution. Here, we stress their fundamental differences. 1) SMSB is not a simple enantiomeric excess amplification. The concept of ee amplification arises from the asymmetric synthesis domain and refers to a nonlinear dependence between the catalytic initial ee and the product final ee values.[ 65 , 66 , 67 ] This effect occurs because the initially added catalytic scalemic compound forms several homo‐ and hetero‐oligomers exhibiting different enantioselective catalytic properties.[ 66 ] The final ee depends on the initial one in a nonlinear manner. On the contrary, in the case of SMSB, the same final NESS, i.e., the same |ee|value is always obtained without need of chiral initial inductor, it is just the chiral sign that is determined by an initial fluctuation. SMSB belongs to the framework of coupled reaction networks, in systems under selective energy input, where there is nonlinearity between the chemical forces and their chemical currents. 2) In open systems, NESSs remain stable as long as the far‐from‐equilibrium conditions are maintained. This means that from the point of view of synthetic chemistry, they are under “thermodynamic control.” In contrast, asymmetric synthesis achieves enantioselectivity under “kinetic control” because it is governed by the differences in free energy between reagents and diastereoisomeric transition states. 3) SMSB systems can lead either to a racemic NESS or to a scalemic NESS for the same reaction parameters just by changing the boundary conditions. Since the system dynamics and final states can be quite different for the same reaction mechanism, structural features alone or reaction‐coordinate approaches are insufficient to describe such irreversible phenomena.[ 68 ] 4) SMSB does not occur because of stochastic event in a high exergonic chemical reaction. SMSB occurs in the deterministic thermodynamic limit and assuming local equilibrium (local detailed balance).[ 69 , 70 ]

SNA shows the analogy between enantioselective systems able to yield SMSB and those implying competition between nonenantiomeric replicators. Therefore, significant changes are expected in the chemical selectivity between competing replicators when the system is driven far from equilibrium and when the initial conditions are changed.
