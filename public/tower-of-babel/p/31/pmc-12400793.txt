Which Plant Traits Increase Soil Carbon Sequestration? Empirical Evidence From a Long‐Term Poplar Genetic Diversity Trial
Authors: John L. Field, Brandon P. Sloan, Matthew E. Craig, Parker Calloway, Sarah L. Ottinger, Thomas Mead, Rose Z. Abramoff, Mirko Pavicic Venegas, Hari B. Chhetri, Kathy Haiby, Udaya C. Kalluri, Wellington Muchero, Christopher W. Schadt, Melanie A. Mayes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlants play a key role in mediating soil response to global change, and breeding or engineering crops to increase soil organic carbon (SOC) storage is a potential route to land‐based carbon dioxide removal in agricultural systems. However, due to limited observational datasets plus shifting paradigms of SOC stabilization, it is unclear which plant traits are most important for enhancing different types of soil organic matter. Existing long‐term common gardens of genetically diverse plant populations may provide an opportunity to evaluate biological controls on SOC, separate from environmental or management variability. Here we report on soil and root chemical data collected for 24 genotypes within a 13‐year‐old common garden in northwestern Oregon planted with a large natural variant population of Populus trichocarpa . Fractionating surface soil (0–15 cm) revealed substantial variation in stocks of mineral‐associated organic matter (MAOM; 18–67 t C/ha) and particulate organic matter (POM; 2–22 t C/ha). Tree genotype explained 24% and 26% of the MAOM and POM stock variability, respectively, after controlling for background variability. We found minimal association between SOC concentration and either aboveground tree productivity or root biomass recalcitrance (C/N ratios and lignin content). In contrast, root elemental content appeared influential for MAOM‐C concentration, which showed a strong positive association with root aluminum (Al) and a strong negative association with root boron (B) and magnesium (Mg). Furthermore, root concentrations of these elements were highly heritable (57%–78%) and not simply a reflection of background variation in soil elemental concentrations. We estimate that surface SOC stocks under these 24 genotypes have diverged at rates of up to 1.2–4.3 t C/ha/year. These results suggest that long‐term genetic diversity trials have value for elucidating biological controls on soil organic matter dynamics, and that traits associated with root elemental content may be a useful target for enhancing biosequestration.

Introduction

Soils constitute a key global carbon stock underpinning ecosystem health and agricultural system productivity. Soil organic carbon (SOC) response to global change remains persistently uncertain despite improved understanding of other parts of the carbon cycle (Varney et al. 2022). Such predictions are challenging because warming affects both plant productivity (i.e., carbon inputs to the soil) and turnover of existing soil carbon (Bradford et al. 2016), and may have differential effects on different forms of SOC (Lugato et al. 2021). Importantly, these responses to global change are mediated by plant traits. For example, the effects of elevated CO2‐driven increases in plant productivity on SOC can depend on plant traits related to nutrient acquisition (Terrer et al. 2021). Other plant feedbacks to warming or drought that might be important to soil function and carbon storage include changes in fine‐root biomass, chemistry, or root exudation (Wang, Defrenne, et al. 2021; Wang, Chen, et al. 2021; Williams and de Vries 2020). Better understanding of plant trait controls on both SOC formation and turnover is key to improved carbon cycle projections in the context of global change.

Such improved understanding is also important for climate change mitigation. Global decarbonization scenarios project a need for 5–180 gigatons of carbon removal (Gt C) from the atmosphere by the end of the century (IPCC 2022). Historically, human agricultural practices are responsible for 116 Gt C that have been lost from soils to the atmosphere (Sanderman et al. 2017), compared to 500 Gt C of cumulative fossil fuel emissions to date (Friedlingstein et al. 2025). Reversing these losses and restoring agricultural soils towards or beyond their natural SOC storage levels is a carbon removal opportunity with substantial agricultural and environmental co‐benefits (Bossio et al. 2020). The universal adoption of agricultural conservation management practices such as tillage intensity reduction, cover‐cropping, and conversion of degraded agricultural land to perennial crops has the potential to remove 1–3 Gt C/year from the atmosphere globally (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2019; Paustian, Larson, et al. 2019), while other “frontier” methods (i.e., methods that have not yet been applied at scale due to technical or economic barriers) could plausibly double this removal potential (Paustian, Larson, et al. 2019).

One such frontier method is to breed or engineer agricultural crops (e.g., annual food crops, pasture grasses, or biomass crops) for traits that would lead to enhanced “biosequestration” of SOC (U.S. DOE 2008) beyond what is possible with current commercial crop varieties and best management practices (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2019; Paustian, Larson, et al. 2019), an approach sometimes termed “carbon farming” (Jansson et al. 2021). There is particular research interest in optimizing biosequestration potential under dedicated perennial energy crops (Jansson et al. 2010), which are projected to play a large role in future decarbonization plans. Various plant trait targets have been proposed for enhancing SOC biosequestration, including increased belowground carbon allocation, more chemically recalcitrant root biomass, root architecture or exudation supporting greater soil microaggregate formation, and root exudate chemistries that enhance specific SOC‐promoting microbial associations (Bardgett et al. 2014; Fierer and Walsh 2023; Jansson et al. 2010, 2021; Kalluri et al. 2020; Poirier et al. 2018; C. Wang and Kuzyakov 2023; Yang et al. 2021). However, there are few studies quantifying the potential impact of these traits beyond back of the envelope calculations and some exploratory modeling work (e.g., Cotrufo et al. 2024; Paustian et al. 2016).

There have recently been several major paradigm shifts around soil organic matter (SOM) dynamics and the plant traits most likely to influence them. First, while much past research has focused on SOM formation from aboveground litter, newer work suggests that roots and other belowground inputs are stabilized into SOM with much higher efficiency and thus play an outsized but understudied role in increasing soil carbon storage (Berhongaray et al. 2019; Sokol and Bradford 2019). Such belowground inputs are particularly important for bioenergy crops since aboveground biomass is periodically harvested and exported from the system. Second, while SOM was previously thought to be composed of chemically recalcitrant plant tissues and microbial by‐products inherently resistant to further decomposition, it is now known that stable organic matter in mineral soils mostly consists of simple compounds that have been spared from further microbial processing due to physical separation, chemical protection, or functional complexity in microbe–substrate interactions (Lehmann et al. 2020; Lehmann and Kleber 2015; Schmidt et al. 2011). The classification of different organic matter pools has also increased in importance. Particulate organic matter (POM) is made up of partially decomposed plant tissues that can be physically protected from soil microbes in microaggregates or soil pore structures (Lavallee et al. 2020), whereas mineral‐associated organic matter (MAOM) consists of microbial necromass or simple plant‐derived compounds adsorbed to clay minerals (Cotrufo et al. 2022; Rowley et al. 2018; Sokol et al. 2019) or adsorbed to and co‐precipitated with iron, manganese, and aluminum (oxyhydr)oxides (Rasmussen et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2016). Finally, in addition to being a biomass source for SOM formation, plants are also known to affect the decay of existing SOM via priming (Dijkstra et al. 2021; Poirier et al. 2018). In particular, low molecular weight compounds exuded by plant roots can exchange with clay‐surface‐adsorbed compounds to destabilize existing MAOM or indirectly stimulate greater microbial decomposition of existing organic matter stocks (Jilling et al. 2021; Keiluweit et al. 2015). MAOM is a particularly important target for enhancement since it may prove more resilient to future climate warming than POM (Lugato et al. 2021).

Under the older paradigms, strategies for enhancing biosequestration included selection of plants with more chemically recalcitrant biomass; that is, higher C/N ratios, lignin, suberin, or phytoliths (Harman‐Ware, Sparks, et al. 2021; Parton et al. 1987; Poirier et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2021). In contrast, the newer paradigms imply that the opposite strategy may be more effective; that is, that higher‐quality biomass can be processed more efficiently by microbes and result in greater levels of microbial biomass, which in turn then leads to more durable MAOM storage (Cotrufo et al. 2013; Poirier et al. 2018). The latter approach is supported by both short‐term soil incubations (Ridgeway et al. 2022) and plant microcosm studies (Rossi et al. 2020). MAOM formation might also be increased through selecting plants with high rates of root exudation and rhizodeposition (Jansson et al. 2021; Poirier et al. 2018; Wang and Kuzyakov 2023), whereas POM might potentially be increased via fast fine root turnover, recalcitrant biomass chemistry, or high levels of root mucilage, exudation, or mycorrhizal association that promote soil aggregation (Beattie et al. 2024; Fierer and Walsh 2023; Jansson et al. 2021; Poirier et al. 2018; C. Wang and Kuzyakov 2023).

Understanding of SOM drivers has been advanced using data from agricultural or ecological monitoring networks, soil survey databases, and meta‐analyses (Hansen et al. 2024; Heckman et al. 2023; King et al. 2023; Lugato et al. 2021) that are rich in environmental variability, but often contain only limited representation of plant functional traits beyond primary production. In contrast, long‐term (decade or older) common garden experiments—that is, multiple species or varieties grown together at the same site—offer the potential to disentangle the relative importance of myriad plant traits on soil carbon concentrations and stocks without confounding from different environmental or management conditions (Poirier et al. 2018; Waring et al. 2022). Such studies provide mixed evidence of interspecies or intervariety effects on SOC. Long‐term common gardens with different tree species grown in monoclonal stands have demonstrated differences in litter layer carbon stocks (Vesterdal et al. 2008) in one experiment, and differences in soil pH and cation content (Reich et al. 2005) and SOM decomposition potential (Hobbie et al. 2007) but not mineral soil horizon carbon stocks (Mueller et al. 2012) in another experiment. Pregitzer et al. (2013) sampled individual trees from among 5 genotypes of Populus angustifolia planted in three long‐term polyclonal grid‐planted common gardens in Utah, finding a correlation between tree size and SOC concentration, but no consistent genotype effect. Waring et al. (2022) sampled a 9‐year‐old common garden with monoclonal plots of 19 different tree species, finding significant differences in soil bulk density and carbon stocks, the latter positively associated with tree productivity but negatively associated with litter recalcitrance.

In this work, we sampled soil and root biomass from individual trees of 24 different genotypes established in a long‐term (13‐year‐old) polyclonal grid‐planted poplar common garden in Oregon, fractionated the 0–15 cm depth soil samples into MAOM and POM, and performed statistical modeling to quantify broad‐sense heritability and phenotype effects on soil carbon concentrations and stocks. The goals of this study were to retrospectively (a) explore whether substantial differences in SOC are measurable across this site, (b) quantify any associations between recalcitrance‐related plant traits and SOC differences, and (c) screen for evidence of differences in plant traits and SOC levels by tree genotype. This work builds on previous evidence of genotype‐driven differences in soil pH, base cations, and bulk density across this common garden (Craig et al. 2024). The underlying motivations of these investigations are to identify high‐priority trait targets and inform the upper potential for enhanced SOC biosequestration in an important second‐generation bioenergy feedstock crop, and to better understand plant trait controls on soil function relevant to global change feedbacks.

Methods

GWAS Population and Experimental Site

The Center for Bioenergy Innovation, GreenWood Resources Inc., and the University of British Columbia have assembled a diverse research population of Populus trichocarpa (commonly known as black cottonwood) to support genome‐wide association studies (GWAS). This GWAS population includes more than 1100 unique genotypes collected across the western U.S. and British Columbia (Figure S1). P. trichocarpa is a dioecious species (thus an obligate outbreeder) that has not previously been domesticated, so this species and research population feature high genetic and trait diversity. This GWAS population has been replicated in a number of common garden plantings and studies to explore the genetic controls of biomass yield and phenology (Evans et al. 2014), tissue lignin content (Xie et al. 2018), the associated soil microbiome (Schadt et al. 2024), and other favorable properties for bioenergy production (Happs et al. 2021).

One of these GWAS common garden sites was established in April 2009 near Clatskanie, Oregon (46°07′16″ N, 123°16′13″ W; Figure 1a). This region features a warm‐summer Mediterranean climate (Köppen‐Geiger classification “Csb”; Beck et al. 2018), with mean annual precipitation of 1,430 mm and temperature of 10.5 C (Western Regional Climate Center, n.d.). The site is located on diked former floodplains of the Columbia River and experiences warm wet summers and cool dry winters. It is mapped in the Soil Survey Geographic (SSURGO) database (Ernstrom and Lytle 1993) as silt loam from the Wauna and Locoda series, though our own texture measurements (see Methods section 2.3) show silty clay and silty clay loam in the 0–30 cm surface layer (Figure S2). The soils are rated as deep, poorly drained, strongly acidic, and exhibiting redox mottling suggestive of significant fluctuation between anaerobic and aerobic conditions (National Cooperative Soil Survey, USDA 2005). Since the common garden was originally established for evaluation of aboveground phenotypes, no specific site‐level sampling of SOC or other soil characteristics was conducted at the time of establishment.

Common garden location and sampling design. (a) Location of poplar common garden site near Clatskanie, in northwest Oregon. (b) Inset map showing the 69 individual trees sampled in this work, representing 24 different genotypes replicated across three spatial blocks. Grey hatching at eastern and western parts of blocks 1 and 3 denotes areas that were coppiced in 2012.

Prior to the GWAS planting, commercial hybrid poplar ( P. trichocarpa x P. deltoides ) was grown on the site for approximately 20 years, with the most recent rotation from 1995 to 2007. The different GWAS varieties of P. trichocarpa were established in a polyclonal gridded planting, with each genotype replicated across three spatial blocks, and trees completely randomized within each block (Figure 1b). The planting grid has 3 by 3 m row spacing (c. 1100 trees per hectare) for a total plot area of 3.1 ha, with a double row of buffer trees surrounding the plot. In 2012, the aboveground portion of ~1/2 of the easternmost experimental Block 1 and ~1/5 of the westernmost Block 3 were coppiced (Figure 1b) and allowed to regrow, thus all the trees were functionally either 10 or 13 years old at the time of this study. At the time of our initial study design, 449 of the genotypes had all three of their spatial replicate trees still alive.

Soil and Root Sampling

Of the > 1100 P. trichocarpa genotypes originally established in this common garden, we down‐selected 24 genotypes for soil and root sampling based on survival of all three tree replicates and extreme values of foliar chemistry that might affect litter recalcitrance or soil microbial activity. Specifically, we selected for extreme values of ferulic and para‐coumaric acid (Tuskan et al. 2019) which are known to affect soil microbial function (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2007), and the ratio of lignin syringyl to guaiacyl monomer units (Harman‐Ware, Macaya‐Sanz, et al. 2021; and personal communication with A. Harman‐Ware) which is known to affect biomass recalcitrance to biochemical conversion (Alexander et al. 2020). Replicates of two of these selected genotypes had suffered mortality since the prior observations used for study design, so we were able to sample 69 individual live trees, resulting in n = 3 replicates for 22 genotypes and n = 2 and n = 1 for the remaining two genotypes.

Soils and roots were sampled during field campaigns in June and October 2022, with 23 genotypes sampled in June and the remaining genotype in October. While poplar roots can grow deep into the soil profile (Tufekcioglu et al. 1998), here we focus on the 0–15 cm and 15–30 cm surface layers, since that is where the majority of poplar root biomass, SOC stocks, and SOC response to new poplar establishment have previously been shown to manifest (Berhongaray et al. 2019; Martani et al. 2021; Qin et al. 2016). These surface layers are also more practical for sampling larger numbers of trees by hand without hydraulic soil sampling (impractical at this densely planted site) and for tracing and collecting fine root samples from the target trees (essential for capturing the phenotypes of our select genotypes of interest). Two adjacent soil cores were collected approximately 1 m from the stem of each target tree using a 5 cm inner diameter slide hammer soil core sampler and split into increment depths of 0–15 cm and 15–30 cm (Figure 2). The first (“density”) core was used to evaluate soil moisture, texture, and bulk density (BD), and the second (“chemistry”) core for measurement of SOC and other aspects of soil chemistry, as described in the next section. The 0–15 cm chemistry cores were also fractionated for measurement of MAOM‐C and POM‐C. The collection of two separate cores ensured that sufficient soil mass was available for all required analyses and allowed us to oven‐dry a full separate soil core for accurate BD determination. We also collected fine root samples from the 0–15 cm surface layer by tracing roots emerging from the target tree stems, through the root flare, to lateral roots, and then to fine root bunches of diameter less than 2 mm. The collected soils and roots were stored in a cooler on ice packs (–4C) and shipped back to the lab for analysis.

Schematic of poplar trial and soil sampling, illustrating the location of soil core and fine root sampling relative to the rows of poplar trees and the overlap of their root systems, and the types of analysis conducted on each sample. Scales are exaggerated for illustrative purposes (photo by the authors; root clipart from https://www.hiclipart.com/free‐transparent‐background‐png‐clipart‐dvzrp).

Given the relatively tightly‐spaced polyclonal grid planting of this site, we expected substantial root intermingling and enmeshing between neighboring trees, which might obfuscate the soil carbon impacts of any individual tree. To quantify the amount of root intermingling in the zone from which we collected our soil cores, we also conducted exploratory root tracing on fine root bunches excavated randomly approximately 1 m from 43 target trees. We found that 53% of those roots traced back to the target tree, 16% originated from a neighboring tree (in some cases more than one row away), and the remainder were of indeterminate origin (e.g., extended too deep for hand excavation). This confirmed that while there is some overlap of the root systems of these grid‐planted trees, our soil sampling zone (Figure 2) contains a strong plurality of roots from the target tree.

Laboratory Analysis of Soils and Roots

Density core soil samples from both the 0–15 cm and 15–30 cm depth increments were individually evaluated for moisture, gravimetric water content, bulk density (dry soil mass divided by core volume; soil cores from this site were stone‐free), and texture (Bouyoucos hydrometer method; Gee and Or 2004); the remaining sample mass was then air dried and archived. Soils from the chemistry cores (0–15 cm and 15–30 cm) were sieved to 2 mm and stored at 5°C until subsamples were taken and dried for further analysis. Soil pH was measured in a 0.005 M CaCl2 solution mixed 2:1 with soil using a glass electrode (Orion Versastar Advanced Electrochemistry Meter Thermo Scientific). Separate subsamples were air dried, ground, and analyzed for total carbon and nitrogen using an Elementar Unicube Trace CN analyzer (Elementar Corp., Ronkonkoma, NY).

Additional subsamples from the 0–15 cm chemistry cores were separated into MAOM and POM fractions by size (Cambardella and Elliott 1992). We limited our fractionation to the 0–15 cm layer due to the substantial effort involved and for consistency with our fine root sampling. A 5 g subsample of each soil was suspended in sodium hexametaphosphate (5% w/v) and placed on a reciprocal shaker (120 rpm) overnight to disperse aggregates. These solutions were then sieved (53 μm) with the aid of deionized water rinsing into a coarse POM fraction and a MAOM fine fraction. The recovered soil fractions were dried, weighed, ground, and analyzed for carbon and nitrogen in the same manner as the bulk soil subsamples. In several cases, the sum of the carbon recovered in the individual fractions differed from the carbon measured in the bulk samples by more than 30%; so these soils were re‐fractionated. The final fractionation dataset had a median c. 17% under‐estimate of total bulk SOC, likely attributable to MAOM losses during POM fraction rinsing or from particularly C‐rich MAOM residues retained on the glassware after drying (J. Jastrow personal communication). We assume that carbonates were negligible in these acidic soils.

Fine root samples were thoroughly rinsed with DI water, dried, ground, and sent to the University of Georgia Agricultural & Environmental Services Laboratories (AESL) for tissue chemistry analysis. Total aluminum (Al), boron (B), calcium (Ca), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), molybdenum (Mo), sodium (Na), nickel (Ni), phosphorus (P), lead (Pb), sulfur (S), and zinc (Zn) content was evaluated via microwave‐assisted nitric acid digestion and inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry. Levels of root Cd, Mo, and Pb largely fell below instrument detection limits, so they were excluded from further analysis. Cr was also omitted given it was not measured for one genotype. Total carbon and nitrogen (N) were evaluated with an Elementar Vario Max Total Combustion Analyzer. Acid detergent lignin content was evaluated with an Ankom 2000 Fiber Analyzer to determine acid detergent fiber, followed by digestion in 72% sulfuric acid to isolate the lignin fraction. In addition, subsamples of bulk 0–15 cm soils were also sent to AESL for measurement of Mehlich‐1 extractable nutrients (Ca, Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Mo, Na, Ni, P, Pb, Zn), cation exchange capacity (CEC), percent base saturation, and extractable Al. We excluded soil Cd, Cr, Mo, and Pb from further analysis due to the lack of corresponding root concentration data.

Aboveground Trait Data

The Clatskanie common garden has been subject to extensive aboveground phenotyping since establishment, including periodic measurements of diameter at breast height (DBH; Chhetri et al. 2020; Evans et al. 2014; Slavov et al. 2024). We used an allometric equation from Truax et al. (2014) to estimate total aboveground biomass (stem + branch) from DBH (biomass=0.071·DBH2.4055) and calculated the average annual productivity rate (i.e., growth) over 10 years of available observations (2009–2019). Note that for the 16 coppiced trees, we summed the aboveground biomass estimated from the DBH in 2012 (prior to coppicing) and the latest observed year, to estimate the total biomass growth over both coppice cycles.

Statistical Analysis

We used broad‐sense heritability [H2; Equation (1)] to quantify the variance in soil C (including bulk soils and the individual POM and MAOM fractions) and above‐ and below‐ground tree phenotypes attributable to tree genotype. For each phenotype and soil property, we estimated H2 by fitting a linear mixed effects model with genotype as a random effect (Chhetri et al. 2019) and soil texture, coppicing, and experimental block as fixed effects to control for spatial differences in environment and management, then extracted the estimated random effect (σG2) and residual variances (σE2). Additionally, we fit a mixed effects model with only a genotype random effect to check the importance of spatial fixed effects. All mixed‐effects models were fit using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2021). (1) H2=σG2σG2+σE2

Next, we performed regularized regression to explore the effects of site environmental and management factors, soil properties, and tree traits on soil C concentrations in the 0–15 cm surface layer. Regularized regression reduces over‐fitting and highly variable parameter estimates arising from correlated predictors. Specifically, we used the LASSO method in the R package glmnet (Friedman et al. 2010) within the tidymodels framework (Kuhn and Wickham 2020), which also performs feature selection by shrinking less influential parameters to 0. We tuned the regularization parameter using 10‐fold cross‐validation repeated 10 times, then performed 10‐fold cross‐validation 20 times on the tuned model to estimate the sampling distributions of individual model parameters and overall model predictive performance. Using this procedure, we tested three combinations of predictors to include in our model alongside block, soil texture, and coppicing: all root and soil chemistry predictors (“Root + Soil”), root chemistry only (“Root Only”), and soil chemistry only (“Soil Only”). Additional details and regularized regression results are given in Section S3. Our approach provides a reproducible analysis that objectively controls for correlated predictors and overfitting while quantifying the sensitivity of the regression to our data sample.

POM‐C, MAOM‐C, and total bulk SOC in the 0–15 cm layer were all individually analyzed for both broad‐sense heritability and for associations with tree traits using the methods described above. Soil carbon concentration data (mg C per g dry soil as measured by the elemental analyzer) were analyzed directly, and also combined with bulk density measurements to calculate per‐area carbon stocks (t C per ha), which were then analyzed separately. When calculating H2 for 0–15 cm total soil C stocks, we extracted the best‐linear unbiased predictors of genotype level effects and used them to estimate the rate at which the carbon stocks under different genotypes had diverged over the 13 years between common garden establishment and soil sampling.

Analysis code and datasets of tree DBH and coppicing history from 2010 to 2019; fine root C, N, lignin, and elemental content from samples collected in 2022; and soil texture, pH, bulk density, and C & N content (both bulk soil and individual POM & MAOM fractions) from cores collected in 2022 are openly available in Zenodo (Sloan et al. 2025). Genotype‐level results for key soil properties and tree traits are summarized in Supplemental Table S1.

Results

SOC Concentrations and Stocks

The median total SOC concentration measured in the 0–15 cm soil cores of the 69 target trees was 52 mg C/g soil (ranges from 23 to 91 mg C/g soil), while the mineral‐associated organic matter carbon (MAOM‐C) and particulate organic matter carbon (POM‐C) median values were 36 mg C/g soil (18–64 mg C/g soil) and 7 mg C/g soil (2–17 mg C/g soil), respectively (Figure 3). Associated median soil C/N ratios for bulk soil and the MAOM and POM fractions were 14 (9–16), 13 (10–15), and 20 (12–27), respectively. The median total 0–15 cm SOC stock at this site was 68 t/ha, comprised primarily of MAOM‐C (48 t/ha). Mean SOC concentrations and stocks measured for individual genotypes are reported in Table S1, and Figure S3 highlights the relatively strong correlation observed between concentrations and stocks.

SOC and Tree Phenotype Heritability

The broad‐sense heritability analysis (Figure 4) showed no influence of tree genotype on total SOC concentration in the 0–15 cm surface layer, but C concentrations in the individual MAOM and POM fractions showed marginal influence of genotype (H2≈0.1). We found stronger influence of tree genotype on soil bulk density (H2=0.23), and a strong inverse correlation between total SOC concentration and soil bulk density, which ranged from below 0.6 g/cm3 in high‐SOC soils to above 1.0 g/cm3 in low‐SOC soils (Figure S4a). As a result, 0–15 cm MAOM‐C and POM‐C stocks show over two times higher influence of tree genotype (H2=0.24 and H2=0.26, respectively) than their respective concentrations alone.

The broad sense heritability of various plant traits and soil properties across 24 genotypes at the Clatskanie common garden site. Biomass growth rate is the only aboveground property (red). Root properties (green) include the concentrations of various elements, plus lignin content and C/N ratio. Soil properties (blue) include the concentrations of various elements, soil pH, concentrations and stocks of particulate‐ and mineral‐associated organic matter, bulk density, soil C/N ratio, base saturation (“Base Sat”), and cation exchange capacity (CEC). Soil properties are evaluated for the 0–15 cm surface layer unless otherwise noted. The red dashed line indicates 10% of the observed total variability is explained by genotype, a common threshold for evaluating heritable effects.

Both MAOM‐C and POM‐C are more influenced by tree genotype than is total SOC, regardless of whether stocks or concentration units are considered. The broad‐sense heritability of MAOM‐C is more sensitive than POM‐C to background environmental variation (i.e., soil texture, coppicing and spatial block; Figure S5), though this is consistent with longer‐lived MAOM showing greater influence of pre‐GWAS‐establishment conditions than POM (Lavallee et al. 2020). We also collected 15–30 cm soil cores and analyzed for total SOC, but did not fractionate them. Interestingly, combining the data from the 0–15 cm and 15–30 cm samples into a composite 0–30 cm estimate reduced the apparent influence of tree genotype on both total SOC concentration (H2=0.06) and stock (H2=0). This might reflect a compounding of random measurement errors, and real inconsistencies in texture and other properties across the two soil layers (Figure S4b). Soil pH appears moderately influenced by tree genotype (H2≈0.29), although it only varies from 5.2 to 5.6 across samples.

We found that the growth rate of aboveground biomass (genotype means ranging from 2.1–10.4 dry kg/tree/year; Table S1) exhibited high heritability (H2=0.59). Of the two phenotypes we measured as representative of root litter recalcitrance, root C/N ratio (genotype means range from 46 to 92) appears heritable (H2=0.47), but root lignin (genotype means range from 28% to 37%) does not (H2=0). The relatively low variability in root lignin across the 24 genotypes (Figure S5) may contribute to the lack of heritability in this analysis; previous studies have identified a specific genetic locus controlling root lignin in hybrid poplar (Yin et al. 2010). Of the more than dozen root elements evaluated, many show significant variability (Figure S5c) and high heritability (green in Figure 4). Specifically, root Al, Fe, Ca, Mg, and B have more than 50% of their variability explained by genotype after controlling for site variables (H2>0.5). Interestingly, bulk soil P, Na, Mn, and Mg also show high levels of tree genotype influence, and many other soil elements a moderate amount (blue in Figure 4). There is generally little or no correlation between plant and soil element levels (Figure S6), consistent with previous observations at this site (Craig et al. 2024). These results indicate that roots are not simply mirroring the surrounding soil chemistry and might even be selectively depleting certain elements from their surrounding soil.

Drivers of SOC Variability at Clatskanie

We next used regularized regression to quantify the effects of site factors, soil properties, and plant traits on the observed SOC data. We focus here on the MAOM‐C concentration results because: (a) the MAOM‐C concentration model had the best performance (median R2≈0.5; Section S3), (b) MAOM‐C and Total C concentration and stock regressions had similar predictor effects, and (c) POM‐C was poorly explained by the model given its small fraction at the site (Figure 3). Additionally, we focus on the regression results for all root and soil chemistry predictors (“Root + Soil”) and root chemistry predictors only (“Root Only”) as these two models had the best performance and many of the root and soil chemistry variables were heritable (Figure 4). Results from the other regression models are available in Sect S3. Note, this analysis omitted the foliar traits (ferulic acid, para‐coumaric acid, and lignin S:G ratio) considered in the original genotype selection due to their lack of observed relationship with SOC in the resulting dataset (Figure S7).

Aboveground productivity factors (i.e., aboveground growth rate and coppicing, shown in red) generally have a small positive influence on MAOM‐C concentration, approximately 1 mg C/g soil per standard deviation increase in the predictor (Figure 5). We observed a strong negative relationship (4–6 mg C/g soil per SD) between soil texture (silt + clay content) and MAOM‐C. There is also a moderate negative effect of soil pH on MAOM with “Roots Only” predictors.

The effects of select aboveground, soil, and root predictors on mineral associated organic matter (MAOM) C concentration. The selected predictor effects are standardized by their respective standard deviation (SD) and the distributions correspond to 200 repetitions of 10‐fold cross‐validation.

Root lignin has a small positive relationship with MAOM‐C, counter to the notion in more modern SOC stabilization paradigms (Poirier et al. 2018) that lower‐quality litter might increase POM at the expense of MAOM. Root C/N is an important SOC control in legacy biogeochemical models, but surprisingly, it has minimal effect on MAOM‐C in this analysis. Instead, the largest effects come from other aspects of root and soil chemistry, with root Al, Na, and S having a strong positive relationship with MAOM‐C, and root Mg and B having a negative relationship. Similarly, soil Al, Fe, Cu, Na, K, Mg, and Zn are positively associated with MAOM‐C, while Mn, Ni, Ca, and P are negatively associated. The large opposite effects of root Mg and soil Mg on MAOM‐C are noteworthy.

Genotype Influence on Total Soil C Stock

We estimated the rate of divergence of SOC stocks under each genotype from the overall sample mean over the 13‐year lifetime of the common garden. Figure 6 shows the genotype effect size on SOC stock estimated based on the mean of measured SOC stocks for each genotype (grey), and from the heritability analysis which controls for soil texture, coppicing, and experimental block (Section 3.2; black). The genotypes with the highest mean present‐day SOC stocks (> 80 t C per ha) are BESC‐35 and BESC‐131, and that with the lowest mean SOC stock (30 t C/ha) is GW‐9768 (Table S1).

Discussion

Even with limited replication (n = 3), we detected genotype‐driven differences in MAOM‐C stocks that account for ~1/4 of the total observed MAOM‐C stock variability (Figure 4). Legacy SOC models like Century (Parton et al. 1987) and DayCent (Parton et al. 1998) consider plant productivity and litter quality as the main plant determinants of SOC. In contrast, we found MAOM‐C and total SOC stocks to be most strongly associated with root element concentrations (particularly Al, Mg, and B), with smaller effects from tree growth rate, root lignin, and other aspects of root chemistry (Figure 5). These results are consistent with recent theory (Cotrufo et al. 2013) and experimental evidence (Ridgeway et al. 2022; Rossi et al. 2020) that increasing lignin content may be a relatively ineffective target for enhancing biosequestration. Root elemental content was more strongly associated with MAOM‐C levels and was highly heritable across these genotypes, suggesting these may be plausible trait targets for plant breeding or engineering efforts. However, additional field observations and laboratory manipulation would be needed to validate these findings and pinpoint the underlying mechanisms at work.

These data provide a point of reference for the potential to enhance biosequestration by selecting for SOC‐associated traits in P. trichocarpa . Even though we have only sampled a small portion of this GWAS population, the observed rates of SOC divergence between the highest‐SOC genotype and the sample median suggest a potential for selection to improve sequestration by 0.4–1.8 t C/ha/year in the 0–15 cm surface soil layer (Figure 6). To put those rates in perspective, a meta‐analysis by Qin et al. (2016) estimated that commercial hybrid poplar increases 0–30 cm soil C stocks at an average rate of 0.3 t C/ha/year when grown on land previously under annual crops, or loses soil C at a rate of 1.4 t C/ha/year when grown on land that was formerly forested. Poplar field trials elsewhere in Oregon have shown no SOC stock differences compared to adjacent agricultural fields (Sartori et al. 2007), or sequestration of only 0.1 t C/ha/year (Collins et al. 2020).

The observed association between aboveground biomass and MAOM‐C is potentially indicative of a trade‐off between allocating additional carbon belowground for sequestration versus maintaining or increasing aboveground productivity (Jansson et al. 2021). While the total amount of carbon allocated belowground is known to be an important determinant of SOC, the limited association between aboveground biomass and MAOM‐C present in this dataset implies that this trade‐off may be less stark than previously thought. This may be because our aboveground biomass estimates fail to capture significant differences in allometry or litterfall dynamics between individual trees, because belowground allocation is highly variable in this population such that belowground inputs do not correlate with aboveground productivity, or because the physical or chemical form of those belowground inputs is more important than their magnitude.

Retrospectively Evaluating SOC Changes in Common Gardens

Detecting any genotype‐driven differences in soil carbon stocks in the Clatskanie common garden is notable since the site was not originally designed for biogeochemical research, and thus it lacked characterization of initial SOC and other soil properties at the time of establishment and is subject to significant root intermingling between neighboring trees in the polyclonal grid planting design. Despite these challenges, this garden and others featuring highly diverse GWAS populations are potentially valuable for isolating the broader biogeochemical impacts of individual plant traits. Our focus on soil organic matter has implications for carbon sequestration and for nutrient mineralization and plant nutrition. If the plant traits influencing different soil organic matter fractions are affected by changing temperature, moisture, and atmospheric CO2 levels, then these common garden studies may also prove useful for untangling key global change feedbacks on carbon storage and soil health.

These results reinforce the value in investigating individual soil C fractions (Hansen et al. 2024; King et al. 2023; Lugato et al. 2021) rather than total SOC, which has been the focus of many previous common garden studies at other sites (Mueller et al. 2012; Pregitzer et al. 2013; Waring et al. 2022); but which did not show any influence of genotype at our site. Similarly, genotype effects were more evident on soil bulk density than soil carbon concentration, as observed in some previous studies conducted in this and other common gardens (Craig et al. 2024; Waring et al. 2022). This study highlights the importance of measuring bulk density and evaluating C stocks (rather than just concentrations) of different organic matter fractions when attempting to detect plant‐driven differences.

Retrospective SOC analyses in common gardens are often made challenging due to limited biological replicates and statistical power, and a lack of initial SOC measurements at the time of establishment that could be subtracted from present day measurements to directly estimate SOC changes over time. In this analysis, we assumed that soil texture, coppicing, and spatial block capture the background environmental variability at Clatskanie. Although imperfect, these predictors would be static over the life of the garden, and they have a large influence on the heritability (Figure S5) and regression results (Figure 5). Our relatively disperse soil sampling across the common garden (Figure 1b) precluded us from applying empirical corrections to remove background environmental variability from other sources, as has been done in some previous trait studies in this population (Evans et al. 2014). Future retrospective analyses of sites without initial SOC characterization should aim for denser soil sampling designs in order to enable more meaningful spatial corrections.

Comparisons of carbon stocks based on repeated measurements of soil carbon concentration over time can also be confounded by corresponding changes in soil bulk density, such that different masses of soil are captured in fixed‐depth cores collected at different points in time (von Haden et al. 2020). If a given management change or treatment simultaneously affects soil carbon concentration and bulk density—e.g., a change in tillage practice (Ogle et al. 2019) or grazing, or an application of an organic amendment (von Haden et al. 2020)—it can be appropriate to apply a correction to fixed‐depth sampling results in order to compare carbon changes within a consistent mass of soil over time. While we recognize the observed strong correlation between SOC and bulk density (Figure S4a) implies that these trees have affected both carbon concentration and bulk density simultaneously, we have elected not to implement an equivalent soil mass correction in the interest of a more transparent and conservative analysis and for consistency with previous studies (e.g., Waring et al. 2022).

Insights Into MAOM‐C Stabilization Mechanisms

Root trait effects on mineral association are less well understood than their effects on decomposition and soil aggregation (Poirier et al. 2018), and many of the results reported here do not cleanly map to older versus more modern SOC stabilization paradigms. In several cases, similar results have been observed in previous studies. Our finding of a positive effect of root lignin on MAOM‐C, plus the positive soil Fe and Al effects, are consistent with previous work demonstrating preferential binding of lignin to iron and aluminum oxides (Hall et al. 2016; Heckman et al. 2013). This may be an important stabilization mechanism in these Wauna series soils that show redox mottling indicative of Fe oxide formation (National Cooperative Soil Survey, USDA 2005). The moderate negative effect of soil pH on MAOM may indicate less microbial decomposition at lower pH, or of greater soil weathering at wetter, more acidic, sites leaving more secondary Fe and Al phases that effectively bind organics (Heckman et al. 2023). The latter mechanism is supported by the pH effect disappearing in the “Root + Soil” model, along with the positive soil Al and Fe effects.

Our finding of outsized effects from root and soil nutrients and trace elements on SOC, plus their heritability, suggests those might be alternate targets for plant bio‐design, at least with respect to this site. Low leaf Mg (Yang et al. 2022), low fine root Ca (Silver and Miya 2001), and high fine root Al (in arbuscular mycorrhizal‐associated trees; Zhao et al. 2023) have all previously been associated with higher recalcitrance (i.e., lower short‐term litter decomposition rates) of those tissues. In our study, these same properties in fine roots are associated with higher MAOM‐C levels. The positive soil Mg effect we observed is consistent with MAOM‐C stabilization via cation bridging, yet we may not expect this mechanism to be as important at a wet, acidic site like Clatskanie given the relatively low Ca and Mg concentrations there (King et al. 2023). Our observation of large positive effects of both root Al and soil Al on MAOM‐C matches previous study findings in acidic, wet forests (Hobbie et al. 2007; Poirier et al. 2018; Scheel et al. 2008), though different potential underlying mechanisms are suggested in the literature. Scheel et al. (2008) performed soil incubation experiments to show that root Al helps precipitate dissolved C, slowing further microbial decomposition via reduced bioavailability. Alternately, Zhao et al. (2023) found evidence of Al reducing soil microbial activity in arbuscular mycorrhizal tree root decomposition. Additionally, our finding of strong positive effects of soil Al and Fe on MAOM‐C is consistent with stabilization via an inner‐sphere bonding mechanism common in temperate forests (Heckman et al. 2023; King et al. 2023; Rasmussen et al. 2018).

It is important to note that the tight correlations between some elements (e.g., Al, B, and Mg; Figure S8) make their individual effects difficult to discern, even using regularized regression. Furthermore, heritability analysis (Figure 4) suggests that there is a tree genotype effect on levels of certain elements in the surrounding soils, presumably due to plant uptake and depletion or transfer of elements from deep soils to surface soil horizons over time (Oostra et al. 2006; Reich et al. 2005). To the extent that these trees are selectively enhancing or depleting specific elements in the surface soil, it becomes even more challenging to separate the effects of soil elemental concentration versus root elemental concentrations on MAOM‐C. The lack of initial soil characterization at the time of garden establishment makes it difficult to interpret the causality of these results and the degree to which root elemental uptake could be manipulated as a practical biosequestration target.

Limitations and Future Work

The Clatskanie common garden was established in a marginal lowland site with poorly drained alluvial soils and with a shallow water table, and the site is prone to flooding. The MAOM dominance at this site is consistent with previous meta‐analyses showing greater MAOM abundance under humid soil conditions (Heckman et al. 2023), managed landscapes (Lugato et al. 2021), and in temperate forests (Sokol et al. 2022); though it prevents us from drawing insights into plant controls on POM. The strong negative relationship between finer soil texture and MAOM‐C observed here runs counter to the expectation that soils with greater clay content provide greater capacity for sorption to mineral surfaces and thus higher MAOM content (Hansen et al. 2024; Heckman et al. 2023). This may indicate that soil microbial activity and associated MAOM formation are lower in fine‐textured, poorly drained soils across this relatively wet site, or alternately that MAOM‐forming plant inputs are systematically lower or MAOM destabilization systematically higher.

Given the outsized importance of MAOM‐C at Clatskanie, future belowground sampling efforts should also consider root exudation, an understudied plant trait and possible biosequestration target (Jansson et al. 2021; Panchal et al. 2022). Root exudates provide a source of carbon for microbial biomass growth, a source of organic compounds that can be directly sorbed to soil minerals, or in the case of organic acids, a priming agent that destabilizes existing MAOM (Keiluweit et al. 2015). Thus, it would be useful to quantify both the magnitude of root exudation and its chemical quality in order to understand whether it will have a net positive or negative effect on total SOC (Poirier et al. 2018).

Finally, our low spatial sampling density and low number of available biological replicates per genotype (n = 3) limit the statistical power of our genotype–SOC stock analysis (Figure 6), leaving those results susceptible to error and with limited potential for spatiotemporal extrapolation. Belowground sampling under a larger portion of this GWAS population might allow us to link any observed SOC differences with specific poplar genome features with much greater statistical precision. Belowground sampling of roots and especially soil carbon is typically laborious and expensive (Paustian, Collier, et al. 2019). To the extent that studies like this can inform what types of soil C measurements are most likely to show genotype influences, and which belowground plant traits are most strongly associated with SOC outcomes, it paves the way for more targeted sampling campaigns at GWAS scales that might elucidate the biological controls on SOC with greater detail and certainty.
