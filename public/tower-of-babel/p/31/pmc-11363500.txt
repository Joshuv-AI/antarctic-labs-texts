Water transfer contributes to water resources management: Crisis mitigation for future water allocation in the Yellow River basin
Authors: Cheng-Yao Zhang, Taikan Oki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryOptimizing water allocation is a crucial strategy for water planning in large basins, such as the Yellow River basin. The transforming water supply pattern, triggered by inter-basin water transfer, urges the optimization and refinement of water allocation. Here, we predict the future water demand for different sectors at the provincial level and carry out future water allocation based on a multi-objective allocation model. The results imply that “demand increases in midstream and water deficits in downstream” would persist by 2050, and the demand structure highlights potential sectoral rivalry for water resources in each province. Water transfer will be the “antidote” to water disputes and will account for 19.8% and 34.5% of the water demand in 2030 and 2050, respectively. This study provides a basis for the policymaking of water management and establishes the future allocation pattern from water resources to sectors in each province in the Yellow River basin.

Introduction

The Yellow River is known as the mother river of the Chinese nation, providing 2.7% of the total water resources to feed 12% of the population and irrigating 15% of the arable land.1,2 Urbanization and climate change have hastened the depletion of water resources in the basin. Multiple demands, centering on agricultural, industrial, domestic, public, and ecological water uses, have intensified rivalry for water withdrawal among different sectors and provinces along the river.3,4,5,6 The lack of water resource management in the basin resulted in serious consequences: the lower reaches were cut off for 22 of the 28 years from 1972 to 1999.7 In 1987, the Yellow River water allocation scheme 1987 (the 1987 Scheme) provided the first allocation scheme for resolving water rights disputes but only specified water consumption targets at the provincial level.1,8,9 In recent years, the disparity between water rights in the downstream and midstream has indicated an inequitable water allocation, posing challenges in meeting water demand across different sectors in each province.6,8,10,11

Rational allocation of water resources should be a long-term strategy involved in the water management of basins.12,13,14 Optimization of water allocation has emerged as a key concern in the future development of the Yellow River basin.15,16 The long-term plan requires optimizing and refining the allocation scheme by considering the water supply structure and priority for meeting the water demand of different sectors in each province. However, few studies advocate for filling the gap in optimizing the water allocation scheme for different sectors at the provincial level. The time frame and boundary conditions of the water allocation scheme depend on the future water supply structure of the basin, which will affect the decision-making process and priority of allocation. The South-to-North Water Transfer Project is expected to mark a turning point in the future water supply structure in the basin (Figure 1).17,18,19 The Eastern and Central Routes have provided Shandong and Henan provinces with 5.3 and 15.4 billion m3 of water by 2021, directly benefiting over 100 million people. The Western Route, scheduled to take effect by 2030, aims to deliver 8.0 billion m3 of water to the middle and upper reaches, fundamentally promoting water availability in those areas. Water transfer will profoundly change the water supply pattern and determine water deployment in the future.20,21 Therefore, future schemes should coordinate the allocation of different water sources to enhance efficiency. However, there is a lack of research on the allocation of multiple water sources, including water transfer strategies, in the Yellow River basin.Figure 1South-to-North Water Transfer Project in China (made by the authors)

Future allocation requires water demand predictions under multiple social-economic development scenarios for anticipating uncertainty.22,23,24,25 A body of literature emphasizes the assessment of water scarcity over allocation,26,27,28,29,30 and conducts grid-level-based assessments to capture spatial variations of water resources.31,32,33,34,35 However, this methodology could make it hard to represent the real case because the locations of water abstraction and use may not be captured within a grid, especially for water transfer.36,37 Besides, the research sectors are limited and there are few discussions on social-economic development scenarios.38,39,40,41 To refine the allocation scheme for the Yellow River basin, it is imperative to undertake sectoral predictions at the provincial level under different development scenarios.

While evaluations exist regarding water resources in the Yellow River basin,29,42,43,44,45 research on future water allocation and planning remains limited. To fill this gap, this study first predicted the future demands for different sectors at the provincial level under Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs).46 The methodology incorporates the social, economic, and natural variables, including GDP, population, water use intensity, and evapotranspiration. Subsequently, a multi-objective optimization model is developed to maximize the economic and social benefits of water allocation at the provincial level across multiple sectors, which can contribute to the long-term water resources planning in the basin. The methodology integrates various factors, including administrative divisions, economic sectors, water sources, supply cost and benefit, and policies. A notable innovation is the inclusion of inter-basin water transfer in the allocation process, with discussions on future transfer strategies. On this basis, the future water allocation scheme for the Yellow River basin is proposed for establishing the allocation pattern from water sources to sectors, thereby facilitating water management policymaking and enhancing basin sustainability.

Literature review

Comprehensive basin-wide methodologies such as integrated river basin management have been implemented to effectively manage water resources sustainably.47 In some developing countries such as China and India, basin management primarily focuses on infrastructure development and engineering construction cooperation, lacking consistency in river basin management.48 Decentralized management has resulted in regional interests prevailing over fairness and efficiency basin-wide, highlighting the necessity of unified management.43,47,49

Predicting the future of water resources is fundamental for implementing sustainable management practices.50 However, much of the research has focused on the geographical context and scale of climate change impacts on water resources,51,52,53 with limited attention given to quantifying future changes in water demand. Predicting future water demand across various sectors can help clarify the roles and relationships of multiple stakeholders within a basin and anticipate potential crises. Sabzi et al. (2019)54 revealed future water stress hotspots in the Red River Basin by predicting water demand and available water, relying on fitted curves based on historical data. Hassan et al. (2019)55 assessed future water demand impacts in the Indus River Basin but only discussed irrigation and domestic use. Transboundary basins often involve multiple water management agencies and users with different interests, emphasizing the importance of water demand management.56

Socio-economic drivers may significantly increase water demand. Future water demand should not be viewed solely as a constant threshold for water supply57,58,59; rather, their potential changes need to be considered.60 Scenario analysis can aid policymakers in understanding basin dynamics over the long term.61 A study on the Pearl River Basin conducted by Yan (2018)62 demonstrated that scenario analysis can predict the reasons for water supply-demand imbalances and identify optimal strategies from various scenarios. An assessment of future water demand highlights economic growth as the most significant driver of future water scarcity, outweighing any actual savings from technological advances and improved water efficiency.63 Similarly, Yang et al. (2021)64 emphasized the strong correlation between socio-economic development and river systems, primarily influenced by factors such as per capita GDP, urbanization rate, and population density. Therefore, multi-scenario predictions of socio-economic water demand are of paramount importance for the rational allocation of water resources in uncertain future trends.56

The Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) aim to qualitatively and quantitatively describe alternative social and environmental development pathways for the 21st century.46,65 The formulation of five distinct SSPs scenarios reflects varying developmental contexts and the disparate magnitudes of global challenges associated with climate change mitigation and adaptation: a) SSP1: Sustainability − Taking the green road (low challenges to mitigation and adaptation); b) SSP2: Middle of the Road (moderate challenges to mitigation and adaptation); c) SSP3: Further fragmentation between regions (extreme challenges to mitigation and adaptation); d) SSP4: Increase in inequality across and within regions (high challenge to adaptation but low challenge to mitigation); 5) SSP5: Fossil-fueled development − Taking the highway (high challenge to mitigation but low challenge to adaptation).25 The SSPs methodology is widely employed for estimating fundamental factors such as population, GDP, and urbanization.25,66 Additionally, the SSP methodology finds applications in water demand prediction.24,67

Water resource allocation constitutes a pivotal aspect of integrated river basin management.68 Compared to single-objective planning, multi-objective planning offers a more robust and accurate method to address complex allocation issues.69 Multi-objective optimization models could integrate economic, social, and environmental objectives, and offer optimal solutions under various scenarios, and consider priorities of water demand, making them widely applied in water allocation issues.69,70,71,72,73 Tabari and Soltani (2013)74 proposed a multi-objective optimization model for the joint utilization of surface water and groundwater, employing the non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm (NSGA-II) to illustrate the optimal trade-offs between objectives. Chang et al. (2016)75 used the NSGA-II model to optimize the scheduling of reservoirs in Taiwan, considering both agricultural and public water demands. The results indicated that the NSGA-II model could effectively search for optimal water allocation sequences and achieve much lower seasonal water shortage rates compared to simulation methods. Naghdi et al. (2021)72 employed the multi-objective optimization model to maximize water supply and minimize groundwater extraction from the basin. Therefore, this study established a multi-objective optimization model to describe the water allocation process in the Yellow River basin and seek the optimal scheme that maximizes economic and social benefits.

For the Yellow River basin, water resource allocation has emerged as a paramount research focus.24,29,76 Nonetheless, investigations into these issues remain considerably limited, with a substantial research gap persisting. Yin et al. (2017)24 evaluated alterations in renewable water resources and water withdrawals for agricultural, domestic, and industrial purposes within the basin, indicating potential declines in future food production due to water scarcity in the middle and lower reaches. But this study incompletely addressed all water use sectors and could not explore feasible water allocation schemes due to its failure to assess from an administrative perspective. Guo et al. (2022)9 scrutinized water demand for 2030 from the vantage point of water resource carrying capacity and conducted two scenario analyses. While this study roughly allocated water based on demand, it neglected overall basin benefits, thereby inadequately supporting the enhancement of existing allocation schemes. Zhang et al. (2023)77 introduced a model for water resource allocation in the basin and proposed detailed allocation schemes. Nevertheless, this allocation scheme disregarded inter-basin water transfers, and water demand was merely predicted based on historical data fitting, disregarding other socio-economic changes. Wang et al. (2023)78 formulated a water allocation model to adjust basin water allocation schemes and develop new water rights for the upper, middle, and lower reaches, albeit without specifying the water supply proportions for different water sources. A notable aspect of previous research is the limited consideration of future shifts in water supply structures, especially the potential changes of water transfers, and the absence of long-term planning. Therefore, optimizing water allocation schemes should be based on the future socio-economic development, encompassing benefit objectives of stakeholders and evolving water supply structures.

Results

Future prediction of water demand of multi-sectors under shared socioeconomic pathways at the provincial level

We first carry out future water demand predictions for the agricultural, industrial, domestic, public, and urban ecological sectors across 8 provinces under five SSPs (Figure 2). These predictions involve sector-related social, economic, and natural variables, providing insights into future water resource demand under the storylines of SSP1 to SSP5. Population and GDP vary according to SSPs, and changes in other factors, such as irrigation efficiency, water use intensity in different sectors, water-saving rate in different sectors, and different cooling systems of thermal power, are also examined. The results demonstrate significant geographical disparities in future water demand across provinces, with water demand in densely populated and industrially developed regions (Shandong and Henan provinces) being roughly five times that of less developed regions (Qinghai and Ningxia provinces). Under SSP1 to SSP3, the water demand of each province is projected to remain steady or increase slightly by 2050 (Figure 3), placing enormous strain on the water supply. Therefore, there will be a long-term dilemma of “water surplus in the upper reaches, fast growth in the midstream, and water deficit in the downstream” (Figures S1 and S2). Beyond 2050, the future water demand of each province is expected to decline under SSP1 to SSP3, with the reduction rate of demand pressure being determined by variables such as population, economic development rate, and water use intensity (Figures S3–S13). However, in SSP4 and SSP5, the water demand in each province exhibits a trend of sharp decline and a delay in reaching the peak, respectively. The future structure of water demand among sectors within provinces serves as a basis for refining allocation (Figure S14). Agricultural water demand will dominate future water demand in each province, particularly in upstream provinces. Downstream provinces such as Henan and Shandong will exhibit significant industrial water demand, comprising half or more of the agricultural demand. The ratios of industrial water demand in Shaanxi, Shanxi, and Henan provinces are expected to increase by 2050 and 2100 due to industrial structural transformations. However, future changes in industrial water demand ratios are not dramatic due to improvements in water-saving efficiencies and policy goals. Domestic water demand will account for a second large ratio of water demand in most provinces, driven primarily by the population scale in SSP scenarios. The public and urban ecological water demand varies regionally, with a greater share observed in the middle and lower reaches.

Future water allocation for multi-sectors in provinces based on water transfer in 2030

Optimizing and refining “the 1987 Scheme” necessitates clarifying the current and future water supply structure in the Yellow River basin. Each province relies on three water supply sources: surface water, groundwater, and water transfer, supplying water for agricultural, industrial, domestic, public, and urban ecological sectors. Hence, water allocation should reflect the balance of supply from different sources and the water demand and order of withdrawal from each sector. The current water allocation in the Yellow River basin relies on “the 1987 Scheme,” which provides only a rough provincial water consumption quota without further guidance for sectoral allocation within each province (Figure S15). Therefore, we propose a multi-sectoral water allocation model at the provincial level to refine and optimize the existing scheme. This model involves various factors pertinent to the water allocation process, including provinces, economic sectors, water transfer, water sources, and water supply costs & benefits. Full details are provided in the STAR Methods section.

This study conducts the future water allocation in 2030 based on the future water demand under the sustainable development scenario (SSP1), as depicted in Figure 4. Surface water, groundwater, and water transfer contribute varying proportions of water to the allocation, with respective supply ratios of 62.9%, 17.3%, and 19.8%. Water transfer is projected to surpass groundwater as the predominant water source for the basin’s allocation. Considerable disparities exist in water allocation among provinces located in the upper, middle, and lower reaches of the basin. The three upstream provinces of Qinghai, Ningxia, and Gansu collectively allocate 21.7% of water resources, while the two midstream provinces of Shanxi and Shaanxi allocate a combined 22.4% of water resources. It is similar to the 18.8% of water resources allocated to the downstream province of Shandong. Such disparities are primarily attributed to differences in economic development and industrial scale between the eastern and central-western regions. The provinces of Shandong and Henan, recognized as the "economic leaders" of the basin, remain to account for a substantial portion of water consumption, approximately 36.9% of the total. The water transfer would reduce the water shortage in the downstream regions by supplying 3.98 billion m3 of water. Additionally, due to its extensive agricultural base (over 100 million mu of arable land), Inner Mongolia will take up a significant portion of the water allocation, amounting to 19.0%. Water transfer will contribute to crisis mitigation for water resources allocation until 2050

The Western Route of the South to North Water Transfer is projected to be fully constructed, with a water delivery capacity of 18 billion m3 by 2050. Figure 6 shows the detailed future water allocation in 2050. While surface water remains the primary water source, its supply ratio has shrunk to 53.8%. A similar trend is visible in groundwater supply, with its ratio decreasing to 11.8%. Conversely, water transfer, with a 14.7% increase compared to 2030, will bear 34.5% of the basin’s water demand, nearly three times the capacity of groundwater. This suggests that water transfer by 2050 will provide the basin with more available water to cope with the risk of high-intensity water demand over the following three decades. Furthermore, the inter-basin water transfer will alleviate the overexploitation of water resources in the basin, thereby reducing groundwater depletion and supplementing environmental water. The middle and lower reaches of the basin continue to be the primary water consumers, with the allocation ratio for provinces largely stable. In contrast with 2030, the proportion of water allocation in different sectors gradually changes, with the agricultural sector steadily shrinking while the industrial and domestic sectors grow. By 2050, there will be sufficient water to fulfill the basin’s water demand, enabling basin authorities to invest water resources in ambitious ecological and industrial development.Figure 6Future water allocation in 2050

Future water allocation in 2050

The decision-making framework for water resource allocation by 2050 is described in Figure 7A, based on the current administrative division, water transfer projects, and water use sectors. The water supply strategy of combining inter-basin water transfer and local water sources in the basin is expected to be initially formed in 2030 and fully realized in 2050 (Figure 7A). The replenishment of extraterritorial water resources at the billion m3 level will increase allocable water by 27.2% in 2030 and 46.1% in 2050 compared to 2020. Thus, even though the future water demand predictions indicate that high-intensity water demand will continue until 2050, the total water deficits in the basin can be mitigated, resulting in a surplus of 13.9% in 2050. Under such circumstances, comprehensive water planning at the basin level can make more efficient use of water resources, particularly by optimizing the allocation of water transfer. Thus far, the East and Middle Routes' water supplies have been planned to satisfy the specific regional requirements, leaving room for improvement in allocation efficiency (Figures S1 and S2). It is estimated that the water transfer will contribute approximately 20% of the water supply for the provinces in 2030, increasing to over 33% by 2050, as illustrated in Figure 7B. Discussion

Future water demand prediction for multiple sectors in provinces is of great importance in developing long-term water allocation schemes. Our results indicate that significant water demand growth over the next three decades will be concentrated in the middle and lower reaches, especially in Henan Province. A recent evaluation of water supply challenges in the Yellow River basin also suggests that the peak pressure will manifest downstream.29 All scenarios predict a persistent high-intensity water demand situation until 2050, except for the SSP4 scenario. The predicted peak demand period is consistent with the previous study24 and is primarily determined by population and economic development trajectories. The agricultural sector will remain the largest water user in the basin, but future agricultural water demand is expected to decline slightly due to improved irrigation efficiency (Figure S4). However, it is essential to remain vigilant against the potential harm resulting from unmet agricultural water demand, which could lead to reduced food production.24,29 Future demand for industrial water by 2050 under SSP1 to SSP4 shows a trend with less drastic change, influenced by industrial water efficiency targets and the adoption of water-saving technology (Figure S7). With anticipated changes in population and economic development, water demand in the domestic and public sectors is projected to rise by 2040 (Figure S9). Alongside this shift, the basin will face challenges of clean water supply in densely populated areas and reducing pollution to ensure water security. From a demand-side perspective, we recommend prioritizing the establishment of regional water conservation goals, including stricter water quota standards and higher penetration rates of water-saving technologies in regions with large water demand and developed economies. Additionally, implementing differentiated sectoral standards, focusing on improving water use efficiency in high water-consuming sectors such as agricultural and domestic sectors, along with offering water conservation incentives and subsidies.79

The modeling analysis of water allocation demonstrates the future allocation pattern of water resources in the Yellow River basin from different sources to multiple sectors at the provincial level. While several studies on water allocation in the Yellow River basin have proposed various allocation models, most of these models have neglected to incorporate water transfer into their allocation schemes or conduct long-term planning.6,76,77,78 This study emphasizes the critical role of water transfer in future water allocation to mitigate potential crises. Inter-basin water transfer, anticipated to change the water supply structure, stands as a strategic approach to mitigate future water competition. The replenishment of extraterritorial water resources will increase the allocable water by 27.2% and 46.1% in 2030 and 2050, respectively, compared with 2020 (Figure 7). The high-quality water resources brought by water transfer could address the water quantity and quality requirements of domestic water demand. Considering the reality of declining groundwater levels, groundwater pollution, and the insecurity of ecological water use in the basin, water transfer emerges as the most direct way to address these challenges.18,80 Building upon our previous research,6,79 this study provides a more comprehensive dataset on water benefits and supply costs, encompassing various aspects such as water use sectors and water sources. On this basis, the proposed allocation scheme outlines a detailed allocation structure for water sources, administrative units, and sectors, which has not been addressed in previous studies. A study that only considered provincial water allocation provided a 2030 allocation plan based on principles of fairness and efficiency, allocating 42.1%, 22.3%, and 33.9% of water supply to upper, middle, and lower provinces, respectively.78 This outcome shares similar results with our provincial allocation scheme, where the allocation proportions for upper, middle, and lower reaches are 40.7%, 22.4%, and 36.9%, respectively. However, in contrast, the proposed scheme in this study refined the scope to include multi-sectoral allocations within each province, providing insights into the specific allocation from different sources. In this scheme, groundwater usage is restricted for the agricultural, domestic, and public sectors to prevent contamination. Water transfer is employed to alleviate water shortages in various provinces. In Henan, 29.9% of transferred water will be allocated to support the industrial sector, while in Inner Mongolia, 75.4% of transferred water aims to safeguard agricultural production. Furthermore, considering the favorable outcomes achieved by the eastern and central routes of south-to-north water transfer, there is growing momentum in emphasizing the anticipated impact of the western route.81,82,83,84 We explored the comprehensive effects achievable with the full implementation of south-to-north water transfer. Full-scale inter-basin water transfer holds the potential to meet the projected water demands of various provinces within the basin, augmenting the available water supply by over 40% by 2050. Concurrently, the allocation structure within provinces gradually transitions from the predominance of agricultural water use to an increasing allocation proportion for other sectors.

Conclusions

The intensification of water use conflicts among provinces and sectors and the divergence of water resource allocation from actual demands reveal substantial deficiencies in the prevailing management system. The forthcoming implementation of inter-basin water transfer is anticipated to catalyze a holistic transformation in water resource management. In this context, this study predicted future water demand under Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). Fluctuations in water demand over time are observed within a range of −66%–43% from 2030 to 2100 (relative to 2020) under five scenarios. By 2050, the water demand in various provinces will remain stable or slightly increase, implying that the substantial water supply pressure in northern China will persist for the next three decades. There will be substantial geographical disparities in future water demand across provinces, with intra-provincial differences potentially reaching up to 5-fold. Meanwhile, challenges related to "excessive upstream water, rapid growth in the midstream, and inadequate downstream water" are anticipated to manifest.

This study incorporates water transfer into the water resource management in the basin, and the results show that the replenishment of extraterritorial water resources will increase the allocable water in 2030 and 2050 by 27.2% and 46.1% compared with 2020. The future allocation schemes suggest that water transfer is poised to bring about a notable improvement in the water supply pattern, with the potential to meet approximately 20% and 30% of the water demand within the provinces of the basin by 2030 and 2050, respectively. This transition is anticipated to supplant groundwater as the second major water source in the basin. The refined scheme, spanning from three water sources to eight provinces and further to five sectors, is proposed to balance water withdrawal between upstream and downstream areas and optimize water management in the basin.

Limitations of the study

The water demand prediction for each province is based on shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs). However, this does not imply that policymakers are restricted to fixed scenarios; rather, it offers an analytical method. Future studies could explore additional simulated scenarios incorporating a broader range of regional development factors, thereby providing more evidence for policymaking.

The allocation model proposed in this study may have significant implications for water resource management. Nevertheless, the model does not encompass factors such as sediment transport in rivers, water quality improvement, and pollutant control. Future endeavors could further enhance the comprehensiveness and applicability of the optimization model by incorporating environmental development objectives and aligning them with regional environmental policies.

Optimizing water allocation in the Yellow River basin is a highly complex task, especially when integrating water transfer. Further considerations should include rational scales for inter-basin water transfer, water transfer efficiency, engineering timelines, and a coordinated management system spanning the entire basin.

Method details

Future water demand prediction

The prediction results of GDP, population, and per capita GDP under the SSP scenarios in China are shown in Figures S16–S18. The data is sourced from the SSP database hosted by the IIASA Energy Program at https://tntcat.iiasa.ac.at/SspDb.

Agricultural water demand prediction

The prediction of agricultural water demand is determined by the value added of the primary industry under the SSP scenarios, the water use intensity per unit value added by agriculture, and the decline rate of water use intensity per unit value added by agriculture, as shown in Equation 1. Industrial water demand prediction

In this study, industrial water use means the net water consumption, which can be divided into water use by industries and cooling water consumption. Most studies generally only consider cooling water consumption because it constitutes a significant portion of water usage in the industrial sector. Allocation model

This study abstracts the water allocation process as a multi-objective optimization issue, including economic objective (maximum economic efficiency) and social objective (minimum water deficit). Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm-II (NSGA-II) is used to solve this multi-objective optimization model. In this model, the water resources are classified into three types: surface water, groundwater, and water transfer. The water users are the agricultural, industrial, domestic, public and urban ecological sectors. The model takes into account the water supply order among different sectors within each province. In addition, water supply costs in different regions are differentiated to support solving the model (Tables S1–S4).
