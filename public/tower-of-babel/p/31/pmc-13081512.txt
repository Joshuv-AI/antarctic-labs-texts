Integrating the Triple Jump Exercise and gamification (G-Jump) to enhance learning outcomes among anesthesia students: a preliminary quasi-experimental study
Authors: Hasanali Jafarpoor, Hossein Ghasemi Falavarjani, Amirhossein Shokrani, Yosef Mortazavi, Asal Kazemzadeh, Fatemeh Hasaninejad Darzi, Fatemeh Behnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn health profession education, assessment functions not only as a measure of performance but also as a learning opportunity. The Triple Jump Exercise (TJE) is a formative assessment method that promotes self-assessment, problem-solving, and knowledge integration. However, its traditional format often lacks engaging elements, which may reduce its effectiveness. The present study introduces an innovative model, G-Jump, which integrates TJE with gamification based on the philosophy of “assessment for learning” and seeks to optimize this formative assessment model among anesthesia students.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study included 45 anesthesia students from Babol University of Medical Sciences, randomly assigned to three groups: control, TJE, and G-Jump. All groups received identical instructions, but the assessment method varied across groups. Data were collected through performance tests (pre-test, post-test, and follow-up) and the Vidakis satisfaction questionnaire, and analyzed using R software (version 4.5.0).ResultsThe G-Jump group scored significantly higher post-test mean score (16.14 ± 1.72) compared to the TJE (14.57 ± 1.64) and control (13.3 ± 2.55) groups (p < 0.001). Background

Assessment is a cornerstone of modern education, evolving from a tool of evaluation into a means of promoting learning. In the “assessment for learning” paradigm, the process guides and enhances student learning through continuous, interactive methods [1–4]. This shift requires a holistic approach that considers acceptability, interactivity, and educational impact of assessment methods [5].

In medical education, the rapid expansion of knowledge and the rise of digital technologies highlight the need for innovative assessment methods. Memorization alone is no longer adequate; students must learn to access, analyze, and apply information effectively [6, 7]. Closed-book exams in formative assessment, although useful for assessing basic knowledge, are insufficient for promoting deep and applied learning. They may also be prone to exacerbating student anxiety, a common adverse outcome associated with conventional formative assessment [8]. In contrast, open-book assessments with targeted feedback encourage deeper understanding and conceptual mastery [9, 10]. Studies such as Minder et al. (2018) and Vidya (2019) reported that 71% of medical students preferred open-book formative assessments [11, 12]. Combining open-book formats with other methods helps cultivate problem-solving, self-assessment, and lifelong learning skills [13, 14].

Innovative assessment methods in medical education include 360-degree evaluations, Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE), portfolios, reflective questions, peer assessment, and the Triple Jump Exercise (TJE) [15–17]. Developed at McMaster University, TJE is a three-phase model—closed-book test, open-book test, and oral presentation — designed to assess analysis, problem-solving, and knowledge integration [18, 19]. TJE encourages self-directed learning, information synthesis, and critical inquiry [20, 21]. Although it requires considerable time, resources, and training [22] Studies by Chian (University of Hong Kong) and Farias (Brazil) confirmed its validity and ability to support student learning across stages [20, 21].

However, traditional TJE may not fully engage or motivate students [23, 24]. Integrating gamification into TJE offers a potential solution. Gamification elements — such as competition, real-time feedback, rewards, and ranking — increase engagement and make assessments more meaningful [25]. While prior studies have examined TJE and gamification separately, none have explored their integration in anesthesia education.

To address this gap, the present study introduces an innovative model, G-Jump, which integrates TJE with gamification based on the philosophy of “assessment for learning.” This model aims not only to assess knowledge but also to enhance cognitive learning through real-time feedback, scientific inquiry, reflection, and active engagement. Accordingly, this preliminary study seeks to optimize formative assessment among anesthesia students at Babol University of Medical Sciences through the G-Jump model, which complements rather than replaces traditional testing methods.

Methodology

Study design, sample size, and participants

This quasi-experimental study was conducted at Babol University of Medical Sciences with undergraduate anesthesia students selected through convenience sampling.

To determine the sample size, G*Power software (version 3.0.10) was used with the following parameters: effect size (f) = 0.40 (based on the study by Poodineh Moghadam et al.),α = 0.05, and power (1 − β) of 0.80 [26]. The design included three groups, three measurement points, and an assumed correlation of 0.50 among repeated measures. The software indicated that 45 participants were required, with 15 students assigned to each group (control, TJE, and G-Jump).

To control for prior knowledge and reduce confounding factors, participants were stratified by pre-test performance (high, medium, low) and then randomly allocated to the three groups. The control group completed closed-book assessments, while the two intervention groups experienced TJE or G-Jump.

Data were collected through performance tests at three stages: pre-test, post-test (one week later), and follow-up (two weeks later). Inclusion criteria required students to have at least one semester of academic experience; exclusion criteria included absence from tests, withdrawal, or lack of consent.

Expert panel and educational content

An eight-member expert panel was formed, comprising specialists in medical education, anesthesiology, and instructional design with expertise in gamification. Four members held PhDs in medical education or instructional design, two were anesthesiologists with teaching experience, and two were researchers in gamification and educational technology.

The panel selected two topics aligned with the national anesthesia curriculum: “Management of Cardiac Arrhythmias During Anesthesia” and “Management of Postoperative Complications.” All groups received the same content, instructor, duration, and schedule; the only independent variable was the assessment method (closed-book, TJE, or G-Jump). Test procedures differed among groups to isolate the effect of the intervention.

G-Jump implementation

The G-Jump model combined TJE with gamification following the Werbach–Hunter framework, which includes defining objectives, identifying behaviors, describing players, designing activities, integrating game elements, and implementation [27]. Students received a detailed guidebook and attended an orientation session to ensure familiarity with the process.

First jump: closed-book test

Students answered case-based MCQs in a competitive, points-based format. Correct answers earned points with live leaderboard updates. Those scoring ≥ 60 points received a “Badge of Excellence.” The winner was determined by the highest score within the allocated time.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using R software version 4.5.0. Descriptive statistics included means and standard deviations. Normality and homogeneity were tested using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA compared group performance, with ANCOVA controlling for grade point average (GPA) and age. Gender differences were tested using ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

The mean ages were 21.46 ± 1.31, 21.13 ± 0.34, and 21.00 ± 0.37 years, respectively, and the mean GPAs were 16.10 ± 1.46, 16.01 ± 1.57, and 16.26 ± 1.33. These similarities confirm effective randomization and baseline comparability across groups. Overall, 73.3% of participants were female, a factor considered in subsequent analyses.

Pre-test, post-test, and follow-up performance scores

The average pre-test scores were nearly identical among the three groups (control: 7.46 ± 2.00; TJE: 7.46 ± 1.31; G-Jump: 7.40 ± 1.67), indicating comparable baseline knowledge before the intervention.

Following the intervention, post-test scores increased significantly in all groups. The mean post-test scores were 13.3 ± 2.55 (control), 14.57 ± 1.64 (TJE), and 16.14 ± 1.72 (G-Jump). The G-Jump group achieved the highest mean score, followed by TJE and then the control group.

Follow-up assessments two weeks later demonstrated sustained improvements. Mean scores were 13.73 ± 1.88 for the control group, 15.33 ± 0.87 for TJE, and 16.80 ± 1.72 for G-Jump. Notably, the G-Jump group not only maintained but slightly improved its average score over time.

Performance outcomes and learning trajectories

Analysis of variance revealed significant group differences in post-test and follow-up scores, while pre-test scores remained homogeneous (Table 2). Tukey’s post-hoc test indicated that in the post-test, G-Jump outperformed the control group (p = 0.0016), whereas other pairwise differences were non-significant. At follow-up, both G-Jump (p < 0.001) and TJE (p = 0.025) scored significantly higher than the control group, but no significant difference emerged between G-Jump and TJE (Table 3).

To assess whether the observed differences were influenced by gender, a two-way analysis of variance was conducted. The results indicated that gender had no significant effect on performance at the post-test stage (F [1, 41] = 2.044, p = 0.160) or the follow-up stage (F [1, 41] = 0.90, p = 0.348). This confirms that the performance improvements were primarily attributable to the type of intervention rather than gender differences. Furthermore, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to control for age and GPA. After adjusting for these covariates, the effect of the intervention type remained significant for both post-test (p = 0.003) and follow-up scores (p < 0.001), further validating the results (detailed ANCOVA results are presented in Supplementary Table S3).

Boxplot analysis confirmed that the G-Jump group achieved the highest post-test scores, with a markedly higher median and broader distribution, indicating stronger performance and engagement. The TJE group showed moderate improvement with a median of 14.3, while the control group had the lowest median and narrowest range. Learning trajectory analysis demonstrated that all groups began at similar baseline levels. G-Jump exhibited the greatest increase from pre-test to post-test and sustained the highest scores during follow-up, showing better short-term retention. TJE also outperformed the control group, which showed minimal improvement and the lowest follow-up performance (Fig. 2).

Scientific search skill evaluation

In the second stage (open-book test) of TJE and G-Jump, students accessed online and printed resources to analyze clinical scenarios and document their search process.

Commonly used keywords in students’ searches included: Anesthesia and arrhythmia management, Management of postoperative nausea and vomiting, Cognitive dysfunction after anesthesia, Anesthesia drug side effects, Drug-induced complications in anesthesia, Intraoperative embolism management, Ventricular tachycardia management during anesthesia. These patterns reflect a focus on critical anesthesia complications, though keyword combinations were often ineffective, yielding superficial results.

Many students relied on general search engines (e.g., Google, Wikipedia, Healthline) rather than specialized databases such as PubMed or UpToDate. Documentation of search strategies was frequently incomplete, and critical evaluation of retrieved content was limited. Overall, only 26.6% of students used credible scientific sources, indicating moderate skill levels in resource selection, content synthesis, and evidence-based reasoning. Examples of student search processes are presented in the Supplementary Figure S1.

Student satisfaction with the G-Jump method

Student satisfaction with the G-Jump assessment was measured using the Vidakis Game Satisfaction Questionnaire (maximum score = 40). The G-Jump group reported a mean score of 34.8 ± 1.32 (87% of the total), with scores ranging from 32 to 36. Based on standard interpretation criteria, 93.3% of participants demonstrated “high satisfaction” (scores > 33) and 6.7% moderate satisfaction (Scores 17–32). No participants reported low satisfaction (Supplementary Table S4). These findings indicate strong acceptance of the G-Jump model. The questionnaire demonstrated strong internal reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.933.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of integrating the Triple Jump Exercise (TJE) with gamification (G-Jump) on anesthesia students’ learning outcomes, engagement, and information-seeking skills. By bridging the gap between structured problem-based assessment and engagement-driven mechanics, the G-Jump model offers a scalable framework for shifting formative assessment from a static evaluation of knowledge to a dynamic driver of learning. Our findings demonstrate that G-Jump effectively facilitated significant improvements in short-term knowledge acquisition, high levels of learner satisfaction, and identified critical gaps in self-directed information-seeking behaviors.

At the Reaction level of Kirkpatrick’s model, 93.3% of G-Jump participants reported high satisfaction. This aligns with the evidence from Vidakis et al. (2020) and Subhash & Cudney (2018), who identified gamification as a potent driver of motivation in medical education. The positive experience reported by students was likely sustained by the narrative design, real-time feedback, and the structured application of the Werbach-Hunter framework [28, 29].

From a theoretical perspective, the G-Jump framework is grounded in Self-Determination and Flow theories [30, 31]. By satisfying learners’ psychological needs for autonomy through self-paced progression and competence via immediate feedback, the intervention successfully fostered intrinsic motivation. While recent literature cautions that gamification can inadvertently trigger unhealthy competition or psychological stress [32, 33] G-Jump mitigated these risks by prioritizing self-referenced progress over peer rivalry. Furthermore, the design maintained an optimal balance between task challenge and student skill level, preventing cognitive overload and facilitating a “flow” state [34]. This design choice aligns with Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick’s model of formative feedback (2006), which posits that high-quality feedback functions as a motivational mechanism to support self-regulation and bridge performance gaps [35]. However, the potential “novelty effect” must be acknowledged; future longitudinal research is required to determine whether these high engagement levels persist once gamified assessments become a routine component of the curriculum.

In addition to cognitive and motivational factors, the G-Jump model incorporated narrative and aesthetic elements to sustain learners’ attention and engagement throughout the assessment process. Consistent visual design, immersive storytelling, and real-time narrative feedback helped maintain focus and emotional involvement during task completion [36, 37]. Although satisfaction was measured using a standardized instrument, the positive responses may partially reflect the clarity, coherence, and meaningful feedback embedded in the system design. Future studies should further examine the specific contribution of narrative and aesthetic components to learner motivation and learning outcomes [28, 38].

Regarding the Learning level of Kirkpatrick’s model, the G-Jump group achieved significantly higher post-test and follow-up scores than other groups. These findings are consistent with evidence suggesting that open-book assessments with feedback support deeper understanding and longer retention [12]. Similarly, Chian et al. (2020) demonstrated that a well-designed Triple Jump aligned with educational objectives can enhance self-assessment and clinical analysis [20]. While prior studies have examined gamification and the Triple Jump Exercise separately, the present study extends existing literature by demonstrating the educational value of integrating these two approaches within a single formative assessment model. In the G-Jump model, staged tasks of increasing complexity—combined with scoring, badges, and personalized feedback—supported active learning and sustained engagement, consistent with constructivist, cognitive load, and scaffolding theories. Nevertheless, the observed improvements in follow-up scores must be interpreted with caution. Since the follow-up occurred only two weeks post-intervention, these results reflect short-term knowledge retention rather than long-term consolidation. Additionally, the positive effects observed at follow-up may partly be influenced by novelty or heightened engagement associated with the gamified intervention. Therefore, the learning outcomes should be interpreted as short-term effects, and future studies with extended follow-up periods are required to assess durable knowledge retention and transfer to clinical practice.

A primary objective of this study was to support self-directed learning and information-seeking skills. During the open-book stage, we observed that most students relied on general search engines (Google, Wikipedia) rather than specialized medical databases (PubMed, UpToDate). This pattern confirms findings by Shamsaee et al. (2022) and Mikalef et al. (2017), indicating that even within a gamified, high-engagement environment, students often lack the structured training required for sophisticated evidence-based retrieval [39, 40]. This finding is a critical contribution of our study: it suggests that while gamification successfully increases the quantity of engagement, it does not automatically improve the quality of scientific inquiry. Future iterations of the G-Jump model should therefore incorporate explicit modules or “quests” focused on information literacy to ensure that the autonomy provided by the model results in high-quality evidence-based reasoning [41].

Statistical analyses (ANOVA and ANCOVA) indicated that performance differences were primarily attributable to the educational intervention rather than confounding variables. By standardizing instructional content and environments across groups, this study reinforces the internal validity of the G-Jump model. The results suggest that the model is both feasible and adaptable for competency-based anesthesia programs, where clinical reasoning and rapid information retrieval are paramount [42].

Beyond quantitative outcomes, G-Jump enhanced not only theoretical understanding but also clinical reasoning, reflective analysis, and information-seeking behavior. This supports the concept of “assessment as learning,” in which engagement with assessment tasks directly promotes learning and self-regulation [4]. In summary, this study contributes to medical education by presenting a structured model that transforms formative assessment into a learning intervention. G-Jump demonstrates that by carefully calibrating competition, narrative, and feedback, educators can enhance learner engagement and autonomy without compromising the analytical rigor of the Triple Jump Exercise.

Conclusion

The G-Jump model integrates the Triple Jump Exercise with gamification to create a learner-centered assessment that enhances motivation, engagement, and reflection among anesthesia students. Grounded in constructivist, cognitive load, and self-determination theories, it transforms assessment into a process that promotes feedback, progressive challenge, and self-directed learning. Rather than serving as a summative endpoint, assessment becomes a central component of the learning experience.

While performance gains were significant, students’ information search skills require structured training to fully realize the model’s potential. This study demonstrates that innovation in assessment design does not depend on extensive resources but on the strategic integration of motivational and reflective components.

However, due to the short follow-up period, single-institution setting, and potential observer bias (Hawthorne effect), conclusions regarding long-term impact should be drawn cautiously. Future multi-institutional studies with extended observation and embedded learning analytics are recommended to evaluate scalability, personalization, and sustained learning effects.

In summary, G-Jump represents a feasible, innovative, and pedagogically sound formative assessment model. It offers a practical framework for fostering competency, self-regulation, and lifelong learning—core objectives of modern medical education.
