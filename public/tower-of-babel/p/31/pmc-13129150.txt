Burden of Respiratory Syncytial Virus in Older ADults in Nagasaki, Japan (Nagasaki ROAD): Preliminary Results (April 2024–March 2025) of a Prospective, Multicenter, Epidemiological Study
Authors: Takahiro Takazono, Norihito Kaku, Naoki Hosogaya, Kiyoyasu Fukushima, Ryota Takao, Naoki Iwanaga, Takuya Izumikawa, Mai Kato, Kentaro Tajima, Yufan Ho, Katsunori Yanagihara, Hiroshi Mukae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOlder adults (OAs) and individuals with underlying comorbidities are at increased risk for severe respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) disease (e.g., lower respiratory tract disease [LRTD]) and related adverse outcomes. However, RSV is underdiagnosed and underestimated in OAs, posing a growing concern in super-aged societies like Japan.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, epidemiological study (Nagasaki ROAD) aimed to enroll approximately 2700 participants aged ≥ 50 years with acute respiratory infection (ARI) seeking care at outpatient/emergency/inpatient settings at 15 medical institutions/hospitals/clinics in Nagasaki and its surroundings. Enrolled participants were followed for 180 days. RSV positivity in nasopharyngeal swab samples, detected by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, served as the primary diagnosis of RSV-related ARI/LRTD (RSV-ARI/LRTD). Hospital-wide and non-hospital-wide screening were adopted based on site-level operational realities and clinical workflows.ResultsData at enrollment (baseline/day 1) were analyzed from 1056 participants who were recruited between April 1, 2024 and March 31, 2025. The approximate positivity rate of RSV-ARI was 5.0% (n = 53), peaking from June to September 2024. The approximate positivity rate of SARS-CoV-2-ARI was 25.4% (n = 268), and influenza-ARI was 19.6% (n = 207). Hospitalization at or within 7 days of day 1 was observed in 20.8% (n = 11) of participants with RSV-ARI, 13.1% (n = 35) with SARS-CoV-2-ARI, and 13.0% (n = 27) with influenza-ARI. The most common comorbidities-of-interest were asthma (17.0% [n = 9]), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (15.1% [n = 8]), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (7.5% [n = 4]) in participants with RSV-ARI. The observed need for oxygen supplementation was higher in participants with RSV-ARI (13.2% [n = 7]) than with SARS-CoV-2-ARI (3.4% [n = 9]) or influenza-ARI (8.7% [n = 18]).ConclusionsThese year 1 results contextualize the RSV disease burden in OAs aged ≥ 50 years in Nagasaki, Japan, providing an important basis for understanding RSV in Japanese OAs; findings complement existing evidence. Future analyses of all clinical outcomes through 180-day follow-up could further inform targeted prevention strategies in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01312-9.Keywords: Acute respiratory infection, Disease burden, Epidemiology, Japan, Older adults, Positivity rate, Prospective cohort study, Respiratory syncytial virus, Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, Upper and lower respiratory tract disease

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? In super-aged societies like Japan, the lack of routine monitoring of adult respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) contributes to its underdiagnosis and underestimation in older adults, which hinders the understanding of RSV disease seasonality, severity, healthcare burden, and limits the ability to assess the true impact of preventive measures (e.g., vaccination) for RSV in this vulnerable population.The Nagasaki ROAD study was designed to complement the limited data on RSV among older adults locally, by capturing RSV burden (medically attended) across a wider range of outcomes (e.g., hospitalizations, RSV-related complications, and exacerbation of underlying conditions) and healthcare settings (i.e., outpatient, emergency, and inpatient settings) across RSV seasons over 2 years in Nagasaki, Japan. What was learned from this study? This year 1 analysis (April 2024–March 2025) reported an approximate baseline (day 1) 5.0% positivity rate of RSV-related acute respiratory infection in older adults aged ≥50 years in Nagasaki, Japan, suggesting that RSV could contribute to respiratory illness among symptomatic individuals.Given the local considerations in routine clinical practice, resources, and infrastructure, conducting population-based prevalence studies is challenging; thus, this study analyzed the approximate positivity rate of RSV among medically attended symptomatic individuals as a feasible alternative for understanding RSV burden.Findings provide an important basis to better understand RSV in this local population, with future analyses of all clinical outcomes through a 180-day follow-up period expected to provide a more complete characterization to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection is known to occur frequently during childhood, causing considerable morbidity and mortality in newborns, infants, and young children [1–3]. Increasingly, RSV is also being recognized globally as a respiratory virus of concern affecting individuals beyond childhood [4–7].

Particularly, older adults and individuals with underlying chronic medical conditions are at increased risk for RSV disease and RSV-related adverse outcomes, including hospitalizations and complications such as pneumonia, exacerbation of asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or heart failure [8, 9]. Such an increased risk may generally be attributed to immunosenescence, underlying comorbidities, and frailty [10, 11], resulting in a greater RSV burden in this population [12, 13]. RSV is therefore a growing concern in super-aged societies such as Japan [14].

RSV is frequently undetected and considerably underestimated among older adults in clinical practice due to non-specific symptoms, lack of RSV-specific antiviral treatment, and lack of routine testing [3]. Highly sensitive, simple, and quick tests could improve diagnostic precision for acute respiratory infections (ARIs) [15]. Additionally, in contrast to the well-established surveillance systems in Japan for pediatric RSV cases, as well as for other respiratory infections in the general population (e.g., COVID-19 Registry Japan [COVIREGI-JP], the REpository of Data and Biospecimen of INfectious Disease [REBIND] Project, and various influenza-like illness surveillance networks) [16, 17], adult RSV cases lack routine monitoring locally. Consequently, RSV seasonality, severity, and healthcare burden in older Japanese adults are poorly understood, and characterizing the true impact of RSV preventive measures (e.g., vaccination) in this population remains challenging.

Furthermore, comprehensive epidemiological studies on RSV in older adults locally are limited [6]. Recent studies have found RSV rates to be ~ 2–3% for those aged ≥ 60 or ≥ 65 years through 2019–2024 [18–20], and only one surveillance study examined RSV-positive hospitalizations through 2022–2024, reporting no notable seasonal patterns [20]. By restricting RSV case identification to selected healthcare settings (e.g., inpatients only) [15, 18–21], these studies have limited generalizability across different care settings.

The present Nagasaki ROAD study aims to address gaps in the broader landscape (e.g., occurrence, hospitalizations, and complications) of RSV-ARI cases in adults aged ≥ 50 years seeking medical advice locally within outpatient, emergency, and inpatient settings (herein referred to as “medically attended”), across RSV seasons over 2 years. In this paper, we present the baseline (day 1) data of participants enrolled in year 1 from April 1, 2024 to March 31, 2025, and provide initial insights on the patient profile, comorbidity pattern, and clinical burden of RSV in Japan’s older population.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective, multicenter, epidemiological study is enrolling older adults aged ≥ 50 years with medically attended ARI between April 1, 2024 and March 31, 2026, from 15 medical institutions, hospitals, or clinics in Nagasaki and its surroundings (Figure S1).

Japan has one of the most rapidly aging populations worldwide [14], and Nagasaki is a region with a notably high proportion of older adults [22]. As of October 1, 2024, older adults aged ≥ 65 years have accounted for approximately one-third of the 1,250,705 inhabitants in Nagasaki Prefecture [22]. In addition to its demographic profile, Nagasaki is a relatively small prefecture featuring unique inter-dependencies and inter-connectivity of healthcare infrastructure and delivery systems [23]. The robust inter-hospital collaborations in the area facilitate continuity of care and follow-ups with patients after their initial visits, supporting longitudinal data collection and reducing loss to follow-up. Furthermore, as a key region promoting academic research and shaping healthcare policy across Japan [24], Nagasaki is suitable for enrollment of study participants.

Generally, participant recruitment into the Nagasaki ROAD study focused on identification and enrollment of medically attended ARI cases presenting to diverse healthcare settings within the Nagasaki area. As such, recruitment pathways encompassed a variety of approaches adapted to site-level operations and patient flow to sustain recruitment of a representative, real-world sample. These approaches included department-based screening, hospital-wide screening, and multi-department collaborative recruitment.

The overall study design is presented in Figure S2. After enrollment (baseline/day 1), participants were followed for 180 days and were considered to have completed the study upon completion of all scheduled assessments on day 180. Baseline (day 1) data are reported in this paper for participants enrolled in year 1 (April 1, 2024–March 31, 2025).

Study participants did not receive study-specific treatments and would continue receiving their routine medications and medical care from their primary treating physicians during the study.

Study Population

Participants aged ≥ 50 years with medically attended ARI at outpatient, emergency, and inpatient settings were continuously enrolled based on the pre-specified eligibility criteria (Table S1). The enrolled population included individuals with comorbidities of interest that are potential risk factors for severe RSV disease, including chronic pulmonary conditions (e.g., COPD, asthma), cardiac disorders (e.g., heart failure, myocardial infarction), stroke, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney or liver diseases, cancer, and immunocompromised states due to chronic condition or medication [25].

An episode of ARI was defined as the presence of ≥ 2 respiratory signs/symptoms for ≥ 12 h, or ≥ 1 respiratory sign/symptom and 1 systemic sign/symptom for ≥ 12 h. An LRTD episode was defined as the presence of ≥ 2 lower respiratory signs/symptoms (including ≥ 1 lower respiratory sign) for ≥ 12 h, or ≥ 3 lower respiratory symptoms for ≥ 12 h. Detailed descriptions of respiratory signs/symptoms included in the case definition of ARI and LRTD are provided in Table S2.

Data Collection

Biological Samples and Laboratory Assays

Nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected from all participants at day 1 (time window: day 1–7) and initially frozen at each participating facility. These samples were thereafter transferred to and analyzed by the Department of Laboratory Medicine, Nagasaki University Hospital, where the presence of RSV and other respiratory viruses/bacteria was detected using a qualitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) multiplex assay (BIOFIRE® Respiratory 2.1 plus Panel [26]). Participating facilities were blinded to the assay results, with clinical diagnosis and treatment decisions made independently by the attending physicians at each facility. Positivity of RSV and/or other respiratory viruses/bacteria in nasopharyngeal swab samples served as the primary diagnosis of RSV-ARI/LRTD and/or non-RSV-ARI, respectively, at enrollment.

Variables

All study variables (Appendix 1) were extracted from electronic medical records, screening log sheets, laboratory tests, and questionnaires, and documented in electronic case report forms (eCRF). Study monitoring was performed to ensure an accurate and complete recording of source data in the eCRF. Table S3 summarizes the timepoints at which study activities were performed for the record and assessment of study variables during the 180-day follow-up period from day 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed were the occurrence of RSV-related complications, hospitalizations, and deaths in all participants with RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD through follow-up.

The secondary outcomes included occurrence of RSV-ARI/LRTD episodes, RSV-ARI/LRTD-related signs/symptoms, and ARI caused by non-RSV respiratory viruses/bacteria (including single/co-infection); score of health-related quality of life assessed using the 12-Item Short-Form Health Survey Version 2 (SF-12v2™ Health Survey © 2009 by QualityMetric Incorporated and Shunichi Fukuhara. All rights reserved. SF-12® is a trademark of Medical Outcomes Trust [SF-12v2 Acute, Japanese Version 2.0]) [27] in all participants, including hospitalized participants; occurrence of hospitalizations, RSV-related complications, ARI sign/symptoms, and HCRU in hospitalized participants with serologically confirmed RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD; and with stratification by non-RSV respiratory viruses/bacteria, occurrence of ARI symptoms/signs, complications, hospitalizations, and deaths in all participants. The exploratory outcome assessed the factors associated with the occurrence of RSV-ARI/LRTD, complications, and hospitalizations.

In this paper, baseline (day 1) data for year 1 participants are reported for the positivity rate of RSV-ARI/LRTD and non-RSV respiratory viruses/bacteria, monthly distribution of ARI episode onset, and clinical symptoms/signs reported during ARI episodes; all other primary, secondary, and exploratory outcomes planned will be analyzed at future data cuts. Given that enrolment was based on convenience sampling of those voluntarily seeking care and consenting to participate, rather than population-based, the reported positivity rates are approximates of the proportion of positive cases with RSV or non-RSV respiratory pathogens among those recruited in the study cohort.

Statistical Analysis

An appropriate target sample size was deduced by assessing the precision for the sample sizes of 2000, 3000, 4000, and 5000 participants. For example, with an expected 4.0% rate of RSV-related hospitalization, a sample size of 3000 participants would yield a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.94–10.08% (exact; Clopper–Pearson); with an expected 15.0% rate of RSV-related complication, a sample size of 3000 participants would yield a 95% CI of 8.14–24.46% (exact; Clopper–Pearson). The complete list of assumptions applied for the estimation is available in Appendix 2. A minimum enrollment of 2700 participants was estimated to provide sufficient precision.

For the overall study, the analysis set included all enrolled participants, excluding those with invalid data and/or invalid informed consent. All primary and secondary outcomes would be reported as descriptive statistics. Imputation of missing data was not conducted. All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software Version 9.4.

For the current analysis of baseline (day 1) data for year 1 participants (April 1, 2024–March 31, 2025), the analysis set included participants for whom RT-PCR RSV results were available, excluding those with protocol deviations that led to exclusion.

As participant screening and enrollment varied by site based on site-level operations and clinical workflows, additional stratification by screening methodology explored whether such differences affected recruitment patterns. Participants were stratified by hospital-wide screening sites versus department/investigator-specific, non-hospital-wide screening sites. Among all 15 participating sites, six used the hospital-wide screening approach. Participants were also stratified into subgroups of inpatients (i.e., participants who were hospitalized due to ARI at or within 7 days of day 1) versus outpatients (i.e., participants who were not hospitalized due to ARI at or within 7 days of day 1).

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles derived from the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Participants; international guidelines including the Declaration of Helsinki and Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) International Ethical Guidelines; Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Code of Federal Regulations Title 21; and all applicable regulations and local laws governing research involving human participants. Approval for the study protocol was obtained from the Nagasaki University Hospital Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 23112004). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, and participants’ personal data were de-identified to the sponsor and retained under local data protection law and privacy legislation.

Results

Through year 1 of Nagasaki ROAD (April 1, 2024–March 31, 2025), 1082 participants aged ≥ 50 years with ARI were enrolled. After excluding participants with invalid informed consent, ineligibility (including ARI symptoms/signs determined to be caused by aspiration pneumonia), and no available RT-PCR RSV results, a total of 1056 enrolled participants were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Participant disposition for enrollment between April 2024 and March 2025. ARI acute respiratory infection, RSV respiratory syncytial virus, RT-PCR reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Of the 1056 participants analyzed, 61.5% (n = 649) and 38.5% (n = 407) were enrolled via hospital-wide and non-hospital-wide screening, respectively.

RSV Positivity Rate (Approximate) and Monthly Distribution of Onset

The approximate positivity rate of RSV (RSV-ARI +) was 5.0% (n = 53), of which 24.5% (n = 13) reported LRTD (RSV-LRTD +). RSV-ARI + peaked in the overall population from June to September 2024 (Fig. 2a), with similar trends observed in participants recruited through hospital-wide and non-hospital-wide screening (Fig. 2b); RSV cases rose and declined sharply among outpatients compared with inpatients (Fig. 2c).

Inpatients were defined as participants who were hospitalized due to ARI at or within 7 days of day 1, whereas outpatients were participants who were not hospitalized due to ARI at or within 7 days of day 1. ARI acute respiratory infection, RSV respiratory syncytial virus

Among 649 participants enrolled via hospital-wide screening and 407 enrolled via non-hospital-wide screening, RSV-ARI + was 4.6% (n = 30) and 5.7% (n = 23), respectively, and of which RSV-LRTD + was 26.7% (n = 8) and 21.7% (n = 5), respectively.

Positivity Rate (Approximate) of Other Respiratory Viruses/Bacteria

Overall, non-RSV respiratory viruses/bacteria were detected in 65.2% (n = 689) of all analyzed participants (Table S4). Notably, the approximate positivity rate of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2-ARI +) was 25.4% (n = 268), of which 7.1% (n = 19) reported LRTD (SARS-CoV-2-LRTD +). The approximate positivity rate of influenza A or B (influenza-ARI +) was 19.6% (n = 207), of which 14.0% (n = 29) reported LRTD (influenza-LRTD +). Across participants enrolled via hospital-wide versus non-hospital-wide screening, SARS-CoV-2-ARI/LRTD + were largely similar, whereas a notably higher influenza-ARI + (24.2% [n = 157] versus 12.3% [n = 50]) and a lower influenza-LRTD + (8.3% [n = 13] versus 32.0% [n = 16]) was observed for hospital-wide screening compared with non-hospital-wide screening.

Co-infections in RSV-ARI + participants were due to SARS-CoV-2 (3.8%; n = 2), human rhinovirus/enterovirus (1.9%; n = 1), or parainfluenza virus 3 (1.9%; n = 1), with no co-infections due to influenza A or B detected; none of these co-infections were in RSV-LRTD + participants.

In RSV-ARI + participants overall (LRTD and non-LRTD), the mean (standard deviation [SD]) age was 67.3 (10.3) years, with the majority (37.7%; n = 20) aged 70–79 years. Of 53 RSV-ARI + participants, 50.9% (n = 27) were female, 49.1% (n = 26) were male, 34.0% (n = 18) lived with family members having ARI symptoms within the past 2 weeks (3.8% [n = 2] with children aged < 5 years), and 88.7% (n = 47) had any past/current medical conditions. The most common conditions of interest included asthma (17.0%; n = 9), COPD (15.1%; n = 8), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (7.5%; n = 4). The majority of participants were vaccinated with COVID-19 vaccines (77.4%; n = 41) or influenza vaccines (54.7%; n = 29), while none received RSV vaccines.

In SARS-CoV-2-ARI + participants overall (LRTD and non-LRTD), the mean (SD) age was 65.5 (9.4) years, with the majority (32.5%; n = 87) aged 50–59 years. Of 268 SARS-CoV-2-ARI + participants, 51.5% (n = 138) were female, 48.5% (n = 130) were male, 27.6% (n = 74) lived with family members having ARI symptoms within the past 2 weeks (0.7% [n = 2] with children aged < 5 years), and 79.9% (n = 214) had any past/current medical conditions. The most common conditions of interest included asthma (9.0%; n = 24), type 2 diabetes mellitus (3.7%; n = 10), and COPD (2.2%; n = 6). The majority of participants were vaccinated with COVID-19 vaccines (78.4%; n = 210) or influenza vaccines (57.1%; n = 153), while 0.4% (n = 1) received RSV vaccines.

In influenza-ARI + participants overall (LRTD and non-LRTD), the mean (SD) age was 64.0 (10.1) years; the majority (38.6%; n = 80) were aged 50–59 years. Of 207 influenza-ARI + participants, 50.2% (n = 104) were female, 49.8% (n = 103) were male, 27.5% (n = 57) lived with family members having ARI symptoms within the past 2 weeks (2.4% [n = 5] with children aged < 5 years), and 82.1% (n = 170) had any past/current medical conditions. The most common conditions of interest included asthma (11.6%; n = 24), type 2 diabetes mellitus (7.7%; n = 16), and chronic kidney disease (2.9%; n = 6). The majority of participants were vaccinated with COVID-19 vaccines (54.1%; n = 112) or influenza vaccines (42.0%; n = 87), while none received RSV vaccines.

Characteristics of RSV-ARI + , SARS-CoV-2-ARI +, and influenza-ARI + participants recruited via hospital-wide versus non-hospital-wide screening were largely similar to their respective overall populations, except for the notably different proportion of participants who were hospitalized at or within 7 days of day 1 (i.e., hospitalization rate on day 1).

Overall, hospitalization rate on day 1 was 20.8% (n = 11) for RSV-ARI + participants, 13.1% (n = 35) for SARS-CoV-2-ARI + participants, and 13.0% (n = 27) for influenza-ARI + participants. Depending on the screening approach, hospitalization rates differed notably for RSV-ARI + (13.3% with hospital-wide screening, 30.4% with non-hospital-wide screening, and 20.8% overall) and influenza-ARI + participants (7.0% with hospital-wide screening, 32.0% with non-hospital-wide screening, and 13.0% overall).

Clinical Symptoms/Signs Reported During ARI Episodes

In all participants with ARI overall, upper respiratory symptoms, lower respiratory symptoms/signs, and systemic respiratory symptoms/signs were reported in 75.9% (n = 801), 92.0% (n = 971), and 85.2% (n = 900) of participants, respectively. These symptoms/signs were reported in 83.1% (n = 539), 91.8% (n = 596), and 86.6% (n = 562) of participants recruited specifically through hospital-wide screening, and 64.4% (n = 262), 92.1% (n = 375), and 83.0% (n = 338) of participants recruited specifically through non-hospital-wide screening.

The list of symptoms/signs reported overall, and stratified by hospital-wide and non-hospital-wide screening are presented in Table 4. Specifically, the need for oxygen supplementation was observed to be overall higher in RSV-ARI + participants (13.2% [n = 7]) than in SARS-CoV-2-ARI + (3.4% [n = 9]) or influenza-ARI + participants (8.7% [n = 18]). Additionally, fever was reportedly lower in RSV-ARI + participants (24.5% [n = 13]) than in SARS-CoV-2-ARI + (47.4% [n = 127]) or influenza-ARI + participants (64.3% [n = 133]).

Discussion

Findings from this baseline (day 1) analysis of year 1 participants (April 1, 2024–March 31, 2025) in Nagasaki ROAD contextualize the RSV disease burden and severity among older adults aged ≥ 50 years in the Nagasaki area of Japan, with an approximate 5.0% positivity rate for medically attended RSV.

At first visit, the higher hospitalization rate and need for oxygen supplementation with RSV-ARI (20.8% and 13.2%), than with COVID-19 (13.1% and 3.4%) or influenza (13.0% and 8.7%), reflected a greater clinical severity and healthcare burden from RSV, which could partly be attributed to the low rates of RSV vaccination meant to prevent severe disease [28]. Although co-infections are not uncommon in respiratory illness, the majority of RSV cases appeared unaccompanied by other pathogens in our detection. Future data readouts from this study are expected to provide more complete results and insights.

Baseline characteristics such as cohabitation with family members with recent ARI symptoms and relevant medical history highlight the importance of RSV prevention. For example, 3.8% of participants with RSV-ARI were living with children aged < 5 years, suggesting a possible child-to-older-adult transmission route that remains to be verified.

Additionally, as most participants with RSV-ARI (88.7%) reported past or current medical conditions, underlying comorbidities may be of interest in older adults with RSV given their association with greater RSV disease burden [8]. To that end, older adults with comorbidities may benefit from increased recognition and RSV prevention initiatives.

Recruitment practices across sites in Nagasaki ROAD were heterogeneous, though all sites consistently adhered to participant eligibility criteria. In this baseline (day 1) analysis of year 1 participants, positivity rates (approximate), patient characteristics, and RSV symptoms/signs appeared broadly similar across screening approaches, except for the notably higher hospitalization rate at the first visit with non-hospital-wide screening (30.4%) than hospital-wide screening (13.3%) for participants with RSV-ARI. This difference likely reflects more frequent non-hospital-wide screening in specialized settings, such as emergency departments, where first-visit hospitalization rates are high due to symptom severity. In contrast, hospital-wide screening likely captured more outpatient cases, which may have been less severe. Since other patient characteristics were similar across screening approaches, selection bias from physician availability, infrastructure, or resources may not be pertinent.

Prior to Nagasaki ROAD, studies on RSV burden in Japan were limited in scale relative to Western countries [9, 12], and had limited generalizability to Japanese adults as RSV data were mostly derived secondary to influenza, community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), or other respiratory infection-specific outcomes [20, 29–37]. In studies that derived RSV data secondary to influenza, case definitions often required fever, which is common for influenza-like illness but not RSV-ARI [38]); our results show lower fever rates for RSV-ARI (24.5%) than COVID-19 (47.4%) or influenza (64.3%), highlighting the differences in fever presentation. As varying case definitions could underestimate RSV prevalence/positivity, future RSV surveillance methods could consider a more clinically relevant definition not requiring fever to capture RSV presentations more accurately, a data gap currently addressed by Nagasaki ROAD.

Correspondingly, the approximate positivity rate of medically attended RSV at baseline (day 1) in year 1 of Nagasaki ROAD (5.0%) aligns with the global prevalence of 1–10% in older adults aged ≥ 50 years [39], highlighting RSV as a likely public health issue among older adults in Japan. This rate was numerically higher than those previously reported across local studies from 2019–2024 (2.4–2.8%) [18–20]. These numerical differences may reflect the expected disease severity in studied healthcare settings, swab type, RSV case definition, analysis duration, and COVID-19-related health policy changes. Nonetheless, findings from Nagasaki ROAD complement previous studies and continue to build the evidence base for RSV burden among local older adults.

Despite prior demonstration of RSV burden, it is an under-recognized virus in older adults in high-income countries [40]. Although recent evidence has associated RSV with similar or even greater severity than COVID-19 or influenza in hospitalized patients aged ≥ 60 years [41], there is an absence of adult RSV surveillance systems similar to those instituted for COVID-19 and influenza [16, 17]. The resultant systemic underestimation of RSV may translate into poorer clinical management in older adults. Therefore, there is a substantial unmet need to improve recognition of RSV as a key respiratory virus of interest in Japanese older adults and its prioritization in public health and clinical efforts.

Older adults may have increased RSV-related risk due to specific comorbidities, particularly cardio-pulmonary conditions. A systematic review reported that RSV-infected adults with asthma or COPD had significantly higher hospitalization risks than those without (adjusted incidence rate ratios: asthma, 6.7–8.2; COPD, 9.6–9.7) [42]. RSV infections could also worsen existing comorbidities and increase healthcare resource use. For example, RSV-hospitalized adults aged ≥ 50 years with pre-existing cardiovascular disease showed a higher risk of in-hospital acute cardiac events (adjusted risk ratio [ARR]: 3.51; though attribution to RSV alone is uncertain), ICU admission (ARR: 1.54), invasive ventilation (ARR: 2.00), and in-hospital death (ARR: 1.77) [43]. Overall, comorbid older adults bear a heightened RSV burden due to both infection and exacerbation of underlying conditions.

Cardio-pulmonary conditions are highly prevalent and of particular interest as comorbidities in older adults in Japan. COPD prevalence in those aged ≥ 65 years was 16.9% [44], and for congestive heart failure, it was 11.9% in ages 60–84 years and 25.3% in ages ≥ 85 years, respectively [45]. Nagasaki ROAD similarly shows high prevalence of comorbidities in older Japanese adults, with 17.0% and 39.6% of participants with RSV-ARI reporting past/current cardiac and respiratory disorders, respectively. Since these comorbidities are often age-related, their prevalence is expected to rise with Japan’s rapidly aging population [14], placing more individuals at risk of severe RSV infections. As RSV vaccination has been increasingly recommended for preventing severe outcomes in older adults at increased risk [4, 46], this subgroup may benefit from targeted preventive strategies.

Limitations to the Nagasaki ROAD study should be acknowledged. Firstly, only Nagasaki-area sites were selected, which may have limited the generalizability to the broader Japanese population. Nevertheless, Nagasaki includes urban and semi-urban areas with population densities comparable to other regions, making the selected sites reasonable proxies for areas with similar healthcare coverage and city composition. Estimates of RSV infection occurrence in Nagasaki ROAD therefore remain relevant and complement existing local research on RSV burden in contextualizing healthcare policies and increasing disease awareness.

Selection bias may have also arisen from treatment-seeking behavior in patients, recruitment patterns in real-world clinical practice, and the need for nasopharyngeal swabbing. As individuals with mild or non-specific symptoms may not seek medical advice and consequently may not be enrolled into Nagasaki ROAD, the true RSV positivity rate may have been underestimated. Additionally, the actual sample size in year 1 was smaller than initially estimated due to real-world recruitment challenges. As a result, study estimates may have yielded wider confidence intervals that reflect reduced precision, and findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly for smaller subgroups of patients. Nevertheless, as a descriptive observational study, this did not compromise data validity, and the data remain valuable in providing real-world insights into the RSV burden. Patients with critical illnesses affecting one’s ability to provide consent (e.g., dementia or stroke) [47] were precluded, potentially underrepresenting older adults with these illnesses. In super-aged societies like Japan, such illnesses are not uncommon, with dementia being projected to affect more than 20% of adults aged ≥ 65 years in 2030 [48]. Lastly, patient characteristics may differ between those who consent to participate and be swabbed, versus those who do not.

The Nagasaki ROAD study is ongoing, and the full year 1 and year 2 data analyses (i.e., 180-day follow-up period completed) will be published in the next few years. These findings will enhance the understanding of RSV burden among older adults in Nagasaki, with important implications for public health efforts locally. Such efforts include RSV-related education and prevention strategies like vaccination, as well as inclusion of older adults in the existing surveillance system for comprehensive and accurate characterization of RSV occurrence in at-risk populations.

Conclusions

Baseline results of Nagasaki ROAD from April 2024–March 2025 revealed an approximate 5.0% positivity rate of medically attended RSV, a 20.8% hospitalization rate, and a 13.2% oxygen supplementation rate for RSV upon first visit in older adults aged ≥ 50 years with ARI in the Nagasaki area. These findings provide context for understanding the local disease burden. Although not fully generalizable to the broader population, findings highlight the patient profile for RSV and serve as a foundational step toward a better understanding of RSV, its burden, and related patient characteristics in Japanese older adults. Future analyses could provide a complete characterization of the clinical impact of RSV to inform preventative strategies for this target population.
