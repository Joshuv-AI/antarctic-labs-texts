Context-dependent modulation of the TAK1–MAPK axis by ginsenoside-based formulations along the NAFLD–HCC continuum
Authors: Hyun Kyung Lim, Jae-Yong Oh, Kyu Nung Chung, Byong Chul Yoo, Jae Youl Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a dynamic metabolic liver disorder that can progress to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Stress-responsive signaling pathways, particularly the transforming growth factor-β–activated kinase 1 (TAK1)–mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) axis, play context-dependent roles in hepatocyte survival, disease adaptation, and tumorigenesis.MethodsTwo ginseng-derived formulations with distinct biological properties were investigated: a non-cytotoxic Rg5/Rk1–triterpene complex and a cytotoxic Rg3/paclitaxel liposomal formulation. Cytotoxicity and signaling responses were evaluated in HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells, MKN1 gastric cancer cells, and HEK293T cells. Cell viability was assessed using MTT assays. TAK1–MAPK signaling was analyzed by Western blotting and overexpression experiments, and cellular thermal shift assays were performed to examine target engagement.ResultsRg5/Rk1–triterpene enhanced TAK1 autophosphorylation and activated downstream MAPK signaling without inducing cytotoxicity, consistent with an adaptive stress-response profile. This modulation was accompanied by selective upregulation of the RNA editing enzyme ADAR2, while global translational regulators such as eIF6 remained unchanged. In contrast, Rg3/paclitaxel liposomes induced cytotoxic stress and suppressed MEK–ERK signaling in hepatic and gastric cancer cells, supporting a pro-apoptotic mechanism. These opposing effects demonstrate formulation-dependent regulation of the TAK1–MAPK signaling axis.ConclusionThese findings suggest that ginseng-derived formulations differentially modulate the TAK1–MAPK pathway and may represent a context-dependent signaling framework relevant to different stages of liver disease progression.Keywords: Ginsenoside Rg5, Ginsenoside Rg3, TAK1, NAFLD, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), recently reframed under the broader concept of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide, affecting approximately 25–30% of the global adult population [[1], [2], [3]]. Its prevalence continues to rise in parallel with obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and other components of metabolic syndrome [1,2,4]. NAFLD is now recognized as a progressive multisystem disease that substantially increases liver-related mortality and contributes to cardiovascular and malignancy-associated risks [1,5]. Importantly, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) associated with NAFLD may develop even in the absence of advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis, posing significant challenges to current surveillance strategies and highlighting the need for early and sustained therapeutic intervention [6].

Despite extensive research efforts, effective pharmacological therapies for NAFLD remain limited. Current clinical management relies primarily on lifestyle modification, including weight loss, dietary intervention, and increased physical activity; however, long-term adherence and durability of response are often poor [7,8]. In addition, increasing evidence highlights substantial inter-individual and stage-dependent heterogeneity in NAFLD pathogenesis and progression, which limits the effectiveness of uniform therapeutic approaches across diverse patient populations [9].

Although multiple pharmacological agents targeting metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, or fibrosis are under active clinical investigation, no single therapy has demonstrated consistent efficacy across the full spectrum of NAFLD progression [10]. These limitations have prompted growing interest in mechanism-based therapeutic strategies that enhance hepatocellular stress tolerance and resilience, rather than inducing nonspecific cytotoxicity.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways play central roles in integrating metabolic stress and inflammatory cues in steatohepatitis and liver tumorigenesis [[11], [12], [13]]. Among upstream regulators, transforming growth factor-β–activated kinase 1 (TAK1) functions as a critical signaling hub linking cytokine receptors, pattern-recognition receptors, and intracellular stress-response pathways [14,15]. TAK1 is a member of the MAP3K family and plays a central role in coordinating inflammatory and stress-responsive signaling pathways [[14], [15], [16]]. Upon activation by upstream stimuli such as cytokines, Toll-like receptors, and metabolic stress signals, TAK1 phosphorylates downstream MAP kinase kinases (MAP2Ks), which subsequently activate MAPK pathways including ERK, JNK, and p38 [14,16]. Through this signaling cascade, TAK1 regulates diverse cellular processes such as inflammatory responses, cell survival, apoptosis, and tissue remodeling [[15], [16], [17]]. Dysregulation of TAK1 signaling has been implicated in chronic inflammatory diseases, metabolic disorders, and tumorigenesis, highlighting its importance as a context-dependent regulator in liver disease progression [16,17]. Accumulating evidence indicates that TAK1 exhibits context-dependent roles during liver disease progression, acting as a protective regulator under early metabolic stress while contributing to pathological signaling during advanced stages of steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and hepatocarcinogenesis [[15], [16], [17]].

Ginseng (Panax ginseng Meyer) has long been used in traditional medicine for metabolic and inflammatory disorders, and modern pharmacological studies have identified ginsenosides as its principal bioactive constituents [18,19]. Among these, ginsenoside Rg3 has been extensively characterized for its anticancer properties, including inhibition of angiogenesis, modulation of the tumor microenvironment, and sensitization of cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents such as paclitaxel [20,21]. Accordingly, liposomal formulations combining Rg3 with paclitaxel have been developed to enhance tumor targeting while mitigating systemic toxicity [22,23].

In contrast, ginsenosides Rg5 and its isomer Rk1 exhibit relatively low cytotoxicity while possessing potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective activities mediated through modulation of NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways [24,25]. When combined with triterpene-rich extracts derived from Achyranthes bidentata, such formulations may influence intracellular stress-response signaling without compromising cell viability, a property that is particularly advantageous in chronic metabolic liver diseases requiring long-term preservation of hepatocyte function [26].

In this study, we investigated two ginseng-derived formulations with distinct biological profiles—Rg5/Rk1–triterpene and Rg3/paclitaxel liposomes—and examined their effects in hepatocellular and cancer cell models. By focusing on the TAK1–MAPK signaling axis and its downstream regulatory pathways, we aimed to establish a stage-specific therapeutic framework spanning NAFLD progression to hepatocellular carcinoma.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Rg3/paclitaxel liposomes

Paclitaxel (120 mg), ginsenoside Rg3 (180 mg), and phosphatidylcholine (1200 mg) were dissolved in 10 mL of an organic solvent mixture composed of ethanol (100%) and chloroform at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. The solution was subjected to rotary evaporation under reduced pressure at 40 °C for 30 min to completely remove the organic solvents and form a uniform lipid film. The dried lipid film was hydrated with 24 mL of a 5% (w/v) glucose solution, followed by gentle agitation. The resulting suspension was sonicated using short ultrasonic pulses (5–10 pulses at approximately 2 s intervals) to facilitate vesicle dispersion. The liposomal suspension was subsequently freeze-dried to obtain a powdered formulation. The final Rg3/paclitaxel liposomes exhibited an average particle diameter of approximately 100 nm and were reconstituted immediately prior to experimental use.

Preparation of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene complex

The Rg5/Rk1–triterpene formulation was prepared as an emulsified mixture according to the composition shown in Table 1. Ginsenoside Rg5/Rk1 and Achyranthes bidentata extract standardized to contain 20% triterpenes were combined with excipients, including egg powder, maltodextrin (DE 10–15), and water, based on the total emulsion content. All components were thoroughly mixed to generate a homogeneous emulsion. The resulting mixture was freeze-dried and subsequently pulverized to obtain the final emulsified powder. The prepared Rg5/Rk1–triterpene complex was stored under dry conditions and reconstituted immediately before use in cell-based experiments.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity assay

HepG2 (human hepatocellular carcinoma), MKN1 (human gastric carcinoma), and HEK293T (human embryonic kidney) cells were cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For cytotoxicity assays, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at densities adjusted according to incubation time to maintain logarithmic growth. Specifically, HepG2 cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells/mL for 24-h assays, 2.5 × 105 cells/mL for 48-h assays, and 1.25 × 105 cells/mL for 72-h assays, with a volume of 100 μL per well. After approximately 18 h of attachment, cells were treated with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene or Rg3/paclitaxel at final concentrations of 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, or 100 μg/mL and incubated for 24, 48, or 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Following drug treatment, culture medium was removed, and MTT solution was added. Cell densities and incubation durations were adjusted according to the growth characteristics of each cell line to maintain logarithmic growth conditions and comparable cell confluence at the time of analysis.

Western blotting and overexpression experiments

For Western blot analysis, HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL (1.8 mL per well) and cultured for approximately 18 h prior to drug treatment. Cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene or Rg3/paclitaxel for 24 h, harvested, and lysed to obtain total cellular protein. Protein samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting to evaluate total and phosphorylated forms of TAK1, MAPKKs, and MAPKs. For overexpression experiments, HepG2 cells were seeded at 3 × 105 cells/mL in 6-well plates and transfected with plasmids encoding TAK1 using Lipofectamine 3000 (400 μL per well). After 24 h of expression, cells were treated with the indicated drug concentrations for an additional 24 h, followed by protein extraction and Western blot analysis.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

CETSA was performed to evaluate the interaction between Rg5/Rk1–triterpene and TAK1 based on thermal stabilization of the target protein. HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-cm dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL (3.8 mL per dish) and transfected with plasmids encoding TAK1 using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 24 h of expression, cells were treated with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene (25, 50, or 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and aliquoted into PCR tubes (100 μL per tube), with nine tubes prepared per experimental group. Samples were heated at temperatures ranging from 39 °C to 55 °C in 2 °C increments for 3 min at each temperature point. Following thermal treatment, cells underwent freeze–thaw cycles to release soluble proteins. Protein extracts were analyzed by Western blotting to assess TAK1 thermal stability and phosphorylation status.

Results

Cytotoxicity profiles of ginsenoside-based formulations in hepatic, gastric, and non-hepatic cell lines

To evaluate the safety and anticancer properties of the two ginsenoside-based formulations, cytotoxicity assays were performed in HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells, MKN1 gastric cancer cells, and HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells. HEK293T cells were included as a non-hepatic epithelial cell model to assess potential nonspecific cytotoxicity and to determine whether the observed effects were preferentially associated with cancer cells rather than general cellular toxicity. MKN1 gastric cancer cells were included to determine whether the cytotoxic effects of the formulations were restricted to hepatocellular carcinoma cells or represented a broader anticancer activity in epithelial cancer cells. Treatment with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene did not induce significant cytotoxicity in HEK293T cells at concentrations up to 100 μg/mL for up to 72 h, with cell viability remaining comparable to untreated controls across all time points (Fig. 1A). Similarly, MKN1 cells exhibited minimal sensitivity to Rg5/Rk1–triterpene, showing no substantial reduction in viability even at the highest concentration tested (Fig. 1B). Co-treatment of MKN1 cells with Rg3/paclitaxel and increasing concentrations of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene did not further enhance cytotoxicity relative to Rg3/paclitaxel alone, indicating an absence of nonspecific synergistic toxicity (Fig. 1C).

Cytotoxicity profiles of ginsenoside-based formulations in hepatic and cancer cell lines. HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells (A), MKN1 gastric cancer cells (B), and HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells (D, E) were treated with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene or Rg3/paclitaxel liposomes at the indicated concentrations for 24, 48, and 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using MTT assays and expressed as a percentage relative to untreated controls. Combination treatment of Rg3/paclitaxel with increasing concentrations of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene in MKN1 cells is shown in panel C. Rg5/Rk1–triterpene exhibited minimal cytotoxicity across all tested cell lines, whereas Rg3/paclitaxel induced time- and dose-dependent reductions in cell viability.

Consistent with these observations, HepG2 cells also tolerated Rg5/Rk1–triterpene treatment well, with no significant reduction in viability observed across concentrations and time points examined (Fig. 1D). In contrast, treatment with Rg3/paclitaxel liposomes resulted in pronounced, dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells, with marked decreases in cell viability observed after 48 and 72 h of exposure (Fig. 1E). Comparable cytotoxic effects were also observed in MKN1 gastric cancer cells (Fig. 1B), whereas HEK293T cells showed reduced sensitivity to Rg3/paclitaxel relative to cancer cell lines (Fig. 1A). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Rg5/Rk1–triterpene exhibits minimal intrinsic cytotoxicity, whereas Rg3/paclitaxel retains robust anticancer activity across epithelial cancer cell types.

Activation of the TAK1–MAPK signaling cascade by Rg5/Rk1–triterpene

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the non-cytotoxic yet biologically active profile of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene, we examined its effects on the TAK1–MAPK signaling axis in HepG2 cells. Treatment with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene resulted in a marked increase in autophosphorylation of TAK1 at Thr187, a critical activation site required for TAK1 kinase activity (Fig. 2A). Enhanced Thr187 phosphorylation was further confirmed in TAK1 overexpression experiments, supporting direct or proximal engagement of TAK1 by the formulation (Fig. 2B).

Rg5/Rk1–triterpene activates the TAK1–MAPK signaling pathway in HepG2 cells. HepG2 cells were treated with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene for 24 h, and phosphorylation of TAK1 at Thr187 was analyzed by immunoblotting (A). Direct activation of TAK1 was further examined using TAK1 overexpression experiments, which confirmed enhanced Thr187 phosphorylation upon compound treatment (B). Cellular thermal shift assays demonstrated increased thermal stability of TAK1 in the presence of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene, indicating direct target engagement (C). Activation of downstream MAPK signaling was assessed by measuring phosphorylation of MAPKKs (MKK3/6 and MKK4/7) (D) and MAPKs, including ERK1/2, JNK1–3, and p38 isoforms (E). These results indicate that Rg5/Rk1–triterpene activates the TAK1–MAPK signaling axis.

Direct target engagement was further assessed using cellular thermal shift assays, which demonstrated increased thermal stability of TAK1 in the presence of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene, consistent with compound–protein interaction (Fig. 2C). Downstream of TAK1 activation, phosphorylation of MAPK kinases MKK3/6 and MKK4/7 was significantly increased following treatment (Fig. 2D). Concordantly, robust activation of MAPKs, including ERK1/2, JNK1–3, and p38 isoforms, was observed (Fig. 2E), indicating broad activation of canonical TAK1-dependent MAPK signaling pathways.

Suppression of MAPK signaling by Rg3/paclitaxel and its association with cytotoxic response

In contrast to the signaling activation observed with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene, treatment with Rg3/paclitaxel resulted in pronounced suppression of MAPK signaling in HepG2 cells. Specifically, phosphorylation of MEK1/2 and its downstream effector ERK1/2 was reduced in a dose-dependent manner following Rg3/paclitaxel exposure (Fig. 3A), indicating inhibition of pro-survival MAPK signaling.

Phosphorylation levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 were analyzed by Western blotting to evaluate the effect of the formulation on MAPK signaling. (B) To determine whether inhibition of ERK signaling influences the cytotoxic response to Rg3/paclitaxel, HepG2 cells were pretreated with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene and subsequently treated with Rg3/paclitaxel in the presence or absence of the ERK inhibitor U0126 (25 μM). Cell viability was measured using the MTT assay. (C) The same experimental design described in panel (B) was applied to MKN1 gastric cancer cells to examine whether ERK pathway modulation similarly affects the cytotoxic response in another epithelial cancer cell model. To examine the functional consequence of ERK suppression, HepG2 cells were pretreated with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene to elevate ERK activity and subsequently treated with Rg3/paclitaxel in the presence or absence of the ERK pathway inhibitor U0126. Under these conditions, pharmacological inhibition of ERK signaling further reduced cell viability in Rg3/paclitaxel-treated HepG2 cells, supporting the interpretation that ERK pathway suppression contributes to the cytotoxic response (Fig. 3B).

A similar downward trend in cell viability was observed in MKN1 cells following combinatorial treatment, although the effect was less pronounced than that observed in HepG2 cells (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings support a role for MEK–ERK pathway modulation in shaping the cytotoxic response to Rg3/paclitaxel, with the clearest effect observed in HepG2 cells.

Differential modulation of RNA editing enzymes and translational regulators

To determine whether ginsenoside-based formulations modulate additional molecular regulators implicated in liver disease progression, we examined the expression of RNA editing enzymes and translational regulators in HepG2 cells. Treatment with Rg5/Rk1–triterpene resulted in modest upregulation of ADAR1 and a more pronounced, selective increase in ADAR2 expression (Fig. 4A). In contrast, Rg3/paclitaxel exerted minimal effects on ADAR2 levels, indicating distinct regulatory profiles between the two formulations.

Expression of the translational regulator eIF6 was not substantially altered by either Rg5/Rk1–triterpene or Rg3/paclitaxel treatment (Fig. 4B), suggesting that the observed biological effects are unlikely to be mediated by global changes in translational capacity.

Discussion

NAFLD is increasingly recognized as a dynamic and stage-dependent disease in which hepatocytes are continuously exposed to metabolic overload, oxidative stress, lipotoxicity, and inflammatory signaling [[1], [2], [3],27]. The capacity of hepatocytes to adapt to these chronic insults determines whether disease progression is arrested at the level of simple steatosis or advances toward non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma. Central to these adaptive and maladaptive responses is the TAK1–MAPK signaling axis, which integrates upstream inflammatory and metabolic cues with downstream pathways governing cell survival, stress tolerance, proliferation, and tissue remodeling [[10], [11], [12]].

A growing body of experimental and clinical evidence supports a dual and context-dependent role for TAK1 in liver pathophysiology. During early disease stages, TAK1 activity is indispensable for maintaining hepatocyte integrity, limiting reactive oxygen species accumulation, and coordinating controlled inflammatory responses that facilitate tissue repair [11,12,14]. Genetic ablation or functional impairment of TAK1 in hepatocytes has been shown to exacerbate liver injury, accelerate fibrosis, and promote spontaneous hepatocarcinogenesis, underscoring its protective role in hepatic homeostasis [13]. In this context, our observation that Rg5/Rk1–triterpene enhances TAK1 autophosphorylation and activates downstream MAPK signaling without inducing cytotoxicity is highly consistent with a hepatoprotective and disease-modifying function during early NAFLD.

Importantly, the biological consequences of TAK1 signaling are not static but evolve as disease progresses. In advanced NAFLD and established HCC, persistent activation of TAK1 and its downstream effectors, including NF-κB and MAPKs, may shift from adaptive to maladaptive, fostering a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment characterized by chronic inflammation, enhanced cell survival, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion [12,13]. In this setting, therapeutic suppression of MAPK signaling becomes advantageous [12,13,28]. Consistent with this paradigm, our data demonstrate that Rg3/paclitaxel effectively inhibits MEK–ERK signaling and induces robust cytotoxic stress in hepatic and gastric cancer cells, supporting its application in advanced disease stages where elimination of transformed cells is required [4,5,10].

The contrasting effects of Rg5/Rk1–triterpene and Rg3/paclitaxel on the TAK1–MAPK axis highlight the context-dependent nature of MAPK signaling during liver disease progression. In the present study, Rg5/Rk1–triterpene enhanced TAK1 autophosphorylation and activated downstream MAPK signaling without inducing detectable cytotoxicity, a profile consistent with adaptive stress-response signaling in hepatocytes. In contrast, Rg3/paclitaxel suppressed MEK–ERK signaling and induced cytotoxic responses in cancer cell models, consistent with the established role of MAPK inhibition in anticancer therapeutic strategies [12,13,28]. These findings suggest that distinct ginsenoside-based formulations may differentially modulate MAPK signaling depending on the cellular context and signaling state. However, because the present experiments were primarily conducted in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, these observations should be interpreted as a mechanistic framework describing context-dependent signaling modulation rather than direct evidence of stage-specific therapeutic effects across the NAFLD–HCC progression spectrum [[11], [12], [13],16]. Beyond canonical MAPK signaling, our study highlights additional regulatory layers that may contribute to long-term disease modulation. Stress-responsive signaling pathways, including MAPK cascades, have been reported to influence RNA-editing enzymes through transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, suggesting a potential link between TAK1–MAPK activation and ADAR regulation [11,16,29]. RNA editing, mediated by adenosine deaminases acting on RNA (ADARs), has emerged as an important post-transcriptional mechanism shaping transcriptomic diversity in cancer and chronic liver disease. Reduced ADAR2 activity has been associated with aberrant RNA editing patterns, oncogenic transcript diversification, and poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and gastrointestinal malignancies [10,29]. The selective upregulation of ADAR2 observed following Rg5/Rk1–triterpene treatment may therefore contribute to stabilization of hepatocellular identity and limitation of malignant transformation during chronic metabolic stress.

Notably, the absence of significant changes in eIF6 expression suggests that the observed biological effects are not driven by global alterations in ribosome biogenesis or translational capacity. Instead, ginsenoside-based treatments appear to selectively modulate signaling and post-transcriptional regulatory pathways while preserving basal translational homeostasis [8,9,30]. Such selectivity is particularly advantageous for chronic diseases such as NAFLD, where long-term safety and avoidance of cumulative toxicity are critical considerations.

From a broader therapeutic perspective, ginseng-derived compounds exemplify a class of natural products that exert pleiotropic yet regulated biological effects. Unlike single-target synthetic drugs, ginsenosides modulate interconnected signaling networks in a context-dependent manner, allowing for fine-tuning of cellular responses rather than binary pathway inhibition [8,9]. This property aligns well with emerging concepts in precision hepatology, which emphasize timing, disease stage, and network-level modulation over uniform pathway suppression [31].

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The mechanistic observations were primarily obtained using HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells as a simplified in vitro model. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a mechanistic framework describing context-dependent modulation of the TAK1–MAPK signaling axis rather than definitive evidence of stage-specific therapeutic effects across the entire NAFLD–HCC progression spectrum. Future studies using non-malignant hepatocyte models and in vivo disease progression systems will be necessary to further validate the stage-dependent roles of these formulations.

In conclusion, this study provides a mechanistic basis for a stage-specific therapeutic framework in which ginseng-derived formulations are deployed according to disease context along the NAFLD–HCC continuum. By integrating TAK1–MAPK signaling dynamics, post-transcriptional regulation, and cytotoxic stress responses, we propose a unified model that spans disease prevention, stabilization, and treatment. Future in vivo studies using NAFLD–HCC progression models will be essential to validate this strategy, define optimal dosing and sequencing regimens, and assess long-term safety.
