Beyond the Infarct Core
Authors: Yoriyasu Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has transformed the early prognosis of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI); however, successful epicardial recanalization does not necessarily translate into myocardial recovery. Some patients recover uneventfully, whereas others develop heart failure, recurrent ischemic events, stroke, or cardiovascular death despite timely reperfusion. Contemporary post-STEMI risk assessment must therefore move beyond confirming patency of the infarct-related artery toward identifying patients whose myocardium remains vulnerable after reperfusion.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Zhang et al1 address this challenge by developing interpretable machine learning models that integrate clinical, angiographic, and cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) features to predict long-term major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) after STEMI. The investigators analyzed 483 patients with first-diagnosed STEMI who underwent successful primary PCI within 12 hours of symptom onset and serial CMR within the first week and at approximately 1 month. During a median follow-up of 89.3 months, 98 patients (20.3%) experienced MACCEs. Using 24 demographic, clinical, angiographic, and CMR-derived variables, the authors compared naive Bayes, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). Model interpretation was performed using SHapley Additive exPlanations.

Several aspects of this study merit emphasis. First, the model was not restricted to static baseline variables. By incorporating the 1-month change in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), the authors created a landmark-style reassessment model. Early post-PCI LVEF may be influenced by myocardial stunning, edema, and acute loading conditions. In contrast, improvement or deterioration by 1 month more directly reflects the early trajectory of ventricular recovery and remodeling. Thus, the model is better interpreted as a post-acute reassessment tool than as a conventional admission risk score.

Second, the final XGBoost model retained 11 variables: microvascular obstruction (MVO), infarct size, Killip class, 1-month LVEF change, intramyocardial hemorrhage, body mass index, age, peri-infarct T1, diabetes mellitus, remote myocardial T1, and infarct core T1. Although the naive Bayes model achieved numerically high discrimination, XGBoost provided more balanced overall performance across precision-recall metrics, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, and Brier score. This distinction is important. In clinical prediction, discrimination alone is insufficient; risk estimates must also be calibrated, interpretable, and biologically credible.

This point is consistent with broader experience in machine learning for acute myocardial infarction. In a national registry of more than 750,000 patients, Khera et al2 found that contemporary machine learning models did not substantially improve discrimination for in-hospital mortality compared with logistic regression, although XGBoost and meta-classifier models improved calibration and risk resolution. Thus, the clinical value of machine learning should not be judged by the C-statistic alone. Calibration, reclassification, interpretability, external validation, and actionability determine whether a model can be trusted at the bedside.

Third, MVO emerged as the most influential CMR feature in the XGBoost model. This finding is pathophysiologically coherent. MVO represents failed tissue-level reperfusion despite successful epicardial recanalization. Its prominence should not be regarded as an unexpected algorithmic discovery, but rather as a data-driven reconfirmation of a well-established biological signal. Klug et al3 previously demonstrated that early MVO assessed by CMR was independently associated with long-term morbidity after acute myocardial infarction, even after adjustment for age, ejection fraction, and infarct size. Zhang et al1 extend this concept by showing that MVO remains dominant even when considered together with infarct size, LVEF recovery, intramyocardial hemorrhage, T1 indices, and clinical variables.

The study also fits with recent observations regarding the temporal behavior of MVO. Bodi et al4 reported that MVO was present in 44% of patients 1 week after reperfused STEMI and persisted into the chronic phase in a smaller subset; persistent MVO was associated with adverse left ventricular remodeling. These findings suggest that MVO may identify not only the extent of acute myocardial injury but also impaired microvascular repair. This distinction is clinically meaningful: if MVO is only a marker, its role is prognostic; if it reflects an incomplete repair process, it may become a therapeutic target for post-infarction repair.

Fourth, Zhang et al1 appropriately look beyond the infarct core. The inclusion of remote myocardial T1 is particularly noteworthy. Remote T1 was inversely correlated with 1-month LVEF recovery, suggesting that tissue characteristics outside the infarct zone may reflect a global ventricular response to STEMI. This finding is consistent with Shanmuganathan et al,5 who demonstrated that acute T1 abnormalities in both infarcted and noninfarcted myocardium predicted long-term adverse events after STEMI and improved risk prediction beyond conventional CMR markers. However, caution is warranted. In the present study, overall T1 values did not differ significantly between patients with and without MACCEs, and the prognostic contribution of remote T1 was mainly revealed through interaction-sensitive modeling. Remote myocardial T1 should therefore be considered a promising but exploratory biomarker rather than an established clinical decision variable.

The contrast between conventional regression and machine learning is also instructive. In Zhang et al,1 Cox regression identified clinically plausible predictors of MACCEs, including Killip class, MVO, 1-month LVEF change, infarct size, and diabetes mellitus. XGBoost additionally highlighted nonlinear and interaction-dependent contributions, including infarct core T1 and remote T1. This is where interpretable machine learning may add value: not by replacing clinical reasoning, but by revealing relationships that conventional models may not fully capture.

The limitations are equally important. First, the model was developed in a specific CMR environment and lacks external validation. This is a major issue for CMR-based machine learning because mapping sequences, scanner platforms, contrast protocols, segmentation methods, image quality thresholds, and timing after infarction all influence derived features. A model that performs well within one technical ecosystem may not generalize to another. Second, the study population consisted of patients who survived to undergo serial CMR and had analyzable images. Patients with early death, severe instability, contraindications to CMR, inadequate image quality, or events before the 1-month reassessment may be underrepresented. Therefore, the model is most applicable to stable post-STEMI survivors undergoing serial CMR, not necessarily to all-comer STEMI patients.

Third, intramyocardial hemorrhage was treated as a binary variable because of limitations in quantitative assessment. This approach may underestimate its true prognostic contribution; quantitative assessment of hemorrhage or iron deposition may better capture the severity of microvascular injury. Fourth, MACCE prediction was modeled as a binary classification problem despite long-term follow-up and variable event timing. Although practical, this approach does not fully address censoring, competing risks, or changing risk trajectories. Future studies should evaluate time-to-event machine learning or dynamic prediction frameworks.

Beyond these study-specific issues, future work should meet contemporary standards for clinical prediction models. PRIME and TRIPOD+AI emphasize transparent reporting of data sources, preprocessing, missing data handling, feature selection, calibration, subgroup performance, external validation, and clinical utility.6,7 For CMR-based machine learning, this also requires careful evaluation of model transportability across scanners and institutions, assessment of calibration in clinically relevant subgroups, and greater reproducibility through sharing of model specifications, code, and data dictionaries whenever feasible.

Zhang et al1 provide an important step toward interpretable CMR-based post-STEMI risk stratification. The value of their study lies not only in model performance, but also in the biological coherence of the strongest signals: MVO, infarct size, Killip class, LVEF recovery, intramyocardial hemorrhage, and T1-derived features. The next question is not simply whether we can build a better algorithm, but whether such a model can be translated into care by supporting closer surveillance, optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy, intensified cardiac rehabilitation, earlier repeat imaging, or enrollment in trials targeting microvascular repair and adverse remodeling.

Opening the infarct-related artery remains the first victory in STEMI care. The next will be to identify, early and accurately, patients whose myocardium remains vulnerable after reperfusion and to intervene before adverse remodeling becomes irreversible.
