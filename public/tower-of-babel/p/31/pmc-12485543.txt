Human-centric AI: An interview with Edith Luhanga
Authors: Edith Luhanga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEdith Luhanga is an assistant research professor at Carnegie Mellon University Africa in Rwanda. Her work focuses on designing and evaluating technologies that leverage artificial intelligence (AI) to promote behavioral change in low-resource communities. She is currently working on digital interventions for maternal health, child nutrition and online safety, and financial inclusion in Rwanda, Tanzania, Kenya, and South Africa. Edith holds a PhD in information science (ubiquitous computing) from the Nara Institute of Science and Technology in Japan and an MSc in advanced computing science and BEng (Hons) in electronic and computer engineering from the University of Nottingham in the UK. In this interview, Edith shares her experience as a human-centric AI researcher, along with her opinions about ethical AI and her thoughts on current technology developments in African communities.

Main text

Edith Luhanga

What would you like to share about your background? For example, have key moments or mentors inspired your career as a researcher?

Edith Luhanga: When smartphones were becoming more popular around 2011, I took a class on ubiquitous computing as part of my master’s degree. I got really excited about the idea of using these devices to detect patterns in people’s behavior and deliver just-in-time interventions to improve their health, especially because my relatives in rural areas had struggled to access affordable, quality care when I was growing up. Smartphones seemed like a faster and more affordable way to deliver healthcare to people while new facilities were still being built. I wanted to learn how to design such solutions—which features to include and how to design seamless interfaces—and that curiosity ultimately led me to pursue a PhD in the field.

Almost two decades later, many studies have explored context recognition and context-aware applications, but we still lack clear evidence on which digital strategies work best, for whom, and why. What drives me now is contributing to building such an evidence base by using frameworks such as the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST).

You’ve worked on health, financial inclusion, and cybersecurity. How do you decide what problems to focus on?

E.L.: I began by working on health applications, which remain my passion, but exploratory studies and co-design efforts made me realize that financial constraints are a major barrier to health-promoting behaviors. I also found that a lack of trust can keep people from using apps. Trust depends not only on feeling that information is credible but also on knowing that any private information shared remains private. Perceived usefulness (or performance expectancy) is one of the two main drivers of technology adoption (according to models such as the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2 [UTAUT2]). To build health applications that people would actually use, I needed to better understand the challenges of cybersecurity and financial inclusion, which is why I expanded the scope of my work to these domains.

How do resource constraints or local infrastructure shape the way you work with data and design tech interventions?

E.L.: The data we wanted for our studies were not always available. In most cases, the features we wanted simply did not exist, but in other cases the data were not in a digital format or researchers faced access restrictions. For several studies, we had to build our own data pipelines and datasets, which took time. But our struggles have made me aware of the importance of the open data movement, and I have made sure that the datasets we create are publicly and freely available.

What have you learned from working closely with people in local communities when designing tech solutions?

E.L.: I have become more aware of power dynamics. At the start of co-design sessions, there is usually hesitation and disbelief that technology can solve a problem, especially among participants with lower digital literacy. I later realized that part of this hesitation is that it can feel risky to speak up without knowing whether an idea is “right” and even riskier to tell a “tech expert” that a concept would not work. Showing a working prototype helped participants see possibilities and share their needs more openly, but it sometimes biased them too strongly toward the design we presented. I am now experimenting with ways to provide participants with just enough of a mental model of what is possible—for example, what AI techniques can realistically do—before introducing techniques such as SCAMPER to unlock their imagination.

What does ethical AI mean to you in practical terms?

E.L.: Being ethical is to do no harm, but harm is not always apparent until after an AI system is deployed. Some real-world examples include Amazon’s hiring algorithm, which was biased against women, and Facebook’s facial recognition, which mislabeled Black people. I have also learned that perceptions of harm can shift over time and across communities.

My work is grounded in human-centered design, so I approach ethics by mapping both direct and indirect stakeholders and ensuring that we understand the stories that algorithms tell or might tell about a group. Much of my work is still in the pilot phase, but as we move toward large-scale deployment and evaluation, I want to ensure that we regularly audit the models and validate the emerging stories with target communities and domain experts to assess whether they could be harmful.

What do you wish more tech companies or researchers understood about designing for African communities?

E.L.: The GSMA 2025 Mobile Economy report shows that only 29% of people in sub-Saharan Africa use mobile internet—and much of this gap is due to low adoption rather than lack of coverage. Cost and limited awareness of the internet’s value are often cited as the main barriers. In my culture we say, “a good thing should always be told to a friend.” In the West, the equivalent might be, “good news travels fast.” If people still do not see the value of the internet, then perhaps we have not yet created resources that are truly valuable to them.

I witnessed the mobile money revolution firsthand: how the ability to easily send and receive money sparked demand for mobile phones, how cheap handsets accelerated adoption, and how that adoption boosted trade and improved livelihoods. Today, the data confirm mobile money’s massive economic impact on sub-Saharan Africa.

The question now is, what will create that same kind of demand for the internet—and for digital health, education, and agriculture in everyday life? We have yet to find the answer, but doing so is essential to bridging the digital divide.
