Introduction

Human roles refer to the behaviours and responsibilities individuals assume in performing their daily activities.1 These roles are defined and constructed according to the level of participation in them and the values that individuals assign to them. Participation is shaped by personal and environmental circumstances and requires a balance between needs, desires and social expectations.2 The value assigned to roles reflects their perceived importance, sense of responsibility and commitment to the actions they involve.3 Role participation usually changes across life stages or after significant events, such as a stroke,4 influencing identity, lifestyle organisation and community interactions.1 Therefore, identifying these changes is essential for addressing the daily life needs of individuals after a stroke.

The Role Checklist is a commonly used questionnaire to assess perceived participation in human roles and their assigned value.5 This instrument was developed and has evolved in line with conceptual updates to the Model of Human Occupation.1 This Checklist is also consistent with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health.6 The introduction of the ‘dimensions of doing’7 distinguished occupational performance from occupational participation, which subsequently informed adaptations of the Role Checklist.8 Currently, three versions of the Role Checklist are available.9 10 The first version evaluates past, present and expected future participation, as well as the value assigned to roles.5 The second version adds an evaluation of the performance quality.11 The third version excludes past participation and the value assigned to roles, replacing them with a dimension of role satisfaction with current participation and the desirability of future performance.8

Given these variations in the Role Checklist, selecting the most suitable version for a specific population requires careful consideration. In the context of individuals with stroke, evaluating past participation can provide insights into roles that shaped identity and related expectations.12 This version of the Role Checklist that maintains the dimension of ‘past participation’ supports occupational history and client-centred interventions. Similarly, the version evaluating perceived role values helps understand which roles remain central to identity for each individual, even when their ability to participate in the roles included in the instrument is impaired after a stroke.13 By contrast, the dimension of role satisfaction is usually linked to current participation, which may introduce bias when stroke survivors are experiencing functional limitations.14 This dimension may also be shaped by contextual factors that do not necessarily reflect personal priorities.15 On the other hand, evaluating role values provides a more stable measure, less dependent on the individual’s current condition. Likewise, expectations of future role participation provide a more realistic perspective than the desirability of participation, since personal expectations integrate an introspective assessment of personal, functional and contextual factors involved in future role participation, while desirability may reflect purely aspirational goals that ignore potential limitations in role participation.16 17 Assessing expectations of future role participation enables clinicians to identify realistically achievable roles and design interventions to support current participation.

For individuals with stroke, the Role Checklist may be useful for monitoring changes in role participation and for understanding the value assigned to each role.18 After a stroke, individuals tend to reduce role participation due to physical, cognitive and psychosocial sequelae.19 This reduction affects emotional well-being,20 satisfaction of personal needs and perceived quality of life.21 These individuals may need to restructure their occupations, which can lead to reduced motivation and diminished meaning in daily routines.22 For these reasons, monitoring changes in participation in valuable roles can help identify needs, guide rehabilitation plans and prevent secondary complications. Although the Role Checklist, across its three versions, has been translated into 13 languages, including Spanish,23 the psychometric properties for individuals with stroke remain unknown. Hence, this study aims to evaluate the psychometric properties of the original Role Checklist in a sample of Spanish-speaking stroke survivors.

Method

The research adhered to the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) guidelines, ensuring the quality of the methodological process.

Participants and sample size

The inclusion criteria for the study participants were: (1) clinical diagnosis of stroke; (2) mild-to-moderate poststroke sequelae, as measured by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, a standard tool in Spain for assessing stroke severity24; (3) over 18 years of age; and (4) fluency in Spanish. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of more than one stroke episode; (2) to be hospitalised; (3) any diagnosis of brain damage other than stroke; (4) moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment reported in the medical record25; (5) severe intellectual disability reported in the medical record; (6) diagnosis of movement or musculoskeletal disorder; (7) uncorrected sensory deficits reported in the medical record; and (8) difficulties in understanding verbal or written language.

Two separate sample sizes were calculated to ensure adequate reliable estimation of parameters: one for the internal consistency and another for the test–retest and inter-rater reliability. For internal consistency, Bonett’s formula for Cronbach’s alpha sample size was used.26 27 This was based on the participation subscale, which contains the largest number of items (30). A minimum Cronbach’s alpha value (H0) of 0.70 was established as the baseline28 with an expected value (H1) of 0.80.27 A significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 85% were assumed. Considering a possible 30% sample loss,29 the estimated required sample size was 177 individuals. For the test–retest and inter-rater analyses, Bonett’s formula for calculating intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) sample sizes was applied.30 A minimum ICC value (p0) of 0.6028 and an expected ICC value (p1) of 0.9031 with two observations were defined. Assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%27 and a possible 5% sample loss, the required sample size was 15 individuals with stroke.

Instruments

Sociodemographic data, lifestyle and clinical characteristics

Socio-demographic, lifestyle and clinical data were collected using an ad hoc questionnaire. Sociodemographic data included sex, age, marital status and educational level. Lifestyle variables comprised self-reported daily sleep duration (average in hours) and self-reported daily physical activity (average daily minutes of light-intensity physical activity, according to WHO recommendations).32 Clinical characteristics were the type of stroke (ischaemic, haemorrhagic and lacunar) and the time since the onset of the most recent stroke episode (months).

The Role Checklist adapted for stroke survivors

The Role Checklist comprises 40 items organised into two subscales. Subscale 1 evaluates perceived role participation across three subgroups of items according to the temporal frames of past, present and expected future role participation. Each subgroup of items includes an evaluation of 10 human roles: student, worker, volunteer, caregiver, home maintainer, friend, family member, religious participant, hobbyist and participant in organisations.33 Although these roles represent diverse life domains, they are conceptually grouped under the activity participation construct, and the range of items is intended to capture the complexity of real-world occupational participation. The responses of this part of the instrument indicate whether the individual participates in each human role at each temporal frame. Subscale 2 measures the perceived value or importance for the individual of each of these roles, rated as ‘very valuable’, ‘somewhat valuable’ or ‘not at all valuable’. The Role Checklist takes 20–30 min to complete, and it is suitable for adults, including those aged over 65 years. This instrument presents acceptable test–retest reliability in healthy English-speaking adults.5 The Spanish translation showed strong linguistic correlation, with a Kappa index of 0.91 for subscale 1 and 0.80 for subscale 2.23 The Spanish version of the Role Checklist, adapted to evaluate individuals with stroke, is provided as online supplemental file 1.

Data collection

Participants were recruited from 12 randomly selected primary care centres covering urban and rural areas in the province of Granada, Spain. Potential participants were identified and informed about the study by a health professional in each centre. If potential participants expressed interest in participating and met the selection criteria, they were contacted to confirm their participation and schedule the evaluation session. Evaluations were conducted in person at primary care centres by an occupational therapist with more than 10 years of experience in stroke evaluation. Each evaluation session lasted approximately 45 min. The Role Checklist was administered as an interview to ensure that participants fully understood the meaning of each role and could provide accurate responses.

After completing the evaluation, 30 participants were randomly selected for a second evaluation session to assess test–retest and inter-rater reliability. To ensure that the medical conditions of the participants did not change from the first to the second session, the researchers verified that there had been: (1) no new stroke, (2) no other brain damage, (3) no hospitalisation, (4) no osteoarticular injuries or trauma affecting functionality, (5) no diagnosis of psychiatric disorder and (6) no institutionalisation. These criteria were verified the day before the session through a brief telephone interview. Participants were then randomly assigned to two groups: 15 individuals to the retest evaluation group and 15 individuals to the inter-rater evaluation group. For the test–retest evaluations, the same occupational therapist administered the Role Checklist again 2 weeks after the first evaluation. This time interval was chosen based on previous studies that used this timeframe for assessing test–retest in similar evaluation instruments.34 This second evaluation lasted approximately 20–30 min.

For the inter-rater reliability assessment, an independent occupational therapist, trained in neurorehabilitation and with clinical experience in working with individuals after stroke, administered the Role Checklist 2 days after the first evaluation. This occupational therapist was blinded to the participants’ responses from the first evaluation. The same environmental conditions as in the first session were maintained, and participants received the same instructions as in the first evaluation. This second evaluation lasted approximately 20–30 min.

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted with SPSS V.28 for Windows.35 Descriptive statistics (means, SD, frequencies, percentages, ranges, minimums, maximums and percentiles) were used to summarise sample characteristics and the Role Checklist results. Responses from each subscale and participants’ responses with respect to the temporal frame (subgroup of items) of the Role Checklist were calculated. Categorical responses for subscale 1 of the instrument were coded as 0 or 1, corresponding to ‘not participate’ and ‘yes, I participate’. Categorical responses for the subscale of ‘value assigned to roles’ were coded as 0, 1 or 2, corresponding to ‘not at all valuable’, ‘somewhat valuable’ and ‘very valuable’, respectively.

The reliability of the Role Checklist was assessed in terms of homogeneity and stability. First, the internal consistency was examined separately for each subscale using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Cronbach’s alpha is used to assess the internal consistency of measurement instruments, indicating, in this case, the degree of coherence among items within a subscale intended to measure the same participation-related domain.36 A Cronbach’s alpha value greater than 0.70 was considered acceptable internal consistency.37 A CI of 95% was calculated for each analysis. Second, the item-total correlations of each subscale were examined to identify items that might not be correlated with the measurement construct. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was employed to examine the item-total correlations, with a value greater than 0.20 considered acceptable.38

The temporal consistency of the Role Checklist was evaluated using a test–retest analysis. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the ICC for each subscale of the Role Checklist, based on a two-way random effects model with absolute agreement, single measures and 95% CI. Correlations higher than 0.60 were considered acceptable.28 Measurement error was examined by calculating the SE of measurement (SEM) and the minimum detectable change (MDC) 95% IC.39 SEM was estimated using the baseline SD of each subscale and the corresponding ICC of test–retest, according to the formula SEM=SD × √(1 – ICC). MDC95 was calculated using the formula MDC95=1.96 × √SEM × 2.40

Then, the consistency of responses when the Role Checklist was administered by different evaluators was examined with an inter-rater analysis. This analysis was also performed by calculating the ICC for the two subscales of the Role Checklist, with a two-way random effects model and 95% CI.41 The values obtained by the first evaluator were compared with those obtained by the second evaluator.

Floor and ceiling effects were assessed by calculating the proportion of participants scoring the minimum and maximum possible values for the Role Checklist subscales, ranging from 0 to 30 points in subscale 1 and 0 to 20 points in subscale 2. In addition, the percentage of participants who achieved the minimum and maximum possible scores was calculated considering the participants’ responses across the temporal frame of items of subscale 1: past role participation (0–10 points), current role participation (0–10 points) and expectations of future role participation (0–10 points). Floor and ceiling effects for each subscale and temporal frame were considered present if more than 15% of participants scored the lowest or highest possible value.41

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Test–retest reliability

The temporal stability of the Role Checklist was confirmed, as the test–retest analysis showed good agreement between the two evaluations for both subscales of the Role Checklist, with an ICC >0.80. Subscale 1 showed an ICC value of 0.88 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.96). If the participants’ responses with respect to the temporal frame are analysed, past role participation yielded an ICC value of 0.88 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.96), current participation an ICC of 0.86 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.95) and expected future participation an ICC of 0.83 (95% 0.53–0.94). Subscale 2 exhibited an ICC of 0.84 (95% 0.53–0.94).

The SEM estimates were 1.43 for the subscale of role participation and 0.88 for the subscale of value attributed to the total number of roles, reflecting the expected variability of scores due to measurement error. The corresponding MDC95 values were 3.96 and 2.43 for subscale 1 and subscale 2, respectively, indicating the minimum change required to be interpreted as a real change beyond measurement error.

Inter-rater reliability

The consistency of responses when the Role Checklist was administered by two independent evaluators was statistically significant and high in both subscales of the instrument. The inter-rater reliability was excellent, with an ICC value above 0.99. Floor and ceiling effects

No floor effect was observed for any part of the Role Checklist, as fewer than 15% of participants achieved the minimum possible score on both subscales. Subscale 1 showed no participants scoring the minimum possible value. Considering the participants’ responses with respect to the temporal frame of items of subscale 1, no floor effect was observed in the items on past role participation (n=0), current role participation (n=4; 2.2%) and expectations of future role participation (n=2; 1.1%). Subscale 2 indicated that 0% of participants achieved the minimum score.

Similarly, no ceiling effect was observed on subscale 1, as fewer than 15% of participants achieved the maximum possible score, as recommended by evidence. This finding was consistent across the temporal frame of items of subscale 1: past role participation (n=3; 1.7%), current role participation (n=0) and expected future role participation (n=1; 0.6%). On subscale 2, 6.1% (n=11) reached the maximum possible score.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Role Checklist in a sample of Spanish-speaking individuals with stroke. In terms of scale reliability, the results for subscale 1 showed good internal consistency, and the item-total correlations indicated strong associations, except for some items of the past role participation. This exception may be attributed to potential inaccuracies in participants’ perceptions of their activity participation level before the stroke.42 Otherwise, perceived self-efficacy and social support appear to influence perceived participation among individuals with stroke, potentially biasing retrospective judgements of participation before stroke.43 44 On the other hand, subscale 2 showed a lower internal consistency. This finding may be attributed to several factors. First, the personal evaluation of the importance assigned to each role may require higher cognitive effort from the individual due to its abstract nature. When combined with accumulated fatigue during the assessment, this may have resulted in less consistent responses and may partly explain the observed reliability estimates.45 Second, the original Likert-type response options in the subscales of the instrument may not be sensitive enough to adequately capture personal experiences, motivations or cultural contexts, potentially limiting response variability.46 Third, a social desirability bias may cause individuals to give socially desirable responses.47 However, the item-total correlations indicated that the items of this subscale are coherent with each other and effectively capture the perceived value or importance assigned to roles. In addition, the results regarding the SEM indicated a low level of measurement error.

Regarding the temporal stability, the Role Checklist exhibited good reproducibility over time for both subscales, as evidenced by the results obtained 2 weeks after the initial administration of the instrument. This finding means that the participants’ responses remained consistent over time, supporting the reliability of the Role Checklist in measuring role participation in Spanish-speaking stroke survivors. In this line, it can be concluded that the Role Checklist is a stable instrument for assessing role participation at different time frames. These results are consistent with those from the previous Spanish translation of the Role Checklist, which also reported high test–retest reliability,23 as well as other studies evaluating the test–retest reliability of different language versions of the Role Checklist.48 The inter-rater consistency of responses on the Role Checklist, when administered by two different evaluators, was also good for both subscales. These results show the coherence of the items of the questionnaire in evaluating role participation and the assigned value or importance to roles. These findings are also in line with other similar studies, which indicate that the reliability of the Role Checklist remains robust regardless of the evaluator who administers it.5 49 Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution and primarily as indicative of feasibility, as the subsample sizes used for these analyses fall below COSMIN recommendations.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the use of the Role Checklist as a useful tool for assessing role participation and the value assigned to roles in Spanish-speaking individuals with chronic-phase stroke who have no difficulties understanding verbal or written language. The measurement error indices provided information on the reliability of the instrument and the magnitude of change required to reflect a true change in role participation in the study sample. The SEM suggested that individual scores fluctuated slightly due to inherent measurement error, while the minimal detectable change indicated that observed changes exceeding the reported threshold can be used to interpret future patients’ outcomes as true changes in participation or perceived value of roles. Considering the items of subscale 1, clinicians can identify individual’s past role participation and compare it with their current role participation, facilitating the development of rehabilitation strategies aligned with the person’s occupational history. Assessing expectations for future role participation can allow clinicians to understand which roles are realistically achievable for the patient. On the other hand, by focusing on the subconstruct represented by the items of subscale 2, understanding the value assigned to common human roles in our society may provide key information for promoting motivation and engagement in role participation among stroke survivors.

Study participants had already been discharged, and the evaluation of role participation was conducted across different recovery stages, reflecting the clinical diversity commonly encountered in everyday stroke practice. This included variability in time since the most recent stroke onset and the possibility of recurrent stroke. The mean age of the sample was approximately 63 years, and participants were predominantly male (around 70%). These characteristics are generally comparable to those reported in stroke populations in Spain,50 51 although the over-representation of men may limit the generalisability of the findings. This clinical heterogeneity may be associated with greater variability in functional status and rehabilitation trajectories, which could have influenced the score distribution and feasibility results observed in the present validation study. Future research would help clarify whether the measurement properties of the instruments differ according to these subgroups.52 Lastly, although this study collected information about lifestyle characteristics through a self-reported ad hoc questionnaire, a more systematic assessment could provide additional insight into factors influencing participation after stroke. Future research should therefore consider using validated and reliable instruments to measure these variables, which would enhance methodological rigour and facilitate comparability across studies.

Limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, most participants were in the chronic phase of stroke, which limits understanding of role participation in the acute phases. Role participation in the acute phase may differ from the chronic phase, as individuals may still be adapting to functional changes. Second, participants with recurrent stroke were not excluded, meaning that the study sample may include individuals with this condition. Although this reflects real-world clinical populations, individuals with multiple stroke events may exhibit greater functional heterogeneity,52 which could affect the feasibility and performance of the assessment tool. Future studies should consider analysing measurement properties separately in first-ever and recurrent stroke populations. Third, the sample was based on a population from a single Spanish region, which may limit the representativeness of Spanish-speaking individuals with stroke. However, the participants were recruited from community settings at 12 randomly selected primary care centres. Fourth, there was limited heterogeneity in the sample’s sex, with a lower representation of women. Nevertheless, the diverse sociodemographic and educational backgrounds of the participants may contribute to a more representative sample of the stroke population. Fifth, although the sample sizes of the test–retest and inter-rater analyses met the requirement from an a priori ICC-based power calculation, they do not meet the COSMIN-recommended thresholds for optimal methodological quality. Therefore, test–retest and inter-rater results should be interpreted with caution. Finally, it should be noted that, regarding lifestyle variables, this study recorded daily sleep duration (averaged over 7 days) and daily physical activity using self-reports. Future studies aiming to describe these variables should consider collecting them systematically using a standardised assessment instrument validated for the Spanish population with stroke.53 54

Conclusions

The study findings indicate that the Role Checklist is a reliable instrument for evaluating role participation in Spanish-speaking individuals with stroke. It presents a lower reliability for assessing the assigned value to roles. However, most of the associations between items and the overall score were acceptable, which reinforces the ability of the Role Checklist to assess role participation. Additionally, the Role Checklist also has excellent temporal stability and high agreement when administered by different clinical evaluators. Future studies could investigate how role participation and the importance assigned to roles relate to other health outcomes. The Role Checklist is particularly useful for this purpose, as few instruments are available to assess role participation.
