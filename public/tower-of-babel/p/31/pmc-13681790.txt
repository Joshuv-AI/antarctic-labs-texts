Extended evaluation of activated carbon–embedded glucomannan membranes for the adsorption of structurally and charge-distinct dyes
Authors: Masahide Hagiri, Taisei Igari, Keita Kashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveKonjac glucomannan (KGM)/activated carbon (AC) composite membranes were previously shown to retain the intrinsic adsorption capability of AC when removing methylene blue. However, the applicability of this composite system to structurally and electrostatically distinct dyes, and its robustness to variations in KGM source have not been fully evaluated. This study extends the earlier work by examining the adsorption of methyl violet B (MV), a bulkier cationic dye, and methyl orange (MO), an anionic dye, using KGM from a different supplier.ResultsStable KGM/AC membranes were fabricated and showed no leakage of AC during experiments. Both MV and MO exhibited Type-I Langmuir adsorption behavior, and the maximum adsorption capacity increased linearly with the AC mass fraction (MFAC), demonstrating that AC embedded in the membrane remained accessible. The Langmuir equilibrium constant showed no systematic dependence on MFAC, indicating that the neutral KGM matrix did not alter the affinity of AC for the dyes. The membrane prepared with a different KGM lot performed comparably to that in the previous study, confirming reproducibility. These findings demonstrate that the KGM/AC membrane maintains AC functionality across dyes with molecular properties and is robust with respect to variation in polymer source.

Introduction

The release of synthetic dyes into aquatic environments poses serious risks to ecosystems and human health [1]. Even at low concentrations, dyes such as methylene blue (MB) and methyl violet B (MV) are toxic and persistent, highlighting the need for reliable removal methods using biodegradable and reusable materials. Among available technologies, adsorption remains one of the most effective and economical approaches for dye-containing wastewater [2, 3].

Composite adsorbents that combine activated carbon (AC) with polymer matrices have been widely investigated because polymer incorporation can improve the processability, moldability, and mechanical stability of AC-based materials [4, 5]. Most studies, however, focus on composites prepared with synthetic polymers, with less attention to biodegradability and environmental impact [6, 7].

In contrast, biodegradable and naturally derived polysaccharides represent environmentally advantageous matrices, yet comparatively few studies have explored their use for embedding AC. Such matrices offer biocompatibility and degradability while potentially preserving the adsorption capability of incorporated AC [8–11].

Our group has previously explored the use of natural-polymer matrices to incorporate functional inorganic or carbon-based adsorbents [12, 13]. For example, we fabricated a chitosan/Prussian blue composite membrane and demonstrated that it functioned as an effective ion-exchange material for selective cesium uptake [12], highlighting the potential of polysaccharide-based membranes to preserve the performance of immobilized adsorbents. Building upon this concept, our recent study developed a free-standing konjac glucomannan (KGM)/AC composite membrane and showed that it retained the intrinsic adsorption performance of AC when removing MB [14]. The non-ionic KGM matrix facilitated dye diffusion without electrostatic interference, and kinetic and isotherm analyses confirmed the preservation of AC’s adsorption characteristics. However, that study evaluated only a single cationic dye and employed KGM from a single supplier.

The present study further validates the design principles of KGM/AC composite membranes by examining their generality and robustness using dyes with different structures and charges. To assess the broader applicability of the composite design, we first investigated the adsorption of MV, a bulkier triphenylmethane dye with structural features distinct from MB. Owing to its larger molecular size and different hydrophobic–hydrophilic balance, MV provides a more stringent test for polymer-based adsorbents [15].

In addition, although methyl orange (MO), an anionic azo dye with a molecular weight comparable to MB, was included only as a limited supplementary experiment in our previous study, the present work provides a more systematic evaluation of MO adsorption. This enables a clearer assessment of whether dye molecules with opposite charge characteristics can access the internal activated carbon surfaces within the KGM matrix. Additionally, the KGM used in this study was sourced from a different manufacturer. Because the physicochemical properties of KGM can vary depending on production conditions, confirming membrane performance with a different lot is important for demonstrating reproducibility and robustness. Taken together, this study provides broader validation of the KGM/AC membrane concept by examining structurally and electrostatically distinct dyes and by confirming reproducibility with KGM from a different supplier.

Results and discussion

Stable KGM/AC membranes were successfully fabricated even when KGM obtained from another supplier was used, demonstrating the reproducibility of membrane preparation across material sources. The membranes exhibited sufficient mechanical integrity for batch type isothermal adsorption experiments, and no structural degradation or leakage of AC particles was observed. Our previous kinetic study on MB adsorption confirmed that adsorption equilibrium is reached within 72 h for KGM/AC membranes. Membranes immersed in MV aqueous solutions also reached adsorption equilibrium after 72 h, at which point the solution attained the equilibrium concentration Ceq [mg·L− 1], and the corresponding adsorption capacity was defined as qeq [mg·g− 1].

The relationship between qeq and Ceq, namely the MV adsorption isotherm at 25 °C, showed that the amount of MV adsorbed increased with increasing equilibrium concentration and eventually approached a constant value at higher concentrations. This behavior is characteristic of a Type-I adsorption isotherm [16], also known as a Langmuir type isotherm. In addition, the adsorption capacity increased systematically as the AC mass fraction in the membrane (MFAC) value increased from 0.50 to 1.0, supporting the validity of the composite design over a broad range of AC contents.

The Langmuir model describes monolayer adsorption under the assumptions that (i) no interactions occur between adsorbed molecules, (ii) adsorption sites and adsorbate molecules correspond on a one to one basis, and (iii) the surface is homogeneous. Here, qmax [mg·g− 1] and K [L·mg− 1] denote the maximum monolayer adsorption capacity and the Langmuir equilibrium constant, respectively. The plot of Ceq/qeq versus Ceq shown in Fig. 1 yielded a straight line, whose slope and intercept correspond to 1/qmax and 1/(K·qmax), respectively.

From this analysis, the values of qmax and K were obtained. The resulting qmax values for membranes with MFAC values of 0.50, 0.75, 0.83, and 1.0 were 392, 479, 604, and 685 mg·g− 1, indicating a strong positive correlation between MFAC and qmax. The adsorption capacity of MV obtained in this study was greater than the values generally reported for AC [18]. This is likely due to the characteristics of the AC used here, which possesses an exceptionally high specific surface area. In contrast, for MFAC = 0, that is, for the KGM membrane containing no AC, the adsorption capacity was extremely low and did not yield a meaningful isotherm (not shown). Because the number of adsorption sites was insufficient, the saturated adsorption amount was approximated as the simple average of multiple adsorption trials and was estimated to be 14.0 mg·g− 1, which reflects the inherently low adsorption capability of KGM as a neutral polysaccharide lacking strongly ionizable groups [19–21].

To further evaluate charge-dependent generality, a similar analysis was performed for MO. Despite the difference in charge between the cationic dyes MB and MV and the anionic dye MO, the adsorption isotherms obtained for membranes with MFAC values of 0.50, 0.75, 0.83, and 1.0 showed the same trend: the amount of MO adsorbed increased with increasing equilibrium concentration and approached a constant value at higher concentrations. The adsorption capacity also increased as MFAC increased from 0.50 to 1.0. The Langmuir plot could be applied without difficulty, and the parameters qmax and K were estimated. The results indicated a clear correlation between MFAC and qmax, showing that the relationship between activated carbon content and adsorption capacity was preserved for both cationic and anionic dyes. For MFAC = 0, however, the adsorption capacity was too low for the Langmuir plot to be applicable. Therefore, the saturated adsorption amount was approximated as the simple average of multiple adsorption trials and was estimated to be 6.95 mg·g− 1.

The maximum adsorption capacity qmax increased with increasing MFAC, providing quantitative support for the validity of the KGM/AC composite design and indicating that the AC embedded in the KGM/AC membranes retained sufficient adsorption capability. In contrast, the Langmuir equilibrium constant K is inherently more sensitive to experimental variability due to the structure of the Langmuir equation. The values obtained for MV, ranging from 9 to 36, fall within a comparable range and do not show any systematic dependence on MFAC. This absence of a clear trend suggests that the KGM matrix does not affect the affinity of the AC adsorption sites toward dye molecules.

KGM is a neutral polysaccharide that lacks strongly ionizable groups and interacts with AC mainly through weak hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces [22, 23]. Thus, adsorption proceeds predominantly on the AC surface, while the KGM network serves as a hydrophilic support for molecular diffusion.

Next, the relationship between the maximum adsorption capacity qmax and MFAC was examined to clarify whether the AC embedded in the KGM/AC membranes retains its intrinsic functionality. Figure 2 shows qmax plotted as a function of MFAC for both MV and MO. The data for MFAC = 1 (raw AC) and MFAC = 0 (pure KGM membrane) are also included.

The plots of MFAC versus qmax showed clear linearity, with coefficients of determination of R2 = 0.985 for MV and R2 = 0.939 for MO. This strong correlation indicates that dye adsorption capacity is directly proportional to the AC content in the membrane, demonstrating that the AC within the KGM/AC membranes retains its full inherent adsorption capability.

Overall, the results provide broader validation of the KGM/AC composite membrane concept. The membrane maintained stable performance for both cationic and anionic dyes examined in this study, and the strong linear MFAC–qmax correlation confirmed that the embedded AC particles remained fully accessible for adsorption. The use of KGM from a different supplier did not affect membrane formation or adsorption behavior, indicating robustness toward variations in polymer source. Moreover, the neutral KGM matrix allows solutes to diffuse freely to the AC surface. These findings support the utility of KGM/AC membranes as reliable and environmentally compatible materials for dye adsorption.

Materials and methods

Distilled and ion-exchanged water was used throughout the experiments. KGM powder (Amorphophallus konjac) was obtained from Shimizu Chemical (Hiroshima, Japan); this KGM source differs from that used previously. Wood-derived AC from Futamura Chemical Co., Ltd. had an average pore size of 1.1–1.3 nm and a specific surface area of 1200–1800 m2·g− 1. MV, MO, PEG200, and potassium hydroxide were used as received.

Composite membranes were prepared following our previously reported procedure, except for the use of a different KGM supplier [14]. In brief, AC was dispersed in water, mixed with 1.0 wt% KGM solution and PEG200, cast into Petri dishes, dried, and then immersed in 2.5 M KOH solution for 30 s at room temperature to insolubilize the KGM. The membranes were subsequently rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. Residual PEG200 was removed by immersion in hot water (80 °C) for 10 min prior to use. Here, WAC [g] is the mass of AC added to the precursor suspension, WKGM [g] is the mass of KGM contained in the precursor suspension.

Equilibrium adsorption experiments were performed using MV or MO aqueous solutions. A membrane sample was placed in 10 mL of dye solution and shaken at 100 rpm for 72 h at 25 °C. The initial concentration was set at 620 mg·L− 1 for MV and 500 mg·L− 1 for MO. Adsorption isotherms were not constructed by varying the initial dye concentration. Instead, for each MFAC value, at least six equilibrium data points were obtained by varying the membrane area, and hence the membrane mass, at a fixed initial concentration. This procedure produced different equilibrium concentrations depending on membrane loading. The dry membrane loading ranged from 0.010 to 0.10 g per 10 mL of dye solution for MV and from 0.018 to 0.20 g per 10 mL for MO. Each adsorption condition was measured once. The initial pH values of the dye solutions were approximately 4.7 for MV and 5.3 for MO, and no further adjustment was made.

The residual dye concentration Ceq was determined spectrophotometrically (λ = 590 nm for MV, λ = 465 nm for MO). The membrane was then dried at 120 °C for 3 h to obtain the dry mass wm. Limitation

The dye adsorption experiments in this study were limited to equilibrium measurements and did not include kinetic analyses; thus, further work is needed to clarify factors governing adsorption rates. In addition, each adsorption condition was measured once, and therefore further replicate experiments would be useful to improve the statistical reliability of the isotherm parameters. Adsorption performance can still vary between suppliers of commercial AC. The potential for regenerating and reusing KGM/AC membranes requires further assessment. Because these membranes are composite materials, thermal regeneration is not feasible, which places constraints on repeated use.
