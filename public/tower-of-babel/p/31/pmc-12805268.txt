Introduction

There has been a growing interest in cardiac contractility modulation (CCM), which involves delivering high-voltage, nonexcitatory impulses during the absolute refractory period using an implanted device; strengthening myocardial contraction; and facilitating reverse cardiac remodeling through various mechanisms including changes in cellular calcium handling and metabolism.1

CCM has predominantly been studied among patients with New York Heart Association functional class II–IV heart failure (HF), with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of >25% to <45% and a QRS duration of <130 ms. CCM has been shown to improve functional status and quality of life, with scarce randomized and observational data suggesting improvements in mortality and hospitalizations although needing further investigation.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 However, no practice guideline recommendations currently exist reflecting the paucity of evidence for improvement of long-term outcomes, apart from acknowledgment of small improvements in functional status and quality of life by the 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure and 2021 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute and Chronic Heart Failure.7

Nonetheless, careful patient selection remains crucial, acknowledging the spectrum of possible interactions of CCM devices with other cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), as well as influence on long-term advanced HF optimization strategies if any. We present a patient with systolic HF experiencing inappropriate subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD) shocks in the presence of a CCM device.

Case report

We present a 67-year-old man with a medical history significant for coronary artery disease and inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction 12 years ago with percutaneous coronary intervention to the right coronary artery (RCA) with multiple stents, subsequent RCA stent occlusions and chronic total occlusion of RCA, ischemic cardiomyopathy with reduced LVEF (22%) status post Optimizer Smart System CCM device (Impulse Dynamics Inc) insertion at an outside facility 7 months ago, 35% infarcted myocardial area on nuclear stress test with minimal ischemia, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) status post EMBLEM S-ICD (Boston Scientific) placement at an outside facility 1 year ago for primary sudden cardiac death prevention on propafenone 225 mg twice daily and metoprolol succinate 50 mg daily, end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis, severe obstructive peripheral arterial disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia who presented to the emergency department after experiencing 2 S-ICD shocks. Home medications included propafenone, metoprolol succinate, aspirin, atorvastatin, amlodipine, Entresto, sevelamer, magnesium oxide, and oxycodone. Prepresentation electrocardiogram (ECG) noted a sinus rhythm, right bundle branch block with QRS duration of 194 ms, and heart rate of 66 beats per minute (bpm) (Figure 1).

On presentation to the emergency department, the patient was relatively asymptomatic with no chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, lightheadedness, or dizziness. He did not report recent weight gain, swelling, or orthopnea. Blood pressure was 83/42 mm Hg with a pulse rate of 50 bpm, respirations of 20 breaths per minute, temperature of 36.5°C, and oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. ECG revealed intermittent sinus bradycardia with frequent ventricular ectopy (Figure 1). Telemetry noted frequent runs of NSVT. Laboratory work revealed sodium of 129 mmol/L, potassium of 4.6 mmol/L, magnesium of 2.6 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen of 50 mg/dL, creatinine of 8.44 mg/dL, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide of >70,000 pg/mL, high-sensitivity troponin at 979 ng/L with a delta of −7%, hemoglobin of 12.7 g/dL, white blood cell count of 13.2 × 10(9)/L, platelets of 206 × 10(9)/L, and lactate of 1.9 mmol/L. Chest radiograph noted cardiac enlargement, without overt pulmonary edema, and CCM and S-ICD were visualized (Figure 2). His last dose of propafenone was that morning; thus, amiodarone initiation was deferred. Given relative hemodynamic stability and asymptomaticity, intravenous lidocaine initiation was also held off. Propafenone was discontinued.

Portable chest radiograph noting right-sided cardiac contractility modulation device and left-sided subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator.

A cardiac device interrogation of his S-ICD revealed T-wave oversensing, with the patient experiencing inappropriate shocks despite spontaneous termination of NSVT (Figure 3). The SMART Pass filter was already on. During reprogramming, the S-ICD SMART Charge duration was increased from 3.84 seconds (14 intervals) to 4.8 seconds (17 intervals). Shock zone was increased from 230 bpm to 250 bpm. He was programmed in the primary vector with a gain setting of 1×. We tested all 3 sensing vectors (primary, secondary, and alternate). A change in vector and the gain setting were discussed and discouraged by technical support owing to the same or worse sensing issues compared with the primary vector.

He continued to have frequent NSVT overnight. The next day, he was started on an intravenous amiodarone infusion and subsequently transitioned to oral amiodarone with a slow oral load given his history of gastrointestinal side effects with oral amiodarone. Transesophageal echocardiogram done during incessant ventricular ectopy noted LVEF of 24% with severely generalized left ventricular hypokinesis and scarring and akinesis of basal to mid inferior segments, moderate mitral annual calcification with mild regurgitation, moderately reduced right ventricular (RV) systolic function (fractional area change 22%) with mildly enlarged RV size and RV systolic pressure of 41 mm Hg (right atrial pressure 15 mm Hg), mild to moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and moderately enlarged inferior vena cava with reduced collapse.

Patient required as-needed midodrine and arterial line placement for closer hemodynamic monitoring to facilitate hemodialysis. Over the hospital course, ventricular ectopy substantially improved with a reduction in the frequency of NSVT and improvement in hemodynamics (Figure 4). He was evaluated by the advanced HF and heart transplant team for planning of advanced HF therapies. He decided to forego aggressive medical interventions and expressed a preference for palliative care.

Discussion

Incessant ventricular ectopy in advanced HF with hemodynamic compromise may necessitate advanced HF therapies including expedited heart transplant evaluation. In this case, prompt recognition of possible device cross talk, appropriate reprogramming, and pharmacologic antiarrhythmic therapy facilitated patient stabilization. Our patient’s S-ICD experienced oversensing, leading to multiple inappropriate shocks. We also noted a lack of device markers during the last treated episode, which the device technical support team suspected was related to the storage capacity of the device. Device cross talk was not felt to be the cause of oversensing and inappropriate shocks. Our patient had been on propafenone for a month for PVCs and NSVT, which was likely pursued owing to limited pharmacologic antiarrhythmic options; however, that may have led to increased proarrhythmia in his case given underlying ischemic cardiomyopathy with reduced LVEF. Although the exact etiology of inappropriate therapies is unclear, it is possible that propafenone could have contributed to oversensing owing to its ECG effects including QRS widening and QT prolongation.

Undersensing and oversensing owing to interaction with CCM have been previously reported.8, 9, 10, 11 Concomitant device use is listed as a warning in the S-ICD product manual. The product manual also specifies that paced-ECG should be used to ensure the patient passes the screening criteria. However, in our patient’s case, the CCM was placed after the S-ICD elsewhere. CCM/S-ICD interaction was tested at the time of the procedure, and there was none.

The CCM product manual lists inappropriate ICD behavior as a result of interaction with the CCM as a potential adverse effect. Sensing configuration change could have been a consideration, although that was not appropriate for our patient. The S-ICD declares tachycardia when the 4 R-to-R interval average enters the therapy zone. Once a tachycardia is declared, the 4 R-to-R interval average must become longer (in milliseconds) than the lowest rate zone plus 40 ms for 24 cycles for the device to consider the episode to have ended. For capacitor charging to start, 3 conditions must be met: X of Y criterion is satisfied (18 of 24), persistent requirement is satisfied, and the last 2 certified intervals are in the treatable zone. Reconfirmation before therapy delivery requires the last 3 consecutive detected intervals (regardless of whether the intervals are certified or suspect) to be faster than the lowest therapy zone. Therefore, we are unable to determine whether the ventricular tachycardia run without markers in Figure 3C was sensed appropriately, although, based on the 18 of 24 criteria, it most likely was.

The SMART Charge is a feature designed to prevent inappropriate S-ICD shocks. The “Shock Zone” uses rate as the only criterion to determine whether a rhythm will be treated with a shock. By extending the SMART Charge time from 3.84 to 4.8 seconds, we extended the number of ventricular events needed to occur at the specified interval before therapy is delivered. Increasing the shock zone from 230 to 250 bpm also made it less likely for nonphysiological events and oversensing to cause the device to discharge. No change was made to the conditional shock zone given that this zone uses discriminators to determine whether a shock is warranted to treat an arrhythmia.

Based on outside records, given the patient’s generally poor functional status in the setting of end-stage renal disease on dialysis and peripheral arterial disease with chronic lower extremity wounds, the decision was made to proceed with S-ICD instead of cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) with a defibrillator. For patients who do not meet criteria for CRT, CCM may play a role in HF device-based management. Analysis from the Mannheim Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy Registry and the Mannheim Cardiac Contractility Modulation Observational Study noted comparable 12-month improvements in New York Heart Association class, LVEF, and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion with CCM and CRT with a defibrillator.12 Ongoing advances in CCM device technology such as the integration of ICD therapy (CCM-D, Optimizer Integra) may help streamline CIED management and broaden adoption. However, there is a need for further elucidation of the impact of CCM on long-term hard cardiovascular outcomes such as mortality and HF hospitalizations, to guide patient selection, set therapy expectations, and facilitate broader coordination of HF therapies. Careful consideration of patient factors, overall prognosis, and possible technical challenges with device implantation and management is essential. Further research is needed to investigate the feasibility, compatibility, and safety profile of CCM among patients with HF also needing implantation of other CIEDs.

In conclusion, among patients with multiple cardiac devices, there is a need for careful analysis of delivered therapies and device detection to avoid attributing inappropriate therapies to device interactions, acknowledging the potential for drug-device interactions.
