Techniques and challenges in studying photosynthetic diversity in freshwater submerged vascular plants
Authors: Ellie Loew‐Mendelson, David Wickell, Calista Mrozinski, Lizzy Iliana Doan, Jason Hupp, Richard L. Vath, Lingling Yuan, Karolina Heyduk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the photosynthetic diversity of aquatic plants rivals that of terrestrial species, the environmental conditions underlying that diversity fundamentally differ. Despite these environmental differences, aquatic and terrestrial plants have convergently evolved carbon concentrating mechanisms (CCMs). However, characterization of these pathways in submerged plants has lagged behind terrestrial systems due to methodological constraints of the aquatic environment. Here we review and evaluate contemporary methods for detecting CCMs in aquatic plants. Physiological methods, including gas exchange and isotopic analyses, provide valuable insights into terrestrial plants but face significant challenges in aquatic systems. Biochemical assays of organic acid accumulation reliably detect CCMs in aquatic species but may struggle to detect weak or non‐canonical CCMs in submerged plants. Gene expression assays of canonical CCM pathway genes may provide a sensitive tool for characterizing photosynthetic diversity in a wide range of aquatic species. While the methods and frameworks developed for terrestrial plants do not necessarily directly translate to aquatic systems, extending these methods and integrating multiple lines of evidence can improve our ability to characterize photosynthetic diversity in aquatic plants.

Photosynthesis is central to life on Earth, converting sunlight and carbon dioxide (CO2) into organic molecules that provide energy for nearly every living organism on the planet. The most common photosynthetic pathway in green plants—C3 photosynthesis—is characterized by Rubisco fixing carbon from CO2 directly into a three‐carbon compound that is eventually converted to sugars to sustain plant growth (Calvin and Benson, 1948). In low‐carbon environments—for example, when stomata close in response to drought—the reduced availability of CO2 drives an increase in a competing reaction called photorespiration, where free oxygen is fixed in place of carbon. Photorespiration is energetically costly, and plants living in environments that promote photorespiration have frequently evolved mechanisms to minimize it (Hagemann et al., 2016; Sage and Stata, 2021). Since the initial description of C3 photosynthesis by Calvin, Benson, and Bassham (Calvin and Benson, 1948) and subsequent discovery of oxygenation by Rubisco (Bowes et al., 1971), characterizing the diverse ways that plants minimize photorespiration has become a central goal of modern plant physiology.

Among the processes that minimize photorespiration, carbon concentrating mechanisms (CCMs) are of interest for their ability to saturate Rubisco with CO2 despite environments that limit carbon. In vascular plants, the two most common CCMs are Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) and C4. Remarkable for their convergent evolution in every major lineage of vascular plants, both CAM and C4 use phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) to fix atmospheric CO2 into four‐carbon intermediates, typically malate, that serve as carbon reserves (Sage et al., 2011; Gilman et al., 2023) (Figure 1). PEPC activity is separated temporally by CAM plants, where CO2 fixation by PEPC occurs at night and stored malate is decarboxylated during the daytime (Pucher et al., 1947) (Figure 1A). In the C4 pathway, PEPC activity is separated from Rubisco spatially by sequestering PEPC in the mesophyll cells and Rubisco in specialized bundle sheath cells or, in the case of a few single‐celled C4 plants (Figure 1B), within intracellular compartments (Voznesenskaya et al., 2001, 2002). In both C4 and CAM, CO2 is subsequently released from the four‐carbon intermediate and concentrated around Rubisco, allowing Rubisco to be saturated with carbon, even when CO2 supply is otherwise environmentally limited.

In terrestrial systems, CO2 limitation is typically due to water limitation or high temperatures. Low water availability can reduce stomatal conductance and limit the supply of CO2 inside the leaf, and high temperatures alter the kinetics of Rubisco such that it begins to favor oxygen over CO2, thereby increasing photorespiratory rates (Lorimer and Andrews, 1973; Jordan and Ogren, 1984). CAM and C4 were initially characterized in terrestrial plants (Thomas, 1949; Kortschak et al., 1965; Winter and Smith, 1996; Sage, 2004), and model plants in CCM research are overwhelmingly terrestrial species (Sage et al., 2011; Gilman et al., 2023), with the exception of the nonvascular algal CCMs (see Beardall and Raven, 2020). Subsequent research has shown that both CAM and C4 are present in vascular aquatic species (Madsen and Sand‐Jensen, 1991; Keeley, 1998; Bowes et al., 2002). This notable case of evolutionary convergence is evidence of a common selective pressure—reduced availability of CO2 to Rubisco—in both terrestrial and aquatic environments. In aquatic environments, poor diffusion of CO2 through the water, its rapid depletion during the day by algae, and the predominance of bicarbonate over dissolved CO2 present a similarly low‐carbon environment for submerged plants (Keeley and Sandquist, 1992; Stumm and Morgan, 2013; Kaijser et al., 2021).

While aquatic vascular plants evolved from terrestrial ancestors, CAM and C4 have arisen independently in aquatic lineages and exhibit photosynthetic strategies that do not map neatly onto terrestrial definitions of CAM or C4 (Sage et al., 2011; Gilman et al., 2023). For instance, terrestrial CAM species are typically defined by a tight association between stomatal regulation to reduce water loss and accompanying succulence; aquatic CAM plants do not generally exhibit either of these traits, posing unique challenges for assessing CAM activity in aquatics. Likewise, terrestrial C4 is defined by specialized Kranz anatomy to facilitate the separation of PEPC activity, but submerged aquatic C4 species typically lack Kranz anatomy and often maintain C3 and C4 pathways within the same cell (Bowes, 2011), a variation that is only occasionally seen in terrestrial plants (von Caemmerer et al., 2014).

In combination with a high degree of plasticity exhibited by many aquatic plants (described in full below), it is likely that CCMs are being misclassified, underestimated, or entirely overlooked in aquatic plants using conventional approaches. With a fuller understanding of how aquatic plants assimilate carbon, we can begin to answer questions about the convergent evolution of this trait, the ancestral state of photosynthesis in aquatic lineages, and the effects of photosynthetic traits on community ecology for aquatic plants. For example, did photosynthetic pathways shift before or after the transition to aquatic habitats? Are CCMs associated with other traits in submerged aquatics, such as growth patterns or resilience to global change?

CAM and C4 are complex traits that are thought to arise via the co‐option and regulatory rewiring of genes in existing pathways (Reyna‐Llorens and Hibberd, 2017; Heyduk et al., 2019; Wai et al., 2019). In part due to this complexity, neither can be understood as a single binary trait but rather as a dynamic suite of traits that can vary in degree, timing, and context (Engelmann et al., 2003; Lauterbach et al., 2017; Winter, 2019). Trait variation is particularly true of CAM plants, which span a continuum from weak and facultative forms to strong constitutive CAM. This complexity means that the presence or absence of CAM cannot always be inferred from a single assay. Additionally, due to their environmental plasticity, plants may need to be examined under different environmental conditions to distinguish their photosynthetic pathway. Detecting and classifying CCMs therefore requires multiple lines of evidence, integrating physiological, biochemical, and genetic data.

In this review, we examine the physiological, biochemical, and molecular methods that have been applied to identify CCMs in submerged aquatic vascular plants, highlighting their strengths and limitations. We review a rich history of aquatic plant ecophysiology, assess whether methods employed for terrestrial organisms can provide meaningful information for aquatics (Appendices 1 and 3), and provide new data on molecular methods (Appendix 2) for characterizing aquatic CCMs. We further highlight the importance of evaluating CCMs in submerged aquatic plants without being confined to the framework of past investigations in terrestrial systems. By providing a comprehensive overview of considerations and methods for non‐model aquatic plants, we aim to facilitate further research on the diversity of aquatic plant physiology.

AQUATIC CHARACTERISTICS THAT IMPACT PHYSIOLOGY

Vascular aquatic plants rely on the aquatic habitat to complete their life cycle, living partially or completely submerged in water. Among flowering plants, vascular aquatic plants comprise 407 genera representing just 1.5% of species but are the product of a remarkable 97 independent transitions from terrestrial to aquatic habits (Meseguer et al., 2022). In particular, adaptations to the challenges of submerged life have resulted in enormous variation in aquatic plant leaf form and function. Leaves for aquatic species can range from emergent, floating, to submerged (Figure 2). Within submerged leaves, leaf morphology can be whole, highly dissected, or even fenestrated. The internal structure of leaves can be a few cells thick or have large internal air spaces called aerenchyma. Submerged aquatic plants have leaf arrangements that can be defined by two categories: (1) caulescent, with long leafy stems (e.g., Elodea Michx., Egeria Planch. [Hydrocharitaceae]), and (2) rosette, with ribbon‐like or needle‐like leaves (e.g., Vallisneria P. Micheli ex L. [Hydrocharitaceae], Littorella P. J. Bergius [Plantaginaceae], Isoetes L. [Isoetaceae]) (Sculthorpe, 1967) (Figure 2A–G).

Leaves of submerged aquatic plants often have a lower density of stomata relative to terrestrial plants or may lack functional stomata entirely (Sculthorpe, 1967; Iida et al., 2016; Horiguchi et al., 2019; Li et al., 2019). Without functional stomata, aquatic plants do not experience the same trade‐off as terrestrial plants with regard to carbon acquisition and water loss (Bowes et al., 2002). Gas exchange occurs passively, with dissolved CO2 diffusing directly across the epidermal layer, meaning that concentration gradients determine uptake of CO2 as opposed to the cadence of stomatal conductance that we see in terrestrial plants (Maberly and Spence, 1983). For example, in environments where CO2 is more abundant at night (e.g., heavily vegetated lake environments with high nocturnal respiration), passive diffusion may result in uptake of carbon at night, which is then stored in C4 acids for later decarboxylation and refixation (Van et al., 1976; Holaday and Bowes, 1980).

Aquatic plants are also adapted to low‐carbon environments, as CO2 diffuses 104 times more slowly in water versus air and its availability fluctuates both seasonally and daily (Stumm and Morgan, 2013). Dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) originates from both minerals (e.g., sediments) and CO2 from the atmosphere. In the water, DIC exists as multiple molecular species that are buffered in equilibrium with one another; the fluctuation between CO2, bicarbonate (HCO3 −), and carbonate (CO3 2−) is described as the carbonate system (Figure 2H). A plant's access to carbon is mediated by equilibria both within the water and between the water and the atmosphere. In slow‐moving or unstirred water (e.g., river water), CO2 abundance can be very high compared to atmospheric CO2 (Stumm and Morgan, 2013). The pH of water bodies largely determines the relative makeup of DIC species (although temperature and salts contribute as well). Many water bodies where plants can be found exist at pH values where HCO3 − predominates (Figure 2H). However, CO2 is the only form of dissolved carbon that can passively diffuse across plant membranes, meaning that HCO3 – is not a readily accessible form of carbon for plants. Direct HCO3 − uptake is an active process, which led to scientific debates in the early 1900s about how aquatic plants facilitate movement of HCO3 – across membranes (Wilmott, 1921; James, 1928). No active transport has been documented in freshwater vascular plants, but it is now widely agreed that submerged vascular plants can acquire carbon via micro‐acidification of the surrounding layer around the leaf. This involves converting HCO3 − to CO2 so that it can freely diffuse across membranes (also called “apoplastic conversion”) (Smith and Walker, 1980; Prins et al., 1982; Cavalli et al., 2012; Rubio et al., 2017; Poschenrieder et al., 2018; Li et al., 2025). In select marine seagrasses, active transport of HCO3 − may occur through an H+‐energized membrane protein (Rubio et al., 2017); algae and cyanobacteria also have well‐documented HCO3 – transport mechanisms (Walker et al., 1980). For a review of the extensive CCMs in algae, see Giordano et al. (2005) and Beardall and Raven (2020).

The aquatic habit is clearly different from a terrestrial environment, resulting in vast differences in how submerged plants access and use CO2. Testing submerged aquatics’ photosynthetic physiology requires methods that consider the differences in aquatic versus terrestrial environments.

ISOTOPE DISCRIMINATION AND CARBON UPTAKE

Carbon naturally occurs as two stable isotopes, 12C and 13C. These isotopes occur at different abundances in the air, with the 13C isotope of CO2 comprising only a small fraction of atmospheric CO2 (~1%). Carboxylating enzymes discriminate between 12C and 13C to varying degrees, resulting in quantifiable differences in stable isotope ratios (δ13C) of 13C/12C relative to a carbon standard (Farquhar et al., 1989). Rubisco discriminates against 13C to a greater extent than PEPC, resulting in a less negative δ13C value (i.e., closer to atmospheric) for plants that use a greater proportion of PEPC fixation (Osmond et al., 1973). For typical C3 plants, this value ranges from −32‰ to −22‰, while CAM and C4 plants display values higher than −20‰. While carbon isotope ratios do not differentiate between C4 and CAM (both pathways use PEPC fixation), this method remains a powerful tool because fresh or dry leaves can be used as the source material, including samples from herbaria (see Crayn et al., 2015 for a study of more than 1800 species). Due to the ease of obtaining material and the relative accessibility of stable isotope labs, carbon isotope ratios have been used widely in the terrestrial plant literature to document the diversity of C4 and CAM across plant lineages (Silvera et al., 2010; Horn et al., 2014; Heyduk et al., 2016).

While carbon isotope ratios are a straightforward method for delineating species that use CCMs in terrestrial plants, they are not a viable method for aquatics due to two main biases. The first bias is generated from the carbon source—carbon can enter aquatic ecosystems through diffusion from the atmosphere, mineral weathering, or respiration from aquatic biota. Each source (atmosphere, minerals, biota) carries a different isotopic ratio of 12C and 13C, meaning that δ13C is variable in aquatic systems and is largely dependent on carbon source (Campeau et al., 2017). The second bias is generated via fractionation, where carbon isotopes are incorporated at different rates during the conversion between carbon species. Fractionation results in dissolved HCO3 − containing more 13C compared to dissolved CO2 by approximately 8–12‰ (Mook et al., 1974). The δ13C of plants may therefore contain signals of carbon source or carbon species as opposed to reflecting discrimination by carboxylating enzymes. The result is that aquatic plants exhibit a broad and variable range of isotope values, ranging from −50.0‰ to −7.5‰, regardless of their presumed photosynthetic pathway (Keeley and Sandquist, 1992; Chappuis et al., 2017).

One area where δ13C might be useful is distinguishing between plants that uptake HCO3 – and those that do not. For example, a study by Maberly et al. (1992) shows a relationship between HCO3 − uptake and δ13C ratios where plants able to take up HCO3 − had less negative values (−21.40‰ to −11.03‰), whereas plants primarily using CO2 had very negative δ13C values (−34.5‰ to −30.0‰). Chappuis et al. (2017) sampled plants across different water bodies and found that δ13C values of submerged aquatic plants varied more widely in the pH range between 7 and 8, when bicarbonate is the most common carbon form. Similarly, Pronin et al. (2024) found that aquatic vegetation using HCO3 − showed large variability in δ13C (−33.1‰ to −9.9‰), whereas CAM and C3 plants lacked variability and showed comparable δ13C ranges. While there might be interest in using δ13C to understand carbon acquisition (e.g., using CO2 versus HCO3 −), this metric cannot be used to distinguish between Rubisco and PEPC carboxylation in aquatic species.

GAS EXCHANGE AND CARBON ASSIMILATION RATES

Direct measurement of CO2 uptake is routinely used in terrestrial plant physiology, and the ability to track CO2 uptake in the dark has been key to describing CAM species and their ecophysiological dynamics (Nobel, 1976; Black and Osmond, 2003). Such methods are typically not destructive and can be done in situ, allowing measurements on plants under a variety of natural and experimental conditions. For submerged aquatic species, there is an added complication that measurements must be conducted while the plant is under water. Moreover, while we can measure the ambient CO2 concentration in the air, direct measurements of dissolved CO2 in water are difficult due to pH‐dependent interconversion with HCO3 −, low rates of gaseous diffusion, and steep environmental gradients of CO2 near the boundary layer of leaves. Historically, researchers have solved these issues in measuring CO2 uptake in aquatic plants by bubbling air with a known concentration of CO2 through a chamber containing a plant sample. Infrared gas analyzers (IRGAs) were then used to measure the change in CO2 concentration in the air of the chamber over time (Van et al., 1976; Holaday and Bowes, 1980; Keeley and Bowes, 1982). Others have measured oxygen evolution as a proxy for photosynthetic rates, most commonly using a Clark‐type O2 electrode (Van et al., 1976; Colmer and Pedersen, 2008). Oxygen evolution, however, can only be a proxy of the light‐coupled Rubisco carboxylation and thus does not inform on the presence or activity of a CCM.

Measuring gas exchange has been largely standardized for terrestrial plants thanks to the development of commercial gas analyzers, many of which are “open” systems where a continuous stream of air is moved across the leaf sample (e.g., LI‐6800 Portable Photosynthesis System; LI‐COR, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA). In such systems, the difference in CO2 and water vapor between the air stream before and after it interacts with a leaf are used to compute photosynthesis and transpiration rates (Busch et al., 2024). The amount of CO2 in the air stream is measured by an IRGA, while water vapor can be measured either via an IRGA or relative humidity sensor. Gas analyzer systems are powerful tools to measure terrestrial carbon assimilation, and modifications to chamber design have allowed measurements on entire plants (Huber et al., 2018), soils (Macias et al., 2025), or even ecosystems (Baldocchi, 2020). However, the nature of aquatic plants requires modifications to the gas analyzer chamber to accommodate a water column. LI‐COR's aquatic chamber (LI‐COR 6800‐18), for example, improves upon earlier work that used a flask to submerge an aquatic plant. In the case of the LI‐COR chamber, air is constantly circulated across the water column in an open system and the gas differentials in the headspace are used to estimate assimilation rates (Figure 3, Appendix 1). The advantage of such an open gas exchange system is that it allows researchers to obtain measurements on equilibrated samples, even if it takes some time for the plant sample and media to come to equilibrium (see Appendix 1 for example methods for measuring aquatic species). The built‐in fluorometer also provides a mechanism to measure photosystem function at the same time as gas exchange.

Net assimilation as measured by the LI‐6800 is the difference between photosynthesis and respiration. The CO2 compensation point (Γ, units for aquatic plants: µL CO2/L) is the CO2 concentration at which the rate of respiration matches the rate of photosynthesis, making the net assimilation zero. The CO2 compensation point can help differentiate between C3 and C4 plants, the latter of which have lower Γ due to the use of PEPC for carboxylation (Krenzer et al., 1975). The environment heavily influences Γ in aquatic plants. For example, Salvucci and Bowes (1981) incubated submerged plants in two different environments to mimic summer and winter (heat/high light versus cold/low light), resulting in considerably lower Γ during the heat/high light treatment. Hydrilla verticillata (L.f.) Royle has a Γ of 10 µL/L during the heat/high light treatment, whereas during the cold/low light treatment the Γ is 84 µL/L. Egeria densa Planch. showed a similar pattern, with 17 µL/L versus 43 µL/L in the summer versus winter treatments. Aquatic plants have the ability to shift Γ in response to environment regardless of PEPC activity; for example, Cabomba caroliniana A. Gray is reported to have Γ values of 10 µL/L during heat/high light and 82 µL/L during cold/low light, but PEPC activity is consistently low across treatments, suggesting that submerged macrophytes may be able to modulate Γ through other means (Salvucci and Bowes, 1981). No recent work has investigated what might be driving plasticity in Γ from freshwater submerged plants, but we are hopeful that with new advances in IRGA technology further work can uncover more of the unique mechanisms that aquatic plants use to prevent respiration and adapt to their low‐carbon environments.

CARBON LABELING

Carbon labeling is a useful tool to track the assimilation and flow of carbon through plants. Pulse‐chase studies feed labeled carbon to plants (formerly labeled 14C, modernly 13C; Pang et al., 2021) followed by tissue sampling for a period ranging from seconds to minutes. As the labeled carbon assimilates into the plant via initial photosynthetic carboxylation reactions, the time series sampling demonstrates the path of the labeled carbon through metabolites (Jones et al., 1983). This method was foundational in the original description of the C3 pathway and aided in the description of C4 (Kortschak et al., 1965) and CAM (Thomas and Ranson, 1954). In typical C3 plants, labeled products are overwhelmingly recovered in 3‐phosphoglycerate and sugar phosphates, but in the case of CAM and C4 plants, the majority (>70%) of the label is recovered in four‐carbon compounds (Hatch et al., 1967; Hatch, 1971).

Multiple carbon labeling experiments using aquatic plants in the Hydrocharitaceae have recovered considerable 14C in C4 acids, generally malate and aspartate (Degroote and Kennedy, 1977; Browse et al., 1980; Holaday and Bowes, 1980; Reiskind and Bowes, 1991). However, the labeled organic acids did not appear to move through the C4 pathway's decarboxylation and subsequent refixation by Rubisco. Instead, malate stays relatively stable during light respiration—even as a large pool—and is steadily lost during dark respiration, suggesting that the malate pool might be mostly metabolized via the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Browse et al., 1980); these C4 acid pools could simply be used for homeostasis in the cell or for temporary storage of carbon reserves (Smith and Raven, 1976; Martinoia and Rentsch, 1994) (Figure 1C). Notably, carbon labeling in Hydrilla verticillata in initial research by Holaday and Bowes (1980) did not find rapid turnover of C4 acids, but C4 photosynthesis was later concluded based on multiple lines of evidence, such as PEPC localization (Reiskind et al., 1997).

Carbon labeling can extend beyond metabolite formation and also indicate carbon allocation and fixation dynamics. In Hydrilla verticillata, labeled carbon indicated that the species fixed primarily CO2 instead of HCO3 – and that Rubisco was more active than PEPC, which had an enzymatic activity of 18 µmol/mg Chl h–1 (µmol per mg‐chlorophyll‐hour; samples were normalized by chlorophyll abundance and time) (Van et al., 1976). Later work in Hydrilla reported PEPC activity of 330 µmol/mg Chl h–1 (Holaday and Bowes, 1980) using the same methodology. One possible explanation for these conflicting measurements is that the plants were kept in different conditions; as described earlier in this review, aquatic plants’ physiological response seems to rely heavily on the environment and should be considered before experimental setup and in interpreting published results.

Carbon labeling technology has advanced significantly since these studies were published; newer technologies and analytical methods, like metabolic flux analysis, can provide higher resolution of metabolic turnover and product–precursor relationships (Koley et al., 2024). An exciting development in current carbon labeling techniques is the ability to integrate labeling into metabolomics, and we provide a brief discussion below (see “Acid accumulation and malate cycling”). Future work that analyzes when and where metabolites are formed will be crucial to our understanding of how carbon flows through submerged aquatic plants and how that changes under different environments.

ENZYMES: LOCALIZATION, ACTIVITY, AND ABUNDANCE

Another approach to understand photosynthetic pathway diversity in aquatic species is to track enzymes, either in their gene expression or localization, enzyme activity and kinetics, or via screens of post‐translational modifications. Enzymatic methods that assay PEPC location, activity, or kinetics are available, but these often require specialized tools or equipment. Enzyme localization at the cell level can help clarify the dynamics of single cell‐C4 mechanisms, as in Hydrilla verticillata (Reiskind et al., 1989). However, this method requires raising antibodies specific to PEPC for hybridization, followed by transmission electron microscopy to visualize sites where the antibody indicates PEPC is present within cells. PEPC activity in aquatic plants has been measured through combining plant extracts with phosphoenolpyruvate substrate, but this method requires access to a mass spectrometer or other liquid scintillation technology (Van et al., 1976; Jahnke et al., 1991; Boyd et al., 2015). PEPC activity can also be measured using several different reaction proxies; for example, hydration of carbonic anhydrase (Boyd et al., 2015) or the reduction of oxaloacetic acid by NADH (which can be measured on a spectrophotometer; Ashton et al., 1990). Finally, PEPC kinetics assays are an excellent tool for measuring PEPC affinity and carboxylation rate, both of which can be diagnostic of PEPC used for C4 photosynthesis in terrestrial plants (DiMario and Cousins, 2019). However, kinetic assays require purified proteins, either via antibody capture or through purification via Escherichia coli, and neither method is necessarily straightforward to establish in a lab.

An alternative to enzymatic measurements is gene expression assays, which are relatively straightforward to set up in a lab and can be used across both model and non‐model species. For example, although PEPC is present in all plants and functions to replenish citric acid cycle intermediates (O'Leary et al., 2011), its role as the initial carboxylation enzyme in C4 and CAM plants results in its transcriptional upregulation relative to C3 taxa (Heyduk et al., 2019). Extensive transcriptomic studies have demonstrated increased expression of PEPC and, in CAM species, a shifted peak in expression to the late afternoon and evening (Ming et al., 2015; Wai et al., 2019; Wickell et al., 2021; Heyduk et al., 2022). In Portulaca oleracea L., a C4 plant that can use CAM under drought, CAM pathway genes, including the PEPC copy used for CAM, are upregulated exclusively under drought, and upon rewatering are no longer upregulated (Ferrari et al., 2020a, 2020b). The increase in transcript expression in CCM taxa could be a useful tool for screening aquatic species, which are recalcitrant to other methods (described above). Gene expression is typically measured using whole leaf or whole plant extractions, and researchers should take care to design gene expression assays with plant morphology in mind. For example, Gu et al. (2015) found that Egeria densa has different isoforms of PEPC expressed in leaves versus stems, and the isoform in the stem may be involved in a CCM; had only leaves been sampled, this tissue‐specific CCM expression would have been undetected. We illustrate the applicability of using gene expression assays for detecting CCMs in aquatic plants in Appendix 2.

ACID ACCUMULATION AND MALATE CYCLING

Overnight acid accumulation in photosynthetic tissues is a strong indicator of CAM (Figure 1A) and can be measured through a variety of methods that range from indirect (e.g., total titratable acidity) to direct (e.g., enzymatic assays) measures of malate. Diel titratable acidity has been used to screen potential CAM species across many aquatic genera (Keeley, 1998). While there are no issues with using titratable acidity as an indicator of CAM with aquatic species, there are two significant caveats. The first is that titratable acidity does not measure for malate specifically, and aquatic plants may be accumulating other acids that could mask the pattern of malate; notably, this is an issue for terrestrial plant measurements as well (Lüttge, 1988; Franco et al., 1992; Freschi et al., 2010). The second caveat is that titratable acidity may not detect weak CAM phenotypes (Winter and Smith, 2022), as the acid accumulation may be very low or variable between replicates, which may be the case for many aquatic plants.

Historically, titratable acidity is used as a first pass to determining CAM activity because the protocol is relatively simple and less expensive than direct measurements of malate. However, malate is not the only acid that can accumulate in plant cells, and quantifying metabolites directly can reveal what is causing diurnal shifts in plant tissue acidity. For example, Keeley (1998) reports a significant difference in morning versus evening acid accumulation (ΔH+) for Vallisneria americana Michx., but malate accumulation did not cycle diurnally (Table 1). Direct measurements of malate concentration for aquatic plants have mostly been published pre‐2000, and the standard method at the time was enzymatic assays (Degroote and Kennedy, 1977; Browse et al., 1980; Madsen, 1987; Keeley, 1998). These assays rely on the principle that malate dehydrogenase catalyzes a reaction between l‐malate and NAD+ to create NADH; the amount of l‐malate can be calculated from the increase of NADH (Bergmeyer, 1983). This is measured via spectrophotometric absorbance but uses volatile chemicals; a safer alternative is pre‐mixed colorimetric assays (e.g., MAK511 from Millipore Sigma), in which NADH reduces a colored reagent and can also be measured on the spectrophotometer.

Careful optimization of protocols is necessary to obtain better data for aquatic species. For example, it may be necessary to alter protocols to detect the low amounts of acid accumulation in aquatic plants. Many studies that track accumulation of leaf acids in terrestrial plants use a boiling method to break open cell walls (e.g., Heyduk et al., 2020), but studies have found macerating tissues prior to titrations yields better signals in aquatic species (Keeley, 1982; Madsen, 1987). Likewise, using greater amounts of tissue and homogenizing leaves in liquid nitrogen may yield more accurate results (Table 1, see Appendix 2 for two methods).

As an alternative to targeted metabolite measurements, metabolomics holds great promise for understanding acid and metabolite flux in aquatic plants, although perhaps not as a first pass screening tool. Metabolomics methods characterize a large swath of metabolite abundances within organisms but require access to a liquid chromatography or gas chromatography mass spectrophotometer (LC‐MS/GC‐MS). These methods, paired with labeled carbon methods, could provide insight into the first products of photosynthesis in aquatic species and thus determine with greater accuracy whether a species is using a CCM. Additionally, the ability to track the abundance of many hundreds of metabolites may demonstrate that other metabolic pathways are important for the aquatic lifestyle or may uncover novel routes of carbon flux that have not been described in terrestrial species.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

We are not the first to point out that submerged aquatic plants do not fit into the discrete categories of C3, C4, and CAM as described by terrestrial literature; in fact, most of the work reviewed here makes explicit mention of the fact that aquatic plants seem to have some but not all of the characteristics of a CCM pathway (be that C4 or CAM). Our ability to characterize CCMs in submerged species is also complicated by the environmental plasticity observed since the earliest studies on aquatic plant photosynthesis (Wilmott, 1921; James, 1928). The need to better characterize photosynthesis in submerged plants is clear, as accurately describing the diversity of these pathways will improve our understanding of the evolution and ecological roles of aquatic species. Climate change and anthropogenic alterations to aquatic ecosystems (e.g., eutrophication) are altering species’ presence and competitive dynamics, including expansion of invasive species’ ranges. The lack of data on the physiological response of aquatic plants to variable environments makes it difficult to predict whether aquatic nuisance species will expand their range. The management of nuisance aquatic plants is a growing area of research (June‐Wells et al., 2013; Lech and Willig, 2021; Miller et al., 2022), and a better understanding of a plant's photosynthetic physiology can help us more accurately determine how submerged species are affected by management practices such as herbicide treatment. To untangle how submerged plants—both native and nuisance—are responding to global change, we need to establish an expectation of their current physiology. Likewise, before we can begin to determine evolutionary origins and consequences of CCMs and their associated traits (e.g., ancestral state reconstruction, trait–trait and trait–environment correlations), we need to be able to accurately describe photosynthetic traits and how they differ among species.

Given the importance of defining and describing photosynthesis in aquatic submerged plants, this review highlights opportunities to remedy the methodological gaps that remain. The LI‐6800‐18 is a promising new tool to help describe photosynthetic patterns in aquatics, as the portable machine can be used in the field to obtain measurements unaffected by greenhouse tank conditions. Particularly exciting is the prospect of measuring plants in the field across an environmental gradient, or in micro‐environments within a single water body and season, to better understand the plasticity of these CCMs. Higher‐resolution carbon labeling will also help untangle the dynamics of when and why aquatic plants form C4 molecules and the fate of those molecules in metabolism. Finally, integrating gene expression with metabolite assays can provide a clearer understanding of flux through particular metabolic pathways; continued efforts in this area can help us better describe aquatic plant photosynthetic metabolism without the constraints of terrestrial CCM definitions.
