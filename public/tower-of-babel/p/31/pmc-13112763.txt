The illusion of improved nurse staffing levels in South Korea during COVID-19: a statistical artifact of policy changes and declining patient volume
Authors: Kyounga Lee, Yunmi Kim, Hyun-Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimSouth Korea revised its nurse staffing grade indicators in 2018 by changing the evaluation criteria from nurse-to-bed ratios to nurse-to-patient ratios, enabling hospitals to qualify for higher inpatient reimbursement rates. Beginning in 2020, during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, this policy was extended to all medical institutions except tertiary hospitals. This study investigated whether the revised indicators resulted in actual increases in nurse employment and examined their implications for addressing persistent nurse shortages during the COVID-19 pandemic in South Korea.MethodsA longitudinal study analyzed data from 1,152 hospitals between 2019 and 2023, examining changes in nurse staffing levels and related indicators. The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and generalized estimating equations.ResultsBy 2023, 35.9% of hospitals achieved the highest nurse-to-patient ratio grade (Grade 1), compared to only 3.0% under the nurse-to-bed ratio indicator. However, 20.8% of hospitals showed improved nurse-to-patient ratios without corresponding changes in nurse-to-bed ratios, suggesting that the improvements may be due to shifts in patient numbers rather than actual increases in nurse employment. The relative risk (RR) of improving nurse-to-bed ratio grades during the pandemic was less than 1 (RR 0.88–0.94), indicating a worsening trend, while nurse-to-patient ratio grades improved (RR 1.19–1.22).ConclusionThe revised indicators improved apparent compliance, but this change was driven by metric changes and reduced patient admissions, not a substantive reinforcement of staffing levels. Without sufficient recruitment, rising post-pandemic patient volumes may exacerbate nurse workloads and compromise care quality. Policymakers should closely evaluate the outcomes of staffing policies and ensure adequate incentives for nurse recruitment.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic profoundly affected healthcare systems worldwide, exposing limitations in medical infrastructure and workforce capacity [1]. Nurses played a pivotal frontline role in patient care during the pandemic, underscoring the critical importance of adequate nurse staffing for patient safety and treatment outcomes [2]. Despite numerous policy interventions implemented worldwide to address nursing shortages, many countries, including South Korea, continue to struggle with inadequate nurse staffing. Recent post-pandemic reports indicate that workforce shortages have worsened rather than improved [3–5].

South Korea has long experienced persistent nurse shortages, with the number of active nurses per capita remaining below the OECD average. This shortfall has resulted in higher nurse-to-patient ratios, placing a significant burden on nursing staff. In response, the South Korean government introduced various institutional changes to secure additional nursing personnel. One key policy is the nurse staffing grade system, an essential metric for assessing nurse staffing levels in hospitals. This system determines inpatient reimbursement rates based on the bed count per nurse, thereby incentivizing hospitals to hire more nurses [6]. Introduced in 1999, the nurse staffing grade system classified hospitals into seven tiers, from Grade 1 (highest) to Grade 7 (lowest), based on the number of beds assigned per nurse. In Grade 1, a nurse is responsible for the fewest number of beds, whereas in Grade 7, a nurse is responsible for the most beds.

However, using bed count as a criterion produced significant unintended consequences. Hospitals with lower bed occupancy rates were assigned worse grades despite having relatively fewer patients per nurse. Because nurse staffing grades directly influence hospital revenue through inpatient reimbursement, hospitals with lower grades faced financial strain. This created a vicious cycle wherein these hospitals could not afford to hire additional nurses and, in some cases, were forced to reduce staff [7]. To address these issues, Korea revised the nurse staffing grade system’s criteria from nurse-to-bed ratios to nurse-to-patient ratios in 2018. This change allowed hospitals to receive higher inpatient reimbursements even if their nurse staffing levels remained constant. The government mandated that at least 70% of the additional revenue generated from the revised system be allocated toward direct nursing personnel costs and other workforce-related improvements. Initially, the revised criteria applied only to hospitals in regions with low bed occupancy rates. However, starting in 2020, during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, the policy was extended to all medical institutions except for 45 tertiary hospitals [7]. According to Korea’s hospital classification system, tertiary hospitals are the highest-level medical institutions specializing in advanced and complex treatments. There are 45 such hospitals nationwide, and they were excluded from the revised system because they already maintain relatively sufficient nurse staffing and generate adequate medical revenue (since 2024, the revised system has also been applied to tertiary hospitals).

As a result, many institutions saw an improvement in their nurse staffing grade —a key indicator of nurse staffing levels—even without hiring additional nurses, solely due to the change in grading criteria. This policy change raises a critical question: do improvements in staffing grades reflect a substantive change in nurse staffing levels, or are they primarily an artifact of the revised evaluation criteria during a period of fluctuating patient volume?

The pandemic led to fluctuations in inpatient volume, with non-COVID-19 hospitalizations declining while admissions for COVID-19 increased [8, 9]. Consequently, nursing workload dynamics shifted substantially. Although resources were redirected toward the care of patients with COVID-19, care for non-COVID-19 patients was adversely affected [10]. As the economy stabilizes in the post-pandemic period and patient volume rebounds, concerns have emerged that worsening nurse shortages may lead to burnout and increased patient loads [11–13]. If patient volume rises without corresponding increases in nurse staffing, care quality may decline and nurse workloads may become unsustainable [14].

Aim

Given this background, this study aimed to evaluate nurse staffing levels in South Korea during the COVID-19 pandemic by comparing two grade metrics: the nurse-to-bed ratio and the nurse-to-patient ratio. Specifically, we examined discrepancies between these two grading systems to determine how the choice of criterion affects the interpretation of staffing adequacy during a period of fluctuating patient volume.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized publicly available data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) and employed a longitudinal design to analyze changes in nurse staffing levels from 2019 to 2023, spanning the period during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. The analysis included hospitals and general hospitals in South Korea for which data on both nurse-to-bed and nurse-to-patient ratios were available.

Data source and sample

We used prospectively collected cohort data from 2019 (pre-pandemic) to 2023 (post-pandemic) on hospitals and general hospitals nationwide, including information on hospital characteristics, nurse staffing levels, patient numbers, and bed counts. The study excluded: (1) hospitals that were newly established or closed during the study period, (2) hospitals that changed classification between hospital and general hospital, and (3) specialized institutions such as military hospitals, police hospitals, and national leprosy hospitals. The final analysis included 1,152 hospitals (265 general hospitals and 887 hospitals), representing 66.4% of South Korea’s total hospitals and general hospitals as of 2023.

Research variables

The dependent variables were the nurse staffing grades based on bed count per nurse (nurse-to-bed ratio) and patient count per nurse (nurse-to-patient ratio). In this study, staffing grades were used as the primary measure of nurse staffing levels because they are the principal policy indicator used by the South Korean government to monitor and regulate nursing workforce standards. In addition, because the administrative data did not provide raw headcounts of employed nurses, these grades served as an objective proxy for the staffing environment recognized by the National Health Insurance system. The staffing grades are determined according to standardized criteria established by the national government and are applied uniformly across medical institutions in South Korea. Accordingly, nurse staffing levels were categorized into seven grades in alignment with this national standard. Grade 1 is the highest staffing level and is defined as having 2.5 or fewer beds or patients per nurse. Grade 2 is 2.5 or more but less than 3.0; Grade 3 is 3.0 or more but less than 3.5; Grade 4 is 3.5 or more but less than 4.0; Grade 5 is 4.0 or more but less than 4.5; Grade 6 is 4.5 or more but less than 6.0; and Grade 7 is 6.0 or more [15, 16].

The independent variable was time (year), which was selected to capture continuous changes in nurse staffing grades throughout the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2023). This longitudinal approach allowed us to identify year-to-year changes in staffing grades as hospitals adapted to the prolonged public health crisis and fluctuating patient volume. To minimize confounding by inherent institutional differences, we included a comprehensive set of control variables grouped into structural, clinical, and economic factors. These control variables included hospital classification, establishment type, location, number of general ward beds, non-insured bed percentage, intensive care unit (ICU) bed ratio, presence of resident physicians (interns and residents), specialist staffing levels, number of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machines, and whether the hospital was designated as a dedicated COVID-19 inpatient facility [15–17]. Hospitals were classified according to the Korean Medical Service Act, which distinguishes between hospitals and general hospitals. A hospital is defined as a medical institution with at least 30 beds, whereas a general hospital must have at least 100 beds, a minimum of seven medical departments, and dedicated specialists in each department. Establishment type was categorized as public or private, and location was classified as either capital/metropolitan areas or other regions, including rural areas. The number of general ward beds was divided into four groups: fewer than 50 beds, 55–99 beds, 100–199 beds, and 200 or more beds. The non-insured bed percentage was defined as the proportion of private rooms and VIP suites that are not covered by national health insurance, requiring patients to bear 100% of the room charges out of pocket. The ICU bed percentage was calculated as the proportion of ICU beds relative to the total bed count. The presence of resident physicians was determined by whether the hospital employed interns and residents, and specialist staffing level was measured as the number of specialists per 100 beds. The availability of high-cost medical equipment, particularly MRI machines, was assessed by the number of MRI machines per 100 beds. Finally, hospitals were categorized based on whether they were designated as COVID-19 inpatient facilities during the pandemic, as such hospitals could not admit non-COVID-19 patients.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed to evaluate changes in nurse staffing levels following the transition in grading criteria. The specific analytical steps and their rationales were as follows. First, to demonstrate how two grading metrics—the nurse-to-bed ratio and the nurse-to-patient ratio—could yield discrepant results despite the same underlying nurse staffing levels, we compared the distribution of staffing grades in 2023 using frequencies and percentages. This step was intended to provide empirical evidence that policy-driven changes in calculation methods can produce conflicting interpretations of staffing adequacy.

Second, to assess longitudinal changes in staffing levels between 2019 and 2023, hospitals were categorized into three groups according to grade change: Improved, No Change, or Deteriorated. We also analyzed whether the discrepancy between bed-based and patient-based grades in 2023 had increased, decreased, or remained unchanged relative to the 2019 baseline. Descriptive statistics and the chi-square (χ²) test were used to examine the statistical significance of these shifts. This analysis was intended to clarify the practical implications of the policy transition by determining whether institutional staffing levels were substantively improved or merely reclassified.

Finally, to account for the within-cluster correlation inherent in longitudinal data with repeated observations from the same hospitals, a Poisson ordinal regression model with generalized estimating equations (GEE) was used. In this model, nurse staffing grades were reverse-coded so that higher numerical values represented better staffing levels. Consequently, a relative risk (RR) greater than 1 indicates a higher likelihood of being in a better staffing category than in the reference year. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Gachon University (Approval No. 1044396-202501-HR-021-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Gachon University in accordance with the Bioethics and Safety Act of Korea because the study used publicly available hospital-level disclosure data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) website rather than individually identifiable patient information. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for human subjects set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Comparison of bed-based vs. patient-based nurse staffing grades in 2023

To empirically demonstrate the discrepancy between the two staffing metrics, we first compared the distribution of nurse staffing grades in 2023 based on bed count versus patient count (Fig. 1). This comparison served as the baseline for our analysis, highlighting how the choice of grading criterion can lead to divergent interpretations of staffing adequacy even among the same hospitals. Among the 1,152 hospitals analyzed in 2023, a marked contrast was observed: only 3.0% (34 hospitals) were classified as Grade 1 (< 2.5) based on the bed-to-nurse ratio, whereas 57.6% (663 hospitals) remained in the lowest category, Grade 7 (> 6.0). However, when the same institutions were evaluated using the patient-to-nurse ratio, the proportion of Grade 1 hospitals increased more than tenfold to 35.9% (413 hospitals), and the proportion of Grade 7 hospitals decreased substantially to 32.0% (369 hospitals).

Changes in nurse staffing grades between 2019 and 2023

Between 2019 and 2023, changes in nurse staffing grades revealed a substantial discrepancy depending on the metric used. Whereas only 18.5% of hospitals showed improvement in staffing grades based on the nurse-to-bed ratio, 41.8% showed improvement when evaluated using the nurse-to-patient ratio. When the bed-based criterion was used—which reflects staffing levels relative to fixed hospital capacity—20.3% of institutions experienced deterioration in staffing grade. Thus, the proportion of hospitals with declining staffing levels exceeded the proportion with improvement (18.5%) during the pandemic. In contrast, under the patient-based metric, a greater proportion of hospitals improved than declined. Furthermore, 20.8% of hospitals (240 hospitals) showed no change in bed-based grades despite improvement in patient-based grades (Table 1).

Poisson ordinal regression analysis of nurse staffing grade changes

The GEE-based Poisson ordinal regression analysis, adjusted for institutional characteristics, identified distinct longitudinal patterns in the two staffing metrics (Table 3). For bed-based staffing grades, the relative risk (RR) ranged from 0.88 to 0.94. Because the grades were reverse-coded such that higher values indicated better staffing, an RR below 1.0 indicates that hospitals were 6% to 12% less likely to attain a better staffing grade than in 2019. In contrast, patient-based staffing grades showed significant improvement, with RR values ranging from 1.19 to 1.22 (p < .05), indicating a 19% to 22% higher likelihood of classification into a better staffing grade. These opposing trends resulted in a widening discrepancy between the two criteria over time (RR = 1.47–1.50).

Discussion

This study analyzed longitudinal discrepancies between nurse-to-bed and nurse-to-patient ratios to determine whether apparent improvements in nurse staffing grades during the COVID-19 pandemic reflected a substantive expansion of the nursing workforce or a policy-driven illusion created by changes in evaluation criteria and fluctuations in patient volume.

The following discussion interprets the study findings.

First, when assessing nurse staffing levels in South Korea using the nurse staffing grade system, higher staffing grades (indicating more nurses) appeared to increase during the pandemic. Although some studies have reported improvements in nurse staffing levels in Korean hospitals during the COVID-19 pandemic [18], caution is warranted when interpreting these findings, as they may not reflect a true increase in the number of nurses employed. Our finding suggest that improvements in nurse staffing grades during the pandemic should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect not only the substantive hiring of additional nurses but also the transition from nurse-to-bed to nurse-to-patient ratios or a fluctuations in patient admissions. This is evident in Table 1, which shows that while bed-based nurse staffing grades remained unchanged between 2019 and 2023, patient-based nurse staffing grades improved in 20.8% of institutions. Because the number of hospital beds is generally fixed, the absence of change in bed-based grades implies that the absolute number of nurses did not increase substantially; therefore, the improvement in patient-based grades likely reflects reduced patient volume rather than workforce expansion. Furthermore, the finding that 20.3% of institutions experienced deterioration in bed-based grades suggests that some hospitals may have reduced their nursing staff after the pandemic. This discrepancy is further illustrated in Fig. 1: in 2023, few hospitals achieved high bed-based grades, whereas a substantial number received favorable patient-based grades. These findings indicate that although South Korea’s nurse staffing levels appeared to improve when viewed through administrative grades, this pattern reflects a technical shift rather than a substantive improvement in actual staffing conditions. In addition, Tables 2 and 3 indicate that improvements in patient-based nurse staffing grades exceeded those in bed-based grades, resulting in a widening discrepancy between the two measures. This discrepancy provides statistical evidence that the transition in grading standards led to overestimation of staffing improvement during the pandemic, while the underlying nursing resource environment may in fact have weakened.

Despite various policy interventions and financial investments implemented worldwide during the COVID-19 pandemic to secure additional nursing personnel [4, 19], many hospitals maintained or even reduced the number of employed nurses [20, 21]. Numerous studies have consistently shown that hiring additional nurses improves patient outcomes [22–24], particularly in high-risk departments such as ICUs and emergency departments [25]. In addition, hospitals with better patient-to-RN ratios reported significantly lower mortality rates among patients with COVID-19 [17]. Nevertheless, additional nurse recruitment has remained challenging in Korean hospitals.

Although the COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical importance of adequate nursing personnel, nurse shortages persist in hospitals worldwide, including those in South Korea [21]. Because infectious disease outbreaks such as COVID-19 may become more frequent in the future, inadequate nurse staffing could pose a substantial threat to patient care during future healthcare crises.

Another pressing concern is whether hospitals are adequately prepared for a post-pandemic rebound in patient volume in the setting of insufficient nurse recruitment. During the pandemic, hospitals worldwide prioritized patients with COVID-19, leading to a decline in non-COVID-19 hospitalizations [8]. Although the workload associated with caring for patients with COVID-19 increased, the overall number of hospitalized patients decreased, possibly resulting in an artificial improvement in nurse staffing grades [18]. If hospitals do not proactively recruit and train additional nurses, a return to pre-pandemic hospitalization levels could compromise the provision of high-quality care and potentially lead to widespread burnout and increased turnover. Indeed, several studies have reported a sharp rise in nurse turnover following stabilization of the COVID-19 situation [5, 11].

Many countries have identified ineffective policies, regulations, and strategies as major contributors to nursing workforce shortages [26]. In Korea, the nurse staffing grade system classifies hospitals into Grades 1 to 7 based on bed-to-nurse ratios. To place this system in an international context, although Grade 1 is the highest administrative category, it corresponds to an actual clinical staffing assignment of approximately 1:9–12 nurse-to-patient ratio [27, 28]. This standard is less stringent than the 1:5 ratio mandated in California and the 1:8 ratio often cited as a safety threshold in the United Kingdom [22, 29]. To promote nurse employment, the Korean government has implemented various direct and indirect financial incentives and disincentives [6]. As shown in this study, revision of the nurse staffing grade system allowed hospitals to receive higher inpatient reimbursement without necessarily increasing the number of employed nurses. In addition, reimbursement differentials between nurse staffing grades have been widened, rewarding hospitals that hire more nurses with higher payments while penalizing those with insufficient staffing. However, our findings indicate that these policy efforts have not effectively translated into substantive reinforcement of nurse staffing levels, underscoring the need for a thorough review of the system [7]. The structure of incentive and reimbursement systems plays a crucial role in recruiting and retaining nurses [30]. If financial incentives and disincentives are insufficient, hospitals are unlikely to invest in hiring additional nurses. For example, in the US healthcare system, where financial support and compensation for nurses may be inadequate, nurses face higher layoff rates than physicians [30]. Furthermore, hospitals often fail to allocate sufficient financial resources to nursing staff, contributing to high turnover and persistent workforce shortages [13, 31]. These patterns suggest that the root cause of the nursing shortage is not necessarily a lack of available nurses, but rather hospitals’ failure to provide safe working environments, competitive salaries, and sufficient benefits [11].

Research indicates that the most frequently cited organizational resources desired by nurses are staffing support and financial assistance [13]. Another critical issue highlighted in this study is the oversupply of hospital beds in Korea. Figure 1 demonstrates that when the nurse staffing grade criteria shifted from bed-based to patient-based, the number of hospitals classified as Grade 1 increased dramatically. Table 2 further supports this finding by showing that the difference between bed-based and patient-based nurse staffing grades widened in many institutions, indicating low bed occupancy rates. This reflects the problem of an excessive inpatient bed supply in Korea, where the number of hospital beds per capita is among the highest in the OECD [32]. An oversupply of beds increases operational costs, prompting hospitals to reduce expenditures in other areas. Since nursing staff constitute the largest segment of hospital personnel, hospitals may attempt to cut costs by reducing nurse employment or limiting wage increases. In some cases, hospitals may hire less skilled workers or increase working hours to further reduce labor costs [33]. This could result in a vicious cycle of declining nurse employment, reduced recruitment of skilled nurses, and lower wages. Studies indicate that investing in nursing resources is linked to improved clinical outcomes without significant cost differences [34]. Therefore, reducing and optimizing unnecessary bed supply is essential to ensure that operating costs are directed toward critical areas such as nurse employment and workforce improvement.

Policy implications

The findings of this study suggest that the improvements in staffing grades observed following the COVID-19 pandemic may reflect an administratively driven shift rather than a substantive expansion of the actual nursing workforce. Because adequate nurse staffing is essential for positive patient outcomes, healthcare institutions must actively pursue the recruitment of additional nurses. Under the current grading system, it remains difficult to determine whether nursing resources have been genuinely strengthened in the post-pandemic period. If the government continues to promote nurse recruitment through an incentive-based system rather than legal mandates, the financial measures must be substantial enough to fundamentally influence hospital decision-making. Our results indicate that current policy efforts have not effectively translated into a practical expansion of the workforce because the financial rewards for achieving higher grades often do not sufficiently offset the labor costs associated with hiring additional nurses. To address this issue, reimbursement differentials between staffing grades should be widened substantially, thereby creating a clear economic imperative for hospitals to prioritize the recruitment and retention of nursing personnel. In addition to strengthening financial incentives, the government must implement rigorous monitoring to verify that these incentives and subsidies are used strictly for nurse recruitment and workplace improvement rather than being absorbed into general operational expenses.

Finally, a strategic approach to addressing the structural oversupply of hospital beds is essential. By optimizing bed supply, which is currently among the highest per capita in the OECD, the government can help hospitals break the cycle of cost-cutting in nursing staff and ensure that healthcare resources are directed efficiently toward building a sustainable and resilient nursing workforce.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the revision of the nurse staffing grade criteria was primarily intended to support small and medium-sized hospitals in rural areas, which face greater challenges in securing nursing staff. Consequently, tertiary hospitals—which generally maintain sufficient nurse staffing and high bed occupancy rates—were excluded. Therefore, this study does not account for changes in nurse staffing levels at tertiary hospitals, where severely ill patients were concentrated during the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, external factors that could influence changes in nurse staffing grades, such as economic conditions, regional differences, and hospital management practices, were not fully accounted for. Third, an important limitation is the inability to verify actual changes in the number of employed nurses because of data constraints. Because staffing levels were measured using categorical grades rather than raw employment data, improved grades may have reflected decreased patient volume rather than active hiring. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may not fully distinguish between a substantive increase in the nursing workforce and a technical shift in ratios driven by fluctuating bed or patient counts.
