Diagnostic Accuracy of Point-of-care Ultrasonography for Endotracheal Tube Confirmation: Interpreting Evidence beyond Pooled Estimates

Dear Editor,

We have recently come across a systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) on the accuracy of point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS) for confirming endotracheal tube (ETT) placement in adult emergency departments and critical care settings.1 The authors of this study are to be commended for their diligence in compiling information. Their attempts to shed some much-needed light on the role of ultrasonography in ETT confirmation, a field where time, precision, and reliability are inextricably linked to patient survival.

The pooled sensitivity of 91% and the pooled specificity of 98% indicate the high discriminatory potential of POCUS. However, interpreting these results requires an understanding of the nuances of the methodology, which could affect the results. A critical issue is the heterogeneity of the reference standards. Although waveform capnography is the gold standard for confirming ETT placement, as suggested by the recent SRMA.2 The use of composite reference standards, such as auscultation, chest radiography, and direct laryngoscopy, could introduce differential verification bias. The use of heterogeneous reference standards in the meta-analysis could lead to an artificially high pooled specificity. The pooled sensitivity estimates could also not be interpreted with any degree of accuracy.3 A sensitivity analysis using capnography as the reference standard would have improved the overall validity of the results and made them more comparable to previous high-quality reviews.4

The reduced pooled sensitivity reported in this analysis, compared with previously conducted meta-analyses, should be a subject for careful and critical thought. The implications of even a small reduction in sensitivity should be seen as having significant clinical consequences when managing the airway, since esophageal intubation, or failure to achieve successful endotracheal intubation, has the potential for causing hypoxic brain injury, cardiac arrest, or death. The inclusion of intensive care unit (ICU) patients, cardiac arrest scenarios, and operators with potentially diverse backgrounds should be viewed as positively affecting external validity. However, the exclusion of patients with difficult airway scenarios in some of the original studies raises a concern with regard to spectrum bias and generalizability to difficult airway patients.5,6 In high-acuity environments and resource-limited settings, difficult airway patients often represent the population with the greatest clinical consequence for modality effectiveness.

Another issue of interpretation arises from the analysis of time-to-confirmation outcomes. The significant heterogeneity reported here, as measured by I 2 values exceeding 99%, substantially reduces the reliability of the pooled mean differences. The varying definitions of start and end times, as well as the inclusion or exclusion of cardiopulmonary resuscitation periods, are a complex issue that is not easily quantitatively aggregated. The latest recommendations on methodology for diagnostic meta-analysis suggest that it is not advisable to aggregate data on heterogeneous time endpoints without a common definition.7

Similarly, operator dependence remains a primary factor in assessing the clinical utility of POCUS for confirming the airway. Ultrasound interpretation, by its very nature, remains a skill-dependent evaluation, and the training criteria for operator competence, while included, were neither standardized nor clearly reported. Blinding of the reference standard assessors was also unclear for a large number of studies, suggesting the potential for detection bias. In the context of well-resourced academic centers with established ultrasound curricula, high diagnostic accuracy can be assumed to be reproducible; however, caution needs to be exercised when extending this assumption to peripheral centers.4,8 Capnography, on the other hand, has the advantage of providing an objective display that is operator-independent and has been outcome-validated for decades.

The suggestion that current airway management guidelines may warrant re-evaluation in light of the growing body of POCUS-based data must be viewed within the context of the hierarchy of evidence. While the data on diagnostic accuracy are certainly compelling, the studies included in the analysis were predominantly observational. They did not measure patient-centered outcomes such as hypoxic injury, aspiration, or survival. Guideline committees generally consider outcome-based data before an adjunctive modality is considered primary and confirmatory.9,10 The current state of the data would suggest the most reasonable view is that POCUS is an adjunctive modality, particularly useful in low perfusion states, CPR, or in the absence of waveform capnography.

Despite these considerations, the meta-analysis makes a useful contribution to the growing body of knowledge supporting the use of ultrasound in airway management algorithms. The high specificity and low percentage of undetected esophageal intubations reported in the included studies again emphasize the promise of POCUS in preventing catastrophic confirmation errors. The focus for future research should be on the development of standardized ultrasound techniques, competency criteria, exclusive use of reference standards, and comparative studies that evaluate clinically relevant outcomes.11 These studies will be required to determine the exact role that POCUS has in current airway safety.

In summary, though the diagnostic accuracy appears robust, the variability in reference standards, operator training, and timing definitions cautions against definitive conclusions regarding the replacement of established modalities for confirmation. A guarded, evidence-based approach to the incorporation of POCUS into multimodal verification strategies for airway management appears the most reasonable approach, awaiting the availability of more high-quality information.
