Congenital transtentorial parahippocampal herniation presenting with new-onset seizures in a 14-year-old boy
Authors: Mohammed Elsheikh, Osman EO Elamin, Maha Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital transtentorial herniation of the parahippocampal gyrus is an uncommon developmental anomaly that can mimic pathological brain herniation. Unlike the acute form associated with raised intracranial pressure, this benign variant usually remains asymptomatic and is often discovered incidentally during neuroimaging for unrelated conditions. We report the case of a 14-year-old previously healthy boy who presented with a new-onset generalized tonic–clonic seizure followed by transient postictal confusion. Neurological and systemic examinations, as well as laboratory investigations and electroencephalography, were unremarkable. Brain MRI demonstrated a focal herniation of the left parahippocampal gyrus through the tentorial incisura with preserved cortical architecture and no evidence of mass effect, edema, or abnormal enhancement—findings consistent with a congenital (idiopathic) transtentorial herniation. The patient was managed conservatively and discharged on carbamazepine. A follow-up MRI was advised but could not be performed due to local instability. This case highlights the importance of recognizing congenital transtentorial herniation as a benign, non-emergent entity that can simulate pathological herniation or neoplasm on imaging. Awareness of its characteristic MRI features helps prevent unnecessary investigations or surgical interventions.Keywords: Congenital transtentorial herniation, Parahippocampal gyrus, Idiopathic brain herniation, Benign developmental variant, Seizures, Adolescent

Introduction

Transtentorial brain herniation is typically associated with elevated intracranial pressure resulting from trauma, tumors, or intracranial fluid accumulation [1]. However, congenital herniations, particularly those involving the parahippocampal gyrus, are increasingly identified incidentally due to the widespread use of high-resolution MRI in children and adolescents [2,3].

These congenital herniations are usually asymptomatic and exhibit distinct imaging characteristics compared to acute herniations. They typically lack features such as mass effect, midline shift, or compression of adjacent structures [4]. Recognizing this benign condition is essential to avoid misdiagnosis, particularly confusion with neoplastic lesions [3,5].

Here, we present a case of a 14-year-old boy with new-onset seizures found to have a congenital transtentorial herniation of the parahippocampal gyrus, a rare and benign developmental variant that can mimic pathological herniation on imaging.

Case report

A 14-year-old previously healthy boy presented to the emergency department after experiencing a generalized tonic–clonic seizure lasting approximately 2 minutes, followed by postictal confusion for about 15 minutes. There was no prior history of seizures, head trauma, or central nervous system infection, and his family history was negative for epilepsy. He had no significant past medical history.

On physical examination, there were no dysmorphic or syndromic features suggestive of tuberous sclerosis complex, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1), or type 2 (MEN 2). The skin examination was unremarkable, with no café-au-lait macules, nevi, or warts. Cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, and neurological examinations were all within normal limits. The patient was alert, oriented, and hemodynamically stable following recovery.

Baseline laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal function tests, serum glucose, and electrolytes, were within normal ranges. A toxicology screen was negative, and electroencephalography (EEG) showed no epileptiform discharges.

Brain MRI revealed a focal herniation of the left parahippocampal gyrus through the tentorial incisura (Figs. 1 and 2). The herniated tissue demonstrated preserved gray–white matter differentiation, with no evidence of mass effect, midline shift, edema, hydrocephalus, or abnormal enhancement. There were no features suggestive of a neoplasm, stroke, or intracranial hypertension. These findings were consistent with a congenital (idiopathic) transtentorial herniation, also known as a non-emergent parahippocampal herniation.

Axial T1-weighted MRI showing left parahippocampal gyrus herniation through the tentorial incisura without mass effect. (A) At the level of the quadrigeminal cistern. (B) At the level of the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles.

Coronal T2-weighted MRI demonstrating absence of midline shift or ventricular compression. (A) At the level of the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles. (B) At the level of the cerebellum.

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with corresponding ADC maps demonstrated no restricted diffusion. Post-contrast T1-weighted images were obtained and showed no abnormal enhancement. There was no mass effect, midline shift, edema, hydrocephalus, or cisternal compression, and no susceptibility artifact suggestive of hemorrhage. Perfusion imaging and MR spectroscopy were not available as part of the routine protocol. Image quality was diagnostic and not limited by motion artifact.

The patient received supportive management with a single dose of intravenous diazepam and was subsequently discharged on carbamazepine 200 mg once daily. A follow-up MRI after 3 months was recommended to assess the stability of the lesion; however, the patient did not attend the follow-up due to the prevailing unstable situation in the country. No subsequent clinical or seizure follow-up data are available. This represents a limitation of the report. Follow-up remains planned if circumstances permit.

Discussion

The parahippocampal gyrus and uncus are situated on the medial aspect of the temporal lobe, adjacent to the tentorial incisura. Herniation in this region is commonly associated with increased intracranial pressure secondary to trauma, mass lesions, or cerebral edema [1,6]. However, congenital herniations, though rare, may occur without associated pathology or clinical symptoms [4].

Yavarian et al. [3] described such cases as incidental MRI findings, characterized by preservation of normal brain architecture and absence of neurological manifestations. Ram and Sherry further emphasized that idiopathic transtentorial herniations should be regarded as “do-not-touch” lesions, highlighting the importance of distinguishing them from pathological entities to avoid unnecessary surgical intervention [7].

Congenital or idiopathic parahippocampal/uncal herniations through the tentorial incisura have been noted as incidental MRI findings and must be distinguished from urgent descending transtentorial herniation [6]. Ram and Sherry coined the term “non-emergent descending transtentorial temporal lobe herniations” and advised a conservative “do-not-touch” approach [7]. Misinterpretation as a medial temporal tumor can lead to unnecessary evaluations or treatments [5]. Cases in children, including adolescents, have also been documented [8].

Typical imaging features of congenital transtentorial herniation include a well-defined protrusion of the parahippocampal region through the tentorial notch, preserved gray–white matter differentiation, absence of mass effect, midline shift, or brainstem compression, and a stable appearance on follow-up imaging [9].

Many reports show consistent imaging findings of a localized, well-circumscribed herniation of the parahippocampal gyrus or uncus into the tentorial notch, with preserved brain signal, no diffusion restriction or abnormal enhancement, and no midbrain or cisternal compression; follow-up imaging confirms stability [1,6]. Idiopathic cortical herniations have also been reported in other brain regions, supporting the concept of benign developmental variants [4,7].

This benign entity contrasts with acute uncal herniation, which typically results from raised intracranial pressure and is characterized by midbrain and cranial nerve compression, manifesting as anisocoria, altered consciousness, and brainstem dysfunction [9]. Conversely, pathological uncal herniation may arise from hemorrhage, tumors, or mass effect, and in severe cases may exhibit the Kernohan–Woltman notch phenomenon [9,10].

Differential diagnoses include medial temporal lobe tumors (eg, low-grade gliomas), encephaloceles, and herniations secondary to increased intracranial pressure or mass effect [10,11]. Misinterpretation of these benign congenital variants as pathological lesions can lead to unwarranted diagnostic procedures or surgical exploration [5].

Studies in pediatric and adolescent populations by Duarte et al. and Koc et al. [8] suggest that congenital transtentorial herniation may result from variations in Dural folding or altered cerebrospinal fluid dynamics during brain development [3,12]. Similar to other atypical pediatric neuroimaging presentations that may mimic pathological conditions, congenital transtentorial herniation underscores the importance of careful image interpretation in children, as variant findings can resemble serious disease entities and potentially lead to overtreatment. Recent pediatric reports describing unusual imaging patterns, such as atypical presentations of Sturge–Weber syndrome, further emphasize the need for cautious radiologic–clinical correlation in neurologically stable patients [13,14].

Magnetic resonance imaging remains the modality of choice, offering detailed visualization of the medial temporal lobe and adjacent structures [5,8]. When the imaging features are typical and the patient is neurologically stable, conservative management and periodic imaging follow-up are sufficient, as invasive interventions are rarely indicated [2].

Although detected during seizure workup, the congenital herniation cannot be conclusively identified as the epileptogenic focus. The absence of diffusion restriction, edema, mass effect, or electroencephalography abnormalities suggests that the lesion is likely incidental. Therefore, the causal relationship between the imaging finding and seizure remains uncertain [15].

This case illustrates a rare but clinically significant finding—a congenital transtentorial herniation of the parahippocampal gyrus incidentally detected in a 14-year-old boy presenting with seizures. Accurate recognition of this condition through proper imaging assessment is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and avoid unnecessary interventions.
