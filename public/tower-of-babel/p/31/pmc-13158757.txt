Hyperpyrexia with hemodynamic instability after sublingual misoprostol: case report and literature review
Authors: Paola A Castellanos, Juan C Ureña, Angie J Acero, Víctor S Rangel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMisoprostol is widely used in obstetric practice for the prevention and treatment of postpartum hemorrhage due to its uterotonic efficacy, oral administration, and stability at room temperature. Although generally considered safe, rare but severe adverse reactions have been reported. We present the case of a 27-year-old woman who developed hyperpyrexia (41°C), hemodynamic instability, hypoxemia, and generalized seizures shortly after receiving 1000 µg of sublingual misoprostol for postpartum uterine atony. Life-threatening differential diagnoses, including sepsis, pulmonary thromboembolism, eclampsia, and amniotic fluid embolism, were excluded. The patient required intensive supportive care, with complete clinical recovery and no sequelae. A review of the literature shows that similar cases are uncommon but share features such as sublingual administration, high doses, and a self-limited course with supportive management. This report highlights the importance of recognizing misoprostol-induced hyperpyrexia to guide appropriate management and avoid unnecessary interventions.Key words: hyperpyrexia, misoprostol, hemodynamic instability

Introduction

Misoprostol, a synthetic analogue of prostaglandin E1, has been evaluated as an alternative to conventional parenteral uterotonics for the management of postpartum hemorrhage.1 This option is appealing due to its potent uterotonic effect, oral route of administration, and stability at room temperature. Nonetheless, the occurrence of hyperthermia episodes with temperatures exceeding 40°C has raised concerns regarding the safety of this therapeutic approach.2

Sublingual administration is associated with faster absorption and higher peak plasma concentrations compared with the vaginal and rectal routes.3,4 The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics recommends a regimen of 800 μg of sublingual misoprostol for the treatment of postpartum hemorrhage.5 Although its use is generally considered safe, in the present case the patient developed a rare adverse effect that posed a clinical challenge to the care team and is sparsely reported in the literature.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient. Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario Mayor–Méderi (Approval No. CEISH-2025059).

Case presentation

We present the case of a 27-year-old patient, G2A1, at term (39.1 weeks), with a history of gestational diabetes managed non-pharmacologically and classified as high obstetric risk. She presented to the emergency department for induction of labor in the context of her underlying condition.

On admission, vital signs were within normal limits (blood pressure 112/76 mmHg, heart rate 86 bpm, respiratory rate 17 breaths/min). Obstetric examination showed a cervix patent to the uterine cavity. Antepartum fetal monitoring demonstrated an ACOG Category I tracing. Capillary glucose measurements were within targets in >90% of checks. She had received complete prenatal care, with a negative TORCH screen and an oral glucose tolerance test abnormal at the first measurement.

Admission to the labor and delivery unit was decided for induction of labor, and a fetal biophysical profile was requested, which was reported as 10/10. Given an unfavorable Bishop score, 25 µg of misoprostol was administered intravaginally for cervical ripening. Six hours later, cervical changes had progressed, so spontaneous progression of labor was allowed. The patient subsequently requested epidural analgesia; after its administration, intrapartum fetal monitoring showed absent uterine activity, for which titratable oxytocin augmentation was initiated. After 3 hours of augmentation, complete dilation and effacement were achieved, and transfer to the operating room was indicated for delivery.

An uncomplicated vaginal delivery was performed, with assisted placental expulsion and a first-degree vaginal laceration, which was repaired immediately. During the immediate postpartum period, the patient developed active bleeding secondary to transient uterine atony, without hemodynamic or neurological compromise. Management included oxytocin 30 IU intravenously, methylergonovine 0.2 mg intramuscularly, tranexamic acid 1 g intravenously, and misoprostol 1000 µg sublingually, achieving adequate hemorrhage control and improved uterine tone.

Fifteen minutes later, while in the recovery unit, the patient developed generalized shivering, sustained tachycardia (170 bpm), hyperthermia with a temperature of 41°C, blood pressure of 123/93 mmHg, arterial desaturation below 70%, and neurological deterioration characterized by unresponsiveness to her surroundings, followed by a generalized seizure. In response, orotracheal intubation and sedation were performed, with subsequent transfer to the operating room for close monitoring and ventilatory support. Initial laboratory studies showed a complete blood count and liver function tests within normal ranges, as well as arterial blood gases consistent with respiratory acidosis.

Given the clinical presentation, pulmonary thromboembolism (PT) was initially suspected, with amniotic fluid embolism (AFE) considered a less likely differential diagnosis. However, computed tomography pulmonary angiography and transthoracic echocardiography ruled out pulmonary embolism, revealing instead signs of right ventricular overload and indirect pulmonary hypertension. Cranial computed tomography showed no pathological findings. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit, where a diagnosis of shock secondary to misoprostol administration was established. Sedation and vasopressor support were gradually withdrawn, and successful extubation was achieved 12 hours later. No further febrile episodes were documented beyond 6 hours after the event. The patient was discharged on the fourth day following the event, without sequelae or additional complications.

Search strategy

A literature search was performed in PubMed, SciELO, and Google Scholar, complemented by manual screening of reference lists. The search combined MeSH terms and free text keywords in English and Spanish related to misoprostol, hyperpyrexia, hyperthermia, hemodynamic instability, hypotension, shock, and sublingual or oral administration. No date or language restrictions were initially applied, and studies published up to January 2026 were considered. Priority was given to primary studies (randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and case series) that provided detailed and disaggregated reporting of adverse effects associated with sublingual misoprostol, including not only thermoregulatory disturbances such as fever and hyperpyrexia, but also effects on cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, respiratory, and neurologic systems.

Discussion

Misoprostol is one of the most widely used drugs in obstetric practice, available in multiple formulations and with widespread global use. Numerous studies have confirmed its safety and high effectiveness in the management of early pregnancy loss and in cervical ripening, in both gynecologic and obstetric procedures. Nevertheless, the occurrence of rare adverse reactions, such as the 1 described in this case report, underscores the importance of further elucidating its mechanisms of action and potential adverse effects.

One of the earliest descriptions in the literature of hyperthermia as an adverse effect was reported by Chong et al6 in 1997, based on a published case report. Since then, additional evidence has expanded the understanding of this reaction. A plausible pathophysiological mechanism underlying the systemic febrile response observed in some patients involves the activation of prostaglandin E–related receptors by misoprostol, triggering thermoregulatory pathways similar to those mediated by endogenous prostaglandins. This activation leads to a shift in the hypothalamic thermoregulatory set point, particularly within the anterior preoptic area, promoting a rapid increase in core body temperature. Clinically, this can present as hyperpyrexia, at times accompanied by an exaggerated systemic response.7,8 Ethnic factors may influence susceptibility to these adverse effects; however, no consistent region-specific patterns have been identified across different populations.9

Case reports describing fever accompanied by cardiopulmonary deterioration after misoprostol administration are rare in the literature. Nevertheless, published cases share common clinical features, including sublingual administration, use of higher doses (>600 mcg), and a self-limited course that resolves with supportive care and inpatient observation. Likewise, studies evaluating the risk of these reactions compared with other uterotonics have demonstrated a significantly increased risk of fever with misoprostol, with pooled estimates suggesting nearly a fivefold higher risk compared with both oxytocin (RR: 4.75; 95% CI, 2.82–8.01) and methylergometrine (RR: 4.97; 95% CI, 3.47–7.14).10

Several observational studies and clinical trials have evaluated the occurrence of adverse events associated with misoprostol use across different clinical settings and dosing regimens, particularly at doses exceeding those recommended in the medical literature. Taken together, these studies reaffirm the overall safety profile of the drug; however, they also highlight that misoprostol is not a risk-free intervention in the therapeutic management of obstetric patients. Additionally, its mechanism of action may be associated with effects on multiple organ systems, including neurological, cardiovascular, and gastrointestinal systems, with a potential increase in the risk of severe adverse events in certain patients. Across published case reports, initial management has focused on the evaluation and exclusion of potentially life-threatening differential diagnoses, most commonly sepsis, AFE, eclampsia, and PT. In this context, clinical decision making requires careful interpretation of the patient’s clinical course and ancillary test results to rule out these conditions and subsequently attribute the presentation to a complication secondary to misoprostol use.11 In such scenarios, management should primarily focus on supportive measures, including aggressive hydration, active cooling, and the administration of antipyretic agents.12

Although the effects described and summarized in this review are uncommon, it is essential that healthcare professionals who use misoprostol across clinical settings understand its potential risks and the frequency with which they occur. This knowledge is key to guiding timely therapeutic interventions and to avoiding inappropriate treatments, such as the unnecessary initiation of antibiotic therapy or anticoagulation regimens. It should also prompt critical reflection on whether the doses currently used in different clinical scenarios (particularly with sublingual administration) should be reconsidered in order to mitigate these risks.

This consideration is particularly relevant in contexts with sociodemographic constraints where, unfortunately, access to alternative therapies for postpartum hemorrhage (such as methylergonovine) is limited. In these settings, the use of misoprostol in critically ill patients becomes central once again, underscoring the need to recognize and anticipate its potential adverse effects.
