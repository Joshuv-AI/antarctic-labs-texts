Hepatocellular Carcinoma Recurrence in Patients with Chronic Hepatitis C Infection Treated with Direct-Acting Antivirals: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Kristina Lindsley, Margaret Burroughs, Candido Hernandez-Lopez, Yi Wang, John Marcinak, Stephanie E. Chiuve, Barbara A. Haber, Jennifer Uyei, Soodabeh Navadeh, Giuseppe De Marco, Deanna D. Hill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionReports of direct-acting antivirals (DAA) use for chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection after successful treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) have suggested higher rates of HCC recurrence. However, other studies have indicated no increased risk of HCC recurrence.MethodsTo estimate the comparative risk of early HCC recurrence in adults successfully treated for HCC and subsequently treated with interferon (IFN)-free DAAs versus not treated with DAAs for chronic HCV, we conducted a systematic literature review and meta-analysis following established guidelines.ResultsForty-one cohort studies (10,563 total participants) were identified. The following mutually exclusive comparisons to DAA therapy were determined: IFN-based therapy; no DAA; and no antiviral therapy. Random effects meta-analysis results indicated no increased risk of HCC recurrence with IFN-free DAA therapy when compared with IFN (combined risk ratio (RR) 1.29 [95% CI 0.69, 2.40] at 12 months, 0.88 [95% CI 0.56, 1.39] at 24 months, and 0.67 [95% CI 0.51, 0.88] at longest follow-up), no DAA (combined RR 0.55 [95% CI 0.25, 1.23] at 12 months, 0.67 [95% CI 0.51, 0.86] at 24 months and 0.72 [95% CI 0.61, 0.85] at longest follow-up), or no antiviral therapy (combined RR 0.95 [95% CI 0.76, 1.20] at 12 months, 0.48 [95% CI 0.11, 2.12] at 24 months, and 0.42 [95% CI 0.24, 0.73] at longest follow-up). Results were confirmed in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsThere is no evidence to suggest that DAA therapy has a higher risk of early HCC recurrence when compared with IFN, no DAA, or no antiviral therapy at any timepoint analyzed. Hence the benefit–risk profile of DAAs in HCV remains positive in relation to the risk of early HCC recurrence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-025-01180-9.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Initial reports suggested that direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) for chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection might increase the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) recurrence after successful HCC treatment.The review aimed to determine whether DAAs for chronic HCV infection increases the risk of early HCC recurrence compared to interferon (IFN)-based therapy, no DAA therapy, or no antiviral therapy. What was learned from the study? The meta-analysis found no increased risk of early HCC recurrence with DAA therapy compared to IFN, no DAA, or no antiviral therapy at various follow-up times.The findings support the continued use of DAAs for HCV treatment in patients with a history of HCC, as the benefit–risk profile remains positive regarding early HCC recurrence.These results may guide future research and clinical guidelines, ensuring that DAAs are not withheld from patients with HCV because of concerns about HCC recurrence.

Introduction

Early reports of interferon (IFN)-free direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy use for chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection after successful treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) raised concerns that DAA therapy was associated with an increased risk of early HCC recurrence. In a small study of 58 evaluable patients published by Reig et al. in 2016 [1], the study authors concluded that patients treated with DAAs after complete response (CR) to HCC treatment had an unexpected, increased risk of early HCC recurrence (radiologic tumor recurrence in 27.6% of patients with a median follow-up of 5.7 months). Prior HCC treatment interventions received by patients included radiofrequency ablation (RFA), transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), and resection. The study design did not include a concurrent comparator group to minimize potential bias and patients who received interferon (IFN) with DAA therapy and those with prior liver transplant were excluded. The median time from the start of DAA therapy to recurrence was 3.5 months.

In contrast, other published studies have indicated no increased risk of early HCC recurrence. Since the approval of IFN-free DAA therapies (second-generation DAAs) to treat HCV infections in 2011, DAA therapy has been integral in the treat-to-cure treatment paradigm for HCV-infected patients even after successful treatment of HCC [2, 3]. In patients with advanced HCV disease, the European Association for the Study of the Liver treatment guidelines recommend that: “Patients with complete response to HCC therapy should be treated for their HCV infection according to the general recommendations in patients without HCC” [4]. Notably, the time of HCV treatment after successful HCC treatment is not specified in the guidelines and there is no universally accepted standard for time of HCV treatment initiation before, during, or after HCC treatment.

A meta-analysis published in 2017 included 17 studies that reported on HCC recurrence following DAA therapy in patients with chronic HCV who received curative HCC treatment [5]. Despite differences in study design and patient characteristics across studies and the analysis being conducted separately by treatment group rather than comparatively, DAA therapy was found to not be associated with a higher rate of HCC recurrence compared with IFN treatment.

Likewise, in a 2018 review of published data, Roche et al. [6] concluded that DAA therapy should not be withheld from patients with HCV infection who have undergone resection or ablation of HCC on the basis of the high rate of sustained virologic response (SVR; > 90%) and safety profile of DAAs. The review summarized several studies, some of which reported high rates of HCC recurrence coinciding with HCV clearance following DAA therapy and some that found no evidence to support an increased risk of HCC recurrence followed by successful HCV cure.

Given the uncertainty of existing evidence, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) initiated an investigation under Article 20 (including a subsequent request for a Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee [PRAC] Assessment) for a non-interventional post-authorization safety study (PASS) to evaluate the early recurrence of HCC after DAA therapy in patients with HCV infection. As a result of recruitment challenges in enrolling patients who were DAA naïve for the PASS, a systematic literature review (SLR) and meta-analysis was deemed necessary to further assess the evidence.

Therefore, in response to the regulatory request received from the EMA to the Marketing Authorization Holders (MAHs) of the DAA Consortium (comprising AbbVie Inc., Gilead Sciences Inc., and MSD B.V.), an SLR and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the comparative risk of early HCC recurrence in individuals who were successfully treated for HCC and subsequently treated with DAAs versus not treated with DAAs for chronic HCV. This work presents an updated synthesis of the literature and novel analysis of the data, including defining the patient population to represent the indication for DAAs, distinguishing comparators to DAAs based on the reported treatment that was received by the patients in the control group, and analyzing the risk of early HCC recurrence at specific follow-up times (i.e., at 12 months, at 24 months, and at longest follow-up).

Methods

This SLR and meta-analysis was conducted according to methods described in the Cochrane Handbook [7] and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA 2020) statement [8]. A detailed protocol was developed and registered prospectively with the European Post-Authorisation Studies (EU PAS) register (registration number EUPAS41657), and is available in the EMA Catalogue [9] This research is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Study Identification

We conducted a comprehensive, highly sensitive search in collaboration with a medical librarian to identify relevant studies from the published literature, including journal articles and conference abstracts, and studies recorded in clinical study registries. Searches were executed on 5 May 2021 and updated on 3 October 2023 using the OvidSP platform, inclusive of Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases, to identify relevant publications with no date, language, or geographical restriction. The Dialog® platform was used to conduct searches for BIOSIS and the LILACS website was used to search the Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature database. Additionally, clinical study registries were searched to identify studies that may not have been published and the bibliographies of relevant review articles were manually screened.

The search strategy included a combination of search terms and indexing terms related to the eligible population (patients with chronic HCV and previous HCC) and interventions (DAAs). The complete search strategy with results from each data source are provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1–S7).

Each unique publication identified through the search was evaluated in a two-step process to assess eligibility based on the a priori inclusion and exclusion criteria using DistillerSR (Evidence Partners). First, two reviewers (KL, JU, Arianna Nevo (AN), Allie Sosinsky (AS), Henna Khan (HK)) independently screened each title and abstract for all records. Records assessed as not relevant by both reviewers were excluded and records assessed by both reviewers as definitely or possibly relevant were retained for full-text review. Second, the eligibility of each full-text publication was assessed by two independent reviewers. The reason for exclusion was documented for excluded studies. In instances where there was uncertainty or disagreement regarding a record’s eligibility, a third reviewer (KL or AS) adjudicated the decision to include or exclude.

Studies with 5% of patients or more who had or were undergoing liver transplantation for HCC treatment were excluded from the review because HCV-infected patients with liver transplantation comprise a different patient population than those without liver transplantation. Studies that enrolled 10% of patients or more with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) coinfection were eligible for inclusion in the review and sensitivity analysis, but not eligible for inclusion in the primary analysis. Information for other comorbidities, including diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis, also were collected from included studies.

Data Collection and Outcomes

Data extraction was conducted by two independent researchers (AN, HK) and results cross-checked to ensure accuracy. In instances of disagreement, a third reviewer (KL, AS) checked against the original source documents to ensure credibility and accuracy. Information on study design, patient eligibility criteria, study population/patient characteristics, interventions and comparators, and outcomes were extracted. When results for the same study were reported in more than one publication, the relevant records were grouped per study according to the location, date and duration of the study, intervention details, number of participants and baseline data, and author information per methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook [7]. Missing data were addressed also following best practices outlined in the Cochrane Handbook [7] and outliers were checked against the original source document for confirmation of results. For all studies included in the review, quality assessment was performed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11]. Small study effects, which may be due to reporting bias, were examined using funnel plots and the Egger’s test for asymmetry for meta-analysis with 10 or more studies [12]. The consistency of results and confidence intervals were used to ascertain the certainty of evidence for each outcome.

Three main comparators were identified among the included studies: IFN, no DAA (as reported by study authors as not receiving DAAs with no indication whether IFN or another antiviral therapy was administered, or no antiviral therapy was administered), and no antiviral therapy (explicitly reported by study authors as not receiving any antiviral therapy).

The outcome variables of interest collected from eligible studies included risk of early HCC recurrence and incidence of HCC recurrence. The primary timepoint of interest was within 12 (± 3) months of follow-up and secondary timepoints of interest were within 18 (± 3) months and 24 (± 3) months of follow-up. As many studies did not report outcomes at a specific follow-up time, HCC recurrence also was analyzed at the longest follow-up time as reported by individual studies. As a result of the lack of data, incidence and hazard ratio measures were not included in the meta-analysis and instead are summarized narratively.

Statistical Methods and Analysis

A feasibility assessment was conducted to evaluate the comparability across studies and the variability in outcome reporting. Pairwise meta-analysis was performed for the risk of HCC recurrence, defined as the number of patients with HCC recurrence (n) out of total number of patients at risk (N). All studies considered eligible based on the feasibility assessment were included in the primary meta-analysis, with each of the three comparators analyzed separately (DAA versus IFN, DAA versus no DAA, and DAA versus no antiviral therapy).

The frequentist method was used for the meta-analysis using the metafor package in R 3.6.1 [13]. The log-odds transformation was applied to the data for analysis, assuming the transformed values followed a normal distribution. The delta method was used to transform the analysis results back to percentage for interpretation. Results for all meta-analysis are presented as RR (risk ratio or relative risk), for the DAA group compared with the risk of HCC recurrence for the comparator group, with 95% CI using the random effects model as the predefined primary method; results for the fixed effect model are presented in Supplementary Materials. Absolute risk (AR) is reported as the proportion of HCC recurrences that occurred in a group over the total number of people in that group per 100 individuals. No data were imputed for the purposes of this review. Data were analyzed on the basis of a complete case analysis as reported by the individual published studies.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2 statistic [7]. The test for heterogeneity also was assessed, with a p value of less than 0.05 indicating presence of heterogeneity. I2 values, which provide an estimate of the percentage of variability in effect estimates that is due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error, were interpreted according to the Cochrane Handbook as follows: 0% to 40%, might not be important; 30% to 60%, may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50% to 90%, may represent substantial heterogeneity; and 75% to 100%, considerable heterogeneity [7]. As the interpretation of I2 values overlap, we also examined the (1) magnitude and direction of effects, (2) strength of evidence for heterogeneity, and (3) uncertainty in the value of I2 when the number of studies was small. Of note, the I2 statistic has a substantial bias when the number of studies analyzed is small, in the positive direction when the true fraction of heterogeneity is small and the negative when the true fraction of heterogeneity is large [14]. Thus, for meta-analyses with a small number of studies, the I2 statistic should be interpreted with caution and the direction of effect and overlap of the confidence intervals among studies used to examine statistical heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the potential impact of decisions made on the basis of the feasibility assessment to exclude certain studies from the primary analysis based on study design issues and/or lack of description of DAA exposure. Reasons for exclusion from the primary analysis based on the feasibility assessment included median follow-up less than 12 months (the pre-specified eligibility criteria required a minimum follow-time of 12 months) and DAA exposure not described (the pre-specified eligibility criteria restricted IFN-free DAA exposure to one of 12 specific DAAs (Table 1), or combinations of the specific DAAs). No included study enrolled more than 10% of patients with HBV or HIV coinfection. The sensitivity analysis was performed using the same methods as with the primary analysis and comprised the same studies included in the primary analysis plus the addition of studies that were not eligible for the primary analysis.

Overall, 49 publications (30 journal articles and 19 conference abstracts) reporting 41 unique studies [15–53] were included. Study characteristics

ANRS National Agency for Research in HIV and Viral Hepatitis, CR complete response, DAA direct-acting antivirals, DCV daclatasvir, HCV hepatitis C virus, IFN interferon, LDV ledipasvir, NCT unique identification code given to each clinical study record registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, NR not reported, PSM propensity score matching, RBV ribavirin, SD standard deviation, SOF sofosbuvir, SVR sustained virologic response, IQR interquartile range, NA not applicable

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics were comparable within the main inclusion and exclusion criteria. Most studies (24) were conducted in Asia (Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan); nine were conducted in Europe (France, Italy, Poland, Romania, and Spain); one each in the USA, Canada, and Egypt; one in Europe and the USA; and four did not report the country. The median age of study participants for all studies was above 55 years (range 56–74 years). Sex ranged from 39.3% [20] to 87% [48] male; only one conference abstract reported demographic characteristics [40]. No studies reported on race, ethnicity, or rheumatoid arthritis (a predefined comorbidity of interest) at baseline.

The eligible treatment sequence for all patients was curative HCC treatment followed by DAA or comparison treatment for HCV. The most frequently reported types of prior HCC treatment were surgery/resection (20 studies, median 34.6%, range 3.2–100%), ablation (20 studies, median 67%, range 13–100%), and radiation therapy (3 studies, median 6.1%, range 2–18%); patients with prior liver transplantation were not eligible for this review because HCV-infected patients with liver transplantation represent a different patient population than those without liver transplantation.

The intervention for all included studies was IFN-free DAA (second-generation DAAs). In four studies, some patients in the IFN group also may have received one of three IFN-containing DAA regimens (telaprevir, simeprevir, or vaniprevir) (Kinoshita 2019 [25], Mashiba 2018 [29], Nagata 2017 [36], and Tahata 2020 [43]). Thirty-four studies compared DAAs with a single comparator group and seven studies compared DAAs with two comparator groups (DAA versus IFN versus no antiviral treatment). Study-specific details for treatment characteristics are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S9).

Studies Comparing DAA Versus IFN

Seventeen studies compared IFN-free DAA regimens with IFN (6802 participants), including seven three-group studies that also evaluated no antiviral therapy (Table 2). Most studies were conducted in Asia (Japan, n = 8; Taiwan, n = 3; and South Korea, n = 1); one each in the USA and Europe (Italy); and three did not report the country. The median age ranged from 60.2 [26] to 74.1 [24, 25] years. One study included 9% of patients who had HBV coinfection (Chi 2019 [20]). No study reported including patients with HIV coinfection at baseline. Total study duration ranged from 38 [20] to 298 [35] months for 12 studies; five studies did not report a total study duration.

The DAA treatment groups included any combination or mixture of the 12 eligible DAAs (Table 1), with or without ribavirin (RBV). The length of DAA treatment, when reported, was 12 or 24 weeks. The IFN group comprised treatment with IFN or Peg-IFN, with or without RBV. The length of IFN treatment, when reported, was 12 to 48 weeks. In four studies, some patients in the IFN group also may have received one of three IFN-containing DAA regimens (telaprevir, simeprevir, or vaniprevir) (Kinoshita 2019 [25], Mashiba 2018 [29], Nagata 2017 [36], and Tahata 2020 [43]).

Studies Comparing DAA Versus No DAA

Fourteen studies (1766 participants) compared IFN-free DAA regimens with a no DAA comparator group; all were two-group studies (Table 2). Seven studies were conducted in Asia (Japan, n = 6; Taiwan, n = 1); five in Europe (France, n = 2; and one each in Italy, Poland, and Romania); one in Africa (Egypt); and one in Europe and the USA. The median age ranged from 56.7 [21] to 77 [27] years. No patient was reported to have HIV coinfection; one study included 3 (2%) with HBV coinfection [27]. Total study duration was reported by only five studies, ranging from 18 [34] to 191 [32] months, with nine studies not reporting a total study duration.

The DAA treatment groups included any combination or mixture of the 12 eligible DAAs (Table 1), with or without ribavirin (RBV). The length of DAA treatment, when reported, was 12 or 24 weeks. For all studies, the no DAA group was reported only as not receiving DAAs with no indication whether IFN or another antiviral treatment was administered.

Studies Comparing DAA Versus No Antiviral Therapy

Seventeen studies (4452 participants) compared IFN-free DAA regimens with no antiviral therapy, including seven three-group studies that also evaluated IFN therapy (Table 2). Most studies were conducted in Asia (Japan, n = 6; Taiwan, n = 3; and South Korea, n = 1); four in Europe (one each in France, Italy, Romania, and Spain); one in Canada; and two did not report the country. The median age ranged from 61.0 [47] to 74.1 [24] years. One study included < 10% of patients who had HIV coinfection (Perello 2019 [40]). Total study duration ranged from 18 [40] to 209 [31] months, with eight studies not reporting a total study duration.

The DAA treatment groups included any combination or mixture of the 12 eligible DAAs (Table 1), with or without ribavirin (RBV), for 12 or 24 weeks. For all studies, the comparison group was described as receiving no antiviral therapy or no treatment.

Summary of Reported Outcome Data

Most studies reported the risk of HCC recurrence assessed by imaging with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or ultrasonography (US). Study-specific HCC recurrence outcome definitions are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S10). Outcomes were analyzed by comparison group: IFN, no DAA (as reported by study authors as not receiving DAAs with no indication whether IFN or another antiviral therapy was administered, or no antiviral therapy was administered), and no antiviral therapy (explicitly reported by study authors as not receiving any antiviral therapy). Most studies reported the risk of HCC recurrence as a proportion at 12 months, 24 months, or longest follow-up; 13 of 17 studies comparing DAA versus IFN, 14 of 14 studies comparing DAA versus no DAA, and 15 of 17 studies comparing DAA versus no antiviral therapy (Supplementary Material Table S11). Only two studies reported HCC recurrence at 18 months (Muzica 2022 [34] and Perello 2019 [40]), each of which evaluated a different comparator and neither was eligible for the primary analysis, thus precluding meta-analysis for this timepoint. Of 37 studies that reported the risk of HCC recurrence as a proportion, 23 studies were eligible for primary analysis and 14 studies were included in sensitivity analysis only.

One study comparing DAA versus IFN, five studies comparing DAA versus no DAA, and four studies comparing DAA versus no antiviral therapy reported incidence of HCC recurrence (Table 3). Two studies comparing DAA versus IFN, five studies comparing DAA versus no DAA, and four studies comparing DAA versus no antiviral therapy reported HCC recurrence as a hazard ratio (Table 4). Studies Comparing DAA Versus No DAA

At 12 months follow-up

Three studies (374 patients) compared IFN-free DAA regimens versus no DAA (as reported by study authors as not receiving DAAs with no indication whether IFN or another antiviral therapy was administered, or no antiviral therapy was administered) at 12 months (Fig. 2). Results from these studies reported the risk of HCC recurrence ranging from 2 to 20 recurrences per study for patients treated with DAA (AR 18 per 100 individuals) and 12 to 25 recurrences per study for patients receiving no DAAs (AR 27 per 100 individuals). The combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.55 [95% CI 0.25, 1.23] (Fig. 2), indicating favorable results for DAA therapy compared with no DAA therapy with considerable statistical heterogeneity (I2 statistic 65.7%).

With the inclusion of two additional studies that were not eligible for primary analysis, Ikenaga 2022 [23] and Warzyszynska 2018 [50] (DAA exposure not described), the sensitivity analysis results significantly favored DAAs (random effects RR 0.59 [95% CI 0.37, 0.94], Supplementary Material, Fig. S9; fixed effect RR 0.68 [95% CI 0.49, 0.93], Supplementary Material, Fig. S10).

At 24 months follow-up

Six studies (727 patients) reported risk of HCC recurrence at 24 months. HCC recurrence ranged from 1 to 24 recurrences per study for patients treated with DAA (AR 20 per 100 individuals) and 5 to 46 recurrences per study for patients not treated with DAA (AR 38 per 100 individuals). The combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.67 [95% CI 0.51, 0.86] (Fig. 3), indicating favorable results for DAA compared with no DAA with no statistical heterogeneity (I2 statistic 0.0%).

Results using the fixed effect model were the same as primary analysis results using the random effects model (fixed effect RR 0.67 [95% CI 0.51, 0.86]; Supplementary Material, Fig. S2) as no statistical heterogeneity was detected (I2 statistic 0.0%). No sensitivity analysis was conducted for this outcome because all studies at this timepoint were included in the primary analysis.

At longest follow-up

Nine studies (925 patients) reported risk of HCC recurrence, which ranged from 1 to 36 recurrences per study for patients treated with DAA (AR 31 per 100 individuals) and 5 to 75 recurrences per study for patients not treated with DAA (AR 57 per 100 individuals). The combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.72 [95% CI 0.61, 0.85] (Fig. 4), indicating favorable results for DAA compared with no DAA and low statistical heterogeneity (I2 statistic 15.9%).

Fixed effect model and sensitivity analyses also favored DAA therapy compared with no DAA. Results using the fixed effect model were similar to the primary analysis results using the random effects model (fixed effect RR 0.75 [95% CI 0.66, 0.85]; Supplementary Material, Fig. S5) as no statistical heterogeneity was detected (I2 statistic 0.0%). The inclusion of five additional studies that were not eligible for primary analysis, namely Cabibbo 2019 [17], Ikenaga 2022 [23], Muzica 2022 [34], and Warzyszynska 2018 [50] (DAA exposure not described), and El Kassas [21] (generic DAA exposure), did not impact the meta-analysis results (random effects RR 0.72 [95% CI 0.62, 0.84], Supplementary Material, Fig. S6; fixed effect RR 0.75 [95% CI 0.67, 0.84], Supplementary Material, Fig. S7). Funnel plot examination did not suggest small study effects due to potential publication bias (Egger’s test p value = 0.079, Supplementary Material, Fig. S11).

Studies Comparing DAA Versus No Antiviral Therapy

At 12 months follow-up

Five studies (2381 patients) reported risk of HCC recurrence at 12 months, which ranged from 0 to 53 recurrences per study for patients treated with IFN-free DAAs (AR 26 per 100 individuals) and 8 to 263 recurrences per study for patients not treated with antiviral therapy (AR 26 per 100 individuals). The combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.95 [95% CI 0.76, 1.20] (Fig. 2), indicating no difference between DAA and no antiviral therapy and no statistical heterogeneity (I2 statistic 0.0%).

Results using the fixed effect model were the same as primary analysis results using the random effects model (fixed effect RR 0.95 [95% CI 0.76, 1.20]; Supplementary Material, Fig. S1) and low statistical heterogeneity was detected (I2 statistic 19.5%). The inclusion of three additional studies that were not eligible for primary analysis, namely Mashiba 2022 [30], Ohki 2019 [38], and Perello 2019 [40] (DAA exposure not described), did not impact the meta-analysis results (random effects RR 0.91 [95% CI 0.75, 1.09], Supplementary Material, Fig. S9; fixed effect RR 0.91 [95% CI 0.75, 1.09], Supplementary Material, Fig. S10).

At 24 months follow-up

Four studies (2325 patients) reported risk of HCC recurrence at 24 months. HCC recurrence ranged from 1 to 69 recurrences per study for patients treated with DAA (AR 34 per 100 individuals) and 28 to 525 recurrences per study for patients not treated with antiviral therapy (AR 51 per 100 individuals). The combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.48 [95% CI 0.11, 2.12] (Fig. 3), indicating no difference between DAA and no antiviral therapy with possible considerable heterogeneity (I2 statistic 97.3%).

Fixed effect model and sensitivity analyses were similar to the primary analysis using the random effects model. The fixed effect combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.98 [95% CI 0.80, 1.21] (Supplementary Material, Fig. S2), indicating no increased risk between DAA and IFN with substantial statistical heterogeneity (I2 statistic 90.1%). The inclusion of two additional studies that were not eligible for primary analysis, Mashiba 2022 [30] and Yoshida 2017 [53] (DAA exposure not described), did not impact the meta-analysis results (random effects RR 0.63 [95% CI 0.31, 1.26], Supplementary Material, Fig. S3; fixed effect RR 0.89 [95% CI 0.75, 1.06], Supplementary Material, Fig. S4).

At longest follow-up

Nine studies (2887 patients) reported risk of HCC recurrence, which ranged from 1 to 61 recurrences per study for patients treated with DAA (AR 30 per 100 individuals) and 20 to 525 recurrences per study for patients not treated with antiviral therapy (AR 58 per 100 individuals). The combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.42 [95% CI 0.24, 0.73] (Fig. 4), indicating favorable results for DAA compared with no antiviral therapy with considerable heterogeneity (I2 statistic 90.8%).

Fixed effect model and sensitivity analyses also favored DAA therapy compared with no antiviral therapy. The fixed effect combined RR for HCC recurrence was 0.58 [95% CI 0.50, 0.68] (Supplementary Material, Fig. S5), indicating no increased risk between DAA and no antiviral therapy with substantial statistical heterogeneity (I2 statistic 87.9%). The inclusion of six additional studies that were not eligible for primary analysis (DAA exposure not described), namely Chan 2017 [18], Mashiba 2022 [30], Mori 2022 [33], Ohki 2019 [38], Perello 2019 [40], and Yoshida 2017 [53], did not impact the meta-analysis results (random effects RR 0.55 [95% CI 0.41, 0.75], Supplementary Material, Fig. S6; fixed effect RR 0.68 [95% CI 0.62, 0.74], Supplementary Material, Fig. S7); however, small study effects were observed which may suggest potential publication bias (Egger’s test p value < 0.05, Supplementary Material, Fig. S12).

HCC Recurrence Reported as Incidence and Hazard Ratios

Data for HCC recurrence reported as incidence and hazard ratios are reported in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. With a median timepoint ranging from 16 to 24 months, patients treated with DAA therapy had comparable incidence rates of HCC recurrence compared with patients treated with IFN, no DAAs, or no antiviral therapy. Incidence rates for patients treated with DAAs ranged from 4.06 to 56.02 per 100 person-years. One study, Teng 2020 [47], reported high incidence rates for patients treated with IFN: 38.51 per 100 person-years before propensity score matching (PSM) and 32.78 per 100 person-years after PSM. Teng 2020 [47] also reported high incidence rates for patients not treated with antiviral therapy: 66.53 per 100 person-years before PSM and 67.35 per 100 person-years after PSM. Three studies reporting incidence rates for patients treated with no DAAs ranged from 7.92 to 20.76 per 100 person-years. Hazard ratios were reported in six studies ranging from 0.24 to 1.21 with most studies favoring DAA therapy; however, the results were not significant (p > 0.05) and meta-analysis was not performed.

Assessment of Studies Using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS)

We assessed risk of bias of the included studies using the NOS (Supplementary Material, Fig. S13) [11]. Across the studies, the total NOS score ranged from one to eight stars (maximum of nine). As expected, the 11 conference abstracts scored the lowest indicating a higher risk of bias (ranging from one to four stars, with the exception of Chan 2017 [18] which we assessed at six stars). This was due to the lack of available data reported in conference abstracts including details of DAA exposure, ascertainment of HCC recurrence, and follow-up times. The journal articles scored higher, ranging from five to eight stars suggesting moderate to high quality (except for Minami 2016 [31] and Vukotic 2017 [49] which were letters to the editor that scored two and three, respectively). The journal articles typically described information around the DAA-treated cohorts, confirmation of prior HCC, reporting of DAA exposure, and HCC recurrence ascertainment. One key issue for all included studies was the lack of standardized follow-up times between treatment groups.

Discussion

This comprehensive SLR and meta-analysis found no comparative risk of early HCC recurrence in individuals with chronic HCV who were successfully treated for HCC and subsequently treated with IFN-free DAAs versus not treated with DAAs. Furthermore, results statistically favored DAA versus no DAA at 24 months follow-up, and DAA versus IFN, no DAA, and no antiviral therapy at the longest follow-up time. Absolute risks for HCC recurrence also indicated a lower or same (± 1) risk in the DAA group as in comparator groups for all analyses with the exception of DAA vs IFN at 12 months.

Among the 41 included studies, 10 studies compared IFN-free DAA with IFN, 14 studies compared DAA with no DAA, 10 studies compared DAA with no antiviral treatment, and seven studies were multi-arm studies that compared DAA, IFN, and no antiviral treatment. All studies among the three comparison groups were comparable within the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Small variations in demographics and clinical characteristics were observed across the studies, but these variations were considered negligible and generally comparable. Median follow-up times for all studies ranged from 5.9 to 91.3 months. On the basis of the similarity of comparison and follow-up durations, analysis was performed separately for each comparison (DAA vs IFN, DAA vs no DAA, DAA vs no antiviral treatment) using studies with binary HCC recurrence data available for 12 months, 24 months, and longest follow-up durations. The outcome of interest of HCC recurrence was reported as a binary outcome in 13 studies for DAA vs IFN, 14 studies for DAA vs no DAA, and 15 studies for DAA vs no antiviral therapy. Four studies were not eligible for meta-analysis on the basis of lack of outcome data for risk of HCC recurrence [24, 25, 46, 49].

The summary estimates across all analyses support no increased risk of early HCC recurrence with DAAs versus any comparator, although substantial statistical heterogeneity assessed using the I2 statistic, which provides an estimate of the percentage of variability in effect estimates due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error, was observed when comparing DAAs versus IFN and DAAs versus no antiviral therapy. The statistical heterogeneity observed was driven by one three-group study (Teng 2020 [47]). Teng et al. [47] reported that patients in the DAA group were significantly older, had more advanced fibrotic status, and had a higher proportion of the HCV genotype 1 than the IFN group at baseline. Given that the observed statistical heterogeneity was driven by one study and the direction of effect consistently favored DAAs in most studies, the interpretation of the results indicates no increased risk in HCC recurrence with DAAs overall. Additionally, results from the fixed effect model and sensitivity analyses were similar to those of the primary random effects model analyses. Among the three comparison groups, studies that were not eligible for primary analysis were retained for sensitivity analysis: three studies reported median follow-up times < 12 months in at least one group (Mashiba 2018 [29], Woo 2019 [51], and Yasui 2017 [52]), 12 studies did not describe DAA exposure (Cabibbo 2019 [17], Chan 2017 [29], Ikenaga 2022 [23], Mashiba 2022 [30], Mori 2022 [33], Muzica 2022 [34], Ohki 2019 [38], Perello 2019 [40], Warzyszynska 2018 [50], Woo 2019 [51], Yasui 2017 [52], and Yoshida 2017 [53]), and one study likely used generic DAA therapy (El Kassas [21]). Results from the sensitivity analyses with the addition of these studies were similar to the primary analysis and did not substantially impact the interpretation of results.

The generalizability of the study population reflects the selection criteria which were adapted from the indication labels of DAAs of interest and designed to represent the well-defined HCV population. As such, studies that enrolled patients (> 5% of the study population) who had received liver transplantation prior to or during DAA treatment were not eligible for this review. Studies that enrolled more than 10% of patients with HBV or HIV coinfection were eligible for inclusion in this review but not eligible for inclusion in the primary analysis; however, none of the studies included more than 10% of patients who had HBV or HIV coinfection.

A strength of this SLR and meta-analysis was the assessment of three comparator groups, with differentiation between patients who were confirmed to have IFN exposure, those for whom IFN exposure status was not specified (no DAA group), and those who did not receive IFN (no antiviral therapy group). Combining patients who received active treatment and those who received no treatment within the same comparator group could lead to unreliable results and potential misinterpretation due to differences in effects [54]. Analyzing each group separately provides more accurate estimates by reducing heterogeneity within each comparator group and facilitates investigation of potential confounding that may be related to comparator choice (e.g., confounding by frailty or indication) [55]. An additional strength of the current report was the alignment of follow-up time across studies included in the meta-analysis. It is thought that providing pooled risk estimates at specific cut points during follow-up time can provide more clinically informative data and the ability to evaluate various lengths of follow-up than analyses that do not account for variability in follow-up time across studies.

The meta-analysis was limited by the number of studies that met the SLR inclusion criteria and by the available variables of interest in the identified studies, which had limited analyzable data. Less analyzable data limits the feasibility of more formal methods to control for heterogeneity, such as meta-regression as investigations of heterogeneity when there are very few studies. For example, although the proportion of patients with complete response to therapy was higher among patients treated with DAAs than those treated with IFN (Chi 2019 [20], Imai 2020 [24], Kinoshita 2019 [25], Mashiba 2018 [29], Minami 2016 [31], Nagaoki, 2019 [35], Teng 2020 [47], and Vukotic 2017 [49]), data were not available to assess the impact of treatment response in the analyses. Additionally, the outcome definition and ascertainment methods in selected studies varied, limiting the ability to evaluate important quality factors of the included studies and possibly correct for any deficiencies identified. No individual patient-level data (IPD) were reviewed to conduct this SLR and meta-analysis; it was not feasible to make a causality assessment at the individual case level based upon the research activities performed for this review.

A previously published IPD meta-analysis analyzed 977 patients from 21 studies who had HCV-related cirrhosis and HCC [56]. Although a high rate of HCC recurrence was observed among patients treated with DAAs (recurrence rate 20 [95% CI 13.9, 29.8]), no difference in the risk of HCC recurrence was found between DAA-exposed and DAA-unexposed patients using propensity score matching with 328 DAA-unexposed patients from the ITA.LI.CA cohort. Another SLR of 31 studies investigated HCC recurrence among patients treated with DAAs (30% [95% CI 26%, 34%]) and found the risk of HCC recurrence was significantly lower with DAA versus IFN (combined RR 0.64 [95% CI 0.51, 0.81]) and with DAA versus no intervention (combined RR 0.68 [95% CI 0.49, 0.94]) [57].

Variations in methods and outcomes also could indicate the presence of publication bias. On the basis of the framework presented in the Cochrane Handbook [7], a complete assessment of risk of bias due to missing results was not feasible. However, the potential for publication bias was observed in the records assessed for eligibility during the screening process. At the full-text screening level, studies were assessed against the PICO eligibility criteria (Table 1) and 12 references were excluded because of “outcome out of scope” due to the reference not reporting an identified outcome related to risk of HCC recurrence (PRISMA flow diagram, Fig. 1). Studies not reporting on HCC recurrence that would otherwise fit the PICO eligibility criteria have the potential of contributing to the publication bias due to missing results (HCC recurrence results could be missing from the publications because of various study-specific reasons, e.g., outcome not of interest or not specified in the study protocol, outcome not collected, outcome not experienced by any patient, outcome not reported if observed in < 10% of patients). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis of DAAs versus no antiviral therapy at longest follow-up suggested potential small study effects (Egger’s test p value < 0.05, Supplementary Material, Fig. S12); however, these results should be interpreted with caution given the overall agreement in the direction of effect and the inclusion of studies within the sensitivity analysis that did not meet the criteria for the primary analysis.

Conclusions

As observed from this SLR and meta-analysis, there is no evidence to indicate that DAA therapy has a higher risk of HCC recurrence when compared with IFN, no DAA therapy, or no antiviral therapy at any timepoint analyzed. Results from the fixed effect models and sensitivity analyses corroborate with the primary analyses. The previously established favorable benefit–risk profile for DAAs remains positive in regard to the risk of early HCC recurrence.
