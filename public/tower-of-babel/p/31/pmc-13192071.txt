Association between frailty and chronic lung diseases in middle aged and older population
Authors: Dezheng Zhou, Xinyi Hu, Jinbo Li, Sisi Cai, Yujia Shang, Zhenglong Chai, Xinyue Ji, Jianping Liu, Xiaohong Zhang, Kaiwang Cui, Xiangwen Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have linked frailty in older adults to poor health outcomes, particularly chronic lung disease (CLD). However, this study innovatively concentrated on changes over time rather than solely on the initial frailty status, aiming to investigate the relationship between initial frailty status, its progression, and the incidence of CLD.MethodsUsing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), frailty was assessed with the Rockwood frailty index. Among 6,943 participants, 4,624 (66.6%) were robust, 2,140 (30.8%) prefrail, and 179 (2.6%) frail. Changes in frailty status were evaluated over a two-year follow-up, with CLD identified through self-reported physician diagnoses. Cox proportional hazards models calculated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusting for confounders.ResultsRobust individuals who progressed to pre-frail or frail status had a higher risk of CLD (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.27–1.93). Conversely, frail participants who improved to robust or pre-frail status showed a lower risk (HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.37–1.61). Additionally, pre-frail participants who regained robust status had reduced CLD risk (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.44–0.80).ConclusionsFrailty progression increases CLD risk, while recovery decreases it, underscoring the importance of frailty screening in clinical practice for preventing and managing CLD in the ageing population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27240-4.Keywords: Frailty, Chronic lung disease, CHARLS, Older adults, Epidemiology

Background

Population aging has emerged as a global phenomenon, with China currently hosting the largest elderly population worldwide. According to the 2020 national census data, there are over 260 million individuals aged 60 and above in China, presenting significant challenges to the healthcare system [1]. Among various issues stemming from population aging, frailty is particularly concerning [2, 3]. Frailty is an age-related clinical condition marked by a decline in the physiological capacity of various organ systems. It is characterized by multiple functional impairments and reduced physiological reserves, leading to increased vulnerability and a decreased ability to cope with stressors in individuals [4–6]. This condition heightens dependence, fragility, and mortality risk. Compared to their non-frail counterparts, frail older adults exhibit greater susceptibility to adverse health outcomes such as falls, disability, delirium, emergency hospital admissions, and even death [7]. Thus, interventions to prevent or slow the progression of frailty among older adults, before it leads to significant functional decline, are key concerns for health­care policy and provision [8, 9]. While some leading health authorities and organizations have recommended that frailty should be identified in all healthcare encounters with older adults, particularly those with multimorbidity [10, 11].

Concurrently, this increased longevity is accompanied by a rising prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases [12]. As the primary contributors to the global health burden, chronic diseases represent a significant obstacle to China’s efforts in achieving the “Healthy China 2030” initiative [13, 14]. Chronic lung diseases (CLD) encompass a range of conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic bronchitis, interstitial lung disease (ILD), pulmonary heart disease, emphysema, and other persistent pulmonary disorders; however, lung tumors are excluded from this classification [15, 16]. Patients suffering from CLD frequently experience severe respiratory symptoms and endure a diminished quality of life [17]. Notably, both the Global Status Report on Noncommunicable Diseases published in 2012 and the Global Burden of Diseases Report released in 2019 identified CLD as the third leading cause of mortality worldwide [18]. Prior research has established a bidirectional relationship between frailty and respiratory illnesses, but evidence linking chronic lung diseases—such as chronic bronchitis, pulmonary heart disease, emphysema, and other persistent pulmonary conditions—with frailty in older adults remains limited [19]. A recent meta-analysis revealed that frailty is highly prevalent in CLD and is strongly associated with worse disease progression, higher all-cause mortality (HR: 2.64, 95% CI 1.74–4.02), and increased risk of hospitalizations (OR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.05–2.61) [20]. Furthermore, previous studies have predominantly concentrated on baseline frailty status without accounting for changes in frailty over the course of follow-up assessments [21]. In contrast to a single evaluation of frailty at baseline, examining fluctuations in frailty status may provide a more comprehensive understanding of biological associations, including the relationships between frailty progression and CLD. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that interventions can potentially reverse frailty outcomes [22, 23]. Evaluating the risk of incident CLD in individuals who recover from frailty is essential for substantiating the need for targeted interventions addressing frailty within respiratory illness management. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the associations between changes in frailty status and risks for incident CLD.

In the present study, we utilized the prospective cohorts from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Our objective was to investigate the associations between changes in frailty status and the risk of incident CLD. We hypothesized that an increase in frailty status would correspond with a heightened risk of incident CLD, whereas a recovery in frailty status would be associated with a reduced risk of incident CLD.

Method

Study design and participants

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative longitudinal survey targeting individuals in China aged 45 years or older. All participants underwent face-to-face interviews conducted by trained staff using a standardized questionnaire to gather data on sociodemographic information, lifestyle factors, and health-related information. Following the initial assessment, CHARLS participants were revisited and interviewed every two years through face-to-face Computer Assisted Personal Interviews (CAPI) for ongoing data collection. In this study, wave 1 (2011) of CHARLS was designated as the baseline, while wave 2 (2013) served as the second survey period. We utilized data from both the baseline and second survey to assess the dynamic nature of frailty status over time. Additionally, subsequent waves—culminating in wave 4 in 2018—enabled continuous tracking of outcomes, facilitating longitudinal monitoring of changes and developments in frailty status. The CHARLS received approval from the Ethics Review Committees of Peking University, and informed consent was obtained from each participant involved in this cohort study.

The selection process of the study population is illustrated in Fig. 1. The data for our study were obtained from the CHARLS. Among the 17,235 participants aged ≥ 45 years from CHARLS, we first excluded 5,290 individuals due to lung disease (n = 1,795) or missing data necessary for calculating the frailty index (FI) at baseline (2011) (n = 3,485), as well as those with missing data on lung disease status (n = 10). Furthermore, at wave 2 (2013), an additional 3,435 participants were excluded: this included those with lung disease (n = 448) and those lacking complete FI calculation data (n = 2,987). Ultimately, we also removed an additional 1,567 participants who had incomplete covariate data. As a result, a total of 6,943 participants remained for inclusion in the final analyses.

Assessment of frailty

A common approach for measuring frailty is the calculation of a FI, based on the proportion of measured health deficits that individuals accrue with age [24]. In this study, we constructed the FI following standard procedures described previously after screening baseline self-reported or measured data. Based on the data of CHARLS, we selected 30 items for FI construction, involving disease, symptoms, disability, physical function, depression, and cognition (see Supplementary Table S1). Each deficit was dichotomized or mapped into the 0–1 interval according to corresponding cut-off values, with 0 (absence of a deficit) and 1 indicating the maximal expression of the deficit (the unhealthiest state). A higher value of cognitive score indicated a worse, frailer status. For each participant, FI was calculated by the sum of the present health deficits divided by 30. Thus, the FI was a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, and a higher FI indicated a higher level of frailty. As suggested by previous studies, frailty status was further categorized into three levels: robust (FI ≤ 0.10), pre-frail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25) [25, 26]. Changes in frailty status were evaluated by frailty status at baseline and the second survey was conducted two years after the baseline (see Supplementary Figure S1). Total FI and change in FI were also used to assess the dynamic nature of frailty status. Total FI was calculated by the FI at baseline plus the FI at the second survey, while change in FI was calculated by the FI at the second survey minus the FI at baseline.

Covariates

The covariates considered in this study included age, sex, education level, marital status, residence, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Education level was specifically categorized into three distinct groups: illiterate individuals, those with a primary school education or below, and those with a middle school education or higher. Marital status was classified into two categories: married or partnered individuals and those with other marital statuses (including separated, divorced, unmarried, or widowed). Smoking status was divided into three categories: never smokers, former smokers who have smoked at some point in their lives but do not currently smoke, and current smokers. Similarly, drinking habits were categorized as never drinkers, ever drinkers who have consumed alcohol at any time but do not currently drink it regularly, and current drinkers.

Outcome ascertainment and follow-up

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of CLD. CLD encompasses a range of conditions, including chronic bronchitis, pulmonary heart disease, emphysema, and other chronic pulmonary disorders; however, lung tumors were excluded from this definition. In this study, diagnoses of CLD were both self-reported by participants and confirmed by physicians. Data regarding CLD were collected by trained investigators who posed the question: “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor with CLD (excluding tumors or cancer), such as chronic bronchitis, pulmonary heart disease, or emphysema?” A participant was classified as having physician-diagnosed CLD if they answered ‘yes’ and the onset time was recorded at baseline and during follow-up assessments. Investigators verified the accuracy of these responses through double-checking procedures. In analyzing baseline frailty status, follow-up commenced from wave 1 of CHARLS. For analyses concerning changes in frailty status, follow-up began during wave 2 of CHARLS. The follow-up period concluded upon the first observation of CLD or at a predetermined censoring date if it occurred earlier. The final survey date attended by each participant served as the censoring date for data collection purposes. The most recent survey available prior to this analysis was CHARLS wave 4 conducted in 2018 [27–29].

Statistical analyses

For descriptive statistics, continuous variables were presented as mean [standard deviation (SD)] or median [interquartile range (IQR)], while categorical variables were reported as number (percentage).

Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) along with its 95% confidence interval (95% CI), to explore the relationship between the change in frailty status and the risk of developing chronic liver disease (CLD). Two Cox regression models were fitted with robust participants as the reference. Model 1 was unadjusted, whereas Model 2 was multivariable-adjusted, accounting for factors such as age, sex, education, marital status, residence, body mass index, smoking, and alcohol consumption. The similar methodologies were also used to examine the associations between changes in FI and total FI with the incidence of CLD. The changes in FI and total FI were categorized into quartiles, with the lower quartiles serving as the reference group. Furthermore, we conducted a trend test by treating quartiles as a continuous variable [30, 31]. The proportional hazards assumptions for these Cox regression models were verified using Schoenfeld residuals [32, 33]. And all cox model satisfied the proportional hazards assumption (all P-values > 0.05).

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we repeated the analyses after addressing the missing values for these covariates through multiple imputations using chained equations. Second, to minimize the reverse causality, the main analyses were repeated after excluding participants who occurred CLD in the first year of follow-up. Third, stratified analyses were performed by sex and age (middle-aged: < 65 years; older: ≥ 65 years). The statistical significance of interaction was evaluated by the likelihood ratio test [34, 35]. Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which the FI was modeled as a continuous variable instead of quartiles.

All statistical analyses were carried out by Stata SE, version 15.0 (Stata Corp LP., College Station, Texas, USA). All P-values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

The results were consistent with the main analysis, when we imputed the missing covariates (supplementary Tables 2–4), or excluded participants who occurred CLD in the first year of follow-up (supplementary Tables 5–7). In the stratified analyses, the results were similar in males and females (supplementary Tables 8–10) or in middle-aged and older participants (supplementary Tables 11–13). Meanwhile, the interaction of FI with sex or age were not significant. When the FI was used as continuous, each 0.1 point increase in total FI or ΔFI were associated with a 18% (HR = 1.18; 95% CI 1.05–1.31) or 24% (HR = 1.24; 95% CI 1.18–1.29) higher risk of incident CLD, respectively (supplementary Table 14).

Discussion

By analyzing the prospective cohort study CHARLS, we identified associations between changes in frailty status and the risk of CLD occurrence. Participants classified as pre-frail or frail exhibited a heightened risk for incident CLD compared to their fit counterparts. Furthermore, robust participants who transitioned to a pre-frail or frail state also demonstrated an increased risk of CLD relative to stable robust individuals. Conversely, pre-frail participants who reverted to a frail state, as well as frail participants returning to either a frail or pre-frail condition, showed a diminished risk of incident CLD when compared to stable participants. Additionally, higher total FI scores and increases in FI were linked with elevated risks of incident CLD.

As the global older population continues to expand rapidly, frailty is becoming an increasingly prevalent condition. It has been demonstrated that there was a close association between frailty status and respiratory health in previous studies [36, 37]. Observational studies in ILD have shown that frailty is associated with mortality and adverse health outcomes such as quality of life [38]. A systematic review on frailty impact in chronic respiratory disease concluded that frailty negatively affects the quality of life and increases the risk of mortality in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis/ILD [39]. Besides, frailty is considered an independent risk factor for hospital admissions and mortality in ILD, including pre- and post-lung transplantation [40]. In our study, we observed consistent results indicating elevated risks of incident CLD in pre-frail and frail participants compared to robust participants. Basic research suggests that the biological mechanisms linking frailty with CLD may involve shared physiological pathways, such as systemic inflammation, endocrine/metabolic dysfunction, and physical function (lower limb muscle strength, exercise capacity, physical activity) [41–44]. Notably, our results remained significant even after adjusting for traditional respiratory risk factors. These findings further support the notion of frailty as an independent risk factor for CLD.

Our study also investigated the associations between changes in frailty status and incident CLD—an aspect not previously examined. In a study of 42 775 community-dwelling older participants, 42.8% experienced a change in frailty status (13.7% improvement and 29.1% deterioration) during a mean follow-up of 3.9 years [45]. The dynamically changing nature of the frailty states in CHARLS can also be confirmed in our study. Importantly, we found that robust participants who progressed to pre-frail or frail status exhibited increased risks of incident CLD compared to those who remained stable in their robust condition. This finding underscores the detrimental impact of frailty progression on incident CLD.

Although there is no statistical significance between the change from pre-frail state to frail state and incident CLD, which may be due to a small number of participants, the results showed a trend of increasing risk of incident CLD. Moreover, given that the transition from pre-frail state to frail state has been demonstrated to increase mortality and the incidence of cardiovascular disease, it is reasonable to suggest the change from pre-frail state to frail state may have an adverse impact on the incidence of CLD [38, 46]. Conversely, decreased risks of incident CLD were observed among frail participants who recovered to either robust or pre-frail statuses and among pre-frail individuals returning to robust status when compared with their stable counterparts. This suggests substantial benefits associated with reversing frailty concerning incident CLD outcomes.

Beyond the categorized assessments of frailty status, we further examined its dynamic nature through the total FI and the changes therein. Higher total FI scores and increases in FI were correlated with elevated risks for incident CLD, thereby corroborating our earlier findings.

The results of our study hold significant potential to inform clinical practice and public health policy. However, frailty was mentioned very briefly in the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease international guidelines or in the Global Initiative for Asthma international guidelines [47, 48]. The COPD-X Plan, a guidelines document in Australia and New Zealand, was one of the few sources that dedicated significant attention to frailty-related issues [49]. It is essential that frailty assessment be integrated into routine CLD management, particularly within the older population. We recommend identifying individuals exhibiting frailty or pre-frailty as primary targets for preventing adverse CLD outcomes or related events. Furthermore, it is imperative that robust individuals assess risk factors associated with frailty, enabling early identification of at-risk individuals and facilitating timely implementation of appropriate prevention strategies aimed at delaying the progression of frailty.

Moreover, it is important to note that frailty represents a reversible state, this is evidenced by observations indicating reduced CLD risks among individuals who successfully reversed their frailty status. Consequently, substantial efforts must be dedicated to implementing effective interventions for reversing frail conditions. In contrast to established frailty, pre-frailty may present a more promising golden period for intervention regarding CLD prevention due to its higher likelihood of transitioning towards robust status.

As far as we are aware, there has been no previous research investigating the associations between changes in frailty status and risks of incident CLD. Besides, we utilized the prospective cohorts from the nationally representative sample of CHARLS, which has high response rates and data quality. Furthermore, the diverse sensitivity analyses also ensured the robustness of our results and enhanced the reliability of our findings.

However, some limitations should also be highlighted. Firstly, recall bias cannot be fully excluded since our research data were based on self-reported. The absence of standardized objective assessments (e.g., spirometry, CT imaging) complicates distinguishing true CLD cases from undiagnosed or asymptomatic individuals, potentially biasing prevalence estimates. Secondary, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding such as dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and comorbidities (e.g., diabetes) may independently influence CLD risk, which could potentially bias our observed frailty-CLD association. Third, some categories contain fewer individuals, for instance, those who progressed from robust to frail or from frail to robust, which have to merge with proximate categorization, precluding further detailed, individual analysis. Future research should employ longitudinal cohort designs with extended follow-up periods to capture dynamic frailty transitions and integrate objective biomarkers (e.g., inflammatory cytokines) and comprehensive lifestyle assessments to elucidate the biological pathways linking frailty and CLD.

Conclusion

Different changes in frailty status are associated with varying risks of incident CLD. Specifically, a deterioration in frailty status correlates with an increased risk of CLD, while improvement in frailty status is linked to a reduced risk. Future research should focus on developing targeted preventive strategies aimed at slowing the progression of frailty and implementing interventions designed to reverse frailty within the context of CLD control and management practices.
