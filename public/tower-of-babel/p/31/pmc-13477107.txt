Association between preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and outcomes after deep hypothermic circulatory arrest: A cohort study of 1300 patients
Authors: Alexander C. Gregg, Arnar B. Ingason, Nathnael Feleke, Katherine Krieger, Matthew Rotoli, Mohamed Rahouma, Giovanni Soletti, Francesca Pisano, Shamah Shiyam, Charles Mack, Eilon Ram, Christopher Lau, Leonard N. Girardi, Mario Gaudino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the association between preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and outcomes following cardiovascular surgery with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA).MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis. All cardiovascular surgical procedures with DHCA between July 1997 and January 2025 were included. Maximally selected rank statistics identified the optimal NLR cutoff. This NLR cutoff was used for comparisons between high-NLR and low-NLR cohorts. Multivariable regression analysis assessed independent associations with outcomes. NLR was evaluated as a continuous variable to assess associations irrespective of derived cutoff. The primary outcome was the composite of in-hospital major adverse events (MAE), consisting of operative mortality, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, reexploration for bleeding, tracheostomy, and new postoperative dialysis. The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality.ResultsOur analysis included 1335 patients, with 16.7% (223/1335) in the high-NLR cohort (≥6.22). The high-NLR cohort had increased incidence of smoking (62.8% [140/223] vs 52.1% [579/1112]; P = .004), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (26.9% [60/223] vs 14.1% [157/1112]; P < .001), peripheral vascular disease (11.7% [26/223] vs 7.3% [81/1112]; P = .04), and renal disease (37.2% [83/223] vs 12.3% [137/1112]; P < .001). The incidence of MAE was higher in the high-NLR cohort (11.7% [26/223] vs 5.8% [64/1112]; P = .002). Median follow-up was 10.6 years (95% confidence interval [CI], 9.9-11.6 years). Ten-year survival was lower in high-NLR cohort (52.7% [95% CI, 46%-60.5%] vs 71.3% [95% CI, 68.2%-74.5%]). On multivariate analysis, NLR was associated with increased risk of MAE (odds ratio, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.00-1.07; P = .02) and all-cause mortality (hazrd ratio, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.02-1.05; P < .001).ConclusionsPreoperative NLR was associated with increased risk of morbidity and all-cause mortality independent of comorbidities following cardiovascular surgery with DHCA.

Preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is associated with postoperative morbidity and mortality after deep hypothermic circulatory arrest.

Central Message

An elevated preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is independently associated with increased morbidity and mortality after cardiovascular surgery requiring deep hypothermic circulatory arrest.

Perspective

Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest remains integral to complex cardiovascular surgery but carries substantial inflammatory and ischemia–reperfusion risk. Preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, through its association with adverse postoperative outcomes, may serve as a simple, readily available tool to enhance perioperative risk stratification and potentially inform future optimization.

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a cellular index derived from the complete blood count (CBC), has emerged as a marker of baseline inflammatory dysregulation.1,2 This ratio, which reflects the relative activity of the innate (neutrophils) and adaptive (lymphocytes) immune system, may indicate a patient's underlying inflammatory state and quantify susceptibility to further immune perturbation.3 This concept has been particularly relevant in cardiovascular surgery, where chronic inflammation contributes to disease pathogenesis, as in the case of coronary artery atherosclerosis or aneurysm dilation.1,4,5 This baseline inflammatory dysregulation also may influence cardiac care, predisposing patients to an increased risk of complications when undergoing intervention. This phenomenon has been observed in prior series of thoracoabdominal and abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, in which an elevated NLR, reflecting the potential for a disproportionate inflammatory response, has been associated with increased rates of morbidity and mortality.6, 7, 8 Additional studies have supported these findings, reporting similar associations between elevated preoperative NLR and adverse outcomes across multiple cardiovascular interventional domains, including coronary artery bypass grafting, transcatheter aortic valve replacement and mitral valve surgery.9,10

Despite this recognition of NLR as a prognosticator in cardiovascular intervention, its role in the setting of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) has not been characterized to date. DHCA is used in complex aortic surgery to facilitate cerebral protection and operative exposure; however, the arrest period and subsequent reinitiation of perfusion are intrinsically linked to profound ischemia–reperfusion injury and systemic inflammatory activation.11 Both experimental and clinical studies have reported a duration-dependent relationship between circulatory arrest time and postoperative neurologic or end-organ injury, highlighting the cumulative ischemic and inflammatory burden associated with the DHCA mechanism.11, 12, 13 In this context, NLR represents a biologically plausible biomarker for quantifying a patient's baseline inflammatory milieu and propensity for dysregulated inflammatory response to injury. The significance of NLR in this framework has not yet been systematically evaluated. Accordingly, the present study sought to evaluate the association between preoperative NLR and adverse postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery requiring DHCA.

Methods

Study Design

This study was a single-center retrospective cohort analysis conducted via a prospectively maintained institutional database at a high-volume tertiary center. The study was approved by the Weill Cornell Institutional Review Board (approval 1607017424, approved October 17, 2024). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

All consecutive patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery using DHCA between July 1997 and January 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Patients were included who had a preoperative CBC available for NLR calculation. NLR was calculated as the ratio of the absolute neutrophil count over the absolute lymphocyte count.14,15 When multiple preoperative CBCs were available, the NLR value closest to the time of surgery was used. Perioperative clinical data were obtained from the institutional database. Survival outcomes were ascertained through in-person clinical follow-up and/or linkage to the National Death Index.

Surgical Technique

Our group's approach to DHCA has been described previously.16 In brief, the majority of procedures were performed via median sternotomy, with preferential use of central arterial cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass. Patients were initiated on cardiopulmonary bypass, with pump flows maintained at 2.4 L/minute/m2 during normothermia and 1.8 L/minute/m2 during cooling. Patients were cooled to a target core temperature of 18 °C for a minimum of 30 minutes. Three minutes prior to circulatory arrest, 500 mg of methohexital was administered for neuroprotection. Patients were then placed in Trendelenburg position, and systemic circulation was arrested. During DHCA, retrograde cerebral perfusion was delivered via the superior vena cava cannula at a flow rate of 150 to 300 mL/minute at 14 °C, with central venous pressure maintained at <30 mmHg. On resumption of systemic circulation, retrograde cerebral perfusion was continued until full cardiopulmonary bypass flow was reestablished. Controlled systemic rewarming was then initiated, maintaining a temperature gradient of no more than 10 °C between the perfusate and the patient's core temperature. Near-infrared spectroscopy was used to confirm symmetric cerebral cooling and warming throughout. Cardiopulmonary bypass was discontinued at a core temperature of 35 °C.

Outcome Assessment

The primary outcome was the composite of in-hospital major adverse events (MAE), including operative mortality (OM; defined as death prior to discharge or within 30 days of index operation), myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), reexploration for bleeding, tracheostomy and new postoperative dialysis. The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequency counts with percentages and compared between the cohorts using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using Mann-Whitney U test, or as means with standard deviations and compared using t-test based on normality testing via Shapiro-Wilk.

Maximally selected rank statistics were used to identify an optimal cutoff value for preoperative NLR based on its association with all-cause mortality. This outcome-oriented approach systematically evaluates all possible dichotomization points and selects the threshold that maximizes the standardized log-rank statistic with respect to the outcome of interest.17 Mortality was selected for cutpoint derivation given its objective, time-to-event nature and suitability for log-rank–based optimization. The resulting NLR cutoff was used solely for descriptive and univariate comparisons between high- and low-NLR cohorts, whereas all primary inferential analyses were performed using NLR as a continuous variable.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent associations with the primary outcome. Included covariates, selected a priori, were NLR, age, sex, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), prior CVA, preoperative renal disease, New York Heart Association heart failure class, ejection fraction, surgical year, connective tissue disease, and prior cardiovascular surgery. Identical covariates were used in multivariable Cox regression to identify independent associations with the secondary outcome.

Results were reported as odds ratio (OR) or hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to estimate mortality, with cohorts compared using the log-rank test. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant without multiplicity adjustment.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) within RStudio. The following R packages were used: “maxstat,” “tableone,” “survival,” and “survminer.”17, 18, 19, 20

Operative Data

Patients in the low-NLR cohort were more likely to undergo elective surgery (73.9% [822/1112] vs 35.9% [80/223]; P < .001) (Table 2). The median cardiopulmonary bypass time was shorter in the low-NLR cohort (137 [IQR, 123-160] minutes vs 146 [IQR, 129-172] minutes; P < .001), as was the median duration of circulatory arrest (22 [IQR, 17-33] minutes vs 25 [IQR, 20-34] minutes; P = .001). Cardiac ischemic time did not differ significantly between the 2 cohorts. The low-NLR cohort was more likely to undergo concomitant aortic valve replacement (22.5% [250/1112] vs 15.2% [34/223]; P = .02). Postoperative Outcomes

The low-NLR cohort experienced a significantly lower incidence of MAEs compared with the high-NLR cohort (5.8% [64/1112] vs 11.7% [26/223]; P = .002) (Table 3). This difference was driven by lower rates of OM (1.9% [21/1112] vs 4.5% [10/223]; P = .035), tracheostomy (1.2% [13/1112] vs 4.5% [10/223]; P = .001), and dialysis (1.3% [14/1112] vs 4.9% [11/223]; P = .001) in the low-NLR cohort. Discussion

Procedures requiring DHCA subject patients to profound physiologic stress, driven by global ischemia, reperfusion injury, and systemic inflammatory activation.11,13 In this first study to evaluate the relationship between NLR, a marker of baseline inflammatory dysregulation and a surrogate of inflammatory responsiveness, and DHCA, preoperative NLR was found to be independently associated with an increased risk of postoperative morbidity and mortality following cardiovascular surgery with DHCA. Although this association is biologically consistent with previously reported associations between NLR and postoperative outcomes for other cardiovascular interventions,1,6, 7, 8, 9, 10 it has not been characterized in the hypothermic circulatory arrest literature until now. The relevance of these findings is underscored by the expanding patient population undergoing complex thoracic aortic and arch surgery, in whom periods of hypothermic circulatory arrest remain central operative strategies. Specifically, registry data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons reported a 25% increase in the use of hypothermic circulatory arrest between 2011 and 2014, highlighting the current clinical footprint of hypothermic circulatory arrest in modern cardiovascular surgery.21

The clinical significance of these data is best interpreted through comparison of the univariate and multivariable analyses. When stratified by the internally derived NLR cutoff (6.22), the high-NLR cohort had a greater comorbidity burden, including higher rates of smoking, COPD, peripheral vascular disease, and renal disease, conditions previously associated with elevated NLR.22, 23, 24 These pathologic differences resulted in distinct operative environments, with the high-NLR cohort more frequently undergoing urgent/emergent procedures with longer cardiopulmonary bypass and circulatory arrest times. Consistent with these distinctions, this cohort had higher incidences of OM and postoperative complications with worse long-term survival. Overall, the results of this univariate stratification highlight the presence of clinically distinct inflammatory phenotypes within the study population, supporting the potential role of NLR in unrefined perioperative risk stratification.

More critically, however, the principal findings of this study are derived from the multivariable analyses. When analyzing the entire study population without NLR stratification and adjusting for comorbidities in multivariable models, preoperative NLR was found to be independently associated with an increased risk of both mortality and morbidity. Specifically, preoperative NLR was associated with both outcomes, with each unit increase associated with a 3% to 4% higher adjusted risk. Importantly, this association persisted alongside other established predictors of adverse events following circulatory arrest, including prior CVA, preoperative renal disease, and COPD, supporting the robustness and independence of this finding.6,25

Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed association between preoperative NLR and DHCA postoperative outcomes are not explained solely by differences in baseline comorbidity burden resulting in altered inflammatory function, but instead may reflect an underlying biological vulnerability influencing postoperative outcomes. In support of this concept, a prior retrospective series of approximately 500 total aortic arch repairs performed with DHCA found that the development of a severe systemic inflammatory response was independently associated with major adverse complications, implicating inflammatory responsiveness itself as a potential determinant of postoperative risk.26 In this context, an elevated preoperative NLR may identify patients with heightened inflammatory susceptibility that amplifies the deleterious ischemia–reperfusion injury inherent to DHCA.

From a clinical perspective, these findings may have several implications. Most directly, preoperative NLR may represent a simple, readily available biomarker for identifying high-risk patients undergoing DHCA. Given the routine availability and low cost of CBC,1 incorporation of NLR into preoperative risk assessment may facilitate more refined perioperative stratification in patients undergoing cardiac procedures necessitating DHCA.

Beyond risk stratification, preoperatively NLR also may serve as a potentially modifiable index of optimization. Prior studies have reported that interventions such as tobacco cessation and improved maintenance of COPD are associated with reductions in baseline NLR.22,23 In light of the association between NLR and adverse outcomes observed in the present study, it is plausible that attenuation of preoperative systemic inflammation may translate into improved perioperative outcomes. This hypothesis is indirectly supported by prior randomized clinical trials, such as CANTOS,27 COLCOT,28 and LoDoCo2,29 which reported that reductions in basal inflammatory burden were associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes.30 However, to date no prospective studies have specifically evaluated whether targeted preoperative modulation of NLR alters postoperative risk in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Accordingly, while these data suggest a potential role for NLR as both a prognostic marker and a therapeutic target, validation will be required before NLR-guided preoperative optimization strategies can be recommended.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. First, this was a retrospective, single-center observational cohort study, which limits causal inference and may restrict the generalizability of these results to other institutions with differing perioperative management or case mixes. Although multivariable adjustment was performed for a broad range of confounders, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured or unidentified variables cannot be excluded. Second, NLR is a nonspecific marker of systemic inflammation and may be influenced by acute physiologic stress or pathologic conditions. This limitation is particularly relevant in patients presenting with acute dissection or rupture, in whom inflammatory activation may reflect both chronic dysregulation and acute disease-related responses. However, NLR was modeled as a continuous variable to mitigate arbitrary threshold effects, and its independent association with outcomes suggests that it captures biologically meaningful risk. Importantly, while acute inflammatory activation may limit the interpretability of NLR as a modifiable preoperative target in emergent presentations, it does not invalidate its utility as a prognostic biomarker for risk stratification in this population. Finally, this study was not designed to establish or validate a specific NLR cutoff for prognostication; rather, it aimed to first characterize the association between preoperative NLR and DHCA postoperative outcomes, providing a foundation for future prospective and mechanistic investigation.

Conclusions

Preoperative NLR was associated with increased risk of both in-hospital MAE and all-cause mortality following cardiac surgery using DHCA. This association persisted after adjustment for variables known to increase NLR and operative risk, suggesting that baseline inflammatory dysregulation may exert an independent influence on postoperative outcomes. Taken together, these findings support the potential use of preoperative NLR as a prognostic biomarker in patients undergoing DHCA and raise the possibility that NLR may represent a modifiable index for preoperative risk optimization. Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to determine whether NLR-guided strategies can have a meaningful effect on improving outcomes in patients undergoing procedures requiring DHCA.
