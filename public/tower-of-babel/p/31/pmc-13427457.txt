CaMKIIγ/δ contributes to mitochondrial metabolic adaptation in skeletal muscle during endurance training
Authors: Ryoka Tsukahara, Yasuo Kitajima, Yuka Ichihashi, Hikaru Iwase, Shun Ohki, Yusuke Sotomaru, Tomohiro Kurosaki, Yohei Kawano, Tomoharu Yasuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) is activated in skeletal muscle with exercise, yet its physiological role in endurance training adaptation remains unclear. This study determined whether endogenous CaMKIIγ/δ in skeletal muscle is required for endurance training–induced metabolic remodeling and exercise adaptation.MethodsWe generated male skeletal muscle-specific CaMKIIγ/δ knockout (CaMKII mKO) mice and assessed muscle phenotype, exercise capacity, and training adaptation. Acute exercise–induced CaMKII activation was evaluated by phosphorylation status. Transcriptomic changes were analyzed by RNA sequencing before and after 4 weeks of treadmill endurance training. Mitochondrial protein abundance, ultrastructure, and bioenergetics were examined by immunoblotting, transmission electron microscopy, and Seahorse extracellular flux analysis in myotubes with acute CaMKIIγ/δ deletion.ResultsAcute treadmill exercise induced CaMKII phosphorylation in muscle without altering total CaMKII abundance. CaMKII mKO mice showed normal muscle mass, grip strength, and baseline performance. However, endurance training–induced improvement in running capacity was significantly blunted. Transcriptomic analyses revealed downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic gene programs in CaMKII-deficient muscle at baseline and after training. OXPHOS complex protein abundance was partially reduced at baseline and markedly reduced across complexes I–V after training. CaMKII deficiency increased ultrastructurally abnormal mitochondria without reducing mitochondrial number. Consistently, CaMKII-deficient myotubes showed lower absolute per-well oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification rate.ConclusionsIn male mice, endogenous CaMKIIγ/δ in muscle is dispensable for baseline locomotor performance but essential for endurance training-induced metabolic remodeling and mitochondrial integrity. These findings support a role for CaMKII in linking contraction-induced calcium signaling to metabolic adaptation in muscle.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle comprises approximately 40% of body mass and serves as a central organ for locomotion, thermogenesis, and whole-body energy metabolism [1]. Beyond its mechanical function, skeletal muscle is a major determinant of metabolic health and longevity, as reduced muscle mass or strength is strongly associated with increased all-cause mortality [2]. A defining feature of skeletal muscle is its remarkable plasticity, enabling dynamic adaptation to diverse physiological demands such as exercise. Endurance exercise induces angiogenesis, mitochondrial remodeling, and enhanced oxidative metabolism, whereas resistance exercise can promote protein synthesis and hypertrophic growth, depending on the mode and loading characteristics of the exercise stimulus [3]. Despite the profound impact of exercise on muscle health and systemic physiology, the molecular mechanisms that couple repeated contractile activity to long-term metabolic adaptation remain incompletely understood.

Calcium signaling represents a fundamental mechanism linking muscle contraction to adaptive remodeling [4,5]. During exercise, motor neuron activity triggers repetitive calcium ion release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), generating transient but recurrent elevations in intracellular calcium [6]. These calcium signals engage a network of calcium-sensitive effectors, including calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase (CaMKK), calcineurin, and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) [5,[7], [8], [9]]. Among these, CaMKII is abundantly expressed in skeletal muscle and is rapidly activated by contractile activity, yet its physiological function in adult muscle remains poorly defined. This gap is notable given the central role of CaMKII in calcium signal decoding in excitable tissues such as the brain and heart [10].

CaMKII occurs in four isoforms (α, β, γ, and δ) with distinct tissue distribution and biological functions. In adult skeletal muscle, CaMKIIβ, CaMKIIγ, and CaMKIIδ are expressed, whereas CaMKIIα is largely restricted to the central nervous system. During muscle contraction, Calcium-dependent activation of CaMKII leads to autophosphorylation at Thr286 or Thr287, conferring sustained kinase activity beyond the initiating calcium transient. In addition to this canonical Ca2+-dependent mechanism, CaMKII signaling may be modulated by exercise-associated reactive oxygen species (ROS). In human skeletal muscle, antioxidant administration attenuated the increase in Thr286-CaMKII phosphorylation induced by sprint exercise in severe acute hypoxia, supporting a contribution of redox signaling to the acute CaMKII response [11]. Similarly, supplementation with a mango leaf polyphenol extract combined with quercetin attenuated the exercise-associated Thr287-CaMKIIδD response during high-intensity exercise in human skeletal muscle [12]. Moreover, the magnitude and pattern of CaMKII signaling during exercise and training are influenced by the degree of exercise-induced fatigue and by oxygenation and recovery conditions [13,14]. These findings suggest that CaMKII may integrate contraction-associated Ca2+ signals with the metabolic and redox milieu generated during exercise. In both rodents and humans, phosphorylation of CaMKIIγ and CaMKIIδ at Thr287 is robustly increased following acute endurance-type exercise [7,13,15], suggesting that these isoforms act as physiologically relevant sensors of contractile calcium signals. We therefore focused on CaMKIIγ and CaMKIIδ because both are expressed in adult skeletal muscle, are activated by endurance-type exercise, and may have overlapping functions in metabolic adaptation. Although CaMKIIβ is also expressed in skeletal muscle, its muscle-intrinsic contribution to endurance training adaptation remains less well defined. However, whether CaMKIIγ and CaMKIIδ are required for endurance training-induced adaptation in skeletal muscle remains unknown.

Calcium-dependent signaling has long been implicated in transcriptional programs governing mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, including pathways involving class IIa histone deacetylases (HDAC4/5), myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2), and the transcriptional coactivator peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) [16,17]. CaMK family members have also been linked to fiber-type gene regulation (e.g., MyHCIIA) and myogenic differentiation [[18], [19], [20]]. Nevertheless, much of the existing evidence in skeletal muscle relies on non-physiological experimental systems, including overexpression of CaMKI or CaMKIV, which are not appreciably expressed in adult muscle [16], pharmacological CaMKII inhibition with limited specificity [[20], [21], [22]], or studies focused on muscle progenitor cells rather than mature myofibers [20]. As a result, the physiological role of endogenous CaMKII in intact adult skeletal muscle, particularly in the context of exercise training, has remained unresolved.

At the organismal level, insight into CaMKII function in skeletal muscle has been further obscured by systemic genetic models. For example, global deletion of CaMKIIβ results in impaired motor performance and reduced voluntary activity [23], phenotypes largely attributable to cerebellar dysfunction rather than intrinsic muscle defects. Conversely, forced expression of CaMKIIα or CaMKIIβ in skeletal muscle alters contraction kinetics and fatigue resistance [18,24], but these approaches introduce non-native isoforms and rely on electroporation-based gene delivery that can cause muscle damage [[25], [26], [27]]. Thus, whether CaMKII under physiological expression levels contributes to endurance training–induced metabolic remodeling in vivo has remained an open question.

Here, we hypothesized that endogenous CaMKII contributes to integrating contraction-associated calcium signaling with metabolic adaptation during endurance training. To directly test this hypothesis, we generated skeletal muscle–specific CaMKIIγ/δ knockout mice and examined muscle phenotype, exercise performance, and endurance training adaptation under physiological conditions. By integrating in vivo training paradigms with transcriptomic, biochemical, ultrastructural, and cell-autonomous metabolic analyses, we show that CaMKIIγ/δ contributes to mitochondrial integrity, metabolic remodeling, and the adaptive response to endurance exercise.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were housed under controlled conditions (20–22 °C, 50% humidity, 12h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and chow. The skeletal muscle-specific Mlc1-Cre mouse strain was described previously [28,29]. Camk2g flox and Camk2d flox mice were generated at RIKEN RCAI and crossed to generate CaMKIIγ-deficient (Mlc1-Cre; Camk2gf/f), CaMKIIδ-deficient (Mlc1-Cre; Camk2df/f), and CaMKIIγ/δ double-deficient (Mlc1-Cre; Camk2gf/f; Camk2df/f) mice for muscle-specific deletion. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines. All experiments were approved by Hiroshima University's Animal Care Committee (Ref. No. A24-183). All mice used in this study were male and aged between 2 and 3 months at the time of experimentation. This initial mechanistic study was conducted in male mice to maintain a single-sex experimental cohort and minimize biological heterogeneity associated with sex-dependent responses to endurance training. CaMKIIγ- and CaMKIIδ-single knockout mice were used for validation of isoform-specific deletion and baseline phenotyping, including body and muscle weights, grip strength, and maximal running capacity. The endurance-training study and subsequent transcriptomic, biochemical, ultrastructural, and cell-based experiments were conducted using CaMKIIγ/δ double knockout mice and the corresponding control mice, unless otherwise indicated.

Generation of CaMKIIγ- and CaMKIIδ-deficient mice

To generate CaMKIIγ (Camk2g) and CaMKIIδ (Camk2d)-deficient mice, exons 6–10 of Camk2g or exons 7–8 of Camk2d were flanked by loxP sequences and inserted into a plasmid containing neomycin resistance and diphtheria toxin A cassettes. The targeting vectors were electroporated into C57BL/6-derived Bruce4 ES cells, and correctly recombined clones were selected by PCR. Heterozygous ES clones were injected into blastocysts to generate chimeric mice. Primer sequences are in Supplementary Table 1.

Acute exercise bout

Mice were habituated to a motorized treadmill (MELQUEST) on two occasions (10 min at 10 m/min and 0° grade) prior to the experimental exercise bout. As previously reported [30], for the acute exercise bout, mice completed 60 min of running on the treadmill (10° grade) at repetitions of variable exercise intensity (10 m/min, 10 min; 12.5 m/min, 2.5 min; 15 m/min, 15 min; 17.5 m/min, 2.5 min; 20 m/min, 30 min). Immediately after completion of the exercise bout, mice were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane by inhalation for approximately 20 s and killed by cervical dislocation. Gastrocnemius muscles were then dissected promptly and transferred to ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails for protein extraction within approximately 3 min after exercise cessation.

Grip strength

Mice were allowed to grasp a horizontal grid connected to a small-animal grip strength meter (MELQUEST) with forelimbs. The force applied to the grid each time before the animal lost its grip was recorded. Grip strength was assessed without prior familiarization; measurements were performed on two consecutive days and averaged for analysis. The average of the two measurements was normalized to the whole-body weight.

Running capacity assessment and exercise training

Mice were acclimated to a motorized treadmill (MELQUEST) through two sessions (10 min at 10 m/min, 0° grade) before testing. As in our previous study [31], mice performed a 5-min warm-up at 10 m/min. The test began at 10 m/min for 30 min, increasing by 2 m/min every 15 min, with no incline. Mice were encouraged to run with a soft brush. Exhaustion was defined as the inability to resume running for 15 s despite light brushing. After testing, the trained group underwent 4 weeks of treadmill training, running 5 days per week. Training consisted of 30 min at varying speeds (10 m/min, 5 min; 12 m/min, 5 min; 14 m/min, 5 min; 16 m/min, 5 min; 18 m/min, 10 min). All trained mice ran equal distances. After 4 weeks, maximal exercise capacity was reassessed 24 h after the end of the training session. Twenty-four hours after the post-training exercise-capacity test, tissues were collected from non-fasted mice at rest. Mice were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane by inhalation for approximately 20 s and killed by cervical dislocation. Muscles were dissected immediately after cervical dislocation and processed according to the downstream analyses described below.

Tissue samples

Immediately after cervical dislocation, tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, and soleus muscles were dissected individually. Excess fat, connective tissue, and tendons were removed. Muscles were processed immediately according to the intended downstream analysis. For acute-exercise Western blotting, gastrocnemius muscles were transferred directly into ice-cold RIPA buffer. Muscle samples intended for RNA or protein analysis were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after dissection and stored at −80 °C. Some muscles embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound and frozen in isopentane cooled with liquid nitrogen for histological analysis, while gastrocnemius samples intended for TEM were immediately placed in fixative as described below. Brain tissue was frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

Western blot analysis

Skeletal muscle samples were lysed with RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific), protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). When necessary, nuclear extracts were prepared using NE-PER® nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction reagents (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. 78833) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentrations were measured in duplicate using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. 23227). Lysates were adjusted to a final protein concentration of 1.0 μg/μL using the corresponding lysis buffer, and 15 μg of total protein was loaded per lane for all tissue and cell lysate immunoblot analyses, including CaMKII, phospho-CaMKII, OXPHOS complex, and HDAC4 assessments. Nuclear extracts were also loaded at 15 μg per lane. Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Invitrogen). Following transfer, membranes were stained with Ponceau S to assess protein transfer and total protein loading. For total protein normalization, the total Ponceau S signal intensity across the entire lane was quantified using ImageJ (NIH). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were detected using the ECL Prime system (GE Healthcare), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ (NIH). Total CaMKII was quantified as the integrated intensity of the CaMKII-immunoreactive band cluster migrating between the 70- and 80-kDa molecular weight markers, which was markedly reduced in CaMKII mKO samples. Phospho-CaMKII was quantified using the corresponding phospho-CaMKII-immunoreactive band cluster within the same 70–80 kDa molecular weight range. For Figure 1A, phospho-CaMKII was normalized to pan-CaMKII to assess exercise-induced CaMKII phosphorylation. In all other immunoblot analyses, target protein signals, including total CaMKII, phospho-CaMKII, OXPHOS complexes, and HDAC4, were normalized to the total Ponceau S signal intensity in the corresponding lane. All samples included in a given comparison were processed and imaged under identical conditions, and exposure times were selected to avoid signal saturation. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total mRNA was extracted using ISOGEN II (Nippon Gene) and the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized with a ReverTra Ace kit (TOYOBO). qPCR was performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO) on a BioRad real-time PCR system following the manufacturer's instructions. The Tbp gene was used as an internal reference gene for normalization of all qPCR analyses, based on its reported stability across muscle-specific genotype and exercise intervention in adult mouse skeletal muscle and its previous use in skeletal muscle/muscle stem cell qPCR analyses [32,33]. Primer sequences are in Supplementary Table 1.

BRB-seq (RNA-seq)

For baseline transcriptomic analysis, gastrocnemius muscles from four independent control mice and four independent CaMKII mKO mice were analyzed. For the endurance-training transcriptomic analysis, gastrocnemius muscles from four independent mice were analyzed in each group: Control-Pre, CaMKII mKO-Pre, Control-Post, and CaMKII mKO-Post. Each RNA-seq library was generated from RNA isolated from one individual mouse, and no samples were pooled.

BRB-seq [34] was performed for preparing libraries with some following modifications. Barcoded oligo-dT based primer (5′-GCCGGTAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGTTCTACAGTCCGACGATCNNNNNNNNNNCCCCCCCCCTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTV-3’; (10) N=UMI, (9) C = cell barcode) was used for single-stranded synthesis and Second Strand Synthesis Module (#E6111, NEB) was used for double-stranded cDNA synthesis. In-house MEDS-B Tn5 transposase [35,36] was used for tagmentation and amplified by 10 cycles of PCR using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (#M0530, Thermo Scientific) and following primers (5′-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACACindexGTTCAGAGTTCTACAGTCCGA-3′, 5′-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGATindex GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGT-3′). 19bp of barcode read (Read1) and 81bp of insert read (Read2) were obtained on Illumina NovaSeq6000.

Data processing (BRB-seq)

Read1(barcode read) was extracted by using UMI-tools (1.1.4) with the following command “umi_tools extract –I read1.fastq --read2-in = read2.fastq --bc-pattern = NNNNNNNNNNCCCCCCCCC --read2-stdout”. Adaptor sequence and low quality sequence were removed and read length below 20bp were discarded by using Trim Galore (0.6.10), and reads were mapped to the GRCm38 reference using HISAT2 (2.2.1). Read counts for each gene and each sample was obtained by summarizing bam file which was produced by featureCounts (2.0.6). Across the BRB-seq dataset, 21,094 genes were detected, with more than 10,000 genes detected in each individual sample. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.40.2), and genes with an adjusted P value (padj) < 0.1 were considered differentially expressed.

Histological analysis of the skeletal muscle

Muscle tissues from both genotypes were mounted on cork disks, frozen, and stored. Cryosections (10 μm) from the mid-belly region were fixed with 2% PFA and blocked with 5% goat serum or the M.O.M kit (Vector Laboratories) for 60 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed, and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, slides were mounted in VECTASHIELD PLUS (Vector Laboratories) and imaged using a Keyence BZ-X810 microscope. Primary antibodies: anti-MyHCI (MABT838, 1:100, Merck), anti-MyHCII (NCL-MHCf, 1:100, Leica), anti-Laminin (L9393, 1:400, Sigma). Secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 350 (A21140, 1:400, invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 546 (A21123, 1:800, invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 647 (A21245, 1:800, Invitrogen).

Quantification of myofiber cross-sectional area and fiber-type composition was performed as described previously [37,38]. Individual myofibers were classified as Type I or Type II according to MyHCI and MyHCII immunoreactivity, respectively. Myofiber borders were identified by laminin staining. The cross-sectional area of individual Type I and Type II fibers was measured using ImageJ (NIH). For each mouse, the mean cross-sectional area of each fiber type was calculated from the analyzed fibers and used as one biological replicate for statistical analysis. Fiber-type composition was assessed by manually counting Type I and Type II fibers in each image. The percentage of each fiber type was calculated as the number of fibers of the corresponding type divided by the total number of myofibers in the image. Values from the analyzed image fields were averaged for each mouse before group-level statistical analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM images were obtained from gastrocnemius muscle samples of three independent control mice and three independent CaMKII mKO mice. All analyzable images obtained from the animals in each group were used for quantitative analyses. For electron microscopic observations of skeletal muscle, tissue samples were fixed in 2% formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences), 2.5% glutaraldehyde (TAAB Laboratories Equipment Ltd) in 0.1M cacodylate buffer (pH. 7.4) solution (nacarai tesque) for 10 days at 4 °C. Tissue samples were washed five times 20 min in 0.1M cacodylate buffer, post-fixed with 1.5% osmium tetra-oxide (TAAB Laboratories Equipment Ltd) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for 2 h, and washed again three times 10 min in 0.1M cacodylate buffer. The samples were dehydrated in ethanol (30, 50, 70, 90, and 100%) and embedded in the TAAB EPON 812 resin (TAAB Laboratories Equipment Ltd), and the resin was polymerized with stepwise increase in temperature (37 °C for 12 h, 45 °C for 6 h, 60 °C for 60 h). The fixed samples were cut in 70 nm sections on the ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica Microsystems GmbH) on a grid. Ultrathin sections were stained with 3.0% uranylacetate aqueous solution 12 min, and lead staining solution (Sigma Aldrich) for 3 min at room temperature. TEM images were acquired using a JEM-1400Plus (JEOL) TEM with 80 kV of acceleration voltage and a sCMOS camera.

Intermyofibrillar mitochondria were identified between myofibrils, whereas subsarcolemmal mitochondria were identified beneath the sarcolemma. TEM images were obtained from gastrocnemius muscles of three independent control mice and three independent CaMKII mKO mice. Mitochondrial morphology was assessed using previously reported ultrastructural criteria [39,40]. Mitochondria were classified as altered when they exhibited one or more of the following features: (i) reduced matrix electron density with matrix dilution, vacuolization, or cavitation; (ii) fragmented or ballooned cristae with intracristal swelling; (iii) partial or complete separation of the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes; or (iv) mitochondrial swelling. Mitochondria lacking these features were classified as normal. All analyzable images obtained from the control and CaMKII mKO groups were used for quantitative analyses.

Primary muscle stem cell isolation and culture

Muscle stem cells (MuSCs) were isolated and digested with type II collagenase as described previously [37,38]. MuSCs were cultured in growth medium (GM) containing DMEM, 30% fetal bovine serum, 1% chicken-embryo extract, 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Myogenic differentiation was induced in differentiation medium (DM) containing DMEM, 5% horse serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. For forced expression of CRE Recombinase, cells were transfected with the pRP[Exp]-CMV > Cre vectors (VectorBuilder) using Lipofectamine LTX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Measurements of cellular oxygen consumption

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured with a Seahorse XF Pro analyzer (Agilent Technologies) using the Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies, Cat. No. 103015-100). MuSCs (5 × 103) were plated in an XF Cell Culture Microplate (Agilent Technologies) and induced to differentiate. For each experiment, identical initial numbers of control and CaMKII-deficient MuSCs were plated in each well and differentiated under identical conditions. OCR and ECAR are presented as per-well measurements obtained under matched initial seeding conditions. One hour before the assay, the culture medium was replaced with XF DMEM Medium containing the substrates (1 mM sodium pyruvate, 4 mM glutamine, and 25 mM glucose) (Agilent Technologies). The assay was performed using intact differentiated myotubes; therefore, exogenous ADP was not added. OCR and ECAR were measured at 37 °C. Three measurement cycles were collected at baseline and after each sequential injection. Oligomycin (1.5 μM) was injected to inhibit ATP synthase, followed by FCCP (0.5 or 1.0 μM) to induce mitochondrial uncoupling and rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 μM each) to inhibit complexes I and III, respectively. FCCP-stimulated maximal OCR was defined as the peak OCR measured after FCCP injection. The OCR remaining after rotenone and antimycin A injection was considered non-mitochondrial OCR. The 1.0 μM FCCP condition is shown in Figure 7D–F, whereas the 0.5 μM FCCP condition is shown in Figure S8A–C.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to genotype; samples and image files were coded prior to analysis. Sample sizes (n) are reported in the figure legends and represent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. For in vivo experiments, n indicates the number of individual mice per group. For cell-based assays, n indicates the number of independent experiments using separately prepared primary cultures from independent MuSC isolations. For immunoblotting of tissue samples, each data point represents a lysate prepared from one individual mouse, and n indicates the number of independent mice per group. For TEM analyses, images were obtained from three independent control mice and three independent CaMKII mKO mice, and all analyzable images obtained from these animals were used for quantification. Data are presented as mean ± SD. For normally distributed variables, differences between two groups were evaluated using an unpaired Student's t-test. For three or more groups, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's test, or two-way ANOVA with Tukey's or Šídák's test, as appropriate, were used. Exact P values are shown in the figures when P ≥ 0.0001; smaller values are indicated as P < 0.0001. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism11.

Results

Acute exercise induces rapid phosphorylation of CaMKII in skeletal muscle

To establish whether CaMKII is acutely engaged by physiological exercise in mice, we examined CaMKII abundance and phosphorylation immediately following a single bout of treadmill running. Total CaMKII protein levels in the gastrocnemius muscle were unchanged after 1 h of exercise compared with sedentary controls (Figure 1A). In contrast, CaMKII phosphorylation was significantly increased in exercised muscle (Figure 1A), indicating rapid post-translational activation without changes in protein expression. These results demonstrate that CaMKII is acutely activated in mouse skeletal muscle by contractile activity, consistent with observations in rats and humans [7,13,15], and support a conserved role for CaMKII as a sensor of exercise-induced calcium signaling.

Generation and validation of skeletal muscle-specific CaMKIIγ/δ knockout mice

To directly investigate the physiological role of endogenous CaMKII in adult skeletal muscle, we generated mice lacking CaMKIIγ and CaMKIIδ selectively in skeletal muscle. Camk2g and Camk2d floxed alleles were crossed with Mlc1-Cre mice to generate skeletal muscle-specific CaMKIIγ/δ knockout mice (CaMKII mKO) (Figure S1A–F). Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed robust reduction of Camk2g and Camk2d transcripts in multiple skeletal muscles, including tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, and soleus (Figure 1B). Deletion efficiency was highest in predominantly fast-twitch muscles, with a more modest but significant reduction in soleus. Consistently, CaMKII protein abundance was markedly reduced in gastrocnemius muscle (Figure 1C). Importantly, Camk2g and Camk2d expression in brain was unaffected, excluding off-target recombination in the central nervous system (Figure 1B). To assess potential compensation by other CaMKII isoforms, we evaluated Camk2a and Camk2b transcript expression in gastrocnemius muscle. Camk2a mRNA was not detected in either genotype under the qPCR conditions used, whereas Camk2b mRNA was readily detected. We therefore assessed CaMKIIβ protein abundance. Neither Camk2b mRNA nor CaMKIIβ protein abundance differed significantly between control and CaMKII mKO mice (Figure S2A, B), providing no evidence for baseline compensatory upregulation of CaMKIIβ. Comparable, isoform-specific gene suppression was observed in CaMKIIγ- and CaMKIIδ-single knockout mice (Figures S3A and S4A, respectively), validating the specificity and efficiency of the genetic model.

Despite prior reports implicating CaMKII in myogenic differentiation in vitro [20], CaMKII mKO mice displayed normal body weight, absolute muscle mass, and muscle mass normalized to body weight (Figure 1D,E). Similar results were obtained in CaMKIIγ- and CaMKIIδ-single knockout mice (Figure S3B, C and S4B, C, respectively). These findings indicate that CaMKIIγ/δ is dispensable for muscle growth and maintenance under basal conditions in vivo.

Loss of CaMKII impairs oxidative metabolic gene programs in resting skeletal muscle

To determine whether CaMKII deficiency alters skeletal muscle gene expression, we performed RNA sequencing of gastrocnemius muscle from control and CaMKII mKO mice. Across the BRB-seq dataset, 21,094 genes were detected, with more than 10,000 genes detected in each individual sample. Principal component analysis revealed clear segregation of the two genotypes (Figure 2A). Using an adjusted P value (padj) < 0.1 as the threshold, differential expression analysis identified 105 upregulated and 191 downregulated genes in CaMKII mKO muscle. We next assessed whether CaMKII contributes to adaptation to endurance training. Mice were subjected to a 4-week treadmill training protocol, and endurance capacity was evaluated before and after training (Figure 3C). Pre-training maximal running time did not differ between genotypes (Figure 3D). In contrast, control mice exhibited a robust increase in endurance capacity following training, whereas this improvement was significantly blunted in CaMKII mKO mice (Figure 3D,E). These findings demonstrate that CaMKIIγ/δ is specifically required for training-induced enhancement of endurance performance.

Analysis of myofiber composition revealed no genotype differences in the proportion of Type I or Type II fibers at baseline in either gastrocnemius or soleus muscle. In addition, no significant pre-to-post changes in Type I or Type II fiber proportions were detected within either genotype (Figure 4C,E). However, after training, CaMKII mKO muscles exhibited a higher proportion of Type I fibers and a correspondingly lower proportion of Type II fibers than control muscles in both gastrocnemius and soleus. Thus, the observed difference in fiber-type composition was detected after training and was not present before training.

Cross-sectional area increased selectively in type II fibers of the gastrocnemius in both genotypes (Figure 4D,F). After training, Type II fiber cross-sectional area in gastrocnemius and both Type I and Type II fiber cross-sectional area in soleus were lower in CaMKII mKO mice than in control mice. These findings indicate that CaMKII deficiency is associated with altered post-training myofiber characteristics. However, total muscle mass did not differ between genotypes, and the increased proportion of Type I fibers in CaMKII mKO muscle did not indicate a loss of an oxidative fiber phenotype. Thus, these histological alterations alone did not provide a clear explanation for the blunted training-induced improvement in endurance capacity. We therefore next examined whether CaMKII deficiency impaired metabolic remodeling during endurance training.

We next examined OXPHOS complex abundance in gastrocnemius muscle collected before (Pre) and after 4 weeks of endurance training (Post) (Figure 5F). Under pre-training conditions, OXPHOS complexes I and II were reduced in CaMKII mKO muscle, whereas complexes III–V were not significantly altered. In control muscle, endurance training significantly increased the abundance of OXPHOS complexes III, IV, and V. In contrast, no OXPHOS complex showed a significant change after training in CaMKII mKO muscle. Consequently, all OXPHOS complexes I–V were significantly reduced in CaMKII mKO muscle after training. Given the central role of these complexes in ATP production, these data indicate a failure of bioenergetic remodeling during endurance training in the absence of CaMKII.

To examine whether CaMKII deficiency was also associated with altered redox-related transcriptional responses, we analyzed antioxidant- and redox-related genes in the same RNA-seq dataset. The training-associated expression patterns shown in Figure 5G were derived from the Pre and Post groups. The training-associated expression pattern of Sod2, Txn2, Prdx3, Gpx3, Prdx5, Prdx1, Cat, Gclm, Prdx6, and Gsta3 was attenuated in CaMKII mKO muscle (Figure 5G). Notably, Sod2, Txn2, and Prdx3 expression was significantly lower in CaMKII mKO than in control muscle after training (Sod2, P < 0.0001; Txn2, P = 0.0013; Prdx3, P = 0.0090). These findings suggest that CaMKIIγ/δ deficiency is associated with impaired antioxidant transcriptional remodeling during endurance training.

To explore downstream effectors that may mediate CaMKII-dependent regulation of metabolic gene expression, we examined HDAC4, a class IIa histone deacetylase. Nuclear HDAC4 abundance was higher in CaMKII mKO muscle than in control muscle under sedentary conditions and was further increased after acute exercise (Figure S7A). These findings indicate that CaMKII deficiency is associated with increased nuclear HDAC4 abundance in skeletal muscle and identify HDAC4 as a candidate downstream pathway for further investigation.

To determine whether CaMKII regulates mitochondrial abundance or structural integrity in skeletal muscle, we examined mitochondrial ultrastructure by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in untrained control and CaMKII mKO gastrocnemius muscle. Discussion

This study identifies CaMKII as an important regulator of skeletal muscle adaptation to endurance exercise. Although CaMKII activation during acute exercise has been documented in humans and mammals [7,13,15], its role in endurance training adaptation has remained unclear. Here, we show that CaMKIIγ/δ contributes to the full adaptive response to endurance training. Skeletal muscle lacking CaMKIIγ/δ exhibited coordinated downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic gene programs, accompanied by reduced OXPHOS complex protein abundance, affecting a subset of complexes at baseline and all complexes I–V after training. Consistent with impaired bioenergetic capacity, CaMKII-deficient myotubes generated by acute Camk2g/d deletion showed lower absolute per-well oxygen consumption, including diminished FCCP-stimulated maximal respiration, and CaMKII mKO muscle displayed an increased proportion of ultrastructurally abnormal mitochondria. Together, these findings support a role for CaMKIIγ/δ in endurance training–induced metabolic remodeling and respiratory reserve capacity, providing a cellular basis for the blunted improvement in running performance observed in CaMKII-deficient mice.

Consistent with prior studies [7,30,41], we observed robust CaMKII phosphorylation in skeletal muscle immediately after acute treadmill exercise, whereas total CaMKII abundance remained unchanged. This indicates rapid CaMKII activation through post-translational mechanisms rather than altered protein expression. Such activation is consistent with a Ca2+-dependent mechanism in which repeated contraction-associated calcium transients promote CaMKII autophosphorylation at Thr286 or Thr287. In our model, a single 1-hour treadmill bout was sufficient to induce this response, in agreement with human studies of endurance-type exercise [7]. These observations support the notion that CaMKII is engaged early during exercise and may initiate signaling events relevant to endurance training adaptation.

Recent work has highlighted the difficulty of resolving individual CaMKII isoforms and splice variants in skeletal muscle by immunoblotting with commercial antibodies [42]. Accordingly, the total CaMKII and phospho-CaMKII immunoblots in the present study were interpreted as combined CaMKII signals rather than as isoform-specific measurements. The marked reduction of these signals in CaMKII mKO muscle, together with transcript-level validation of Camk2g and Camk2d deletion, supports loss of the combined CaMKIIγ/δ-associated signal. However, the individual contributions of CaMKIIγ, CaMKIIδ, and their splice variants cannot be distinguished by these immunoblots.

CaMKII deficiency reduced expression of genes involved in energy metabolism, including oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis. Previous studies have linked CaMK signaling to mitochondrial biogenesis through non-physiological models, such as forced CaMKIV expression [16], or through exercise-induced regulators such as Perm1 [30]. In contrast, our data show that loss of endogenous CaMKII in adult skeletal muscle is associated with reduced OXPHOS complex abundance, accumulation of structurally abnormal mitochondria, and impaired mitochondrial respiration in myotubes following acute Camk2g/d deletion. These findings indicate a close link between CaMKII function and mitochondrial metabolic competence, although the relative contributions of impaired biogenesis versus dysfunction of existing mitochondria remain unresolved.

Despite these metabolic alterations, CaMKII mKO mice displayed preserved grip strength and maximal running performance under untrained conditions, indicating that CaMKIIγ/δ is dispensable for baseline locomotor function within the tested range. This apparent dissociation may reflect the partial nature of the baseline biochemical phenotype: OXPHOS complexes I and II were reduced, whereas complexes III–V were not significantly altered. In addition, maximal running time is an integrated whole-body outcome rather than a direct measure of mitochondrial respiratory capacity in skeletal muscle. Thus, residual oxidative capacity in skeletal muscle, together with preserved function in non-skeletal muscle tissues, may be sufficient to maintain baseline exercise performance in untrained mice. Importantly, preserved baseline running performance should not be interpreted as evidence of normal muscle metabolism. This dissociation suggests that CaMKII becomes critical during endurance training-induced metabolic remodeling rather than steady-state muscle performance. Other signaling pathways may partially compensate for the loss of CaMKII under basal conditions. In contrast, CaMKII deficiency markedly impaired endurance training adaptation. To our knowledge, this study provides the first direct genetic evidence that CaMKIIγ/δ is required for training–induced enhancement of endurance capacity in skeletal muscle. The pronounced reduction of all OXPHOS complexes after training in CaMKII-deficient muscle indicates a failure of bioenergetic remodeling essential for endurance adaptation. Because mitochondrial ultrastructure was assessed only in untrained muscle, we cannot determine whether endurance training further exacerbated mitochondrial structural abnormalities or altered mitochondrial size. Nevertheless, the post-training molecular phenotype supports impaired bioenergetic remodeling as a plausible contributor to the blunted endurance adaptation.

Exercise-induced redox signaling contributes to endurance training adaptation and to the induction of endogenous antioxidant defense systems [43]. In the present study, the training-associated expression pattern of multiple antioxidant- and redox-related genes was attenuated in CaMKII mKO muscle. This pattern included genes involved in mitochondrial redox regulation, peroxide detoxification, and glutathione metabolism, such as Sod2, Txn2, Prdx3, Gpx3, Prdx5, Prdx1, Cat, Gclm, Prdx6, and Gsta3. These findings suggest that impaired antioxidant transcriptional remodeling may contribute to the defective metabolic adaptation observed in CaMKII-deficient muscle. However, because ROS production, oxidative damage, and antioxidant enzyme activity were not directly measured, the present study does not establish whether CaMKIIγ/δ deficiency reduces antioxidant capacity or increases oxidative stress during exercise.

Consistent with previous reports [44,45], endurance training increased muscle mass similarly in control and CaMKII mKO mice without altering muscle mass normalized to body weight. Myofiber-type composition was comparable between genotypes at baseline, and no significant pre-to-post changes in the proportions of Type I or Type II fibers were detected within either genotype. However, after training, CaMKII mKO muscles exhibited a higher proportion of Type I fibers and a correspondingly lower proportion of Type II fibers than control muscles in both gastrocnemius and soleus. Cross-sectional area increased selectively in Type II gastrocnemius fibers in both genotypes [[46], [47], [48]], whereas post-training cross-sectional area was lower in CaMKII mKO mice for Type II gastrocnemius fibers and for both Type I and Type II soleus fibers. Thus, CaMKII deficiency was associated with altered post-training myofiber characteristics. However, the present data do not establish whether these post-training myofiber characteristics contribute to the blunted endurance-training adaptation.

At the ultrastructural level, CaMKII-deficient skeletal muscle exhibited a higher proportion of mitochondria with abnormal morphology and a relative reduction in structurally intact mitochondria. Because cristae architecture is essential for mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production [39,40], these abnormalities strongly suggest mitochondrial dysfunction. In parallel, CaMKII-deficient myotubes showed reduced OXPHOS complex abundance and impaired maximal mitochondrial respiration, supporting an in vitro bioenergetic phenotype associated with CaMKII deficiency. However, mitochondrial content and morphology were not directly assessed in myotubes; therefore, the present data do not establish whether the ultrastructural abnormalities observed in adult skeletal muscle are reproduced in vitro. Together, these defects likely contribute to the impaired endurance training adaptation observed in CaMKII-deficient mice.

Calcium/CaMK signaling has been reported to regulate gene expression in skeletal muscle through phosphorylation of class II HDACs, including HDAC4 and HDAC5 [19,49]. CaMKII has been shown in cardiomyocytes to directly phosphorylate HDAC4, thereby promoting its nuclear export and preventing its nuclear import [50]. Whether this regulatory mechanism operates similarly in adult skeletal muscle remains unresolved. In the present study, nuclear HDAC4 abundance was increased in CaMKII mKO muscle, raising the possibility that impaired HDAC4 phosphorylation and nuclear export contribute to suppression of mitochondrial gene expression. However, nuclear HDAC4 abundance alone does not establish impaired HDAC4 phosphorylation or nuclear export, nor does it demonstrate that HDAC4 causally mediates the observed transcriptional phenotype. Additional studies assessing HDAC4 phosphorylation, nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, and functional rescue will be required to define the contribution of HDAC4 to CaMKII-dependent metabolic adaptation in skeletal muscle.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all in vivo experiments in this study were performed using male mice. Therefore, whether CaMKIIγ/δ has similar roles in endurance training–induced metabolic remodeling in female mice remains to be determined. Post-training tissues were collected 24 h after the terminal maximal exercise-capacity test. Although this standardized the recovery interval across groups, residual effects of the final test on molecular and biochemical measurements cannot be excluded. Attempts to measure respiration in permeabilized adult skeletal muscle fibers yielded unstable oxygen-flux traces after fiber contracture developed during sample preparation. These measurements were therefore not considered sufficiently reliable for quantitative analysis and were not included in the study. The intact differentiated myotube Seahorse assay was used as a complementary cell-autonomous model, rather than as a direct substitute for ex vivo respiration measurements in adult muscle fibers. All Seahorse assays were initiated with identical numbers of MuSCs per well and differentiated under identical conditions; however, post-assay normalization to cell number, protein content, DNA content, or myotube area was not performed. Thus, OCR and ECAR should be interpreted as per-well measurements obtained under matched initial seeding conditions. Nevertheless, lower absolute oxygen consumption and diminished OXPHOS complex abundance in CaMKII-deficient myotubes support reduced mitochondrial oxidative capacity in vitro. In addition, metabolic parameters during exercise were not assessed and will be important in future studies. Furthermore, endurance-training adaptation and molecular responses to exercise were not examined separately in CaMKIIγ- or CaMKIIδ-single knockout mice. Although neither single-isoform knockout line showed alterations in basal muscle phenotype or baseline exercise capacity, the relative and potentially overlapping contributions of CaMKIIγ and CaMKIIδ to training-induced metabolic remodeling remain to be determined. Finally, CaMKIIβ remains intact in this model, and its contribution to endurance training adaptation warrants further investigation.

In summary, this study demonstrates that CaMKII deficiency in skeletal muscle broadly impairs energy metabolism and prevents full adaptation to endurance exercise training. Supported by convergent in vivo, transcriptomic, biochemical, ultrastructural, and cell-autonomous metabolic evidence, our findings support a role for CaMKIIγ/δ in metabolic remodeling and the full adaptive response to endurance training in skeletal muscle.
