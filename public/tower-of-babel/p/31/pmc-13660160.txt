Safety, immunogenicity, efficacy, and effectiveness of mpox vaccines in pregnant persons, children, and adolescents: a protocol for a living systematic review and meta-analysis

Smallpox vaccination cessation has further heightened population vulnerability to MPXV. In response, MVA-BN and LC16m8, vaccinia-based vaccines originally developed for smallpox, have been repurposed for mpox, demonstrating promising safety and immunogenicity profiles. Background

Mpox has re-emerged as a global public health threat following the 2022 outbreak, with rapid international spread and evolving epidemiological patterns. First identified in humans in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), the disease has remained endemic in several African countries [1]. The global epidemiology of mpox changed dramatically with the 2022 outbreak, which marked its spread beyond endemic zones into over 100 non-endemic countries and led to recent declarations of public health emergencies by the Africa CDC and the World Health Organization. Monkeypox virus (MPXV) is classified into two recognized clades (strains): clade I, comprising subclades Ia and Ib, and clade II, comprising subclades IIa and IIb [1, 3]. These clades exhibit differences in transmissibility, pathogenicity, and case fatality rates [2]. Clade IIb MPXV emerged as the predominant strain, primarily transmitted through close contact, often during sexual interactions, disproportionately affecting men who have sex with men. By 2024, over 97,000 laboratory-confirmed cases and 186 associated deaths had been reported globally across 117 countries, with a notable resurgence in African regions driven by Clade Ib MPXV [3].

Despite its historically self-limiting clinical course, mpox can lead to severe disease in immunocompromised populations, young children, and pregnant persons. In endemic areas such as the DRC, pediatric populations have borne a disproportionate burden of morbidity and mortality. Notably, infants under 12 months of age face mortality rates four times higher than adults [4]. Compared to adults, evidence on mpox in children remains limited [5]. However, children under five and immunocompromised individuals are likely at higher risk of developing severe disease if infected [6]. The evidence on mpox during pregnancy is limited and suggests that infection may be associated with severe outcomes, including a high risk of perinatal loss (77.0%) and vertical transmission (62%) [7–9]. Vaccination represents a key strategy for mpox prevention and control.

The development of vaccines for mpox has leveraged historical insights from the smallpox eradication program. Smallpox vaccines, particularly first-generation vaccinia-based formulations, provided significant cross-protection against MPXV, with early studies estimating their effectiveness at approximately 85% [10]. These findings established the foundation for subsequent vaccine development aimed at addressing mpox.

Modern mpox vaccines have been developed using advanced platforms, including non-replicating Modified Vaccinia Ankara (MVA) vectors [11]. Two vaccines have received emergency use authorization during the global mpox outbreak: JYNNEOS (MVA-BN) and LC16m8, demonstrating promising safety and immunogenicity profiles [12]. However, the development and deployment of mpox vaccines face several challenges. These include heterogeneity in reported immunogenicity outcomes, a lack of standardized correlates of protection, and logistical hurdles in vaccine distribution, particularly in endemic regions with limited healthcare infrastructure [13, 14].

Despite these developments, available evidence on mpox vaccines remains limited and fragmented, largely derived from adult populations, and particularly scarce in pregnant persons, children, adolescents, and immunocompromised individuals [15]. To date, no clinical trials have evaluated the use of the Modified Vaccinia Ankara–Bavarian Nordic (MVA-BN) vaccine in pediatric populations [5]. Furthermore, no mpox vaccine is approved for use during pregnancy. The MVA-BN vaccine, based on a live modified non-replicating strain of vaccinia virus, poses no theoretical risk, with animal studies showing no adverse fetal effects [15, 16]. Data from fewer than 300 pregnancies indicate no increase in adverse outcomes [17]. However, its use is generally advised against during pregnancy unless the potential benefits of preventing mpox outweigh the unknown risks [18, 19]. A recent Policy Commission advised that pregnant persons should be allowed to participate in clinical trials [18]. Approximately 350 pregnant persons and 250 children aged 2 years or younger are expected to participate in a study, which will evaluate the safety and immunogenicity of the MVA-BN® vaccine in these populations for the first time [19, 20].

As mpox continues to pose a threat to global health security, particularly with the emergence of new clades such as Clade Ib, the accelerated development and equitable distribution of effective vaccines are critical [1]. Evidence spans multiple outcomes, including safety, efficacy/effectiveness, and immunogenicity, and remains heterogeneous and rapidly evolving. A living systematic review is therefore needed to generate a continuously updated and integrated evidence base across populations, with particular focus on pregnant persons, children, and adolescents, where data remain scarce and urgently needed to inform policy and clinical decision-making.

Methods

This LSR evaluates the safety, efficacy/effectiveness, and immunogenicity of mpox vaccines across populations, with particular focus on pregnant persons, children, and adolescents. It will follow Cochrane and World Health Organization (WHO) methods [21–23] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement [24–26]. Different study designs will contribute according to the outcome assessed and the available evidence. Randomized trials and observational comparative studies will contribute to the assessment of efficacy/effectiveness and safety, while non-comparative studies, including case reports, will be particularly relevant for identifying rare or unexpected adverse events. Immunogenicity outcomes will be informed by studies reporting humoral or cellular immune responses. Results will be synthesized separately by outcome and study design. Two protocols were registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database following the PRISMA-P statement [27]: one for adults and pregnant persons (CRD42024554330) and the other for children and adolescents (CRD42024556977). The PRISMA-P checklist is included as supplementary material.

Search strategy

Literature searches will be conducted in major bibliographic databases (e.g., MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, LILACS), complemented by trial registries and grey literature sources. A comprehensive search of these databases will be conducted from inception to the present without any language restrictions. Biweekly searches will then be run to ensure the inclusion of the latest relevant reports. Reference lists of relevant studies will also be screened. Ongoing trials will be identified through trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP).

Regulatory sources (e.g., FDA, EMA, CDC) will be reviewed to capture relevant safety signals and early evidence.

Eligibility criteria

Study designs

This review will include randomized clinical trials (RCT), quasi-experimental, and observational (comparative and non-comparative) study designs, regardless of publication status, year, and language. Eligible designs include phase I–IV trials, cohort, case–control, cross-sectional studies, interrupted time series, before–after studies, registries, and case series. Case reports will only be considered if reporting previously unknown or unexpected adverse events. Lastly, we will include pre-clinical studies of vaccines in the development pipeline for mpox.

Participants

Participants will include newborns, infants, children, adolescents, pregnant persons, and their fetuses, and adults, irrespective of prior exposure to mpox, comorbidities, immune status, and risk group. Regarding the sample size, we will include observational studies reporting safety and efficacy or effectiveness outcomes with sample sizes of at least 50 subjects and immunogenicity studies with outcome data for at least ten subjects in one or more groups. Case reports of infrequent adverse events will be included regardless of sample size. Animal studies will be included only for new mpox vaccines in the development pipeline.

Outcomes will be analyzed separately by population group (adults, pregnant persons, children, and adolescents).

Interventions

This living systematic review will consider mpox vaccine candidates, licensed mpox vaccines, and vaccinia-based vaccines used for mpox prevention. As some vaccinia-based vaccines are being rebranded for mpox, we will include studies where vaccinia-based vaccines have been repurposed for mpox vaccination, focusing on safety, efficacy, effectiveness, and immunogenicity outcomes. Additionally, the inclusion of different vaccine platforms reflects the current landscape of mpox vaccination and allows for comparative and subgroup analyses across vaccine types.

For studies involving vaccinia-based vaccines used for smallpox prevention, we will specifically extract safety outcomes. However, for immunogenicity outcomes, only mpox-specific immune responses will be considered, as we aim to assess the vaccine’s direct impact on mpox immunity.

Additionally, immunogenicity outcomes related to vaccinia-based vaccines will be summarized narratively to provide contextual information and will not be incorporated into mpox-specific immunogenicity analyses. Studies assessing the duration of immunity following historical vaccinia-based vaccination will be excluded.

Comparators

Any control group will be considered, whether it involves standard care, no intervention, a different mpox vaccine, or any other “active” comparator, irrespective of co-interventions. Additionally, non-comparative studies will be included. The presence of a control group will not be obligatory.

Measures of effect

Odds ratios (ORs), risk ratios (RRs), and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) will be extracted for dichotomous outcomes, and Mean Difference (MD) or Standardized MD (SMD) will be extracted for continuous outcomes. We will report Vaccine efficacy/effectiveness (VE) for relevant clinical trials (of efficacy) and post-implementation observational studies (of effectiveness). We will also calculate proportions with 95% CIs for non-comparative studies.

Outcomes will be assessed across all populations included in the review. Safety, efficacy/effectiveness, and immunogenicity will be evaluated with analyses conducted according to study design and data availability. Data extraction and management

Selection

Pairs of reviewers will independently screen each title and abstract. We will obtain the full texts for any studies or reports deemed potentially relevant. Working in pairs, reviewers will then independently assess these full texts and document reasons for excluding studies that do not meet the criteria. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with the review team. This process will be conducted using the web-based platform Nested Knowledge (https://nested-knowledge.com/).

Data collection

Data for the study will be collected and securely stored using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted on data servers at the Institute for Clinical Effectiveness and Health Policy in Buenos Aires, Argentina. Before formal data extraction begins, we will pilot the process on a sample of at least ten studies. Data will be independently extracted by pairs of review authors using a REDCap form, with any disagreements resolved through team discussion. If necessary, we will contact study authors to clarify any insufficiently reported data. Information on funding sources will also be gathered for each study included in the living systematic review (LSR).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias will be assessed using design-specific tools. For randomized controlled trials, we will apply the Cochrane risk of bias tool—version 2 (RoB2), covering five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, selective reporting, and an overall risk of bias evaluation [31]. For non-randomized intervention studies, the ROBINS-I tool will be utilized [32]. For controlled before-after studies, we will examine baseline measurements, characteristics of studies using a secondary site as a control, blinded assessment of primary outcomes, reliability of primary outcome measures, and follow-up of both professionals and patients to guard against exclusion bias. In uncontrolled before-after studies, the same criteria as controlled before-after studies will be applied, except for baseline measurement and characteristics for studies using a second site as control. For interrupted time series studies, we will evaluate the risk of bias across seven domains: independence of the intervention from other changes, pre-specified shape of the intervention effect, likelihood that the intervention did not influence data collection, blinding of outcome assessors to intervention allocation, completeness of outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and any other bias sources. For controlled interrupted time series studies, we will add three additional domains specific to design-related validity threats: imbalance of baseline outcome measures, baseline comparability of intervention and control group characteristics, and protection against contamination. For non-comparative studies, the NIH Quality Assessment Tool will be applied [33]. After answering the different signaling questions, yes, no, cannot be determined, not applicable, or not reported, the raters will classify the study quality as good, fair, or poor. We will use the classifications low, high, or unclear risk of bias for consistency with the other designs. In the Summary of findings tables, we will summarize the GRADE certainty of evidence from comparative studies. The estimates will be downgraded for serious and very serious imprecision if 95% CIs crossed the null effect and the limits were < 0.95 and/or > 1.05, and < 0.5 and/or > 1.50, respectively, and for serious and very serious inconsistency if I2 values were > 60% and > 75%, respectively.

Data synthesis plan

If suitable data are available, we will conduct meta-analyses for each comparison following the guidelines in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Analyses will be conducted separately by outcome (safety, efficacy/effectiveness, and immunogenicity) and according to study design. We will use a random-effects meta-analysis for the primary analysis. We will also carry out proportion meta-analyses to summarize frequencies from single-sample studies. Data analysis will be performed in R statistical software [34], primarily using the Meta, Metafor, and Tidyverse packages [35]. We will calculate pooled hazard ratios, risk ratios, or odds ratios with a 95% confidence interval (CI) for dichotomous outcomes and mean differences or standardized mean differences for continuous outcomes. For non-comparative studies, we will calculate proportions with a 95% CI. To report efficacy or effectiveness outcomes, we will convert other outcome measures to vaccine efficacy/effectiveness, when possible, by assessing the risk of disease in vaccinated versus unvaccinated individuals and calculating the percentage risk reduction. Adjusted effect measures will be prioritized over unadjusted estimates. Heterogeneity will be assessed and explored through subgroup analyses. Narrative synthesis will be performed when meta-analysis is not feasible. Both pre-licensure and post-licensure evidence will be included to provide a comprehensive assessment of vaccine performance. Case reports and small case series will be included in the narrative synthesis and excluded from quantitative meta-analyses. Analyses will be stratified by population (children, adolescents, adults, and pregnant persons).

Sensitivity analysis

Additional sensitivity analyses will be undertaken by excluding high-risk bias studies.

Data visualization

We will utilize an online interactive dashboard for data visualization, employing Microsoft Power BI. The most relevant variables related to pregnant persons and safety and effectiveness outcomes for infants and children will be selected and displayed in figures and tables. Since this project is a living systematic review, the living meta-analysis section will be accessible to users as an interactive tool developed as a Shiny application in R [36]. The application will enable users to display meta-analyses of interest by applying filters such as age, region, vaccine platform, vaccine doses, and comparators. Additionally, predefined subgroup analyses will be available for users to choose from. The research team will develop an algorithm to determine the endpoints for each study in the living meta-analysis. The researchers will conduct a validation process to ensure the accuracy of the endpoint selection algorithm.

Discussion

This living systematic review will synthesize available evidence on the safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of mpox vaccines in pregnant persons, children, and adolescents. These populations remain underrepresented in clinical trials despite being at increased risk of severe disease. Given the expansion of mpox outbreaks beyond endemic regions and the limited vaccine data available in these groups, a timely evaluation of the evidence is necessary. The design includes biweekly updates and subgroup analyses to account for potential heterogeneity across study populations, vaccine platforms, and clinical outcomes. Findings from this review are expected to support public health decision-making and guide future recommendations for vaccination in maternal and pediatric populations.

This review has potential limitations, including heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and populations, as well as the predominance of evidence derived from adult populations. Additionally, we anticipate including historical evidence from the pre- and peri-smallpox eradication era, which may be incompletely reported. However, the living systematic review approach allows continuous incorporation of emerging data, which is particularly relevant in the context of evolving outbreaks and limited evidence in key populations. This approach is expected to strengthen the evidence base for decision-making and support the development of targeted vaccination strategies in maternal and pediatric populations.
