Different results with early single-stage and staged repair in infants with ileal atresia: a two-case report on the effect of timing and treatment approach
Authors: Layth J.M. Saada, Jamil Saada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Ileal atresia represents a major cause of newborn intestinal obstruction, requiring prompt surgical correction. Although a preferred procedure is the primary anastomosis in stable patients, the timing and surgical procedure can play a pivotal role in affecting morbidity, nutritional state, and the course of hospitalization. This article highlights the importance of these factors.Case presentation:We describe two neonates diagnosed with isolated ileal atresia and treated using two different techniques. In case 1, early primary anastomosis was performed on day 5 of life. This resulted in an uncomplicated postoperative period, along with the early introduction of oral feeding and a relatively short duration of hospitalization. On the other hand, case 2 underwent early stoma creation at birth, which was followed by poor weight gain, repeated infections, electrolyte disturbances, and severe peristomal skin excoriation caused by a high-output stoma. At nearly 3 months of age, the patient subsequently underwent delayed definitive repair with a primary end-to-end ileo–right colic anastomosis, after which catch-up growth was achieved, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.Clinical discussion:These cases demonstrate how surgical timing and approach may influence recovery and growth. Early single-stage repair, when feasible, is associated with shorter hospitalization and lower stoma-related morbidity. In contrast, staged management is accompanied by stoma-related complications and delayed nutritional recovery.Conclusion:Early single-stage repair should be prioritized in stable neonates with ileal atresia. When enterostomy is unavoidable, early stoma closure is advised to prevent malnutrition and caregiver strain.

Introduction

Ileal atresia is a well-established cause of neonatal intestinal obstruction, frequently requiring early surgical intervention[1,2]. Advances in neonatal surgery and perioperative care have led to a growing preference for single-stage primary anastomosis in stable neonates, given its association with earlier restoration of bowel continuity, reduced stoma-related morbidity, and shorter hospital stay[2,3].

However, the optimal surgical approach remains controversial[4], with significant variability in practice influenced by intraoperative findings, surgeon preference, and institutional protocols[5,6]. The anatomical classification of jejunoileal atresia may also influence operative strategy. While primary repair is generally advocated when feasible, staged repair with temporary enterostomy remains appropriate in the presence of bowel edema, intra-abdominal contamination, or instability[5]. In such cases, early stoma closure, typically within 6–8 weeks, is increasingly recommended, even in low-weight infants, to reduce stoma-related morbidity[7,8].HIGHLIGHTSEarly diagnosis and referral enable stabilization and increase the likelihood of a successful single-stage repair.Single-stage primary anastomosis, when feasible, minimizes stoma-related complications, facilitates earlier enteral feeding, and reduces overall hospital stay.Delayed definitive repair increases the risks of malnutrition and infection, ultimately leading to prolonged hospitalization, higher healthcare costs, and greater resource utilization.Early stoma closure, as soon as safely possible, promotes timely catch-up growth and improves long-term outcomes.

This report presents two infants with isolated ileal atresia who were managed using distinct approaches to highlight the clinical, nutritional, and economic implications of surgical timing and strategy. This report follows the SCARE 2025 criteria[9].

Methods

We retrospectively describe two infants with isolated ileal atresia treated at our institution during the same admission period in July 2025. Case 1 was referred during the early neonatal period and underwent primary ileo–ileal anastomosis, whereas case 2 was transferred after initial stoma formation and a complicated postoperative course, subsequently undergoing delayed definitive repair with end-to-end ileo–right colic anastomosis. Clinical data, including presentation, operative findings, and postoperative course, were obtained from patient records.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 4-day-old full-term male neonate was referred to our center due to progressive abdominal distension, poor feeding, and failure to pass meconium since birth. He was delivered at a local hospital via spontaneous vaginal delivery at 37 weeks of gestation to a healthy 31-year-old G5P5A0 mother. The pregnancy was poorly monitored, with no documented antenatal ultrasound screening. Aside from maternal hypothyroidism managed with oral levothyroxine and a urinary tract infection in the third trimester treated with oral antibiotics, the pregnancy was otherwise uneventful.

At birth, the infant was found to be vigorous and weighed 3300 grams but was discharged against medical advice at 7 hours of life. On the second day of life, the family sought medical attention at a local hospital due to abdominal distension, poor feeding, and absence of bowel movements. He was admitted with a clinical diagnosis of neonatal sepsis, and a full septic workup was done, including blood cultures and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis. The CSF showed pleocytosis (20 WBC/mm3), elevated protein (97 mg/dL), and low glucose (46 mg/dL), raising suspicion for meningitis. Empirical antibiotic treatment was started; however, both blood and CSF cultures returned sterile.

Given progressive abdominal distension and persistent failure to pass meconium, a lower gastrointestinal (GIT) contrast study was performed, showing an unused microcolon without radiological features suggestive of Hirschsprung’s disease (Fig. 1.A). An upper GIT contrast study showed no evidence of midgut volvulus or malrotation but revealed signs of distal small bowel obstruction (Fig. 1.B). Based on these findings, the patient was referred to our center on day 4 of life for surgical evaluation and further management. Figure 1.A. Lower GIT study revealed an unused microcolon with no features of Hirschsprung disease. B. Upper GIT study indicated distal small bowel obstruction without volvulus or malrotation.

A. Lower GIT study revealed an unused microcolon with no features of Hirschsprung disease. B. Upper GIT study indicated distal small bowel obstruction without volvulus or malrotation.

On admission to our hospital, the patient was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Physical examination found the infant hypoactive, dehydrated, and jaundiced, with marked abdominal distension. Capillary refill time was below 2 seconds. Vital signs were within acceptable limits. His weight at admission was 2800 grams. Abdominal examination showed generalized distension and tenderness with no palpable mass, and digital rectal examination was normal. Initial laboratory investigations revealed hypernatremic dehydration (serum sodium 155 mmol/L), elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN 38 mg/dL), creatinine 0.48 mg/dL, and total serum bilirubin of 25 mg/dL. Sepsis markers were negative (C-reactive protein 2.1 mg/L), and repeated blood and urine cultures were also negative. Coagulation studies showed a mildly prolonged prothrombin time (16.5 seconds). Liver function tests were otherwise normal. Abdominal radiographs suggested distal small bowel obstruction, with significantly dilated small bowel loops (Fig. 2). Abdominal and transfontanelle ultrasounds did not show any remarkable findings. Abdominal radiographs (anteroposterior and lateral views) suggested distal small bowel obstruction, with significantly dilated small bowel loops.

Initial management focused on stabilization. The patient was kept nil per os (NPO) with nasogastric decompression and was started on intravenous antibiotics and fluid resuscitation to correct dehydration and electrolyte imbalances. Hypernatremia gradually improved to 145 mmol/L, and renal function normalized, with BUN decreasing to 28 mg/dL. Phototherapy was started for neonatal jaundice, resulting in a gradual reduction of total serum bilirubin to 17.7 mg/dL prior to surgery.

Following stabilization, an exploratory laparotomy was performed on hospital day 2 (day 5 of life). Intraoperative findings showed type IIIb distal ileal atresia (Fig. 3). Multiple dilated proximal small bowel loops terminated in a blind-ending, dilated distal ileal segment measuring approximately 15 cm. The distal 20 cm of ileum was narrow and collapsed, accompanied by a microcolon. Resection of the dilated ileal segment was performed, followed by a primary ileo-ileal anastomosis. Due to a serosal injury sustained during manipulation, a second anastomosis was required. The abdomen was closed after ensuring distal bowel patency. Figure 3.Intraoperative image showing type IIIb ileal atresia with multiple dilated proximal loops, a blind-ending and dilated distal ileum segment (~15 cm), a collapsed distal segment (~20 cm), and an associated microcolon.

Intraoperative image showing type IIIb ileal atresia with multiple dilated proximal loops, a blind-ending and dilated distal ileum segment (~15 cm), a collapsed distal segment (~20 cm), and an associated microcolon.

Postoperatively, the patient remained NPO with nasogastric drainage and continued on intravenous fluids and antibiotics. Electrolyte levels and renal function remained stable. The last recorded total bilirubin level was 5.8 mg/dL. The patient passed bowel movements on postoperative day 3. Enteral feeding was gradually initiated on day 4 and successfully advanced to full oral feeds.

During hospitalization, echocardiography revealed a small secundum atrial septal defect II and mild peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis. A postoperative day 10 abdominal radiograph demonstrated acceptable bowel gas distribution (Fig. 4). By this time, the infant was clinically stable, tolerating full enteral feeds, passing stools, and exhibiting appropriate weight gain. He was discharged home on day 15 of life (postoperative day 10, hospital day 11) with a weight of 2900 grams. A follow-up was scheduled with pediatric surgery, neonatology, and pediatric cardiology clinics. Figure 4.Postoperative radiograph showing acceptable bowel gas pattern.

Postoperative radiograph showing acceptable bowel gas pattern.

At follow-up, within 1 week of discharge, the infant remained clinically stable and had gained 200 grams over the preceding 6 days.

Case 2

This 2-month-and-21-day-old full-term male baby was referred to our center for definitive surgical management of ileal atresia after a complicated postoperative course related to stoma formation and repeated hospitalizations at local facilities, as documented in his medical records.

He was born at 37 weeks of gestation via spontaneous vaginal delivery to healthy, non-consanguineous parents (G1P1A0 mother), with a birth weight of 2950 grams. Antenatal ultrasound suggested the presence of intestinal atresia. Soon after his birth, he exhibited signs of neonatal intestinal obstruction. A lower GIT contrast study was performed, and abdominal radiography revealed findings consistent with jejunal or ileal atresia.

An exploratory laparotomy was performed on day 1 of life. A distal ileal segment, approximately 15 cm in length as reported, was resected, and both a proximal ileostomy and a right colostomy were performed. He was managed in the NICU for approximately 10 days. His early postoperative course was marked by several instances of hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypoalbuminemia, and wound infection (Fig. 5). He was administered intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and albumin twice. Enteral feeding was gradually started from postoperative day 3, and he was discharged on day 10 of life. Figure 5.Postoperative wound infection involving both stoma sites: medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy.

Postoperative wound infection involving both stoma sites: medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy.

Within the following weeks, the infant developed progressive failure to thrive (FTT), poor weight gain (with a weight of 2100 grams at 1 month of age), and clinical signs of malabsorption with a high-output ileostomy producing yellowish-white effluent. He required two readmissions, staying for about 2–3 days in each instance. With progressive nutritional decline, he was then transferred to a more specialized institution for thorough evaluation and treatment. Upon arrival there, he was described as febrile, malnourished, dehydrated, and irritable, with severe peristomal dermatitis surrounding the ileostomy site (Fig. 6). His weight at admission was 2400 grams. Immunizations were up to date as of 1 month of age. Laboratory investigations showed significant electrolyte disturbances, including hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, low serum bicarbonate, metabolic acidosis, and elevated lactate levels. His blood cultures yielded Staphylococcus epidermidis. Furthermore, he had a positive result for rotavirus and adenovirus on stool antigen assays. In addition, he had anemia (Hgb 7.3 g/dL) requiring packed red blood cell transfusion and transient thrombocytosis. Figure 6.Severe peristomal skin excoriation surrounding medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy, which is related to the enzymatically active effluent.

Severe peristomal skin excoriation surrounding medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy, which is related to the enzymatically active effluent.

Management at that center included keeping the patient NPO and starting intravenous fluids tailored to correct his electrolyte disturbances. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was commenced, and a 7-day course of intravenous vancomycin was administered for the bloodstream infection. Electrolyte corrections, particularly for magnesium and phosphate, were performed as needed. Peristomal dermatitis was managed with multiple topical applications. He also received intravenous fluconazole for prophylaxis, along with multivitamins and trace elements.

During hospitalization, an upper GIT contrast study showed a grade III gastroesophageal reflux but was otherwise unremarkable. Abdominal and transfontanelle ultrasounds showed no abnormalities. Due to persistently high-output ileostomy and poor enteral feeding tolerance, the patient remained dependent on TPN throughout the 2-week admission. Once stabilized, he was referred to our pediatric surgery department for definitive surgical management.

On admission to our hospital, the patient was stable, active, and well-appearing, with a weight of 3250 grams. Vital signs were within normal limits. Physical examination showed the presence of both an ileostomy and a colostomy on the right side of the abdomen, accompanied by mild to moderate peristomal dermatitis (Fig. 7); otherwise, it was unremarkable. Genital examination revealed a left hydrocele. Development was appropriate for age. Figure 7.Right-sided double-barrel stomas with surrounding mild to moderate peristomal dermatitis.

Right-sided double-barrel stomas with surrounding mild to moderate peristomal dermatitis.

Initial laboratory investigations, among others, included a complete blood count, serum electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, arterial blood gases, C-reactive protein, and renal function tests, all of which were within acceptable limits. Blood, urine, and wound cultures were obtained. A Telebrix contrast enema demonstrated a normal rectum and colon up to the level of the right-sided stoma (right colon) (Fig. 8). The patient was kept NPO, maintained on intravenous fluids, and prepared for surgical intervention. Figure 8.Contrast enema (Telebrix) demonstrating a normal-caliber rectum and colon up to the right-sided colostomy.

Contrast enema (Telebrix) demonstrating a normal-caliber rectum and colon up to the right-sided colostomy.

An exploratory laparotomy was performed the following day. Intraoperative findings revealed approximately 120 cm of remaining small bowel, including the jejunum and proximal ileum, with the absence of the cecum and appendix (Fig. 9). Adhesiolysis was performed. Both the medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy were reversed, and a primary end-to-end ileo–right colic anastomosis was subsequently fashioned. The previously constructed medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy are shown within the black rectangle.

Intraoperative photograph demonstrating preserved jejunum and proximal ileum (approximately 120 cm), with absence of the cecum and appendix. The previously constructed medial ileostomy and lateral colostomy are shown within the black rectangle.

The patient continued to be kept on NPO with nasogastric decompression until day 3 after surgery, at which time he passed stools. Enteral feeding was started gradually at this point, but this was complicated by an episode of green vomitus, making it necessary to insert the nasogastric tube again and return to a temporary NPO status. On day 4 after surgery, his vomiting resolved, after which oral feeding was safely reintroduced using Neocate® formula.

Throughout the hospital stay, the patient remained afebrile and hemodynamically stable, with no clinical or laboratory evidence of sepsis. All previously obtained cultures returned negative. Laboratory monitoring showed mild anemia, transient thrombocytosis, and hypokalemia, all of which were appropriately corrected. The rest of the postoperative course was uneventful, and follow-up abdominal radiographs showed acceptable findings. On postoperative day 7 (hospital day 8), the patient was discharged in stable condition, tolerating feeds and passing regular bowel movements.

At outpatient follow-up 1 week later, he remained clinically well and had gained 200 grams (3450 grams). His wound healing was good, with the resolution of peristomal skin changes (Fig. 10), though he suffered from diarrhea complicated by perineal excoriation. After 6 weeks, there was further weight gain up to 5 kg, and his scalded perineum had healed with satisfactory abdominal wound healing. Discussion

Surgical approach and timing

Existing literature supports primary single-stage repair, when possible, because it is associated with reduced hospital stays and prevents stoma complication risks. However, staged repairs are still indicated in cases of neonates with hemodynamic instability or significant intra-abdominal contamination to prevent potential anastomotic complications[5]. In case 2, the decision for initial stoma creation may have been influenced by intraoperative findings, patient condition, or institutional considerations that were not available in the referral documentation. Nevertheless, the prolonged duration before definitive repair likely contributed to stoma-related morbidity[7]. Several studies have reported that there is no higher risk for postoperative complications when early closure is done within the first 6 weeks, even with low-birth-weight babies, challenging the traditional wait-for-weight approach. Therefore, after stabilizing the patient, definitive surgery should be considered[7,8,10]. While concerns about adhesions remain, evidence shows no difference between early and delayed closures, even among necrotizing enterocolitis cases[11]. The presence of a stoma may delay nutrition rehabilitation and increase weight loss, as shown in case 2. In combination with stoma-related problems, this supports early closure as soon as safely possible to prevent further clinical deterioration.

Nutritional outcomes and failure to thrive

The relatively longer interval from the time of the initial surgery to definitive repair in case 2 is linked to significant malnutrition. The weight dropped from 2950 g at birth to 2100 g after 1 month, meaning that there was a loss of almost 29% of the weight. There was severe malnutrition and failure to thrive. While the weight improved subsequently, it was still low, being 2400 g, and despite approaching the age of 3 months, he weighed only 3250 g, which corresponds to the normal weight of a 1-month-old infant.

Several reasons may have contributed to the development of FTT in case 2, including a persistent high-output ileostomy, ongoing fluid and electrolyte imbalances, recurrent diarrhea, and prolonged TPN dependence due to poor enteral tolerance[12–14]. Importantly, the distal ileum and the ileocecal valve were resected in this patient, which may have exacerbated malabsorption and contributed to excessive stoma output, necessitating long-term monitoring for vitamin B12 deficiency during growth. Together, these factors probably contributed to delays in gut adaptation and catch-up growth[15], features frequently observed in stoma-associated intestinal insufficiency, which substantially delay the achievement of enteral autonomy and complicate TPN weaning. Severe malnutrition in neonates is strongly associated with long-term consequences, including increased perioperative risks, delayed recovery, and potential neurodevelopmental impairments[16]. However, case 1 showed consistent weight gain and full tolerance of enteral feeds soon after the operation, indicating metabolic and nutritional benefits from early single-stage correction.

Encouragingly, emerging data suggest that timely stoma closure may improve the growth of such babies. Notably, infants with proximal ileostomies or extensive small bowel resections, historically linked to impaired growth, have been found to exhibit similar catch-up growth 1 year after their stoma is reversed compared to babies with distal stomas or limited resections, as reported by Schattenkerk et al[7].

Stoma-related morbidity, infectious complications, and healthcare burden

Case 2 developed severe peristomal dermatitis from continuous ileostomy effluent leakage, resulting in significant skin breakdown, dehydration, and metabolic strain, requiring intensive topical and systemic management[17]. This was further complicated by a Staphylococcus epidermidis infection and concurrent rotavirus and adenovirus enteritis, necessitating prolonged IV antibiotics and TPN while delaying enteral feeding. The patient’s compromised nutritional state likely predisposed him to these infections, which, in turn, worsened his metabolic condition[15]. Consequently, total hospitalization reached approximately 38 days, nearly three times that of case 1 (13 days), who experienced none of these complications. Case 1 benefited from early surgery, minimal line exposure, and rapid nutritional rehabilitation, avoiding stoma-related morbidity entirely[6,8].

These findings highlight the broader healthcare burden and caregiver stress associated with staged repair: prolonged NICU stay, dependence on TPN, problems with the stoma, recurrent infections, and increased nutritional support[5,18,19]. Furthermore, both cases have shown system issues that can affect neonatal outcomes. In case 1, the absence of antenatal care delayed recognition of intestinal obstruction, whereas in case 2, limited early postoperative follow-up preceded the development of further complications.

Conclusion

These two cases showed differing postoperative courses following distinct surgical approaches for ileal atresia. Early referral and primary repair were observed alongside shorter hospitalization, the absence of stoma-related morbidity, and more favorable nutritional progression. These observations are in line with previous reports supporting single-stage anastomosis when clinically feasible.
