A Cross-sectional study on the link between mediterranean diet adherence and overactive bladder in American adults
Authors: Li Wang, Shan Yin, Jian-wei Yang, Kang-yu Wang, Kun-peng Li, Shun Wan, Si-yu Chen, Li Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOveractive bladder (OAB) is a prevalent condition that negatively impacts quality of life. This study evaluated the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet, measured by the alternative Mediterranean diet (aMED) score, and OAB among U.S. adults under 65 years of age.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2005 to 2018. This analysis evaluated the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet, assessed by the alternative Mediterranean diet (aMED) score—a 0–9 scale based on intake of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, red/processed meats, alcohol, and the ratio of monounsaturated to saturated fat—and the prevalence of overactive bladder (OAB) in U.S. adults under 65 years of age. The aMED score was categorized into quartiles, and its association with OAB was examined using weighted logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models, and stratified analyses, with adjustments for relevant confounders.ResultsAmong 23,104 participants, higher aMED scores were associated with lower odds of OAB (odds ratio [OR] = 0.996, 95% CI 0.993–0.999, p = 0.007). In the fully adjusted model-accounting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, poverty-to-income ratio, education level, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, energy intake, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and cancer-individuals in the highest aMED quartile (Q4) had significantly lower odds of OAB compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (OR = 0.832, 95% CI 0.707–0.979, p = 0.028). The restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis indicated a nonlinear association between the aMED score and OAB, with poverty level potentially modifying this relationship.ConclusionGreater adherence to the Mediterranean diet (MD) correlates with a reduced prevalence of OAB among U.S. adults aged 20–65. this study provides cross-sectional evidence supporting dietary interventions for OAB prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01100-0.Keywords: Overactive bladder, Dietary, NHANES, Alternate mediterranean diet score

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a chronic urological disorder that disrupts the daily lives of millions, characterized by frequent urination, urgency, and nocturia [1]. The condition is especially common in women, with a significant portion of the U.S. adult population affected [2]. Although OAB is associated with aging, its exact cause is multifactorial, involving inflammatory processes, changes in bladder function, and metabolic imbalances [3]. Traditional treatments, such as pharmacotherapy, often result in limited effectiveness and undesirable side effects, underscoring the need for alternative approaches, including dietary modification [4].

Growing evidence suggests that diet plays a critical role in modulating inflammation and metabolic function, both of which are implicated in OAB pathophysiology [5]. The Mediterranean diet (MD), characterized by high consumption of plant-based foods and healthy fats, and low intake of red meat, is acknowledged for its anti-inflammatory effects and its potential to lower the risk of various chronic diseases [6]. The traditional Mediterranean diet score (MDS) was originally developed based on dietary habits in Mediterranean countries, with scoring cutoffs reflecting region-specific consumption patterns. However, these criteria may not be directly applicable to non-Mediterranean populations, such as those in the United States, where dietary practices differ substantially. To address this limitation, the alternative Mediterranean diet (aMED) score was developed as an adaptation of the MDS, using cutoffs based on median intakes within the U.S. population. The aMED score has since been widely applied in American cohorts, including NHANES, and has demonstrated strong validity in predicting diet-related health outcomes [7]. Given our use of U.S. nationally representative data, the aMED score provides a more appropriate and culturally relevant measure of adherence to Mediterranean diet principles than the traditional MDS. While studies have shown the MD’s impact on cardiovascular and metabolic health, its influence on urological conditions, particularly OAB, is less studied. Diet may significantly influence the management of OAB symptoms due to the involvement of inflammation and metabolism in its development [8].

This research examines the link between MD adherence and OAB in adults aged 20–65 using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data. This age group is critical as lifestyle factors like diet have significant impact during this period, and it allows for analysis without the confounding effects of age-related comorbidities common in older populations [9]. Understanding this relationship could provide valuable insights into non-pharmacological interventions for OAB management.

Methods

This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available NHANES data, which are collected by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) using a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to assess the nutritional and health status of U.S. children and adults [10]. NHANES data are obtained through standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory measurements conducted by trained personnel. All protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Because the data are de-identified and publicly accessible, no additional ethical approval was required. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

A cross-sectional study utilized data from the NHANES spanning 2005 to 2018, including a total of 70,190 participants. The following groups were excluded from the study: pregnant individuals (n = 757), participants younger than 20 years or older than 65 years (n = 39,488), participants with missing data on overactive bladder (OAB) or aMED score (n = 4,868), and participants with missing or implausible dietary intake data on Day 1 (n = 1,973). Dietary intake deemed implausible was categorized as less than 500 kcal or more than 3,500 kcal for women, and less than 800 kcal or more than 4,200 kcal for men [11], Consequently, the final analysis included 23,104 participants (Fig. 1).

Assessment of aMED and OAB

Dietary intake data in NHANES were collected using the standardized 24-hour dietary recall method. The first recall was conducted in person at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) by trained dietary interviewers, and the second recall was performed by telephone 3–10 days later. The Automated Multiple-Pass Method (AMPM), developed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, was applied to minimize recall bias. This structured interview technique guides participants through multiple passes, including time-of-day queries, food category cues, and reviews of potentially forgotten items, thereby improving completeness and accuracy. To enhance portion size estimation, respondents were provided with food models, measuring guides, and visual aids. All interviewers underwent rigorous training and certification, and continuous quality-control procedures were implemented jointly by the CDC and USDA. For the present study, Day 1 dietary recall data were used in the main analyses, while the average of two days’ recalls was used in sensitivity analyses to further reduce potential recall error [12].

The MD adherence was evaluated by converting 24-hour dietary recall data into equivalent units according to USDA food patterns [13]. Then, following the method described by Gaskins et al. [14], We modified the aMED score for use with 24-hour dietary recall data [7]. The aMED score comprises nine elements: vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fruits, red and processed meats, fish, alcohol, and the monounsaturated to saturated fat ratio. The aMED score, ranging from 0 to 9, reflects the level of adherence to the MD, with higher scores denoting greater adherence (Table S1). the primary independent variable was the quartiles of the aMED score.

OAB is characterized by a constant urge to urinate, frequently associated with urinary urgency incontinence (UUI) and nocturia. Participants were queried about UUI by asking, “In the past year, have you experienced any urine leakage or loss of control due to an urgent need to urinate, preventing you from reaching the toilet in time?” The severity of UUI was further evaluated by asking participants, “How often does this happen?” Nocturia was evaluated by inquiring, “Over the past 30 days, how often per night did you usually wake up to urinate between going to bed and waking up in the morning?” The Overactive Bladder Symptom Score (OABSS) was used to assess the overall severity of OAB, as shown in Figure S1. Individuals with a total OABSS of 3 or above were classified as having OAB [15].

Covariates

This study adjusted for a range of demographic, lifestyle, and health-related variables, consistent with prior NHANES-based analyses. Demographic factors included age (modeled as both continuous and categorical: 20–34, 35–49, and 50–65 years), sex (male or female), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Hispanic, and Other Race), marital status (married, living with partner, divorced, separated, widowed, or never married), education level (less than 9th grade, 9–11th grade, high school graduate, some college, or college graduate and above), and the poverty-to-income ratio (PIR, modeled as continuous and categorized as ≤ 1.3, 1.3–3.5, > 3.5, or missing). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never, former, or current), alcohol consumption (never, former, or current; with missing values coded separately), and total energy intake (kcal/day, continuous). Health-related covariates included body mass index (BMI, continuous and categorized as < 25, 25–29.9, and ≥ 30 kg/m²) as well as the presence of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and cancer, each classified as yes or no based on self-reported physician diagnosis or examination data. For covariates with more than 2% missing values, indicator (dummy) variables were created to retain participants in the analytic models.

Statistical analyses

Following NHANES guidelines (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm), we merged seven survey cycles utilizing complex sampling weights (MEC examination weight). the baseline characteristics were displayed in tables, with continuous variables shown as weighted means with standard errors (SE), and categorical variables as weighted counts and proportions. We examined the association between aMED score, treated as both a continuous and categorical variable (quartiles), and OAB using multinomial logistic regression across three models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race), and Model 3 (adjusted for age, sex, race, marital status, PIR, education level, smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, BMI, hypertension, CVD, energy intake, and cancer).Additionally, trend tests were conducted accordingly. We utilized restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression and piecewise linear regression to analyze the dose-response relationship between aMED and OAB prevalence, incorporating the average of two-day dietary recalls for sensitivity analysis. Finally, interaction tests were utilized to assess heterogeneity between subgroups. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3, considering a p-value below 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants by OAB status

This study included 23,104 participants from seven NHANES cycles, who were grouped according to their OAB status. Compared with individuals without OAB, participants with OAB were older, more likely to be female, had a higher prevalence of Non-Hispanic Black ethnicity, a lower poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), and a higher BMI. They also showed a greater proportion of being widowed or divorced, lower levels of educational attainment, lower alcohol consumption, and higher smoking prevalence. Furthermore, OAB participants demonstrated a higher prevalence of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. In dietary assessment, participants with OAB had lower aMED scores and were more frequently represented in the lower adherence subgroups (Table 1). In contrast, individuals in the highest quartile of Mediterranean diet adherence (Q4) exhibited reduced nocturia scores (0.87 ± 0.02) and lower OABSS (1.05 ± 0.02) (Table 2).

Multivariate regression analysis

The weighted multivariable regression analysis showed an inverse association between the aMED score and the prevalence of OAB. Models 1, 2, and 3 yielded odds ORs of 0.990 (95% CI: 0.988–0.993, p < 0.0001), 0.985 (95% CI: 0.982–0.988, p < 0.0001), and 0.996 (95% CI: 0.993–0.999, p = 0.007), respectively (Table 3). In the unadjusted model, participants in the highest aMED quartile (Q4) had lower odds of reporting OAB (OR = 0.700, 95% CI: 0.611–0.801, p < 0.0001). This association remained significant after full adjustment (OR = 0.832, 95% CI: 0.707–0.979, p < 0.0001), with a p-value for trend < 0.05. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis further indicated a nonlinear association between aMED score and OAB prevalence (p for nonlinearity = 0.004, Fig. 2). In analyses of individual aMED components, higher intakes of nuts (OR = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.977–0.999) and fish (OR = 0.979, 95% CI: 0.967–0.991) were most strongly associated with lower odds of reporting OAB (Table 4). A likelihood ratio test found no significant difference between the segmented linear regression model and the logistic regression model (p = 0.551, Table 5).

Stratified and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analysis suggested that PIR modified the association between aMED score and OAB, with an interaction p-value of 0.041 after adjusting for covariates (Table 6). Sensitivity analysis using the average of two days’ dietary intake in multinomial logistic regression produced consistent findings, showing an inverse association between aMED score and OAB prevalence, with significantly lower odds observed in the highest aMED quartile (Q4) (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This study examined the association between adherence to the MD, measured by the aMED score, and the occurrence of OAB among U.S. adults aged 20–65 years. We found that higher aMED scores were associated with a reduced risk of OAB, and this association remained significant after adjusting for multiple sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders. Restricted cubic spline analysis further suggested a nonlinear relationship, in which moderate adherence to the MD conferred the greatest benefit.

The MD may influence OAB risk through several biological pathways. Its emphasis on olive oil, fish, fruits, vegetables, and nuts provides abundant polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, and other bioactive compounds with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [16, 17]. These nutrients may reduce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which play a role in bladder dysfunction [18]. Chronic inflammation can impair detrusor muscle and nerve function, contributing to urgency and frequency symptoms. Omega-3 fatty acids have been shown to modulate bladder inflammation [19], while nuts and plant-based foods supply antioxidants such as vitamin E and unsaturated fats, further supporting bladder health [20]. From a behavioral perspective, individuals with high MD adherence may also maintain healthier lifestyles overall, including lower consumption of processed foods and better weight control, which may indirectly lower OAB risk.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies linking diet quality to urinary health. A large cohort study in women identified obesity, smoking, and carbonated drink intake as OAB risk factors, whereas higher consumption of vegetables, bread, and chicken was protective [21]. Another study in men reported a surprising protective effect of daily beer intake on OAB onset, potentially due to bioactive compounds acting on β2 adrenergic or anticholinergic receptors, despite alcohol being commonly considered a bladder irritant [5, 22]. In our analysis, alcohol consumption contributed to the aMED score, and although its effect was not significant in the fully adjusted model, a protective tendency was observed in partially adjusted analyses. Moreover, other indices of dietary quality, such as the health nutrition index, have demonstrated inverse associations with OAB, whereas inflammatory diets were linked to increased risk [4, 23]. Our study extends this evidence by evaluating the overall dietary pattern through the aMED score within a nationally representative population, highlighting that moderate MD adherence may be particularly beneficial.

The use of the aMED score warrants discussion. The original Mediterranean Diet Score was developed in Mediterranean countries, where dietary habits differ substantially from those in the U.S. The aMED was specifically adapted for NHANES data to better capture adherence to core Mediterranean diet principles within an American context. It emphasizes intake of vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, fish, whole grains, and the ratio of monounsaturated to saturated fat, while accounting for moderate alcohol intake. However, the aMED does not differentiate alcohol types, which may vary in their health effects, and it may incompletely reflect cultural dietary variability across U.S. populations. Despite these limitations, aMED remains a widely applied and validated index for assessing Mediterranean diet adherence in epidemiological studies using NHANES data.

Strengths and limitations

This study is subject to several limitations, including its cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, reliance on self-reported dietary and health data, and potential residual confounding. Nonetheless, the findings highlight a potential role of dietary patterns—particularly higher nut and fish intake—in the management and prevention of OAB symptoms. Prospective longitudinal studies and dietary intervention trials are needed to further clarify these associations and explore underlying biological mechanisms.

Conclusion

In summary, greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with lower odds of OAB in U.S. adults, particularly among women. Given the limitations of pharmacological therapies, dietary modification may represent a low-cost, sustainable, and non-pharmacological strategy for OAB management. Future longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these associations and further explore the underlying mechanisms.
