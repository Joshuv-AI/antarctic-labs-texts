Technostress among healthcare professionals in Spain: a cross-sectional survey on the impact of digital health tools
Authors: Pilar Ficapal-Cusí, Joan Torrent-Sellens, Oriol Yuguero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess the prevalence of technostress among healthcare professionals (physicians and nurses) in Catalonia, Spain, and examine its association with sociodemographic characteristics and the contextual use of digital health tools.DesignCross-sectional survey.SettingHealthcare institutions in Catalonia, including hospitals, primary care centres and social healthcare facilities.ParticipantsA convenience sample of 587 healthcare professionals (423 physicians and 164 nurses) who responded to an anonymous online questionnaire.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the level of technostress, assessed using the Technostress Creators Scale. Background

Healthcare organisations are undergoing a continuous process of digitalisation, significantly transforming the delivery of healthcare services. While digitalisation offers numerous advantages—such as enhanced communication between professionals, more efficient patient management and reduced waiting times—research presents conflicting findings regarding its impact on the working conditions of healthcare professionals and the quality of care provided by healthcare organisations.1 Digital technologies (DT) have introduced new tasks for healthcare professionals, reshaping their roles in clinical practice, patient interactions and administrative processes.2 In this study, the term ‘digital technologies (DT)’ refers to the core set of tools currently used by healthcare professionals in clinical environments, including electronic health records, telemedicine platforms, e-prescribing systems and digital communication interfaces. The growing demand for digital skills can be a significant source of stress, particularly when professionals lack proper training or familiarity with emerging technologies.1 Research has highlighted several challenges related to DT in healthcare, including insufficient training in the use of digital tools for remote consultations,3 increased documentation burdens that reduce the time available for direct patient care4 and the perceived usability of DT being linked to physician burnout.5 Other reported issues include concerns over data privacy management,6 administrative overload,7 inadequate technical support and the additional responsibility of training colleagues.8 Furthermore, poor usability of information systems remains a persistent challenge.5 In short, many healthcare professionals can perceive digitalisation as a potential source of exclusion or as a factor that diminishes their sense of competence, ultimately increasing their stress levels.

Technological stress, or technostress (TS), is a subdimension of stress that arises from an individual’s use of DT. The term was introduced when technologies were beginning to gain prominence in everyday life. It was originally defined as ‘a modern disease of adaptation caused by an inability to cope with or deal with new computational technologies in a healthy manner’.9 Later, TS would not be considered a disease but rather any negative impact directly or indirectly caused by technology, including emotional aspects.10 Subsequently, it has been conceptualised as an adaptation problem for individuals when struggling to cope with the associated physical, social and cognitive demands of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) and computing.11 12

According to the transactional model of stress (TMS), technologies create an environment with certain characteristics, the individual evaluates these technological conditions and, if they perceive them as overwhelming or difficult to manage, they become technostressors.13 14 Literature identifies five TS creators: techno-complexity (TC, ‘too difficult’), techno-invasion (TI, ‘always connected’), techno-insecurity (TIN, ‘being replaced’), techno-overload (TO, ‘too much’); and techno-uncertainty (TU, ‘too many changes’).15

Research indicated that individual differences and perceptions of technology may influence and amplify TS.1416,18 However, findings on the relationship between TS and sociodemographic variables (gender, age, education, job tenure, work experience, etc) and technology characteristics remain inconsistent.19,24 In the healthcare field empirical, few studies evidenced the prevalence of TS and their association between sociodemographic characteristics.25 26 For example, for a sample of physicians in Switzerland, healthcare professionals experienced moderate TS in their daily work, differing according to environments and professions. Physicians showed significantly higher TS compared with nurses. Furthermore, it was revealed that with increasing educational level of individuals, the experienced TS increased significantly.27 Another finding reported that physicians and nurses with greater interaction with DT experienced higher levels of TS and rated their digital competence lower compared with other professions.2

A body of research conceptualised TS as a multidimensional construct.12 But studies that analyse each technostressor separately to identify specific influences among healthcare professionals are scarce.27,29 Beyond the healthcare context, findings identified significant differences in technostressors based on sociodemographic variables and indicated that creators of TS can affect users differently depending on their settings.2530,32 These findings are not conclusive but suggest that certain groups may experience different levels of exposure to technostressors, which highlights the need for a detailed analysis of individual and contextual factors regarding the prevalence of TS.8 15 33

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of TS among healthcare professionals in our region, analysing its relationship with sociodemographic characteristics, workplace settings and contextual situation of technology usage. First, differences in the prevalence of TS and TS creators were analysed, specifically comparing doctors and nurses. Second, the association between individual characteristics of healthcare professionals and TS was explored, and the technostressors included TO, TI, TC, TIN and TU. Finally, we examined the situational context of technology use defined by the DT usage domain, the purpose of DT use and the degree of voluntary usage. The situational context was associated with different dimensions of TS and TS creators, based on healthcare professional groups. The research questions were:

To what extent do healthcare professionals experience TS? Are there differences between physicians and nurses?

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional online survey of healthcare professionals in Catalonia, Spain, to assess the prevalence of TS and its dimensions, as well as their association with sociodemographic characteristics and the situational context of technology use.

Survey administration and sample

The sample was obtained by non-probabilistic sampling using a convenience sample of healthcare professionals, including physicians and nurses employed in the health sector of Catalonia. The survey was disseminated through a combination of professional networks, including the official channels of regional medical and nursing associations, and open calls on social media platforms. Given the nature of the dissemination strategy, it is not possible to determine the exact number of healthcare professionals who received the invitation or had access to the questionnaire. However, we estimate that the survey was potentially accessible to over 25 000 physicians and nurses in Catalonia through professional associations and social media platforms. This limitation precluded calculation of a precise response rate but highlights the need for caution when interpreting the representativeness of the sample.

A total of 772 responses were collected, of which 587 were complete. The inclusion criterion for analysis required that all questions/items be answered, and incomplete or inconsistent responses were excluded.

Data were cross-sectionally collected using an online, self-reported survey Qualtric platform. Data were collected using previously proposed and validated questionnaires and required approximately 10 min to complete. It was distributed via email through professional medical and nursing associations and social networks. The survey was anonymous and required informed consent for participation. Participation was voluntary and no participant received any kind of gratification. Respondents were informed of the security and privacy of the data they provided. The questionnaire was sent to all professionals forming the study universe. It was conducted between 1 March and 30 April 2024 in a post-pandemic context where digital tools had already become widely integrated into clinical practice.

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of a defined sampling frame. However, the final sample of 587 professionals (423 physicians and 164 nurses) satisfies standard methodological criteria for cross-sectional studies. According to Green’s rule of thumb for regression analysis (N≥50 + 8 m),34 our sample size provides sufficient power to examine associations with multiple predictors. Additionally, for subgroup analysis, a minimum sample size of 151 individuals is required to estimate a population proportion with 95% confidence and a precision of±0.08, assuming a conservative proportion of 0.50 (which yields the highest variance). Our study meets this requirement for both physicians and nurses, thus ensuring acceptable precision in the estimation of proportions within each professional group. Our sample meets this criterion and provides sufficient power for descriptive and bivariate analyses.

Measurement instruments

Technostress Creators Scale

TS was measured using the Spanish version of the Technostress Creators Scale (TCS),12 adapted and validated into Spanish and used in other studies.35 The TCS is a 23-item instrument that assesses five dimensions or technostressors: TO: work overload due to technology (five items); TI: interference with personal life (four items); TC: difficulty dealing with technology (five items); TIN: fear of being replaced by technology (five items); and TU: uncertainty due to constant changes in technology (4 items). Participants scored the items on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, with 1 being ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 ‘strongly agree’. The overall TS level was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the scores across all five dimensions.

The internal consistency of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α). In this study, the overall reliability coefficient was 0.90, indicating strong internal consistency. Similarly, the internal consistency of each subscale demonstrated good reliability, with all α values exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70.36 Specifically, the reliability coefficients were: TO=0.79, TI=0.83, TC=0.89, TIN=0.85 and TU=0.77.

Demographic and work-related characteristics

Questions were used to capture participants’ personal and work-related characteristics, including gender, age, professional profile, work setting, years in practice and tenure in the healthcare centre.

Usage of DT by healthcare professionals

The usage of DT in daily work practices was documented. It was assessed across nine key activities in clinical practice using a 5-point Likert scale (1 - very little use, 5 - very high use). Additionally, the intention to use was assessed through an 11-item inventory, adapted from a questionnaire designed to measure both the intensity of DT use and the intention to use it.37 According to the TMS, this assessment considered the technology usage domain,38 the purpose of technology39 and the degree of voluntary usage of technology.40 The items were also measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 - strongly disagree, 5 - strongly agree). DT usage in daily work practices was obtained as the arithmetic mean of the scores for the nine key activities in clinical practice.

The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency (α=0.77), indicating acceptable reliability.

Common method bias

Since all variables in this study were measured using self-reports, we assessed the potential presence of Common Method Bias (CMB) using Harman’s Single-Factor Test.6 41 This test was conducted through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with an unrotated solution to examine whether a single factor accounted for most of the variance in the dataset.42 The analysis revealed that the first extracted factor explained well below the 50% threshold, indicating that no single factor dominates the variance structure. Therefore, there is no strong evidence suggesting the presence of CMB in this study.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using frequency distribution and univariate analysis. Descriptive statistics to assess the prevalence of TS and technostressors among healthcare professionals. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each technostressor (TO, TI, TC, TIN and TU) based on our selected sociodemographic and work-related characteristics and situational context for the organisational usage of DT. The reliability values for each subfactor were calculated using Cronbach’s α.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and skewness and kurtosis values were used to assess normality. The Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied to compare differences across multiple non-normally distributed groups. If a significant difference was found among groups, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the p value, ensuring that statistical significance was not inflated due to multiple testing. Post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction were conducted only for variables showing significant differences in the Kruskal-Wallis test, as these analyses are intended to locate specific group differences once a global difference is detected.

Inclusion and ethics statement

The survey was anonymous, but participants were required to provide informed consent. The study adhered to the recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki. Data management was conducted in compliance with Spain’s Organic Law 3/2018 on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPD-GDD 3/2018) and the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, Regulation 2016/679).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research

Results

The study sample consisted of 587 healthcare professionals: 423 (72.1%) physicians and 164 nurses (27.9%). The majority of respondents were women (69%) and aged over 40 years (78.1%). The mean age of the sample population was 50.1 years. A significant portion of the sample (80.9%) had 10 or more years of work experience, and 36.3% had been employed at their current care centre for over 20 years. More than half of the participants (52.9%) worked in hospital settings (see table 1).

Technological usage of DT

The table 1 summarises the perceptions of healthcare professionals (doctors and nurses) regarding digital health tools. Practically all groups of healthcare professionals (physicians and nurses) use DT, mostly in management and clinical practice (79.9%). The most frequent activities included requesting and obtaining test results (77%), clinical follow-ups (75.8%) and patient referrals (70%). Overall, 77.3% of participants acknowledged that digital tools were essential for their professional activities (physicians, 78.5%; nurses, 74.4%). About 64.6% identified as regular users of digital platforms both professionally and personally (physicians, 60.3%; nurses, 75.6%). However, only 38.2% of participants reported prior experience with telemedicine systems (39.5%, physicians; 34.8%, nurses).

Around half of the healthcare professionals noted that their colleagues frequently used DT (physicians, 58.9%; nurses, 51.2%) and felt encouraged by their workplace to use digital health applications (physicians, 55.1%; nurses, 59.1%). Less than half of the healthcare professionals (48.4%) believed they had easy access to digital tools (49.2% physicians, 46.3% nurses), while 43.8% thought the same for citizens (46.1% physicians; 37.8% nurses). Only 29.8% reported that patients requested the use of digital tools (physicians, 33.3%; nurses, 20.7%). Over half of participants found digital tools beneficial for managing patient scheduling and optimising visits (doctors, 66.0%, nurses, 65.9%). But only a 40.5% found digital tools easy to use for their tasks (40.7% doctors, 40.2% nurses). A notable 65.2% reported significant technical issues that interfered with their work (physicians, 65.7%, nurses, 64.0%).

Levels of TS and subscales: differences between physicians and nurses

When examining the general construct of TS, the data revealed a moderate arithmetic mean score of 2.93±0.60 (mean±SD) (observed range=1.00–4.78; theoretical range=1–5). The mean score across the five dimensions was used to calculate the overall value. The results showed that the levels of TS did not present significant differences between doctors and nurses. In the case of the subscales, after applying the Bonferroni correction (α=0.01), only the TU subscale maintained a significant difference (Z=40.853, p<0.001), with nurses reporting higher scores than physicians (3.53 vs 3.29). The remaining subscales (TO, TI, TC and TIN) did not present significant differences (p>0.01 in all cases). Both groups reported low levels of insecurity derived from the use of technologies (TIN), and physicians reported the highest level of stressor is overload due to the use of technology (TO) (see table 2).

Young and middle-aged physicians differed significantly in TC, TIN and TU compared with older groups (51–60 and >60) (p<0.001, p<0.001, p<0.01). The older the physicians, the higher the levels of TS and its dimensions. Physicians over 60 years of age showed the highest scores. Experience of less than 20 years was associated significantly with lower levels of TS (p<0.01), TC (p<0.001) and TIN (p<0.001) compared with those with more than 20 years of experience. In relation to tenure in healthcare centre, differences were observed in the level of TS between physicians with less than 1 year of permanence and those with more than 20 years (p<0.01), showing a tendency to increase TS as seniority increases.

Regarding the technology usage domain (table 4), physicians who considered that the tools are not easy to use, or present technical issues experienced a higher level of TS and technostressors. While physicians who had experience in telemedicine and digital health experienced lower TS (p<0.01) and lower level of TC (p<0.001). Physicians who are regular users of social networks and digital platforms also experienced lower TC (p<0.01). In relation to the purpose of technology, physicians who found digital tools useful for managing patient scheduling and optimising visits reported fewer TS and TU. Regarding its use in different activities, significant differences were only observed in the management of appointments and referrals and temporary disabilities. Greater use in these activities was associated with TO (p<0.001). Finally, regarding the degree of voluntary usage of technology, physicians who considered digital tools to be very useful for their work experienced less TC (p<0.01). Having easy access to digital health tools was associated with lower levels of TS (p<0.001) and with significant differences in several dimensions (p<0.05; p<0.01; p<0.001). Physicians who also perceived that citizens can easily access these tools experienced lower levels of TS (p<0.001), TO (p<0.001), TC (p<0.01) and TIN (p<0.01). However, physicians who indicated that their service encourages the use of DT and apps reported higher levels of TS (p<0.05) and TU (p<0.01). Whether colleagues use digital health and applications often is associated with higher TU (p<0.05).

Insights from nurses

A similar analysis was conducted for nurses, as shown in table 5. Gender and work setting did not appear to influence TS levels significantly. There was a tendency for increased TS and TC in older age groups. Significant differences were observed in the TS and TC scores according to age (p<0.001). Younger nurses (21–30 years) reported lower levels of TS compared with older nurses (51–60 and >60) (p<0.001). Nurses in the 31–40 and 51–60 age groups also differed significantly (p=0.003). The youngest (21–30 years) reported experiencing less TS than the 51–60 age group (p=0.01). TI also presented significant differences by age (p<0.05). Nurses with more work experience (>20 years) had higher levels of total TS (p<0.001). In this regard, differences were also observed between 6 and 10 years and more than 10 years of experience with respect to the level of TO (p<0.01), TC (p<0.001) and TIN (p<0.05). Nurses with higher tenure in the same health centre (>20 years) showed significantly higher levels of total TS (p<0.001) than nurses with fewer years in the centre. Tenure in the healthcare centre generates significant differences in TU. Significant differences were found between professionals with less than 1 year in the centre and the groups of 1–5 years (p=0.023), 6–10 years (p=0.007), 11–20 years (p=0.001) and more than 20 years (p=0.002). After Bonferroni correction, no post-hoc comparison in relation to other dimensions of the TS was significant.

Discussion

The study aimed to assess the prevalence of TS among healthcare professionals in Catalonia (a region of Spain), analysing its relationship with sociodemographic characteristics, workplace environments and the contextual factors related to technology use. There is existing evidence of TS in education professionals, particularly linked to the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic,43 but notably, this is the first descriptive study of TS among healthcare professionals in Spain.

The present study demonstrated a high degree of DT integration in healthcare practice, with 79.9% of professionals actively using them in daily clinical and managerial tasks. The most frequent applications include accessing test results (77%), clinical follow-ups (75.8%) and managing referrals (70%). A significant 77.3% of respondents view digital tools as essential to their work, reflecting their perceived value in enhancing healthcare delivery. Nonetheless, only 38.2% reported prior experience with telemedicine, suggesting that, despite broad digital adoption, specific domains such as remote care remain underused and warrant further exploration and support.44

The levels of TS among the healthcare professionals in our sample were found to be in the medium-to-high range (2.93/4.78). No significant differences were found between professional categories (physicians and nurses), although nurses exhibited higher levels of TU compared with physicians.

Prior studies have indicated that sociodemographic factors can shape individuals’ experiences with technostressors, as well as their perceptions and acceptance of technology.17 Nevertheless, findings on the relationship between TS and factors such as gender and age remain inconsistent across studies.25 32 Recent research in healthcare settings found no significant relationship between TS levels and demographic characteristics.45 In our study, gender and work setting did not significantly influence TS levels in either group, in line with other studies.30,33 However, another sociodemographic variable such as age, professional experience and tenure at the healthcare centre emerged as key factors associated with TS in healthcare professionals.

Among physicians, older professionals experienced significantly higher overall TS, as well as increased levels of TIN and TC compared with younger and middle-aged colleagues. These findings aligned with previous research suggesting that older individuals tend to experience greater TC. However, unlike some earlier studies that identified higher levels of TO and TI among younger professionals,30 no significant differences were observed in these dimensions within the healthcare sector. Similarly, among nurses, older age groups reported significantly higher levels of total TS and TC compared with their younger counterparts. TI also varied significantly with age. This suggests that the impact of TS may vary depending on the specific factors shaping individuals’ work environments.16 Previous studies suggested that managing ICTs may demand a level of cognitive flexibility that tends to decrease with age.17

In the same line, higher levels of TS, TC and TIN were found among more experienced physicians, with TC also increasing among those with longer tenure in the same healthcare centre. Likewise, nurses with over 20 years of professional experience and extended tenure at the same institution reported elevated levels of TS, showing significant differences in TO, TC, TIN and TU. These findings highlight how experience and tenure shape different TS in healthcare settings.

Factors related to the use of DT also influenced the TS perception. Both physicians and nurses who found digital tools difficult to use or who encountered technical issues reported higher levels of TS. Conversely, professionals with experience in telemedicine and digital health exhibited lower TS and TC scores. Notably, voluntary use of DT mitigated TS, as those who perceived DT as useful in their practice reported significantly lower TS levels (p<0.001). Recognising the utility of ICTs and their contribution to improving professional activities serves as a protective factor against TS and its subscales. For nurses, TS is often associated with difficulties in accessing useful digital resources. This reinforces the idea that training in digital competencies and skills is essential to reducing technology-related stress. Equipping nurses with the tools and knowledge to navigate digital health technologies can help alleviate their TS and enhance their confidence in using these tools.

Another key finding is that despite experiencing TS, the majority of professionals perceive digital tools as useful in clinical practice. This positive perception has also been associated with lower levels of TS. However, one of the primary sources of anxiety and uncertainty is the technical problems often encountered with health-related apps and digital tools. Addressing these technical issues should be a priority to improve professionals’ confidence and reduce TS in healthcare settings. A proposed action could be that each time a software update or changes to computer programmes occur, professional support should be provided, along with actionable recommendations to address the situation. Support initiatives of this type have already been successfully implemented, for example, in surgery applied robotics.46

Among physicians, the use of social media emerged as a factor associated with lower levels of TS, as did familiarity with digital health tools. Considering this, we believe promoting digital health training is crucial as a protective measure against TC and TO. In Catalonia, a recent study47 highlighted the importance of assessing digital health competencies to develop strategies that prevent the onset of TS. Despite the acknowledged benefits of DT, healthcare professionals faced notable challenges. Only 40.5% considered these tools easy to use, and 65.2% reported experiencing significant technical issues that interfered with their work. Additionally, perceptions regarding accessibility varied, with only 48.4% of professionals and 43.8% of citizens deemed to have easy access to digital health tools.

Interestingly, the organisational environment also influenced TS. Healthcare professionals working in institutions that actively promoted DT usage reported higher TS levels, suggesting potential pressures associated with mandatory digitalisation. Furthermore, perceptions of accessibility affected stress levels; professionals who believed that digital health tools were easily accessible—both to themselves and to citizens—reported lower TS and lower scores across multiple technostressor dimensions.

Practical implications and limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the challenges faced by different sociodemographic groups and can inform the development of targeted interventions and support systems to effectively address and reduce TS across diverse healthcare professionals. The data point to a strong uptake of DT within healthcare systems, with nearly 80% of professionals using them routinely, particularly for test results, patient monitoring and referrals. This widespread usage aligns with efforts to modernise healthcare services. Furthermore, a large majority of respondents recognise DT as essential to their work. However, limited exposure to telemedicine highlights a key gap. Policy efforts aimed at expanding training, infrastructure and access to telemedicine could help ensure a more equitable and comprehensive digital transformation across the sector.

In this regard, public health managers can play a key role in developing targeted initiatives to support both individuals and organisations—such as educational programmes, training, resources and guidelines adapted to specific demographic groups—and implement strategies to enhance technology adoption and improve user experiences within healthcare settings. Such efforts can help improve user experience, reduce resistance to technology and ensure that digital health solutions truly enhance clinical practice and patient care.

This research has led to several findings that may advance the current literature. Nonetheless, there are also certain limitations. The research relied on a non-probabilistic convenience sample and employed a cross-sectional design. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate longitudinal approaches and adopt more diverse methodologies, including experimental designs. New longitudinal studies measuring the effect of sociodemographic variables over time should be conducted. Additionally, the TS assessment was self-administered, which may introduce bias in how participants evaluate their own situations. Self-reported data can sometimes reflect subjective perceptions rather than objective measures, which is a potential constraint in interpreting the findings. Nonetheless, the study provides valuable insights into the levels and characteristics of TS among healthcare professionals and highlights areas for future intervention.

This study adopted an exploratory approach, with no intention of establishing causal relationships. Its objective was to identify worker profiles based on their levels and patterns of TS and to explore differences according to sociodemographic variables. The absence of multivariate analyses may entail the presence of confounding factors. Future studies will incorporate multivariate and predicting models controlling for these variables to provide more robust evidence.

Other limitation is its reliance on data collected via social networks and email, targeting a diverse population. While this approach may introduce variability, we aimed to ensure the sample’s representativeness by including professionals from both rural and urban settings, as well as individuals from different professional categories and age groups. Moreover, the inability to determine the number of professionals who received the survey prevented us from calculating a response rate. This represents a limitation in terms of assessing potential non-response bias. However, the final sample size satisfies general methodological criteria for exploratory cross-sectional analyses. The study did not specifically explore the impact of newer technologies such as artificial intelligence, which may generate different sources of TS. Future studies should investigate how emerging tools, including artificial intelligence-driven systems, affect different professional groups. Moreover, future research should also extend to other cultural contexts to improve our understanding of this phenomenon by offering a more holistic perspective, considering the interaction between technology, culture and individual characteristics in shaping TS experiences.

Finally, the limited availability of bibliographic references to compare our results makes it more challenging to draw robust conclusions.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on TS, specifically by validating its associations with demographic and work-related characteristics, the technology usage domain, the purpose of technology and the degree of voluntary usage of technology among healthcare professionals—physicians and nurses. These findings offer valuable insights into the dynamics of TS in healthcare settings.

This study highlights the urgent need to address the moderate to high levels of TS experienced by healthcare professionals in hospital settings, in line with recent research.17 We believe these findings have significant implications for the management of healthcare workers and the prevention of burnout among professionals. One of the key stressors contributing to burnout in healthcare is the introduction of electronic health records, which has transformed daily workflows.48

While increasing attention is being given to the digital gap49 affecting many patients due to the emergence of digital health, this gap can also impact healthcare professionals if proper training and gradual adaptation to new technologies are not implemented. As digital competencies are increasingly required of healthcare professionals, it is crucial for healthcare institutions and their leaders to recognise the phenomenon of TS.

Institutional awareness should translate into proactive measures to mitigate TS, including the implementation of training programmes, progressive adoption of technologies and strategies to prevent its escalation. Such actions could help reduce the negative effects of TS, improve the well-being of healthcare workers and ultimately prevent burnout. By addressing these challenges, institutions can better equip their staff to navigate the growing demands of digital healthcare effectively and sustainably.
