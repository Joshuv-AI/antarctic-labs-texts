During plant development and in response to environmental cues, ARGONAUTE (AGO) proteins regulate gene expression at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. Tomassi et al. (2025), using a biochemical approach, identified that iron superoxide dismutase 1 (FeSOD1), a key enzyme in reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, forms a complex with ARGONAUTE 1 (AGO1). This interaction enhances AGO1 stability under copper (Cu) deficiency in Arabidopsis. The study investigates how Cu-induced oxidative stress affects AGO1 stability and hints at a role for FeSOD1 in maintaining AGO1 homeostasis, providing new insights into the redox regulation of gene silencing components in response to micronutrient stress.

AGO1 homeostasis

Identifying the plant developmental stages and environmental cues that trigger AGO accumulation or turnover, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms, has long been of great interest. Many investigations focused on AGO1, which is the founding member of the AGO family. AGO1 is a ubiquitously expressed ARGONAUTE protein which has a role in many aspects of plant development as well as a function during response to both biotic and abiotic stresses. Therefore, regulating the amount and stability of AGO1 is of utmost importance for the plant. Degradation of unloaded or non-functional AGO1 also importantly serves as a quality control of the RNAi pathway. Regulation of AGO1 levels relies on complex interplay between post-transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms (Fig. 1).

Regulation of AGO1 homeostasis. The fine-tuning of AGO1 contents relies on post-translational- and ubiquitin-mediated mechanisms. Thus, both AGO1 mRNA and protein levels are modulated by several different pathways. AGO1 transcripts are under the control of a self-regulation pathway involving two miR168 isoforms. These miR168 isoforms are loaded into either AGO1 or AGO10, triggering the degradation of AGO1 transcripts and the inhibition of AGO1 translation, respectively. AGO1 protein could act both in the cytoplasm and in the nucleus. Upon shuttling from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, AGO1 protein is under the control of post-translational modifications. Several studies characterized that AGO1 is directed either for degradation to the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) or to autophagy involving protein ubiquitination (Ub). The viral suppressor of RNA silencing, P0, targets the cytoplasmic AGO1 predominantly to autophagy (Derrien et al., 2018). The F-BOX WITH WD-40 2 protein (FBW2) directs AGO1 probably to the UPS (Ré et al., 2020). The destabilization of AGO1 occurs when bound to mRNA targets (Ré et al., 2020) or to prevent the loading of illegitimate small RNA (Hacquard et al., 2022). The mechanisms controlling the AGO1 nuclear pool content are still unknown.

Seminal studies showed that post-transcriptional processes fine-tune AGO1 contents through miRNA. Indeed, the 21 nt miR168 cleaves AGO1 mRNA and the 22 nt miR168 isoform loads into AGO10 to inhibit AGO1 translation (Mallory and Vaucheret, 2009; Iki et al., 2018; Fig. 1). The developmental defects observed in both ago1 and ago10 mutant plants are mainly explained by the de-regulation of AGO1 contents (Vaucheret et al., 2004). Such an miRNA pathway feedback loop or rheostat fine-tunes the amount of AGO1 protein in accordance with the changing size of the cellular miRNA pool (Dalmadi et al., 2021).

In addition to post-transcriptional regulation, the AGO1 protein content is also regulated at the level of its stability and degradation (Fig. 1). In experiments mimicking a viral infection, Derrien et al. (2018) used the P0 viral suppressor of RNA silencing to show that AGO1 degradation is triggered by the autophagy pathway. Although AGO1 ubiquitination was not directly demonstrated, this study shows that such post-translational modifications are likely to be involved in AGO1 stability. Examples of AGO1 regulation in non-viral contexts remain limited. It has been reported that UV-induced stress leads to AGO1 degradation by regulating the expression of the F-BOX WITH WD-40 2 protein (FBW2), which is involved in the selective targeting of AGO1 for proteolysis (Ré et al., 2020; Hacquard et al., 2022; Fig. 1).

AGO1 and stresses

AGO1 has key functions during biotic and abiotic stress responses. Indeed, AGO1 binds virus-derived siRNA during viral infection. Moreover, a large set of miRNAs are induced by various environmental stresses, indicating the presence of signaling pathways that converge on AGO1 function. For example, during copper (Cu) deficiency, the SPL7 transcription factor induces the expression of Cu-miRNAs which in turn regulates targets controlling Cu homeostasis (for a review, see Pilon, 2017). One of these targets is the CuZ/N superoxide dismutase (CSDs), which are substituted by FeSOD1 (Fe-superoxide dismutase 1), which is implicated in Cu adaptation mechanisms and was shown in Tomassi et al. (2025) to interact with AGO1. A distinct aspect of AGO1 activity under stress conditions involves its intracellular relocalization to cytoplasmic granules or condensates. Heat stress promotes AGO1 accumulation in cytosolic condensates where it co-localizes with components of siRNA bodies and stress granules. This relocalization is thought to protect AGO1 from degradation and may enhance its activity in gene silencing pathways during stress (Blagojevic et al., 2024).

Beyond its cytoplasmic roles, AGO1 also associates with chromatin and promotes the transcription of target genes in response to diverse stimuli including jasmonic acid (JA), flg22 treatment, and cold stress (Liu et al., 2018). Genome-wide analyses have shown that AGO1 binds to chromatin regions of stimulus-responsive genes, facilitating their transcriptional activation (Liu et al., 2018). This chromatin association is often mediated by specific small RNAs and involves interactions with chromatin-remodeling complexes. For instance, AGO1 participates in the co-transcriptional regulation of MIR161 and MIR173, which are critical for salinity tolerance. Under salt stress conditions, AGO1 binds to chromatin at the MIR161 and MIR173 loci, leading to transcriptional repression of these genes that correlates with decreased levels of primary miRNA transcripts (pri-miRNA161 and pri-miRNA173) but increased the stability of the corresponding mature miRNAs in the cytoplasm. This suggests a dual role for AGO1 in both transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation during stress responses (Dolata et al., 2016).

These observations highlight the central role of AGO1 as a regulatory hub during plant responses to environmental cues, integrating multiple signaling inputs to coordinate both transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory programs.

AGO1 post-translational modifications

Among the post-translational modifications (PTMs) that trigger AGO1 degradation, ubiquitin-mediated degradation mechanisms are the most well documented (Fig. 1). All AGO proteins feature an unstructured N-terminal extension (NTE). NTEs vary greatly among AGO proteins and are generally enriched in amino acid residues that promote flexibility, forming a disordered region that is likely to be unfolded (Martín-Merchán et al., 2023). The NTE regulates the intracellular localization and protein interaction of AGO. Indeed, the NTE of AGO1 controls its shuttling from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (Bologna et al., 2018) and its relocalization into condensate upon heat stress (Blagojevic et al., 2024). In addition, the NTE of Arabidopsis AGO1 and human Ago2 acts as a structural switch, allowing accessibility to the AGO RNA-free state to autophagy receptor cargo (Bressendorf et al., 2025).

Arginine dimethylation (R-met) has been shown recently to occur symmetrically within the NTE of Arabidopsis AGO1 (Martín-Merchán et al., 2024). Alteration of R-met modifies the NTE interactome and promotes phasiRNA loading without affecting miRNA loading or AGO1 intracellular localization (Barre-Villeneuve et al., 2024)

Tomassi et al. (2025) found that AGO1 turnover occurs under Cu deficiency, and suggest that oxidative stress might contribute to AGO1 destabilization. Oxidation is known to direct proteins to the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) for degradation (Jung and Grune, 2013). However, in mammals, AGO2 hydroxylation has been shown to be important for its stability and for efficient RNAi (Qi et al., 2008). In addition, miRNAs could also be oxidized, leading to mismatch in RNA duplex and to the initiation of apoptosis (Wang et al., 2015). These studies in different model organisms highlight that hydroxylation could trigger either AGO destabilization or stability. Thus, it will be of interest to determine whether AGO1 is indeed oxidized during stress and to identify the amino acid residues exhibiting hydroxylation upon ROS burst.

AGO1 needs a ROS bodyguard

AGO1 is a central regulator of stress responses and, consequently, its levels and localization must be tightly regulated. AGO1 content and intracellular distribution appear to depend on the NTE, as well as on interacting proteins and AGO1 PTMs. Interestingly, protein oxidation often happens during stresses. Given that oxidative bursts, through ROS production, are common responses to many environmental cues, they may play a key role in signaling pathways that regulate cellular homeostasis and stress responses. Indeed, environmental factors such as sunlight (UV-A and UV-B), heat, heavy metals, and pathogen attack that trigger ROS production affect various cellular components, including proteins. Thus, it is likely that protein oxidation should be prevented by ROS-scavenging mechanisms or be used as a regulatory signal by itself.

The deleterious effects of ROS are controlled by the production of antioxidant metabolites (i.e. flavonoids) and/or enzymes (i.e. SOD). Tomassi et al. (2025) suggest that AGO1 is a target of ROS and that its interaction with FeSOD1 may protect it from oxidation. Although Arabidopsis AGO1 oxidation remains to be demonstrated, it could be assumed that it may have a regulatory function such as AGO1 degradation and/or relocalization. Indeed, in addition to their damaging effects, ROS can also act as signaling molecules and alter phase separation properties, leading to condensate formation. For example, in tomato during heat stress, the oxidation of a transcription factor (TMF) leads to its sequestration by phase separation, and to a delay in flowering (Huang et al., 2025). Therefore, several questions related to AGO1 stability or relocalization upon oxidative stress exposure could be raised (Fig. 2). Does the AGO1 oxidized form move to stress granules? Could it be to target it for degradation? What could be the impact of oxidation on AGO1 function? The study by Tomassi et al. (2025) suggests that oxidized AGO1 is less functional and is targeted for degradation, as demonstrated in the fsd mutant. During Cu stress, AGO1 would be protected from oxidation by FeSOD1 to ensure the proper regulation of Cu gene expression. It would be interesting to know if other SODs interact with AGO1 during different stresses and if such interaction is direct or indirect. Additionally, since the NTE of AGO1 is modified by PTMs, such as arginine methylation, this raises the question of whether these modifications could protect AGO1 from oxidation either directly or indirectly by recruiting protective proteins such as FeSOD1 (Fig. 2). AGO1 is a key ARGONAUTE protein involved in stress responses; however, the Arabidopsis genome encodes 10 AGOs. Are these also influenced by oxidative stress? In conclusion, the study by Tomassi et al. (2025) adds a new layer of complexity to AGO1 post-translational regulation. As research advances, the connections between physiology, cell biology, and molecular biology are becoming increasingly evident, pointing toward a more holistic understanding of the cell under stress.

Putative role of ROS in the control of AGO1 homeostasis. Upon exposure to environmental cues, ROS are produced and target various cellular components, including proteins. Tomassi et al. (2025) found that under Cu deficiency (low Cu), which triggers ROS production, AGO1 becomes destabilized. Moreover, the ROS-scavenging enzyme FeSOD1 was found to form a complex with AGO1, preventing its degradation. Thus, oxidative burst and ROS scavenging may influence AGO1 intracellular dynamics and loading of small RNA, representing a novel mechanism for fine-tuning AGO1 in response to environmental stress.
