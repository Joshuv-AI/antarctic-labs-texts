Prevalence and distribution of human papillomavirus genotypes in women living with HIV: a cross-sectional study in Iran
Authors: Nastaran Tavakoli Dastjerd, Ladan Abbasian, Seyed Reza Mohebbi, Lida Moghaddam Banaem, Masoumeh Farrokh Ashtiani, Zohreh Rostamafshar, Hoorieh Soleimanjahi, Behrang Sarshari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen living with Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) have reduced ability to clear human papillomavirus (HPV) infections due to decreased levels of CD4+ T lymphocytes. This study aimed to determine the Prevalence and Distribution of Human Papillomavirus Genotypes in Women Living with HIV: A Cross-Sectional Study in Iran.MethodsEighty women living with HIV participated in this study. HPV DNA was extracted and universal primers (MY09/11 and GP5+/6+) were used in a single polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect HPV. Negative samples were re-amplified using GP5+/6+ primers in nested PCR. Finally, HPV-positive samples were genotyped using a Real-Time PCR kit. The data collected in this study were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4 software.ResultsHPV prevalence among women living with HIV was 76.25%. High-risk (HR) and low-risk (LR)- HPV genotypes were detected in 67.21% and 31.14% of participants, respectively. Multiple HR-HPV infections were found in 71.42% of HPV-positive cases. The most common HR-HPV genotypes were HPV 39 (37.70%) and HPV 52 (36.56%). For LR-HPV genotypes, HPV 44 (13.11%) was the most common type, followed by HPV 6 (3.27%) and HPV 11 (1.63%). Abnormal cervical cytology was significantly associated with HR-HPV infection (14.63%, P-value = 0.026). No significant association was found between HPV prevalence and risk factors such as marital status, education level, age at first sexual intercourse, smoking history, CD4+ T lymphocyte counts or duration of Antiretroviral therapy (ART). Age had a somewhat significant association with the occurrence of HPV infection with a p-value of 0.051.ConclusionsHR-HPV and multiple HR-HPV infections were highly prevalent among women living with HIV in Iran. HPV 39 and 52 being the most common types in our study. Preventive measures like vaccination and regular screening may improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11805-6.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Human immunodeficiency virus, Cervical cancer, Cervical cytology

Introduction

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are spread through sexual contact from one person to another [1]. HPV is a sexually transmitted infection and belongs to the papillomaviridae family [2]. HPV is a small, non-enveloped virus with a closed circular double-stranded DNA genome [3]. It contains eight open reading frames (ORFs) including a non-coding region (NCR), an early region (E1, E2 E4-E7) and a late region (L1, L2) [4]. HPV-related cancers are caused by the expression of two major oncoproteins: E6 and E7, whose combined activity contributes to the development and persistence of cancer [5].

To date, more than 200 genotypes of HPV have been identified and sequenced [6]. Forty of which infect the anogenital tract and oral mucosa [7]. Mucosal HPV types are classified based on their carcinogenic potential into two categories: high-risk (HR)-HPV, which includes types: 16-18-31-33-35-39-45-51-52-56-58-59-68-73 -82 [8, 9] and low-risk (LR)-HPV, which includes types: 6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, 70, 72, 81 and 89 [10, 11].

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignancy among women worldwide [4, 7] with HR-HPV genotypes responsible for about 90% of cases [2, 6]. Among these, HPV types 16 and 18 alone account for about 70% of cervical cancers [3, 12].

Several variables influence HPV infection, including: age, marital status, educational level, early age at first sexual intercourse, number of sexual partners, history of other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), smoking history, HIV infection, use of oral or hormonal contraceptives and CD4+ T lymphocyte count [13–15].

Regular screenings are recommended for the early detection of precancerous lesions as well as invasive cervical cancer [16, 17]. Health care programs aimed at controlling and monitoring human papillomavirus (HPV) infection have implemented two main strategies: extensive screening and/or vaccination programs. While developed countries adopt these strategies they remain economically inaccessible in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). As a result, the incidence of cervical cancer remains high in these regions [18, 19].

HIV is an important risk factor for HPV infection. Women living with HIV are more likely to acquire STIs including HPV compared to HIV-negative women [20, 21]. HIV infection increases the persistence and rate of HPV infection due to a decrease in CD4+ T lymphocytes [22]. Co-infection with HPV and HIV reduces the probability of clearing the HPV infection and increases its persistence compared to women who are not infected with HIV. This is because immunosuppression caused by HIV impairs the ability to clear the HPV infection and can lead to the reactivation of latent HPV during co-infection [2, 23]. Women living with HIV are at a higher risk of contracting multiple HR-HPV types. In other words, immunodeficiency associated with HIV contributes to the development of precancerous lesions more frequently than in the general population [24].

Treatment with antiretroviral drugs (ART) reduces the occurrence of cervical precancerous lesions, supports HPV clearance, increases the life expectancy of women living with HIV and ultimately decreases the incidence of cervical cancer [25].

In Iran, limited studies have investigated the prevalence of HPV genotypes in immunocompromised individuals. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to determine the prevalence and distribution of Human Papillomavirus Genotypes in Women Living with HIV: A Cross-Sectional Study in Iran. Additionally, the association between effective variables and HPV prevalence were analyzed in this study.

Methods

Study design and patients

This cross-sectional study was conducted at HIV clinic of Imam Khomeini hospital, a referral university hospital in Tehran. Eighty women living with HIV who were referred to the HIV clinic for cervical cancer screening were included through convenience sampling from March to September 2024. The patients’ ages ranged from 23 to 66 years and none of the patients had received the HPV vaccine. After obtaining ethical approval (IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1402.366), informed consent was obtained from all participants. A questionnaire was used to register the socio-demographic information (Supplementary file-1). All liquid-based samples were collected by a midwife and sent for HPV testing. Also all cervical samples evaluated for abnormal cervical cytology using the Bethesda reporting system.

Sample collection

Cervical swab samples were collected using a sterile brush in transport medium, preservative, and antibacterial medium. To obtain the cell pellet for HPV testing, the solution containing the cervical cells was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, 500 µl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to each tube and the samples were stored at -20 °C.

DNA extraction

HPV DNA was extracted using the High Pure Viral Nucleic Acid Kit (Roche, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. 200 µl of cervical swab samples were used for each extraction and the extracted HPV DNA was stored at -20 °C until PCR was performed.

HPV DNA detection and PCR protocol

To ensure the quality of the DNA extraction process, the human beta-globin gene (a housekeeping gene) was used as an internal control and was amplified using the PCO3/PCO4 primer set, producing a 110- bp PCR product. Cervical swab samples that tested positive for the beta-globin gene were then tested for HPV DNA using two pairs of universal primers (MY09/11 and GP5+/6+) in the first round of PCR (single PCR). These primers amplify the L1 region of the HPV genome, producing PCR products of 450 bp and 150 bp, respectively (Table 1). The 25 µl PCR reaction mixture for both MY09/11 and GP5+/6+ primer sets included: 12.5 µl Taq DNA Polymerase 2x Master Mix RED (AMPLIQON, 1.5 mM Mgcl2), 5 µl of sample DNA, 6.5 µl of H2O, 1 µl of each primer set (0.5 µl (10 pmol/ul) each of forward and reverse primers). Amplifications were carried out for 45 cycles with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 54 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products (5 µl) from both MY09/11 and GP5+/6+ primer sets were electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel and visualized under ultraviolet transillumination.

Nested PCR

Negative cervical swab samples were re-amplified using the GP5+/6+ primer set in the second round of PCR (nested PCR) (Fig. 1). The 25 µl PCR reaction mixture for the GP5+/6+ primer set contained 12.5 µl of Taq DNA Polymerase 2x Master Mix RED (AMPLIQON, 1.5 mM Mgcl2), 0.5 µl of sample, 11.5 µl of H2O, and 0.5 µl (10 pmol/ul) of GP5+/6+ primers. Amplification was carried out for 45 cycles under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 54 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products (5 µl) were electrophoresed on 1% agarose gel and visualized under ultraviolet transillumination.

Algorithm of HPV DNA detection: 80 cervical swab samples from HIV-positive women were first tested using the PCO3/4 primer set to amplify the human beta-globin gene. Positive samples for beta-globin were then tested for HPV DNA using a first round of PCR (single PCR) with MY09/11 and GP5+/6+ universal primers. Negative samples in the first round of PCR with MY09/11 were re-amplified using the GP5+/6+ primer set in a second round of PCR (nested PCR), with 0.5 µl of the MY09/11 PCR product from the previous step as the template

HPV genotyping

All cervical swab samples that tested positive for HPV DNA were genotyped using a Real-Time PCR kit (Sacace, HPV Genotypes 21 Real-TM Quant, Italy) for the identification, typing, and quantification of HR-HPV genotypes (16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82) and LR-HPV genotypes (6, 11, 44), based on the manufacturers protocol. The sacace kit contained 20 µl of PCR-mix 21 paraffin sealed (containing buffer, dNTPs, primers, and probes), 10 µl of Taq DNA Polymerase, 5 µl of HPV genotypes 21 positive control, 5 µl of negative control, and 5 µl of the sample. Finally, amplification was performed using the 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4. The chi-square test was used to evaluate the association between HPV prevalence and the study variables. a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis with the odd ratio at 95% confidence of intervals was used to assess potential of effective variables on occurrence of HPV.

Results

HPV DNA detection in cervical swab samples

This study was performed on 80 women living with HIV aged between 23 and 66 years (mean age 43 ± 8.69). HPV DNA was detected in 48.75% (39/80) and 41.25% (33/80) in cervical swab samples using MY09/11 and GP5+/6+ primers, respectively (Supplementary file-2). HPV-positive samples were detected in 23.75% (19/80) of cases using both MY09/11 and GP 5+/6+ primers in the first round (single PCR) of PCR. Moreover, 10% (8/80) of samples were found positive in the second round (nested PCR) of PCR (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes and multiple HR-HPV infections

The overall HPV prevalence among women living with HIV was 76.25% (61/80). Among these women HR-HPV genotypes were found in 67.21% (41/61), while LR-HPV genotypes were observed in 31.14% (20/61). Multiple HR-HPV infections were observed in 71.42% (30/42) of cases, of which 86.67% (26/30) had 2–4 genotypes, and 13.33% (4/30) had more than four genotypes. Among the HR-HPV genotypes, HPV39 (37.70%) and HPV52 (36.56%) were the most common. Other detected HR-HPV genotypes included: HPV73 (13.11%), HPV16 (9.83%), HPV18 (8.19%), HPV45 (8.19%), and HPV68 (8.19%). Among LR-HPV genotypes, HPV44 (13.11%) was the most frequent, followed by HPV6 (3.27%) and HPV11 (1.63%) (Fig. 3).

Cytological results and HPV genotypes status

Cervical cytology results showed that 7.5% (6/80) of women had abnormal cervical cytology. HPV DNA was detected in 100% (6/6) of the patients with abnormal cervical cytology. Among these six patients, one had a low-grade intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), two had both LSIL and atypical squamous cell of undetermined significance (ASC-US), and three had high-grade intraepithelial lesion (HSIL). Of the patients with abnormal cytology, four (66.67%) had multiple HR-HPV genotypes. All six patients with abnormal cytology results had HR-HPV genotypes (P-value = 0.026), while none of the patients with only LR-HPV genotypes showed abnormal cytology results (Fig. 4). HPV52, followed by HPV39 and HPV73, was observed in HSIL, while HPV52 along with HPV genotypes 39,31, and 68 was detected in LSIL and ASCUS cases.

Regarding marital status, 37.50% (30/80) of patients were single and 62.50% (50/80) were married. There was no significant difference in HPV prevalence between these groups: 80% (24/30) in single women and 74% (37/50) in married women (Table 3) (P-value = 0.313; OR = 1.908) (Table 4).

Women living with HIV were also classified by educational level: Elementary, High school, and College. The majority of patients 87% (70/80) had high school education with the highest HPV prevalence observed in this group at 75.71% (53/70) (Table 3) (P-values = 0.822 and 0.394; ORs = 1.417 and 2.370) (Table 4).

There was no significant association between socio-demographic findings (marital status and educational level) and HPV prevalence in our study. Age had a somewhat significant association with the occurrence of HPV infection with a p-value of 0.051. If this association were considered significant, it could be said that with each year of age increase in the participants, the risk of HPV infection increased by 9%.

The impact of age at first sexual intercourse, history of smoking and HPV prevalence

The age at first sexual intercourse among women living with HIV was classified into four groups (Table 3). Most patients had their first sexual intercourse between the ages of 15 and 17 years (group 2, 31.25% ,25/80) and 22 years (group 4, 31.25%, 25/80). The highest HPV prevalence, 88% (22/25), was observed in group 2 (P-value = 0.221; OR = 1.095) (Table 4).

Regarding smoking history among women living with HIV, 47.50% (38/80) were smokers and 52.50% (42/80) were non-smokers. HPV DNA was found in 78.94% (30/38) of smokers and 71.42% (30/42) of non-smokers (Table 3) (P-value = 0.159; OR = 0.411) (Table 4).

HPV prevalence was not significantly difference with age at first sexual intercourse and history of smoking in patients in this study.

The effect of CD4 + T lymphocyte count, duration of ART use and HPV prevalence

CD4 + T lymphocyte counts were classified into four groups. most women living with HIV had a CD4 + T lymphocyte count greater than 500 cells/mm3 70% (56/80), while the highest HPV prevalence 80% (4/5) was observed in the second group of patients with a CD4 + T lymphocyte count between 300 and 400 cells/mm3(Table 3). This difference was not significant (P-value = 0.956; OR = 1.000) (Table 4).

Finally, although most patients in this study had been using antiretroviral therapy (ART) for more than 11 years, HPV DNA was detected in 90% (9/10) in patients who had used ART for 3 to 5 years (Table 3). There was no significant association between duration of ART and HPV prevalence (P-value = 0.206; OR = 0.921) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study showed a high prevalence of HPV infection 76.25% in women living with HIV, which is close to the findings of Faye et al., who reported a 78.95% prevalence of HPV in HIV- positive women in Senegal [28]. Other studies have reported prevalence rates of 36.9% [29] and 70.3% [30]. Due to the high prevalence of HPV in our study and considering that none of the patients participating in this study had received the HPV vaccine, it is recommended for the general population especially in at-risk populations to use vaccination against HPV.

The most common HR-HPV genotypes were HPV39 (37.70%) and HPV52 (36.56%). Other HR-HPV genotypes detected included HPV73 (13.11%), HPV16 (9.83%), HPV18 (8.19%), HPV45 (8.19%) and HPV68 (8.19%). The prevalence of HR-HPV genotypes 16 and 18 were the fourth and fifth most common HR-HPV genotypes in our study, respectively. However, the low prevalence of these common types may be due to the small sample size in our study. In contrast, HPV16 and HPV18 were among the most common genotypes in HIV-infected women reported in the studies by Aziz et al. [29] and Camargo et al. [31].

Among the 18 common HR-HPV genotypes analyzed, HPV26 was the only HR-HPV genotype not found in our study. Similar, HPV26 was also not detected among HIV-infected women in the study by Faye et al. [28].

We found that the most common LR-HPV genotype was HPV44 (13.11%). Other LR-HPV genotypes, such as HPV6 (3.27%) and HPV11 (1.63%) were the second and third most common LR-HPV genotypes, respectively. In the study reported by Aziz et al., HPV11 (6.15%) was the most common LR-HPV genotype [29].

HIV-induced immunodeficiency in HIV-infected women leads to a reduced ability to clear HPV, increasing the likelihood of persistent HPV infections, especially multiple HPV infections with various HPV genotypes, in this group of patients [28].

The prevalence of multiple HR-HPV infections in HIV-infected women was 71.42%, Which is close to 78.70% reported by Camargo and colleagues in Colombia [31].

Cervical cytology results showed that only six of the HIV-infected women had abnormal cervical cytology (7.5%). This may be due to the high level of CD4 + T lymphocyte count (≥ 500 cells/mm3) in most patients (56/80) in our study (Table 3). However, we observed a significant association (P-value = 0.026) between abnormal cervical cytology and HR-HPV genotypes, and HPV52 followed by HPV39 and HPV73, was found in cases of HSIL. Similar to our study, other studies have also reported abnormal cervical cytology results in HIV-infected women [13, 31] However, the frequency abnormal cervical cytology in HIV-infected women in those study was higher than that reported in ours.

A significant association between HPV prevalence and the age group of patients (38 to 45 years) was reported by Behzadi et al.; in HIV-infected women in Fars province, Iran [20]. Similar to the study by Behzadi et al.; most of the women living with HIV (81.81%) participating in our study were between 36 and 45 years old (Table 3), which could be due to increased sexual activity in this age group. In addition, we observed a somewhat significant association between the age of patients and the prevalence of HPV (P-value = 0.051) (Table 4).

Other socio-demographic findings such as marital status and education level of women living with HIV were analyzed in this study. More than half of the patients participating in our study were married. However, the highest prevalence of HPV (80%) was detected in the group of single HIV-infected women, though this association was not statistically significant. Similar to our findings, a high prevalence of HPV in single HIV-infected women (63.64%) was reported in a study by Tartaglia and colleagues in central and eastern Italy [2]. Increased sexual activity and multiple sexual partners in this group may raise the risk of contracting HPV infections [28].

The results also showed that most of the women living with HIV who participated in our study had a high school education, and a high prevalence of HPV (75.71%) was observed in these group. Similar to our finding, the study by Tartaglia et al. reported a significant association between HPV prevalence (54.17%) and a high school education level in central and eastern Italy [2].

The association between HPV prevalence and other variables such as history of smoking, age at first sexual intercourse, and years of ART use in women living with HIV was evaluated in our study, but no statistically significant association was observed.

Finally, association between CD4 + T lymphocyte count and the prevalence of HPV infections in HIV-infected women was analyzed. A decrease in CD4 + T lymphocyte count, especially below 200 cells/mm3, in HIV-infected patients leads to impaired host immune responses and increases the risk of HPV and multiple HPV infections in these patients [2, 6, 31]. In the current study, we did not observe a significant association between this variable and HPV prevalence. Due to the patients’ long-term use of antiretroviral therapies (≥ 11 years), most of them had a CD4 + T lymphocyte counts above 500 cells/mm3, and HPV prevalence in these patients was 76.78%. In contrast to our finding, another study reported a significant association between low CD4 + T lymphocyte count and the prevalence of HPV in HIV-infected women [2].

In the current study, we faced had some limitations. All women participating in our study were HIV-positive. If we had compared the prevalence of HPV genotypes between HIV-infected women and HIV-negative women, we might have achieved more comprehensive results. Additionally, due to the limitations in sample size, which may explain differences between our findings and those of other studies. Our results indicate a high prevalence of HPV among HIV-infected patients, emphasizing the importance of education on condom use and the necessity of vaccination.

Conclusion

In the current study, we observed a high prevalence of HPV (76.25%) and multiple HR- HPV genotypes (71.42%) among HIV-infected women. Abnormal cervical cytology was detected exclusively in women with HR-HPV genotypes. HPV39 and HPV52 identified as the most common genotypes in our study, respectively. Since HPV vaccination is not part of Iran’s national routine immunization program, the need to include at least immunocompromised groups with high-risk sexual behaviors in the national vaccination strategy is increasingly evident. Given the HPV strains identified in this study, vaccines covering these strains are of particular importance. Similar to the nonavalent HPV vaccine, which can cover more genotypes, particularly HPV 52 that showed a high prevalence in our study. The use of the quadrivalent vaccine in Iran might explain the high prevalence of non-16 and non-18 strains, as some individuals may have received this vaccine, potentially leading to a higher circulation of other strains in the community and this highlights the importance of a larger study with a higher sample size.
