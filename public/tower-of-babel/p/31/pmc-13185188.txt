Late-onset dyskeratosis congenita due to a TERC (n.269G > C) variant—first reported case from Indonesia: a case report
Authors: Benedreky Leo, Intan Hartandy, Susanna Hilda Hutajulu, Mardiah Suci Hardianti, Kartika Widayati, Johan Kurnianda, Ibnu Purwanto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDyskeratosis congenita (DC) is a rare inherited bone marrow failure syndrome caused by defective telomere maintenance. It often presents with mucocutaneous features, cytopenias, and progressive organ involvement, but remains underrecognized in resource-limited settings.Case presentationWe report a 32-year-old Indonesian male of Javanese ethnicity who presented with progressive anemia, thrombocytopenia, and bilateral hip pain. Physical examination revealed long-standing reticulate hyperpigmentation and premature graying. Bone marrow evaluation showed aplastic anemia, requiring periodical packed red cells transfusions. Telomere length testing demonstrated markedly shortened telomeres, and genetic analysis identified a heterozygous TERC mutation (n.269G > C) in both the patient and his mother. The patient also developed avascular necrosis of both hips, requiring bilateral hip replacement.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of considering telomere biology disorders such as DC in patients with unexplained cytopenias and premature aging features. Early recognition is essential to guide appropriate management and avoid potentially harmful therapies, particularly in resource-limited settings where these conditions may be underrecognized.

Introduction

Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is a rare inherited disorder caused by genetic mutations that impair telomere maintenance [1]. It affects 1 in 100,000,000 individuals, with a male predominance [2, 3]. Although it is classically characterized by a triad of mucocutaneous features—reticulate hyperpigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia—its clinical presentation can be highly variable, particularly in adult-onset or atypical forms [4]. This phenotypic variability reflects the underlying genetic heterogeneity [5]. We report the case of an adult Asian male presenting with cytopenias and musculoskeletal complications, who was ultimately diagnosed with DC via genetic testing. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of DC reported from Indonesia.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old Indonesian male of Javanese ethnicity presented with worsening fatigue and bilateral hip pain. Laboratory evaluation revealed hemoglobin of 7 g/dL and a platelet count of 40 × 10⁹ cells/L. White blood cell count remained within normal range. He reported progressive fatigue since the age of 28, approximately 2 months after receiving the second dose of the Sinovac COVID-19 vaccine, though no direct causality was established. At symptom onset, laboratory testing demonstrated severe anemia with a hemoglobin level of 2 g/dL and thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 23 × 10⁹/L. The patient received packed red cell (PRC) transfusions, resulting in an increase in hemoglobin to 9 g/dL. During subsequent follow-up, hemoglobin levels gradually declined to 6–7 g/dL, necessitating PRC transfusions approximately every 6 months. Platelet counts remained relatively stable in the range of 30–50 × 10⁹ cells/L, and he never exhibited clinical bleeding or received platelet transfusions or other therapies. White blood cell counts were generally within normal limits, although intermittent decreases to as low as 1.9 × 10⁹ cells/L were observed, followed by spontaneous recovery.

Physical examination revealed prominent reticulate hyperpigmentation over the thorax, abdomen, neck, and lower extremities, reportedly present since age 11. He also had diffuse graying of the scalp hair, contributing to an older-than-chronological appearance. He denied oral leukoplakia or nail dystrophy (Fig. 1a–c). Over the past year, he developed progressive bilateral hip pain and was eventually unable to walk. Bone marrow aspiration revealed hypocellular marrow, consistent with aplastic anemia. Family history revealed that both his mother and maternal grandmother had similar hyperpigmented reticular skin lesions beginning during adolescence (around 14 years of age), but without hematological symptoms. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) telomere length testing performed on peripheral blood samples revealed markedly shortened telomeres in both the patient (5.4 kb at 32 years of age) and his mother (4.5 kb at 60 years of age), both below the 10th percentile for age. Genetic testing using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel for dyskeratosis congenita—including ACD, NHP2, RTEL1, TPP1, CTC1, NOP10, TERT, WRAP53, DKC1, PARN, TINF2, TERC, USB1, NAF1, AK2, and POT1—also performed on blood samples, identified a heterozygous TERC gene mutation (n.269G > C) in both the patient and his mother, confirming the diagnosis of DC. Testing of other asymptomatic family members was not performed. The grandmother was deceased and thus not available for further testing. The patient remains clinically stable on supportive care, with ongoing need for periodic PRC transfusions. Discussion

This case highlights the heterogeneous presentation of dyskeratosis congenita (DC), particularly in adults. Although the classic mucocutaneous triad was incomplete, the combination of early-onset reticulate pigmentation, premature graying, family history, and bone marrow failure raised suspicion for a telomere biology disorder, particularly DC. Telomere length analysis in both the patient and his mother revealed markedly shortened telomeres. Subsequent genetic testing using a NGS panel identified a heterozygous TERC gene mutation (n.269G > C) in both individuals, confirming the diagnosis of DC. To the best of our knowledge, this variant has not been previously reported in association with dyskeratosis congenita.

TERC, the RNA template component of the telomerase complex, is essential for telomere elongation and maintenance [6, 7]. It is particularly critical in highly proliferative tissues such as the bone marrow, skin, and gastrointestinal epithelium [8]. Deficiency or mutation in TERC leads to accelerated telomere shortening, cellular senescence, and genomic instability, which underlies the pathogenesis of telomere biology disorders such as DC [9–11].

TERC mutations, typically inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, often exhibit variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. They are also associated with later onset and progressive telomere shortening across generations, leading to more severe symptoms in children than in their parents, as demonstrated by the asymptomatic mother and grandmother who experienced hyperpigmentation without other symptoms [5, 12–14].

TERC mutations give rise to a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, including aplastic anemia, dental and hair loss, aseptic necrosis of the femoral head, abnormal skin pigmentation, premature hair graying, liver cirrhosis, hearing loss, hyperhidrosis, hypogonadism, pulmonary fibrosis, and malignancy—several of which were observed in our patient and his mother [11, 12]. Consistent with prior reports, bone marrow failure and abnormal skin pigmentation represent two of the most frequently observed clinical features in individuals with TERC mutations and were prominent findings in our patient’s family, supporting the pathogenic relevance of the identified variant [11–14]. In vivo studies further support these clinical observations by demonstrating that reduced TERC expression disrupts the function of bone marrow skeletal stem/progenitor cells, leading to premature cellular senescence, impaired hematopoietic support, and skeletal complications such as osteopenia, avascular necrosis, and increased fracture risk [15, 16].

The phenotypic spectrum associated with TERC mutations contrasts with the classical triad of dyskeratosis congenita—nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, and oral leukoplakia—which is more commonly linked to DKC1 mutations and X-linked inheritance [17–19]. The inheritance pattern significantly influences disease severity in dyskeratosis congenita; X-linked forms are generally associated with earlier onset and more severe clinical manifestations than autosomal dominant disease [13]. Autosomal recessive DC is less common and is clinically more similar to X-linked disease [20, 21]. The marked genetic heterogeneity of DC contributes to its wide-ranging clinical presentations and highlights the critical importance of genetic testing, as reliance on clinical findings alone may lead to misdiagnosis.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of DC in Indonesia. The scarcity of data from Indonesia is likely due to limited access to specialized diagnostic tools, such as telomere length analysis and next-generation sequencing. Currently, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative therapy for DC; however, it is not widely available in Indonesia [1]. Gene therapy and telomerase activation remain investigational [22]. Accordingly, the management of this patient remains supportive, focusing on transfusion support and orthopedic intervention. The main strength of this case report is the identification of a heterozygous TERC variant (n.269G > C), which expands the known mutational spectrum of TERC-associated DC. The main limitation of this case is that we were unable to perform telomere length analysis and genetic testing on the patient’s grandmother (deceased) and other asymptomatic relatives, which restricted the ability to fully assess the variant's segregation and penetrance within the family.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the clinical heterogeneity and diagnostic challenges of DC, particularly in the context of TERC mutation. The absence of the classical triad highlights the importance of a high index of suspicion, especially in patients presenting with unexplained bone marrow failure, premature aging features, and family history of similar findings. Telomere length analysis and genetic testing are mandatory for confirming the diagnosis. As the first reported case of DC in Indonesia, this report emphasizes the need to improve access to telomere biology diagnostics and raise awareness of telomere disorders in regions where such cases are underrecognized. In the absence of curative therapy such as HSCT, individualized supportive care remains the cornerstone of management.
