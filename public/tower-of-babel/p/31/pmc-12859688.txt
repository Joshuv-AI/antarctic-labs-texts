Molecular Detection and Genetic Diversity of Bat‐Associated Bartonella spp. in Bat Ectoparasites Collected From Ratchaburi Province, Thailand
Authors: Sakone Sunantaraporn, Supaporn Wacharapluesadee, Patarapol Maneeorn, Chatuthanai Savigamin, Luke W Rimmer, Nitis Rattanagitpaisan, Thanapat Pataradool, Rungfar Boonserm, Somchai Panphuangkaew, Prateep Duenkae, Padet Siriyasatien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionBats are known to harbour Bartonella bacteria, which can be found in several species of bats. Various ectoparasites can infest bats and harbour the bacteria, serving as important vectors for diverse Bartonella species. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and diversity of Bartonella spp. in bat ectoparasites, as well as to identify the species of ectoparasites involved.MethodsBat ectoparasites were collected from four species of insectivorous bats in Ratchaburi, central Thailand. The ectoparasites were identified using the Cytochrome oxidase subunit I (COI) gene (bat flies, fleas and bugs) and the 16S rRNA gene (mites and ticks). Bartonella was screened and sequenced using the rpoB and gltA genes, followed by phylogenetic relationships and haplotype diversity analysis.ResultsA total of 106 bat ectoparasites were identified, including Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp., Nycteribia sp. (bat flies), and other ectoparasites such as Spinturnix sp. (mites), Cimex sp. (bugs), Ixodes sp. (ticks) and Orchopeas sp. (fleas). The detection of Bartonella, based on rpoB and/or gltA, was positive in 48 specimens (45.28%) of Phthiridium sp. (32.08%, n = 34), Raymondia sp. (9.43%, n = 10), Nycteribia sp. (1.89%, n = 2), Spinturnix sp. (0.94%, n = 1) and Cimex sp. (0.94%, n = 1). Phylogenetic analysis of Bartonella rpoB sequences revealed the presence of six distinct lineages (I–VI), whereas five lineages (I–V) were identified based on gltA sequences. Genetic diversity analysis showed high diversity, shared between bat hosts and their ectoparasites.ConclusionsThis study represents the first report of Bartonella spp. detected in bat ectoparasites in Thailand, thereby providing novel molecular evidence of Bartonella diversity and circulation within bat ectoparasite populations.Keywords: Bartonella, bat fly, ectoparasites, genetic diversity, Thailand

Introduction

Bats are flying mammals belonging to the order Chiroptera, with approximately 1432 species identified worldwide (Buckles 2015). These play a significant role in ecosystems, particularly as potential reservoirs for several viruses that pose threats to both humans and animals. Notably, bats have been implicated as hosts for viruses such as MERS‐CoV, Nipah virus, SARS‐CoV, Hendra virus, Ebola virus and the current SARS‐CoV‐2 virus, responsible for the ongoing COVID‐19 pandemic (Ruiz‐Aravena et al. 2022). In addition to their role in viral transmission, bats are also known to harbour various bacteria and protozoa that are of public health concern (Dhivahar et al. 2023). It is hypothesized that bats act as potential reservoirs for many important bacterial species, including Leptospira sp., Rickettsia sp., haemotropic Mycoplasma, Coxiella burnetii and Bartonella sp. (Mühldorfer 2013). Furthermore, they have been linked to protozoan parasites such as Toxoplasma gondii, Leishmania sp. and Trypanosoma sp. (Chomel et al. 2014), which can cause human and animal diseases. The diversity of pathogens carried by bats underscores their complex role in the transmission dynamics of zoonotic diseases.

Bartonellosis is an emerging infectious disease of zoonotic origin, caused by facultative intracellular alpha‐proteobacteria belonging to the genus Bartonella (Breitschwerdt et al. 2010). This disease is transmitted by a wide range of blood‐sucking insects (Breitschwerdt et al. 2010; Breitschwerdt 2014). Several Bartonella species are crucial to human infections, including B. henselae, B. quintana and B. bacilliformis (Karem et al. 2000). Evidence has recently emerged of bat‐associated Bartonella infections in humans and animals, such as Candidatus Bartonella mayotimonensis (Veikkolainen et al. 2014; Lilley et al. 2017). Bats act as reservoir hosts for various Bartonella species, capable of harbouring and transmitting these bacteria without exhibiting symptoms of illness. Several Bartonella species, including B. bacilliformis, B. henselae and B. clarridgeiae, have been identified in bats, with transmission occurring through vectors such as fleas or ticks that infest these animals (Karem et al. 2000). Although humans are not typically infected directly by bats, the bacteria can be spread through these vectors, and in rare cases, humans may be exposed through direct contact with bat saliva, urine or faeces (Allocati et al. 2016).

Hematophagous ectoparasites, including bat flies, fleas, ticks and bugs, have been documented as infesting a range of bat species (Stuckey et al. 2017; Szentiványi et al. 2019). Bat flies have been shown to act as vectors for the Rift Valley fever virus, Coronavirus and the Plasmodium parasite, thereby transmitting these agents to bats (Szentiványi et al. 2019; Phumee et al. 2025). Bat flies are obligately hematophagous insects belonging to the families Nycteribiidae and Streblidae, which are specific to bats. Their morphology is distinctive, exhibiting a flattened, spider‐like shape and the absence of eyes or wings (Dick and Patterson 2006). Several studies have demonstrated that bat ectoparasites can act as vectors for a range of zoonotic pathogens, including Bartonella bacteria (do Amaral et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2025).

In Thailand, however, the knowledge of Bartonella in bats remains limited. Two reports have been published on the detection of Bartonella bacteria in bats in different regions (McKee et al. 2017; Poofery et al. 2022). However, the presence of these bacteria in ectoparasites remains to be elucidated. The present study aims to (i) detect Bartonella bacteria in bat ectoparasites based on two genetic markers, including rpoB and gltA genes, (ii) investigate the genetic diversity of Bartonella in ectoparasites and (iii) identify the species of ectoparasites based on mitochondrial DNA markers. The findings of this study may contribute to an understanding of the relationships of Bartonella bacteria, bat ectoparasites and bat hosts.

Materials and Methods

Bat Ectoparasite Specimen Collection

Bat sampling was conducted during the rainy season, between October 2021 and August 2022, at Khao Chong Phran cave, located in the Photharam district of Ratchaburi Province (13°43′06.88″ N, 99°46′22.11″ E) (Figure 1). Bats were captured using mist nets and harp traps set near roosting sites and at the cave entrance between 6.00 and 7.00 PM. Species identification was performed on‐site based on morphological characteristics, after which the bats were released. The ectoparasites (bat flies, mites, ticks, fleas and bugs) were removed using forceps and placed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing a 70% ethanol solution. All specimens were subsequently submitted to the Center of Excellence in Vector Biology and Vector‐Borne Disease, Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, for photography and further DNA extraction.

Map of Thailand and the collection sites, bat species and bat ectoparasites composition from Ratchaburi province (https://d‐maps.com/).

Genomic DNA Extraction

To avoid external contamination, each insect was surface‐sterilized prior to DNA extraction using established protocols (La Scola et al., 2001), and the specimens were subsequently rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. Each specimen was subjected to lysis in a solution of 200 µL of lysis buffer CL (GeneAll, Seoul, Korea) with 20 µL of proteinase K (FAVORGEN, Ping Tung, Taiwan), followed by grinding with a sterilized plastic pestle. The specimens were then incubated at 56°C for 16 h. Genomic DNA was extracted from individual ectoparasites using the Genti32 automated nucleic acid extraction method. The DNA concentrations of each specimen were determined using a Nanodrop 2000c (Thermo Scientific, USA). The extracted genomic DNA was stored at –20°C for an extended period until required for use.

Molecular Identification of Bat Ectoparasites

In this study, the ectoparasites (bat flies, bugs and fleas) were identified at the genus level based on molecular characterization. A PCR was conducted to amplify the partial mitochondrial cytochrome C oxidase subunit I (COI) gene using the primers LCO1490 (5′‐GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG‐3′) and HCO2198 (5′‐TAAACTTCAGGG TGACCAAAAAATCA‐3′) (Folmer et al. 1994). The PCR was conducted under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min, followed by 5 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 45°C for 40 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min. This was then followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 51°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min. The final extension was conducted at 72°C for 10 min. PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 25 µL, comprising Green Hot Start PCR Master Mix Direct‐Load 2× (Biotechrabbit, Berlin, Germany), 10 µM of each primer, and 2 µL of the DNA template. The successful amplification of the COI gene was determined by the presence of a band at approximately 700 base pairs (bp).

Ticks and mites were identified using PCR based on the 16S rRNA gene of the mitochondrial DNA with primer sets 16S+1 and 16S−1 (Black and Piesman 1994). PCR master mix was prepared in a final volume of 25 µL, which contained 12.5 µL of Green Hot Start PCR Master Mix Direct‐Load 2× (Biotechrabbit, Berlin, Germany), 8.5 µL of deionized water, 2 µL of DNA template, and 0.6 µL of each 10 µM primer concentration. PCR conditions were 94°C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of 94°C for 15 s, 55°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 7 min. The PCR amplicon is about 401–406 bp (Wattanamethanont et al. 2018).

Detection of Bartonella DNA in Bat Ectoparasites

Bartonella bacteria were screened in all DNA specimens based on two housekeeping genetic markers: rpoB (RNA polymerase β subunit) (Oksi et al. 2013) and gltA (Citrate synthase) (Norman et al. 1995) genes. Conventional PCR was performed in a final volume of 25 µL; the PCR mixture contained 5 µL of DNA templates, 12.5 µL of Green Hot Start PCR Master Mix Direct‐Load 2× (Biotechrabbit, Berlin, Germany) and 0.5 µL of 10 µM of each primer. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C and 57°C for rpoB and gltA genes for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min; and the final extension at 72°C for 7 min (Promrangsee et al. 2019). The PCR products were separated on a 1.5% (W/V) agarose gel electrophoresis, and the expected PCR bands (452 bp for rpoB and 379 bp for gltA genes) were visualized with Quantity One Quantification Analysis Software Version 4.5.2 (Gel DocEQ System; Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) after being stained with ethidium bromide. In this study, Bartonella sp. DNA detected from cattle lice by Promrangsee et al. (2019) was used as a positive control, whereas deionized distilled water was used as a negative control in each assay.

Nucleotide Cloning, Purification and Sanger Sequencing

All positive amplified products for rpoB and gltA of Bartonella were ligated into the pGEM‐T Easy Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) by using rapid DNA ligation kits (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Ligated plasmids were used for the transformation into the competent cell (Escherichia coli DH5α) by heat shock technique at 42°C according to the protocol recommended (Promega, Mandison, WI, USA). The recombinant plasmid DNAs were screened by blue–white colonies selection. The white colonies suspected to contain the inserted gene were cultured. The plasmid DNA was then extracted using the FavorgenPrep Plasmid Extraction Mini Kit (FAVORGEN, Ping Tung, Taiwan) following the manufacturer's instructions. The purified plasmid DNA was sequenced by the commercial service at Macrogen Inc., South Korea.

For species confirmation of ectoparasites, the amplified products were purified using the FavorgenPrep GEL/PCR Purification Kit (FAVORGEN, Ping Tung, Taiwan). Direct sequencing was carried out using the corresponding forward and reverse primers for COI and 16S rRNA amplification by Macrogen Inc., South Korea.

Phylogenetic Construction, Splits Tree Network, Haplotype Network and Genetic Diversity Analysis

The obtained sequences were subjected to manual editing and alignment using BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor Version 7.2.5 (Hall 1999). The consensus nucleotide sequences were then compared to previously published sequence data in the GenBank database using the BLAST tool online (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The phylogenetic tree was constructed by employing maximum likelihood (ML) analysis and a bootstrap test with 1000 replications in MEGA software version 11 (Tamura et al. 2021). The phylogenetic tree based on COI sequences of bat flies was reconstructed using the General Time Reversible model with gamma‐distributed rate variation (GTR+G). For Bartonella, phylogenetic relationships were inferred from rpoB and gltA sequences alongside reference sequences retrieved from GenBank. Model selection identified the Hasegawa–Kishino–Yano model incorporating gamma‐distributed rate heterogeneity and a proportion of invariant sites (HKY+G+I) as the best‐fitting substitution model for rpoB, whereas the Tamura 3‐parameter model with gamma‐distributed rate variation (T92+G) was determined to be the most appropriate model for gltA. The tree was edited by FigTree version 1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/fig‐tree). The network phylogeny was performed using Splitstree CE version 6.2.2 with the Neighbour‐net method. The DnaSP version 6 software (Rozas et al. 2017) was utilized to calculate genetic polymorphism, including the number of haplotypes (H), the number of variable sites (S), the average number of nucleotide differences (k), the haplotype diversity (Hd), and the nucleotide diversity (π). The construction of the haplotype network was facilitated by the Population Analysis with Reticulate Trees (PopART) version 1.7 (Leigh and Bryant 2015), employing the TCS (Templeton–Crandall–Sing) method (Clement et al. 2002).

Statistical Analysis

The prevalence rate was calculated from PCR‐positive specimens using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corp., USA). The prevalence rate was calculated by dividing the number of positive specimens by the total number of specimens in each species, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were generated. A chi‐square test was performed in SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., USA) to evaluate the association between bat species, bat ectoparasite and Bartonella detection. Statistical significance was defined as a p < 0.05.

Results

Bat Ectoparasites Identification

A total of 300 bats were sampled across four insectivorous bat species: Chaerephon plicatus (Wrinkle‐lipped free‐tailed bat) (n = 99), Hipposideros larvatus (Intermediate Leaf‐nosed bat) (n = 61), Rhinolophus coelophyllus (Croslet horseshoe bat) (n = 12) and Rhinolophus pusillus (Least horseshoe bat) (n = 128). Of these, 72 bats (24.00%) were infested with ectoparasites (Table 1), with R. pusillus being the species most commonly infested. A total of 106 bat ectoparasites were collected from the 72 infested bats, including bat flies (n = 98), bugs (n = 4), ticks (n = 2), mites (n = 1) and fleas (n = 1) (Table 1). Molecular identification of bat flies based on COI sequencing revealed that they belonged to the families Nycteribiidae (77.55%, n = 74/98) and Streblidae (22.45%, n = 21/98). BLAST analysis of the nucleotide sequences indicated that the bat flies exhibited 95%–96% identity with Phthiridium szechuanum (accession no. NC_068222), 97.48% identity with Nycteribiidae sp. A (accession no. OQ184568) from Hong Kong and 90.06%–94.66% identity with Raymondia sp. A (accession no. OQ301747), also from Hong Kong. As all sequence identities were below the commonly accepted 98% threshold for species‐level identification, the bat flies in this study were assigned only to the genus level: Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp.

A phylogenetic tree based on partial COI sequences was constructed to demonstrate the classification of bat flies (Figure 3). The tree revealed that Phthiridium sp. was divided into two clades with a bootstrap support value of 90%. All Nycteribia sp. sequences were closely related to Nycteribiidae sp. A from Hong Kong (accession number OQ184568) with a bootstrap value of 98%, and Raymondia sp. was genetically close to Raymondia sp. A from Hong Kong (accession number OQ184568). The intraspecific divergence analysis showed that Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. exhibited higher intraspecific genetic divergence (12.67% for Phthiridium sp. and 6.61% for Raymondia sp.), whereas Nycteribia sp. exhibited the lowest intraspecific genetic divergence (0.53%). The identification of bugs based on the COI gene revealed 87.63% nucleotide similarity with Cimex hemipterus (accession number MG770889). A comparative analysis of the 16S rRNA genes of mites was conducted using BLASTn, which identified a close match to Spinturnix myoti (accession number FJ225946), exhibiting 83.08% nucleotide similarity. Ticks were demonstrated 95.92% (RABT1) and 95.54% (RABT2) of 16S rRNA nucleotide similarity with Ixodes kopsteini (accession number U95890).

Molecular Screening of Bartonella sp. by rpoB and gltA Amplification

Conventional PCR was used to screen bat ectoparasites for the presence of Bartonella DNA. In total, 48 specimens (45.28%, 95% CI: 36.14–54.76) tested positive among Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp., Nycteribia sp., Spinturnix sp. and Cimex sp., based on amplification of the rpoB and/or gltA genes. Screening with the rpoB marker detected Bartonella in 36.79% (95% CI: 28.21–46.30) of specimens, whereas 20.75% (95% CI: 14.06–29.50) were positive using the gltA marker. Notably, Nycteribia sp., Spinturnix sp. and Cimex sp. produced no PCR amplification for the gltA gene (Table 2). The analysis did not reveal a statistically significant association between bat species and Bartonella detection (χ 2 = 6.661, df = 3, p = 0.0835). Although R. pusillus exhibited a higher proportion of positive specimens compared with the other species, the overall variation did not reach statistical significance, likely due in part to the small sample sizes of R. coelophyllus, H. larvatus and C. plicatus. In order to ascertain whether there was a difference in the positivity of Bartonella PCR among bat ectoparasite species, a chi‐square analysis was performed, which revealed a statistically significant association between ectoparasite species and Bartonella detection (χ 2 = 23.39, df = 6, p = 0.0007). This finding indicates that Bartonella infection was not uniformly distributed across ectoparasite taxa. It is noteworthy that Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. exhibited comparatively higher proportions of Bartonella‐positive specimens, whereas Ixodes sp., Orchopeas sp. and Nycteribia sp. showed little to no detection.

Phylogenetic Analyses of Bartonella sp. in Bat Ectoparasites

Phylogenetic analysis of the rpoB sequences revealed the presence of six distinct lineages (I–VI) (Figure 4). The lineage I, which was the most prevalent, comprised 16 Bartonella sp. isolates from Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp. Lineage II consists of isolates from nine sequences of bat flies (Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp.) and one sequence of bat mite specimens in our analysis. Lineages III and V consisted of bat fly specimens and sequences closely related to Bartonella isolated from bats in Thailand. Lineage IV consisted of a single sequence of Bartonella sp. isolated from bat bugs and sequences of Bartonella sp. isolated from bats in Thailand. Finally, lineage VI contained only three sequences of Phthiridium sp. isolates from this study. On the basis of the gltA sequences, the phylogenetic analysis classified our sequences into five distinct lineages. Lineages I and V were genetically close to Bartonella sp. detected in bats from Thailand and Vietnam, whereas lineages II and IV were grouped with Bartonella sp. reported in bats from Thailand. Lineage III formed a separated group with two sequences from other reference gltA sequences (Figure 5). Furthermore, the splits tree network from rpoB and gltA (Figure 6) sequences exhibited a relative distribution of Bartonella lineage with the identical pattern observed in the ML phylogenetic tree.

Genetic Diversity and Haplotype Analysis

The TCS haplotype network analysis was performed based on rpoB and gltA sequences (Figures 7 and 8). Of the 39 rpoB sequences, 35 haplotypes were identified, with an overall high haplotype diversity of 0.991 and a relatively low nucleotide diversity of 0.07574. Twenty‐four distinct haplotypes were identified in Phthiridium sp., seven in Raymondia sp., two in Nycteribia sp. and one each in Cimex sp. and Spinturnix sp. Subsequent analysis of 22 gltA sequences identified 15 distinct haplotypes, exhibiting an overall high haplotype diversity of 0.926 and a low nucleotide diversity of 0.06947. Phthiridium sp. was identified into 12 haplotypes, whereas Raymondia sp. was identified within 4 haplotypes. Furthermore, haplotype analysis indicated that Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. were shared within Haplotype 2 (Table 3 and Figure 8). The haplotype network revealed that the majority of Bartonella haplotypes identified in ectoparasites were distributed across two bat hosts: R. pusillus and R. coelophyllus (for rpoB and gltA), and R. pusillus and H. larvatus (for rpoB).

Discussion

The present study reports the molecular detection and genetic diversity of Bartonella spp. in bat ectoparasites collected from four bat species, including C. plicatus, H. larvatus, R. coelophyllus and R. pusillus. Species identification of ectoparasites from this study was not performed based on morphological character; however, molecular identification was used to confirm their species. The molecular identification of bat flies was based on the COI gene, which demonstrated that bat ectoparasites consisted of two families of Nycteribiidae and Streblidae. Previous research has surveyed and identified bat fly species, consisting of 43 Nycteribiidae and 16 Streblidae, which were associated with 63 bat species in Thailand (Samoh et al. 2021). The bat flies identified in this study were classified into three genera according to their phylogenetic classification of COI sequences: Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp. Among these, Phthiridium sp. was identified as the most prevalent bat fly. Furthermore, Raymondia sp. and Phthiridium sp. were found to infest bat species, including H. larvatus and R. pusillus. This finding aligns with the observations from previous studies, which reported that Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. infested R. pusillus specimens from Hong Kong (Poon et al. 2023). Furthermore, Nycteribia sp. collected from R. coelophyllus was found to be closely related to Nycteribiidae sp., which was found in Rhinolophus affinis from Hong Kong (Poon et al. 2023). A previous report by Peng et al. (2024) demonstrated that Phthiridium sp. was infested in R. pusillus and Raymondia sp. found in Hipposideros dyacorum from east coast Malaysia. In Thailand, three genera of Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp. have been infested in several host bat species (Samoh et al. 2021). Other ectoparasites collected in this study were molecularly identified as Ixodes sp. (ticks), Orchopeas sp. (fleas) and Cimex sp. (bugs) from C. plicatus, while Spinturnix sp. (mites) from R. pusillus. Furthermore, co‐infestation of Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. in R. pusillus and Phthiridium sp. and Ixodes sp. in C. plicatus. It is noteworthy that the bat fly species and other ectoparasites identified in this study may represent a potentially novel species. This discovery offers valuable insights into the diversity of ectoparasites associated with bats and emphasizes the need for ongoing research into the ecological interactions between bats and their ectoparasites. Understanding these relationships could be critical in elucidating the transmission dynamics of zoonotic diseases, including those caused by Bartonella species.

In the present study, the prevalence rate of Bartonella was 45.28% (48/106) in bat ectoparasites, including bat flies (Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp.), mites (Spinturnix sp.) and bugs (Cimex sp.). Several studies have reported the diversity of Bartonella species in bat ectoparasites from different regions, such as bat flies in Madagascar (Brook et al. 2015), Zambia (Qiu et al. 2020) and Brazil (Franco et al. 2025). In Asia, bat flies have been identified as vectors of Bartonella species. A study by Han et al. (2022) demonstrated the presence of diverse Bartonella sp. in several bat fly species, including Penicillidia monoceros, Phthiridium sp. and Nycteribia sp. from China. Previous reports have revealed Bartonella species in the genera Nycteribia and Penicillidia from Japan (Nabeshima et al. 2020). Lee et al. (2021) detected Bartonella species in the family Nycteribiidae (Phthiridium sp., Nycteribia spp. and Penicillidia sp.), an unidentified Nycteribiidae species, and the family Streblidae (Brachytarsina sp.). More recently, the diversity of Bartonella species was demonstrated in both Nycteribiidae sp. and Streblidae sp. collected from Malaysia (Peng et al. 2024). The present study identified bat‐associated Bartonella species in the most prevalent bat fly species, namely, Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. This finding is consistent with the recent investigation by Kuang et al. (2022), which identified diverse Bartonella in Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp. in Yunnan Province, China.

In the present study, one specimen of mites (Spinturnix sp.) was found to be positive for Bartonella sp. Previous studies have demonstrated bat mites as a potential vector for the transmission of Bartonella to bat hosts (Hornok et al. 2012; Ikeda et al. 2020; Reeves et al. 2016). For instance, a study conducted in Poland revealed the presence of similar genotypes of Bartonella among bats and their S. myoti mites (Szubert‐Kruszyńska et al. 2019). Additionally, a study by Han et al. (2022) identified novel Bartonella genotypes of Bartonella sp. in both Spinturnix sp. and Eyndhovenia sp. in China. Recent research has also revealed the detection of Bartonella sp. in bat mites, specifically Spinturnicidae sp., collected in Yunnan Province, located in the southwestern region of China (Kuang et al. 2022). Furthermore, Bartonella sp. was detected in one specimen of bugs (Cimex sp.). Additionally, previous research has demonstrated the presence of Bartonella DNA in two specimens of bat bugs (Cimex pipistrelle) from Central Europe (Kejíková et al. 2022).

This study did not include confirmation of Bartonella infection in mammalian hosts, isolation and cultivation of the bacteria, or experimental evaluation of infectivity. Consequently, reliance solely on partial gene sequences detected in arthropods is insufficient for a robust assessment of zoonotic risk. Similar limitations have been reported in previous investigations, where Bartonella DNA was identified in a variety of ectoparasites without definitive evidence of their ability to maintain or transmit the pathogen. For example, studies in rodents and their associated ectoparasites demonstrated high Bartonella prevalence and considerable genotype diversity, while emphasizing the influence of ecological and temporal factors on host–arthropod infection dynamics rather than confirming transmission competence (Kamani et al. 2013; Gutiérrez et al. 2014). Likewise, Kabeya et al. (2010) detected Bartonella tamiae DNA in chigger mites and ticks but highlighted the need for bacterial isolation and experimental infection studies to establish biological relevance. Collectively, these findings underscore that PCR‐based detection alone cannot substantiate zoonotic transmission.

In this study, Bartonella was identified in bat ectoparasites based on two genetic markers, with sequence identities ranging from 88.42% to 98.89% for rpoB and from 91.56% to 99.74% for gltA. Additionally, it was determined that according to the gene‐sequence‐based criteria proposed by La Scola et al. (2003) for identifying novel Bartonella species, the cutoff values for the rpoB and gltA genes were set at 95.4% and 96.0%, respectively. Twenty‐two (22/39) of the rpoB sequences shared similarity below the cutoff value, whereas six (6/22) of the gltA sequences showed similarity scores less than the cutoff value. It is therefore assumed that the novel Bartonella sp. was present in our investigation. The gltA and rpoB genes are widely used molecular markers for the detection, identification and phylogenetic analysis of Bartonella species. The gltA gene, being highly conserved among Bartonella spp., is commonly employed for species‐level identification, whereas the rpoB gene provides greater discriminatory power due to its higher sequence variability (La Scola et al. 2003). In this study, PCR amplification targeting rpoB demonstrated good sensitivity and specificity for the detection of Bartonella spp. In contrast, gltA‐based PCR showed evidence of cross‐amplification with Providencia spp. in some specimens, indicating potential limitations in its specificity.

Phylogenetic analysis classified the Bartonella sp. into six lineages (I–VI) based on rpoB sequences and five lineages (I–V) based on gltA sequences. The Bartonella lineages based on rpoB were shared within lineage I by bat flies (Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp.) and within lineage II by bat flies (Phthiridium sp. and Raymondia sp.) and bat mites. (Spinturnix sp.), whereas the remaining lineages (III–VI) and all Bartonella lineages based on gltA sequences were shared by bats and ectoparasites, as previously reported in the GenBank database. It was demonstrated that bat/ectoparasites‐associated Bartonella lineages (McKee et al. 2016; Kuang et al. 2022). Previous studies have shown that Bartonella lineages or genotypes have a broad host range (Mhlanga et al. 2024). The Bartonella haplotype observed in this study appears to be shared among closely related host species of bat, specifically R. pusillus and R. coelophyllus, or R. pusillus and H. larvatus. This finding suggests that bats may play a pivotal role in the transmission, genetic evolution and diversification of Bartonella sp. (Han et al. 2022; Poofery et al. 2022). The genetic diversity of Bartonella detection in bat ectoparasites is higher than that of Bartonella in bats, as was previously reported (Judson et al. 2015). This study revealed the diversity of haplotypes in both the rpoB and gltA genes. The proliferation of this bacterium within the digestive tract of multiple hosts, including lice, fleas and ticks, has been demonstrated to facilitate interactions between disparate lineages or genotypes. This phenomenon has been shown to result in the occurrence of gene recombination, a process which is believed to be a contributing factor to haplotype diversity (Furquim et al. 2021; Sepúlveda‐García et al. 2022). The observation of gene recombination in Bartonella has led to the hypothesis that it may serve as an evolutionary strategy that contributes to the pathogenicity of the genus (Buffet et al. 2013). However, the limitation of the haplotype network and diversity analyses in this study is the small sample size derived from a single geographic location. Future research should incorporate larger sample sizes across multiple, geographically diverse areas to allow for more robust comparisons and a comprehensive understanding of genetic diversity and population structure. The role of bat ectoparasites as a reservoir of Bartonella bacteria remains unclear. Bat fly larvae have been observed to feed on the “milk” glands for larva development, which may in fact promote the vertical transmission of certain bacteria, including Arsenophonus, Wolbachia and Bartonella (Szentiványi et al. 2019). A study by Morse et al. (2012) demonstrated the presence of Bartonella sp. in the pupae stage and adult female bat flies, suggesting vertical transmission during the developmental stage and incriminating bat flies as potential reservoirs of Bartonella bacteria. Previous research reported the isolation of Bartonella from bat flies, thereby demonstrating the possibility of bat flies as vectors of Bartonella bacteria (Nabeshima et al. 2020).

The presence of Bartonella DNA in specimens may be attributed to environmental or handling contamination during specimen processing. To minimize this risk, ectoparasites were individually collected in separate sterile tubes, and their surfaces were decontaminated before DNA extraction, following established protocols (La Scola et al. 2001). Most of the ectoparasites collected in this study were observed in blood‐feeding or post‐blood‐feeding stages, suggesting they may have acquired Bartonella from infected bats. The detection of Bartonella DNA may reflect residual bacterial material within the ectoparasites’ gut contents acquired during feeding, rather than indicating that these arthropods are competent hosts capable of sustaining or transmitting the bacteria. This finding is consistent with several previous investigations that used PCR‐based detection of Bartonella DNA but were unable to confirm vector competence or true infection in other ectoparasites, including ticks and fleas (Billeter et al. 2008; Morick et al. 2010; Tsai et al. 2011; Reeves et al. 2015). Nevertheless, the detection of Bartonella DNA alone, without bacterial cultivation, cannot substantiate whether these arthropods are capable of maintaining, replicating or transmitting the pathogen. Further research, including bacterial cultivation and experimental infection studies involving Bartonella and their arthropod hosts, is required to determine their potential transmission capacity.

A limitation of the present study is the absence of bat blood specimens for Bartonella detection, which prevented comparative analyses of Bartonella infections in ectoparasites and their bat hosts from the same geographic areas. This limitation hinders our ability to elucidate the ecological relationships between hosts and ectoparasites and to determine whether identical Bartonella lineages circulate between them. Future studies should include both bat blood and ectoparasite specimens collected from the same locations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of host–vector–pathogen dynamics.

Conclusion

This study highlights the diversity of Bartonella lineages detected in obligate ectoparasites, including bat flies (Phthiridium sp., Raymondia sp. and Nycteribia sp.), mites (Spinturnix sp.) and bat bugs (Cimex sp.), collected from four species of insectivorous bats. PCR‐based detection of the rpoB and gltA genes revealed bat‐associated Bartonella spp. in bat ectoparasites for the first time in Thailand. Nevertheless, because these findings are based exclusively on molecular evidence, further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between Bartonella lineage diversity and pathogenicity in bats and their associated ectoparasites. The potential role of bat ectoparasites in Bartonella transmission will be more clearly defined through integrated epidemiological investigations, bacterial isolation and controlled laboratory infection experiments. Moreover, as this study was limited to ectoparasites collected from a single bat host location, future research should incorporate multiple sampling sites and a broader diversity of bat host species to better characterize the distribution and ecological relevance of Bartonella within bat‐associated systems.
