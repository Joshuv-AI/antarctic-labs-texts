A rare cause of renal abscess: Brucelloma
Authors: Jack Clarke, Imal Hemachandra, Robert Pickles, Avi Raman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genitourinary system is affected in 10 % of patients with Brucellosis infection. However, renal abscess (or Brucelloma) is remarkably rare. Introduction

Brucella is a Gram-negative bacterium that causes brucellosis, also known as Mediterranean fever. It is one of the most common zoonotic diseases worldwide with 4 known species able to infect humans: B. melitensis, B. canis, B. abortus and B. suis. A recent review found the most common manifestations of brucellosis to be fever (78 %), malaise (71 %) and fatigue (39 %)1,2 making presentation typically vague and diagnosis is normally delayed as a result. The most common organ systems to be affected are musculoskeletal and neurological, with the genitourinary system being affected in 10 % of individuals. The most common genitourinary manifestations are epididymitis or orchitis, with interstitial renal disease remaining remarkably rare, and renal abscess (or renal brucelloma), even rarer. Only 7 cases reports have been described in the English language literature, with this case being the first described in Australia.

Case

A 37-year-old male was referred from a rural care centre with a lesion in the anterior midpole of the left kidney, which had not been present on a non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of their lumbar spine 6 weeks prior during investigation of lower back pain. Their presenting complaint was 10 weeks of severe back pain associated with 5 weeks of fever, 20kg weight loss, left anterior thigh paraesthesia, arthralgia and lethargy. He had no relevant travel history. Bucella exposure was attributed to his recreational boar hunting. On arrival, temperature was 37.9 °C with otherwise normal observations. Physical examination demonstrated bilateral axillary lymphadenopathy, right upper quadrant abdominal tenderness, palpable hepatosplenomegaly with no skin wounds identified. White cell count was 6.1 x10ˆ9/L (normal), CRP 14 mg/L [ref: <5 mg/L] and eGFR >90 mL/min/1.73mˆ2.

CT chest, abdomen and pelvis revealed a 34 mm × 24 mm x 23 mm thick-walled cystic lesion in the anterior midpole of the left kidney with fat stranding in its vicinity (Fig. 1.) associated with a few abnormal sub-centimetre retroperitoneal lymph nodes. New hepatomegaly and splenomegaly were now noted. CT lumbar spine showed no spinal abscess or change in pre-existing spinal stenosis. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no valvular abnormalities or vegetations.

Brucella suis was isolated in 3 consecutive blood cultures confirming the diagnosis of subacute brucellosis. In retrospect, previous CT lumbar spine non-contrast 6 weeks earlier and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) spine without gadolinium 4 weeks earlier (Fig. 2) showed disc bulges with S1 nerve root compression and other changes that were retrospectively re-assessed as probable Brucella spondylodiscitis.

The patient was treated non-operatively with gentamicin 5mg/kg intravenously daily for 7 days, in combination with rifampicin 600 mg daily and doxycycline 100 mg twice a day. Rifampicin and doxycycline were continued on discharge for a 3-month course. Follow up MRI Spine with gadolinium after 6 weeks demonstrated interval bony erosion of the L5 and S1 endplates. He was otherwise asymptomatic, except for pruritis caused by his antibiotic therapy. 12 week follow up demonstrated stable changes at L5 and S1 and suppressed inflammatory markers at the time of completing antibiotics (normal serum C-reactive protein and blood white cell count). He will be followed up closely to detect relapse early, initially with monthly blood tests for inflammatory markers and repeat imaging of his spine and abdomen at 3 months after stopping antibiotics.

Discussion

There are around 500,000 cases of brucellosis annually worldwide,3 with lower socioeconomic countries having higher incidence. Typical transmission occurs after consumption of bovine or goat products infected with B. abortus or B. melitensis such as meat, milk and cheeses of these animals. Once a common abattoir associated disease In Australia, B. abortus has been eradicated nation-wide since 1989 through large-scale livestock inoculation programmes, with B. suis now the most prevalent strain.4 The annual incidence in Australia remains low at around 1.6 cases per 1,000,000 population with the most common method of transmission being contact of mucous membranes or abrasions of the skin whilst hunting or handling the carcasses of wild pigs. Our case represents the first reported case of renal brucelloma in Australia.

Muñiz-Suárez5 and Onaran6 report separate cases of patients with larger (42 mm diameter), more complex, renal brucelloma extending into surrounding tissue planes. These required treatment with simple nephrectomy and open surgical excision respectively. Both patients completed 6 weeks of rifampicin and doxycycline post-operatively, reporting to be asymptomatic at 6 weeks post-operatively. These cases were limited by absence of follow up past this point as recurrence typically happens months post initial treatment. The relapse rate is approximately 10 % in patients treated with 6 weeks of antibiotics.7

This case suggests brucelloma alone is not an absolute indication for drainage as previously postulated. Li8 reports the case of a 45-year-old male from China who underwent ultrasound guided aspiration of his renal brucelloma which demonstrated similar rate of reduction in abscess size from 32mm to 10 mm diameter at 4 months. After cessation of antibiotics at this point, there was recurrence of systemic brucellosis 6 months later. Although this was a different strain (B. melitensis) with minocycline and moxifloxacin being administered initially, and minocycline and rifampicin post-recurrence, it suggests antibiotic choice and duration play a more important role in treatment than surgical drainage in uncomplicated lesions.

One final interesting aspect of our case was observation of the timeframe of brucelloma formation with the appearance of normal renal parenchyma on non-contrast CT 6 weeks prior to abscess detection on repeat CT.

Extended combination antibiotic treatment with doxycycline and rifampicin forms the mainstay of treatment for renal brucelloma, with lesions that are complex or extending into the perinephric tissues requiring surgical excision or drainage. Cases that involve osteoarticular disease, neurobrucellosis or endocarditis require prolonged antibiotic courses of at least 3 months to minimise the risk of relapse.9 However, we propose that uncomplicated smaller renal lesions approximately 30mm in diameter and under, with no extracapsular extension, do not routinely require drainage, except for diagnostic purposes. Although rare, solid organ involvement should always be considered in patients with brucellosis.
