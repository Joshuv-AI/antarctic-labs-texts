PAX5 alleviates atherosclerosis by transcriptionally regulating GSDMD and inhibiting STAT3-mediated pyroptosis
Authors: Xiangkui Wang, Peipei Wu, Yadi Shen, Shiwei Xu, Qi Wan, Qilun Zhang, Ran Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to elucidate the role of PAX5 in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (AS) and its underlying mechanisms, with a particular focus on the transcriptional regulation of GSDMD and STAT3-mediated pyroptosis.MethodsSerum PAX5 and GSDMD levels were detected in AS patients and healthy controls. In vitro, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were stimulated with oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) and transfected with PAX5 overexpression vectors, followed by assays for cell viability, tube formation, lipid accumulation, LDH release, and pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β, IL-18) secretion. Immunofluorescence and dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed to verify the co-localization of PAX5 and GSDMD, and the transcriptional interaction between PAX5 and the GSDMD promoter. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) ubiquitination assays were conducted to determine whether PAX5 promotes GSDMD poly-ubiquitination. In vivo, ApoE⁻/⁻ AS mouse models were treated with AAV-mediated PAX5 overexpression, followed by histopathological assessment of atherosclerotic lesions, lipid metabolism detection, and Western blot analysis of pyroptosis and STAT3 signaling-related proteins.ResultsSerum PAX5 was significantly downregulated in AS patients, while GSDMD was upregulated. In vitro, PAX5 overexpression reversed ox-LDL-induced viability reduction, lipid accumulation, LDH release, and IL-1β/IL-18 hypersecretion in HUVECs. Dual-luciferase assay confirmed that PAX5 directly binds to and activates the GSDMD promoter. Ubiquitination assays demonstrated that PAX5 promotes both K48-linked and K63-linked poly-ubiquitination of GSDMD, with the degradation pathway predominating over transcriptional activation, resulting in a net decrease in GSDMD protein abundance. In vivo, PAX5 overexpression reduced atherosclerotic lesion size, lipid deposition, and systemic inflammatory levels, improved serum lipid profiles, and inhibited STAT3 phosphorylation, GSDMD-N generation, and pyroptosis-related protein expression.ConclusionPAX5 alleviates AS progression through a dual regulatory mechanism involving transcriptional activation and ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation of GSDMD, thereby inhibiting STAT3-associated pyroptosis. PAX5 represents a promising therapeutic target for AS intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00905-1.Keywords: PAX5, GSDMD, Atherosclerosis, Pyroptosis, STAT3

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic, progressive vascular disease marked by lipid-induced inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and the formation of atherosclerotic plaques, which constitute the primary pathological foundation for cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and stroke [1, 2]. Pathologically, AS is initiated by endothelial injury, which is followed by the subendothelial retention of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), the recruitment of immune cells, and the formation of foam cells. This sequence of events ultimately leads to plaque growth, vascular stenosis, and instability [3, 4]. Despite the effectiveness of lipid-lowering therapies and antiplatelet drugs, residual cardiovascular risk remains, underscoring the necessity to elucidate novel molecular mechanisms underlying AS progression [5].

Pyroptosis, a pro-inflammatory programmed cell death pathway mediated by gasdermin proteins, has been identified as a significant contributor to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis [6]. This form of cell death is characterized by the formation of pores in the cell membrane, resulting in cell lysis and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) [7, 8]. Gasdermin D (GSDMD), recognized as the primary effector of pyroptosis, undergoes cleavage by activated caspases (including the canonical Caspase-1 and the non-canonical human-specific Caspase-4/5, or their murine homolog Caspase-11), resulting in the production of N-terminal fragments that form membrane pores, thereby initiating cytokine release and cell lysis [9, 10]. A growing body of evidence indicates that GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis is upregulated in AS plaques, particularly within macrophages and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), where it contributes to plaque instability by intensifying inflammation and promoting the formation of necrotic cores [11, 12]. For example, ox-LDL, a well-established risk factor for atherosclerosis, has been demonstrated to induce pyroptosis in macrophages through the caspase-11-GSDMD pathway in murine models. This mechanism not only enhances the inflammatory environment within atherosclerotic plaques but also aggravates plaque instability, which is a pivotal factor in disease progression [13, 14]. Additionally, the expression levels of GSDMD and caspase-4/11 are elevated in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with coronary heart disease, showing a correlation with the severity of coronary artery lesions [15].

Paired-box gene 5 (PAX5), a member of the paired-box transcription factor family, is traditionally recognized as the pivotal regulator of B-cell lineage commitment, playing an essential role in B-cell development by activating B-cell-specific genes and repressing non-B-lineage genes [16, 17]. However, recent studies have elucidated the broader biological functions of PAX5 in non-hematopoietic diseases, such as cancer and metabolic disorders, where it contributes to disease pathogenesis and progression through the transcriptional regulation of target genes involved in cell proliferation, inflammatory responses, and metabolic processes. In relation to the association between PAX5 and atherosclerosis, current research has revealed context-dependent roles: the PAX5/ITGAX complex has been reported to modulate B-cell differentiation and vascular smooth muscle cell function, potentially influencing AS progression [18, 19]. Conversely, inhibition of HMGN2 SUMOylation mitigates atherosclerosis by enhancing PAX5 expression, which induces macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby exerting an atheroprotective effect [20, 21]. In conclusion, evidence from a range of studies indicates that PAX5 plays a complex role in both normal physiological processes and pathological conditions. Its involvement in immune regulation, tumor suppression, and gene expression modulation underscores PAX5 as a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. Elucidating the specific mechanisms through which PAX5 influences these processes could facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting atherosclerosis and associated cardiovascular diseases.

The signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. STAT3 phosphorylation is persistently elevated in AS lesions and promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules, thereby amplifying vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. Notably, STAT3 has been implicated in the regulation of pyroptosis-related gene expression, positioning it as a potential link between inflammatory signaling and pyroptosis execution in AS.

The present study aims to explore the role and molecular mechanisms of PAX5-mediated regulation of GSDMD in the progression of AS through a comprehensive three-tiered approach. Initially, clinical samples were collected to compare the levels of PAX5 and GSDMD between AS patients and healthy controls. Subsequently, in vitro experiments were performed to explore the effects of PAX5 overexpression on endothelial pyroptosis, GSDMD ubiquitination, and STAT3 signaling. Finally, animal models were established to evaluate the impact of AAV-mediated PAX5 overexpression on AS lesion development. Together, these investigations aim to elucidate a novel PAX5-GSDMD-STAT3 regulatory axis in AS, thereby providing new insights into AS pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Patient sample collection and processing

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third People’s Hospital of Bengbu City (Ethics Approval No. K14 [2025]). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In this study, patients diagnosed with AS and healthy volunteers were consecutively recruited from The Third People’s Hospital of Bengbu City between June 2024 and December 2024. AS patients were confirmed by coronary angiography with at least one major coronary artery stenosis of ≥ 50%. The inclusion criteria for the AS group were: (1) age 40–75 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of atherosclerosis by coronary angiography; and (3) no prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) within the past 6 months. The exclusion criteria were: (1) acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina within the past 3 months; (2) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction (ALT > 3× upper limit of normal, or eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2); (3) active infection, autoimmune diseases, or malignancy; (4) current use of immunosuppressive agents or corticosteroids; and (5) refusal to provide informed consent. Healthy controls were age- and sex-matched individuals with no history of cardiovascular disease, confirmed by normal findings on physical examination, electrocardiogram, and echocardiography. A total of 60 participants were recruited, comprising 30 patients diagnosed with AS (AS group) and 30 healthy individuals serving as controls (Healthy group). The clinical baseline characteristics of the participants, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), blood lipid profiles (TG, LDL-C, HDL-C) are summarized in Table 1. The two groups were well-matched in age and sex (P > 0.05), while AS patients exhibited significantly higher LDL-C and lower HDL-C levels, consistent with the clinical features of atherosclerosis. Peripheral venous blood samples (5 mL) were obtained from each participant following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. The collected blood samples were subjected to centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate the serum. The serum was then aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. Serum levels of PAX5 and GSDMD were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits.

Plasmid construction

The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of human PAX5 was amplified from complementary DNA (cDNA) using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the following primers: Forward: 5’-TTGGTACCGAGCTCGGATCCGCCACCATGGATTTAGAGAAAAATTATCCGAC-3′ and Reverse: 5′-ACGGGCCCTCTAGACTCGAGTCAGTGACGGTCATAGGCAG-3′, incorporating BamHI and XhoI restriction sites. The resulting PCR product was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-3×Flag vector to construct the PAX5 overexpression plasmid (OE-PAX5). The empty pcDNA3.1 vector served as the negative control (NC).

Cell culture and transfection

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and 293T cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

For transfection experiments, HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well. When cell confluency reached 70–80%, transfection was performed with OE-PAX5 plasmids or negative control (NC) plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested for subsequent experimental analysis. The efficiency of PAX5 overexpression was verified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and western blot analysis.

Establishment of AS cell model

HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates, and 24 h post-transfection, the cells were treated with 50 µg/mL oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) from Beijing Xiesheng Bio-Technology Limited, Beijing, China, for an additional 24 h. Alternatively, the cells were maintained under standard incubation conditions (3). The cells were categorized into four distinct groups: (1) Control (untreated HUVECs); (2) ox-LDL (HUVECs treated with oxidized low-density lipoprotein); (3) ox-LDL + NC (ox-LDL-treated HUVECs transfected with a negative control plasmid); and (4) ox-LDL + OE-PAX5 (ox-LDL-treated HUVECs transfected with an overexpression PAX5 plasmid).

Cell viability assay

The cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^3 cells per well and subjected to the aforementioned treatments. Following a 24-hour incubation period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was introduced to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 2 h. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Oil red O staining

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained with Oil Red O solution (0.5% in isopropanol) for 15 min at room temperature. Post-staining, the cells were washed with 60% isopropanol and counterstained with hematoxylin for 5 min. Lipid droplets were visualized under a light microscope, and the integrated optical density (IOD) was quantified using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs were cultured in endothelial growth medium-2 (EGM-2). Matrigel, pre-chilled, was thawed overnight at 4 °C, and 150 µL was dispensed into each well of a pre-cooled 48-well plate. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate gelation. HUVECs were serum-starved in endothelial basal medium-2 (EBM-2) for 24 h, subsequently trypsinized, and resuspended at a concentration of 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL. An aliquot of 100 µL of this cell suspension, equivalent to 5 × 10³ cells per well, was seeded onto the Matrigel-coated wells and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h. Tube formation was observed using an inverted phase-contrast microscope at 100× magnification, with images captured from three randomly selected fields per well. Quantitative analysis was conducted utilizing ImageJ software to measure the total tube length and the number of branches.

Immunofluorescence

HUVECs were cultured on glass coverslips placed within 24-well plates and subjected to the previously described treatment protocol. The cells were subsequently fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. The coverslips were then incubated with anti-PAX5 antibody (rabbit, 1:200, ab109443, Abcam) and anti-GSDMD antibody (mouse, 1:200, ab219800, Abcam) at a dilution of 1:200 at 4 °C overnight. This was followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies at a dilution of 1:500 for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI at a concentration of 1 µg/mL for 5 min. Imaging was conducted using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To elucidate the regulatory interaction between PAX5 and the GSDMD promoter, dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted utilizing HEK293T cells. These cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured to reach 70–80% confluency in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were co-transfected with various combinations of reporter plasmids, including the wild-type GSDMD promoter (GSDMD-WT), a mutant GSDMD promoter with a disrupted PAX5 binding site (GSDMD-MUT), or an empty vector control. Additionally, expression plasmids such as the PAX5 overexpression vector (OE-PAX5) or an empty backbone vector were used. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000, with a total plasmid amount of 1 µg per well, and each experimental condition was replicated in triplicate wells. At 48 h post-transfection, cell lysis was achieved using passive lysis buffer, and luciferase activity was quantified employing a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, E1910, USA) [22]. The relative luciferase activity was determined by calculating the ratio of firefly luciferase (Fluc) to Renilla luciferase (Rluc).

Ubiquitination assay

HUVECs were co-transfected with HA-tagged ubiquitin (HA-Ub) plasmid along with the PAX5 overexpression plasmid or empty vector (Control). At 44 h post-transfection, cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 (Abcam, ab141003) for 4 h to inhibit proteasomal degradation. Cells were then lysed in denaturing RIPA buffer (1% SDS) and boiled for 10 min to disrupt non-covalent protein interactions. The lysates were diluted 10-fold and immunoprecipitated with an anti-GSDMD antibody (Abcam, ab219800, 2 µg) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G Agarose beads for 2 h. The immunoprecipitates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and probed with anti-K48-linkage-specific ubiquitin antibody (Abcam, ab140601, 1:1000) and anti-K63-linkage-specific ubiquitin antibody (Abcam, ab179434, 1:1000), respectively. Input samples were immunoblotted for PAX5, GSDMD, and β-actin to confirm transfection efficiency and equal loading.

Development of an atherosclerosis (AS) animal model

Twenty-four specific pathogen-free (SPF) male ApoE-/- mice, aged 4–6 weeks and weighing 18–22 g, were procured from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Laboratory Animal Production License No. SCXK [Zhe] 2024-0004). After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 6 per group): (1) Control group, which was fed a standard chow diet for 12 weeks; (2) AS group, which received a high-fat diet (HFD; comprising 21% fat and 0.15% cholesterol) for 12 weeks [23]; (3) AS + AAV-NC group, consisting of HFD-fed mice injected with an adeno-associated virus negative control (AAV-NC); and (4) AS + AAV-PAX5-OE group, consisting of HFD-fed mice injected with an adeno-associated virus carrying the PAX5 overexpression vector (AAV-PAX5-OE). The AAV (serotype AAV2/8) was administered via tail vein injection at a titer of 1 × 1012 vector genomes (vg)/mouse in 200 µL PBS, and the injection was performed 2 weeks prior to the initiation of the HFD to allow sufficient transgene expression before atherosclerosis induction. PAX5 overexpression in aortic tissues was confirmed by qRT-PCR. The mice were maintained in an SPF facility under controlled environmental conditions, with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, humidity at 55 ± 5%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water.

Sample collection and processing

Following the 12-week intervention period, all mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate serum, which was subsequently stored at -80 °C for the measurement of lipid profiles, including triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), as well as pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, using commercial ELISA kits. Aortic arches were meticulously dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h, and embedded in paraffin for histological analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

Aortic arch tissues were initially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, and embedding in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were subsequently deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to staining using the HE Staining Kit. The staining protocol involved immersion in hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiation with 1% HCl-ethanol for 10 s, bluing in tap water, and counterstaining with eosin for 30 s. Post-staining, sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted using neutral balsam. Morphological assessments were conducted using an Olympus BX53 microscope.

Oil red O staining

For whole aorta en face staining, the dissected aorta was rinsed in PBS, fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min, and washed with 60% isopropanol. The aorta was then stained with a filtered Oil Red O working solution (0.5% in isopropanol) for 15 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed with distilled water, followed by differentiation in 60% isopropanol for 5–10 s. Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 1 min, and sections were mounted with glycerol gelatin. Lipid accumulation was visualized using an Olympus BX53 microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells utilizing the TRIzol reagent. For complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR was conducted on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) employing the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The relative expression levels of PAX5 were determined using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. The primer sequences were as follows: PAX5: Forward 5′-CCCAACCAACCAGTCCCA-3′, Reverse 5′-AGGATGCCGCTGATGGAG-3′; β-actin: Forward 5′-ACCCAGATCATGTTTGAGACC-3′, Reverse 5′-GAGGGCATACCCCTCGTAGA-3′.

Western blot

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Protein concentrations were quantified through the BCA assay. Equivalent protein quantities (30 µg) were resolved via 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes through electroblotting. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubated at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting PAX5 (1:1000, ab109443, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), GSDMD (1:500, ab219800, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), GSDMD-N (1:100, ab215833, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), phosphorylated STAT3 (1:1000, ab76315, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), STAT3 (1:1000, ab68153, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Caspase-1 (1:1000, ab207802, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Caspase-4 (1:1000, ab238124, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Caspase-5 (1:1000, ab40887, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Caspase-11 (1:1000, ab180673, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). For HUVEC samples, Caspase-1, -4, -5 were detected; for mouse aortic tissues, Caspase-11 was detected. β-actin (1:1500, ab6276, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) served as the loading control. Following three 10-minute washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, ab6721, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection reagent, and densitometric analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (v1.8.0, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.2. For comparisons between two groups, an independent samples t-test (two-tailed) was used. To assess statistical significance, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Results are presented as means with accompanying standard deviations (SD), and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Serum PAX5 and GSDMD expression in atherosclerosis patients

The clinical baseline characteristics of the 60 participants (30 AS patients and 30 healthy controls) are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the two groups in age and sex (P > 0.05). However, the AS group exhibited significantly higher levels of TG (P < 0.001) and LDL-C (P < 0.05), as well as significantly lower HDL-C levels (P < 0.01), compared with the Control group, consistent with the typical dyslipidemia profile of atherosclerosis.

The quantitative analysis of GSDMD and PAX5 expression levels was conducted to explore their potential associations with AS, using the Control group as a reference. As illustrated in Fig. 1A and B, the AS group demonstrated a statistically significant increase in GSDMD expression compared to the Control group (P < 0.001). Conversely, PAX5 expression was significantly reduced in the AS group relative to the Control group. These findings suggest that the regulation of GSDMD and PAX5 in the AS group may play a role in the pathological mechanisms underlying AS.

Validation of PAX5 overexpression efficiency in HUVECs

To assess the efficiency of PAX5 overexpression, qPCR and Western blot analyses were performed. As depicted in Fig. 1C, qPCR analysis revealed a significant increase in PAX5 mRNA expression in HUVECs transfected with the OE-PAX5 plasmid compared to the NC group (P < 0.01). Furthermore, as shown in Figs. 1D, Western blot analysis confirmed the upregulation of PAX5 protein levels in the OE-PAX5 group, indicating successful overexpression (P < 0.01). These results collectively confirm that the PAX5 overexpression vector (OE-PAX5) effectively enhances PAX5 protein expression, indicating the successful establishment of the PAX5 overexpression model.

Effect of PAX5 overexpression on the viability of ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs

The CCK-8 assay was performed to evaluate how PAX5 overexpression regulates HUVEC viability under ox-LDL treatment. As shown in Fig. 1E, ox-LDL stimulation significantly reduced HUVEC viability relative to the Control group (P < 0.01). Compared with the ox-LDL + NC transfection group, overexpression of PAX5 (ox-LDL + OE-PAX5 group) significantly restored cell viability (P < 0.05).

Effect of PAX5 overexpression on tube formation capacity of ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs

To explore the regulatory effect of PAX5 overexpression on the angiogenic ability of HUVECs under ox-LDL stimulation, tube formation assay was performed, and two core parameters (number of branches and total tube length) were quantitatively analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2A-C, ox-LDL stimulation significantly reduced the number of branching nodes and total tube length of HUVECs compared with the Control group (P < 0.001). For the PAX5 overexpression intervention, overexpression of PAX5 significantly increased total tube length compared with the ox-LDL + NC group (P < 0.01), whereas no significant difference was observed in the number of branching nodes between the two groups.

Effect of PAX5 overexpression on lipid accumulation in ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs

Oil Red O staining assay was performed to investigate whether PAX5 overexpression regulates ox-LDL-induced lipid deposition in HUVECs. As illustrated in Fig. 2D and E, ox-LDL treatment significantly promoted intracellular lipid droplet accumulation compared with Control group (P < 0.001). Transfection with OE-PAX5 significantly reduced lipid accumulation in the ox-LDL + OE-PAX5 group compared with the ox-LDL + NC group (P < 0.001). The above results indicated that ox-LDL stimulation induces excessive lipid deposition in HUVECs, and this pathological change can be significantly attenuated by PAX5 overexpression (ox-LDL + OE-PAX5 vs. ox-LDL + NC, P < 0.001).

The modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion by PAX5 overexpression in ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs

To investigate the effects of PAX5 overexpression on the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines under ox-LDL stimulation, ELISA was employed to quantify the levels of IL-1β and IL-18. As depicted in Fig. 3A − 3D, stimulation with ox-LDL alone resulted in a significant increase in IL-1β, IL-18, LDH release, and Caspase-1 activity compared to the Control group (P < 0.001). Conversely, the overexpression of PAX5 (ox-LDL + OE-PAX5 group) resulted in a substantial and statistically significant reduction in the secretion of IL-1β, IL-18, as well as LDH release and Caspase-1 activity when compared to the ox-LDL + NC group (P < 0.01).

Regulatory effect of PAX5 on GSDMD promoter activity verified by dual-luciferase reporter assay

To determine whether PAX5 directly activates the GSDMD promoter, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed using wild-type (GSDMD-WT) and mutant (GSDMD-MUT) GSDMD promoter constructs (Fig. 3E). Co-transfection with the PAX5 overexpression plasmid significantly elevated the luciferase activity of GSDMD-WT compared with the NC group (P < 0.001). In contrast, when the predicted PAX5 binding site was mutated (GSDMD-MUT), the activating effect of PAX5 was largely attenuated, with only a minor increase in luciferase activity relative to the NC group (P < 0.05). These results confirm that PAX5 directly binds to the predicted binding site within the GSDMD promoter region to activate GSDMD transcription.

PAX5 promotes K48- and K63-linked poly-ubiquitination of GSDMD

To investigate whether PAX5 promotes GSDMD ubiquitination, a GSDMD ubiquitination assay was performed in HUVECs co-transfected with HA-Ub and PAX5 overexpression plasmid. Following GSDMD immunoprecipitation, ubiquitination levels were assessed by immunoblotting with antibodies against Ub, K48-linkage-specific ubiquitin, and K63-linkage-specific ubiquitin. As shown in Fig. 3F, PAX5 overexpression significantly enhanced both K48-linked and K63-linked poly-ubiquitination of GSDMD compared with the Control group (P < 0.01). Input immunoblots confirmed successful transfection and equal loading. These results demonstrate that PAX5 promotes multi-type poly-ubiquitination of GSDMD through both K48- and K63-linked ubiquitin chains in a dose-dependent manner.

Co-localization and expression patterns of PAX5 and GSDMD in ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to characterize the co-localization and expression changes of PAX5 and GSDMD in HUVECs under ox-LDL stimulation. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (blue), GSDMD was labeled with red fluorescence, and PAX5 was labeled with green fluorescence.

In the Control group, moderate basal expression of both GSDMD and PAX5 was detected, with partial co-localization observed in the cytoplasm. As shown in Fig. 3G, compared with the control group, the green fluorescence intensity of PAX5 in the ox-LDL alone treatment group was significantly decreased, accompanied by a significant increase in the red fluorescence signal of GSDMD. In contrast, compared with the ox-LDL + NC group, the ox-LDL + OE-PAX5 group showed a substantial upregulation of PAX5 green fluorescence intensity. Discussion

Atherosclerosis remains the leading cause of cardiovascular mortality worldwide, a pathological process driven by the complex crosstalk among dysregulated lipid metabolism, chronic vascular inflammation, and endothelial cell dysfunction. Although previous studies have confirmed the critical involvement of pyroptosis and multiple transcription factors in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, the regulatory relationship between the transcription factor PAX5 and the key pyroptosis executor GSDMD in vascular pathological processes has not been fully elucidated. This study is the first to demonstrate that PAX5 alleviates atherosclerosis progression via transcriptionally regulating GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, which further modulates lipid accumulation, endothelial cell injury, and inflammatory responses, as validated by both in vitro cell experiments and in vivo animal models.

PAX5 is a well-recognized master regulator of B lymphocyte development, while GSDMD is the core executor of canonical and non-canonical pyroptosis pathways [24, 25]. Previous studies have explored the role of PAX5 and GSDMD in atherosclerosis (AS) respectively: For PAX5-related research, ITGAX was reported to regulate vascular smooth muscle cell function and inflammatory responses by interacting with PAX5, serving as a potential therapeutic target for AS [20]; Furthermore, inhibition of HMGN2 SUMOylation also alleviates AS progression by upregulating PAX5 expression and subsequently promoting macrophage M2 polarization [26]. For GSDMD-related research, the novel GSDMD-specific inhibitor GI-Y2 exerts anti-pyroptotic effects and attenuates AS development [27]. Macrophage-derived GSDMD also plays a critical role in AS progression, mediating intercellular crosstalk between macrophages via the mitochondria-STING-IRF3/NF-κB axis [28]. Consistent with previous findings, our clinical sample analysis showed that PAX5 expression was downregulated in patients with AS, while the expression of pyroptosis executor GSDMD was significantly upregulated, indicating an inverse correlation between PAX5 and GSDMD during AS progression. Our dual-luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that PAX5 directly binds to the GSDMD promoter, thereby activating its transcription. Notably, overexpression of PAX5 consistently resulted in reduced levels of GSDMD protein, indicating the presence of an additional post-translational regulatory mechanism. Ubiquitination assays revealed that PAX5 facilitates both K48-linked and K63-linked poly-ubiquitination of GSDMD. K48-linked ubiquitination is known to target proteins for proteasomal degradation, which is consistent with the net reduction in GSDMD protein abundance observed upon PAX5 overexpression. In contrast, K63-linked ubiquitination is likely involved in autophagy-lysosome-dependent degradation. These findings suggest a dual regulatory model wherein PAX5 transcriptionally activates GSDMD while concurrently promoting its proteasomal degradation, with the degradation pathway predominating, thereby resulting in a net decrease in GSDMD protein abundance. This dual regulatory mechanism may represent a nuanced strategy by which PAX5 modulates the threshold of GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in endothelial cells during the progression of atherosclerosis.

In contrast to autophagy and apoptosis, pyroptosis exhibits distinct morphological and mechanistic characteristics [29, 30]. Pyroptosis is a form of cell lysis mediated by the Gasdermin family and is characterized by the release of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 [31, 32]. Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between pyroptosis and cardiovascular diseases. Recent research indicates that caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis plays a significant role in the formation and progression of atherosclerosis, particularly during the advanced stages of the disease [33, 34]. This form of pyroptosis emerges as a major cause of mortality for macrophages processing ox-LDL in the context of atherosclerotic disease. Caspase-1 facilitates the conversion of IL-1β precursors into their active form, IL-1β, which subsequently recruits and activates additional immune cells and induces the production of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, chemokines, and adhesion molecules [35, 36]. In in vitro experiments using ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs, we found that PAX5 overexpression alleviated multiple atherosclerosis-related phenotypes, including decreased cell viability, impaired migration and invasion abilities, increased lipid accumulation, and elevated secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. As a key pathogenic factor of atherosclerosis, ox-LDL can induce oxidative stress damage and endothelial cell pyroptosis. Dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed that PAX5 directly binds to the GSDMD promoter, while immunofluorescence assay showed the co-localization of PAX5 and GSDMD proteins in endothelial cells. PAX5 ultimately reduces the protein levels of GSDMD and downstream pyroptosis-related effectors (including Caspase-1, -4, -5 and − 11) through post-transcription regulatory mechanisms such as promoting GSDMD ubiquitination and degradation, which leads to the inhibition of pyroptosis observed in this study. These findings expand the functional understanding of PAX5 beyond its well-known role in B cell development, and identify it as an anti-atherogenic regulatory factor in endothelial cells. Consistent with the in vitro results, ApoE⁻/⁻ mice with PAX5 overexpression showed attenuated atherosclerotic lesions, manifested as reduced lipid deposition, smaller plaque area, and decreased serum LDL-C, IL-1β and IL-18 levels. AS model mice showed significant upregulation of GSDMD and Caspase family members in aortic tissues, which was markedly reversed after PAX5 overexpression, verifying the existence of the PAX5-GSDMD-pyroptosis regulatory axis in vivo. These results indicate that PAX5 plays a protective role in atherosclerosis progression, which may be mediated by its synergistic regulatory effects on lipid metabolism and pro-inflammatory cell death.

Atherosclerosis is initiated by endothelial injury and subendothelial lipid accumulation, which subsequently drives thrombosis, vessel wall thickening and arterial stiffening [35]. The STAT family, comprising seven members (STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5a, STAT5b, and STAT6), serves dual functions in signal transduction and transcriptional regulation [37, 38]. Among these, STAT3 has garnered increasing attention due to its pivotal roles in various biological processes, such as cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, inflammation, immunity, and angiogenesis [39]. The STAT3 signaling pathway plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. Persistent phosphorylation of STAT3 results in the aberrant expression of adhesion molecules. Macrophages modulate the function of vascular smooth muscle cells during the progression of atherosclerosis via the IL-1β/STAT3 signaling axis. STAT3 serves as a critical regulator of gene expression related to macrophage inflammation [40]. Previous studies have highlighted the potential of STAT3 inhibitors as therapeutic agents for atherosclerosis, underscoring the pivotal role of STAT3 in endothelial cell dysfunction, macrophage polarization, inflammation, and immunity associated with atherosclerosis [41]. Consistent with previous reports, our study found that p-STAT3 was significantly upregulated in both ox-LDL-stimulated HUVECs and ApoE⁻/⁻ mouse AS models compared with the control group, verifying the hyperactivation of STAT3 signaling during AS progression, which is consistent with previous findings of elevated p-STAT3 levels in human atherosclerotic plaques and animal AS models. Furthermore, our results showed that PAX5 overexpression significantly inhibited STAT3 phosphorylation without affecting total STAT3 protein levels. These findings indicate that STAT3 hyperphosphorylation is a key event in AS pathogenesis, which promotes the upregulation of pyroptosis-related molecules and amplifies inflammatory responses. However, the precise relationship between STAT3 and GSDMD remains to be fully elucidated. While our data demonstrate that PAX5 overexpression concurrently suppresses STAT3 phosphorylation and reduces GSDMD protein levels, whether STAT3 directly regulates GSDMD transcription or whether these changes occur through independent parallel pathways downstream of PAX5 requires further investigation. Future studies employing STAT3-mediated GSDMD promoter luciferase assays and STAT3 knockdown/overexpression rescue experiments are warranted to clarify this relationship.

Despite the novel findings of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the specific E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for PAX5-mediated GSDMD ubiquitination remains to be identified. Second, the precise relationship between STAT3 and GSDMD has not been experimentally established, and whether STAT3 directly regulates GSDMD transcription or acts through parallel pathways requires further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that PAX5 overexpression alleviates AS progression both in vitro and in vivo via two complementary regulatory mechanisms: On one hand, PAX5 inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation to reduce the transcriptional activation of pyroptosis-related molecules; on the other hand, PAX5 transcriptionally regulates GSDMD expression, thereby downregulating the protein levels of Caspase-1, Caspase-4, Caspase-5, Caspase-11 and GSDMD, and ultimately inhibiting endothelial cell and macrophage pyroptosis as well as the release of downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines. These findings highlight that the PAX5-STAT3-pyroptosis regulatory axis is a key pathway involved in AS pathogenesis, and provide a novel theoretical basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for AS.
