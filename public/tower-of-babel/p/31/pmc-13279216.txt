Sex Differences in Lipids and Lipoproteins and Their Relationship With Cardiovascular Disease: A Prospective Study of UK Biobank Participants
Authors: Rebecca K Kelly, Katie Harris, Paul Muntner, Mark Woodward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSex‐specific differences in serum lipids are recognized, but their relationship with cardiovascular disease (CVD) has not been reliably quantified. We examined sex‐specific associations of major lipids and apolipoproteins with incident CVD.MethodsWe included 432 092 UK Biobank participants without CVD at baseline (2006–2010) and with ≥1 lipid measurement. Age‐adjusted risks were estimated using Poisson regression. Multivariable Cox models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and women‐to‐men ratios of HRs for 1‐SD higher values of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, apoB (apolipoprotein B), apoA1 (apolipoprotein A1), and Lp(a) (lipoprotein [a]).ResultsOver a mean 13.3 years of follow‐up, there were 10 699 and 18 950 cases of CVD in women and men, respectively. CVD risk per 10 000 person‐years was 33.4 (95% CI, 32.7–34.0) for women and 76.6 (95% CI, 75.5–77.7) for men. Low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, apoB, and log Lp(a) were associated with smaller HRs of CVD for women than men (ratio of HR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.91–0.97], ratio of HR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.92–0.97] and ratio of HR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93–0.99], respectively). Triglycerides were associated with larger HRs of CVD in women than men (ratio of HR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.02–1.09]). The association of lower apoA1 with higher CVD risk was stronger in men than women (ratio of HR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.03–1.10]). No sex difference was observed for high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (ratio of HR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.98–1.06]).ConclusionsMen had a higher rate of CVD than women overall. Low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, apoB and Lp(a) had stronger associations with CVD risk in men, whereas triglycerides were stronger in women. ApoA1 was less protective for CVD in women than men.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death in men and women worldwide. 1 Overall, CVD mortality is higher among men than women at younger ages, but rates converge at older ages. 1 , 2 There is established evidence that lipids are risk factors for CVD in both sexes. 3 Serum lipid levels vary by sex, and according to women‐specific factors, such as menopause. 4 , 5 Women tend to have higher high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) than men throughout adulthood, whereas low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) and triglyceride levels tend to be higher in men at younger ages but may exceed those of men in older women. 6

Sex‐specific thresholds for determining CVD risk exist for HDL‐C but not for other serum lipids. 7 Some observational studies have suggested that LDL‐C, which has an established causal link with atherosclerotic CVD, is a less important determinant of CVD risk in women than men. 8 However, other observational 9 and genetic evidence 10 , 11 , 12 has found similar associations of LDL‐C and atherosclerotic CVD in both sexes. Moreover, Lp(a) (lipoprotein [a]) is higher among women than men at older ages, 13 and triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins appear to be a risk factor for premature atherosclerotic CVD in women. 14 Yet, the associations of higher Lp(a) and triglycerides with atherosclerotic CVD have been reported to be similar among both sexes in epidemiological studies. 15 , 16 , 17 Evidence remains limited for the relationship of serum lipids and CVD according to menopausal status, as well as for apolipoproteins and stroke subtypes, particularly hemorrhagic stroke.

We therefore conducted a large epidemiological study, to investigate sex differences in the association of major serum lipids and lipoproteins, including Lp(a), and CVD and CVD subtypes, and whether these associations vary with aging and the menopause in women.

Methods

Subjects and Study Design

The UKB (UK Biobank) is a population‐based cohort study that recruited 500 000 women and men aged 40 to 69 years between 2006 and 2010. 18 Individuals were invited to attend a baseline assessment at 1 of 22 centers, where information on lifestyle and medical history was collected via questionnaires and physical and biological measurements were taken. Linkage to hospital admissions data and mortality records has enabled long‐term follow‐up of participants for health outcomes. Participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social care and the National Health Service North West Multi‐centre Research Ethics Committee (reference number 21/NW/0157). Requests to access the data set and methods from qualified researchers may be sent to UKB (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Participants were excluded from the present study if they withdrew consent (n=1184), had a history of CVD before the baseline assessment (n=40 094), or did not have a baseline measurement of at least 1 serum lipid marker (n=29 947) (Figure S1).

Measurement of Lipids and Lipoproteins

Nonfasting venous blood samples were taken at the baseline assessment using standardized procedures. 19 Serum lipids examined were directly‐measured LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, apoB (apolipoprotein B), apoA1 (apolipoprotein A1), and Lp(a). See Data S1 for further details. 19

Ascertainment of CVD Outcomes

The primary study end point was fatal or nonfatal CVD, and the secondary end points were fatal or nonfatal coronary heart disease (CHD), ischemic stroke, and hemorrhagic stroke. Linkage to hospital inpatient and death data allowed for identification of date and cause of hospital admission(s) or death. CHD was defined using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes I21 to I25, as well as the Classification of Interventions and Procedures, Fourth Revision codes K49, K50, K75, or K40 to K46. Ischemic stroke was defined using ICD‐10 code I63 and hemorrhagic stroke was defined using ICD‐10 codes I60 and I61. CVD was defined as a composite of CHD, ischemic stroke, and hemorrhagic stroke combined, as well as stroke not otherwise specified (ICD‐10 code I64). 20 , 21 Follow‐up for all participants started at the date of the baseline assessment and ended at the date of administrative censoring (October 31, 2022 for England, August 31, 2022 for Scotland, May 31, 2022 for Wales), diagnosis or death, or loss to follow‐up, whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics for women and men were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, means±SDs for approximately normally distributed continuous variables, and medians and interquartile intervals for skewed continuous variables. Triglycerides and Lp(a) concentrations were log‐transformed to obtain approximate normal distributions. 22 Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to estimate the correlation between serum lipids.

Poisson regression models were used to estimate sex‐specific risks and risk differences for CVD outcomes across tenths of serum lipid concentrations. This approach is appropriate for common outcomes and facilitates interpretation on the absolute scale. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for the sex‐specific associations between serum lipids and CVD outcomes, with interaction terms included between each variable and sex. 23 This approach enables time‐to‐event analysis accounting for censoring and variation in follow‐up time. The serum lipid and outcome associations were modeled in categories using tenths (Table S1), consistent with the approach used in prior large‐scale epidemiological studies, 3 and continuously in increments of 1‐SD higher concentrations: 0.85 mmol/L LDL‐C, 0.38 mmol/L HDL‐C; 0.52 log mmol/L triglycerides, 0.24 g/L apoB, 0.27 g/L apoA1, and 1.12 log nmol/L Lp(a). Penalized smoothing splines were fitted within Cox proportional hazards models using 2 degrees of freedom (pspline in the R survival package) to allow for nonlinearity while avoiding overfitting. The degree of smoothing was specified a priori and confirmed by inspection of fitted curves.

All models were adjusted for age at recruitment. Multivariable models were further adjusted for Townsend deprivation index, 24 , 25 smoking status, alcohol intake, diabetes, antihypertensive medication use, lipid‐lowering medication use, glucose‐lowering medication use, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, and menopausal status among women only. Model covariates were selected a priori based on literature supporting a relationship with CVD end points and/or their inclusion in cardiovascular risk algorithms. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 See Data S1 for further details on covariate measurement. Percentages missing for each covariate were <10% (Table S2), except for alcohol intake (18.6% and 10.5% in women and men, respectively).

Sex differences in serum lipids across subgroups of age (<60, ≥60 years), socioeconomic status (low, high, based on the median national Townsend deprivation index score [−0.56]), 25 body mass index (<25, ≥25 kg/m2), smoking status (never smoker, ever smoker) were assessed by adding a 3‐way interaction between sex, subgroup, and the serum lipid of interest to the models. Similarly, we conducted subgroup analyses by menopausal status (premenopausal, postmenopausal) in women, and compared with similarly aged men (≤50 years, >50 years, respectively). The robustness of our findings was examined in sensitivity analyses by (1) excluding participants taking lipid‐lowering medications at baseline, (2) further adjusting for fasting status (hours since last meal), and (3) imputing missing values using multivariate imputation by chained equations with 5 iterations, because percentages of missing were >5% for some lipids (including HDL‐C, apoA1, and Lp(a)) (Table S2). We also conducted sensitivity analyses to correct for regression dilution bias. Sex‐specific regression dilution ratios were calculated among a subsample of participants with follow‐up serum lipid measurements from the first repeat assessment visit in 2012 to 2013 (n=15 455, 3.6% of the main study sample). 30 Regression coefficients were then multiplied by the reciprocal of the regression dilution ratio to correct for regression dilution bias.

Statistical tests were 2 sided, and statistical significance was assessed using 95% CIs, with associations considered statistically significant when the CI did not include the null value. Analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas) and figures were created using R 4.3.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

This study included 432 092 UKB participants (56% women, mean 56.1 years of age). Lipid concentrations were higher among women than men at baseline for LDL‐C (women‐to‐men difference: +0.088 mmol/L), HDL‐C (+0.31 mmol/L), apoA1 (+0.21 g/L), and Lp(a) (+2.7 nmol/L), whereas log triglycerides were lower (−0.37 mmol/L) (Table 1). LDL‐C and apoB were strongly correlated (r 0.960 and r 0.958 in women and men, respectively), as was HDL‐C with apoA1 (r 0.903 and r 0.909) (Figure S2). Triglycerides and HDL‐C were also inversely correlated (r −0.438 and r −0.465).

Over a mean 13.3 years of follow‐up, there were 29 649 (36% among women) cases of CVD, 21829 (32% among women) cases of CHD, 6171 (43% among women) cases of ischemic stroke, and 2109 (56% among women) cases of hemorrhagic stroke. There were 3726 fatal CVD events (33% among women). Age‐adjusted risks of CVD were higher among men than women at all levels of each serum lipid (Figure 1, Table S3). The CVD risk differences between the highest and lowest tenth of each serum lipid were smaller in women than men: LDL‐C (+7.1 versus +36.8 cases in women versus men per 10 000 person‐years), HDL‐C (−37.4 versus −45.4), log triglycerides (+28.7 versus +44.1), apoB (+16.0 versus +47.2), apoA1 (+32.5 versus +45.9), and log Lp(a) (+9.8 versus +30.1). Risks of CHD and ischemic stroke were higher among men than women at all serum lipid levels (Tables S4 and S5). Notable sex differences were not present for risk of hemorrhagic stroke (Table S6).

The HRs for CVD were weaker in women versus men for LDL‐C (women‐to‐men RHR per 1‐SD, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.91–0.97]), apoB (RHR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.92–0.97]) and log Lp(a) (RHR, 0.96 [0.93–0.99]) (Table S7). Conversely, the HRs for CVD were stronger in women than men for log triglycerides (women‐to‐men RHR per 1‐SD, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.02–1.09]). The inverse association of apoA1 and CVD was stronger in men compared with women (RHR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.03–1.10]). There was no discernable sex difference in the association of HDL‐C and CVD (RHR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.98–1.06]).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

In subgroup analyses, HRs for CVD per 1‐SD higher in lipid levels were larger among younger versus older age groups for both sexes (Table S11) and for premenopausal women compared with postmenopausal women (Table S12). The sex difference for triglycerides and CVD was smaller in premenopausal than postmenopausal women when compared with similarly aged men (women‐to‐men RHR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.07–1.36] versus 1.05 [95% CI, 1.02–1.08], respectively). Moreover, HDL‐C and apoA1 was a stronger protective factor in men than women among those with body mass index <25 kg/m2 (women‐to‐men RHR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.04–1.18] and 1.14 [95% CI, 1.07–1.22], respectively) versus those with a body mass index ≥25 kg/m2 (women‐to‐men RHR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.94–1.02] and 1.03 [95% CI, 0.99–1.08]) (Table S13). Sex differences in the relationship between serum lipids and CVD did not vary significantly by socioeconomic status (Table S14) or smoking (Table S15).

In sensitivity analyses excluding participants taking lipid‐lowering medications at baseline, the magnitude of HRs for CVD in both sexes were weaker, but sex differences were the same as those observed in the main analyses. (Table S16). After further adjustment for fasting status, sex differences remained present but were attenuated for the association of log triglycerides, apoA1, and log Lp(a) with CVD (Table S17). The estimates from the analyses imputing missing data did not differ from the main analyses (Table S18). Correction for regression dilution bias resulted in stronger HRs for CVD in both sexes, although sex differences were unchanged (Table S19). DISCUSSION

This study of 432 092 middle‐aged UKB participants examined sex‐specific associations of major lipids and lipoproteins with incident CVD, including CVD subtypes. Higher LDL‐C, apoB, and Lp(a) were more strongly associated with CVD risk in men compared with women. Triglycerides and apoA1 were associated with a greater risk of CVD among women than men.

Prior evidence for a sex‐differential effect of LDL‐C on CVD is mixed. In the current study LDL‐C, measured directly, and apoB may be more important determinants of CVD risk in men than in women. Similarly, another analysis of UKB data observed that genetically determined LDL‐C had a stronger effect on CVD risk in men versus women, 8 although Mendelian randomization studies have found no evidence of sex interactions for LDL‐C and CVD. 11 , 31 The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists collaboration found no sex differences in the effect of statin therapy on reducing LDL‐C and preventing any CVD event. 32 However, a sex‐specific effect on the occurrence of a first CVD event, which is what we examine in this study, was identified, with a slightly greater effect in men (relative risk [RR], 0.72) versus women (RR, 0.85). Because LDL‐C is the main apoB‐containing lipoprotein, our reported sex‐differential results for LDL‐C and CVD may reflect differences in apoB concentration. 31 , 33 Discordantly high apoB relative to LDL‐C, which was observed among men in UKB, is related with higher CVD risk in prior studies. 34 , 35 Additionally, men may have a higher burden of small, dense LDL particles, which are more atherogenic. 14

The Framingham study found that HDL‐C may be more protective for CHD in women than men. 36 In the current study, HDL‐C was associated with a lower risk of CVD in both sexes with no evidence of heterogeneity by sex, with similar findings in the ERFC (Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration). 3 However, in spline analyses, we observed that the risk among women was higher than among men across all levels of HDL‐C, with approximately equivalent risks at the established optimal lower limits of HDL‐C (1.2 and 1.0 mmol/L in women and men, respectively). 28 , 33 Moreover, in the UKB we found that higher apoA1, which some suggest may better reflect HDL functionality, 13 was more strongly inversely associated with CVD risk in men than women. However, the differences in HRs between apoA1 and HDL‐C in our study were modest and may not reflect a true difference in predictive value. Recent evidence from genetic studies does not support a relationship of HDL‐C with CVD risks, 37 and that HDL functionality may be more relevant than concentration. 38 , 39 Unlike findings from the Copenhagen General Population Study and Copenhagen City Heart Study, which reported a U‐shaped association between HDL‐C and CVD mortality at very high concentrations, 40 our data showed a continued inverse relationship with CVD risk even at >2.0 mmol/L. This may reflect differences in outcome definitions, population structure, and the rarity of extreme HDL‐C values in our cohort.

Some existing studies have demonstrated that the relationship between triglycerides and CVD are similar in both sexes, 14 , 16 , 41 , 42 whereas other studies suggest that triglycerides may be a better predictor of CVD in women. 14 , 43 Our study found that higher triglycerides conferred a ≈ 6% excess risk of CVD in women compared with men. Similarly, the Copenhagen City Heart Study 14 found that triglycerides were more predictive of myocardial infarction in women than men, and the ERFC 3 also reported a significant interaction between sex and triglycerides on CHD risk, but the HRs of CVD for higher triglycerides were not significant in either sex. Women have slower clearance of triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins and more prolonged postprandial triglycerides elevations, influenced by hormonal regulation and insulin sensitivity, which may prolong exposure to atherogenic particles. 44 , 45 Additionally, triglyceride levels may be influenced by exogenous estrogen therapies and women‐specific conditions, such as polycystic ovarian syndrome. 46

Higher Lp(a) was associated with CVD risk in both sexes in UKB, but the association was modestly stronger in men, which is consistent with findings for myocardial infarction from the Copenhagen General Population Study. 14 However, another analysis of the UKB did not find a sex‐differential effect of Lp(a) on major cardiovascular events, although Lp(a) was not log‐transformed and analyses were not performed using the same sex‐disaggregated methodology, 23 which may explain this discrepancy. Lp(a) concentration increases in women after age 50 years, coinciding with the typical time of menopause. 17 , 47 Sex differences were not present in the association of baseline Lp(a) with CVD by age or menopausal status in women in this study. Moreover, our analyses of repeated lipid measurements showed that Lp(a) concentrations were stable over time (mean 4.3 years of follow‐up), with comparable regression dilution ratios in women (0.959) and men (0.960), supporting the reliability of baseline values for risk estimation. However, Lp(a) concentrations in UKB were overall higher among women than men (+2.7 nmol/L) indicating that elevated Lp(a) may be a relatively more common risk factor among women.

Several biological and clinical mechanisms may underlie the observed sex differences in lipid‐CVD associations. Estrogen enhances reverse cholesterol transport, increasing HDL‐C, and reducing LDL‐C and triglyceride levels. 13 These effects are attenuated after menopause, which may have contributed to the convergence of CVD risk between sexes with age. 15 Genetic factors, including X and Y chromosome effects, may also influence lipid profiles and vascular biology. 12 Although women had fewer overall CVD events in our cohort, this may reflect later onset and longer life expectancy, rather than lower lifetime risk. 12 , 48 Lastly, reproductive factors and women‐specific conditions, such as pregnancy‐induced hypertension and polycystic ovary syndrome, are associated with greater CVD risk, 4 , 49 yet their influence on the relationship of lipids with CVD remains underexplored. 5

Men had a higher rate of CVD than women at all levels of each serum lipid in our study; therefore, any sex‐based thresholds for lipid markers beyond HDL‐C should consider risk rather than lipid concentrations alone. This is in line with existing guidelines for lipid‐lowering therapy for primary prevention of CVD, which is based on overall CVD risk assessment using equations that include sex. 28 , 33 However, the CVD risk algorithms most commonly used in clinical practice, such as QRESEARCH risk estimator version 3 (QRISK3) and Systemic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2), do not account for sex‐specific risk predictors or risk thresholds. 26 , 27 A recent study of the UKB found that the use of sex‐specific variables (including triglycerides for women and apoA1 and Lp(a) for men) improved prediction of CVD beyond pooled cohort equations but not QRISK3. 48 Similar studies have found improvements in CVD risk prediction when using sex‐specific variables, particularly in middle‐aged adults. 50 , 51 , 52 Our study found that some lipids were stronger predictors of CVD in men (eg, apoB and Lp(a)), whereas others were stronger predictors in women (eg, triglycerides). Sex‐specific risk predictors and thresholds for lipids should be considered in any re‐evaluation of CVD risk calculators, although further cost–benefit analyses are required.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its large sample size, long follow‐up, and comprehensive assessment of lipid markers, including direct measurement of LDL‐C, apolipoproteins, and Lp(a). We also considered CVD subtypes (CHD, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke), although it is likely that these analyses were underpowered to show sex differences for stroke. Baseline measurements of serum lipids, used in the main analyses, will be subject to some regression dilution bias. This may have led to an underestimation of CVD risks in both sexes, as is demonstrated in our sensitivity analyses with correction for regression dilution bias; however, we found that this did not meaningfully influence sex comparisons. Moreover, we could not account for time‐varying covariates, including lipid‐lowering therapy during follow‐up. However, sex differences, measured by RHR, remained similar in sensitivity analyses excluding those taking lipid‐lowering medications at baseline. The use of nonfasting blood samples may have modestly affected lipid concentrations, particularly triglycerides 53 ; however, nonfasting lipids are considered adequate for CVD risk prediction, 29 and findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses with further adjustment for fasting status. Spline estimates at the lower end of the lipid distribution are subject to greater imprecision, with wider CIs and fewer cases, and should be interpreted with caution. The UKB cohort is predominantly of White European ancestry and the least socially disadvantaged, which may limit generalizability. Some lipids had a considerable percentage of missing values which may also be a source of bias. Residual confounding is also possible, despite adjustment for a wide range of covariates.

Conclusions

The associations between serum lipids and CVD differed by sex, with LDL‐C, apoB, and Lp(a) being stronger determinants of risk in men and triglycerides and apoA1 being stronger determinants of risk in women. There was no evidence of sex differences for HDL‐C, which is the only lipid marker with established sex‐specific cut‐points. CVD risk was higher in men than in women across all levels of each serum lipid. Sex‐based thresholds for lipid markers beyond HDL‐C should consider risk rather than lipid concentrations alone.
