Successful Pulmonary Thromboendarterectomy for Malignancy-Associated Septic Chronic Thromboembolic Disease in Children
Authors: Andrés Felipe Rubio-Duarte, Laura Isabel Manosalva-Arciniegas, Diego Márquez-Bohorquez, Carolina Riveros-Bustos, Albert Franz Guerrero-Becerra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension is exceptionally rare in children and is typically associated with major acquired prothrombotic conditions.Case SummaryWe report a 14-year-old girl with acute lymphoblastic leukemia who developed chronic thromboembolic pulmonary artery obstruction following necrotizing pneumonia and septic thromboembolic disease. Computed tomography, ventilation–perfusion scintigraphy, and pulmonary angiography demonstrated complete right pulmonary arterial occlusion with absent perfusion to the right middle and lower lobe territories. Despite mildly elevated pulmonary artery pressures (mean pulmonary artery pressure 23 mm Hg), the patient presented with severe functional limitation, oxygen dependence, and surgically accessible disease. Bilateral pulmonary thromboendarterectomy was successfully performed, achieving restoration of pulmonary perfusion and favorable postoperative recovery. Molecular analysis of the resected thrombotic material identified Mycobacterium tuberculosis.DiscussionThis case highlights the feasibility of pulmonary thromboendarterectomy in pediatric chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension and emphasizes that surgical candidacy should integrate symptom burden, anatomical operability, and multidisciplinary assessment beyond hemodynamic severity alone.Take-Home MessagesPulmonary thromboendarterectomy is feasible and associated with favorable outcomes in pediatric patients and remains the only potentially curative treatment. Early multidisciplinary evaluation is essential to identify operable disease, integrating anatomical burden, clinical and hemodynamic status, and the risk of disease progression.

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a form of pulmonary vascular disease caused by persistent obstruction of the pulmonary arteries by organized thromboembolic material that fails to resolve after an acute embolic event.1 In contrast to acute pulmonary embolism, CTEPH is characterized by fibrotic transformation of thrombus, fixed mechanical vascular occlusion, endothelial dysfunction, and secondary vascular remodeling, which may ultimately lead to right heart failure.2 Pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) is the definitive therapy for operable disease because it directly removes the obstructive substrate and can be curative.3

Although CTEPH is a well-recognized entity in adults, it is exceptionally uncommon in the pediatric population. Pulmonary thromboembolism itself is rare in children and typically occurs in the setting of strong predisposing conditions such as malignancy, systemic infection, or central venous instrumentation.4 Septic pulmonary embolism represents an additional and unusual mechanism that may lead to organized chronic obstruction, yet progression to surgically accessible chronic thromboembolic disease in this context has been only rarely described.5

Because of this rarity, experience with PTE in children is limited to small series and isolated reports. Nevertheless, available data demonstrate favorable outcomes with significant clinical improvement and low perioperative mortality, supporting surgery as the only potentially curative therapy when the disease is operable. We report the case of a 14-year-old girl who developed CTEPH secondary to malignancy-associated septic thromboembolic disease and was successfully treated with pulmonary thromboendarterectomy.

Case Report

History of presentation

A 14-year-old girl with high-risk B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia was receiving immunotherapy with blinatumomab, achieving an appropriate hematologic response. After the first treatment cycle, she developed clinical deterioration secondary to a severe infectious complication characterized by right lower lobe necrotizing pneumonia. A respiratory molecular panel (FilmArray) was positive for respiratory syncytial virus, extended-spectrum β-lactamase–producing Escherichia coli and Enterobacter cloacae, with favorable clinical response after 5 weeks of antibiotic therapy. Subsequently, persistent oxygen dependence prompted further diagnostic evaluation. High-resolution computed tomography demonstrated a septic thrombus within the right pulmonary artery with complete involvement of the right lower lobar branch and absence of distal recanalization (Figure 1B), associated with necrotizing consolidation, pulmonary infarction, and parenchymal necrosis (Figure 1A). In view of the favorable oncologic response, antileukemic therapy was continued. She was referred to our institution for multidisciplinary pediatric heart team evaluation 2 months after the onset of the initial clinical presentation, following completion of antibiotic and oncologic treatment at the referring institution. On admission, transthoracic echocardiography revealed structurally normal cardiac anatomy. The right ventricle was hypertrophied with preserved systolic performance and no right ventricular outflow tract obstruction; a nonmobile thrombus measuring approximately 7 mm was identified along the lateral wall of the right atrium, adjacent to the inferior vena cava inflow. The left ventricle demonstrated normal dimensions and preserved systolic function (ejection fraction 55%). The pulmonary valve exhibited normal opening with trivial regurgitation (peak gradient 4.1 mm Hg; peak velocity 1.02 m/s; velocity time integral 21.9 cm). The pulmonary artery confluence was preserved; the right pulmonary artery measured 13 mm and contained an echogenic intraluminal image extending distally, whereas the left pulmonary artery measured 14 mm.

Past medical history

High-risk B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia treated with blinatumomab immunotherapy.

Differential diagnosis

The diagnosis was established by computed tomography and confirmed by selective pulmonary angiography. Given the unequivocal findings on definitive diagnostic testing, no alternative diagnoses were considered.

Investigations

High-resolution computed tomography demonstrated a septic thrombus within the right pulmonary artery with complete involvement of the right lower lobar branch and absence of distal recanalization (Figure 1B), associated with necrotizing consolidation, pulmonary infarction, and parenchymal necrosis (Figure 1A).

Right heart catheterization showed a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 23 mm Hg and a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 15 mm Hg. Cardiac output was 4.74 L/min/m2. Indexed pulmonary vascular resistance was 1.47 Wood units·m2, and the transpulmonary gradient was 7 mm Hg. Selective pulmonary angiography demonstrated patency of the main right pulmonary artery with complete occlusion of the right lower and medium lobar artery (Figure 2); the right upper lobar branch was unobstructed. Management (medical/interventions)

In view of the favorable oncologic response, antileukemic therapy was continued. Given the presence of surgically accessible chronic thromboembolic obstruction, complete oxygen dependence with severe functional limitation on room air (NYHA functional class IV), and imaging evidence of complete right pulmonary arterial occlusion, pulmonary thromboendarterectomy with concomitant right atrial thrombus resection was undertaken.

Surgery was performed through a median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass. After systemic heparinization, bicaval cannulation was established and extracorporeal circulation initiated, followed by controlled systemic cooling to 20 °C to facilitate distal pulmonary arterial exposure and circulatory arrest. Two vents were placed; one in the superior right pulmonary vein toward the left ventricle and the second in the pulmonary root toward the left pulmonary artery. The pulmonary arteries were exposed, and the aorta was cross-clamped. Antegrade Del Nido cardioplegia was administered to achieve myocardial arrest. Right pulmonary arteriotomy exposed organized, fibrotic thromboembolic material firmly adherent to the arterial wall. A true endarterectomy plane was meticulously identified and circumferentially developed, allowing bloc extraction of chronic thromboembolic casts extending into the segmental and subsegmental branches of the right lower and middle lobes, consistent with Jamieson type II disease (Figure 3). The right upper lobar branch demonstrated preserved intima without macroscopic involvement. The right pulmonary artery was reconstructed and closed in 2 layers with polypropylene 6/0 suture. Left pulmonary arteriotomy revealed smooth, uninvolved endothelium without evidence of thromboembolic disease and was closed primarily. During right atrial exploration, a chronic right atrial thrombus was identified and completely resected. Surgical Findings

Gross appearance of organized thromboembolic material removed during pulmonary thromboendarterectomy, consistent with chronic fibrotic thrombus.

Histopathological examination of the resected thrombi showed organized tissue with focal calcification and no microorganisms on special stains. Molecular testing of thrombotic material identified rifampin- and isoniazid-susceptible Mycobacterium tuberculosis, whereas sputum studies were negative, consistent with paucibacillary pulmonary tuberculosis. In the context of these molecular findings, the lesion was interpreted as infection-associated septic thromboembolic disease occurring within a multifactorial prothrombotic milieu related to the underlying malignancy and systemic inflammatory state.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit under invasive mechanical ventilation with vasoactive support consisting of norepinephrine at 0.2 μg/kg/h, vasopressin at 40 milliunits/kg/h, epinephrine at 0.2 μg/kg/min, and milrinone at 0.375 μg/kg/min. Sildenafil was initiated at a dosage of 12.5 mg orally every 8 hours. After 12 hours, the patient tolerated discontinuation of mechanical ventilation, and sildenafil was subsequently discontinued 48 hours after surgery. Vasoactive support was progressively weaned and completely discontinued by postoperative day 7. In the immediate postoperative period, anticoagulation was maintained with unfractionated heparin, followed by transition to warfarin after 1 week.

Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated improvement in markers of pulmonary hypertension, with a tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity <2.8 m/s. Postoperative computed tomography of the chest showed no residual intraluminal thrombus in the right pulmonary artery, with restoration of adequate flow to all lobar branches (Figure 1C). The patient was discharged home 2 weeks after surgery without the need for supplemental oxygen and with antituberculous therapy initiated.

Discussion

This case describes a 14-year-old patient with acute lymphoblastic leukemia who developed malignancy-associated septic chronic thromboembolic disease (CTEPH) and was successfully treated with PTE. The clinical course illustrates the evolution from acute pulmonary thromboembolic disease associated with severe infection and malignancy-related prothrombotic state to persistent chronic thromboembolic obstruction characterized by fibrotic thrombus organization and pulmonary arterial remodeling.6

PTE remains the treatment of choice for CTEPH with surgically accessible disease because it directly removes the mechanical obstruction and can restore near-normal pulmonary hemodynamics. Contemporary guidelines designate PTE as first-line therapy and emphasize that operability assessment must be conducted by a dedicated multidisciplinary CTEPH team at experienced centers.1 In adult cohorts, where most evidence originates, PTE is associated with marked reductions in pulmonary vascular resistance, improvement in functional status, and sustained survival benefits when performed in high-volume programs.3,7 Most published pediatric PTE series originate from highly specialized centers in high-income countries, where referral networks and multidisciplinary expertise are well established. In this population it has been demonstrated that carefully selected children derive hemodynamic and clinical benefits comparable to that observed in adults, supporting surgery as the only potentially curative intervention.8 Reports of PTE in pediatric patients from Latin America are exceedingly limited, reflecting both the rarity of CTEPH in children and the constrained availability of specialized referral programs capable of performing this technically demanding procedure.9 Alternative strategies, including balloon pulmonary angioplasty and targeted medical therapy, are reserved for inoperable disease or residual pulmonary hypertension but do not replicate the anatomic and physiological correction achieved with endarterectomy in proximal disease.10,11 In this case, balloon pulmonary angioplasty was not considered a suitable therapeutic option given the extensive organized thrombotic burden and complete proximal pulmonary arterial occlusion, whereas pulmonary thromboendarterectomy represented the standard definitive therapy for surgically accessible disease.

CTEPH in children is rare; pediatric pulmonary embolism itself is uncommon and typically occurs in the setting of strong acquired risk factors such as malignancy, sepsis, or indwelling vascular devices.12 In our patient, malignancy, immunosuppression, and infection created a biologically plausible prothrombotic milieu. Sepsis and systemic inflammation promote endothelial activation and coagulation dysregulation, facilitating thrombus formation and organization.12,13 The identification of M tuberculosis within the thrombotic specimen adds a further pathogenic dimension. Tuberculosis is associated with a hypercoagulable state mediated by inflammatory cytokine activation, elevated fibrinogen, and endothelial dysfunction, mechanisms implicated in thrombosis. In paucibacillary disease, microbiologic confirmation may require molecular diagnostics on tissue, particularly when respiratory studies are negative, as occurred in this case.14,15

Conclusions

Collectively, this report adds to the limited evidence base supporting PTE in the pediatric population and demonstrates that definitive surgical management is achievable even in the setting of complex oncologic and infectious comorbidity, provided that patient selection, operative planning, and perioperative management are undertaken within a specialized multidisciplinary CTEPH program with established expertise.
