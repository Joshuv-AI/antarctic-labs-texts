Introduction

Due to an increasingly aging society, the prevalence of orthopedic implants, including total hip arthroplasty (THA), is increasing substantially [1]. In the United States, the annual number of primary total hip arthroplasty procedures rose from 305,082 in 2013 to 594,981 in 2022, and projections suggest it could reach approximately 2.17 million by 2040 [1]. MRI has become very useful in the diagnosis of post‐operative complications associated with joint replacement, such as aseptic loosening or periprosthetic joint infection [2, 3]. Nevertheless, there are challenges with MRI near metal implants: Metal has a higher magnetic susceptibility than soft tissues, which distorts the main magnetic field B0 and leads to a shift in the resonance frequency in the surrounding tissue [4]. Since the resonance frequency of protons forms the basis for spatial encoding in MRI, metal artifacts such as signal pile‐up or loss due to local T2* reduction, as well as geometric distortion can occur [5].

The most commonly used advanced techniques for metal artifact reduction are based on fast or turbo‐spin‐echo (TSE) sequences employing view angle tilting (VAT), slice encoding for metal artifact correction (SEMAC), and multi‐acquisition variable resonance image combination (MAVRIC) [6, 7]. VAT can correct in‐slice artifacts but not through‐slice artifacts, while SEMAC and MAVRIC address both in‐ and through‐slice artifacts [7]. MAVRIC encodes a 3D volume several times with different carrier frequencies of the RF pulses to cover a broad off‐resonance‐induced frequency spectrum [8]. SEMAC, on the other hand, excites 2D slices with VAT and additional 3D‐slice encoding steps to resolve the distorted slice profiles [9]. Both approaches are able to resolve even far off‐resonant signals in so‐called spectral bins, which can then be re‐assigned with proper spatial locations in a post‐processing step. However, while the spectral bin recombination is very effective in correcting major image distortions, several residual artifacts may affect the resulting images. Most prominently, ripple artifacts and blurring are known side effects, making diagnosis in the affected regions challenging [10]. For SEMAC, it has been demonstrated that overlaps between slice profiles can attenuate ripple artifacts [11]. However, this method requires longer acquisition times and increases the specific absorption rate (SAR) of the sequence, making it impractical for clinical applications.

Ripple artifacts associated with SEMAC imaging display a specific frequency distribution, particularly prominent at higher spatial frequencies (~0.5−2.0cm−1) [12]. In the spatial domain, ripple artifacts manifest as a wave‐like pattern with an extent of a few centimeters. Utilizing the discrete 2D wavelet transformation, we can effectively analyze the characteristics of these artifacts. The 2D wavelet domain captures spatial frequencies in localized regions of the image at different scales and orientations [13, 14]. Consequently, wavelets can be employed to represent ripple artifacts in anatomical images.

In this work, a novel wavelet domain filter (WD‐Filter) was developed to reduce ripple artifacts in MRI of total hip arthroplasty without increasing acquisition time. This study involved 100 patients and evaluated the impact of the proposed filter on ripple artifact intensity and overall image quality in compressed‐sensing SEMAC images.

Materials and Methods

The retrospective study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Cantonal ethics committee, Zurich, Switzerland) and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the MRI examination.

Patient Selection

A total of 100 patients (51 females, mean age 64 years (±13 years) and 49 males, mean age 65 years (±13 years)) who underwent MRI of total hip arthroplasty for clinical indications were included in this study. Median time since total hip arthroplasty implantation was 2.0 years (interquartile range: 1.0–6.3 years). Implant materials varied and included ceramic, cobalt–chromium, and titanium femoral heads; primarily titanium femoral stems; and acetabular components composed of high nitrogen stainless steel with a titanium alloy, ceramic with a titanium alloy, titanium, or cobalt–chromium. Ripple artifacts were visually identified jointly by a musculoskeletal radiologist and MR scientist (S.S.G. and J.C.D. with 6 and 2 years of experience, respectively) on the CS STIR SEMAC images. Overall, ripple artifacts were observed in 66 out of 100 image datasets. These artifacts were evident in either one or multiple slices within an image dataset. If there were more than three slices with ripple artifacts per patient, we selected three slices for the correction. Within each selected slice, all identified ripple artifacts were addressed, with one slice potentially containing between one and four artifacts (Figure S2). For image analysis, each ripple artifact was considered as a separate unit for analysis.

Filter in Wavelet Domain

The filtering process for ripple artifact reduction consisted of three steps (Figure 1). The first step was identifying the ripple artifact in the in vivo image. A region of interest (ROI) was manually selected by an MR scientist (J.C.D., 2 years of experience) to specifically target the area associated with the ripple artifacts. Filtering was only used in the ROI to avoid filtering detailed structures within the whole field of view (FOV), as edges or transitions in MR images could also exhibit high absolute values for the detail coefficients (cD) [13, 15].

Subsequently, image data were transformed from image domain to wavelet domain by a two‐dimensional (2D) discrete wavelet transformation (WT; Supporting Information) [14, 16]. This transformation divided the image into the approximation coefficients cA (representing low frequencies) and the detail coefficients cD (representing higher frequencies). The cD is further split into the horizontal (cH), vertical (cV), and diagonal (cD) coefficients to identify changes or edges in their respective directions. Depending on the selected wavelet family, vanishing moments, and decomposition level, high absolute values for cD can be associated with the ripple artifacts [17].

The WD‐Filter used in this study involves the application of Fejér‐Korovkin wavelets (fk), with 6 or 12 vanishing moments (fk6 or fk12), and a decomposition level of 2, to capture all cD coefficients associated with the artifacts. Further details of the fk‐wavelet filter are described in the Supporting Information.

Following the WT, a binary mask based on signal thresholding was applied to each cD. The mask for each cD within the ROI had the following condition: MaskROI=0ifabsci,n>pi,n*maxabsci,n1ifabsci,n≤pi,n*maxabsci,n where c represents the detail coefficient for a specific direction i (horizontal H, vertical V, or diagonal D) and decomposition level n, while p denotes the threshold factor. The value pi,n was fixed for each ci,n. The thresholds pi,n were determined by an musculoskeletal radiologist and two MR scientists (R.S.: 22 years, J.C.D.: 2 years, C.V.D.: 15 years of experience) based on visual inspection of ripple artifact reduction and retention of anatomical detail in ten artifact cases from five in vivo datasets. The optimal thresholds for ripple artifact reduction were pH,1=0.12, pV,1=0.45, pD,1=0.18, pH,2=0.4, pV,2=0.45, pD,2=0.35.

After applying the mask, the inverse wavelet transform was used to reconstruct the image. The WD‐Filter algorithm was implemented in MATLAB R2023b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using the Wavelet Toolbox.

Phantom and In Vivo MR Imaging

For the phantom measurements, a total hip arthroplasty consisting of a titanium stem and a cobalt‐chromium head (Medacta, Castel San Pietro, Switzerland) was embedded into a custom‐built brick structure positioned in a container filled with water. Phantom imaging was performed on a 1.5 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Avanto Fit; Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) using a 32‐channel spine coil and an 18‐channel surface coil for signal reception. The imaging protocol (see Table 1) included a compressed sensing (CS)‐based coronal SEMAC sequence, with two opposite readout directions (H > F and F > H) to shift the ripple artifacts to various locations. Subsequently, the WD‐Filter was applied to the SEMAC images acquired in both directions, producing a total of four images for analysis: two images displaying ripple artifacts at distinct locations, and two corresponding images in which the ripple artifacts were effectively reduced upon filtering (Figure 2).

Image Analysis

Four phantom images (Figure 2) were analyzed by selecting three distinct ROIs in each image. ROI placement was performed by an MR scientist (J.C.D., 2 years of experience). Two of these ROIs included portions of a ripple artifact visible in at least one of the images. The third ROI was positioned in an area containing only water signal, free from both artifacts and brick, and served as a control region representing unaffected signal. For each ROI across all images, intensity values were individually plotted before and after the WD‐Filter application. The alternating signal of the ripple artifacts was also quantified by the mean signal intensity and standard deviation SROI.

For the study, three musculoskeletal fellowship‐trained radiologists (S.S.G., G.W.K., A.A.M. with 5, 6, and 4 years of experience, respectively) independently evaluated the ripple artifacts, comparing the original image to the images processed with the WD‐Filter (Figure S1). To assess the severity of the ripple artifacts before and after filtering, a 4‐point Likert scale was used, labeled as Likert‐RA (Likert‐Ripple Artifact), to measure the intensity of ripple artifacts. The Likert‐RA scores were classified as 1 = none, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, and 4 = severe. Figure 3 shows representative images with ripple artifacts for the Likert‐RA scores, where all readers scored the images identically. Additionally, a 4‐point Likert scale was employed to evaluate image quality (Likert‐IQ), with scores of 1 = poor, 2 = moderate, 3 = good, and 4 = excellent. Scores reflected the global subjective impression of diagnostic image quality, including sharpness, image noise, contrast, and artifacts.

The ripple artifacts demonstrate an alternating signal which can also be measured by the signal intensity standard deviation, SD. For quantitative artifact analysis, the SD was computed within a manually placed ROI around the artifact for the original and filtered images. Here, the relative decrease in SDROI was computed: ΔSDROI=SDROI−SDROIfilteredSDROI where SDROI is the SD within the ROI in the original image, and SDROIfiltered is the SD within the ROI in the image with the applied WD‐Filter. To ensure that the SD in areas not impacted by the WD‐Filter remains unchanged, an additional ROI in the musculoskeletal area (gluteus medius muscle) was designated for comparison (Figure S2). Within this ROI, the SD was also measured (SDMS) in the original image and the image with the applied WD‐Filter. Edge preservation was evaluated in one example by signal intensity profile comparison pre‐ and ‐post filter application in two regions close to the implant (Supporting Information; Figure S3).

Statistics

Variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Likert scores (RA and IQ) and SD before versus after filtering were compared using a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test and a two‐sided paired t‐test, respectively. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant [19]. For the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, effect size r was calculated and interpreted as small (r=0.10−0.29), medium (r=0.30−0.49), or large (r≥0.5) [20, 21]. The inter‐reader agreement for the Likert‐RA and Likert‐IQ scores was assessed using kappa statistics (Fleiss κ) with 95% confidence interval. The level of agreement with a κ value of 0.00 indicated “poor”, 0.00–0.20 “slight”, 0.21–0.40 “fair”, 0.41–0.60 “moderate”, 0.61–0.80 “substantial”, and values from 0.81 to 1.00 “almost perfect” agreement [22]. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 31, IBM).

Rating: Intensity of Ripple Artifacts

The three readers' ratings for the intensity of ripple artifacts are summarized in Table 2a. In the original image, (122±2) ripple artifacts were rated as “severe” (Likert‐RA = 4), (95±3) cases were rated as “moderate” (Likert‐RA = 3), and (32±1) cases as “mild” (Likert‐RA = 2). Upon wavelet domain filtering, the artifact was eliminated in (168±4) (Likert‐RA = 1). An additional (82±4) cases were rated as having “mild” residual ripple artifacts (Likert‐RA = 2). Finally, no ripple artifact was rated as “moderate” or “severe” after application of the WD‐Filter (Table 2a).

The mean Likert‐RA score for the original MR image was 3.4±0.7, while the filtered MR images had a mean Likert‐RA score of 1.3±0.5 across all readers. The Likert‐RA value significantly improved for all readers, with a large effect size (r=0.89). The inter‐reader agreement for the Likert‐RA scale was “almost perfect” at 0.86±0.03 for the original images and 0.90±0.04 for the images with the WD‐Filter.

Rating: Image Quality

Image quality was rated as “poor” (Likert‐IQ = 1) in (156±1) ROIs across readers. Rating of “moderate” (Likert‐IQ = 2) was assigned to (88±3) ROIs, while “good” quality (Likert‐IQ = 3) was noted in (6±2) ROIs.

After applying the WD‐Filter, (108±3) cases were rated as “excellent” (Likert‐IQ = 4). An additional (127±4) ROIs, were rated as “good” (Likert‐IQ = 3), and (15±1) as “moderate” (Likert‐IQ = 2) (Table 2b).

The mean Likert‐IQ score improved from 1.4±0.6 to 3.4±0.6 upon filtering. The Likert‐IQ score significantly improved for all readers, with a large effect size (r=0.89). Regarding the Likert‐IQ scale, the inter‐reader agreement was “almost perfect” at 0.95±0.03 for the original images and 0.96±0.03 for the images with the WD‐Filter.

Quantitative Artifact Assessment

Reader 1 measured a reduction of 19%±9% in ΔSDROI after the WD‐Filter, while reader 2 and reader 3 reported reduction of 19%±11% and 19%±10%, respectively. All readers observed a significant reduction in the value of SDROIfiltered for the corrected image compared to the original image, decreasing from 73±50 to 58±41. In contrast, ROIs placed in the skeletal muscle before and after applying the WD‐Filter showed no significant difference (p≥0.09) in SDMD (10.1±5.3 without WD‐Filter vs. 10.1±5.2 with WD‐Filter across readers).

Discussion

Hip arthroplasty and other metallic implants may cause severe metal artifacts in MRI scans. Several studies have highlighted the performance of SEMAC over standard MR sequences for metal artifact reduction [23, 24, 25, 26]. However, SEMAC can lead to the occurrence of detrimental ripple artifacts in areas near the implant due to the bin combination of SEMAC encodings [9, 11].

To minimize these artifacts, we developed a filter in the wavelet domain (WD). Phantom measurements demonstrated that applying the WD‐Filter effectively reduced ripple artifacts and minimized fluctuations in signal intensity. The WD‐Filter not only diminished the artifacts but also restored the appearance of the water signal, making it comparable to images obtained with the opposite readout direction. To assess its clinical impact, we tested the WD‐Filter in patients with total hip arthroplasty.

The current study addressed 250 image regions with ripple artifacts applying the WD‐Filter, which successfully reduced the severity of these artifacts. With the WD‐Filter, most MR images showed only “mild” or “no ripple artifact”. The average Likert‐RA score improved significantly and there was a significant reduction in the SD within the ROI containing the artifact, confirming the WD‐Filter's effectiveness. After reducing the ripple artifact, it is crucial to ensure that the image retains its diagnostic value. In this context, the WD‐Filter has shown its potential by improving the image quality, as reflected by the significant improvement in Likert‐IQ scores.

There are other approaches to reducing ripple artifacts in MR imaging. In order to achieve a homogeneous image combination, one approach is to increase the width of the slice profile for SEMAC to achieve a slice overlap [11, 27]. This can be achieved by either increasing the bandwidth of RF while maintaining the slice selection gradient or by keeping the RF bandwidth constant and reducing the gradient. However, both approaches have drawbacks, including increased specific absorption rate (SAR) or the need for more SEMAC encoding steps, increasing the acquisition time. Another potential approach is to incorporate a B0 field map during image postprocessing, which has been shown to reduce ripple artifacts and signal displacement [28]. However, it is important to note that acquiring an additional field map prolongs acquisition time, and this method is unable to fully correct for signal fluctuation caused by slice selection profile variations, potentially resulting in residual artifacts.

The developed WD‐Filter is applied after the data acquisition and in the wavelet domain. Wavelet domain filtering is commonly applied in MRI for denoising or compressed sensing [29, 30]. The wavelet transformation (WT) divides anatomical images into approximation coefficients (low frequencies) and detail coefficients (high frequencies). This transformation enables the visualization of characteristics such as noise and allows for differentiation from the image. Depending on the wavelet family used for the WT, different image features become more distinguishable from the main structure of the image [17]. In our approach, we utilized the wavelet domain to distinguish ripple artifacts from the main structures of the image. By selectively filtering the detail coefficients in a region of interest using a threshold based on the maximum absolute value of the coefficient, it is possible to set the detail coefficients corresponding to the ripple artifact to zero while maintaining other coefficients to preserve image detail. Using the inverse WT with the unmodified approximation coefficients and the filtered detail coefficients produces an image with reduced ripple artifacts, allowing the anatomical structures underneath the artifacts to become visible.

Limitations

The WD‐Filter has only been tested using two different 1.5 T MRI scanners from the same vendor in a single‐center setting, as well as two different CS‐SEMAC protocols (12 and 19 SEMAC encoding steps) in patients with total hip arthroplasty. Reader blinding was not fully feasible, as localization of artifact regions required reference to unfiltered images, given that the artifacts were often absent after filtering. In 6% of cases, the corrected images only achieved a “moderate” image quality due to residual artifacts, indicating that the WD‐Filter may not fully address certain shapes or orientations of ripple artifacts. Adjusting the WD‐Filter's threshold factors (pi,n) or considering a different wavelet could mitigate this issue. One drawback of our approach is the manual ROI placement during the filtering process. Consequently, all ripple artifacts in each slice must be manually delineated to obtain a filtered image, which may limit clinical workflow efficiency. Despite these limitations, our WD‐Filter may be the basis for potential automated ripple reduction approaches in the future using deep learning algorithms [31, 32]. While training a deep learning model to reduce ripple artifacts may be challenging due to the lack of ground truth images, the proposed WD‐Filter provides images without ripple artifacts or with reduced ripple artifacts, which may be used for artifact reduction model training. Alternatively, a DL model could be trained for ripple artifact segmentation to automate the ROI placement.

So far, the WD‐Filter has only been evaluated in patients with total hip arthroplasty imaged at 1.5 T. However, ripple artifacts have also been reported at 0.5 and 3 T field strengths as well as in the presence of different implant types and anatomical regions [3, 6, 33, 34]. As these artifacts arise from strong B0 field inhomogeneities in the in‐plane and through‐plane directions, their appearance and intensity may vary depending on field strength, implant shape, orientation, and composition [11]. Since the WD‐Filter targets extreme absolute detail coefficients in the wavelet domain that are directly associated with the artifact, our proposed approach is expected to remain applicable under varying conditions, although parameter tuning may be required.

To better understand the WD‐Filter's capabilities, successive studies should assess the effectiveness of the WD‐Filter across different field strengths and in patients with other metal implants such as knee arthroplasty [3, 24] or to other multispectral imaging techniques like MAVRIC [8].

Conclusion

The developed WD‐Filter successfully reduces ripple artifacts, enhancing the visibility of the tissue near the implant and improving image quality in MR imaging for patients with metal hip implants. While metal artifact reduction sequences generally have longer acquisition times than standard MR sequences, the application of the WD‐Filter during post‐processing allows for its inclusion in daily clinical practice without changing protocols or acquisition times.
