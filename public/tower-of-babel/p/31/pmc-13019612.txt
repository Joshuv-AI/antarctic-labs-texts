Conductive ionic liquid hydrogel filled anti-inflammatory nerve conduit repairs peripheral nerve defect
Authors: Jiahui Song, Chenlong Liao, Runze Jin, Zhengchao Yuan, Xiao Yu, Jie Cui, Yangfan Ding, Hao Feng, Lu Han, Binbin Sun, Mohamed EL-Newehy, Meera Moydeen Abdulhameed, Jinglei Wu, Bingbing Sun, Xiaojing Li, Wenchuan Zhang, Shichao Jiang, Xiumei Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConductive and anti-inflammatory microenvironments have been proven effective for peripheral nerve repair. Herein, a conductive chitosan-hydroxyethyl cellulose/ionic liquid (CS-HEC/IL) hydrogel was constructed, which was injected into a hollow anti-inflammatory poly(L-lactide-co-caprolactone)/luteolin (PLCL/Lut) nanofiber conduit. Such a PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit greatly enhanced the proliferation of Schwann and PC12 cells, andpromotedmacrophage transition to the M2 phenotype. Following implantation into a 10 mm sciatic nerve defect in a rat model, the scaffold was harvested 8 weeks post-surgery for histological and functional assessment. Histological analysis revealed that the conduit markedly enhanced nerve regeneration, as indicated by extensive myelination and axonal outgrowth, while functional assessments indicated improved motor recovery and nerve conduction, highlighting its therapeutic potential. Generally, the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL scaffold is a promising therapeutic strategy for peripheral nerve injuries.Keywords: Ionic liquid hydrogel, Anti-inflammatory drug, Hydrogel-filled conduit, Electrospinning, Peripheral nerve regeneration

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) refers to traumatic nerve structure and function damage, predominantly resulting from trauma, surgery, and infection [1]. Although peripheral nerves possess some regenerative capacity, most PNIs, particularly those with defects exceeding 5 mm, require surgical intervention [2]. Autologous nerve transplantation remains the clinical gold standard for repairing PNI, but its curative effects are impeded due to insufficient donors, susceptibility to secondary injury, and extra surgeries [3]. Nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) have attracted considerable research interest as a promising alternative to autologous transplantation [4]. Hydrogels are widely utilized in NGCs because of their ability to mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM), providing a three-dimensional platform that supports cell growth, migration, and the mediation of peripheral nerve repair [5]. However, a critical issue in nerve regeneration is the disruption of the regenerative microenvironment, including impaired electrical signaling and excessive inflammatory responses.

Peripheral nerves, known for their sensitivity to electrical signals, are significantly controlled by the bioelectricity present [6]. Electrical stimulation (ES) can modulate cell-to-cell interactions, facilitate neurite extension, and promote axonal regrowth by establishing neural signaling connections [7]. Conductive NGCs can accelerate peripheral nerve regeneration by maintaining a bioelectric field to deliver ES, thereby enhancing neural signal transmission [8]. Therefore, conductive nanomaterials or polymers are commonly incorporated into the fabrication of NGCs to fulfill their electroactive requirements [9,10]. However, their application in tissue engineering is hindered by poor dispersibility, potential cytotoxicity, and limited biodegradability [11]. Ionic liquids (ILs), composed of anions and cations, can establish an ion-rich environment to enhance the ionic conductivity with excellent water solubility, biodegradability, and bioactivity [12,13]. ILs such as [Bmim]+[Ac]− have been widely incorporated into hydrogels to create conductive hydrogels for neural tissue regeneration [14].

In parallel, secondary inflammation injury is manifested by the infiltration of pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype macrophages [15]. While M2 macrophages play a critical role in resolving inflammation and promoting tissue repair, their function is typically compromised in the hostile microenvironment, resulting in insufficient support for sustained nerve repair [16]. Therefore, modulating macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype is essential for nerve recovery [17]. Luteolin (Lut), a natural dietary flavonoid with potent immunomodulatory and neuroprotective effects, is an ideal component for immunomodulatory NGC fabrication [18,19].

Here, we present a conduit designed to simultaneously transmit electrical signals and modulate inflammation in PNI. The conduit features an outer layer of electrospun poly(L-lactide-co-caprolactone) (PLCL)/Lut nanofibers that provide structural support and sustained anti-inflammatory effects, and is internally filled with a conductive hydrogel (CS-HEC/IL) which is constructed from chitosan (CS) and hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC), both highly biocompatible and biodegradable, and incorporates the [Bmim]+[Ac]− IL to impart electrical conductivity. The CS-HEC hydrogel forms spontaneously by hydrogen bonding between the amino groups of CS and the hydroxyl groups of HEC, enabling rapid and trigger-free gelation [20]. Ultimately, the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit was implanted into a 10 mm sciatic nerve gap in a rat to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in nerve repair (Fig. 1). This approach offers considerable potential for advancing the field of peripheral nerve repair.

Materials and methods

Materials

Poly(L-lactide-co-caprolactone) (PLCL, LA: CL = 50:50, IV (dl/g): 1.76) was purchased from Jinan Daigang Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP, 920-66-1) was provided by Shanghai Darui Fine Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, 68-12-2) and luteolin (Lut, 491-70-3) were from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chitosan (CS, 9012-76-4) was from Macklin (Shanghai, China). 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid (68162-47-0) and hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC, 9004-62-0) were from Meryer (Shanghai, China). 1-Vinylimidazole (1072-63-5) and acetonitrile (75-05-8) were from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Diethyl ether (60-29-7) was purchased from China National Pharmaceutical Group (Shanghai, China). Schwann 96 cells (SCs) and PC12 cells were obtained from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China).

Construction of PLCL and PLCL/Lut hollow conduits

To prepare the PLCL spinning solution, PLCL (0.8 g) was dissolved in 10 mL of HFIP under magnetic stirring for 12 h at room temperature. Then, Lut (10 mg) was dissolved in DMF (100 μL) and added dropwise to the PLCL solution to prepare the PLCL/Lut spinning solution. Subsequently, electrospinning (Yongkang Leye Technology Development Co., Ltd., SS-3556H, Beijing, China) was carried out at a flow rate of 1 mL/h, a high voltage of 8 kV, and a collection drum speed of 200 rpm to obtain a nerve conduit with an inner diameter of 2 mm.

Synthesis of conductive hydrogels

CS (0.4 g) and HEC (0.3 g) were dissolved, respectively, in 10 mL of deionized water, with the CS solution supplemented with 100 μL of acetic acid to form a solution. Both solutions were stirred overnight. Then, equal volumes were mixed to form the CS-HEC hydrogel.

The [Bmim]+[Ac]− IL was synthesized by reacting 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid (0.82 g) and 1-vinylimidazole (1.41 g) in acetonitrile (30 mL) under a nitrogen atmosphere at 55 °C for 12 h. After completion, the solvent was removed through rotary evaporation, and the resulting mixture was washed extensively with ether. The IL was added to the HEC solution at concentrations of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 g, followed by mixing with the CS solution to obtain conductive hydrogels (CS-HEC/IL) with gradient concentrations of IL.

Construction of hydrogel-filled conduits

CS-HEC and CS-HEC/IL hydrogels were injected separately into the PLCL and PLCL/Lut hollow conduits, respectively. Achieving complete luminal filling required precise adjustment of hydrogels.

Characterization of hydrogel-filled nerve conduits

Morphological characterization of nanofibers and hydrogels was performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Before imaging using a Hitachi TM-1000 (Japan), nanofibers and hydrogels were lyophilized and then coated with gold by sputter deposition for 60 s. Statistical analysis of the fiber diameters of each group was based on measurements of at least 100 randomly selected fibers from SEM images. The synthesis of IL and its incorporation with CS-HEC hydrogel were detected by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, USA). The electrical conductivity of the hydrogels was evaluated with a four-point probe system (ST25588-F01, China). Cell culture, proliferation, and staining assays

The culture medium for SCs and RAW 264.7 cells consisted of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS), while PC12 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 20% horse serum, 10% FBS, and 1% PS.

CS-HEC and CS-HEC/IL hydrogels were subjected to ultraviolet irradiation for 4 h. SCs and PC12 cells were seeded separately onto hydrogels using a seeding density of 1 × 104 cells per well, followed by incubation under standard culture conditions for 5 days. Additionally, ES (100 mV, 1 h/day) was applied to SCs and PC12 cells in the ES group by a direct current power supply (HSPY-36-03, China). For the control group, cells were maintained on hydrogels with no stimulation applied. At 1, 3, and 5 days, cells were treated with a 9:1 mixture of DMEM and CCK-8 reagent, followed by a 60-min incubation period. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected for an absorbance measurement at 450 nm on a microplate reader (BioTek, Germany). Live/dead cell distribution was assessed by Calcein AM/PI double staining, with image acquisition conducted on a fluorescence microscope (LEICA DMi8, Germany).

The bottom of each 24-well Transwell plate was loaded with hydrogels, followed by the addition of serum-free culture medium before insertion of the Transwell inserts. A cell suspension of SCs of 1 × 104 cells/well was carefully seeded onto inserts. After 24 h of incubation, residual cells on the upper side were gently wiped away, while the migrated cells underwent fixation and visualization using a crystal violet-based protocol over 10 min. The inserts were then transferred to new 24-well plates, and the bound dye was eluted using 10% (v/v) acetic acid. To quantify cell migration, the eluate was analyzed by colorimetric detection at 590 nm.

Anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory properties of hydrogels

To assess the activity of the hydrogel materials to counteract oxidative stress, intracellular redox status was evaluated through a ROS fluorescent probe-based method. In brief, RAW264.7 cells (2 × 104 per well) were seeded into plates and incubated under physiological conditions for 1 day. Subsequently, cultures were treated with 100 ng mL−1 of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 1 day to induce oxidative stress, followed by exposure to hydrogel extracts. ROS levels were detected using DCFH-DA at 10 μmol L−1 in the absence of serum, with 20 min incubation in the dark. DAPI was applied to label cell nuclei over a 10 min incubation period. Fluorescent images were subsequently visualized and recorded. The no-LPS group was considered the control, and the LPS-treated group was referred to as the LPS group.

RAW264.7 cells were planted and maintained under standard culture conditions for 1 day. The cells were then exposed to 100 ng mL−1 LPS for 12 h to initiate polarization, followed by 5 days of culture. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min), cells were permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 (5 min) and blocked with 1% BSA (30 min). The primary antibody (rabbit anti-CD206, 1:200, Solarbio) was then incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing, secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:200) and DAPI were used for detection. Samples underwent fluorescence imaging by an inverted fluorescence microscope.

qPCR assay

The RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis from RAW264.7 cells were performed using commercial kits (Novoprotein, China), respectively. The qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Japan) on the LightCycler 480 system. Each experiment was repeated three times independently, and 2−ΔΔCt calculation was employed to quantify gene transcript levels. Primer designs were detailed in Table S1.

Transcriptome profiling

RNA sequencing was carried out using standard protocols (Cloud-seq Biotech, China). Genes with expression values ≥ 1 were considered detectable. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a threshold of ≥4-fold change and P ≤ 0.05. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using DAVID, and redundant terms were summarized and visualized using REVIGO. Target gene candidates were identified via the GeneCards database. Experiments included three biological replicates per group, with RNA extracted and sequenced for each.

Animal experiments

All animal experiment procedures adhered to the guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Donghua University (DWSY202508110189). 24 SD rats were randomly assigned to 4 experimental groups: Autograft, PLCL-CS-HEC, PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC, and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL for in vivo peripheral nerve repair.

Surgical procedure

After anesthetizing rats with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg kg−1), the right sciatic nerve was surgically isolated and a 10 mm segment was excised to form a nerve defect model. In the autograft, the excised tissue segment was reversed and sutured to the proximal and distal stumps. A 10 mm conduit was positioned across the gap in the conduit group, with nerve ends coapted using 8-0 sutures. Analgesia was provided with buprenorphine (0.05 mg kg−1) and animals were evaluated daily for adverse behavioral signs.

Neural histological evaluation

After 8 weeks, rats were euthanized for assessment of nerve regeneration. Specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 days. The central portion of the specimen was processed for epoxy resin encapsulation and sectioned at a thickness of 0.3–0.4 μm. For morphometric analysis, six biological replicates (n = 6) per experimental group were included. Next, Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Toluidine Blue (TB), and Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) staining were performed for tissue morphology and myelination. Additional slices were immunolabeled with antibodies against S100 and NF200. To evaluate the inflammation response and macrophage polarization, slices were stained with iNOS and CD206 antibodies, with nuclei stained with DAPI.

Functional assessment of nerve repair

At 8 weeks post-surgery, bilateral hindlimb muscle specimens were collected and weighed. To assess muscle atrophy following nerve injury, the tissue mass from the experimental limb was divided by the measurement obtained from the opposite limb. Muscles were stained with Masson's trichrome stain.

Electrophysiological assessments were performed by a previous method [21]. Briefly, the exposed sciatic nerve was subjected to ES (3.0 mA, 1.0 Hz). Compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) were captured, and amplitude and conduction velocity were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA and Tukey's tests were used for statistical analysis. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 were considered significant.

Results and discussion

Network pharmacology analysis

To explore the biological functions of natural polyphenolic flavonoids in peripheral nerve repair, luteolin was employed for network pharmacology analysis on peripheral nerve injury. The chemical structural formula of luteolin was shown in Fig. 2A. A total of 412 targets of luteolin were identified and peripheral nerve injury was associated with 10,339 targets, while common targets were 370 (Fig. 2B). These results indicated that luteolin may possess a broad therapeutic potential for peripheral nerve regeneration. Functional analysis identified 62 core targets that formed a central interaction network, including mitogen-activated protein kinase 14 (MAPK14), AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1), glutathione S-transferase pi 1 (GSTP1), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (Fig. 2C). These core targets were predominantly involved in the inflammatory response modulation and tissue regeneration processes.

KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the intersecting targets were mainly enriched in the inflammation-related signaling pathways, such as the AGE-RAGE (advanced glycation end products–receptor for advanced glycation end products) signaling pathway, chemical carcinogenesis–ROS signaling pathway, and lipid and atherosclerosis signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). In addition, GO enrichment analysis highlighted functional changes in cellular proliferation and metabolic regulation, including ATP binding, protein tyrosine kinase activity, and protein kinase activity (Fig. 2E–G).

Characterizations of scaffolds

SEM images showed that electrospun PLCL and PLCL/Lut membranes had clear fiber structures and uniform diameters (Fig. 3A). The average fiber diameter in PLCL and PLCL/Lut groups was 0.76 ± 0.12 μm and 0.78 ± 0.12 μm, respectively (Fig. 3C). These results indicated that the incorporation of Lut did not change the fiber morphology, which was consistent with previous studies [22,23]. The CS-HEC and CS-HEC/IL hydrogels filled in the nerve conduits demonstrated loose and porous structures (Fig. 3B). Further pore size counting results revealed that the addition of IL did not change the pore size of the hydrogels, which were 92.24 ± 19.12 μm and 86.36 ± 16.99 μm for the two types of hydrogels, respectively, and that pore sizes of such dimensions did not affect nutrient exchange and nerve cell growth (Fig. 3D) [24]. The electrical conductivity of CS-HEC hydrogels containing different concentrations of IL was measured. The conductivity was (0.77 ± 0.06)× 10−3, (1.69 ± 0.52)× 10−3, (3.26 ± 0.47)× 10−3, (5.64 ± 1.06)× 10−3, and (7.70 ± 1.23) × 10−3 S/cm for CS-HEC, CS-HEC/0.5%IL, CS-HEC/1%IL, CS-HEC/2%IL, and CS-HEC/5%IL, respectively. These results showed that hydrogel conductivity increased with higher IL content. Notably, the conductivity of samples with 2% and 5% IL was higher than the others (Fig. 3H).

After implantation, the conduit may be subjected to mechanical deformations such as stretching or compression due to physiological movements. Therefore, an ideal conduit should possess certain mechanical strength to withstand these dynamic mechanical challenges [25]. Such mechanical properties are crucial not only for maintaining structural integrity but also for ensuring long-term functional support during peripheral nerve regeneration. The stress–strain curve from the tensile testing is presented in Fig. 3F. The maximum tensile strengths of the PLCL and PLCL/Lut conduits were measured as 12.26 ± 0.72 MPa and 13.25 ± 1.11 MPa, respectively (Fig. 3E). Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups. Both values were higher than the normal neural tensile strength of 11 ± 1 MPa [26]. In the mechanical compression experiment, there were no significant differences in the compression mechanical results between the CS-HEC and CS-HEC/IL hydrogels (Fig. 3G and S1). The results indicated that these conduits can meet the necessary mechanical requirements for nerve repair.

The chemical composition of the CS-HEC/IL hydrogel was analyzed using FTIR. The FTIR spectrum of CS exhibited characteristic absorption bands at 3370, 2860, 1660, 1590, and 1090 cm−1, which were attributed to the stretching vibrations of N–H/O–H, C–H, C=O, NH2 bending, and C–O groups, respectively [27]. Besides, HEC, synthesized by the etherification of cellulose with ethylene oxide, displayed characteristic absorption bands at 2880 cm−1 and 3300 cm−1, corresponding to the CH2 and OH groups, respectively. Additional absorption bands at 1136 cm−1 and 1025 cm−1 were attributed to the stretching vibrations of the glycosidic ring [28]. The CS-HEC and CS-HEC/IL hydrogels exhibited these peaks, which are indicative of the successful incorporation of CS and HEC in the hydrogels. In addition to the hydrogel matrix, the presence of the IL in the hydrogel was also confirmed through FTIR analysis. The synthesized IL showed characteristic peaks at 1644, 1492, and 958 cm−1, corresponding to the C-N, C-B, and = C-H stretching vibrations of the IL, respectively, indicating that the IL was successfully synthesized [29]. Notably, the CS-HEC/IL hydrogel showed the three corresponding characteristic peaks, with the peaks slightly shifted, indicating that IL was successfully incorporated into this hydrogel (Fig. 3I).

The in vitro degradation behaviors of both the conduits and hydrogels were evaluated separately. After 30 days of degradation, the PLCL and PLCL/Lut conduits retained approximately 80% of their original mass, with no significant difference between the two groups. In contrast, the CS-HEC and CS-HEC/IL hydrogels exhibited a significantly faster degradation rate, retaining only about 40% of their initial mass, and no notable difference was found between the two hydrogels (Fig. 3J). These results suggested that the relatively slow degradation of the conduits was beneficial for maintaining structural integrity and providing mechanical support during the early stages of nerve regeneration. On the other hand, the faster degradation rate of the hydrogels likely facilitated the creation of a more dynamic microenvironment, providing space for axonal outgrowth and tissue remodeling. This complementary degradation property between the conduit and hydrogel components may be particularly advantageous in nerve regeneration applications, where sustained support and timely biodegradation are both critical [30]. The cumulative release of Lut from PLCL/Lut conduits was evaluated for up to 30 days in vitro. The cumulative release of Lut was found to be 47.84 ± 1.40% within 30 days, indicating sustained release of Lut from the PLCL/Lut conduits (Fig. 3K). This gradual release of Lut is beneficial for maintaining therapeutic concentrations in the local microenvironment, which may help to modulate inflammatory responses and create a conducive environment for peripheral nerve regeneration.

Cultivation of SCs and PC12 cells with ES in vitro

The cytotoxicity of hydrogels containing different concentrations of IL was first determined by the CCK-8 assay (Fig. S2). SCs showed favorable proliferation in hydrogels containing various concentrations of IL. Although a slight decrease in cell proliferation was observed in the CS-HEC/IL hydrogels with 5% IL group compared to the other groups, the difference was not statistically significant. Based on the proliferation and the corresponding conductivity results, the CS-HEC/IL hydrogel containing 2% IL was selected for further experiments. To investigate the biological performance of hydrogels, SCs and PC12 cells were cultured on various hydrogel substrates. Live/Dead staining was initially used to evaluate the growth status of the cells (Fig. 4A and C). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the majority of cells remained alive, with only about 3% of the total population dead after 5 days (Fig. 4B and D). These hydrogels, with or without ES, did not cause any obvious cell death in SCs and PC12 cells, indicating their good cytocompatibility.

To determine the proliferation of SCs and PC12 cells on hydrogels under ES, cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay on days 1, 3, and 5. No significant differences in SCs proliferation were observed among the four groups on day 1. By day 3, the CS-HEC/IL hydrogel significantly enhanced SCs proliferation compared to the CS-HEC hydrogel. However, on day 5, there was a significant difference between the ES-CS-HEC/IL group compared with the other three groups, indicating a synergistic effect of IL incorporation and ES (Fig. 4F). As shown in Fig. 4G, there was no significant difference between the hydrogel groups after 3 days of PC12 cell culture. At 5 days, PC12 cells cultured on IL-containing hydrogels, particularly in the ES-CS-HEC/IL group where electrical stimulation was applied, showed more favorable proliferation. Overall, the IL-containing scaffolds were not cytotoxic, and the hydrogels were more favorable to the proliferation of SCs and PC12 cells subjected to ES. Efficient clearance of myelin debris following nerve injury is critically dependent on the timely migration of SCs [31]. Therefore, the Transwell assay was used to evaluate the influence of various scaffolds on cell migration. Compared to the control group, all experimental groups promoted the migration of SCs (Fig. 4E). Quantitative analysis indicated that CS-HEC/IL hydrogels, particularly when combined with ES, facilitated higher SCs migration compared to the other groups (Fig. 4H). The integration of electrically conductive scaffolds with ES provides bioelectrical cues that support neuroregenerative processes, including cellular proliferation, migration, and signal transduction [32]. This synergistic approach facilitates the restoration of disrupted neural conduction pathways and preserves the endogenous bioelectrical microenvironment essential for functional nerve regeneration.

Anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory properties in vitro

After PNI, substantial numbers of macrophages recruited to the injury site. These cells initiate an inflammatory response essential for phagocytosis, clearing degenerating axons and myelin debris [33]. Prolonged inflammation in the absence of external intervention may lead to local tissue destruction, fibrosis, and scar formation [34]. To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of Lut in PLCL/Lut conduits, macrophages were co-cultured with scaffold extracts in vitro. DCFH-DA staining was utilized to evaluate the anti-oxidative activity of the material by measuring ROS levels in RAW 264.7 macrophages [35]. The LPS-induced RAW 264.7 macrophages exhibited a marked increase in green fluorescence compared to the LPS + PLCL/Lut and control groups, similar to that of the LPS + PLCL group (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis revealed that the ROS scavenging ratio was significantly higher in the LPS + PLCL/Lut and control groups, which further confirmed the results of DCFH-DA staining (Fig. 5B). Incorporation of Lut into the conduit significantly alleviated the LPS-induced intracellular ROS accumulation.

The CD206 (M2 marker) expression level in macrophages treated with conditioned medium from various groups was evaluated using immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5C). Quantification of CD206 expression in RAW 264.7 macrophages revealed a marked increase in fluorescence intensity in the LPS + PLCL/Lut group, indicating that Lut induced M2 polarization under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 5D). The RT-qPCR analysis results indicated that macrophages in the LPS + PLCL/Lut group presented higher gene expression levels of critical anti-inflammatory mediators, including Arg-1 and IL-10, and lower mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory mediators, such as TNF-α and iNOS (Fig. 5E). These results showed a notable anti-inflammatory function of Lut from LPS + PLCL/Lut scaffolds.

Whole transcriptome RNA sequencing analysis

To ascertain the underlying mechanisms and signaling pathways responsible for the anti-inflammatory properties of PLCL/Lut conduits, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on RAW 264.7 cells in the control and PLCL/Lut groups. A significant difference in transcriptome profiles was observed between the control and PLCL/Lut groups in the differentially expressed gene analysis (Fig. 6F). The volcano plot showed that the PLCL/Lut scaffolds exhibited 1112 downregulated genes and 889 upregulated genes compared to the control group (Fig. 6A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated GO terms were associated with pro-inflammatory response and oxidative stress, including neuroinflammatory response regulation and the ROS biosynthetic process (Fig. 6D). Simultaneously, the upregulated genes were enriched in IL-10 production and anti-oxidative activity (Fig. 6E). The chord plot further suggested a strong association between the anti-inflammatory response and its corresponding differentially expressed genes (Fig. 6B and C).

The Kyoto Encyclopedia for Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the PLCL/Lut scaffold suppressed the neutrophil extracellular trap formation and inflammatory bowel disease, while activating the p53 signaling pathway, which were associated with the polarization of macrophages and neuroprotection (Fig. 6G and H). These results suggested that PLCL/Lut plays a role in macrophage polarization through an immunomodulatory mechanism.

Histological analysis of regenerated nerves

Given the promising in vitro results, we further assessed the efficacy of PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduits in repairing nerve defects in vivo (Fig. 7A). As illustrated in Fig. 7B, nerve conduits from the autograft, PLCL-CS-HEC, PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC, and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL groups were successfully transplanted. Histological evaluation of regenerated nerves in each group was performed after 8 weeks. H&E staining was performed to assess peripheral nerve fiber regeneration, the scar tissue formation, and the degradation of the scaffolds. As shown in Fig. 7C, extensive nerve fiber regeneration was evident in all experimental groups, with no significant fibrotic scarring observed at the repair site. Furthermore, TB staining of regenerated nerves was performed to assess the myelin area and axonal maturation. The results revealed that the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group had more compact nerve fibers and a higher density of myelinated fibers than the other conduits (Fig. 7D). Statistical analysis showed the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group had a higher myelin-positive area percentage than other conduits, with no significant difference compared to the autograft group (Fig. 7F). Myelin sheaths in nervous tissue are specifically labeled with Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) staining, which gives the myelin sheath a bright blue color. LFB staining demonstrated the presence of myelin sheaths in the regenerating nerve fibers of every group (Fig. 7E). As shown in Fig. 7G, the axon diameter in the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit was different from that in the autograft group, but was still much higher than that in the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC conduits. In addition, the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit significantly enhanced both the total number and density of myelinated axons compared to the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups, though still lower than native ones (Fig. 7H and I). A statistical analysis was also performed on g-ratio, an established indicator of the physiological structure and function of neural myelination [36]. The g-ratio, defined as the ratio of the axonal diameter to the total outer diameter of the myelinated fiber, inversely reflects myelin thickness, with a lower g-ratio indicating a thicker myelin sheath and more mature myelination [37]. Notably, nerves regenerated through the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit exhibited a g-ratio distribution closely resembling that of the autograft group, suggesting that the incorporation of IL effectively promoted physiologically relevant remyelination. In contrast, the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups displayed significantly higher g-ratios, which implies thinner myelin sheaths and less efficient myelination (Fig. 7G). This finding underscores the critical role of IL in enhancing myelination and supports its potential as a conductive cue.

Overall, the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit exhibited effective reconstruction of the electrophysiological microenvironment and enhanced nerve regeneration compared to the other NGCs [38]. Although certain morphological and functional parameters in the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group were inferior to those of the autograft, the conduit's overall regenerative performance suggests its strong potential as a viable alternative for repairing long-gap peripheral nerve injuries.

Immunofluorescence staining of regenerated nerves

At 8 weeks post-surgery, middle nerve segments were subjected to immunofluorescence staining for S100 and NF200, markers of SCs maturity and myelinated axon regeneration, respectively [39]. As shown in Fig. 8A, S100 and NF200 were expressed at varying levels across all experimental groups. Nerve sections from PLCL-CS-HEC conduits exhibited limited presence of mature SCs and sparse axonal growth. In comparison, the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC group showed a moderate increase in both mature SCs and axon density. Notably, the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduits supported abundant mature SCs and robust axonal extension throughout the lumen. The expression levels of S100 and NF200, measured as the percentage of positive area, were significantly increased in rats implanted with PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit compared to the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups (Fig. 8C and D). These findings suggested improved SCs maturity and axonal regeneration in the IL-included group. The high conductivity of IL enhances neurocyte growth and supports nerve regeneration under bioelectrical stimulation, consistent with previous research [40].

To evaluate the effect of conduit implantation on the inflammatory response in vivo, immunofluorescence staining was performed 8 weeks after implantation to detect iNOS and CD206, which are markers of M1 and M2 macrophage polarization. The PLCL-CS-HEC group exhibited a higher inflammatory response, with M1 macrophages predominantly localized at the interface between the scaffold and neural tissue. In contrast, the Lut-containing groups (PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL) showed a marked reduction in M1 macrophages and a greater presence of M2 macrophages (Fig. 8B). The PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduits, which release Lut, exhibited a significantly lower percentage of iNOS positive area compared to the PLCL-CS-HEC group (Fig. 8E). In contrast, these Lut-releasing conduits promoted a significantly higher CD206 positive area, suggesting enhanced polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Fig. 8F). Following nerve injury, macrophages are driven toward the M1 phenotype in the stress environment, which exacerbates inflammation and further compromises the regenerative microenvironment. The presence of Lut within the conduit shifts the polarization of macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby mitigating local inflammation and fostering an environment more permissive for neural repair. Previous studies have indicated that polarization of macrophages into the M2 phenotype supports SCs migration and the transcription levels of genes related to myelination, thereby promoting peripheral nerve regeneration through modulation of macrophages [41,42]. Future studies may further investigate the interplay between inflammatory regulation and peripheral nerve regeneration.

Functional evaluation of regenerated nerves

The recovery of motor function was further assessed following treatment. Nerve injury often results in atrophy of the target muscles, while effective neural repair supports their recovery. Thus, recovery of both the morphology and weight of the gastrocnemius muscle provides a reliable indicator for evaluating the extent of nerve regeneration [21]. As shown in Fig. 9B, gastrocnemius muscles were collected from the surgical and normal sides of the different groups. Gastrocnemius muscle atrophy on the injured side was markedly evident in the PLCL-CS-HEC group compared to the contralateral side. However, this atrophy was mitigated in the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group, where the combined presence of Lut and IL was associated with the most notable morphological improvement. The statistical results of the muscle wet weight ratio showed that the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group was closest to the autologous transplantation group and differed significantly from the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups (Fig. 9D). To further evaluate the proportion of muscle fibers and collagen fibers within the gastrocnemius, transverse muscle sections from each experimental group were subjected to Masson's trichrome staining. Greater muscle atrophy severity coincided with increased collagen content. Significantly higher levels of blue collagen were observed in the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups relative to the autograft and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit groups (Fig. 9A). Moreover, quantitative analysis showed that the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group exhibited a larger muscle fiber diameter compared to that in the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups (Fig. 9A and E). Collectively, these findings indicated that the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit could effectively mitigate denervation-induced gastrocnemius atrophy and muscle fiber degeneration.

Electrophysiological assessment of compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs), as shown in Fig. 9C, was performed to evaluate the recovery of nerve conduction. A higher amplitude of CMAPs reflects enhanced nerve conduction and a greater number of functioning axons [43]. Additionally, faster nerve conduction velocity is associated with a greater degree of myelination [44]. The amplitude and nerve conduction velocity of the CMAPs in the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL group were comparable to those in the autograft group, whereas the PLCL-CS-HEC and PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC groups all exhibited only limited improvements (Fig. 9F and G). These findings demonstrated that the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit can substantially improve motor function and nerve conduction following PNI, highlighting its superb therapeutic potential in peripheral nerve regeneration.

Conclusion

In general, we have developed a conduit with conductive and anti-inflammatory properties by combining CS-HEC/IL hydrogels with PLCL/Lut nanofibers for the repair of peripheral nerves. This system can establish a conductive microenvironment, effectively accelerating PNI repair. Furthermore, the slow release of Lut from the conduit promotes M2 polarization of macrophages, regulating excessive inflammatory responses and oxidative stress. The synergistic repair effects provided by the PLCL/Lut-CS-HEC/IL conduit suggest its strong potential as an alternative for the treatment of peripheral nerve injury.
