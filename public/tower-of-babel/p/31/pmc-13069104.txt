Unveiling the poroelastic evolution of agar hydrogels through the drying process
Authors: Abderrahim Ed-Daoui, Noureddine Chafi, Fuad Khoshnaw, M’hammed Benelmostafa, Mohammed Dahmani, Mohammed Benaichi, Amine El Haimeur, Maryama Hammi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Agarose-based hydrogels are widely used in food technology, biotechnology, and biomedical engineering owing to their biocompatibility, tunable microstructure, and well-defined gelation properties. Despite their extensive use, the evolution of their mechanical properties during drying remains insufficiently understood. In this study, we investigate the time-dependent mechanical response of agarose hydrogels subjected to uniaxial compression during controlled ambient drying. Agarose gels of varying concentrations were prepared and characterized using compression testing, dimensional analysis, and microstructural observations. The results reveal a non-monotonic evolution of the Young’s modulus during drying. In the early stages (within the first 24 h), a slight but reproducible decrease in stiffness is observed, which is attributed to mechanical instability of the semi-flexible polymer network induced by shrinkage. At longer drying times (24–72 h), continued water loss leads to pore collapse, network densification, and a pronounced increase in stiffness. These observations indicate the presence of two competing mechanisms governing the mechanical response: network buckling at short drying times and pore buckling at extended drying times. By correlating macroscopic mechanical measurements with microstructural evolution, this work provides a mechanistic framework for understanding the drying-induced mechanical behavior of agarose hydrogels. These findings are relevant for the design and optimization of agarose-based materials in applications where controlled dehydration and mechanical stability are critical.

Introduction

Agar and agarose are natural polysaccharides extracted from red algae and are extensively used in microbiology, food processing, and biotechnology due to their ability to form thermoreversible hydrogels1,2. Agar is composed primarily of agarose (≈ 66 wt%), which is responsible for gel formation, and agaropectin (≈ 33 wt%), which does not contribute to the elastic network. Because agaropectin does not integrate into the gel structure and reduces pore uniformity, purified agarose is generally preferred for applications requiring well-controlled mechanical and transport properties, such as gel electrophoresis and chromatography.

Agarose hydrogels are also widely investigated for biomedical and industrial applications owing to their biocompatibility, high water content, and tunable pore structure. They are used as scaffolds for tissue engineering, matrices for drug delivery, separation media in molecular biology, and thickening or structuring agents in food and pharmaceutical products3–5. In many of these applications, the mechanical properties of the hydrogel—particularly its elastic modulus—play a crucial role in determining performance, durability, and functionality.

At the molecular level, agarose chains self-assemble into helical filaments that further aggregate into semi-flexible fibers. Individual agarose chains have a diameter of approximately 2 nm and a persistence length on the order of 10 nm, whereas aggregated fibers composed of multiple filaments exhibit diameters of 20–40 nm and persistence lengths ranging from hundreds of nanometers to several micrometers, depending on the strength of inter-filament interactions6–11. This hierarchical organization gives rise to a porous, viscoelastic network whose mechanical response is highly sensitive to hydration state and structural rearrangements.

While the mechanical properties of fully hydrated agarose gels have been extensively studied, significantly less attention has been paid to their behavior during drying. Drying induces water loss, shrinkage, and structural reorganization of the poroelastic network, which can profoundly alter mechanical properties. Previous studies have primarily focused on macroscopic shrinkage or drying kinetics, often neglecting the microstructural mechanisms—such as fiber and pore buckling—that may govern the mechanical response during dehydration12,13.

A fundamental question therefore remains unresolved: Which microstructural mechanisms control the mechanical evolution of agarose hydrogels during drying? In particular, it is unclear whether stiffness changes are dominated by deformation of the polymer network itself, collapse of pores, or a combination of both processes occurring at different drying stages.

In this work, we investigate the time-dependent mechanical behavior of agarose and agar hydrogels during ambient drying, with particular emphasis on the evolution of the elastic modulus under uniaxial compression. By combining rheological measurements, dimensional analysis, and microstructural observations, we aim to establish a direct link between drying-induced water loss, microstructural reorganization, and macroscopic mechanical response. The study focuses on identifying distinct drying regimes and the associated deformation mechanisms.

Our results demonstrate that drying induces a non-monotonic evolution of stiffness. An initial decrease in the elastic modulus is observed during early drying, which is attributed to mechanical instability and buckling of the semi-flexible polymer network. At longer drying times, continued evaporation leads to pore collapse and network densification, resulting in significant stiffening. This two-stage mechanism provides a consistent framework for interpreting the mechanical behavior of agarose hydrogels during drying and addresses a gap in the current understanding of drying-induced mechanical transformations in biopolymer gels.

Methods

Agarose samples

The various samples studied in this work consist of a commercial Agarose, type SHE -Reference: S18650, and another powder of Agar, type PSE, Reference: P22606, supplied by SETEXAM specializing in the study and exploitation of algae and marine products (Fig. 1).

The molecular weight (MW) distribution of the Agarose chains was analyzed by size exclusion chromatography, performed by Bio-Mérieux Company (Lyon, France). This analysis revealed that the average molecular weight of the SETEXAM Agarose molecules is approximately 1.88 × 10⁵ g/mol14,15, confirming the suitability of this polymer for hydrogel formation. The relatively narrow molecular weight distribution ensures structural homogeneity and reproducibility across all prepared samples.

The methodology and experimental workflow

Agarose hydrogel samples were prepared by dissolving agarose or agar powder in distilled water at concentrations of 5, 10, or 30 g/L16,17. The solution was homogenized with a magnetic stirrer, and the bottle was covered with aluminum foil to prevent evaporation during heating. The bottle was placed on a hot plate, and the temperature was gradually increased to 99 °C. Once the solution became clear, heating was stopped. When the temperature reached 75 °C, the mixture was poured into cylindrical Duralumin molds (14 × 14 mm), which were covered with coverslips. The mixture was left to cool at room temperature for at least 20 min, allowing the agarose hydrogels to solidify with flat, smooth surfaces suitable for unmolding18–20(Fig. 2). All prepared gels were characterized immediately after formation to ensure identical initial hydration states before the drying experiments.

Uniaxial compression of Agar hydrogels

Compression tests were carried out using a Brookfield force machine applying a vertical force on the samples. The circular plane geometry moved longitudinally with compression speeds between 0.1 mm/s and 10 mm/s on agar gel pellets of various concentrations. A thin layer of gel was added on a sandblasted glass surface to prevent slippage. Based on the load-elongation, and the resulting stress–strain diagram, the elastic modulus (E) was determined from the slope of the linear region of the curve21.

Drying of agarose hydrogels

The drying process of agarose hydrogels was systematically studied to evaluate the changes in both structural and mechanical properties under ambient conditions. Cylindrical samples of agarose hydrogels, initially measuring 14 mm in diameter and 14 mm in height, were demolded and left to dry at room temperature. Dimensional variations were monitored using a high-resolution imaging setup, capturing images at two-hour intervals over 72 h (Fig. 4).

Compression of agarose hydrogels during drying

The drying process involves the removal of water or other fluid phases, which induces changes in both the structure and mechanical properties of the hydrogel. We studied the drying behavior of both agarose and agar hydrogels of varying concentrations by observing their three-dimensional changes over time (see Fig. 5).

Upon unmolding, the agarose hydrogels exhibited smooth surfaces and geometries (height = 14 mm, diameter = 14 mm). Over the first hour of drying at room temperature, slight shrinkage of around 1–2% in height was observed, primarily due to the evaporation of water and buckling of the porous network. Rheology experiments (uniaxial compression tests) were then conducted on the dried hydrogels, which had slightly reduced dimensions (height = 13.7 mm, diameter = 13.8 mm)32–34. This procedure was repeated at intervals of increasing drying duration, from 2 h to 72 h. Drying caused variations in gel volume and mechanical properties, including significant changes in the elastic modulus and rupture stress. The volume of the agarose hydrogels as a function of time was plotted to analyze the contraction behavior during drying35,36.

Diffusion coefficients and drying behavior of agarose hydrogels.

The results presented in Table 1 illustrate the significant influence of agarose concentration on the drying dynamics, water diffusion behavior, and mechanical response of hydrogels. These findings align with earlier studies that highlight the key role of polymer density in controlling water transport and structural evolution during dehydration23,35,39.

At lower concentrations (5–10 g/l), the polymer network is more open and contains a larger volume of free water. This structural configuration facilitates a higher diffusion coefficient (in the range of 1.5–2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s), enabling rapid water loss during drying. As reported by Nguyen et al.23, this accelerated drying promotes early shrinkage, crust formation at the surface, and localized structural changes. Mechanically, these gels exhibit a temporary increase in stiffness due to surface densification but are prone to premature rupture due to the weak internal network and uneven drying-induced stresses24,40.

Conversely, higher-concentration gels (30–50 g/l) form denser networks that impede water mobility, resulting in lower diffusion coefficients (ranging from 8.0 × 10⁻¹¹ to 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s). The drying process in these samples is significantly slower, consistent with previous observations40–42 and confirmed by our Fickian diffusion modelling43,44. These gels maintain their shape for longer periods, with shrinkage occurring more gradually. The delayed water loss leads to pore-level buckling and progressive densification, resulting in a marked increase in mechanical stiffness over time, as shown by the growth of the Young’s modulus beyond 48 h of drying36,39.

This dual-phase behavior—initial fiber network buckling followed by pore collapse—mirrors the two-step mechanical reinforcement process previously described in drying biopolymer gels29,35. The experimental results also reinforce the concept of a concentration-dependent trade-off, where low concentrations favor fast drying but reduced mechanical stability, while high concentrations enable mechanical reinforcement at the cost of slower water diffusion.

These insights are critical for the design and application of agarose hydrogel systems, particularly in biomedical engineering, soft robotics, and tissue engineering, where the balance between drying kinetics and structural integrity is essential22,30,40.

Structural integrity and gel rupture during drying

Mechanical strength initially increased with drying time, reaching a maximum around 48 h, followed by a decline at longer drying times, indicating the onset of brittleness. Elongation at break decreased progressively, reflecting a transition from ductile to brittle behavior.

Higher-concentration gels maintained structural integrity longer than lower-concentration gels. These observations emphasize the importance of controlling drying kinetics to balance stiffness and fracture resistance, consistent with previous studies40–42.
