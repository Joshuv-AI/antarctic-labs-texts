Predictors of damage accrual in childhood-onset SLE: a retrospective analysis from a tertiary lupus centre in Türkiye
Authors: Alperen Eravsar, Kaan Can Demirbas, Ece Aslan, Nergis Akay, Umit Gul, Elif Kilic Konte, Esma Aslan, Aybuke Gunalp, Fatih Haslak, Mehmet Yildiz, Oya Koker, Amra Adrovic, Kenan Barut, Ozgur Kasapcopur, Sezgin Sahin

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSLE is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by multisystem involvement and fluctuating clinical course, often leading to permanent organ damage. Childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) tends to be more aggressive with increased organ involvement compared with adult-onset SLE. Despite advances in treatment, there is a rising incidence of morbidity and chronic damage in cSLE patients. This study aims to evaluate the patterns of damage and identify risk factors associated with damage accrual in a cohort of cSLE patients.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 120 patients meeting the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics 2012 criteria for cSLE, followed by the paediatric rheumatology clinic from 2004 to March 2023. After excluding 18 patients for monogenic lupus or inadequate follow-up, 102 patients were analysed. Damage accrual was assessed using the Paediatric Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology Damage Index (PedSDI) and associations between clinical, laboratory and demographic variables with damage accrual were evaluated using univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsThe mean age at diagnosis was 12.9 years, with a mean disease duration of 6.3 years. At the end of the study, 33.3% of patients had accrued damage (PedSDI≥1). The most frequently involved domains were growth failure (24%), renal (11.8%), neuropsychiatric (8.8%) and mucocutaneous (5.9%). Multivariate analysis revealed that higher median SLE Disease Activity Index-2000 scores, haemolytic anaemia and renal involvement were independent risk factors for damage accrual.ConclusionsOur study confirms that higher median disease activity over time, haemolytic anaemia and renal disease are significant predictors of long-term damage in cSLE. Additionally, growth failure was the most frequently involved domain in PedSDI, followed by renal and neuropsychiatric domains. These findings underscore the importance of early and effective disease management and regular monitoring for these risk factors.

Introduction

SLE is an autoimmune, chronic disease characterised by multiple organ system involvement.1 2 It manifests with a fluctuating clinical course over time and may culminate in permanent organ damage, particularly affecting the renal, central nervous and musculoskeletal systems. The incidence of the disease has increased dramatically over the past 40 years of the 20th century as diagnostic methods became more sensitive.3 The estimated incidence in Europe, Asia and America changes between 1 and 25 per 100 000.4

The majority of SLE cases are diagnosed between the ages of 16 and 55, with paediatric cases constituting approximately 20% of the patient population.5 It is well known that childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) tends to exhibit a more aggressive disease course and an increased tendency for major organ involvement compared with adult-onset cases. Moreover, the types of organs involved, the severity of the disease and overall prognosis significantly differ in the paediatric group, often resulting in a less favourable outcome.6–8 Previous studies have shown that childhood-onset cases accrue more irreversible damage over the long term, especially in musculoskeletal, renal and ocular domains. Even so, renal involvement is considered to be a characteristic presenting feature in cSLE. While some studies suggest that some forms of damage domains might improve over time with aggressive immunosuppressive therapy, comparative studies often report more mortality and irreversible damage in childhood-onset cases.9–12 Of course, advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches have significantly enhanced the survival rates of individuals with cSLE over time.13 14 Paradoxically, despite the improved prospects for survival, there has been an increase in rates of morbidity and permanent organ damage accrual.15

Given the significant impact of damage accrual on quality of life and its association with increased morbidity, identifying risk factors linked to damage occurrence is crucial. However, current findings on these risk factors remain heterogeneous and inconclusive. In line with this, we aimed to evaluate the association of clinical, laboratory and demographic characteristics of our cSLE patients with damage occurrence and enlighten the risk factors associated with chronic damage accrual in patients with cSLE.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

This was a retrospective, observational cohort study. At the beginning of the study, a total of 120 patients who met the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) 2012 criteria were included.16 Patients were followed up in the paediatric rheumatology outpatient clinic at Cerrahpaşa Medical School between 2004 and March 2023 with a standard protocol for a minimum duration of 1 year, with regular visits conducted at intervals of 2–6 months. After an initial review, monogenic lupus cases (n=7) and patients with inadequate follow-up data (n=11) were excluded from the study. Subsequently, a cohort of 102 patients was analysed at the end.

The primary outcome of the study was assessment of frequency and pattern of damage accrual and elucidation of the risk factors associated with it. The collection of demographic characteristics, clinical features, laboratory investigations and damage and disease activity assessments was conducted retrospectively from electronic records and patient files. All the clinical and laboratory items SLICC 2012 criteria were recorded. Routine laboratory work included complete blood count, acute phase markers, liver and renal function tests, serology investigations, complement levels, urine dipstick analysis and antibody tests. In each visit and flare, the SLE Activity Index-2000 (SLEDAI-2K) was used to assess disease activity and inflammation and guide the treatment and management modalities. SLEDAI-2K scores were extracted from patient data at 6-month intervals, including the time of diagnosis and the last visit. The cumulative SLEDAI-2K score over time was defined as the mean scores of each visit per individual patient.

Antibody tests included ANA, anti-double stranded antibody (anti-dsDNA), anti-beta 2 glycoprotein IgM and IgG, anti-cardiolipin IgM and IgG, anti-smooth muscle (anti-Sm), RNP, anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La (SSB) and lupus anticoagulants. All antibodies were studied at the clinical immunology laboratory of our university in a routine clinical setting. Immunoblotting was used to detect anti-Sm, RNP, anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La (SSB) and anti-cardiolipin antibodies. Anti-dsDNA was detected by immunofluorescence microscopy using Crithidia luciliae. Positivity was confirmed at least twice for each patient at different time points. Anti-cardiolipin IgM and IgG, anti-beta 2 glycoprotein IgM and IgG, and lupus anticoagulants were studied annually. ENA profile antibodies including anti-Sm, RNP, anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La (SSB) were studied as a caution and double-check. Besides routine visits, in each flare and hospital admission, complete blood count, acute phase markers, liver and renal function tests, creatine kinase, complete urine analysis, urine protein/creatinine ratio, complement levels and anti-dsDNA were studied.

To calculate average corticosteroid use, the dose of corticosteroids (methylprednisolone or prednisolone) administered at each visit was recorded in mg/kg. Using these values, a cumulative dose was determined for each patient. The mean of these cumulative doses across the cohort was then calculated and patients whose total exposure exceeded this mean were categorised as having received an above-average corticosteroid dose. Additionally, cyclophosphamide (CYC) was administered at a dose of 750–1000 mg/m2/month, based on the National Institutes of Health (NIH) protocol. To improve accuracy and reduce the confounding effect of body surface area differences, we evaluated the total number of pulse CYC treatments rather than the cumulative dose.

To evaluate and quantify cumulative irreversible damage, we used the Paediatric Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology Damage Index (PedSDI) at the last visit. The PedSDI encompasses a clinical assessment of 12 distinct organ systems, with a focus on identifying irreversible changes.17 For comparison, we categorised patients into two groups based on the PedSDI score: those without any damage (PedSDI=0) and those with damage (PedSDI ≥ 1). Clinical and laboratory features were classified according to the SLICC criteria and were used for the comparison to elucidate risk factors associated with damage occurrence.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS V.26.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, IBM). Descriptive data were reported as mean and SD or median and minimum-maximum values for continuous variables according to normality of distribution, and as percentages for categorical variables. Normal distributions of continuous variables were ascertained by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parameters including demographic characteristics, clinical and laboratory manifestations, activity scores and cumulative drug doses were compared between two groups with Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and with χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Logistic regression analysis was used to elucidate the risk factors associated with accrual of damage. First, a univariate analysis was performed with the dependent variable being PedSDI ≥1 to determine potential risk factors. Variables with p values smaller than 0.05 in univariate analysis and other factors that were determined to be potentially risky in previous research (age and disease duration) were further analysed with multivariate regression. While constructing our multivariate model, we aimed for a balance between statistical power and accuracy. Considering the incorporation of 5–6 variables in our model, we aimed for a minimum of 10–20 cases with the outcome for each variable, guiding our sample size decisions.

The disease duration and age at study onset were 6.3±3.2 years and 18.7±4.0 years, respectively. Mucocutaneous manifestations were the most prevalent feature affecting 81.4% of the patients. Among mucocutaneous manifestations, the most common one was malar rash, which was seen in 73.5% of cases, followed by oral/nasal ulcers and photosensitivity. The second most common finding was musculoskeletal involvement, which was seen in 52% of our cohort. Renal involvement was identified in 30.4% of our subjects. Of those with renal involvement, 90% underwent kidney biopsy during the study period, which revealed class III and IV lupus nephritis in 10% (n=3) and 45% (n=14) of those with renal involvement, respectively. Only 13.7% of our patients (n=14) have suffered from neuropsychiatric manifestations.

The most common haematological finding in our patient population was leuko/lymphopenia, affecting 52% of individuals. Additionally, thrombocytopaenia and haemolytic anaemia were observed in 26% and 24.5% of patients, respectively. Low levels of C3 or C4 were seen in 63.7% of all. Moreover, 47.1% of the patients showed decreased levels of both complement proteins. The most common positive antibodies were ANA and anti-dsDNA with positivity rates of 98% and 83%, respectively. The positivity rates of other autoantibodies (anti-RNP, anti-SSA, anti-SSB) are given in table 1.

Almost all patients received hydroxychloroquine (93.1%, n=95) and corticosteroid (89.2%, n=91). Azathioprine was the most commonly used corticosteroid-sparing agent, followed by CYC and mycophenolate mofetil. A statistically significant difference in the number of CYC pulses was observed between patients with damage (median: 3 (0–12)) and those without damage (median: 0 (0–8)) (p<0.001).

On conducting univariate analysis to assess the clinical and laboratory variables between two distinct groups, namely the ‘absence of disease damage group’ (PEDSDI=0) and the ‘presence of disease damage group’ (PEDSDI≥1), we identified significant associations between disease damage accrual and the following variables: serositis (p=0.001, OR=10.0 (95% CI 1.98 to 50.27)), neurological involvement (p=0.003, OR=6.65 (95% CI 1.87 to 22.93)), renal disease (p=0.003, OR=3.78 (95% CI 1.54 to 9.25)), haemolytic anaemia (p=0.003, OR=3.99 (95% CI 1.53 to 10.40)) and leuko/lymphopenia (p=0.021, OR=2.74 (95% CI 1.15 to 6.51)). Furthermore, anti-RNP positivity (p=0.014), over average corticosteroid dose (p=0.016), CYC pulse number (p=0.001) and median SLEDAI-2K score (p<0.001) were associated with accrual of damage. Discussion

This study aimed to determine the frequency and patterns of disease-related damage and to explore the associations between clinical and laboratory variables of cSLE and the presence of damage in major organ systems. Almost one-third of our patients accrued damage over a mean disease duration of 6.3 years. Furthermore, multivariate regression analysis showed that lupus nephritis, presence of haemolytic anaemia and median SLEDAI-2K score throughout disease duration were risk factors associated with damage accrual. These findings highlight the importance of early and robust control of disease activity and a more watchful approach to patients with haemolytic anaemia and lupus nephritis.

In our study, damage accrual (PedSDI>0) was observed in 33.3% of the 102 patients. Our findings align with previously reported damage frequencies of 28%–60%. While damage accrual rates in cohorts from approximately 20 years ago were higher, ranging from 50% to 60%, more recent studies have reported relatively lower rates of 29%–33%.18–22 Despite the similar average disease duration, we believe the decrease in damage rates over the years is primarily due to improved understanding of disease-related damage and advancements in treatment strategies. Renal damage, observed in 12 patients (11.8%), was the most frequently involved damage domain, followed by neuropsychiatric and mucocutaneous domains in 9 (8.8%) and 6 patients (5.9%), respectively, with a mean PedSDI of 0.92 (range 0–7). Similar to other studies, the renal, neuropsychiatric, mucocutaneous and musculoskeletal domains were the most commonly affected. Our data confirmed that cardiovascular damage, diabetes, malignancy and gonadal failure, which are included in PED/SDI, are almost never seen in childhood.19–21 23 24 However, it has been reported that the frequency of these types of damage increases with prolonged disease and follow-up in adult studies.25

The distribution of organ-specific damage varies across numerous studies, with data from nine different cohorts presented in the heat map (figure 2). Renal damage was the most frequently observed type of damage in our patients, with a damage rate of 11.8%, consistent with findings from other studies.13 14 The rate of ocular involvement reported in previous studies ranges from 6% to 43%, whereas only 4% of our cohort developed ocular damage. We attribute this lower rate, particularly the reduced incidence of cataracts commonly reported in other series, to regular 6-month follow-ups with ophthalmologists and the implementation of corticosteroid-sparing treatment strategies based on examination results in our routine practice.19–21 23 Similarly, the 2% rate of musculoskeletal damage observed in our cohort was lower than that reported in many other studies. We believe this reduction is attributable to the adoption of corticosteroid-sparing agents, which likely contributed to the low rate of musculoskeletal involvement, with avascular necrosis observed in only one patient.

The SLICC/American College of Rheumatology (SLICC/ACR) Damage Index (SDI) was adapted to paediatrics by Gutierrez-Suarez in 2006 to create the PED/SDI damage scale for paediatric patients.26 This adaptation included items such as growth failure and delayed puberty, which are considered permanent damage in the paediatric age group, in a multinational cSLE series of 1015 patients. Although damage frequencies across organ domains vary among studies, growth failure is consistently identified as the most common form of permanent damage in nearly all series.26–28 Growth failure, which is thought to be related to chronic disease and the side effects of treatment, has been reported in the range of 15%–40% in previous studies. In our series, when patients were evaluated using growth curves appropriate for their age, growth failure was detected in approximately one-quarter of them. This finding highlights the need for all cSLE patients, especially those in the adolescent age group, to be followed by a multidisciplinary team that includes a paediatric endocrinologist.

The most striking finding in our study was that the presence of haemolytic anaemia during disease follow-up had been identified as a risk factor for predicting long-term organ damage in cSLE patients. A recent study by Rodríguez-Lozano et al with a similar number of patients yielded a similar result.27 However, while Rodríguez-Lozano et al identified the presence of haemolytic anaemia at diagnosis as a risk factor, our study found that haemolytic anaemia at any point during the disease course served as a prognostic factor. A study by Artim-Esen et al demonstrated that the presence of haemolytic anaemia was associated with more active disease and a poorer prognosis, which supports our finding.29

A primary objective of our study was to investigate the relationship between disease activity and permanent organ damage. We found that the cumulative SLEDAI-2K score, reflecting the average disease activity over the follow-up period, was an independent risk factor for predicting damage. Recent studies have shown that higher disease activity at the time of diagnosis is an independent risk factor for organ damage, supporting our findings.19 In addition, our study evaluated the activity score not only at diagnosis but also longitudinally throughout the disease course. This highlights the critical importance of controlling disease activity to prevent damage, a finding highly relevant to clinical practice.

Finally, the association between cumulative corticosteroid dose and damage remains inconsistent across studies. While Brunner et al demonstrated that an increase in cumulative corticosteroid dose is protective, another study showed that it is an independent risk factor for the development of damage.18 23 In our study, univariate analyses demonstrated that corticosteroid use above the average dose (p=0.016) and the number of intravenous CYC pulse therapies (p=0.001) were associated with damage development. There are similar studies indicating that the number of CYC pulse treatments is an independent risk factor for damage.21

This study has a few limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design may introduce bias in data collection and interpretation. Second, the relatively small cohort size may limit the generalisability of the findings. Third, although the follow-up period averaged 6.3 years, it may not fully capture the entire spectrum of damage accrual in cSLE patients, particularly in those who develop damage later in their disease course. Finally, variations in treatment protocols and follow-up practices across different centres may also affect the relevance of the observed outcomes. Finally, while the PedSDI index is used to assess the accrual of permanent damage in cSLE patients, it should be noted that the damage scores paradoxically might improve during the follow-up period, especially when renal involvement improves with intensive therapies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the significant relationship between disease activity and long-term organ damage in cSLE patients. The findings emphasise the importance of early and effective disease control, particularly in patients with lupus nephritis and haemolytic anaemia. Our paediatric lupus cohort most frequently accrued damage in growth. Despite the advances in treatment strategies and a better understanding of the disease, vigilant monitoring and a multidisciplinary approach remain crucial in minimising damage accrual in this vulnerable patient population. Further research with larger, prospective cohorts is necessary to confirm these findings and to refine treatment protocols for optimal patient outcomes.
