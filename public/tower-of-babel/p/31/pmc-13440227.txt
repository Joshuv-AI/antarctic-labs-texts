Unveiling the antibacterial activity and mechanisms of BMS-303141 against Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Junhua Ma, Qiqi Lan, Xuancheng Huang, Zhichao Xu, Zhijian Yu, Zewen Wen, Xiaoju Liu, Zhong Chen, Duoyun Li, Bing Bai, Tieying Hou, Peiyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), particularly methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), poses ongoing clinical challenges, driving an urgent need for novel antibacterial agents. Here, we report the first identification of BMS-303141 (an ATP-citrate lyase inhibitor) as a potent anti-staphylococcal compound that induces multi-pathway cellular disruption. BMS-303141 exhibited potent antibacterial activity against MRSA, with the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ranging from 6.25 to 12.5 μM (2.65–5.30 μg/mL) and inhibited biofilm formation at sub-MIC concentrations. Integrative proteomics and whole-genome sequencing revealed multi-pathway cellular disruption involving cell wall integrity and protein synthesis pathways, as indicated by differentially expressed proteins and mutated genes (Atl, LytH, Rot, OatA, Asd, ClfA, FnbA, SdrD, dltB). Mechanistic studies further demonstrated disruption of membrane phospholipid homeostasis and peptidoglycan homeostasis by BMS-303141. Critically, in mouse wound and thigh muscle infection models, BMS-303141 significantly reduced bacterial burden and accelerated healing, demonstrating therapeutic potential against S. aureus. Together, these findings indicate that BMS-303141 is a promising lead for developing new antibacterial agents against S. aureus and its resistant strains.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), a prevalent Gram-positive pathogen, poses a significant global health threat due to its pathogenicity and drug resistance [1]. This conditional pathogen can induce a broad range of illnesses, spanning from mild skin infections to severe conditions such as pneumonia, infective endocarditis, osteomyelitis, and sepsis, particularly when the host's immune system is compromised [2, 3]. The ability of S. aureus to form biofilms further complicates clinical treatment, as these biofilms can cause device-related infections and increase resistance to both the host immune system and antibacterial drugs. This leads to chronic infections and treatment failure [4, 5]. The widespread use of antibiotics has exacerbated antibiotic resistance in S. aureus, and the emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has further complicated efforts to control transmission and select effective antibacterial agents [6]. To address these challenges, researchers have explored multiple strategies, including targeting the cell membrane and cell wall of S. aureus to develop new antibacterial agents. The cell membrane of S. aureus contains various phospholipids, and changes in its composition can affect cellular metabolism, stress response, virulence, and drug sensitivity [7]. Additionally, peptidoglycan, the main component of the cell wall, and its synthesis and degradation processes are also important targets for antibacterial drugs [8]. Despite ongoing efforts to develop new antibacterial agents, bacterial resistance continues to outpace new drug discovery. The continuous emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria underscores the urgent need for novel antibacterial mechanisms and therapeutic approaches to address this global health challenge.

BMS-303141 is a potent inhibitor of cellular permeable ATP citrate lyase (ACL), affecting cellular metabolism by reducing intracellular acetyl-CoA production through ACL inhibition [9, 10]. In HepG2 cells, BMS-303141 inhibits total fat synthesis with an IC₅₀ of 8 µM without cytotoxicity. Studies demonstrate that oral administration of BMS-303141 in mice effectively inhibits de novo lipogenesis [11]. Furthermore, intraperitoneal injection of BMS-303141 significantly modulates the local and systemic inflammatory profiles and regulates histone acetylation, thereby improving trabecular bone loss in mice with ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis [12]. Additionally, BMS-303141 can decrease the survival rate of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells in a time- and dose-dependent manner [13]. These findings highlight the broad therapeutic potential of BMS-303141 in various diseases, including cancer and metabolic disorders. In our antibacterial activity screening of the small molecule bioactive library, BMS-303141 was identified as an inhibitor of bacterial growth and biofilm formation. Given its previously evaluated safety and tolerability in clinical trials, these findings provide a foundation for further exploring its clinical safety and efficacy in treating bacterial infections.

To date, no studies have reported the anti-S. aureus activity of BMS-303141. This study aims to elucidate the role of this compound in antibacterial applications, thereby offering a novel alternative for the development of its potential uses.

Materials and methods

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration(MIC)

The antimicrobial activity of BMS-303141 against S. aureus, S. epidermidis, E. faecalis and E. faecium was evaluated through microdilution broth assays, performed in accordance with CLSI guidelines (M100-33rd edition and M07-11th edition). The bacterial culture, grown overnight, was standardized to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland units and subsequently diluted 1:100 in cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth (CAMHB). Serial two-fold dilutions of BMS-303141 were prepared in 96-well microplates, with each well containing a 1:1 mixture of drug solution and diluted bacterial suspension (final inoculum: ~ 5 × 105 CFU/mL; total volume/well: 100 µL). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Wells containing only the bacterial culture (without BMS-303141) served as positive controls, while wells containing only CAMHB (without bacterial culture or drug solution) served as negative controls. The MIC value was recorded as the minimal drug concentration of BMS-303141 at which no visible bacterial proliferation was detected after 24 h of incubation.

Growth curve assay

Monoclonal colonies of S. aureus were selected and inoculated into TSB (Tryptic Soy Broth) medium. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C with agitation at 220 rpm overnight until reaching the stationary growth phase. The drugs were then diluted with TSB in a specially designed honeycomb growth curve plate. An equal volume of the adjusted bacterial solution was dispensed into each well. The 96-well plate was transferred to a growth curve analyzer (Finnish Bioscreen), and the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was continuously monitored for 24 h. Growth curves for each strain were constructed based on the recorded OD600 values. Wells without BMS-303141 served as growth controls, while wells containing only TSB medium served as negative controls.

Time-kill assay

The growth curve was evaluated following the methodology described in the previous study [14]. Two strains of S. aureus (YUSA145, SA113) were selected for the time-kill assays. Concentrations of 4 × and 8 × MIC were chosen to evaluate the robust bactericidal kinetics of BMS-303141, as these multiples represent the threshold concentrations at which consistent and significant bactericidal activity was observed and are commonly employed in time-kill evaluations [15, 16]. Bacterial suspensions in the logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.5) were diluted 100-fold and incubated with BMS-303141 (4 × and 8 × MIC) and linezolid (8 × MIC) respectively at 200 rpm on a shaking incubator. At 0, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h, 100 µL aliquots of each suspension were collected for serial dilution and subsequent plate counting. Colony counts were performed after 24 h of incubation, and the number of viable cells was quantified by measuring the colony-forming units (CFU).

Biofilm inhibition and eradication assays

The impact of BMS-303141 on biofilm development by S. aureus was evaluated using crystal violet staining. The experimental method is based on our previous study [17]. Briefly, 100 µL of an overnight bacterial suspension of S. aureus was diluted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL in fresh TSBG (TSB supplemented with 2% glucose) and transferred to a microtiter plate. Subsequently, 100 µL of BMS-303141 was added to the wells at concentrations of 1/2 and 1/4 × MIC, creating a two-fold gradient dilution. Sterile TSBG medium acted as the negative control, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was utilized as the positive control. The incubation of the plates was conducted at 37 °C for a duration of 24 h. Upon completion of incubation, the culture supernatant was gently removed, and the wells were rinsed thrice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The plate was then air-dried at ambient temperature. The biofilms were exposed to a 1% crystal violet (15 min), after which the stain was removed and the plate was cleansed with distilled water to remove unbound dye. Biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring OD570 using a microplate reader.

To evaluate the ability of BMS-303141 to disrupt preformed biofilms, S. aureus was incubated in TSBG medium at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm maturation. Following the initial incubation period, the culture supernatant was carefully removed, and the wells were washed three times with sterile PBS to eliminate planktonic bacteria. Subsequently, 200 µL of fresh TSBG containing various concentrations of BMS-303141 was added to each well, and the plates were further incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Wells treated with sterile TSBG and DMSO served as the negative and positive controls, respectively. After the second incubation, the culture supernatant was carefully removed, and the wells were washed twice with sterile PBS. The biofilms were then stained with 1% crystal violet, and the residual biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring the OD₅₇₀ as described above.

Biofilm visualization by confocal laser scanning microscopy

Biofilm-embedded S. aureus was visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) following treatment with BMS-303141. MRSA YUSA145 and MSSA SA113 were cultured overnight, diluted 1:200 in fresh TSBG, seeded into confocal dishes, and incubated for 24 h to allow biofilm formation. After two PBS washes, the medium was replaced with fresh TSBG containing BMS-303141 (4 ×, 8 ×, and 16 × MIC), linezolid (8 × and 16 × MIC), or DMSO (vehicle control), and incubated for an additional 24 h. Biofilms were then washed with PBS, stained with SYTO 9 (live, green, 500–550 nm) and propidium iodide (PI; dead, red, 600–650 nm) for 30 min, and imaged by CLSM (Olympus FV3000, Japan) using a 100 × oil-immersion objective.

Assessment of cytotoxicity using the CCK-8 assay

HEK-293 T and HUVEC were inoculated into 96-well microplates at densities of 1.25 × 104 cells/well for HEK-293 T and 8 × 103 cells/well for HUVEC. The cells were subsequently maintained in a CO₂ incubator at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ conditions for 24 h. Different concentrations of drugs were added by replacing the culture medium. After 24 h of drug exposure, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay: 10 μL of detection reagent was added to each well, with subsequent incubation for 60–90 min before measuring absorbance at 450 nm. In the experiment, wells containing only medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) were employed as the background control, and wells containing cells without drug treatment were utilized as the cell growth control. Two cell lines were maintained in DMEM medium enriched with 10% FBS. The cell viability was determined using the following formula: Cell viability (%) = (OD sample well—OD pure culture medium well)/(OD drug-free well—OD pure culture medium well) × 100.

Hemolysis assay

A 8% erythrocyte suspension was formulated by diluting defibrinated sheep blood with pre-cooled 1 × PBS. The drug was subjected to a two-fold serial dilution with 1 × PBS in a 96-well plate. Subsequently, 100 µL of the 8% erythrocyte suspension was introduced into each well containing the diluted drug solution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h (final volume: 200 µL/well, red blood cell concentration:4%). After incubation, samples were centrifuged (1500 rpm, 5 min, RT). Aliquots (100 µL) of supernatant were carefully transferred to a fresh 96-well plate for OD550 measurement. In this experiment, DMSO, corresponding to the volume of the drug at the highest concentration, served as the negative control, while 1% Triton X-100 was used as the positive control. The hemolysis rate was calculated using the following formula: Hemolysis rate (%) = (OD experimental well—OD negative)/(OD positive—OD negative) × 100.

Mouse skin wound and leg muscle infection models

As previously reported by Liu et al. [18], 6- to 8-week-old female Balb/c mice were used, with twelve mice per group. A 1:100 dilution of an overnight S. aureus USA300 culture was prepared in TSB medium and grown to the logarithmic phase. The bacterial solution was subjected to centrifugation, rinsed three times with sterile 0.9% NaCl, and adjusted to an appropriate concentration. Mice were rendered unconscious through intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital sodium. The backs of the mice were shaved and depilated using depilatory cream. After local disinfection with alcohol, a 6 mm diameter skin punch was used to create a full-thickness skin wound. A volume of 10 μL of the USA300 bacterial solution (1 × 10⁷ CFU) was applied to each wound. Four hours post-infection, 15 μL of normal saline, BMS-303141 (15 mg/kg), or vancomycin (15 mg/kg) was applied topically to the wound site. The treatment was administered twice daily for two days. Wound healing was documented daily by photography. On Day 5, mice were euthanized, and the wound skin was removed and processed into a homogenate in 1 mL of 0.9% NaCl. A 100 μL aliquot of the homogenate was subjected to a tenfold serial dilution with 0.9% NaCl. Subsequently, 50 μL of the diluted homogenate was spread onto sterile TSB agar plates. Following a 24-h incubation period at 37 °C, viable bacteria in the wound were quantified by counting CFU.

For the deep muscle infection model [19, 20], stationary phase S. aureus USA300 was centrifuged and resuspended in PBS. A volume of 100 μL (2 × 10⁷ CFU) of the bacterial suspension was injected into the right thigh of C57BL mice. After 24 h, a severe and refractory deep infection was established. BMS-303141 and vancomycin (15 mg/kg) were given intraperitoneally every 12 h, totaling four doses. Mice were euthanized 48 h post-infection. The infected thigh tissue was aseptically excised, homogenized, and subjected to serial dilution. Aliquots (100 μL) were spread onto sterile TSB agar plates for bacterial enumeration to assess in vivo antibacterial efficacy.

Mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory. For euthanasia, they were first deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane inhalation and then euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Addition of exogenous additives to assess BMS-303141 antibacterial activity

The impact of exogenous additives on BMS-303141's MIC was evaluated through checkerboard dilution assays in broth media. Tested additive concentrations spanned 0–128 µg/mL. A monoclonal S. aureus strain (MSSA SA113) was cultured overnight in TSB at 37 °C with orbital shaking (220 rpm). The exogenous additives and BMS-303141 were prepared in multiple ratios and combined in various concentration mixtures. The bacterial inoculum was standardized to 5 × 105 CFU per milliliter, with 100 µL aliquots dispensed into microtiter plate wells. Following 24-h incubation at 37 °C, MIC values were established by identifying the lowest drug concentration showing no turbidity. Control experiments confirmed that the exogenous additives, at the tested concentrations, did not inhibit bacterial growth when used alone. Additionally, the MIC of BMS-303141 alone was determined in wells containing only BMS-303141. Wells with only the culture medium served as positive controls for bacterial growth.

In vitro induction of drug resistance

Non-repetitive bacterial strains were collected, and the MIC of the original strains was assessed via broth microdilution. Overnight cultures were grown in TSB at 37 °C with agitation (200 rpm), diluted 1:200 before testing. Various concentrations of BMS-303141 were added to induce resistance. If bacterial growth was poor, the BMS-303141 concentration was reduced, and repeated subculturing was performed. The drug induction dose was gradually increased in accordance with bacterial growth. The MIC values for the induced drug-resistant strains were ascertained via the broth dilution technique. To ensure the stability of the induced drug-resistant strains (i.e., to confirm that drug resistance was due to genetic mutations rather than phenotypic changes), the strains were passaged five times in antibiotic-free medium before their MIC values were re-determined. The interpretation criteria from the 2023 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI M100-Ed33) were used, and the MIC values of the quality control strains were simultaneously determined for quality control purposes.

Whole-genome sequencing and SNP analysis

The MUMmer alignment software was used to perform global alignments between each sample and the reference sequence, identify loci with sequence differences for preliminary filtering, and detect potential SNP loci. For each identified SNP locus in the reference sequence, 100 bp flanking sequences were retrieved. BLAT software was employed to align these sequences with the assembly data for SNP validation. Finally, BLAST, TRF, and RepeatMasker software were employed to predict repetitive sequence regions in the reference sequence. SNPs located within these repetitive regions were filtered out to obtain a set of reliable SNPs.

Protein extraction and preparation for mass spectrometry

Bacteria treated with 1/2 × MIC BMS-303141 for 2 h were collected and resuspended in RIPA buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS, along with protease inhibitors. Mechanical lysis was achieved by vortexing with 0.1 mm glass beads for 1 min, repeated for 3 rounds, and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation (12,000 g, 20 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatant was subjected to protein quantification via BCA assay. For proteomic processing, 100 µg of protein was denatured with 10 mM dithiothreitol (70 °C, 1 h) and subsequently alkylated using 50 mM iodoacetamide (room temperature, 15 min, dark). Buffer exchange was carried out with 0.5 M NH₄HCO₃ (3 × 100 µL washes) using a 10 kDa centrifugal filter. Trypsin digestion (1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio) proceeded overnight at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were reconstituted in 30 µL of 0.1% FA and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (Q Exactive Plus platform).

Membrane permeability assay

Cultures of S. aureus CHS101 and SA113 at mid-exponential phase were collected, washed once or twice with PBS, and reconstituted to an optical density (OD₆₀₀) of 0.5. Subsequent to 20-min incubation with 1 μM propidium iodide (λex/λem: 540/621 nm) in the dark, bacterial suspensions were challenged with BMS-303141 at concentrations corresponding to 1 ×, 2 ×, or 4 × MIC. Treatments with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 1% DMSO were included as positive and vehicle controls, respectively. A Cytation 5 multimode reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) was employed to monitor fluorescence at 5-min intervals for 60 min at 37 °C using black 96-well plates.

Proteomics data analysis

A Q Exactive Plus instrument was utilized to obtain mass spectrometry results. The Proteome Discoverer 2.4 software was used to perform an initial proteomic library search using the S. aureus NCTC 8325/PS 47 reference proteome from Uniprot, obtaining protein abundance values for each control and sample group. Subsequently, the Omicsbean online platform was utilized to process the omics data, generating datasets and images of differentially expressed proteins. Each experimental group included at least three technical replicates. Differential expression was assessed using a threshold of Fold Change ≥ 2 and p < 0.05 to identify up- and down-regulated proteins. Protein interaction networks were visualized using the STRING online platform.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and graph plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.1 statistical software. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and differences among experimental groups were analyzed using t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

BMS-303141 exhibited stronger antibacterial activity against S. aureus and S. epidermidis (MIC: 6.25–12.5 μM (2.65–5.30 μg/mL)) than against E. faecalis. Antimicrobial susceptibility of BMS-303141 against S. aureus

Automatic growth curve analysis revealed that BMS-303141 at 1 × MIC effectively and continuously inhibited the planktonic growth of both MRSA and MSSA compared with the control group. At 1/2 × MIC, BMS-303141 significantly inhibited early bacterial growth and prolonged the time to reach the logarithmic phase in S. aureus. A modest but non-significant inhibitory effect was observed at 1/4 × MIC compared to the untreated control, whereas no significant inhibition was detected at 1/8 × MIC (Fig. 1A–D).

In time-killing studies of logarithmic-phase S. aureus, BMS-303141 demonstrated superior in vitro bactericidal activity compared with linezolid. Treatment with BMS-303141 at 8 × MIC for 4 to 6 h killed the vast majority of planktonic bacterial cells in clinical isolates YUSA145 and SA113 within 24 h (Fig. 1E, 1 F), indicating more rapid bactericidal activity than linezolid at 8 × MIC. Notably, even at a concentration of 4 × MIC, BMS-303141 exhibited stronger bactericidal activity than linezolid at 8 × MIC. Overall, BMS-303141 demonstrates potent activity against planktonic S. aureus in vitro.

BMS-303141 displays antibiofilm efficacy and in vitro safety

The antibiofilm activity of BMS-303141 against S. aureus was first quantified by crystal violet staining (OD₅₇₀). Because biofilm reduction may reflect either decreased bacterial viability or impaired matrix secretion, planktonic growth was concurrently monitored at OD₆₀₀ to differentiate these mechanisms. At 1/4 × and 1/2 × MIC, BMS-303141 significantly suppressed both planktonic growth and biofilm formation (Fig. 2A–D), with biofilm inhibition being more pronounced. Notably, at 1/4 × MIC, biofilm inhibition exceeded planktonic growth suppression, indicating specific antibiofilm activity beyond general antibacterial effects.

We next assessed the efficacy of BMS-303141 against 24-h-old mature biofilms of S. aureus YUSA145 and SA113. Crystal violet staining demonstrated that BMS-303141 significantly disrupted these established biofilms (Fig. 2E). To further visualize architectural changes and bacterial viability, live/dead fluorescence staining was performed using SYTO 9 (live cells, green) and PI (dead cells, red), followed by CLSM analysis. Three-dimensional reconstructions revealed that, compared with the untreated control, linezolid (LZD) exerted only a modest bactericidal effect. In contrast, BMS-303141 at 8 × and 16 × MIC markedly increased the proportion of dead cells and reduced biofilm thickness (Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the potent anti-biofilm capacity of BMS-303141.

Drug resistance mutations map to cell-wall homeostasis and stress-sensing genes

After inducing YUSA145 for 19 generations and CHS101 for 17 generations with 2 × MIC BMS-303141, the MIC values of both strains increased from 12.5 to 50 μM (5.30–21.20 μg/mL) (Fig. 5A, 5B). To identify the genetic basis of resistance, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and non-synonymous SNP analysis were performed on the drug-resistant mutants and their parental strains. Compared with the reference genome, the BMS-303141-induced MRSA YUSA145 strain harbored three non-synonymous SNPs: two in dltB—which encodes the teichoic acid D-alanyltransferase involved in wall teichoic acid synthesis—and one in a histidine kinase that functions as a core sensor of bacterial two-component signal transduction systems. Similarly, the resistant MSSA CHS101 strain carried three non-synonymous SNPs in genes encoding the 30S ribosomal protein S1, the cell wall metabolism sensor histidine kinase WalK, and a member of the Tex protein family (Table 3). In summary, BMS-303141-induced resistance mutations are concentrated in genes critical for cell wall/membrane homeostasis and stress sensing (dltB, walK, histidine kinase, 30S ribosomal protein S1, and a Tex-family member), indicating that these pathways likely constitute the primary targets of this compound.

Proteomics reveals cell-wall, metabolic, redox, and ribosomal perturbations in MRSA treated with BMS-303141

Label-free quantitative proteomics was used to profile the proteome-wide response of MRSA YUSA145 following 2 h treatment with 1/2 × MIC (6.25 μM/2.65 μg/mL) BMS-303141. In total, 1,534 proteins (≥ 1 unique peptide) were confidently identified. Compared with the vehicle control, 341 proteins showed significant differential abundance (|log₂FC|≥ 1, p ≤ 0.05), including 237 up-regulated (69.5%) and 104 down-regulated (30.5%) proteins. Among these, Asd (aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase) was markedly altered; this enzyme is central to the aspartate-family amino acid biosynthetic pathway and crucial for growth, cell wall synthesis and virulence. Proteins involved in cell-wall metabolism (Atl, LytH, OatA, Rot) and adhesion/invasion factors associated with the cell envelope (ClfA, FnbA, SdrD) were similarly affected (Fig. 6A). Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that the upregulated proteins were associated with 175 biological processes, 9 cellular components, 56 molecular functions, and 21 KEGG pathways. By contrast, the downregulated proteins were linked to 107 biological processes, 31 cellular components, 52 molecular functions, and 9 KEGG pathways (Fig. 6B, 6 C). Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment indicated that the oxidation–reduction process was the most significantly enriched biological process among up-regulated proteins, whereas organic substance metabolic processes were most prominently repressed. Regarding molecular function, catalytic activity was the most enriched among up-regulated proteins, whereas ribosomal structural constituents, structural molecule activity, and RNA binding were significantly diminished (Fig. 6D, 6E). This aligns with the observation that oxidative stress elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can modify ribosomal proteins and rRNA, thereby impairing ribosomal function and efficiency. KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that BMS-303141 exposure markedly up-regulated metabolic pathways, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and microbial metabolism in diverse environments. Conversely, ribosomal biogenesis and pyrimidine metabolism degradation were significantly down-regulated (Fig. 6G, 6H).

A total of 341 differential proteins were screened and identified using the UniProt database. Among them, 126 proteins were selected based on their 'Reviewed' status. These were analyzed using the STRING platform to predict protein–protein interactions.A mutual confidence threshold of 0.900 was applied, disconnected nodes were excluded, and the resulting protein–protein interaction network is shown in Fig. 5F. Network analysis revealed that differentially expressed proteins are tightly interconnected with ribosomal components (red dotted circle), underscoring the ribosome's central role in the observed protein expression changes. Specifically, the network included ribosomal proteins involved in the synthesis, assembly, and stabilization of the 30S ribosomal subunit, such as RpsO, RpsZ, RpsR, RpsK, RpsD, and InfC. Additionally, key ribosomal proteins like RpsL and the ribosomal recycling factor Frr, which are essential for translation, were also identified, along with other ribosome-related proteins RpsU, RpmL, Hpf, RpmF, RplU, RplP, RpmB, and RplO. Beyond ribosomal proteins, the interaction network encompassed several functional categories. Ureases (UreA, UreC, and UreG) involved in bacterial nitrogen metabolism were identified. Proteins associated with amino acid metabolism, including threonine dehydrase (TdcB) and glycine cleavage system proteins (GcvPB, GcvPA, and GcvT), were also present. Furthermore, enzymes involved in redox reactions, such as alcohol dehydrogenases (RocA and Adh), aldehyde dehydrogenase (AldA), 2-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (OdhB), and dihydropyridine dicarboxylate reductase (DapB), were identified within the network. In summary, label-free proteomics revealed that BMS-303141 treatment of MRSA YUSA145 broadly perturbed cell wall, metabolic, and redox proteins while markedly down-regulating ribosomal biogenesis factors, suggesting ribosome-centered network disruption.

Dual disruption of membrane permeability and cell-wall integrity by BMS-303141

SNP and proteomic analyses suggest that BMS-303141 may interfere with peptidoglycan homeostasis and membrane phospholipid homeostasis. To test this, we examined its effects on membrane integrity. After exposing bacteria to increasing concentrations of BMS-303141, propidium iodide (PI) staining revealed a dose-dependent rise in fluorescence (Fig. 7A, B), indicating increased membrane permeability. These data suggest that BMS-303141's antibacterial effect may involve disruption of membrane phospholipid homeostasis.

Given that cell membrane damage may be associated with phospholipid anabolism, we investigated whether BMS-303141 could induce cell membrane damage by interfering with phospholipid synthesis and metabolism. Phospholipids, such as phosphatidylglycerol (PG), cardiolipin (CL), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylcholine (PC), are key components of bacterial cell membranes. Therefore, we examined the MIC changes of BMS-303141 against S. aureus SA113 in the presence of exogenous membrane phospholipids. Specifically, in media containing exogenous PE, PG, and CL at concentrations of 128 μg/mL, the MIC of SA113 increased eightfold (Fig. 7C-E). These data support the hypothesis that BMS-303141 may disrupt cell membrane integrity by interfering with phospholipid metabolism.

To examine BMS-303141's interaction with bacterial cell walls, we supplemented the medium with PGN, the primary structural component of Gram-positive cell walls. This significantly reduced the antibacterial activity of BMS-303141 against S. aureus (strains SA113 and YUSA145), increasing the MIC values 4- to eightfold relative to the original values (Fig. 7F, 7G). Collectively, these functional assays demonstrate that BMS-303141 compromises both membrane permeability and cell-wall integrity, and its activity is reversed by exogenous phospholipids or peptidoglycan, suggesting that it likely targets phospholipid metabolism and wall synthesis in S. aureus.

BMS-303141 exhibits in vivo antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus

To evaluate BMS-303141's antibacterial efficacy in vivo, we employed a standardized mouse model of full-thickness skin wounds infected with S. aureus. Bacterial load was assessed following direct drug administration to the wound site and subsequent enumeration of viable bacteria (Fig. 8A). Mice treated with BMS-303141 displayed no visible skin swelling or subcutaneous pus, whereas the normal saline group, which exhibited pronounced redness, swelling, and inflammation. The wound surface in the BMS-303141 group was dry, with accelerated healing, and comparable wound diameter and healing rate to the vancomycin group. By day 7, the wound size in the normal saline group remained largely unchanged. In contrast, the BMS-303141 group demonstrated significantly improved wound healing, with a rate exceeding that of the normal saline group though slightly lower than the vancomycin group (Fig. 8B, 8 C). On the fifth day, viable bacterial counts showed that the bacterial load (CFU) in the BMS-303141 group was one-fourth that of the normal saline group. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in the bacterial load between BMS-303141- and vancomycin-treated groups. (Fig. 8D). To further assess BMS-303141's antimicrobial activity in a deeper tissue infection setting, we established a mouse model of deep thigh muscle infection with S. aureus. BMS-303141 was administered intraperitoneally, and the pathogen load at the infection site was detected and recorded as an evaluation index (Fig. 8F). Compared with the negative control group, the average bacterial load in the thigh muscle wounds treated with BMS-303141 was notably reduced relative to that in both the negative control group and the vancomycin treatment group (Fig. 8E). In summary, across two distinct murine infection models, BMS-303141 markedly reduced bacterial burden and accelerated wound healing, achieving efficacy comparable to or greater than vancomycin.

Discussion

The increasing prevalence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus, particularly methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), coupled with their propensity to form highly recalcitrant biofilms, has severely undermined the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics [21, 22]. In light of the formidable challenges posed by such multi-drug resistant pathogens, there is an urgent imperative to expedite the research and development of innovative antibacterial agents. BMS-303141, a cell-permeable ATP-citrate lyase inhibitor, has been demonstrated to effectively suppress total lipid formation in HepG2 cells, while exhibiting negligible cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 50 μM. Furthermore, in vivo studies in mice have shown that BMS-303141 is well-tolerated following both oral administration and intraperitoneal injection. Although several studies have previously underscored the potential therapeutic applications of BMS-303141 in metabolic disorders, cancer, and inflammatory conditions [23–25], its antibacterial potential remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this investigation of BMS-303141's antibacterial efficacy represents a novel and significant contribution to the field.

Our results demonstrate that BMS-303141 exhibits potent antimicrobial activity against S. aureus. Bactericidal and biofilm assays further highlight BMS-303141's ability to suppress biofilm development, disrupt established biofilms, and kill clinical isolates of S. aureus. Proteomics analysis indicates that BMS-303141 modulates key biofilm-associated proteins (e.g., upregulation of ClfA and downregulation of SdrD and FnbA), suggesting an impact on biofilm regulatory processes [26, 27]. Given its sustained bactericidal activity and biofilm inhibition, BMS-303141 emerges as a promising candidate for combating infections caused by resistant S. aureus and biofilm-associated pathogens.

BMS-303141 also exhibits a favorable safety profile in murine models, with no significant organ damage or cytotoxicity observed [12, 28]. In vivo efficacy studies in mouse wound and deep thigh muscle infection models demonstrate that BMS-303141 effectively reduces bacterial load, with efficacy comparable to vancomycin. These findings support the potential of BMS-303141 as a robust antibacterial agent. An important consideration in interpreting these results is the known activity of BMS-303141 as an ATP-citrate lyase (ACL) inhibitor in eukaryotic cells. If a similar metabolic mechanism were operative in bacteria, one might expect disruption of acetyl-CoA homeostasis and fatty acid biosynthesis. However, S. aureus lacks an ACL homolog and employs alternative routes—namely pyruvate dehydrogenase and acetyl-CoA synthetase—for acetyl-CoA generation, as well as the BKDH-MbcS-FASII pathway for membrane lipid synthesis [29, 30]. This suggests that the antibacterial effect is not mediated by ACL inhibition but rather reflects an off-target activity. To uncover the true mechanism, we turned to proteomics and WGS, which provided insights into the cellular pathways affected by BMS-303141.

Proteomics and WGS have clarified the antibacterial mechanism of BMS-303141 and the adaptive resistance response in S. aureus. Proteomics shows that BMS-303141 up-regulates key enzymes (Asd) related to peptidoglycan biosynthesis, down-regulates key peptidoglycan hydrolases (Atl and LytH), and alters the levels of their transcriptional regulators (SarA and Rot) [31, 32], collectively perturbing peptidoglycan homeostasis. Consistent with this, the addition of exogenous peptidoglycan markedly decreased the bactericidal efficacy of BMS-303141 against S. aureus. The bacterial cell membrane, composed of a phospholipid bilayer, is crucial for maintaining bacterial integrity and protecting against external stress [33, 34]. Many antibiotics exert their effects by altering membrane permeability [35]. Our data show that BMS-303141 increases membrane permeability in a dose-dependent manner, with exogenous phospholipids attenuating its antibacterial efficacy. Based on these findings, we propose that the antibacterial effect of BMS-303141 on S. aureus may involve disruption of membrane phospholipid homeostasis and perturbation of peptidoglycan homeostasis, a key component of the cell wall. BMS-303141 can also affect protein synthesis pathways, as evidenced by altered ribosomal protein interactions involved in the 30S subunit and translation. WGS identified mutations in the dltB and walK genes. These two genes are respectively involved in the D-alanylation of teichoic acid [36–39] and peptidoglycan metabolism. These findings suggest that BMS-303141 inhibits bacterial growth by disrupting membrane phospholipid homeostasis, peptidoglycan homeostasis, and protein synthesis pathways. Proteomics data highlight the impact on cell membranes, cell wall components, and ribosomal proteins, while mutations provide genetic evidence supporting the disruption of peptidoglycan homeostasis and protein synthesis pathways.

In summary, this study provides comprehensive insights into the antibacterial efficacy and mechanisms of BMS-303141. While our findings highlight its potential as a novel antibacterial agent, further investigations are warranted to delineate the exact mechanisms and pinpoint the precise targets implicated in its antibacterial efficacy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that BMS-303141 is a potent anti-S. aureus agent that suppresses biofilm formation and induces multi-pathway cellular disruption. Integrating whole-genome sequencing, SNP profiling, and quantitative proteomics revealed disruption of peptidoglycan homeostasis, membrane integrity and protein synthesis in S. aureus. Functional assays confirmed that BMS-303141 increases membrane permeability and perturbs peptidoglycan homeostasis, effects reversible by exogenous peptidoglycan or phospholipids. In both murine wound and thigh muscle infection models, BMS-303141 significantly reduced bacterial burden and accelerated healing, underscoring its therapeutic potential against S. aureus infections.
