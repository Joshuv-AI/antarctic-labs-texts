Sustainable management of family life and finance in the context of digital capabilities - data flow dynamics
Authors: Jing Liu, Maahi Khan, Fang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, biorefining Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) has gained attention as a promising solution to the challenges of waste management and resource shortages, while also advancing sustainability goals. This research focuses on the European Union, analyzing the material flow and sustainability of biorefining systems and evaluating their impact on society, the economy, and the natural environment. The final products of the integrated material recovery processes, including recycling (18.3 %), heavy metals (3.6 %), fiber (1.4 %), hydrogen sulfate (7.4 %), recoverable water (14.8 %), fertilizer (8.4 %), and electric power (0.126 MWH/t MSW), are derived from the combined operations of material recovery facilities, pulverization, chemical conversion, wastewater treatment plants (WWTP), and combined heat and power (CHP) systems. The CHP system recovers energy from sources such as refuse-derived fuel (RDF), other disposable waste, charcoal, and natural gas produced by material recovery facilities, chemical conversion, and anaerobic digestion (AD) systems. Levulinic acid (LA), priced at 52 Euro/t, generates a profit of 220 Euro/t, excluding any subsidies. Potential reductions in carbon dioxide emissions are estimated at 2.5 and 1.4 kg CO2 equivalents per kilogram of levulinic acid (LA) and fertilizer, respectively, and 0.18 kg CO2 equivalents per megajoule of electrical power. This study underscores the importance of sustainable management practices in the context of digital capabilities, emphasizing the data flow dynamics essential for optimizing family life and finance in achieving sustainability.Keywords: Sustainable management, Family life, Sustainability, Carbon dioxide emissions

Introduction

On an international scale, urban regions are more inclined to experience population growth than rural areas. Although just 30 % of the global population lived in cities in 1950, 50 % of the worldwide population does so now, and experts predict that number will increase to 69 % by 2050 [1]. Thus, cities become large users of global energy and natural resource reserves. At the same time, they also serve as mineral and material collectors due to the distribution of inputs like foodstuffs from rural areas into urban areas. Urban areas also produce enormous waste, with very few minerals returning to the biosphere. This is particularly concerning given that the average MSW generation per capita in the European Union is 484 kg, and the organic fraction of MSW (OFMSW) makes up the most significant percentage of MSW (45%–55 %). Since landfilling waste indicates a linear economy, this method still manages 25 % of municipal solid waste. Alternative treatment techniques, including recycling, combustion, decomposition, or anaerobic digestion (AD), have supplanted landfilling as the primary means of managing municipal solid waste (MSW) [2]. Recycling and AD provide low-value products like fertilizer and biogas, which can be used for energy recovery.

To achieve the goals of the circular economy and waste-free urban systems, the global community must rapidly implement eco-innovative combined approaches for recovery resources from urban, such as biorefinery. According to the European Commission agency in charge of EU environmental policy, in 2011, 2.6 billion tons of garbage was generated throughout the EU. In certain nations, upwards of 82 % of garbage is dumped in landfills, whereas the United States recycles only 42 %. The World Bank predicts that municipal solid waste (MSW) consumption will exceed 12 million tones daily by 2200 if current trends continue [3]. In the next fifteen years, trash production will rise from 1.3 to 1.44 kg per individual daily. Hazards to human health and the integrity of the organic and human living environment are mainly attributable to rubbish. The earth is experiencing resource shortages, rising waste production, and rising product needs. Lessening our reliance on untapped reserves and increasing our recycling rates can help us face these obstacles [4].

Although there are established technologies for the mass conversion of MSW, they have drawbacks, such as producing hazardous materials and pollutants that necessitate disposal via expensive methods [5]. Incineration, the use of anaerobic decomposition (AD), and fertilizer-like output generation are all examples of modern MSW treatment procedures that can be categorized as either thermo chemical [6]. Bottom ash is categorized as not dangerous waste; fly ash includes metals, and oxides of metals, chloride, organic substances, and pollutants in the air control leftover, including chlorine [7]. The burning process of municipal solid waste is a means of regaining energy from waste approved by many waste regulations [8]. Life cycle analysis (LCA) studies have found significant adverse environmental effects due to the release of air pollutants and fly ash removal as the primary barriers of these technologies to the attention of lawmakers [9]. There are technologies to clean up the surroundings after combustion or at the end of the pipe, but they could be more successful and drive up processing costs. It has been shown that decomposing cellulose and then monomers using improved gasification with CO2 recycling can help turn MSW into a clean fuel gas. Compared to thermo chemical preparation, mechanical biological treatment (MBT) of MSW is the most viable option [10]. found that compared to MBT facilities just depending on composting, those that also used AD had a greater waste management efficiency during operation. When CLO is used as a fertilizer for the soil, pollutants and other minerals can leak into the groundwater if the material is not treated beforehand [11]. To ensure sustainability over the long term, it is crucial to recover every pollutant as a valuable resource, as waste materials are a complex mixture of various substances. Materials must be retrieved. A single study (W. [15]) has focused on valorizing the recyclable part of MSW to synthesize Levulinic acid. This dual focus on economic and environmental metrics provides a holistic view of the biorefining process's benefits. The study evaluates the efficiency and environmental benefits of using a Combined Heat and Power (CHP) system to recover energy from refuse-derived fuel (RDF), disposable trash, charcoal, and natural gas. By analyzing the integration of material recovery facilities (MRF), chemical conversion, and anaerobic digestion (AD) systems, the research highlights the potential of CHP systems to enhance energy recovery and reduce environmental impact. This analysis contributes to the understanding of how integrated systems can optimize resource use and minimize waste. The research employs a robust methodological framework, including sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo simulations, to assess the reliability and variability of the results. By examining the material flow and sustainability of biorefining systems within the European Union, this research provides a comprehensive analysis of the socio-economic and environmental impacts of these processes. The study identifies key products such as recycling materials, heavy metals, fiber, hydrogen sulfate, recoverable water, fertilizer, and electric power, highlighting their contribution to a circular economy. Furthermore, the research underscores the profitability of levulinic acid production and its potential to reduce carbon dioxide emissions, thereby advancing sustainability goals. By incorporating the dynamics of data flow and digital capabilities, the study offers valuable insights into optimizing family life and financial management within the context of sustainable practices. This interdisciplinary approach bridges the gap between waste management, economic viability, and digital transformation, providing a robust framework for future research and policy development in the field.

Material recovery facilities (MRF)

Mechanically, the MBCT technique's MRF consists of an air classifier outfitted with a computerized machine to sort through recyclables and determine which are made of paper and which are made of cardboard. Paper and cardboard material that cannot be regenerated is sorted into two bins based on their pictures and weights and then baled for delivery to ground facilities. Recycling facilities like MRF also focus on recycling plastics for resale. After being transported, the stream that included plastic (container, plastic packaging) is separated into three streams by an automatic separating system using several kinds of recognition systems: Al boxes with HDPE (high volume (poly (methylene)); included), PET (poly (methylene), terephthalate; included as 1), and combined plastic waste. If any other flows are suspected of carrying Al, automatically or manually, they will be routed to an eddy state separator. An “Eddy flow” phenomenon happens when a wire is subjected to a fluctuating magnetic field.

The MRF can create RDF, an alternate fossil fuel source with coal, from plastics that are usually difficult to recycle. Maintaining high standards for RDF's thermal values, components, and impurities (such as metals, rocks, and compounds) is crucial if used in industrial burning and energy-generating systems. Consequently, to find and eliminate such components, certain plants have implemented inductive sorting methods (ISM) and X-ray sorting methods. In ISM, materials are moved into a belt system with several sensors behind them. The resulting sensors can identify various kinds of metal, which are subsequently separated by a network of high-velocity air jets connected to the sensors. By comparing their densities, X-rays may differentiate between multiple materials.

New technologies, including microbial electro synthesis (MES), can be used with innovative metallurgical processes to regain metals (F. [23]). This allows for a more refined and intensive form of promoting its value.

Pulping

The pulping or ultra-critical water (430 °C and 240 bars) extraction method distinguishes the curbside-type recyclable material from the plant dry matter component of MSW. The chemicals extracted from municipal solid waste have yet to be included in LCA research investigating their additional benefits. Therefore, this study adds to our knowledge of the advantages and disadvantages of MSW-based biorefinery systems regarding their environmental effects. The entire system is confined inside the designated boundary, beginning with the unbounded MSW at the system's input and extending through turning it into products and, finally, consumption [32]. discuss process modeling and simulation, which derive both the structure's energy and mass circulation rates at its entry and exit points. These records are then characterized and gathered using the Impacts 2002+ (2017) and CML (Center of Environmental Science 2017) methods. Each material's GWP has been determined separately. The long-term viability of potential MSW treatment methods has been considered when implementing this “change-centered” or “consequential” LCA technique. Findings from this study have broad applicability in the fields of synthesis and upgrade design.

Data sources

The data for this study were obtained from various reputable sources, including municipal solid waste management facilities, environmental protection agencies, scientific literature, industry reports, and experimental data. Specifically, information regarding the composition and quantities of MSW was collected from local waste management facilities, while data on environmental impacts such as emissions and pollutants were sourced from reports and databases maintained by environmental protection agencies. Peer-reviewed articles provided insights into the efficiency and output of various biorefining processes, including material recovery, chemical conversion, and anaerobic digestion. Market prices for levulinic acid and other by-products were derived from industry reports and market analysis publications, and primary data on the efficiency and environmental impacts of biorefining processes were obtained from laboratory and pilot-scale experiments.

To assess the reliability of the results, an uncertainty analysis was conducted, involving sensitivity analysis, Monte Carlo simulations, error propagation, and comparisons with literature. Key parameters, such as the efficiency of material recovery processes, market prices of by-products, and emission factors, were varied within realistic ranges to evaluate their impact on the overall results. The Monte Carlo simulation technique was used to simulate a wide range of possible outcomes based on the variability of input parameters, providing a probability distribution of the results. Uncertainties associated with each data source were quantified and propagated through the calculations to determine their effect on the final results, including measurement errors, data reporting inaccuracies, and model assumptions. Finally, the results were compared with findings from other studies to ensure consistency and validate the reliability of our conclusions. By clearly stating the data sources and performing a thorough uncertainty analysis, we provide a transparent basis for assessing the reliability and robustness of our findings.

The energy composition of the MBCT method

RDF and other waste that cannot be recycled (14.8 %) from MRF, plastic (3.7 %) from AD, and metal (3.6 %) from its chemical transformation part are used as fuel in a CHP method that includes a plant material boiler with a real-time feed-water boiler (FWB) pre heater, a steam drum for steam saturation, and a steam super heater. The heating system co-combusts the combined fuel from biogas and reclaims energy from the stack gas to produce flaming steam. A rotating disc at the boiler's base separates ash and other substances from the combustion air. The particles and other fuels can be recovered at the exhaust gas outlet and reintroduced to the heating system for complete burning via charcoal-based adsorption. Emissions, like organic chemicals contributing to urban pollution, can be reduced or eliminated by ensuring the boiler undergoes an entire burning cycle before being shut down. The price of municipal solid waste

Owners of MBCT plants pay an average of 85 Euro/t MSW in trash collection fees to the government agency [37]. In addition, the local government may pay the owners of the MBCT plant a security fee in exchange for the service of processing municipal solid waste. This amounts to 110 Euros for every metric ton of municipal solid waste. So, we may calculate that the COP of municipal solid waste is (85.5–110) = −24.5 Euro/t. This means the owner of an MSW treatment plant is assured of a minimum of −24.5 Euros per metric ton of waste processed. This is a significant financial benefit to the owner of the MSW treatment plant, making waste valorization a more attractive option than waste disposal. When municipal solid waste (MSW) organic matter is valorized into chemicals, MSW treatment facilities become financially self-sufficient.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of biorefining Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) in the European Union (EU), employing a detailed material flow analysis (MFA) model to evaluate the economic and environmental impacts of various biorefining processes. The findings indicate that biorefining MSW can significantly contribute to achieving sustainability goals by recovering valuable materials, reducing greenhouse gas emissions, and promoting a circular economy. The economic analysis demonstrates the profitability of producing levulinic acid (LA) from MSW, with LA priced at 52 Euro/t generating a profit of 220 Euro/t without subsidies. Additionally, the environmental impact assessment highlights potential reductions in carbon dioxide emissions, estimated at 2.5 kg CO2 equivalents per kilogram of LA, 1.4 kg CO2 equivalents per kilogram of fertilizer, and 0.18 kg CO2 equivalents per megajoule of electrical power. The study also underscores the benefits of integrating material recovery facilities (MRF), chemical conversion, and anaerobic digestion (AD) systems with Combined Heat and Power (CHP) systems to optimize energy recovery and minimize waste. Moreover, the research underscores the critical role of digital capabilities in optimizing the efficiency and sustainability of biorefining systems. By leveraging data flow dynamics, the study illustrates how digital transformation can enhance the management of family life and finance, promoting a more sustainable and economically viable future. The integration of advanced technologies in waste management not only improves resource recovery but also fosters innovation and resilience in addressing environmental challenges.

Based on the findings, several policy recommendations are proposed to enhance the effectiveness and adoption of biorefining MSW in the EU. Governments should provide financial incentives, such as grants and tax credits, to support the development and deployment of biorefining technologies, which can help mitigate initial investment costs and encourage wider adoption. Establishing clear regulatory frameworks that promote the use of biorefining processes and ensure compliance with environmental standards is crucial, including setting targets for waste reduction, material recovery, and emission reductions. Encouraging public-private partnerships can foster innovation and investment in biorefining technologies, facilitating knowledge transfer, shared resources, and coordinated efforts in research and development. Implementing educational programs and public awareness campaigns to inform citizens and businesses about the benefits of biorefining MSW and the importance of sustainable waste management practices is essential. Additionally, investing in the necessary infrastructure to support biorefining processes, including the construction of modern MRFs, AD plants, and CHP systems, is vital for the efficient and effective operation of biorefining systems.

While this study provides valuable insights into the potential of biorefining MSW in the EU, several limitations should be noted. The study relies on data from various sources, which may have inconsistencies or gaps; future research should aim to obtain more comprehensive and accurate data to improve the reliability of the findings. Although the study focuses on the EU, the findings may not be directly applicable to other regions with different waste management practices, regulatory frameworks, and economic conditions. Comparative studies across different regions could provide a broader perspective. The study assumes the current state of biorefining technologies; future research should consider the potential impact of technological advancements and innovations on the efficiency and sustainability of biorefining processes. A more detailed lifecycle analysis (LCA) could provide a deeper understanding of the environmental impacts of biorefining processes, including potential trade-offs and unintended consequences. The economic viability of biorefining processes is influenced by market conditions, such as the prices of raw materials and products; future studies should consider the impact of economic fluctuations on the profitability of biorefining MSW.

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of biorefining MSW to contribute to sustainability goals in the EU by recovering valuable materials and reducing greenhouse gas emissions. The findings support the development of policies and infrastructure to promote the adoption of biorefining technologies. Future research should address the limitations and explore new directions to further enhance the understanding and implementation of biorefining MSW.
