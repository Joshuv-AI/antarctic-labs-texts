Multi-center investigation on the influence of clinical characteristics and molecular group A streptococci diagnostics on the management of tonsillopharyngitis in pediatric practices in Berlin—BASE study
Authors: Helena Hain, Viviane Jeanbat, Camille Nevoret, Isabelle Bureau, Tobias Tenenbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A clear distinction between viral, bacterial, or non-infectious tonsillopharyngitis is not always possible due to overlapping clinical symptoms. Patient near point-of-care diagnostics may contribute to supporting clinical assessment and, in turn, guide antibiotic treatment. We performed a prospective cluster-randomized multi-center study with cross-over design in 25 pediatric offices located in and around the city of Berlin, Germany, from December 2023 to May 2024. The primary objective was to determine whether using a rapid molecular point of care test (mPOC) reduces antibiotic prescription rates in children 3 to <18 years of age with febrile tonsillopharyngitis. As a secondary objective, sick days, re-assessments, and hospitalizations were evaluated. A total of 1111 patients were included in the final analysis. A positive test result in the mPOC group was observed in 67.1% (n = 471/702) and 65.8% (n = 194/295) in the standard care (SC) control group (which had optional rapid antigen testing). Interestingly, only 27.9% (n = 114/409) of children in the SC group were managed exclusively on a clinical diagnosis. Overall, two-thirds of all patients received a prescription for antibiotics (67.2%; n = 747/1111), with no significant difference between the study groups. In 95.5% (n = 635/665) of children with a positive test, an antibiotic was prescribed, as well as in 10.8% (n = 36/332) of test-negative cases. The probability of prescribing antibiotics was significantly higher when the McIsaac score was ≥3 (OR =4.67 (CI 95% 3.22–6.79, p < 0.0001)). In patients <5 years, the number of sick leave days was 37% higher than in older ones (IRR = 1.37 (95% CI 1.18–1.59, p<0.0001)). Conclusion: A McIsaac score ≥ 3 and a positive streptococcal test result were strong drivers for antibiotic prescription. No difference in antibiotic prescription rates between study groups was observed. Antibiotic usage was high in our patient cohort and requires further efforts regarding diagnostic and antibiotic stewardship. Tonsillopharyngitis also utilized substantial resources during follow-up. What is Known:• A clear distinction between viral, bacterial, or non-infectious pharyngitis is not always possible due to the overlapping clinical symptoms.• Rapid antigen tests (RADT) or molecular diagnostic tests (mPOC) as part of point-of-care testing may contribute to antibiotic treatment guidance.What is New:• In children with febrile tonsillopharyngitis, we could not observe a reduction in antibiotic prescription rates using mPOC compared to routine care, including RADT.• Further efforts regarding diagnostic and antibiotic stewardship are warranted.Trial registration number (DRKS-ID): DRKS00034362.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00431-026-06818-5.

Introduction

The WHO reported that group A streptococci (GAS) infections are the ninth leading cause of global mortality and infect more than 18 million individuals worldwide [1]. The most medically significant species of group A streptococci is the species Streptococcus pyogenes (S. pyogenes). The only reservoir of S. pyogenes is humans, and transmission is through contact (direct or indirect) and aerosol transmission. Infections can elicit various clinical presentations stemming from tonsillopharyngitis to sepsis and meningitis. By late 2022, the WHO noted increasing rates of severe, sometimes fatal, invasive group A streptococcal (iGAS) disease, especially in 1–10-year-olds across France, Ireland, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, and the UK [2]. Germany also experienced an unusual number of non-severe and invasive group A streptococcal infections in the winter season 2022/2023. The age groups < 15 years and 25–44 years are particularly affected [3].

Sore throats are one of the most common reasons for visiting a primary care physician in Germany and account for ≈2.5% of all consultations [4]. Bacterial pathogens are detected in 20–34% of tonsillopharyngitis cases depending on seasonal and regional conditions and age, but most cases have a viral cause [5–7]. A clear distinction between viral, bacterial, or non-infectious pharyngitis is not always possible due to the overlapping clinical symptoms. However, this distinction is particularly important when prescribing correct antibiotic therapy, as non-beneficial prescribing of antibiotics represents an unnecessary risk for patients and promotes the development of resistance [8, 9]. In practice, the therapy decision stems from the clinical picture, best judgement, and experience with the help of national and international guidelines [10, 11]. The option to perform a bacterial smear test or point-of-care testing (POCT) for S. pyogenes is also woven into patient management algorithms. Depending on the severity of the clinical presentation, a positively detected S. pyogenes infection will usually be treated with antibiotics [11].

Within the context of diagnostic stewardship, rapid antigen tests (RADT, usually lateral flow tests) are available for POCT in the outpatient setting, but reimbursement options are limited [12, 13]. These rapid tests have been shown to reduce antibiotic prescription rates compared to clinical evaluation only [14]. RADTs are based on an immunological procedure and are highly specific, but usually less sensitive than molecular diagnostic methods. A systematic review reported an overall sensitivity of 85.6% and specificity of 95.4%, with considerable variability between test types [12]. This diagnostic uncertainty of RADTs may reduce the trust in negative test results and affect clinical decisions for or against therapy. Consequently, some national guidelines recommend confirmatory throat culture following a negative RADT result [10]. However, due to long turnaround times and additional costs, this approach might often be impractical in outpatient care.

Currently, several molecular detection methods for S. pyogenes infections, based on nucleic acid amplification methods (NAAT), are available by different manufacturers [9, 15, 16]. The diagnostic test used in this study, the ID NOW™ Strep A2 from Abbott Rapid Diagnostics Germany, is a molecular point-of-care detection assay that is based on NEAR (nicking enzyme amplification reaction) technology, has both high specificity (93.4%) and sensitivity (98.5%), and delivers test results within 2–6 min [15]. Studies using the ID NOW™ COVID 19 assays in general practitioner offices in Thuringia revealed that both physicians, medical assistants, and patients appreciated and trusted the rapid results which reduced medical uncertainty and allowed for faster decision-making regarding therapy [17, 18]. Molecular Strep A testing in outpatient settings via POC devices may therefore be a reasonable option [19, 20].

This multi-center, cluster-randomized, cross-over study evaluated the outcome of rapid nucleic acid testing for group A Streptococcus in children when compared to normal practice routines. We hypothesized that immediate accurate diagnosis of Strep A cases would support the clinical picture and thus facilitate the intended treatment plan, especially with negative results. Thus, as part of actionable diagnostic stewardship, implementation of molecular POC testing in outpatients with acute pharyngitis symptoms could potentially reduce the volume of antibiotic prescriptions as well as patient sick days, re-assessments, and hospitalizations.

Methods

Study design

We performed a prospective cluster-randomized multi-center study with cross-over design in 25 primary care pediatric offices located in and around the city of Berlin, Germany, from December 2023 to May 2024. The cross-over design was chosen to (i) account for epidemiological fluctuations of GAS during the study period and (ii) enable all practices to use the molecular testing platform. The practices were randomized with the computer program BiAS [21] to one of two study arms (parallel group trial design, allocation ratio 1:1, Figure S1).

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Landesärztekammer Berlin Eth-55/23 and Landesärztekammer Brandenburg 2023–129-BO) and followed the International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians of all children after a detailed explanation of the study was provided by the practice physician.

Study population

In Germany, children and adolescents typically receive primary care from office-based pediatricians, rather than from family doctors. At participating pediatric practices, children were screened against the inclusion/exclusion criteria and, if eligible, enrolled during the study period. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are illustrated in Table S1.

Study objectives and endpoints

The primary objective of this study was to assess whether the use of rapid molecular tests for Strep A infections (mPOC group; ID NOW™ Strep A2 test, Abbott) reduced antibiotic prescription rates in children \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥ 3 to < 18 years of age with febrile tonsillopharyngitis. Rapid antigen testing was optional in the standard care (SC group). The primary endpoint was measured as a binary event at the patient level and was the decision by the physician to prescribe antibiotics (yes/no) during the initial visit. Note that the “yes” category included prescriptions provided for delayed prescribing (i.e., to be started only if symptoms persisted or worsened).

Secondary objective and endpoints focused on trying to gain evidence on potential economic benefits for the health care system and providers. These included (1) to assess whether the usage of molecular POC test could enable parents to reuptake their work sooner than in the routine cohort, resulting in less parental sick leave days and lower economic burden, (2) to assess whether the usage of molecular POC tests could prevent the necessity of a second appointment at the physician practice up to 14 days after initial visit, and (3) to assess whether the usage of molecular POC test could prevent hospitalization up to 7 days after initial visit.

Data collection

Clinical data (e.g., patient characteristics, diagnostics, therapy, and re-visits) were collected in a medical questionnaire, which was directly filled in by the pediatricians and then collected by the study team. Families were also provided with a follow-up parental questionnaire (PQ) upon enrolment of their child in the study, which they should return to the study coordinator. All data were entered into an electronic database provided by Cemka, to be thereafter verified and analyzed.

To collect samples for testing, the sterile throat swabs provided with the mPOC test were used and then measured directly via the ID NOW™ Strep A2 test platform at the participating practice. Everyone allowed to perform the assay had been previously instructed on how to use the test by the manufacturer.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The sample size calculation is illustrated in Table S2. In the descriptive analysis, quantitative variables were described using the following summary statistics: count, number of missing values, mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum. Qualitative variables were described using the following summary statistics: count, number of missing values, and percentage of each modality calculated on the responses expressed. The descriptive analyses covered all the variables of the study.

For categorical variables, the chi-squared test was applied, apart from theoretical sample sizes that were less than 5; in those cases, Yates continuity correction or Fisher’s exact test was used. For continuous variables, when distribution was close to normal (non-significant Shapiro–Wilk test), a Student’s t-test or analysis of variance was performed. If this was not the case, non-parametric tests were used (Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis).

The primary objective (antibiotic prescription yes/no) was assessed as a proportion, whose numerator was the number of patients that received a prescription (“yes” to primary endpoint) and whose denominator was the number of patients that were eligible to receive a prescription (“yes” or “no” to primary endpoint).

Logistic regression was used to model the response Y (binary response (primary endpoint at patient level)) given the variables of interest. The model was a mixed effects logistic regression, in which physicians and phase were considered as random effects. The multi-level model was specified in reference for the cluster randomized cross-over design (the cross-sectional version) [22].

The subjects without a test result (in the intervention group) were excluded. Sites that dropped out prior to completing the 24-week study plan (12 weeks per phase) were included in the regression analysis, as described in reference [22]. The enrollment of patients by each site (by group and phase) was reported to explain the composition of subjects in the regression model. Subject demographics (including age, gender, and clinical score) were also reported.

The study design involved a cross-over of the practices after 12 weeks so that each group performed both molecular POC testing and standard care during the study period. To mitigate potential carry-over—where clinicians first exposed to mPOC could alter prescribing behavior after cross-over—we implemented the approach detailed in reference [22] and incorporated a carry-over indicator in the regression analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

Overall, 1272 patients were recruited with 1111 included and 161 patients (12.7%) finally excluded (Figure S2). Approximately half of the participants were female (51.0%, n = 566/1109), and the median age for all was 6.8 years (3.0–17.8). Most of the patients had a documented fever higher than 38.5 °C (70.4%; n = 726/1032). The mean duration of fever at inclusion was 2.2 ± 1.3 days (n = 1079; median = 2.0 (0.0–14.0)). The majority of the patients had no underlying conditions (91.2%, n = 1013/1111). Thirty-eight patients (3.4%; n = 38/1105) had already been ill with a S. pyogenes infection in the last 28 days and 35 of those had received antibiotics for that infection (92.1%; n = 35/38). Moreover, 1.1% of the patients (n = 12/1102) had already been treated with antibiotics within the last 28 days for an infectious disease other than tonsillopharyngitis or scarlet fever (Table 1). Significantly more patients in the mPOC group had the diagnosis “tonsillopharyngitis” at inclusion: 92.6% (n = 650/702) versus 89.0% (n = 364/409) in the SC group (p = 0.0406). Inversely, significantly fewer patients in the mPOC group were diagnosed with scarlet fever at inclusion (16.5% (n = 115/698)) versus 24.5% (n = 100/408) in the SC group (p = 0.0011) (Table 1). Overall, 76.0% (n = 844/1111) of the children had no other clinical symptoms apart from those classified for “sore throat.” When they had other symptoms, they were mainly respiratory (3.9%, n = 43/1111) or gastrointestinal (3.5%, n = 39/1111). A positive Strep A test result in the mPOC group was observed in 67.1% (n = 471/702) of tested individuals, compared to 65.8% (n = 194/295) in the SC group using optional antigen tests (Table 2). Only 27.9% (n = 114/409) of children in the SC group were managed based on clinical diagnosis (i.e., no additional diagnostics). Of the children with a clinical score ≥ 3, 69.8% of all performed GAS tests were positive (n = 592/848). In children with a clinical score < 3, 46.9% of all performed tests were positive (n = 67/143) (Fig. 3, Table S3). Additional diagnostics were reported only for eight patients: C-reactive protein, EBV serology, influenza test, and throat swab for other bacteria but not S. pyogenes. Indeed, no patient samples were escalated for culture analysis. Therapy

Almost all patients received a prescription for antipyresis/analgesia (93.2%; n = 1.027/1102), with no significant difference between mPOC and SC groups. Regardless of diagnosis, about two-thirds of patients received a prescription for antibiotics (67.2%; n = 747/1111) for a mean duration of 7.0 ± 1.1 days (n = 744; median 7.0 (3.0–10.0)) (Table 3). There was no significant difference in antibiotic prescribing between the two groups: mPOC group 478/702 (68.1%) and SC group 269/409 (65.8%). The most commonly prescribed agents were penicillins (79.1%), then aminopenicillins (10.6%) and cephalosporins (8.2%). Other classes of antibiotics (mostly macrolides) were applied in only 15 patients (Fig. 1B, Table S4). When a test was used (mPOC or RADT), 93.4% of tested children were treated according to the test result: 95.5% of test-positive children (score ≥ 3) received antibiotics (n = 635/665), 89.2% of test-negative patients did not receive antibiotics (n = 296/332) (Figs. 1C and 3, Table S4 and Table S5). There was no significant difference between the two study groups. From the 747 patients who received antibiotics, 85% had a positive test result (n = 635/747), while 5% had a negative test result (n = 36/747) and 10% (n = 76/747) were not tested. When no antibiotics were prescribed, the pediatricians were asked in the medical questionnaire why they refrained from antibiotic therapy. They reported that the test result was relevant in more cases than the clinical symptoms for their decision not to give antibiotics (58.1% (211/363) test result relevant vs 44.4% (161/363) clinical symptoms relevant, p < 0.0001).

Follow-up and secondary objectives

The mean number of sick leave days issued by a doctor was 3.1 ± 1.5 days (n = 424, median 3.0 (1.0–14.0)) (Table 3). In patients who were less than 5 years old, the number of sick leave days was 37% higher than in older ones (incidence rate ratio = 1.37 (95% CI 1.18–1.59, p < 0.0001)). Other factors (group, score, and gender) did not have a significant influence on the number of sick leave days (Fig. 2B).

According to the parental questionnaire (Table S6), about half of the parents (47.0%; n = 210/447) reported having stayed at home for a period of 3–5 days, 35.6% stayed less than 3 days (n = 159/447), and 17.4% (n = 78/447) were absent for 6 to 10 days (Fig. 2C).

Fifty-one patients (4.6%; n = 51/1111) presented another time to the physician for the same episode of tonsillopharyngitis. Most of them (41.2%; n = 21/51) came back within 3 days. The odds for revisits were 55% lower for girls than for boys; other factors had no significant influence (Fig. 2D).

Overall, 74.9% of the parents who filled in the questionnaire reported antibiotic treatment. Of those, 5.8% (n = 20/344) reported antibiotic intake that was not prescribed by the physician at the initial consultation. In contrast, 7.8% (n = 9/115) of children did not take the initial medication prescribed (Fig. 1D, Figure S6).

Only two hospitalizations in the whole study cohort were noted (one during the day of inclusion, the other following a second consultation at the practice).

Discussion

This is one of the largest prospective clinical studies in the pediatric outpatient setting evaluating the potential of molecular streptococcal-specific POCT diagnostics to guide antibiotic treatment in children with febrile tonsillopharyngitis. In addition to the impact of POC diagnostics (molecular or lateral flow) on antibiotic prescription rates, the role of clinical symptoms was also evaluated. Diagnostic stewardship

Diagnostic (and therapeutic) recommendations for pharyngitis are highly discussed and guidelines vary across countries with no overarching consensus, ranging from relying on clinical scores only without testing (e.g., the UK) up to testing all children > 3 years with absence of viral symptoms, regardless of the clinical score and confirmation culture testing (e.g., the USA) [10]. According to current German guidelines, both supportive diagnostic POC testing and antibiotic treatment are optional for clinical scores of 3 or more in children aged 3–15 years [11].

Recent evaluations have suggested that relying solely on a Centor or McIsaac score ≥ 3 does not provide adequate sensitivity or specificity for diagnosing GAS tonsillopharyngitis [23]. Rapid antigen and molecular testing, however, have been shown to reduce antibiotic overuse for GAS infections in a variety of contexts [24–26]. A systematic review of five studies involving 2545 sore throat patients in primary care was conducted by Cohen et al. Using this data, the authors concluded that using POCTs instead of clinical grading would result in a 25% reduction in antibiotic prescribing [14]. In a prospective study in a children’s emergency department setting in the UK, it was shown that despite a poor RADT sensitivity and the McIsaac score’s poor specificity in children, the combination use led to decreased antibiotic prescribing rates [27].

In our study, physicians seemed to value diagnostic testing as a decision tool: In the control group, they tested 70.1% of included children with a rapid antigen test; we initially anticipated a much lower application of RADTs since an observational study in GP offices in Germany found a rate of 3% [28]. Furthermore, in our physician’s medical questionnaire, the pediatricians confirmed that the knowledge of the laboratory result is important for their treatment decision.

This also reflected in the therapeutic adherence to the test result: Antibiotics were prescribed to 95.5% (635/665) of patients who had a positive test, and 89.2% (296/332) of children who had a negative test did not receive antibiotics. Other studies have shown higher rates of antibiotic treatment in test-negative patients. An earlier primary care study with adults from Germany revealed that 39% of GAS RADT test-negative patients received antibiotic treatment, suggesting low trust in the rapid antigen test result [13].

Since the sensitivity of the molecular test is better than the antigen test, one would assume an even higher confidence in a negative molecular test result. This showed in a retrospective study in outpatient clinics, where mPOC use reduced antibiotic prescription rates in test-negative patients by 44.1%, compared to RADTs [29]. However, in our study, there was no significant difference between the two study groups.

In another large prospective observational study of 339 children, molecular point-of-care test results were also strongly associated with outpatient antibiotic prescription. Compared with 43 of 238 (18%) children who had a negative test, 64 of 70 (91%) children who had a positive result received a prescription. Outpatient antibiotic prescription was also associated with clinical factors, such as being aged 3 years or older or having at least a Centor score of 3 or a FeverPAIN score of 4 criteria, while there was no association with gender [30]. In our study, we also found a high association with clinical score of ≥ 3, and no association with gender. We did not see a significant influence of age, but we did not include children below 3 years.

Antibiotic treatment

Overall, the antibiotic treatment rate in our cohort was very high. There may be several reasons for this phenomenon. First of all, we had a very high prevalence of GAS infections when compared to study cohorts from previous investigations; 84.6% of the children had a clinical score of 3 or more, and 66.7% of all performed tests were positive for GAS—while the usually expected rate of S. pyogenes in children with pharyngitis would be about 20% [5]. This might have been partly caused by our strict inclusion criteria, including only children with a fever of at least 38 °C. Second, 19.5% (n = 215/1100) of all included children were diagnosed with scarlet fever, a condition that typically prompts antibiotic treatment and may thus have contributed to higher antibiotic prescription rates. Although the higher proportion of scarlet fever cases in the SC group is most likely coincidental, it may nonetheless have influenced the comparative analysis between the study groups.

Also, due to the large increase of iGAS infections across Europe in 2022/2023, there might have been an over-intention of our physicians to prevent invasive disease in test-positive children, hence the higher antibiotic rates, even though it has been previously suggested that iGAS cases may not be timewise associated with tonsillopharyngitis in close proximity [31].

Lastly, it remains controversial whether antibiotic treatment is necessary for GAS pharyngitis in general or should be only prescribed in severe cases; this corresponds with not only the guidelines mentioned above but also the rate of children with asymptomatic GAS carriage [32]. The answer to this question is also essential for diagnostic recommendations. Although limited by a small sample (n = 88), a multi-center, randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial showed that amoxicillin reduced fever duration compared with placebo but had similar pain and complication rates [33]. Therefore, the decision to treat with antibiotics even in children with a positive test and symptoms of GAS tonsillopharyngitis should be made with caution.

Influence of mPOC testing

The changing environment, diagnostic accuracy, and impact of rapid molecular testing on workflow and antibiotic prescribing patterns have been demonstrated in recent literature [9, 15, 16]. It has also been proven to be very feasible in regions with high risk for acute rheumatic fever, where high sensitivity is of value [34]. According to Rao et al., 97.1% of cases using molecular POCT led to the correct prescription of antibiotics, while 87.5% of cases using RADT plus confirmatory culture did the same [35].

In a single-center randomized trial in a pediatric emergency department including children aged 3–17 years, establishing a POCT nucleic acid amplification testing program for GAS led to earlier treatment and fewer antibiotic prescriptions compared to GAS culture testing, which may not be routinely used in most emergency departments [36]. In another smaller primary care study including 160 patients also analyzing the usefulness of the ID NOW Strep A2 molecular test, a 55–65% reduction in immediate and deferred antibiotic prescription was shown [37].

Contrary to these findings, our study did not show a significant difference in antibiotic prescription rates when molecular POC tests were used. This might have been caused by different factors: First of all, we had an unexpectedly high use of RADTs in the control group, while other studies compared to no testing or culture. Secondly, the aforementioned high GAS prevalence in our patient cohort caused high antibiotic prescription rates—while the main beneficial influence of POCT on antibiotic rates is ruling out infections and hence avoiding antibiotic prescription, most of our tests were positive. Also, we tested all children in the intervention group, regardless of the clinical severity of the tonsillopharyngitis.

It furthermore warrants consideration that the very high sensitivity of the mPOC test may additionally lead to an increased number of positive results in GAS colonized patients that do not have “true” GAS disease. However, in our study, most included patients had high fever (mean temperature of 39 °C), throat pain, and clinical scores of ≥ 3 in 84.6% (n = 935/1105). Hence, the study population appears to be representative of a clinically relevant, symptomatic cohort. Still, a positive test result is potentially a strong driver for antibiotic prescription. Therefore, the decision to prescribe antibiotics always needs to be seen in the context of the presenting clinical picture. In line with this, a very recent large pediatric study including 1388 patients suggests that introducing rapid molecular POCT into routine clinical care should ensure clear guidelines and training around their use so that clinically useful tests are not inadequately used and results falsely interpreted [38].

Our findings emphasize the importance of clinical evaluation and support the German recommendations of only testing children in case of high clinical scores and when the physician considers antibiotic therapy advisable based on the severity of symptoms [11].

Health resources

Tonsillopharyngitis also utilized substantial resources during follow-up in our study. Parents frequently reported to have stayed at home and also had to revisit their pediatrician for a follow-up visit. In a Swedish study analyzing children with streptococcal tonsillitis, the illness lasted on average 7 days, time to recovery after treatment was 5 days, and the mean period of sick leave of parents was 2.5 days. The total costs of a tonsillitis episode were about 385 US-dollar [39].

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of our study is the large number of included patients, the participation of 25 pediatric outpatient practices in and around Germany’s capital Berlin, the strict inclusion criteria, including only febrile children and only those from 3 years onwards, and the use of a follow-up questionnaire with a large participation rate of 41.3% (n = 459/1111).

But there were also some limitations. Clinical scores were just assessed quantitatively, without asking for the single criteria, and might not have been reliably filled in by the physicians. Furthermore, we did not do confirmatory cultures to verify the different diagnostic tools. Blinding was not possible due to the study design, and more children were included in the intervention group than in the control group. The unexpectedly high rates of RADT usage, test positivity, and antibiotic prescriptions might also limit the comparability of the study and thus skew the potential impact of mPOCT as seen in other studies. Moreover, the test (mPOC or RADT) may have been dominant over the clinical score (McIsaacs score), since in one study arm all participants received a mPOC test. This potential dominance may have had consequences among those tested with mPOC: some participants with a low McIsaac score may have been colonized rather than infected with GAS, i.e., not qualifying for antimicrobial treatment. Lastly, the study was conducted in the surge of high GAS cases across Europe, which may have had an impact on the results.

Conclusion

In summary, in this prospective multi-center study, positive test results and clinical scores ≥ 3 were the strongest drivers for antibiotic prescription in children. We did not find a reduction in antibiotic prescription rates when molecular POCTs were used for all children; however, this might be different in other settings. Our results highlight the importance of the profound clinical evaluation as well as the potential usefulness of POC diagnostics to guide antibiotic treatment.
