Robust myocardium detection and scar severity classification in LGE-CMR using ScarYOLO and contrastive learning
Authors: B. Abinaya, M. Malleswaran, V. Muthupriya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Late gadolinium enhancement cardiac magnetic resonance (LGE-CMR) imaging plays a crucial role in assessing myocardial scar tissues, aiding in the diagnosis and prognosis of cardiovascular diseases. However, accurately classifying scar tissue severity into mild and severe remains a challenge due to low contrast, noise interference, and inter-patient variability in LGE-CMR images. Existing methods often rely on manual assessment or traditional deep learning models that struggle with precise myocardium localization and discriminative feature extraction from scarred regions. To overcome these challenges, we propose a novel framework incorporating ScarYOLO, an optimized YOLOv8-based myocardium detection model, followed by contrastive myocardial scar learning (CMSL) for severity classification. ScarYOLO enhances myocardium localization accuracy, ensuring precise detection of scarred tissue. The detected myocardium is then processed using CMSL, which employs a fine-tuned Xception-based encoder trained on a labeled LGE-CMR dataset. CMSL leverages contrastive self-supervised learning to enhance feature representation and improve class separability between mild and severe scar regions. Additional dense layers and a classification head are appended to the encoder for final severity prediction. The proposed approach enhances myocardial scar detection accuracy while improving robustness in low-contrast LGE-CMR images. By leveraging ScarYOLO for precise segmentation and CMSL for effective classification, our model outperforms conventional deep learning methods in classifying scar tissue severity. Experimental evaluations demonstrate significant improvements in detection precision, classification accuracy, and model generalization, making it a reliable tool for automated myocardial scar assessment in clinical settings.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 32% of all global deaths, with heart failure (HF) and myocardial infarction (MI) being among the most prevalent causes [1]. Although MI and HF are clinical outcomes resulting from ischemic injury and cardiac dysfunction, myocardial scarring specifically refers to the fibrotic tissue that forms as a consequence of such events. Late gadolinium enhancement cardiac magnetic resonance (LGE-CMR) imaging is a gold-standard modality for assessing myocardial fibrosis and scar tissue in patients with ischemic and non-ischemic cardiomyopathies, particularly within the left ventricle [2]. The identification and quantification of myocardial scars in LGE-CMR images are crucial for risk stratification, prognosis, and treatment planning in heart disease patients. Generally, scars are classified as “mild” or “severe” based on quantitative criteria such as the extent of hyperenhancement relative to the total left ventricular myocardium and intensity thresholding techniques—commonly the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) method. Typically, a mild scar is characterized by a smaller, less intense area of hyperenhancement, whereas a severe scar exhibits a larger, more intense fibrotic region. Studies have shown that patients with extensive myocardial scarring in the left ventricle are at a significantly higher risk of adverse cardiac events, including ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death (SCD) [3]. Therefore, accurate detection and severity classification of myocardial scars are essential for early intervention and improved clinical outcomes.

While cine-based myocardial scar detection remains a challenging task due to the lack of direct tissue contrast, LGE-CMR offers a more suitable and reliable modality for clinical scar assessment. Despite this advantage, current manual and semi-automated segmentation techniques suffer from several limitations, leading to variability and reduced reliability in clinical decision-making. Traditional scar segmentation methods rely heavily on radiologists, introducing inter-observer variability due to subjective interpretation. In addition, myocardial scars often exhibit subtle intensity variations in LGE-CMR images, making their differentiation from healthy myocardium challenging due to low contrast. The heterogeneity of scar patterns further complicates automated feature extraction, as myocardial scars vary significantly between patients. Moreover, existing deep learning models often require high computational resources, limiting their real-time clinical applicability. Many current models also struggle with inadequate generalization, failing to perform consistently across diverse datasets, which reduces their robustness in real-world clinical scenarios. Addressing these challenges is crucial for improving the accuracy, efficiency, and reliability of myocardial scar detection and severity classification.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes a novel deep learning-based framework for automated myocardium detection and scar severity classification in LGE-CMR images. Related works

Recent advances in myocardial scar segmentation have been driven by both conventional and deep learning approaches, as highlighted by several key studies. Wu et al. (2021) reviewed recent advances in cardiac fibrosis and scar segmentation from MRI, emphasizing both conventional and deep learning-based approaches. The study discussed the integration of non-contrast-agent modalities like bSSFP cine MRI to enhance segmentation accuracy. Various automated and semi-automated methods were analyzed, highlighting their effectiveness in different datasets such as LGE-CMR scans. Deep learning techniques, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), were explored for improved segmentation performance. The review also addressed challenges and future directions in the field [4]. Zhou et al. [5] proposed a two-stage deep learning framework for scar segmentation using multi-view images, evaluated on datasets containing linear and patchy scars. The first stage employed a 3D view interpolation-based data augmentation strategy, while the second stage introduced the MVCSNet for multi-view co-segmentation with a multi-view feature interaction (MVFI) module. Experimental results showed that the proposed method significantly improved generalization, especially for datasets with fewer samples. MVCSNet outperformed existing segmentation and co-segmentation models across all evaluation metrics. The approach enables more accurate 3D quantitative analysis of scars [5]. O’Brien et al. [6] investigated myocardial scar detection using CT-DE and radiomic features, comparing it to MRI-LGE as the gold standard. The study analyzed CT-DE scans from 18 patients, extracting 93 radiomic features and training machine learning classifiers, including a support vector machine (SVM). The best classifier, using 100 kV images, achieved an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI 0.87–0.90). Combined-energy imaging improved feature selection, increasing high-AUC features to 63. The results suggest that ML-based CT-DE analysis can effectively identify myocardial scar regions [6]. Roslan et al. [7] conducted a systematic review on AI-based scar segmentation in cardiac MRI, analyzing 21 studies from 2012 to 2022. The review included supervised deep learning (76.2%) and unsupervised machine learning (23.8%) methods, evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC). Supervised and unsupervised models showed comparable DSC scores of 0.74 (95% CI [0.67, 0.81]) and 0.71 (95% CI [0.63, 0.79]), respectively. AI methods demonstrated high feasibility for myocardial scar quantification. Performance depended on network architecture, training strategies, and dataset quality [7]. Abdul Qayyum et al. (2024) proposed an unsupervised unpaired domain adaptive scar tissue segmentation framework using a self-attention generative adversarial network. They utilized four LGE MICCAI datasets (2019–2023) and introduced a parallel transformer-based segmentation model. Their approach improved scar segmentation by incorporating style transfer and self-supervised attention mechanisms. Experimental results showed significantly better performance than state-of-the-art methods. The proposed method effectively modeled longitudinal scar changes across different time points [8]. Tobias Hoh et al. [9] investigated the impact of image acquisition resolution on neural network-based scar segmentation using 36 high-resolution LGE k-space datasets from porcine myocardial infarction models. They trained U-Net models on different resolutions (0.7, 1.2, and 1.7 mm) and tested performance across varying resolutions. Networks performed best when training and test resolutions matched, with fractional scar errors increasing for mismatched resolutions. Training on multi-resolution data reduced resolution dependency, improving segmentation robustness. The study highlights the importance of resolution consistency in deep learning-based scar segmentation [9]. Righetti et al. [10] developed a CNN-based deep learning model to detect myocardial scar presence using cine CMR-derived parametric images from 206 patients (158 scar, 48 control) at Centro Cardiologico Monzino. The model incorporated Fourier and monogenic signal analyses to extract left ventricle dynamic features. Using cine images and Fourier-based parametric images, it achieved 79% accuracy (AUC 0.86) at the slice level and 100% accuracy at the patient level. The approach demonstrates potential for contrast-free scar detection. The study suggests its use as a screening tool to guide LGE-CMR prescriptions [10]. Abinaya & Malleswaran [11] proposed the FGSO-HDResC-Net model for scar tissue segmentation and severity classification in LGE-CMR images. The method integrates an optimal segmentation algorithm (OSA) with Flamingo Gannet search optimization for feature extraction and classification. The model achieved 96.45% accuracy, with a true positive rate of 95.42% and a true negative rate of 96.48%. It outperformed traditional models by 10.74% to 14.50% in accuracy. The approach enhances disease assessment and treatment planning for cardiac patients [11]. Kim et al. [12] developed a deep learning-based method for segmental classification of LGE lesions using cardiac LGE data from 170 patients. Short-axis images were transformed into polar space and segmented based on the 16-segment LV model. Pretrained ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B0 achieved the best results, with an AUC of 0.875 and an F1-score of 0.752. The method eliminates the need for manual lesion segmentation. It enables automated and efficient LGE lesion classification with high accuracy [12]. Moccia et al. [13] explored the use of fully convolutional neural networks (FCNNs) for scar tissue segmentation in CMR-LGE images from 30 patients (250 images). A modified ENet model was trained and evaluated using leave-one-patient-out cross-validation. Protocol 2, which restricted segmentation to the left ventricular myocardium, achieved superior performance with a Dice similarity coefficient of 71.25%. This approach demonstrated effective scar segmentation without requiring preprocessing or postprocessing. The study highlights FCNNs’ potential for automated cardiac scar tissue analysis [13].

Liu et al. [14] investigated scar tissue discrimination in glaucoma filtration surgery using Mueller matrix (MM) microscopy and machine learning. Tissue sections of conjunctiva, sclera, and scar tissue were analyzed for polarization characteristics. A discrimination score based on MM parameters showed significant differentiation between tissues (p < 0.001). Virtual staining provided superior contrast compared to conventional methods. The study demonstrated MM microscopy’s potential for ophthalmological pathology and fibrosis assessment [14]. Boribalburephan et al. [15] developed a multi-task deep learning model for myocardial scar (MS) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) classification using 12-lead ECG images. A dataset of 14,052 ECGs with CMR-derived labels was used for training and validation. The model achieved AUCs of 0.838 for MS and 0.939 for LVEF < 50%, surpassing cardiologist performance. A prevalence-specific test dataset recorded an AUC of 0.812 for MS. This approach provides a cost-effective alternative to CMR for cardiovascular screening [15]. Sharma et al. [16] developed a modified GAN-based augmentation method for myocardial scar classification using contrast-enhanced cardiac MRI. The dataset included 91 patients with ischemic myocardial scars, 31 controls, and augmented images (6,684 scar, 7,451 control). A CNN with channel attention modules and MobileNetV2 was trained on augmented data. The PGGAN-based augmentation achieved 93% accuracy, with attention modules further improving performance by up to 5%. This method enhances myocardial scar classification and has significant clinical implications [16]. Leiner et al. [17] reviewed machine learning applications in cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), focusing on image acquisition, reconstruction, analysis, and diagnostic evaluation. The study highlighted advancements in automated segmentation, prognostic information extraction, and deep learning-based scar simulation in late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images. ML significantly reduced segmentation time and improved reproducibility for ventricular mass and volume quantification. Challenges include dataset validation across multiple centers and the need for prospective clinical trials. No specific accuracy metrics were provided, but ML’s impact on efficiency and diagnostic accuracy was emphasized [17]. Karim et al. [18] evaluated algorithms for segmenting fibrosis and scar tissue from late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) images of the left atrium. The dataset included 60 multicenter LGE-CMR images from atrial fibrillation patients, with 30 pre- and 30 post-radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA). Eight algorithms from seven institutions were benchmarked against manual segmentation and standard deviation (SD) and full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) methods. Post-ablation segmentation achieved higher overlap scores (up to 85) compared to pre-ablation (maximum 48). No single algorithm outperformed all others, highlighting the need for further development [18]. Maknuna et al. [19] developed an automated method for scar tissue characterization using machine learning. The dataset consisted of whole slide images (WSI) of hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained scar tissues. Mask R-CNN with ResNet101 as a backbone was used for scar lesion detection, while K-means clustering characterized collagen density and orientation. The model successfully predicted scar images with high accuracy and showed a 50% difference in collagen density between normal and scar tissues. The approach proved time-efficient and effective for objective histological analysis [19]. Brahim et al. [20] proposed ICPIU-Net for automated segmentation of myocardial tissue from LGE-MRI. The dataset included delayed-enhancement MRI scans of the left ventricular myocardium. The method integrated inclusion and classification constraints to address class imbalance and used a decision-fusion technique for final prediction. ICPIU-Net outperformed previous deep learning techniques in segmentation accuracy. The model achieved an accuracy of 84.00%, with challenges in identifying microvascular obstruction (MVO) cases [20]. Torlasco et al. [21] developed a deep learning approach for ischemic scar segmentation from dark blood LGE-CMR images. The dataset included DB-LGE short-axis stacks from 33 patients, with 108 myocardial and 385 scar-labeled CMR images. A dual U-Net CNN was trained to segment myocardium and ischemic scars, using data augmentation for generalization. The model achieved an Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.8–0.85 for scar recognition and ~ 0.7 for myocardium segmentation. Performance could improve with additional data to address underrepresented samples and noise [21]. Qayyum et al. [22] proposed an unsupervised domain adaptive scar tissue segmentation framework using a self-attention generative adversarial network (GAN). The study utilized four different LGE MICCAI datasets (2019–2023) to address challenges in myocardium segmentation due to intensity heterogeneity and non-rigid heart motion. A novel segmentation architecture based on a parallel transformer was introduced and compared with style transfer GAN and self-supervised attention GAN. The proposed method significantly outperformed state-of-the-art techniques in scar segmentation. The approach effectively modeled longitudinal scar changes across different time points [22]. Yang et al. [23] proposed a joint segmentation method for left atrium (LA) anatomy and scar tissue using a multi-view two-task (MVTT) recursive attention model. The study utilized 3D late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) cardiac MR (CMR) images of atrial fibrillation (AF) patients. The MVTT model integrated RNN and dilated residual learning to improve segmentation accuracy. The method achieved a mean Dice score of 93% for LA anatomy and 87% for scar segmentation. It eliminated the need for separate bright-blood scans and image registration, making the process more efficient [23]. Ding et al. [24] proposed the multi-view stereoscopic attention network (MVSA-Net) for 3D tumor classification in automated breast ultrasound (ABUS). The study used 172 ABUS images for training and 42 for testing. MVSA-Net employed a stereoscopic attention module for lesion localization and a Transformer-based classification unit for feature extraction. The model achieved an accuracy of 95.24% and an AUC of 99.76%. The method enhances diagnostic efficiency by reducing ABUS interpretation time [24]. Zhou et al. [25] proposed an edge-enhanced feature-guided joint segmentation model for left atrial (LA) cavity and scar segmentation in LGE-MRI images. The study used the LA Scar QS 2022 validation dataset. A multi-task learning framework with a 3D central difference convolution and a dilated inception module (DIM) was implemented for segmentation. The model achieved Dice scores of 0.875 for LA and 0.631 for scars, with an LA scar accuracy of 0.999. The method improves segmentation performance by leveraging spatial relationships and multi-scale representation [25]. Mamalakis et al. [26] introduced MA-SOCRATIS, an unsupervised automatic segmentation pipeline for left ventricular myocardium and scar in LGE-MRI. The model was evaluated on two datasets: the MS-CMR Seg 2019 challenge dataset (40 patients) and a dataset of 20 patients with challenging scar regions. The method combined multi-atlas segmentation, k-means clustering, and Rician–Gaussian mixture models with watershed techniques. It achieved Dice scores of 81.9% (myocardium) and 70.5% (scar), demonstrating robust performance without training or tuning [26]. Zhang et al. [27] developed a deep learning framework for chronic myocardial infarction (MI) detection using nonenhanced cardiac cine MRI. The dataset included 212 patients with chronic MI and 87 healthy controls, with 80% used for training and 20% for testing. The model extracted motion features from the left ventricle to delineate MI regions. It achieved a sensitivity of 89.8%, specificity of 99.1%, and an AUC of 0.94 on the test set. The MI area and percentage correlated strongly with LGE-MRI (r = 0.88 and r = 0.89, respectively) [27]. Hu et al. [28] developed a multimodal scar tissue discrimination platform integrating a residual network-based CNN, L1-regularized logistic regression, and weighted aggregation for feature fusion. The study used a customized multimodal dataset, including 32-channel collagen fiber images and gene expression data. Their model achieved 98.26% accuracy for image discrimination and 98.23% for multimodal classification. The study also identified 11 potential therapeutic targets related to collagen fiber repair. Future work includes dataset refinement and biological validation of scarring mechanisms [28].

Despite the substantial body of research in myocardial scar segmentation and classification, several limitations persist in the literature. First, the wide variability in imaging protocols, dataset sizes, and annotation standards across studies makes it challenging to directly compare and generalize the findings. Many investigations rely on relatively small, institution-specific datasets, limiting the external validity of their reported performance metrics. In addition, a lack of standardized evaluation criteria and metrics further complicates cross-study comparisons. While deep learning approaches have demonstrated promising results, many studies focus on either segmentation or classification in isolation, rather than integrating both processes for a comprehensive solution. Moreover, the impact of inter-observer variability in manual annotations is not consistently addressed, and several methods remain computationally intensive, which may hinder their real-time clinical applicability. These limitations underscore the need for more standardized, large-scale studies and the development of robust, efficient, and clinically validated models.

Materials and methods

The proposed framework for myocardial scar severity classification in LGE-CMR images consists of four main stages: preprocessing, myocardium detection, feature extraction, and severity classification, as shown in Fig. 1. Initially, ensemble filtering is applied using a combination of median filtering and non-local means (NLM) filtering to enhance image quality by reducing noise while preserving essential myocardial structures. This preprocessing step improves contrast and clarity, which is critical for subsequent processing. Following preprocessing, precise localization of the myocardium is achieved using ScarYOLO, an optimized YOLOv8-based model tailored for cardiac imaging. ScarYOLO’s architecture is refined by modifying the input size and batch size, which enhances detection performance and adaptability to variations inherent in LGE-CMR images. This stage ensures accurate delineation of the myocardium and the associated scarred regions, effectively mitigating issues caused by low contrast and inter-patient variability. Once the myocardium is detected, the segmented regions are processed through contrastive myocardial scar learning (CMSL) for integrated feature extraction and severity classification. In CMSL, data augmentation techniques—such as rotation, flipping, intensity scaling, and Gaussian noise—are applied to bolster model generalization. CMSL computes the contrastive loss using Manhattan distance as the optimal metric, which captures the absolute differences between high-dimensional feature embeddings and ensures robust feature separation between mild and severe scar cases. An Xception-based encoder, pretrained on large-scale medical imaging data, is further fine-tuned on a labeled LGE-CMR dataset to capture robust and discriminative features. This fine-tuning is complemented by the integration of additional dense layers and a classification head that collectively refine the severity prediction, ultimately categorizing the scar tissues into mild or severe classes. By integrating ensemble filtering for noise reduction, ScarYOLO for precise myocardium segmentation, and CMSL—with Manhattan distance-enhanced contrastive loss—for effective feature extraction and severity classification, the proposed methodology achieves significant improvements in detection precision, classification accuracy, and model generalization. Materials

This study utilized 699 late gadolinium-enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance (LGE-CMR) images from a cohort of 157 patients diagnosed with non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy (NIDCM) and mid-wall LGE, acquired at the Royal Brompton Hospital. The images were obtained following a standardized protocol, with an in-plane resolution ranging from 0.64 to 1.97 mm and a slice thickness of 7–10.5 mm. For robust model development and evaluation, the dataset was partitioned into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. Images were selected based on their location below the outflow tract with LGE and sufficient myocardial thickness to support a simulated electrical wave at a 200 ms cycle length, ensuring clinical relevance [29]. Fibrotic regions were identified by two independent expert readers, considering only those located in the intramural and/or subepicardial layers. Fibrosis was confirmed based on visibility in both phase-encoding directions and two orthogonal views. The myocardial borders were delineated in each short-axis slice containing fibrosis, and segmentation was performed using the full-width at half-maximum technique with CMR42 software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc.) [29]. The dataset featured a nearly balanced distribution of scar severity, with approximately 350 images classified as mild scars and 349 images as severe scars, which facilitated unbiased training and evaluation of the proposed model.

Preprocessing

Preprocessing is a critical step aimed at enhancing the quality of LGE-CMR images and improving the performance of subsequent tasks such as myocardium segmentation and scar detection. In our framework, we employ an ensemble filtering approach that combines median filtering and non-local means (NLM) filtering, tailored specifically for LGE-CMR images to boost contrast and clarity.

Myocardium detection using ScarYOLO

ScarYOLO is a domain-adapted implementation of the YOLOv8 architecture, specifically tailored for myocardium localization in LGE-CMR images. While the core components of YOLOv8 are retained, the clinical contribution lies in adapting and optimizing the framework—through input resolution, batch size tuning, and cardiac-specific augmentation—for high-precision myocardium detection under the unique constraints of cardiac imaging. ScarYOLO: myocardium detection process architecture

At its core, ScarYOLO builds on the modified CSPDarknet53 backbone employed in YOLOv8, where the traditional CSPLayer is replaced by the advanced C2f module (Cross-Stage Partial Bottleneck with two convolutions). This module concatenates outputs from multiple bottlenecks—each consisting of two successive 3 × 3 convolutions with residual connections—thus fusing high-level features with detailed contextual information. In addition, the initial stem’s 6 × 6 convolution is substituted with a more efficient 3 × 3 convolution, enabling the network to capture finer details early in the processing pipeline. Furthermore, modifications in the bottleneck layers—specifically changing the kernel size of the first convolution from 1 × 1 to 3 × 3—enhance feature extraction by drawing inspiration from the ResNet block architecture.

Severity classification using contrastive myocardial scar learning (CMSL)

CMSL is a novel approach to severity classification of myocardial scars in LGE-CMR images. CMSL is designed to simultaneously perform robust feature extraction and precise classification of scar tissue into mild or severe categories, leveraging the power of contrastive self-supervised learning, as shown in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Myocardial scar severity classification process using CMSL

Myocardial scar severity classification process using CMSL

The CMSL framework begins with ScarYOLO, an enhanced version of the YOLO (You Only Look Once) model specifically optimized for detecting myocardium regions in late gadolinium enhancement cardiac magnetic resonance (LGE-CMR) images. This step is crucial, as it isolates the myocardium from surrounding structures, ensuring that the subsequent feature extraction focuses exclusively on relevant scar tissue regions. Once the myocardium has been localized, it is passed through an Xception-based encoder that has been pretrained on extensive medical imaging datasets and fine-tuned on a labeled dataset for myocardial scar classification. The Xception encoder is chosen for its depth-wise separable convolutions, which allow for efficient yet powerful feature extraction. The extracted features capture intricate patterns related to scar morphology, texture, and intensity variations, which are crucial for distinguishing between mild and severe myocardial scars.

Medical images often exhibit variations in contrast, noise, and spatial orientation due to differences in imaging protocols and patient anatomy. To enhance the generalization capability of CMSL, extensive data augmentation techniques are applied to the detected myocardium regions. These augmentations include random rotations, horizontal flipping, intensity scaling, and Gaussian noise injection. These augmentations are not merely applied for regularization; they play a critical role in contrastive learning by generating positive pairs—different augmented views of the same myocardium region—ensuring that the model learns invariant representations of scar tissue.

A key innovation in CMSL is the integration of contrastive self-supervised learning to enhance feature representation. Traditional classification models rely solely on labeled data, but contrastive learning allows the model to learn better feature representations even before classification by leveraging similarity relationships between samples. The CMSL model is trained using a contrastive loss that encourages the feature embeddings of augmented views of the same image (positive pairs) to be closer in the feature space, while ensuring that embeddings from different images (negative pairs) are pushed apart. In our approach, positive and negative pairs are defined based on the similarity between feature embeddings extracted from the Xception encoder, but rather than relying solely on Euclidean distance, we use Manhattan distance as the metric in the contrastive loss computation. Manhattan distance has certain advantages over cosine similarity. While cosine similarity is scale-invariant and focuses on the angle between vectors, Manhattan distance is more sensitive to small changes in individual dimensions and tends to be more robust in high-dimensional spaces, which can result in better separation of similar yet distinct features. This improved sensitivity allows for enhanced discrimination between subtle differences in myocardial scar patterns, leading to more robust feature separability and ultimately, improved classification performance.

The choice of the Xception network as the encoder was based on its efficiency and its ability to capture detailed spatial hierarchies through depth-wise separable convolutions. This architecture excels at extracting rich, discriminative features from LGE-CMR images while keeping computational costs relatively low. In our experiments, alternative architectures such as ResNet and EfficientNet were also evaluated, as shown in Table 4; however, the Xception-based encoder consistently outperformed these models in terms of accuracy and generalization. The superior performance of Xception can be attributed to its efficient parameter utilization and its effectiveness in capturing fine-grained details critical for differentiating between mild and severe scar tissues. This demonstrates that the Xception-based encoder outperforms both ResNet and EfficientNet in terms of accuracy, F1-score, and AUC, thereby justifying its selection for the proposed CMSL model. While the proposed framework demonstrates high detection accuracy and robust classification performance, several limitations remain. The current model is limited to binary classification of myocardial scar severity, which does not capture the full spectrum of scar pathology. In addition, the system’s performance, validated on curated datasets, may be affected by variations in image quality and acquisition protocols in real-world clinical settings. Future work will focus on refining segmentation techniques for precise delineation of myocardial scars and expanding the classification to a three-tier system—mild, moderate, and severe—to provide more nuanced risk stratification. Furthermore, integrating domain adaptation strategies and enhancing real-time processing capabilities will be pursued to improve the framework’s generalizability and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

This paper presents a comprehensive deep learning framework for automated myocardial scar severity classification using LGE-CMR images. The proposed approach integrates robust preprocessing via ensemble filtering, precise myocardium detection using an optimized ScarYOLO model, and advanced classification through the contrastive multi-stage learning (CMSL) module. The framework leverages data augmentation with positive–negative pair separation and negative pair removal and employs Manhattan distance for enhanced contrastive learning, leading to superior feature extraction and refined class separability. Fine-tuning with additional dense layers and a dedicated classification layer further boosts the model’s generalization, enabling accurate differentiation between mild and severe myocardial scars. Extensive experimental evaluations demonstrate that the proposed system outperforms conventional methods, achieving high accuracy (96.22%), strong sensitivity (TPR: 96.81%), and excellent specificity (95.58%). Comparative analyses indicate that the use of Manhattan distance provides a marginal performance edge over cosine similarity, while visual assessments via ROC analysis, confusion matrices, and heat maps substantiate the model’s robust discriminatory power. Overall, the framework offers a promising, clinically viable solution for non-invasive myocardial scar assessment, with significant implications for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment planning in cardiovascular disease management. Future work will focus on the precise delineation of myocardial scar regions and expanding the classification task to a three-tier system—mild, moderate, and severe—to further enhance diagnostic specificity and clinical utility.
