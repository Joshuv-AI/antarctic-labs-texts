RB loss modulates chromatin organization by regulating cohesin-dependent loops and enhancer-promoter interactions
Authors: Hanjun Lee, Ioanna-Maria Gkotinakou, Connor G McGrath, Badri Krishnan, Sambhavi Animesh, Isabella Salinas, Robert Morris, Moshe Sade-Feldman, Wilhelm Haas, Michael S Lawrence, Ioannis Sanidas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe retinoblastoma protein (RB) is a well-characterized repressor of E2F transcriptional activity that controls genes involved in cell proliferation during the G1 phase. Here, we examine the effect of RB on chromatin organization and uncover a non-canonical role for RB in which it promotes the removal of cohesin from insulators and thereby induces the expression of adjacent genes. We identify that RB colocalizes with cohesin across the human genome. During mitosis, RB facilitates the removal of cohesin from CTCF sites, with this effect persisting into early G1, impacting loop formation and extrusion at topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries. Chromosome conformation capture assays reveal that RB reduces insulation at TAD boundaries and promotes enhancer-promoter interactions marked by histone 3 lysine 27 acetylation. In this way, RB enhances the transcription of hundreds of genes beyond the E2F transcriptional program, a finding that expands our understanding of this key tumor suppressor and cell cycle regulator.Subject terms: Chromatin structure, Epigenomics, Gene regulation, Transcriptional regulatory elements

Introduction

The retinoblastoma gene RB1 was the first tumor suppressor to be molecularly defined and is often described as a prototypical tumor suppressor1,2. Its protein product, RB, is a critical cell cycle regulator that represses the transcriptional program required for cell proliferation. Active RB associates with E2F-regulated promoters and represses the transcription of cell cycle genes, preventing cell cycle advancement1. Cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) activity phosphorylates RB, inhibiting its association with E2F3–6 and promoting E2F-dependent transcription and cell cycle progression2,7,8.

RB1 is genetically or functionally inactivated in most types of cancer9,10. More frequently, cancer cells acquire mutations that increase cyclin D-CDK4/6 or cyclin E-CDK2 kinase activity, leading to hyperphosphorylation and functional inactivation of RB10. However, some cancers can directly target RB through loss-of-function mutations and loss of heterozygosity in the RB1 gene itself11–13. RB1 mutations are often late events during tumor evolution14,15, and RB-deficient tumors exhibit unique characteristics that are not fully explained by the RB/E2F model and its role in the regulation of cell proliferation16. These include the histological trans-differentiation of adenocarcinoma to neuroendocrine tumors and the development of acquired resistance to targeted therapies14,15,17–19, increased genomic instability associated with compromised sister chromatid cohesion20–22, and heightened metastatic potential in lung and prostate cancers14,15,19. These non-canonical roles of RB raise the question of whether it might have additional functions in transcriptional regulation beyond the direct inhibition of E2F-targeted genes.

Recently, we discovered that RB’s association with E2F-bound promoters represents only a portion of the chromatin-bound population of RB23. RB is also associated with AP-1-bound enhancers and CTCF insulators23, which may suggest an emerging model in which RB regulates transcription by interacting with different types of cis-regulatory elements. Fluctuations in RB phosphorylation at CDK sites control RB distribution between promoters and enhancers but have less influence on RB’s association with insulators23,24. Microscopic studies, including our own, have revealed that RB plays an important role in regulating nuclear morphology25,26. Since CTCF sites are the primary anchors for the cell’s loop extrusion machinery27–30, which regulates transcription partly through chromatin insulation31–33, and since RB’s association with CTCF sites is mostly cell cycle-independent and conserved across cell types23,24, we hypothesized that RB might exert non-canonical transcriptional regulation by modulating DNA loops.

Here, by employing chromosome conformation capture with nucleosome resolution34, we reveal that RB is an essential regulator of cohesin-dependent loops at CTCF insulators. Loss of RB increased the number and size of DNA loops and the stability of topologically associating domains (TADs). We observed that RB and cohesin were associated genome-wide at accessible chromatin regions. In RB-deficient cells, cohesin was less effectively removed from CTCF insulators, resulting in its accumulation and enhanced insulation. Cell cycle synchronization experiments showed that the RB-mediated deregulation of cohesin distribution peaked during mitosis and persisted into early G1. The enhanced insulation at CTCF sites following RB loss diminished enhancer-promoter interactions assessed by histone 3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) HiChIP35. Overall, we demonstrate that by facilitating cohesin removal from insulators and promoting enhancer-promoter interactions, RB activates hundreds of genes outside of the E2F transcriptional program.

Results

RB is a regulator of chromatin organization

To characterize the impact of RB in chromatin organization, we employed Micro-C34, a chromosome conformation capture method with nucleosome resolution. We conducted two complementary sets of experiments in human retinal pigment epithelial cells immortalized by telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT-RPE1) (Fig. 1a, b) to compare the effects of (1) conditional gain of constitutively active RB and (2) chronic RB deficiency. Cell cycle synchronization was used to minimize potential confounding variables between the two experimental systems. In the first set of experiments, we studied the role of unphosphorylated RB, which has been described to trigger visible changes in nuclear architecture and promote chromatin dispersion25. To examine these microscopic findings in chromosome conformation capture, we utilized the same cellular model, replacing the endogenous RB with a constitutively active FLAG-tagged variant (RBΔCDK) via a doxycycline-inducible system23,25,36. The RBΔCDK variant harbors 14 missense mutations that substitute all known endogenous CDK phosphorylation sites with alanine36,37. Following 24 h of doxycycline treatment, cells entered the G0/G1 phase, and upon 72 h, they exited the cell cycle and developed a chromatin dispersion phenotype25. In the second set of experiments, we aimed to distinguish RB’s role in chromatin organization from changes related to cell cycle. To model RB loss, we utilized RB1 knockout hTERT-RPE1 cells generated with the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Supplementary Fig. 1)38. These cells were synchronized in G1 phase via 6 days of contact inhibition and then subsequently released to S phase via trypsinization (Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Chromosome conformation capture recapitulated many of the expected features of chromatin organization, including chromosome territories39, nuclear compartments40, TADs41,42, cohesin-dependent loops27,43, and architectural stripes44 (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). To identify the overall scale at which RB-dependent changes in chromatin organization occur, we measured the contact probability (P(s)), which assesses the likelihood of contact formation as a function of genomic separation (Fig. 1c)43,45. The contact probability curve has been shown to have at least two different regimes in mammalian cells, among which a shallow decay for s < 200 kb has been reported to be produced by cohesin-dependent loop formation43,46. In our chromosome conformation capture, we identified three major regimes in the contact probability curve: the cohesin-dependent decay for s < 200 kb (P(s) ≈ s−0.8), a steeper decay for 200 kb <s < 3 Mb (P(s) ≈ s−1.3), and a variable far-cis scaling for s > 3 Mb (Fig. 1c).

Expression of RB∆CDK caused significant changes in chromatin structures at s > 3 Mb, often associated with far-cis contacts between nuclear compartments43,46. Accordingly, when RB∆CDK was expressed, the most striking change in chromatin organization occurred in the distribution of nuclear compartments and their interactions (Fig. 1d). Nuclear compartments are chromatin features that are thought to be cohesin-independent43. Although changes that occurred between 0 h and 24 h or 72 h of RB∆CDK expression may be associated with G1-arrest, most compartmental changes occurred between 24 h and 72 h of RB∆CDK expression, coinciding with the irreversible cell cycle exit, as previously described25. We observed the spreading of compartment B to adjacent regions and increased spatial distance within compartment B (Fig. 1d), concordant with the previously described heterochromatin dispersion phenotype (Fig. 1e)25.

In contrast, the loss of RB in G1-synchronized cells affected chromatin interaction at s < 200 kb and 200 kb <s < 3 Mb (Fig. 1c). In both regimes, RB loss led to steeper decays, shifting the contact probability curves to P(s) ≈ s−0.85 (s < 200 kb) and P(s) ≈ s−1.35 (200 kb <s < 3 Mb). The steeper decay at the cohesin-dependent regime for s < 200 kb suggested that RB may regulate cohesin-dependent loop formation independently from its effect on cell cycle. Indeed, in cells synchronized in the G1 phase, RB loss increased the number of cohesin-dependent loops by 40.5% (Fig. 1f; p-value = 3.66 × 10−7, two-tailed paired Student’s t-test) and increased the average size of these loops by 16.4 kb (Fig. 1g; p-value = 1.86 × 10−2, two-tailed Student’s t-test). In RB-depleted cells, these loops also produced stronger domains, as evidenced by increased TAD strength in RBKO compared to RBWT (Fig. 1h). These changes were not observed in cells accumulated in S phase (Fig. 1f, g). Furthermore, three-dimensional reconstruction of the chromatin architecture, via a cell population-based algorithm, revealed that RB loss weakens the spatial clustering of acrocentric chromosomes harboring nucleolus organizer regions. Specifically, the average distance between centromeres of acrocentric chromosomes grew from ~1.81 µm in RBWT to ~2.52 µm in RBKO (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). This Micro-C data corroborated previous microscopic observations26, positioning RB as a significant regulator of the cohesin-dependent interphase chromatin architecture. In summary, we observe that RB exerts broad effects on chromatin organization (Fig. 1i), with RB∆CDK expression promoting primarily heterochromatin spreading and dispersion (Fig. 1d, e), and RB loss in G1-arrested cells altering cohesin-dependent loop number, loop length, and TAD boundary strength (Fig. 1g, h).

Genome-wide association of RB and cohesin

Given that the loss of RB promoted cohesin-dependent loop formation, we assessed whether chromatin-bound RB is associated with genomic loci bound by cohesin. For this, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) for SMC3 in hTERT-RPE1 cells. We also performed ChIP-seq experiments for various histone modifications to annotate active promoters and enhancers (H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K27ac) and heterochromatin (H3K27me3) and for transcription factors previously reported to colocalize with RB at cis-regulatory elements (E2F1, CTCF, and c-Jun)23. To assess chromatin accessibility at these loci, we performed assays for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq).

Strikingly, RB showed extensive colocalization with SMC3 in the human genome (Fig. 2a, b): 68.7% of RB binding sites exhibited SMC3 binding as well (Fig. 2a). This overlap was strongest at insulators, where 99.6% of RB binding sites showed SMC3 binding. Notably, the overlap between RB and SMC3 was significantly more extensive than that between RB and E2F1, which was only evident in promoter regions. Besides the association with SMC3, all chromatin regions bound by RB were highly accessible (Fig. 2b).

Cohesin is a loop extrusion machinery that utilizes energy from ATP hydrolysis to form chromatin loops28,29. We wondered, therefore, if RB binding sites also demonstrate insulation of chromatin contacts at a comparable level to that observed at cohesin binding sites. To quantify the strength of insulation, we measured the insulation score from chromosome conformation capture, with more negative values indicating stronger insulation47,48. Indeed, RB binding sites were significantly more insulating compared to the genome-wide average (Fig. 2c; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test), and were comparable to the insulation strength at SMC3 binding sites. At CTCF binding sites, which showed the strongest insulation on average (Fig. 2c; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test), the co-occurrence of RB binding further increased insulation (Fig. 2c; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test). Notably, RB-bound CTCF sites were significantly more accessible compared to non-RB-bound CTCF sites (Fig. 2d; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test), which may contribute to the observed differences in insulation49.

At promoters, RB and SMC3 localization both occurred at a region ~65 bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) (Fig. 2e). RB-bound promoters were more insulating compared to non-RB-bound promoters (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Fig. 8; p-value = 9.0 × 10−240, two-tailed Student’s t-test) and were often co-occupied by E2F1 (Supplementary Fig. 9), leading to decreased nucleosome occupancy at the TSS and more active transcription (Fig. 2g, h; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test). Notably, the increased insulation at RB-bound promoters was significant when it was compared to non-RB-bound promoters with similar transcriptional activity (Supplementary Fig. 8; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test).

In brief, we report that RB-bound chromatin loci were also occupied by SMC3 and exhibited increased insulation. This extensive colocalization of RB and SMC3 on chromatin raises the question of whether RB-mediated regulation of cohesin-dependent loops results from RB’s direct influence on cohesin activity, and whether this regulation is confined to RB-bound loci or occurs genome-wide.

RB promotes cohesin removal from CTCF sites across chromosomes

The extensive co-localization of chromatin-bound RB and SMC3, along with the observed impact of RB loss on cohesin-dependent loop formation and TAD boundary strength, prompted us to investigate RB’s role in cohesin localization. Upon RB depletion, a clear change in the distribution of SMC3 was observed genome-wide. Outside DNA repeats, the binding of SMC3 occurred at three distinct types of cis-regulatory elements: promoters, insulators, and enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 10). In G1-synchronized cells, RB loss led to an average +14.4% increase (95% confidence interval (CI), 11.1%–17.7%; n = 4) in the SMC3 signal across 21,498 insulators (Fig. 3a). We reproduced this finding in biological replicate experiments as well as in different RB1 knockout clones (Fig. 3a). To rule out the possibility that the observed increase is an indirect effect of RB loss, we compared the proteomes of RBWT and RBKO cells and found no significant differences in the protein levels of SMC3 or other core components of the cohesin complex (Supplementary Fig. 11). To exclude the possibility that the observed changes resulted from normalization artifacts, we utilized spike-in DNA from Escherichia coli as an additional control and confirmed the increase in SMC3 signal at insulators (Fig. 3a). Notably, we did not observe analogous increases in SMC3 signal at promoters (+2.8% upon RB loss; n = 4) or enhancers (−3.4% upon RB loss; n = 4), further supporting the specificity of RB’s regulatory role at insulator sites. Notably, the enrichment of signal at insulators occurred across the chromosome and was not confined to RB-bound loci (Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that the observed functional interplay between RB and SMC3 might be distinct from the physical interaction.

To further characterize the functional interplay between RB and SMC3, we performed SMC3 ChIP-seq experiments in RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells synchronized at different phases of the cell cycle: G1, S, and prometaphase (Fig. 3b). Synchronization in prometaphase was achieved via nocodazole treatment. As expected, prometaphase-synchronized cells exhibited the weakest SMC3 binding at CTCF insulators, reflecting the prophase pathway, whereby chromatin-bound cohesin is released from chromosome arms through a Wapl-dependent mechanism50,51. We also reproduced the reduction in cohesin enrichment at CTCF insulators upon G1/S transition, thought to result from the dissolution of CTCF-chromatin interactions by the DNA replication machinery52. Importantly, we identified that RB loss significantly disturbs cohesin release in prometaphase cells, leading to an average +57.6% increase (95% CI, 42.1%–73.2%; n = 2) in the SMC3 signal across 21,498 insulators (Fig. 3b). This change was specific (p-value = 1.26 × 10−3, one-way ANOVA) to insulators and was less pronounced in promoters (+12.2% upon RB loss; n = 2) and enhancers (+8.2% upon RB loss; n = 2). SMC3 ChIP-seq in RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells synchronized in prometaphase using inhibition of Eg5 kinesin activity, as an alternative to nocodazole-induced arrest, similarly revealed cohesin enrichment at insulators following RB loss (Supplementary Fig. 12).

We observed that the RB-mediated deregulation of cohesin distribution peaked during mitosis and persisted into early G1 (Fig. 3b). Supporting this model, we found that the fold change in SMC3 binding upon RB depletion was positively correlated between G1 and prometaphase cells, while S-phase cells showed little to no correlation with either (Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Fig. 10). Notably, a higher correlation was observed in regions that gained SMC3 upon RB loss, compared to regions that lost SMC3. Since RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells synchronized in prometaphase exhibited a dramatic increase in SMC3 binding at insulators, we wondered which sites lost SMC3 during this process. Therefore, we performed a DNA motif analysis on regions that gained or lost SMC3 signal upon RB loss (Fig. 3d). We observed that regions that gained SMC3 were highly enriched with CTCF and BORIS binding motifs, corroborating our findings that SMC3 gain primarily occurs at insulators. Notably, in regions that lost SMC3, we did not observe DNA motif enrichment at a similar level to those that gained SMC3 (Fig. 3d). While AP-1 binding motifs showed statistical significance, AP-1-bound enhancers exhibited an overall increase in SMC3 signal upon RB loss (Supplementary Fig. 13), and only the strongest AP-1-bound enhancers (n = 3652) showed slight reductions in SMC3 signal (−2.3% change upon RB loss). Due to the relatively small number of these sites and the minimal differences in SMC3 signal, we conclude that AP-1-bound enhancers are not the primary source of SMC3 loss.

We then assessed repetitive DNA sequences, where a significant fraction of cohesin may localize (Supplementary Fig. 14)53. As RBWT hTERT-RPE1 cells entered prometaphase, we observed a specific 3.5-fold increase in SMC3 ChIP-seq reads at centromeric α-satellites, with 2.8% of all reads in prometaphase cells arising from these regions. However, RB loss led to an increased SMC3 binding (+10.6% upon RB loss) at centromeric α-satellites in prometaphase cells, ruling out the possibility that the repetitive DNA sequences are the primary source of SMC3 loss upon RB depletion.

Cohesin-dependent loop extrusion is a dynamic process, with the vast majority of cohesin being partially extruded and not statically localized to TAD boundaries (Fig. 3e)28,30. We therefore hypothesized that partially extruded cohesin might be the source of SMC3 loss upon RB depletion. Supporting this hypothesis, in a pooled analysis, we observed a chromosome-wide decrease in SMC3 signal at intermediary non-SMC3 sites upon RB loss (Fig. 3f). These changes were specific to G1 and prometaphase cells and not to cells synchronized in S phase. Sequencing of the input DNA showed no differences in the same regions, confirming that the observed changes in SMC3 signal at intermediary non-SMC3 sites upon RB loss were not due to differential sequencing coverage (Supplementary Fig. 15). Overall, we conclude that RB loss perturbs cohesin release in prometaphase, leading to cohesin enrichment at CTCF insulators and cohesin depletion at intermediary non-SMC3 sites, with these effects persisting into G1.

RB reduces insulation at TAD boundaries

While RB-mediated changes in SMC3 distribution peaks during the mitosis, these cells typically lack nuclear compartments and TADs45. Notably, we observed that this deregulation of SMC3 distribution persists into the G1 phase. This led us to investigate whether RB-mediated changes in SMC3 distribution affect chromatin organization in G1. A strong negative correlation was found between the difference in SMC3 ChIP-seq signal and the change in the insulation score (Spearman’s ρ = −0.87, p-value = 1.17 × 10−3), suggesting that regions that gained SMC3 following RB loss showed increased insulation (Fig. 3g). Further analysis of insulation changes at the entire SMC3-bound or RB-bound genomic loci revealed that RB depletion specifically induced insulation at CTCF-bound insulators and TAD boundaries, while having the opposite effect in promoters and enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 15).

These changes in insulation were also evident from the differential analysis of chromosome conformation capture. Reflecting the overall increase in the number of loops upon RB loss, we observed a 4.7-fold increase in the number of loops specific to RBKO compared to RBWT (Supplementary Fig. 16). Inspection of individual regions in chromosomes 9 and 11 revealed visually striking gains in chromatin contacts and showed that the formation of differential loops in RBKO is driven by an increase in cohesin signal at insulators, including SMC3 and STAG1 (Fig. 3h, i). Additional SMC3 ChIP-quantitative PCR further confirmed the increase of SMC3 binding at chromosome 9 locus following RB loss (Supplementary Fig. 17). Replicate experiments, using spike-in DNA from E. coli as an additional control, corroborated the increased SMC3 binding at these insulators upon RB loss.

RB enhances the non-E2F transcriptional program

Insulators are cis-regulatory elements that act as barriers for enhancer-promoter interactions54. Therefore, we wondered whether the observed interplay between RB and SMC3 at insulators has implications for enhancer-promoter interactions. To quantify enhancer-promoter interactions, we utilized H3K27ac HiChIP in RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells synchronized in G135.

Upon RB loss, we observed a general loss of contacts between sites marked by H3K27ac (Fig. 4a–c). The number of H3K27ac HiChIP peaks decreased 3.3-fold (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 18), and the average strength of HiChIP interactions was weakened in both biological replicate experiments (Fig. 4b). Using ChIP-seq tracks for histone modifications, we further categorized these interactions as enhancer-enhancer, enhancer-promoter, and promoter-promoter interactions (“Methods”). Among the H3K27ac HiChIP peaks shared between RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells, 83.3% represented enhancer-enhancer interactions, 12.0% were enhancer-promoter interactions, and 4.8% were promoter-promoter interactions. Although RB1 knockout cells display slightly increased H3K27ac ChIP-seq signal compared to RB1 wild-type hTERT-RPE1 cells, consistent with transcriptional de-repression23, RB depletion reduced the strength of H3K27ac HiChIP peaks across all three interaction groups (Fig. 4c, and Supplementary Fig. 15). As an orthogonal approach, we performed MED15 ChIP-seq to assess mediator recruitment and observed reduced MED15 occupancy at enhancers and promoters following RB loss (Fig. 4d, and Supplementary Fig. 19). To further investigate whether the role of RB in regulating enhancer-promoter interactions associated with its activity in repressing insulation at TAD boundaries, we compared interactions between enhancer-promoter pairs that cross a TAD boundary (inter-TAD) with those that do not (intra-TAD). To control distance-dependent effects, we selected a subset of enhancer-promoter pairs matched for distance (Supplementary Fig. 20). As expected, intra-TAD enhancer-promoter interactions were stronger and more well-defined, while inter-TAD interactions were weaker and less distinct in both RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 20). Comparing RBWT and RBKO cells revealed that both intra- and inter-TAD interactions weaken upon RB loss, with the RB-dependent effect appearing more pronounced when the enhancer-promoter pair spans a TAD boundary. Consistent with this, MED15 ChIP-seq analysis demonstrated that in RBWT cells, TAD boundaries impose a distance-dependent effect on MED15 ChIP-seq signal and enhancer activity, whereas this pattern is lost in RBKO cells (Supplementary Fig. 20c, d). Together, these findings suggest that RB-mediated repression of insulation at TAD boundaries facilitates increased enhancer-promoter interactions in the presence of wild-type RB, although additional mechanisms may also contribute. Motif enrichment analysis of enhancers with decreased MED15 binding upon RB loss revealed enrichment of AP-1 and TEAD transcription factor motifs (Supplementary Fig. 19). Given that RB has been reported to associate with these enhancer regions23,55, these results raise the possibility that RB regulates enhancer activity through additional mechanistically distinct pathways.

To assess the transcriptional impact of the RB-mediated changes in H3K27ac interactions, we compiled a set of 2656 genes with insulators located between active enhancers and an active promoter (Fig. 5a). From this set, we identified 1754 genes that showed no RB or E2F1 binding at the promoter. We reasoned that this gene set would be ideal for assessing the transcriptional effects of the RB-induced chromatin rearrangements, as potential complications from RB-mediated repression of E2F activity would be minimal. Within this gene set, 181 genes showed increased SMC3 binding at the insulator (cohesin gain), and 44 genes showed decreased SMC3 binding at the insulator (cohesin loss) across experiments, consistent with the overall increase in SMC3 binding at insulators upon RB loss (Fig. 5b). Although RB depletion elevates the average expression levels of genes in both RB/E2F-bound and non-RB/non-E2F-bound promoters (Fig. 5b, left panel), the expression of genes within the “cohesin gain“ gene set was significantly lower compared to the average expression level of genes with non-RB/non-E2F-bound promoters (Fig. 5b; p-value = 0.0368, two-tailed Student’s t-test) or compared to the “cohesin loss” gene set (Fig. 5b; p-value = 9.16 × 10−4, two-tailed Student’s t-test). This indicates that cohesin release at insulators facilitated by RB impacts transcription and that RB acts via this mechanism to safeguard and enhance the expression of multiple non-E2F target genes. As an example, we demonstrate RB’s role in enhancing the expression of lysyl oxidase-like 1 (LOXL1) that correlates with reduced cohesin binding at an insulator site located between LOXL1 promoter and enhancer (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Gene set enrichment and gene ontology analyses of the 181 genes regulated by cohesin gain at an adjacent insulator revealed that RB-mediated dysregulation of SMC3 distribution primarily affects genes regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition by maintaining epithelial characteristics (Fig. 5c, and Supplementary Fig. 22; p-value = 4.84 × 10−4, gene set enrichment analysis), and more specifically extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 5c; p-value = 1.41 × 10−3, gene ontology: biological processes). Consistent with these results, we also observed an increased cellular detachment rate in both RBKO clones assessed compared to RBWT in hTERT-RPE1 cells (Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Fig. 23; p-value < 0.0001, two-tailed Student’s t-test). Consistent with this, wound healing assays revealed an elevated migration rate in RBKO cells compared to RBWT cells (Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Fig. 24). We also observed enrichment of genes associated with myogenesis (Fig. 5c; p-value = 1.27 × 10−2, gene set enrichment analysis), intriguing in light of previous suggestions that RB behaves as a transcriptional activator during this process56–58. While these phenotypes do not represent comprehensive functional validation of RB-regulated chromatin architecture, these cell cycle-independent effects of RB may be a good starting point for connecting architectural chromatin changes to cellular behavior of RB-deficient cells.

To place these transcriptional changes in context, we clustered the RB-deregulated genes based on the transcriptional patterns observed in synchronized populations of RBWT and RBKO cells. We identified five major categories of RB-mediated transcriptional regulation (Fig. 5f). Cluster 3 represents RB-bound genes suppressed by RB in G1, with promoters enriched in E2F binding motifs (Supplementary Fig. 25). Cluster 1 includes cell cycle-dependent genes suppressed in G1 in both RBWT and RBKO cells, whose promoters are enriched in the CHR motif, indicating late cell cycle genes controlled by DREAM and MMB-FOXM159. The other three groups of the RB-deregulated genes show less RB binding, and their transcriptional regulation does not follow the canonical RB/E2F model. Of these, cluster 5, a group of G1-specific genes whose expression is enhanced by RB, exhibited the strongest association with the ‘cohesin gain’ gene set: 54.5% of differentially expressed genes in this gene set belonged to cluster 5 (Fig. 5f; p-value = 5.35 × 10−3, Fisher’s exact test). Notably, the gene set enrichment and gene ontology analyses for cluster 5 genes revealed similar signatures compared to the “cohesin gain” gene set (Supplementary Fig. 26). Overall, our data indicate that rather than targeting random genes, this previously unrecognized RB function may regulate specific biological processes. To further investigate whether these RB-associated transcriptional changes occur population-wide or are driven by a specific cell population, we performed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) experiments in RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells synchronized in the G1 phase via contact inhibition. Principal component analysis revealed that all cells clustered together, with a modest separation between RBWT and RBKO cells, primarily driven, as expected, by differences in RB/E2F target gene expression—cluster 3 (Supplementary Fig. 26). Notably, the scRNA-seq analysis validated the differential expression of cluster 5 genes, where RB functions as a transcriptional activator during G1 (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). While the scRNA-seq experiment corroborated non-canonical transcriptional activation by RB, it did not reveal additional distinct transcriptional profiles at the single-cell level that would support RB’s effect on cellular heterogeneity.

Discussion

Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has shown that chromatin-bound RB is not limited to E2F promoters but is also associated with other cis-regulatory elements23,60–62. Our findings reveal that RB has additional roles in the human genome independent of the suppression of E2F transcriptional program. RB also influences chromatin organization and promotes the expression of hundreds of genes, which are distinct from the group of E2F-regulated genes controlling cell proliferation. We demonstrate that RB shares the same genomic loci with cohesin and indirectly promotes cohesin release from CTCF sites. Additionally, we describe an RB-mediated deregulation of cohesin distribution, which peaks during mitosis and persists into early G1, impacting the loop extrusion at TAD boundaries. In the absence of RB, cells experience increased insulation at TAD boundaries, which is strongly correlated with cohesin enrichment, and a concomitant reduction of enhancer-promoter interactions. Through this mechanism, RB enhances the transcription of non-E2F target genes positioned adjacent to CTCF insulators (Fig. 6).

Canonical and non-canonical transcriptional regulation by RB.

RB can act both as a transcriptional repressor (top) and activator (bottom). In its canonical role, active RB represses transcription at E2F-regulated promoters during the G1-phase. In its non-canonical role described here, active RB enhances transcription at insulator-regulated promoters by promoting cohesin removal from insulator sites. This leads to reduced chromatin insulation and facilitates enhancer/promoter interactions, thereby activating transcription of genes not directly bound by RB/E2F.

The changes in cohesin occupancy upon RB loss qualitatively resemble the accumulation of cohesin at TAD boundaries reported in WAPL-depleted cells46, albeit at a weaker magnitude. Notably, our data indicate that the altered chromatin distribution of cohesin upon RB loss is resolved during cell cycle progression, as the differential binding of SMC3 at chromatin insulators diminishes in S phase. Future studies employing Micro-C profiling at multiple time points following acute RB loss may answer whether the cell cycle- and RB-dependent changes in cohesin distribution result in more persistent defects in higher-order chromatin architecture. In our chromosome conformation capture experiments, RB loss led to increased insulation at TAD boundaries and decreased enhancer-promoter interactions without significantly altering the composition or interactions within nuclear compartments. This observation is consistent with previous work showing that acute cohesin degradation abolishes loop domains but does not disrupt nuclear compartmentalization63. Furthermore, RB-mediated changes in cohesin-dependent loop formation observed in Micro-C experiments were transient and is restored upon S-phase entry, suggesting that it is not likely to be related to RB’s role in epigenetic alterations associated with heterochromatin dynamics64,65. Thus, we interpret that the deregulation of gene expression upon RB loss observed in our cell line model is therefore less likely to be linked to changes in heterochromatin dynamics and the position effects they may exert.

RB has been identified to act as both a suppressor and activator of gene expression36,66. Our study provides mechanistic insights into RB’s role as a transcription activator targeting also genes that are not bound by E2F. We demonstrate that RB enhances the transcription of at least hundreds of genes by promoting cohesin removal from nearby CTCF insulators. Rather than regulating promoter activity, RB modulates insulator activity and enhancer-promoter interactions, affecting genes that appear to be cell type-specific (Supplementary Fig. 22). These observations may broaden our understanding of RB’s role in cancer. RB1 mutations are relatively late events in tumor progression, often associated with metastatic disease or advancement to targeted therapies, and typically occur after cell cycle deregulation16. Recent findings showing that 3D chromatin reorganization is linked to neuroendocrine transformation in castration-resistant prostate cancer67 suggest that RB’s role in regulating chromatin insulators may contribute to cell-to-cell heterogeneity and drive tumor progression. Although our scRNA-seq analysis in RPE1 RBWT and RBKO cells did not reveal such heterogeneity, we acknowledge that in genetically altered cancer models or under therapeutic pressure, these effects could become more pronounced. We appreciate that RB’s involvement in chromatin architecture and its impact on enhancer-promoter interactions provide mechanistic foundations for exploring its role in the activation of lineage-specific programs seen in lung and prostate cancers following RB loss14,15,17–19. In addition, the uncharacterized identified role of RB in regulating enhancer-promoter interactions and activating genes involved in maintaining epithelial cell identity offers a novel mechanistic framework for how RB loss may facilitate the ability of cancer cells to establish metastatic niches68,69.

The genome-wide association of cohesin with RB-bound loci and the extensive role of RB in the regulation of cohesin distribution presents a potential clue to understanding RB’s established role in maintaining genomic stability20–22. RB loss increases genomic instability by disrupting sister chromatid cohesion during mitosis22, a process notably dependent on the cohesin release factor Wapl22. Our discovery that RB regulates SMC3 distribution on chromatin, a phenomenon that peaks during prometaphase, suggests that RB-mediated deregulation of cohesin may be linked to events occurring earlier, potentially in prophase. Although RB and SMC3 co-occupy the same chromatin loci, the presence of RB is associated with reduced cohesin binding, supporting the hypothesis that RB may promote cohesin removal at TAD boundaries during prophase by facilitating the recruitment of Wapl.

During prophase, cohesin is released from CTCF sites in a Wapl-dependent manner50,51, and condensin II mediates the loss of nuclear compartments and TADs by forming compact arrays of chromatin loops70. A fundamental question remains: which of the two pathways in the prophase could be targeted by RB? The role of RB in the chromatin distribution of SMC3, along with the Wapl dependency of compromised sister chromatid cohesion in RB-depleted cells22, supports that RB is important for cohesin activity during mitosis. However, there is currently no evidence of a direct interaction between RB and cohesin, while the RB-mediated cohesin deregulation is genome-wide and not restricted only to RB-bound loci. On the other hand, RB’s interaction with the condensin II subunit CAP-D3 has been reported to regulate condensin II association with chromatin71–73, suggesting that RB may also regulate condensin II activity in prophase. Since depletion of SMC2, a core component of the condensin complex, has been shown to partially rescue interphase nuclear compartments and TADs in prometaphase70, it is possible that RB loss antagonizes condensin II, allowing for cohesin enrichment at CTCF sites. Our findings have mapped the RB-dependent cohesin deregulation to prophase, but further studies are needed to specify the impact of these two possibilities.

In conclusion, we have identified a non-canonical role for RB, in which it regulates how DNA is packaged within the nucleus, thereby enhancing the expression of hundreds of genes independently of E2F transcriptional regulation.

Methods

Cell culture

All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Non-transformed human retinal pigment epithelial cells immortalized by human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT-RPE1) were a gift from Dr. David Pellman’s laboratory (Dana Farber Cancer Institute, RRID: CVCL_4388). Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T) were obtained from ATCC (Cat. # CRL-3216, RRID: CVCL_0063). hTERT-RPE1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, 5% fetal bovine serum, and antibiotics (penicillin, 100 units/mL; streptomycin, 100 μg/mL). HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum, and antibiotics (penicillin, 100 units/mL; streptomycin, 100 μg/mL). RB1 knockdown or the replacement of endogenous RB with the constitutively active RB (i.e., RB∆CDK) were done in cells transduced with pINDUCER11 (Addgene, Cat. # 44363) or pINDUCER20 (Addgene, Cat. # 44012) constructs as previously described14,16. Doxycycline induction was performed by treating cells with doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. # D9891) at 0.5 μg/mL concentration for 48 h. G1 synchronization was performed via contact inhibition through maintaining cells for 6 d in a confluent state. Cells were subsequently released to S phase via trypsinization. For mitotic synchronization in prometaphase, cells were synchronized to G1 by contact inhibition through maintaining cells in a confluent state for 6 days. Cells were subsequently released to S phase by trypsinization and were transferred to T175 (large) flasks to sub-confluent state. After 16 h, cells were treated with 0.1 µg/mL nocodazole (Cell signaling, Cat. #2190) for 6 h or with 5 µM kinesin Eg5 inhibitor (Eg5i) S-Trityl-L-Cysteine (PMID: 16507573) (Tocris, Cat. #2191) for 24 h. Mitotic cells were collected by gentle shaking and tapping of the flask. RB1 knockout cell lines were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 and were previously described21.

Transfections

pINDUCER11 (Addgene, Cat. # 44363, RRID: Addgene_44363), pINDUCER20 (Addgene, Cat. # 44012, RRID: Addgene_44012), pLenti-CMVtight-Puro-DEST (Addgene, Cat. # 26430, RRID: Addgene_26430), and pLenti-CMVtight-eGFP-Puro (Addgene, Cat. # 26431, RRID: Addgene_26431) constructs were packaged in HEK293T cells by transient transfection. Envelope plasmid pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene, Cat. # 8454, RRID: Addgene_8454) and packaging plasmid pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr (Addgene, Cat. # 8455, RRID: Addgene_8455) were utilized for transient transfection. Transfections were carried out using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. # 6365787001). All cell lines were infected with lentiviral particles in the presence of 5 μg/ml polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. # 107689). Stable cell lines were selected for expression of the green fluorescence protein by fluorescence-activated cell sorting or for resistance to specific antibiotics: pINDUCER20, 400 μg/ml G-418 (Gibco, Cat. # 10131035); and pLenti-CMVtight-Puro-DEST, 6 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. # P9620).

Cell cycle analysis

The cell cycle was analyzed for hTERT-RPE1 cells in various conditions. Cells were incubated with 20 μM 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU; Life technologies, Cat. # A10044) for 2 h, fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde in PBS for 15 min, blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, and stained with ClickIT reaction (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 3 mM CuSO4, 50 mM Ascorbic Acid, and 2.5 μM Alexa Fluor-647 azide; Life Technologies, Cat. # A10277) for 30 min and 3 μM 4’,6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole Dihydrochloride (DAPI; Life Technologies, Cat. # D1306) in staining buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% NP-40) for 15 min. Flow cytometry experiments were performed with LSR II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and were analyzed using the FCS Express software (v7.18.0025).

Heterochromatin dispersion analysis

Coverslips with adhered hTERT-RPE1 cells were washed with PBS and fixed by sequentially incubating in 70, 85, and 100% ethanol for 2 min each. Coverslips were dried, and probe mix containing FISH probe for chromosome 7 α-satellite (Cytocell, Cat. # LPE007G) and hybridization buffer B (Cytocell, Cat. # H1000L) was added. Coverslips were affixed to slides and sealed using rubber cement. Slides were denatured at 75 °C and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. Coverslips were removed and washed in 0.25× SSC at 72 °C and 2× SSC (with 0.05% Tween-20) for 2 min each. Coverslips were mounted using Vectashield (with DAPI) and sealed. A Zeiss LSM 710 Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope and Zeiss Zen software were used to acquire FISH images. Images were captured using parameters for each probe that remained consistent across samples and experiments. Images were acquired at room temperature, and a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.40 Oil DIC M27 (Zeiss, Cat. # 420782-9900-000) was used to capture Z-stacks (6–12 sections of 0.5-µM thickness). FITC/Alexa Fluor 488 filter was used to acquire images for chromosome 7 α-satellite. Maximum projections of Z-stacks were used for image analysis. No other image processing was performed. Images were analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ) software. To effectively quantify the dispersion phenotype, we used a skeleton dot length plugin, and macros were made to execute the steps as previously described (14). The following macro steps were written and executed in FIJI: open();setOption(“BlackBackground”, false); run(“Make Binary”); run(“Dilate”); run(“Dilate”); run(“Dilate”); run(“Skeletonize”); run(“Analyze Skeleton (2D/3D)”, “prune=none calculate show display”). Cutoffs of skeleton dot length were used to define the foci categories as compact, diffused, and dispersed: compact, <0.75 µm; diffused, 0.75–1.25 µm; and dispersed, >1.25 µm.

Cell detachment rate analysis

For the cell detachment experiment, hTERT-RPE1 cells were seeded on 24-well plates and synchronized to G1 in confluent culture for 6 days. Cells were collected after treatment with 0.25% trypsin (Corning, Cat. # 25-053-CI) for 1 to 6 min, and the number of cells was assessed using the TC20 automated cell counter (Bio-Rad). Brightfield images of the cells that remained attached on the plate surface after trypsinization were obtained using the Diaphot 300 inverted microscope (Nikon), 4x objective lens.

Wound healing assay

hTERT-RPE1 cells were seeded on 6-well plates and synchronized to G1 in confluent culture for 6 days. On the sixth day of contact inhibition, wounds were generated in a straight line using a 1 mm pipette tip. After scratch, the cell monolayer was washed with PBS to remove detached cells and replenished with fresh reduced DMEM media supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, antibiotics (i.e., penicillin, 100 units/mL; streptomycin, 100 μg/mL) and 1% fetal bovine serum. Serum starvation at 1% fetal bovine serum was performed to prevent cells from proliferating, and its concentration was determined by assessing the minimum concentration of serum that prevents cells from dying. At 0.5% and 0.05% fetal bovine serum, we observed that most cells died due to the lack of sufficient serum. The images were acquired immediately following media replacement (t = 0 h) and after 2 days (t = 48 h) using an inverted microscope (Nikon, Diaphot 300), 4x objective lens. A Bandpass filter was applied to the images (Process > FFT > Bandpass Filter Image, large structure equal to 40 pixels, small structure equal to 3.0 pixels, tolerance = 5%).

Wound healing image analysis

Wound areas were assessed using ImageJ (v1.54 d). Briefly, images were first converted to 8-bit (Image > Type > 8-bit) and the threshold was reset (Image > Adjust > Threshold > Reset). A Bandpass filter was applied to the images (Process > FFT > Bandpass Filter Image, large structure equal to 40 pixels, small structure equal to 3.0 pixels, tolerance = 5%) and the threshold was adjusted to 85% (Image > Adjust > Threshold). An additional filter of minimum radius equal to 5.0 pixels was used (Process > Filters > Minimum > Radius). The area was selected using the wand tool and measured in pixels (Analyze > Measure).

Micro-C

Micro-C experiments were performed using the Micro-C kit (10X wash buffer, NWB solution, chromatin capture beads, streptavidin beads, 20% SDS (Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate), crosslink reversal buffer, MNase enzyme mix, 10X nuclease digest buffer, 100 mM MgCl2, 0.5 M EGTA, end polishing buffer, end polishing enzyme mix, intra-aggregate ligation buffer, intra-aggregate ligation enzyme mix, bridge, 5X bridge ligation buffer, bridge ligase, proteinase K, 250 mM DTT, and HotStart PCR Ready Mix; Dovetail Genomics, Cat. # 21006) via Dovetail Genomics’ Micro-C service (Cat. # 20020). In short, frozen hTERT-RPE1 cells were resuspended in 1X PBS, crosslinked with crosslinked using 3 mM disuccinimidyl glutarate (DSG; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. # A35392) and 1% formaldehyde. MNase reaction was performed using 0.5 μL of MNase enzyme mix in a 100 μL solution of 1X nuclease digest buffer and was stopped by the addition of 5 μL of 0.5 M EGTA. Digested chromatin was solubilized by supplementing with 3 μL of 20% SDS and was captured by chromatin capture beads. End polishing was performed using the end polishing enzyme mix at the end polishing buffer, and crosslinks were reversed by 1X crosslink reversal buffer supplemented with proteinase K. DNA was purified using SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. # B23317). The sequencing library was prepared using the Dovetail Library Module for Illumina (end repair enzyme mix, end repair enzyme buffer, ligation enhancer, ligation enzyme mix, adapter for Illumina, and USER enzyme mix; Dovetail Genomics, Cat. # DG-LIB-001). End repair enzyme mix and end repair buffer supplemented with 12 mM DTT were utilized for end repair, which was followed by adapter ligation and USER digest. 2.5 μL adapter for Illumina, 30 μL ligation enzyme mix, and 1 μL ligation enhancer were used for the 33.5 μL reaction mixture. DNA was then purified with SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. # B23317), ligation captured via streptavidin beads, and underwent Index PCR using the HotStart PCR ready mix. Size selection was performed using SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. # B23317). The final libraries were run on the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina) in technical quadruplicates on a paired-end mode. On average, 1.3 billion reads were sequenced per sample to achieve nucleosome resolution.

Bioinformatics processing for Micro-C

Library quality control was performed on the first 2 million reads via the get_gc.py code provided by Dovetail Genomics (https://github.com/dovetail-genomics/Micro-C). Sequencing reads were aligned to the hg38 human reference build using bwa mem (v0.7.17-r1198-dirty). Pairtools (v0.3.0) were utilized to parse, sort, and deduplicate ligation sequencing reads to valid pairs. On average, 80.0% of sequencing reads were mapped as valid pairs, 13.3% were single-sided, and 6.5% were unmapped. Juicer Tools pre (v1.22.0) was utilized to generate.hic files for the chromosome conformation capture maps at 5 kb resolution. JuiceBox (v1.11.08) was utilized to visualize chromosome conformation capture maps in the.hic format. For.hic files, the aggregate peak analysis was performed using the APA (v1.22.0) algorithm. Most of the subsequent analyzes were performed after converting the.hic files to.cool and.mcool formats using hic2cool (v0.5.1) at 10 kb resolution. The.cool files were matrix balanced using cooler balance (v0.9.2). For analyzes that do not require full resolution,.cool files were coarsened using cooler coarsen (v0.9.2) to 1 Mb resolution and were matrix balanced using cooler balance (v0.9.2). Expected number of cis contacts was quantified using the cooltools expected_cis function (v0.5.2) and was utilized for log2 observed/expected visualization. The visualization of.cool files were performed using cooltools pileup (v0.5.2) and coolpup.py (v1.0.0). For the visualization of the chromosome conformation capture map simultaneously with other sequencing tracks, we utilized pyGenomeTracks (v3.8). The.hic files were converted to.h5 format using HiCExplorer hicConvertFormat (v2.2.1.1) for usage. Normalization methods utilized in this study were as follows:.hic files, Knight-Ruiz matrix balancing;.cool and.mcool files, iterative correction and eigenvector decomposition (ICE); and.h5 files, Knight-Ruiz matrix balancing.

Contact probability

Contact probability, also known as P(s) (22, 24), was measured using cooltools (v0.5.2) at 10 kb resolution. Smoothed aggregate curve was produced using cooltools expected_cis (v0.5.2) and its first derivative was assessed using NumPy gradient (v1.22.3). The visualization of the curves was performed using loglog and semilogx functions of Matplotlib (v3.4.2).

Nuclear compartment analysis

The visualization of the Pearson correlation map was performed using JuiceBox (v1.11.08). Nuclear compartment analysis was performed by quantifying the eigenvector of the chromosome conformation capture matrix using cooltools eigs_cis (v0.5.2). Eigenvector tracks were then exported to bigWig via Bioframe to_bigwig (v0.3.3). The assignment of compartments A/B was performed by comparing the eigenvector track to the ATAC-seq data for hTERT-RPE1 cell lines generated in this study. Saddle plots, generated from cooltools saddle (v0.5.2), were utilized to assess the distribution of compartments and interactions between compartments.

Quantification of insulation score

The insulation score (30-33) was measured from.mcool files using cooltools insulation (v0.5.2) at 10 kb resolution using four different window sizes: 30 kb, 50 kb, 100 kb, and 250 kb. For subsequent analyzes, insulation scores measured from the window size of 100 kb were utilized. Insulation scores were then filtered for good bins via the “is_bad_bin” column of the insulation table and were subsequently exported to bigWig via Bioframe to_bigwig (v0.3.3). The assessment of average insulation score at a region was assessed by deeptools multiBigwigSummary (v3.5.1).

Analyses of cohesin-dependent loops, topologically associating domains, and architectural stripes

The HiCCUPS algorithm (v1.22.0) was utilized to identify chromatin loops from chromosome conformation capture maps, and the HiCCUPS Diff algorithm (v1.22.0) was utilized to identify differential loop formation in G1-synchronized RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells. For the assessment of TADs, the insulation table acquired from.mcool files through cooltools insulation (v0.5.2) were utilized. From the insulation table, good bins and strong boundaries were filtered by “is_bad_bin” and “is_boundary_100kb” columns, respectively, and were utilized as TAD boundaries. For visualization, TADs with sizes from 300 to 400 kb were aggregated using cooltools (v0.5.2). The architectural stripes were identified by the Stripenn algorithm (v1.1.65.15) on.mcool files at 10 kb resolution with default parameters.

3D reconstruction of nuclear organization

3D reconstruction of nuclear organization from chromosome conformation capture maps was performed using the PGS (Population-based Genome Structure) algorithm (v0.0.1). The TADs from cooltools insulation (v0.5.2) were utilized, and the PGS algorithm simulated 100 structures at a 1 Mb resolution. The following values of theta were utilized for modeling: 1, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.02, and 0.01. The nucleus radius were simulated as 5 μm, and the average location of each TAD beads were assessed by taking the average of 100 structures that were generated. For visualization,.pqr files were generated using the PGS hms_to_pdb.py (v0.0.1) function, and a PyMOL (v2.5.2) code for each file was generated to alter the radius of each TAD beads. Mesh visualization was used to create figures, and the structures were ray-traced using POV-Ray (v3.7.0.msvc10).

HiChIP

The HiChIP MNase library was prepared using the Dovetail HiChIP MNase Kit for mammalian cells (Cat. # 21007), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 5 × 106 frozen hTERT-RPE1 cells were used and chromatin was crosslinked with 0.3 M disuccinimidyl glutarate (DSG; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. # A35392) and 1% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. # F8775). The cross-linked chromatin was digested in situ with micrococcal nuclease. The MNase reaction was performed using 0.5 μL of MNase enzyme mix in a 100 μL solution of 1X nuclease digest buffer and was stopped by the addition of 10 μL of 0.5 M EGTA (Dovetail Mico-C Module Box 2, Cat. # DG-NUC-001). The cross-linked, digested chromatin was then extracted upon cell lysis with 1X RIPA and solubilized with 1 μL of 20% SDS (Dovetail Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Core Box 1, Cat. # DG-NUC-001). Chromatin fragments from 1000 ng of lysates were incubated with 300 ng of antibody against H3K27ac (Cell Signaling, Cat. # 8173) overnight for chromatin immunoprecipitation. The antibody-protein-DNA complex was pulled down with protein A/G-coated beads (Dovetail Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Core Box 1, Cat. # DG-NUC-001). Next, the chromatin ends were repaired and ligated to a biotinylated bridge adapter followed by proximity ligation of adapter-containing ends as described by the manufacturer (Dovetail Mico-C Module Box 2, Cat. # DG-NUC-001). After proximity ligation, the crosslinks were reversed, and the associated proteins were degraded by 1X crosslink reversal buffer supplemented with proteinase K (Dovetail Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Core Box 1 and Dovetail Mico-C Module Box 2, Cat. # DG-NUC-001). DNA was purified using SPRI select beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. # B23317), and then converted into a sequencing library using Illumina-compatible adapters (Dovetail Dual Index Primer Set #1 for Illumina, Cat. # DG-PRS-002 and Dovetail Library Module for Illumina, Cat. # DG-LIB-001). Biotin-containing fragments were isolated using streptavidin beads prior to PCR amplification (Dovetail Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Core Box 1 and Dovetail Mico-C Module Box 2, Cat. # DG-NUC-001). Size selection was performed using SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. # B23317). The libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) platform to generate 300 million read pairs with length of 150 bp.

Bioinformatics processing for HiChIP

Adapter trimming was performed using trim-galore in a paired-end mode to remove Illumina adapter sequences. Sequencing reads were aligned to the hg38 human reference build using bwa mem (v0.7.17-r1188). Pairtools (v0.3.0) were utilized to parse, sort, and deduplicate ligation sequencing reads to valid pairs. Juicer Tools pre (v1.22.01) was utilized to generate.hic files for the chromosome conformation capture maps. JuiceBox (v1.11.08) was utilized to visualize chromosome conformation capture maps in the.hic format. For.hic files, the aggregate peak analysis was performed using the APA (v1.22.01) algorithm. Most of the subsequent analyzes were performed after converting the.hic files to.cool and.mcool formats using hic2cool (v0.5.1) at 100 kb resolution. The.cool files were matrix balanced using cooler balance (v0.9.2). Expected number of cis contacts was quantified using the cooltools expected_cis function (v0.5.2) and was utilized for log2 observed/expected visualization. The visualization of.cool files were performed using cooltools pileup (v0.5.2) and coolpup.py (v1.0.0). For the visualization of the chromosome conformation capture map simultaneously with other sequencing tracks, we utilized pyGenomeTracks (v3.8). The.hic files were converted to.h5 format using HiCExplorer hicConvertFormat (v2.2.1.1) for usage. Normalization methods utilized in this study were as follows:.hic files, Knight-Ruiz matrix balancing;.cool and.mcool files, iterative correction and eigenvector decomposition (ICE); and.h5 files, Knight-Ruiz matrix balancing. H3K27ac HiChIP peaks were called using FitHiChIP (v11.0) using valid pairs. The peak file for H3K27ac ChIP-seq was prepared using MACS (v2.2.7.1) using default parameters. The following parameters for FitHiChIP were utilized: BINSIZE, 100 kb; LowDistThr, 200 kb; UppDistThr, 10 Mb; UseP2PBackgrnd, 0; BiasType, 1; MergeInt, 1; and QVALUE, 0.01. To assign each H3K27ac HiChIP peaks as enhancer-enhancer, enhancer-promoter, and promoter-promoter interactions, we assessed ATAC-seq peaks and ChIP-seq peaks for H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K27ac, and H3K27me3 modifications and identified enhancers and promoters for each bin. For each H3K27ac HiChIP peak, we measured the number of enhancer-enhancer, enhancer-promoter, and promoter-promoter pairs, and we assessed the maximum number of pairs using a custom MATLAB (vR2023a) code. The code is available at our GitHub repository (https://github.com/hanjunlee21/Lee-et-al-2023).

ChIP-seq

ChIP assays were carried out on hTERT-RPE1 cell cultures using approximately 5 million cells per sample and per epitope. Cells were washed once with PBS and fixed with 1.5 mM ethylene glycol bis(succinimidyl succinate) (Thermo Scientific, Cat. # 21565) for 30 min at 25 °C and 1% formaldehyde (Sigma, Cat. # F8775) for 10 min at 37 °C. 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 150 mM glycine were used to quench cross-linking. Cell pellets were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and lysates were prepared using 300 μL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 1% SDS, and 0.25% DOC) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors cocktail (PPI) (Thermo Scientific, Cat. # 78442) for 10 min on ice. Cell lysates were diluted with 900 μL ChIP dilution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 0.1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, and 1.84% Triton-X) supplemented with PPI. Chromatin was fragmented to a size of 200–700 bases with Branson 250 sonifier. Solubilized chromatin was further diluted with 1800 μL ChIP dilution buffer and immunoprecipitated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Anti-DYKDDDDK Tag (Cell Signaling, Cat. # 14793) was used for FLAG-RB ChIP, and for other proteins, their primary antibodies listed in the Antibodies subheadings were utilized for ChIP. Antibody-chromatin complexes were pulled down with protein G-Dynabeads (Life Technologies, Cat. # 10004D) for 2 h at 4 °C and washed 3 times with RIPA 150 Wash Buffer (0.1% SDS, 0.1% DOC 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), and 150 mM NaCl), 3 times with RIPA 500 Wash Buffer (0.1% SDS, 0.1% DOC, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), and 500 mM NaCl), 3 times with LiCl wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 0.5% DOC) and once with ice-cold 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8). Chromatin complexes were eluted from the beads using elution buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT) for 1 h at 65 °C and were treated with RNase A (Roche, Cat. # 11119915001) for 30 min at 37 °C. Proteinase K (Thermo Scientific, Cat. # 25530049) treatment and reverse cross-linking was performed for 3 h at 65 °C. After reversal of the crosslinks, immunoprecipitated DNA was extracted with AMP Pure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. # A63881). ChIP DNA was quantified with Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat. # Q32851). 1–2 ng ChIP DNA samples were used to prepare sequencing libraries, using the Ovation Ultralow System V2 kit (TECAN, Cat. # 0344NB-A01). ChIP DNA and input controls were sequenced using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit v2.5 (75 Cycles) (Illumina, Cat. # 20024906) with the NextSeq 500 sequencer (Illumina) or with the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina). For spike-in controls, DNA fragments from genomic DNA of TOP10 strain of E. coli (Invitrogen, Cat. # C404010) were added to the samples before library preparation. 5% of spike-in DNA was added compared to the samples to be sequenced.

ChIP- quantitative PCR

Anti-SMC3 (Abcam, Cat. # ab9263) ChIP assays were carried out on hTERT-RPE1 cell cultures synchronized in pro-metaphase using approximately 5 million cells per immunoprecipitation, as described in the ChIP-seq method. 0.1–0.2 ng ChIP DNA samples were analyzed, along with input DNA, using the Roche LightCycler 480 system, as described before (PMID: 35019938). ChIP real-time PCR primers that were used; CHR9_SMC3_peak_F: 5′- GACAGGGAGTTGGGGTATGAAA-3′, CHR9_SMC3_peak_R: 5′- CATTAAGAACAACGGGGCTGAC-3′, CHR9_non-target_F: 5′- GGTGCTTGTAAAAGGATGCTTAGAT-3′, CHR9_non-target_R: 5′- CTGGCAGGAACAGAAACAGGG-3′. Average scores from three technical replicates were calculated. P-values were calculated with ordinary one-way ANOVA multiple comparisons using GraphPad Prism8 software.

Bioinformatics processing for ChIP-seq

Adapter trimming was performed using trim-galore in a paired-end mode to remove Illumina adapter sequences. Sequencing reads were then aligned using bwa mem (v0.7.17-r1188) either to the hg38 human reference genome build or the chimera build of hg38 human reference genome build and NZ_CP080620.1 E. coli TOP10 strain reference genome build. After the aligned.bam file was sorted and indexed using samtools (v1.10), sambamba (v0.7.1) was utilized to sort, mark duplicate reads, and index.bam files. Then, we filtered reads in the.bam files to produce a file that only contains reads that are not PCR duplicates, with less than or equal to two mismatches, and with mapping quality that is greater than or equal to 30. After indexing the.bam file, deeptools bamCoverage (v3.5.1) was utilized to normalize the number of reads based on counts per million (CPM), and.bigWig files were generated at a 1 bp resolution. Peak calling was performed as previously described (15), using bedGraph2Cluster with the following parameters: fold change, 2; normalization, QNorm; distance method, sqeuclidean; and clustering method, symmetry-collapsed profile and scalar. Alternatively, MACS (v2.2.7.1) was utilized to call peaks. For samples with spike-in controls, spike-in normalization was performed by multiplying the data column in each bedGraph file with a scaling factor. The scaling factor was determined by dividing the number of reads that are aligned to the NZ_CP080620.1 E. coli TOP10 strain reference genome build by the total number of aligned reads and further multiplying the value by 1,000,000 and rounding it. For the assessment of repetitive sequences, sequencing reads were realigned to the hg38 human reference genome build via bowtie (v2.3.5.1) and were analyzed using RepEnrich (v2) using the Python programming language (v2.7.18). The fold change upon RB loss was assessed using the read counts calculated from RBWT and RBKO samples in hTERT-RPTE1 cells, and their z-scores were measured. Statistical significance was measured using the z-score, and the p-values were utilized to generate the Manhattan plot. For the identification of cohesin gain and cohesin loss gene set, a custom MATLAB code was utilized. The code is available at our GitHub repository (https://github.com/hanjunlee21/Lee-et-al-2023). In brief, the list of promoters from Ensembl (v105) and the list of enhancers from histone modification-based clustering analysis of H3K27ac ChIP-seq peaks were assessed. Promoters that were non-RB-bound and non-E2F1-bound and those that were within 60 kb from the nearest enhancer were utilized.

Motif analysis

Motif analysis was performed using the HOMER (v4.11.1) algorithm. Absolute motif analysis was performed using HOMER findMotifsGenome.pl (v4.11.1) without a preset background and a size parameter given by the peak file. Differential motif analysis was performed using HOMER fnidMotifsGenome.pl (v4.11.1) using a different peak file as a background and a size parameter that is given by the peak file.

ATAC-seq

hTERT-RPE1 cells were synchronized to G1 by contact inhibition for 6 days and subsequently released to S phase via trypsinization. Cells were harvested and counted using an automated cell counter (Bio-Rad, TC20). The viability of the cells was assessed at 95% of the total number of cells after staining them with trypan blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #15250061). For each reaction, 100,000 cells were collected and washed with PBS (Phosphate-Buffered Saline). The ATAC-seq was performed using an ATAC-seq kit (Active Motif, Cat. # 53150) as described by the manufacturer. Briefly, cells were resuspended in cold lysis buffer provided by the ATAC-seq kit (Active Motif, Cat. # 53150) and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The nuclei pellet was resuspended in the tagmentation buffer provided by the ATAC-seq kit (Active Motif Cat. # 53150) and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Tagmented DNA was purified and used to generate sequencing libraries following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced to a minimum depth of 25 million paired end reads with an NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina).

Bioinformatics processing for ATAC-seq

Validation of RB1 mutation

For the validation of RB1 mutations, RBWT and RBKO hTERT-RPE1 cells were harvested from culture, and approximately 5 million cells per sample were utilized. After the chromatin was isolated from cells, the chromatin was digested with micrococcal nuclease. 1–2 ng MNase-digested DNA samples were used to prepare sequencing libraries, using the Ovation Ultralow System V2 kit (TECAN, Cat. # 0344NB-A01) and were subsequently sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) using the S4 flow cell. Then, we filtered reads in the.bam files to produce a file that only contains reads that are not PCR duplicates, with less than or equal to two mismatches, and with mapping quality that is greater than or equal to 30.

RNA-seq

hTERT-RPE1 cells after 6 d of contact inhibition or trypsinization were used for RNA-seq experiments. The RNA-seq experiment for each experimental condition was performed in biological triplicates. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep Plus kit (Zymo Research, Cat. # R2073). Multiplexed RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq library prep kit (Illumina, Cat. # RS-122-2301/2), which utilizes Oligo-dT beads to capture polyA tails. Paired-end 150 bp reads were collected by the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina).

Bioinformatics processing for RNA-seq

Adapter trimming was performed using trim-galore in a paired-end mode to remove Illumina adapter sequences. Sequencing reads were then aligned using STAR (v2.7.1a) to the hg38 human reference genome build. A two-pass alignment pipeline was utilized, where the first pass was performed using the sjdbOverhang of 149 and the sjdbGTFfile from Ensembl (v105) and the second pass was performed using the SJ.out.tab files generated from the first pass alignment. Separately, STAR-Fusion (v2.7.1a) was utilized to identify gene fusions using the RNA-seq dataset. The.bam files generated by the STAR aligner were sorted and indexed via samtools (v1.10) and were subsequently merged via samtools merge (v1.10) to create a single file per experimental condition. Deeptools bamCoverage (v3.5.1) was then utilized to normalize the number of reads based on CPM, and.bigWig files were generated at a 1 bp resolution. The calculation of the expression level, on the other hand, was performed on individual replicate experiments using the RSEM (v1.3.1) algorithm in a paired-end mode. After RSEM calculation, the read count table was exported to R programming language (v4.3.0), and DESeq2 (v1.40.2) was utilized to assess the differentially expressed genes. k-means clustering of the RNA-seq expression profiles generated from this pipeline was utilized using a custom MATLAB algorithm on the transcripts per million values calculated from RSEM (v1.3.1). The custom code is available at our GitHub repository [https://github.com/hanjunlee21/Lee-et-al-2023]. In brief, the k-means clustering was performed using a variation of the kmeans function of MATLAB (vR2023a) and identified five major categories of transcriptional regulation in our experiments. The gene set enrichment and gene ontology analyzes were performed using Enrichr and were done by providing the gene symbols generated from analyzes, including the cohesin gain and cohesin loss gene sets as well as the five transcriptional regulation categories.

Single-cell RNA-seq

RBWT and RBKO RPE1 cells arrested in G1 phase by 6 days of contact inhibition were collected for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq). Cells were washed twice with RPMI containing 10% heat-inactivated FCS, filtered through a 50 μm filter (Sysmex, Cat#04-004-2327), followed by cell concentration and viability assessment using trypan blue with a Bright-Line hemocytometer (Cat#1492), with both samples reaching 95% cell viability. Gene expression libraries were constructed using the 10× Genomics GEM-X Universal 3′ Gene Expression v4 reagent kits (1000691, 1000690, and 1000215) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Quality control was assessed for both cDNA and gene-expression libraries using Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Kit (Invitrogen, Cat#Q32854) and the Agilent High Sensitivity Bioanalyzer DNA Kit (Cat#5067-4626). Both samples were sequenced on a single lane of an Illumina NovaSeq X 25B flow-cell to yield a total of ~2 billion reads, using the following read configurations: Read 1- 28 cycles; Read 2- 90 cycles, and dual barcodes with 10 cycles each (i7 and i5 primers).

Proteomics

Cell pellets were processed as previously described74. Briefly, cell pellets were lysed in 500 µL of lysis buffer (75 mM NaCl, 3% SDS, 1 mM NaF, 1 mM beta-glycerophosphate, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 10 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM PMSF and 1X Roche Complete Mini EDTA-free protease inhibitors in 50 mM EPPS, pH 8.5). Proteins were then reduced with 5 mM DTT at 56 °C for 30 min and alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide in the dark at room temperature for 20 min. The reaction was stopped by adding DTT (5 mM final concentration) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Proteins were precipitated by adding one part trichloroacetic acid to four parts protein solution, followed by 10 min of incubation on ice. The precipitated proteins were pelleted by centrifugation (15,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and washed twice with prechilled acetone (−20 °C, 300 µL, 15,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The protein pellets were then resuspended in 1 M urea in 50 mM EPPS, ph8.5 and digested in a two-step process (overnight digestion at room temperature with 1 µg/µL of Lys-C (Wako) followed by 6 h of digestion with trypsin (sequencing grade, Promega) at a final concentration of 1 ng/μL for 6 h at 37 °C). The digest was acidified with 10 % trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and peptides were desalted on C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE) (Sep-Pak, Waters) columns. The concentration of the desalted peptide solutions was measured with a BCA assay, and 25 µg of peptides were aliquoted. The samples were dried under vacuum, and stored at −80 °C. The TMT labeling was performed with 18-plex tandem mass tag (TMT) reagents (Thermo Scientific) as previously described74. The 25 µg of peptide aliquotes were resuspended in 25 µL of 200 mM EPPS (pH 8.5), 30% acetonitrile (ACN). Labeling was performed by adding 70 μg TMT reagent in anhydrous ACN and incubating at room temperature for 1 h. The reaction was stopped by addition of 5% (w/v) hydroxylamine in 200 mM EPPS (pH 8.5) to a final concentration of 0.5% hydroxylamine and incubation at room temperature for 15 min. Samples were acidified with 1% TFA, and samples were combined. The pooled samples were desalted using Sep- Pak C18 SPE cartridges and vacuum dried. The multiplexed samples were fractionated using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) under basic pH, as previously described74, and dried. The dried peptides were resuspended in 5 % ACN/5 % formic acid and analyzed in 3-h runs via reversed phase LC-MS2/MS3 on an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer using the Simultaneous Precursor Selection supported MS3 method75. MS2 spectra were assigned using a SEQUEST-based in-house built proteomics analysis platform using a target-decoy database-based search strategy to assist filtering for a false-discovery rate (FDR) of protein identifications of less than 1 %76. Peptides that matched to more than one protein were assigned to that protein containing the largest number of matched redundant peptide sequences following the law of parsimony. TMT reporter ion intensities were extracted from the MS3 spectra, selecting the most intense ion within a 0.003-m/z window centered at the predicted m/z value for each reporter ion, and spectra were used for quantification if the sum of the S/N values of all reporter ions divided by the number of analyzed channels was ≥10 and the isolation specificity for the precursor ion was ≥0.75. Protein intensities were calculated by summing the TMT reporter ions for all peptides assigned to a protein. The full proteome data was normalized in a two-step process: a row (protein) normalization followed by a column (sample) normalization. The row normalization involved scaling the row intensity values by the global median of all row means divided by each rows mean, adjusting each row mean to equal the global median. Similarly, the column normalization involved scaling the column intensities so that each column median would be equal the average of all sample medians. The proteome profiles were generated from quadruplicate RBWT and RBKO RPE1 cell cultures arrested in G1 phase by 6 days of contact inhibition. The normalized protein intensities were further processed with Perseus software (Version 1.6.15.0)77. Identified proteins were compared for deregulation (FDR-threshold <0.15 and log2 fold change >0.7 or <0.7 between samples) and visualized by using VolcaNoseR package in R-studio.

Statistics & reproducibility

All statistical analysis were performed using the OriginPro (v2023b) software. For comparison of one group against a specific value, two-tailed one-sample Student’s t-test was utilized (Fig. 2c). For comparison of a paired dataset, two-tailed paired Student’s t-test was utilized (Fig. 1f). Comparisons of two groups were performed using a two-tailed two-sample Student’s t-test (Figs. 1g, 2c, d, h and 5b, d, e). To assess the statistical significance of DNA motif enrichment, HOMER78 algorithm’s default two-tailed hypergeometric test, followed by a Benjamini–Hochberg multiple hypotheses correction was utilized. Other enrichment analyzes were performed using a two-tailed Fisher’s exact test. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size, and no data were excluded from the analyzes. The experiment was not applicable for randomization, while differential analysis algorithms for Micro-C, HiChIP, ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, and RNA-seq experiments were blinded to the conditions. The calculated statistical significance is indicated as ∗p-value < 0.05; ∗∗p-value < 0.01; and ∗∗∗p-value < 0.001.
