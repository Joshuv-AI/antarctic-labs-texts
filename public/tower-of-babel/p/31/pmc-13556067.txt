Traditional medicinal knowledge and diversity of medicinal plants in Benin: a systematic review (2014–2023)
Authors: Edna Hounsa, Inés Reigada, Phénix Assogba, Eric Agbodjento, Fréjus Ohouko, Boris Legba, Hornel Koudokpon, Jean Robert Klotoe, Christine Ouinsavi, Víctor López, Leena Hanski, Victorien Dougnon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe richness of flora and traditional medical knowledge in the West African country Benin translate into the integral role of medicinal plants in local healthcare. This study explored the current status of traditional medicine research in Benin by evaluating data from ethnopharmacological and ethnobotanical surveys carried out between 2014 and 2023 and identifying knowledge gaps within the sustainable use of plant-based remedies.MethodsThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol was used to guide the execution of the review. A total of 129 studies identified in scientific databases were analysed. The top 20 cited plant species were subjected to a comprehensive literature review to evaluate the experimental evidence to support the reported traditional uses.ResultsA total of 765 plant species belonging to 141 botanical families were cited in the included studies to treat various animal and human diseases, including infectious diseases, digestive disorders and metabolic diseases. Severe gaps in the documentation of plant collection practices, preparation stability, and preservation techniques were observed. Khaya senegalensis A.Juss. (41 citing studies), Parkia biglobosa (Jacq.) R.Br. ex G. Don (37 citing studies) and Ocimum gratissimum L. (33 citing studies) were the most frequently cited plants. Comparison of the scientific literature on the 20 most cited plant species revealed a lack of experimental evidence, even for the most frequent traditional uses. ConclusionThis study illustrated the wide spectrum of plant species applied in Beninese traditional medicine and the critical lack of experimental evidence for their traditional applications and the conservation of medicinal preparations. These findings underscore the need for sustainable harvesting practices, community-based conservation measures, and evidence-based use of plant resources to preserve biodiversity and support long-term availability.This calls for a national database, quality standards, and better integration of traditional medicine into modern healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05440-0.Keywords: Biodiversity, Medicinal plant, Human disease, Veterinary medicine, Evidence-based phytomedicine, Benin

Background

Despite the immense positive impact of modern medicine on the wellbeing of humankind, traditional medical practices continue to be indispensable for majority of global population. Ethnobotany and ethnopharmacology, which study the relationships between plants and human cultures, are essential to conserve and protect this cultural heritage [1]. These disciplines address the therapeutic applications of plants for the management of both human and animal health. When combined with scientific rigor in safety and efficacy evaluation, this approach can promote inclusive health management, offering accessible and culturally relevant solutions for diverse populations. Additionally, acknowledging these practices can help preserve cultural heritage and create opportunities for developing medicines derived from biodiversity [2]. Gaining insight into centuries-old medicinal plant use by communities harbours potential to enrich modern healthcare, integrating traditional knowledge into a more comprehensive view of health and wellness.

Benin, a West African country bordering Togo, Nigeria, Burkina Faso and Niger, stands out for its rich plant biodiversity. This botanical wealth is the result of a variety of ecosystems, from the lush tropical forests of the south to the vast arid savannahs of the north [3]. Natural resources such as medicinal plants have been used by local communities for several years for a range of uses, such as food, medicine, handicrafts, and spirituality, which support this biological diversity.

Traditional plant-based medicines are acknowledged by the World Health Organization (WHO) as fundamental instruments and means of providing treatments and health promotion to the population. Since 2002, the WHO has been creating plans to promote and advance traditional medicines globally. These plans included the 2002–2005 strategy, “the WHO traditional medicine strategy: 2014–2023,” and a new global strategy for the years 2025–2034, which aim to meet the global needs for traditional, complementary and integrative medicine [4].

In Benin, these areas are of particular importance because the population is strongly dependent on traditional medicine [5, 6]. Approximately 70% of the Beninese population relies on traditional medicine for their primary health needs [7]. Numerous socioeconomic and cultural factors account for this. Herbal medicines are geographically accessible, particularly in rural locations where access to contemporary medical care is often limitted. Additionally, many low-income families find herbal remedies to be appealing alternatives to contemporary care because of their affordability. Finally, traditional medicine is popular throughout the nation, especially in the southern region, where these customs are strongly ingrained, due to the continuation of traditional beliefs and practices that value the use of medicinal herbs [8]. A multitude of ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological studies have been carried out in this country [9, 10]. While extensive ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological data are available, a comprehensive overview of the current scientific knowledge is missing, justifying the focus of this review. Such knowledge would be valuable for guiding biodiversity conservation initiatives and shaping future research projects aimed at advancing evidence-based phytomedicine. The following questions were the focus of our investigation:

What are the top medicinal plants used in Benin, and which diseases they are used to treat?

This study aimed to synthesize data from surveys conducted in Benin between 2014 and 2023 to identify the main medicinal plants used, the diseases they target, and the experimental evidence supporting their traditional uses.

Methods

Study area

The Republic of Benin, a country in West Africa with an estimated 13 million people and a surface area of 114,763 square kilometers, is notable for its diverse geography and culture [3]. Its twelve geographical departments, which range from the southern coastlines (6°N, 2°E) to the northern plateaus and mountains (12°N, 3°E), reflect this diversity. The 77 communes that make up these departments are further subdivided into 546 arrondissements and approximately 5300 villages and quartiers. Beninese villages are scattered around the nation and are frequently anchored in their customs and distinct lifestyles. These villages form the social fabric, where ancestral practices, influenced by the geographical environment and local natural resources, are preserved. Benin harbours a variety of ecosystems, from coastal mangroves and tropical forests to wood savannahs [11]. This ecological diversity, with its plant and animal biodiversity, enables local populations to exploit natural resources to meet their needs [12]. A multitude of medicinal plants are used by the population for their therapeutic virtues, food, handicrafts or for spiritual reasons. Benin is characterized by several ethnic and linguistic groups (the Fon and Yoruba groups, for example) [13].

Benin is divided into three main phytochorological zones, each characterized by a distinct vegetation type (Fig. 1) [3]. The Guinean‒Congolese zone is located in the southern part of the country and is dominated by tropical rainforests. This zone includes the communes of the Atlantic, Ouémé, Mono, Couffo, Littoral and Plateau departments [10]. The Sudanian zone, located in northern Benin, covers the communes of the Alibori, Borgou, Atacora, and Donga departments. This region is distinguished by its savannah landscapes, which contrast with the forests of the south. Finally, the Sudano–Guinean zone is a mixed zone located in the central part of Benin, including the communes of the Collines and Zou departments.

Bibliographic search strategy

This study was conducted in accordance with the “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses” (PRISMA) guidelines, 2020 [14–16]. Only articles focusing on ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological studies conducted in Benin and published between 2014 and 2023 were considered in this review. These data were collected from several databases, such as Google Scholar, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Science Direct. The specific search terms used included medicinal plants, traditional medicine, ethnobotany, ethnomedicine, ethnopharmacology, traditional use of medicinal plants, treatment of diseases by plants in Benin, traditional knowledge of medicinal plants, traditional herbal remedies in Benin, therapeutic uses of plants, indigenous knowledge of medicinal plants and diseases, ethno-medicinal knowledge, endogenous knowledge, herbal recipes, ethno-botanical characteristics, medicinal use, medicinal practices, traditional knowledge, local plant use practices, ethno-medicinal inventory, ethno-botanical practices, ethno-medicinal value, ehnomedicinal application, ethnomedicinal evaluation and comparison of herbal medicine. The data were collected from January 1, 2024, to June 30, 2024.

Inclusion criteria

The articles were selected based on several criteria. They had to have been published between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2023. Only articles written in English or French, the main languages of scientific communication in the country, were included. This linguistic restriction ensured rigorous interpretation of data and access to reliable sources. Studies have also reported on ethnobotanical or ethnopharmacological surveys conducted in Benin, with particular emphasis on medicinal plants used for the treatment of human or animal diseases. In addition, they had to provide explicit information on traditional uses of medicinal plants, including recipes, preparation methods, therapeutic indications and associated cultural elements. Finally, only articles containing complete data and accessible full-text were included, meaning they provided sufficient detail to allow for the extraction, comparison, and synthesis of data across studies.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included articles written in a language other than English or French. Systematic reviews, narrative reviews, scoping reviews or meta-analyses were excluded to avoid double counting of data that had already been synthesized. Similarly, studies solely on experimental bioactivity, chemical characterization or clinical trials without an ethnobotanical or ethnopharmacological component were excluded, just like articles that are not available in full text or lack the minimum information required for data extraction. Finally, non-indexed theses, dissertations or reports were not included due to the lack of peer review, which limits the assurance of methodological rigor and data reliability.

Collection and creation of a database

The variables have been prioritized around the most relevant elements for ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological analysis. The focus was on essential data such as plant parts used, preparation methods, therapeutic uses and formulation and conservation methods. The characteristics of the participants and the context of the survey were also limited to the information necessary for interpretation, including the category of informants (herbalists, traditional healers, etc.), sample size, geographical and phytochorological area, as well as the experience of the practitioners. Finally, only additional information directly affecting the quality and validity of ethnobotanical data, such as harvest seasons, justification of plant combinations and harvesting methods, were retained. .

Five reviewers were involved in the selection and data collection process. The reviewers initially screened the studies independently on the basis of their titles and abstracts and then compared their results. Studies were moved to full-text review if both reviewers agreed that they should be retained. In cases where the two reviewers disagreed on whether a study should be included or excluded, the article is systematically re-evaluated during a consensus meeting. A lead evaluator was consulted when consensus could not be reached immediately. Disagreements were therefore resolved through discussion until a full agreement was reached. No intra-rater reliability coefficient (Cohen’s kappa) was calculated (Supplementary file 1, Fig. S1). A total of 160 articles (Supplementary file 2) were retrieved from the databases. Eleven were deleted because of duplication. Seventeen studies were excluded on the basis of the inclusion/exclusion criteria after assessment of the abstracts, and an additional three were removed because of insufficient information content. As a result, 129 studies were included in the systematic review (Fig. 2).

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel 2010 was used to analyse the collected data. Variables related to each parameter were expressed as percentages. The scientific names of the plants listed were verified at World Flora Online (www.worldfloraonline.org) or Medicinal Plant Names Services (http://mpns.kew.org). The recorded diseases were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) [17]. A Pearson chi-square (χ²) test was used to examine the independence between the variables studied. Specifically, we assessed whether the distribution of ICD-10 disease categories, and the plant species differed according to ethnic origin and phytochorological zone. Associations were considered statistically significant on the basis of p values (5%).

Results

Trends and main features of ethnobotanical research in Benin from 2014 to 2023

Table S1.1 (Supplementary file 1) presents an overview of the 129 research articles included in this study. Publications were evenly distributed between English (49.6%) and French (50.4%). During the 2014–2023 period, the annual number of publications ranged from 6 to 18 per year (Fig. 3). The surveys covered all three phytochorological zones of Benin (Fig. 3). Of the 129 included studies, 65 surveys targeted the Guineo‒Congolese zone. A total of 23 studies covered the whole country, combining data from the Sudanian, Sudano-Guinean, and Guineo-Congolese zones, and 15 studies covered the Sudano-Guinean and Guineo-Congolese zones (Fig. 4).

Ethnic groups involved in the studies

The ethnobotanical studies conducted in Benin between 2014 and 2023 involved various ethnic groups. Among those most studied, Fon (66.7% of the studies) was included in the Fon and related groups. In the Adja and related groups, Adja was represented in 45% of the studies. In Yoruba and related groups, Yoruba was included in 43.4% of the studies. Bariba and related groups accounted for 17.1% of the studies. Dendi represented 15.5% of the studies within Dendi and related groups. For the Peulh and related groups, 14.7% of the studies involved the Peulh. The least represented groups were Ani (7%) for Yoa and related, and Waama (6.2%) for Betamaribé and related (Supplementary file 1, Table S1.2).

No statistically significant differences were detected in the specificity of plant uses according to ethnic origin for disease categories (ICD-10, p = 0.175), the number of plants used (p = 1.000), or the plant species (p = 0.828).

Socio-professional groups targeted in the studies

The ethnobotanical studies conducted from 2014 to 2023 in Benin involved various target socio-professional groups. The general population was included in 51.2% of the studies. Herbalists were involved in 34.1% of the studies, traditional healers in 31%, breeders in 17.8%, and market gardeners in 0.8% (Fig. 5). Traditional healers are practitioners who receive patients in their homes or offices and conduct consultations before providing herbal treatment, which may or may not involve spiritual practices. Herbalists sell medicinal plants in markets and offer remedies without prior consultation. The studies employed two main data collection methods: semi-structured interviews (SSI) and purchasing of medicinal recipes in triplicate (PMRT). The purchase of medicinal recipes in triplicate (PRMT) is an ethnobotanical survey method designed to capture the variability and reliability of medicinal plant use in traditional practice. The method involves visiting the same market herbalist three consecutive times at two-week intervals to purchase remedies for the treatment of a single disease. During these visits, the purchased recipes are classified according to the number of medicinal plants they contain: a large number (GN) on the first visit, a reduced number (NR) on the second visit, and a very reduced number (TR) on the third visit [18].

Distribution of target groups of ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological surveys conducted between 2014 and 2023

The rationale for including PRMT in this study is its ability to assess consistency in plant selection for a given ailment and to identify plants that are commonly used versus those used less frequently, thus providing a measure of cultural consensus. This method has previously been applied in ethnobotanical studies and is considered valid for analysing intra-herbalist variation and the decision-making process of traditional practitioners.

However, PRMT has limitations. These results may be influenced by the seasonal availability of plants, the stock of the herbalist, and temporary changes in local practices. Moreover, this method captures only the remedies offered by a single herbalist at a given time, which may not fully represent the diversity of practices within the community. Despite these limitations, PRMT provides a structured approach to quantify and compare medicinal recipes, thereby improving the reliability and reproducibility of ethnobotanical data collected in markets.

Disease categories covered by the surveys

The analysed studies cover both human and animal diseases. Research on human diseases (75.2%) has dominated, but there has also been a notable focus on animal diseases (16.3%). On the other hand, 4.6% of the studies targeted both human and animal diseases, whereas 3.9% of the studies did not specifically target any disease (for example, the general uses of plants). Studies cover a variety of diseases, including infectious diseases, digestive disorders, metabolic diseases and spiritual diseases. There is a strong focus on infectious diseases such as diarrhea, malaria, and general infections, which are common in the region (Table S1.1).

The human diseases identified in ethnobotanical studies cover a wide range of pathologies classified according to the ICD-10 classification. There is a preponderance of infectious and parasitic diseases, accounting for 46.6% of the cases studied. Digestive system diseases (18.4%), endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases (16.5%) and genitourinary system diseases (14.6%) followed in terms of the frequency of citations. However, a subsection of the studies could not be assigned to a specific ICD-10 category, accounting for 10.7% of the cases (Table 1). The typical infectious and parasitic diseases cited in the surveys include diarrhea [19], malaria [20–24], infectious diarrhea [25], general infections, candidiasis [18], hepatitis [26] and COVID-19 [27, 28]. The endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases cited in the analysed surveys include type 2 diabetes [26, 29, 30], hypertension (HBP) [8, 28], malnutrition [9] and hemorrhoids [29, 31]. Maternal health is also addressed by Towns and van Andel [32], as are reproductive system problems such as sexual weakness and male infertility [33, 34]. Finally, dermatological diseases and skin infections such as scabies were also included in the surveys [35].

With respect to animal diseases, infectious and parasitic diseases are the most frequently mentioned, accounting for 63.0% of cases. Endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases were the next most represented (18.5%), followed by diseases of the digestive system (11.1%). Nervous system diseases (3.7%) and tumors (3.7%) were among those with low frequencies. A total of 11.1% of the diseases listed were not determined (for example, spiritual illness) (Table 1). Studies focusing on specific infectious and parasitic diseases in veterinary medicine include foot-and-mouth disease and Pasteurellosis [36, 37] and animal parasitic diseases such as coccidia, whipworms and strongyles [38]. Insect pests that cause animal and human diseases such as trypanosomiasis should be controlled [39]. Endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases include studies on increasing milk production in local cows [40]. Some studies (4.6%) have focused on undefined subjects, such as plants used in veterinary and human medicine [41], spiritual illnesses [42, 43], and ethnobotanical knowledge and practices in biodiversity management [44, 45].

The 129 included studies were also categorized on the basis of the number of plant species cited. Most studies focused on either a single plant (21.9%) or more than 100 plants (27.2%), with the majority examining fewer than 20 plants (42.1%) (Fig. 6).

Methods for collecting medicinal plants

Data on plant collection methods were found in only one of the 129 studies (0.8%) (Supplementary file 2). This study reported the use of various harvesting techniques, ranging from hand-picking leaves to the use of machetes or cutlasses to cut branches, bark, trunks, and even uprooted roots for certain species.

Plant parts and preparation methods

Leaves were the most widely used plant organ (92.2%), followed by roots (55.8%). Other plant parts were used less frequently (Fig. 7). Among the preparation methods, decoction was the most commonly used method (89.1%), followed by maceration (69%) (Fig. 8).

Table S1.3 (Supplementary file 1) presents a comprehensive overview of the various medicinal plant species cited in the studies for their use in treating human and animal diseases. A total of 765 medicinal plant species belonging to 141 botanical families were cited in the 129 articles included in this study. The most frequently cited botanical families are Fabaceae (12%), Apocynaceae (4.7%), Euphorbiaceae (4.4%), Rubiaceae (4%) and Asteraceae (3.9%). These plants have multiple medicinal uses. Overview of in vitro and in vivo studies on the 20 medicinal plants and their traditional uses

These top-cited plant species were subjected to further literature mining to explore the current scientific evidence on their traditional uses (Table 2). For several widely used medicinal plants, the literature contains very few experimental studies. This was the case for species such as Khaya senegalensis, Nauclea latifolia and Azadirachta indica, for which few studies addressing their reported uses in snakebite management were identified. For Newbouldia laevis, no experimental studies corresponding to the reported traditional uses for paralysis were found. Furthermore, some uses of Moringa oleifera and Bridelia ferruginea reported in the included ethnopharmacological studies have not been accompanied by in vitro or in vivo experimentation.

Our analysis also revealed a lack of biomedical studies on conditions involving treatment with Mangifera indica, including enuresis. Very few scientific data have been obtained for Acanthospermum hispidium, particularly in relation to veterinary applications. For Adansonia digitata, some reported traditional uses, such as oral applications, were not documented in the available scientific literature. No experimental studies have investigated the reported use of Crateva adansonii and Guilandina major for reproductive health, malaria, gastrointestinal disorders or childhood illnesses.

Discussion

General trends in ethnobotanical research in Benin

The purpose of this study was to conduct a systematic review of ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological studies in Benin between 2014 and 2023 to summarize the present state of research on this subject and to guide future research efforts. The bilingual nature of the publications, in English and French, not only facilitates the local dissemination of knowledge but also strengthens the integration of Beninese research within international scientific networks. This bilingual approach highlights the commitment of researchers to ensure the accessibility and recognition of their findings at an international level, in line with trends observed in ethnopharmacology, where knowledge sharing is essential for cross-cultural validation and collaborative research.

The temporal distribution of publications suggests sustained, and at times intensified, research activity, which may reflect periods of increased funding, the establishment of international partnerships, or the initiation of targeted research programmes (Fig. 3). Some studies focusing on the potential of certain medicinal plants in combating COVID-19 between 2020 and 2023 [27, 126, 127] illustrate the capacity of Beninese ethnobotanical research to respond to global health challenges. These works demonstrate not only the mobilization of the scientific community in response to urgent public health priorities but also the continued relevance of traditional knowledge for addressing contemporary infectious diseases.

In a broader context, these trends reflect patterns observed in other sub-Saharan African countries, where ethnobotanical research has established a link between traditional knowledge and modern pharmacological investigations [15, 16]. The observed growth and thematic responsiveness of research in Benin indicate a maturing scientific field that is attentive to local health needs while engaging with international research imperatives, thereby reinforcing the strategic role of ethnopharmacology in sustainable health development and biodiversity conservation.

Geographical coverage and cultural diversity of traditional knowledge

The analysis of ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological studies conducted in Benin highlights uneven geographical coverage across different phytochorological zones [3]. The predominance of research in the Guineo‒Congolese zone (Fig. 4) reflects the emphasis placed on its exceptional biodiversity and the density of local populations possessing extensive traditional knowledge of medicinal plant use. This zone, which includes six of Benin’s twelve departments (Atlantique, Littoral, Ouémé, Mono, Couffo, and Plateau), accounts for 42% (4625/10999) of registered traditional medicine practitioners in Benin [128]. and houses approximately half (6,515,000) of the country’s population (13,035,000). This concentration underscores the central role of this zone in the intergenerational transmission of local knowledge and in the preservation of indigenous healing practices, highlighting the significant contribution of traditional practitioners to community health.

The lower representation of other zones, such as the Sudano-Guinean zone or the combined Sudanian and Sudano-Guinean zones, suggests that certain regions of the country, potentially rich in traditional knowledge and plants with high therapeutic potential, remain underexplored [3, 5]. This observation emphasizes the importance of directing future research towards these understudied areas to gain a more comprehensive understanding of ethnobotanical practices and to fully valorize local resources.

The diversity of the ethnic groups involved in these studies illustrates the cultural richness of traditional knowledge in Benin. The predominance of Fon and related groups, followed by Adja and Yoruba (Table S1.2), indicates that these communities serve as major reservoirs of medicinal knowledge, offering opportunities for new therapeutic discoveries [9, 18, 34]. Moreover, the inclusion of less-studied groups, such as Bariba, Dendi, Yoa, Bétamaribé, and Peulh, reveals latent ethnobotanical knowledge that is still poorly documented, which could enrich the national database of traditional medical practices.

Ethnic origin does not influence the specificity of plant uses for different disease categories, the number of plants used, or the plant species involved. This lack of variation indicates that, despite cultural diversity, traditional knowledge of medicinal plants is widely shared among the communities studied. This highlights the transversal dissemination and resilience of traditional knowledge, reinforcing the relevance of a national and inclusive approach to documenting and valorizing medicinal plants. Strategies for preserving and promoting this heritage should therefore not be limited to specific communities but should target a broader spectrum to ensure the representativeness and sustainability of traditional practices.

The involvement of different types of informants in the general population for common uses, herbalists for the transmission and preservation of knowledge, and moderately solicited healers for specialized applications illustrates the hierarchical organization of traditional knowledge. This diversity of actors confirms the strong reliance on traditional knowledge for multiple applications and emphasizes the importance of its protection and promotion within sustainable development frameworks and public health strategies in Benin.

Human and animal health issues and the therapeutic importance of plants

The ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological research conducted in Benin highlights the central role of medicinal plants in addressing both human and animal health challenges. In humans, the focus on infectious diseases, such as diarrhea, malaria, and other general infections (Table S1.1), emphasizes the significance of these conditions as major public health concerns. Benin is heavily affected by both communicable and noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), the latter accounting for 39% of deaths in 2019 [129]. Among the 129 articles included in this study, 38 specifically reported on malaria management, documenting the use of 222 different plant species. Malaria is a major cause of mortality (in children under five) and morbidity (in adults) in Benin. It accounts for 40% of outpatient consultations and 25% of hospitalizations [130]. Benin is one of the fifteen countries with the highest number of malaria cases and deaths, accounting for 2.1% of all malaria cases and 1.8% of malaria-related deaths worldwide in 2022 [131]. Benin represents 4% of malaria cases in West Africa [131]. Furthermore, digestive, endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases are also extensively covered, with research focused on conditions such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and malnutrition. In Benin, hypertension is a significant health issue requiring continuous monitoring and preventive measures. According to the study of Desormais et al. [132], the prevalence of hypertension was 32.9%, which was similar in men (32.8%) and women (33.0%) in 2015. Arterial hypertension (HBP) is a common condition in both medical consultations (1.5%) and hospitalizations (1.3%). It is one of the main risk factors for cardiovascular diseases [133]. Type 2 diabetes remains a major health issue for the population of Benin [134]. The diversity of conditions studied reflects local health needs and highlights the importance of traditional plant remedies.

In terms of animal health, surveys contribute to improving prevention and treatment strategies for infectious and parasitic diseases in animals, enhancing animal health and agricultural yields [36, 38, 135, 136]. They also emphasize sustainable farming practices, such as environmentally friendly pest management. In dairy production, interventions focused on nutrition and metabolic management of local cows optimize milk output. Finally, cultural and spiritual aspects are considered, recognizing their impact on the management of animal and human health and influencing healthcare practices.

Harvesting practices, sustainability, and conservation challenges

The techniques or methods of plant collection were very rarely described in the studies analysed (Supplementary file 2). Papageorgiou et al. [137] support the findings reported in this study regarding the existence of multiple harvesting techniques, ranging from hand-picking leaves to using machetes or cutlasses to cut branches, bark, or trunks, but they emphasize that sustainable collection requires a much more structured approach. According to these authors, harvesting should be guided by an annual plan specifying the timing, sites, and plant species to be collected. Prior to any harvest, a preliminary visit to the collection sites is recommended to ensure that the plants are at the optimal stage of development. They reported that even in the case of manual harvesting, collectors must ensure that only the necessary parts are removed without uprooting the entire plant. This approach contrasts sharply with some of the practices observed in the reviewed studies, highlighting the need to promote collection methods that support the regeneration and long-term sustainability of plant populations. Other studies have also recognized the relevance of proper medicinal plant collection practices [138, 139]. The European Medicines Agency [140] recommends that particular attention should be given to the collection practices of medicinal plants to preserve the survival of wild species and the balance of the ecosystems on which they depend. Responsible collection not only ensures the sustainable availability of resources but also guarantees the optimal quality of raw materials, as the concentration of active compounds varies according to the season, growth stage and plant part harvested.

Standardizing collection practices could increase the consistency of the plants used in phytotherapy. Mentioned techniques, such as the use of machetes or cutlasses to cut branches, bark, and trunks, as well as uprooting for roots, have been suggested to have significant implications for the sustainability of plant resources [15]. Indeed, collection methods such as cutting with a machete and uprooting can lead to overexploitation and degradation of medicinal plant populations, jeopardizing their sustainability. Picking leaves and gathering fruits are considered less invasive methods, but their ecological impacts are not well documented, as noted by other authors [141, 142].

Harvesting roots or bark has been suggested to have more severe negative impacts on plants and ecosystems compared to collecting leaves [143]. Extensive collection of roots and bark could endanger certain species, highlighting the importance of conservation strategies and sustainable management of plant resources [143]. It is necessary to gather scientific evidence through the evaluation of the pharmacological properties of roots and bark to limit the use of these plant organs to evidence-based applications, disseminate information and discourage the use of roots and bark for applications for which scientific data do not provide evidence of efficacy.

In light of these findings, it could be beneficial to develop and implement conservation strategies tailored to the harvested plant parts and the ecosystems involved. Possibilities to prioritizing the use of leaves over roots or bark could be explored to minimize the impact on plant survival However, it is crucial to keep in mind the known differences in the phytochemistry and pharmacology of different plant organs in order not to promote use of leaves over other plant organs without either established traditional knowledge or experimental evidence. Crop rotation should be encouraged to allow plant populations to regenerate naturally and prevent resource depletion. Establishing community-based management plans involving traditional practitioners, local associations, and forestry authorities would also be advantageous for ensuring the sustainable and shared use of medicinal plants.

Raising awareness and training traditional practitioners in sustainable harm techniques can help modify overly destructive practices and preserve biodiversity. In parallel, rigorous and systematic documentation of local knowledge, including methods of collection, preparation and preservation, could serve as a basis for developing effective public policies aimed at protecting endangered species and promoting sustainable pharmacopoeia.

Preliminary confirmation, methodological limitations, and future research needs

The collection of scientific data on the 20 top-cited plants (Table 2) highlights both preliminary evidence and challenges. For example, some studies have documented the biological properties of these plants in relation to their traditional uses. Extracts of Khaya senegalensis A.Juss. have antimicrobial, antioxidant, anticancer, antiplasmodial, and antiparasitic effects [52, 54, 55, 144–146], whereas the seeds and bark of Parkia biglobosa (Jacq.) R.Br. ex G.Don have demonstrated antihyperglycemic, antioxidant, antiparasitic, and antihyperlipidemic activities [147]. The antimicrobial, antidiabetic, and anti-inflammatory properties of Ocimum gratissimum L. have also been documented in vitro and in vivo [148].

These data support the use of these plants in the traditional treatment of various human and animal diseases reported in this study.

However, several plants widely used in traditional medicine have not been subjected to even preliminary scientific studies. Not because of a lack of pharmacological interest but probably due to methodological, structural and epistemological constraints. Traditional practices are often integrated into cultural, symbolic or ritual contexts that are difficult to reproduce in a standardized experimental setting. For example, the use of Khaya senegalensis, Nauclea latifolia Sm., and Azaradirachta indica A.Juss. in the prevention or management of snake bites is based on local culture which includes spiritual, prophylactic and behavioral dimensions. These aspects cannot be easily translated into experimental models, which mainly focus on direct antivenom activity.

Similarly, the applications of Newbouldia laevis (P.Beauv.) Seem. ex Bureau, in the management of paralysis suffer from the lack of neurobiological models adapted to the syndromes described by traditional healers. Biomedical classifications (central and peripheral paralysis, neuropathies, etc.) do not always correspond to local nosological categories, which complicates translation to experimental research.

Some uses reflect ritual, symbolic or psychological functions such as those associated with Moringa oleifera L. or cultural syndromes associated with Bridellia ferruginea Benth. These dimensions largely escape classical biomedical approaches, which focus on measurable physiological mechanisms. Their evaluation would require interdisciplinary approaches that combine anthropology, psychology, pharmacology and social sciences.

The lack of scientific studies on the use of Mangifera indica L. for enuresis is partly due to the difficulty of studying this condition experimentally. Establishing relevant animal models that accurately replicate human enuresis is complex and requires sophisticated physiological or hormonal manipulations. Moreover, as enuresis is often considered a benign condition, it is not a research priority, which limits funding and scientific interest.

Furthermore, some plants, such as Acanthospermum hispidium DC., remain largely unstudied due to a lack of local infrastructure dedicated to evaluating remedies in veterinary phytotherapy, a field that is still underdeveloped in Benin. This structural deficit limits the capacity to generate rigorous experimental data.

Some well-documented plants, such as Adansonia digitata L., have not yet been explored for certain specific uses (oral applications, for example), primarily owing to a lack of originality and appropriate protocols in existing research programs.

However, the reviewed studies do not account for crucial aspects, such as methods of conservation of formulations, reasons that support the combination of plant or non-plant ingredients, the storage time of preparations and factors influencing the stability of medicinal formulations. This situation can be explained by the fact that ethnobotanical studies generally focus on species inventories and their traditional uses. This explains why the technical aspects of preparation, storage and stability of formulations are often not reported. The complexity of recipes and the confidentiality of traditional knowledge also lead practitioners not to disclose certain information regarding combinations of ingredients or shelf-lives. These gaps limit an overall understanding of the effectiveness and sustainability of ethnobotanical practices.

This work highlights recommendations for future policies and research in traditional medicine in Benin. The creation of a national database of medicinal plants, their uses, and scientific evidences will be essential to favour the integration of traditional medicines in the healthcare system of Benin. It is very important to focus on underexplored regions to document untapped knowledge. Conservation and sustainable exploitation strategies are crucial for preventing resource depletion. The quality standards process and certification ensure the safety and efficacy of plant-based products. Comprehensive and in-depth in vivo and clinical studies are necessary for the successful integration of this natural medicine.

Conclusion

This systematic review of ethnobotanical and ethnopharmacological studies conducted in Benin from 2014 to 2023 provides a comprehensive overview of the evolution and contributions of this field. This study recommends creating a national database; promoting sustainable use; strengthening quality standards; and bridging research, regulation, and clinical application to better integrate traditional medicine into modern healthcare in Benin.
