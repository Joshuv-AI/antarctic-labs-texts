Predicting nurses’ behavioral intention to adopt artificial intelligence in a resource-limited setting: an extended technology acceptance model study in Iran
Authors: Somaye Sohrabi, Hossein Bonakchi, Rahman Kazemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into healthcare systems has the potential to substantially enhance clinical decision-making, workflow efficiency, and quality of patient care. However, the successful implementation of AI technologies largely depends on their acceptance by frontline healthcare providers, particularly nurses. While the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) has been widely used to explain technology adoption, evidence remains limited regarding the role of nurses’ cognitive and attitudinal factors in resource-limited settings.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in 2025 among nurses working in hospitals affiliated with Ilam University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Using a census-based approach, 340 nurses were invited to participate, of whom 199 completed a validated online questionnaire. Data were collected using Davis’s Technology Acceptance Model scales (Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use) alongside an adapted instrument measuring knowledge, attitude, behavioral intention, and practical use of AI. Non-parametric tests and multiple linear regression analysis were performed using SPSS version 26.ResultsMost participants demonstrated low levels of AI-related knowledge (70.4%) and relatively unfavorable attitudes toward AI (66.8%). Beyond technological considerations, fostering positive attitudes and foundational AI literacy among nurses is essential for successful implementation. Background

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly reshaping healthcare delivery by enabling advanced data analysis, clinical decision support, predictive modeling, and workflow optimization. Applications of AI range from diagnostic imaging and risk prediction to electronic documentation and personalized care planning, offering substantial opportunities to improve patient outcomes and system efficiency [1, 2]. Despite these advancements, the real-world impact of AI in healthcare remains contingent upon its acceptance and effective use by healthcare professionals.

Nurses constitute the largest segment of the healthcare workforce and play a central role in patient care, clinical coordination, and the daily operation of health information systems. As primary end-users of many AI-enabled tools, nurses’ perceptions, beliefs, and readiness significantly influence whether such technologies are successfully adopted or ultimately underutilized [3, 4]. Evidence suggests that even highly sophisticated technologies may fail if they are perceived as burdensome, disruptive, or misaligned with clinical workflows [5].

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), originally proposed by Davis, remains one of the most influential theoretical frameworks for explaining user adoption of new technologies [6]. TAM posits that Perceived Usefulness (PU)—the belief that a technology enhances job performance—and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU)—the belief that using the technology requires minimal effort—are the primary determinants of an individual’s intention to use a system. Although TAM has been validated across various healthcare technologies, its explanatory power may be limited when applied in isolation, particularly in complex clinical environments [7].

In healthcare settings, technology adoption extends beyond functional attributes to encompass cognitive and affective dimensions. Nurses’ knowledge of AI concepts and applications shapes their understanding of its capabilities and limitations, while their attitudes reflect broader concerns related to professional roles, ethical implications, patient safety, and trust in automated systems [8]. Studies conducted in high-income countries indicate that healthcare professionals often express positive attitudes toward AI while simultaneously reporting insufficient knowledge and limited practical experience, which can hinder adoption [9].

These challenges may be further amplified in resource-limited settings, where infrastructural constraints, limited access to training, and unequal digital exposure can exacerbate resistance to technological change [10]. Iran, as a middle-income country with heterogeneous healthcare infrastructure, provides a valuable context for examining these dynamics. Existing Iranian studies have primarily focused on physicians or healthcare workers in major metropolitan areas, leaving nurses in less-developed regions underrepresented in the literature [11, 12].

Evidence from comparable middle-income countries reinforces the relevance of this context. Recent cross-sectional studies conducted in neighbouring and structurally similar settings have documented strikingly consistent patterns: moderate-to-low AI knowledge, cautious but not entirely negative attitudes, and a dominant role of attitude over technical perceptions in driving adoption intention among nurses [13]. These cross-national parallels suggest that the barriers to AI adoption in nursing are not unique to Iran but reflect systemic challenges common to healthcare systems in middle-income countries navigating digital transformation under resource constraints. Situating the present study within this regional discourse is therefore essential for understanding the generalisability of our findings and for informing regionally applicable policy responses.

Ilam province, located in western Iran, is characterized by limited technological infrastructure, fewer educational opportunities in emerging digital health technologies, and high clinical workloads. To date, no systematic study has examined AI acceptance among nurses in this setting. Understanding how perceived usefulness, ease of use, knowledge, and attitudes interact to influence nurses’ behavioral intention to adopt AI is essential for designing effective educational strategies and implementation policies.

Accordingly, this study aimed to develop and test an extended Technology Acceptance Model by incorporating nurses’ cognitive (knowledge) and attitudinal factors to predict behavioral intention to adopt AI among nurses in a resource-limited province of Iran. By addressing this gap, the study seeks to generate evidence-based insights to support nurse education, organizational readiness, and equitable digital health transformation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in 2025 across all public hospitals and healthcare centers affiliated with Ilam University of Medical Sciences, located in Ilam province, western Iran. Ilam is considered a resource-limited region in terms of healthcare infrastructure and access to advanced digital health technologies.

Participants and sampling

The target population consisted of all registered nurses actively working in clinical settings within hospitals affiliated with Ilam University of Medical Sciences. A census-based sampling approach was attempted. Based on official records obtained from the university’s Deputy of Treatment, 340 eligible nurses were identified and invited to participate.

Inclusion criteria were:

Being a registered nurse,

Being a registered nurse,

Current employment in a clinical unit, and.

Willingness to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria included incomplete questionnaires with more than 10% missing data.

Of the 340 nurses invited, 199 completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 58.5%, which is acceptable for online surveys conducted among healthcare professionals [14].

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire distributed via professional nursing communication channels. The questionnaire comprised three main sections:

1. Demographic characteristics

This section collected data on age, gender, education level, years of clinical experience, and prior participation in any AI-related training or educational programs.

2. Technology acceptance model (TAM) constructs

Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) were measured using the validated Technology Acceptance Model scale developed by Davis [6]. The instrument consisted of 12 items:

6 items assessing PU (e.g., “Using AI would enhance my job performance”),

6 items assessing PU (e.g., “Using AI would enhance my job performance”),

6 items assessing PEOU (e.g., “Learning to use AI would be easy for me”).

6 items assessing PEOU (e.g., “Learning to use AI would be easy for me”).

Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicated greater perceived usefulness or ease of use.

3. Knowledge, attitude, behavioral intention, and practical use of AI

An adapted and validated questionnaire originally developed by Hamedani et al. was used to assess nurses’ AI-related knowledge, attitude, behavioral intention, and practical use [11].

Knowledge was assessed using 8 multiple-choice items covering basic AI concepts and healthcare applications. For descriptive purposes, scores were categorized into low, moderate, and high levels based on predefined cut-off points. Specifically, scores below 13 were classified as low, scores between 14 and 19 as moderate, and scores of 20 or above as high. These cut-off points were pre-specified based on the original validated instrument and were not derived from the study data. For inferential analyses, knowledge scores were treated as continuous variables.

Practical Use of AI was measured using 12 items assessing current or potential use of AI-based tools in routine nursing tasks. In the extended TAM framework applied in this study, Practical Use of AI was positioned as a potential independent predictor of behavioral intention, based on the theoretical rationale that prior or concurrent experiential exposure to AI tools can shape adoption readiness—a mechanism supported by extensions of the original TAM, including TAM2 and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). Content validity of the instrument was confirmed by a panel of five experts in medical education, nursing informatics, and biostatistics. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, median, and interquartile range) were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As most variables were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to examine associations between TAM constructs, knowledge, attitude, behavioral intention, and practical use.

It is important to clarify that the application of non-parametric tests for bivariate comparisons and the subsequent use of multiple linear regression are not contradictory. Non-parametric tests were appropriate for individual variable comparisons because the raw distributions of knowledge, attitude, and related scores were non-normal. Multiple linear regression, however, does not require normality of the raw independent or dependent variables; rather, it requires that the model residuals be approximately normally distributed. Prior to conducting the regression analysis, all standard assumptions were systematically verified: (a) the dependent variable (behavioral intention) was measured on a continuous composite scale (28 items, range 28–140); (b) normality of residuals was confirmed through normal probability plots (P-P plots) of standardized residuals, which showed approximately normal distributions; (c) independence of residuals was confirmed by the Durbin-Watson statistic (2.298); (d) homoscedasticity was confirmed via scatterplots of standardized residuals against fitted predicted values; (e) absence of influential outliers was verified (all |standardized residuals| < 3.0); and (f) acceptable multicollinearity was confirmed for all predictors (VIF < 5). Given that all assumptions were met, multiple linear regression was retained as the appropriate and justified analytical method.

The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to compare outcome variables across demographic groups.

To identify predictors of behavioral intention to adopt AI, a multiple linear regression analysis using the Enter method was conducted with perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, knowledge, and attitude as independent variables. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values below 5 considered acceptable.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.SBMU.SME.REC.1404.036). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Participant characteristics

Out of 340 eligible nurses invited to participate, 199 completed the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 58.5%. The majority of participants were aged between 35 and 44 years (40.7%), held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (76.4%), and reported more than 10 years of clinical experience. With respect to the primary outcome, behavioral intention to adopt AI, more than half of the nurses (57.3%) reported a moderate level of intention to use AI-based technologies in clinical practice. Correlations between TAM constructs and AI-related variables

Similarly, perceived ease of use (PEOU) was positively correlated with attitude (ρ = 0.184, p < 0.01), behavioral intention (ρ = 0.264, p < 0.001), and practical use (ρ = 0.385, p < 0.001).

Associations between demographic variables and AI acceptance

Comparisons across demographic variables showed that gender was significantly associated with behavioral intention to adopt AI. Male nurses reported significantly higher behavioral intention scores compared to female nurses (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.002).

No statistically significant differences were observed in knowledge, attitude, behavioral intention, or practical use of AI across age groups, education levels, years of clinical experience, or prior participation in AI-related training programs (all p > 0.05).

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of behavioral intention to adopt AI. The regression model was statistically significant (F(5,193) = 17.63, p < 0.001), accounting for 26.6% of the variance in behavioral intention (R² = 0.266; Adjusted R² = 0.247). The Durbin-Watson statistic (2.298) confirmed independence of residuals. Discussion

This study examined the acceptance of artificial intelligence among nurses in a resource-limited province of Iran using an extended Technology Acceptance Model. The findings provide important insights into how technological perceptions, cognitive readiness, and attitudinal factors jointly shape nurses’ behavioral intention to adopt AI in clinical practice.

Overall interpretation of findings

The results revealed that although nurses generally recognized the potential of AI, their foundational knowledge and practical use remained limited. More than two-thirds of participants demonstrated low AI-related knowledge, and nearly half reported minimal practical engagement with AI tools. These findings underscore a critical gap between the growing presence of AI in healthcare discourse and its actual integration into nursing practice, particularly in under-resourced settings.

Despite these limitations, the extended TAM explained 26.6% of the variance in behavioral intention to adopt AI. This level of explanatory power is comparable to, and in some cases higher than, similar technology acceptance studies conducted in healthcare contexts, where reported R² values typically range between 20% and 30% [15, 16]. These patterns are consistent with emerging evidence from comparable middle-income settings. A recent cross-sectional study from a neighbouring regional context found that nurses similarly demonstrated moderate behavioral intention alongside limited AI knowledge, with attitude emerging as the dominant predictor—reinforcing the cross-national robustness of our findings [13]. The alignment between our results and this regional evidence suggests that the barriers to AI adoption identified in Ilam province are not idiosyncratic but represent a broader challenge facing nursing workforces in healthcare systems that are digitally aspirational but infrastructurally constrained.

Role of attitude as the strongest predictor

Attitude toward AI emerged as the strongest predictor of nurses’ behavioral intention. This finding aligns with behavioral theories emphasizing the central role of affective and evaluative responses in shaping intention and behavior. While TAM traditionally prioritizes perceived usefulness and ease of use, our results indicate that nurses’ broader perceptions of AI—encompassing trust, perceived threats to professional roles, and ethical considerations—may exert a stronger influence on adoption decisions.

Similar findings have been reported in studies from both high-income and middle-income countries. Longoni et al. demonstrated that resistance to medical AI is often driven by emotional and identity-related concerns rather than purely technical evaluations [17]. Likewise, a systematic review among European nurses found that positive attitudes were more predictive of AI acceptance than prior exposure or technical familiarity [18].

A critical but underexplored dimension of nurses’ attitudes toward AI is its deep entanglement with core professional values—particularly therapeutic communication and the humanistic foundations of nursing care. Unlike many other professions, nursing is constitutively relational: the therapeutic relationship between nurse and patient, built on empathy, active listening, and compassionate presence, is not merely an instrument of care but its very substance. Nurses may therefore perceive AI not simply as a technical tool to be evaluated for efficiency, but as a potential disruptor of the humanistic essence of their practice. This professional identity threat may explain why attitude operates as a more powerful determinant of adoption intention than even perceived usefulness or ease of use. Erkayıran (2025) demonstrated that when AI-based simulation tools are deliberately integrated into nursing education with an emphasis on therapeutic communication competencies, they can actively enhance rather than erode these skills [20]. This finding carries profound implications for our results: if nurses are exposed to AI in educational contexts that centre humanistic nursing values, their attitudes may shift substantially toward greater openness to adoption. Intervention strategies that reframe AI as a partner in therapeutic care—rather than a substitute for human connection—may therefore be particularly effective in shifting the attitudinal barriers documented in this study.

Organisational and leadership factors also play a critical role in shaping nurses’ attitudes toward AI. Tarsuslu et al. (2025) demonstrated in a sample of clinical nurses that digital leadership behaviours by nurse managers were significantly associated with reduced AI anxiety and more positive AI attitudes, operating through mechanisms of trust-building and institutional modelling [21]. This evidence is directly relevant to the Ilam context: in resource-limited settings where nurses have limited access to AI training and where digital leadership may be underdeveloped, AI anxiety may be particularly prevalent. Investments in developing digitally-oriented nursing leadership may therefore represent one of the most efficient organisational levers for improving AI adoption readiness.

Complementing this perspective, Agaoglu et al. (2025) demonstrated that transformational leadership style and perception of AI use serially mediate the relationship between employee happiness and innovative work behaviour among nurses [22]. This finding extends our understanding of attitude formation beyond individual cognition: nurses’ perceptions of AI—and by extension their behavioral intention to adopt it—are not formed in isolation but are embedded within an organisational climate shaped by leadership quality, institutional culture, and affective wellbeing. The predominantly moderate-to-unfavorable attitudes observed in our sample may therefore partly reflect organisational conditions rather than fixed individual dispositions, suggesting that leadership-level interventions could produce meaningful attitudinal change.

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use

Consistent with the core propositions of the Technology Acceptance Model, both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use were significant predictors of behavioral intention. Nurses who believed that AI could enhance job performance and reduce workload were more likely to express willingness to adopt AI-based technologies. This reinforces the enduring relevance of TAM in healthcare settings, even when applied to complex and emerging technologies such as AI.

Notably, perceived ease of use demonstrated a slightly stronger predictive effect than perceived usefulness. In high-workload clinical environments, particularly in resource-limited settings, technologies perceived as complex or time-consuming may be viewed as additional burdens rather than supports. This finding echoes previous research indicating that usability concerns are a major barrier to digital health adoption among nurses [5]. Accordingly, AI systems designed for nursing practice must prioritize simplicity, transparency, and seamless integration into existing workflows.

Knowledge and its complex relationship with AI acceptance

Knowledge of AI was a significant but comparatively weaker predictor of behavioral intention. This suggests that while basic AI literacy is necessary, knowledge alone may be insufficient to drive adoption. Interestingly, the observed negative correlation between knowledge and perceived ease of use indicates that nurses with greater awareness of AI may hold more realistic—and potentially less optimistic—views about its complexity.

This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that superficial familiarity with technology can lead to inflated expectations, whereas deeper understanding may increase awareness of limitations, risks, and learning demands [3].

Importantly, the concept of ‘knowledge’ as a predictor of AI adoption may need to be understood more precisely as digital literacy—a broader construct encompassing not only factual awareness but also the skills and confidence to engage with digital technologies meaningfully. Agaoglu et al. (2025) demonstrated that digital literacy mediated the relationship between AI usage and creative thinking in nursing students, and that academic support moderated this mediation [23]. This finding suggests that the modest predictive effect of knowledge in our study may reflect the limitations of a declarative knowledge measure, and that more comprehensive digital literacy development—supported by institutional academic scaffolding—may exert stronger effects on adoption intention. Educational interventions should therefore aim not only to increase knowledge but to develop functional digital literacy within a supportive institutional framework.

Gender differences in AI acceptance

Male nurses reported significantly higher behavioral intention to adopt AI compared to female nurses. While nursing is a predominantly female profession, similar gender differences in technology acceptance have been reported in previous digital health studies [19]. These differences may reflect broader sociocultural patterns in technology exposure, confidence, and self-efficacy rather than inherent differences in capability or interest.

This finding highlights the importance of adopting inclusive and gender-sensitive approaches when designing AI training programs, ensuring equal access to learning opportunities and addressing potential confidence gaps.

Implications for nursing practice, education, and policy

The findings of this study carry several important implications. First, AI literacy should be systematically integrated into undergraduate and continuing nursing education, with content tailored to clinical relevance rather than abstract technical concepts. Second, educational interventions should prioritize attitude formation, addressing ethical concerns, professional identity, and trust in AI as a supportive rather than replacement technology. Third, healthcare organizations and policymakers should ensure that AI implementation strategies are accompanied by institutional support, protected training time, and user-centered system design. At the organisational level, developing digitally-oriented nursing leadership is a priority. Evidence indicates that digital leadership and transformational leadership practices can reduce AI anxiety, foster positive attitudes, and create organisational climates conducive to innovation [21, 22]. In resource-limited settings such as Ilam province, where technological infrastructure is developing and leadership capacity may be uneven, targeted investment in nurse manager digital competencies may yield disproportionate gains in workforce AI readiness. Policymakers should consider including digital leadership development as a core component of AI implementation strategies.

For resource-limited settings, these considerations are particularly critical. Without targeted investment in human readiness, the digital divide may widen, and the potential benefits of AI may remain unrealized in regions that could benefit most from efficiency-enhancing technologies.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of a theory-driven extended TAM framework, inclusion of nurses from an underrepresented resource-limited setting, and application of validated measurement instruments. However, limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reliance on self-reported behavioral intention rather than actual AI use may introduce social desirability bias. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single province, which may limit generalizability. Finally, although the model explained a meaningful proportion of variance, other factors such as organizational culture, leadership support, and technological infrastructure were not assessed and warrant further investigation.

Future research directions

Future studies should employ longitudinal or interventional designs to examine causal pathways and changes in AI acceptance over time. Application of structural equation modeling could further clarify direct and indirect relationships among TAM constructs, knowledge, and attitude. Moreover, qualitative research exploring nurses’ lived experiences with AI could provide deeper insight into contextual and ethical dimensions influencing adoption.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that acceptance of artificial intelligence among nurses in a resource-limited setting is shaped not only by perceptions of usefulness and ease of use but, more importantly, by underlying attitudes and knowledge. Extending the Technology Acceptance Model to incorporate these human factors provides a robust framework for understanding AI adoption in nursing. To ensure equitable and effective integration of AI into healthcare, strategies must prioritize building positive attitudes, foundational AI literacy, and user-friendly systems that align with the realities of nursing practice.
