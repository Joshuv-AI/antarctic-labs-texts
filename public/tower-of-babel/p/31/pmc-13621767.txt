The lncRNA-m6A axis in cancer: a bidirectional regulatory network in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance
Authors: Ziqiang Wang, Tianzi Li, Kun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong non-coding RNAs and N6-methyladenosine RNA methylation represent two pivotal layers of gene regulation. Their extensive crosstalk forms a sophisticated bidirectional network that is fundamentally rewired in cancer.Main BodyThis review synthesizes current knowledge to elucidate the principles and consequences of this synergistic axis. We detail how m6A modification dictates long non-coding RNA stability, splicing, localization, and function through recruitment of distinct “reader” proteins, with one “reader” family primarily mediating decay while another promotes stabilization. Conversely, we examine how long non-coding RNAs act as scaffolds, guides, and decoys to modulate the activity and specificity of the m6A machinery, establishing powerful feedforward and feedback loops. This reciprocal regulation converges on multiple cancer hallmarks, including proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, stemness, and therapeutic resistance. We critically discuss experimental strategies to establish causal relationships, including site-directed mutagenesis, CRISPR-based editing, and rescue assays. We also evaluate current methodological limitations in m6A detection, from antibody-dependent approaches to emerging nanopore sequencing, and highlight how single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies can resolve cell-state-specific networks within the tumor microenvironment. From a translational perspective, we compare small molecule inhibitors targeting m6A “writers” with RNA-based therapies, addressing their respective delivery challenges and toxicity concerns. Finally, we outline how m6A-related long non-coding RNA signatures serve as prognostic biomarkers and liquid biopsy tools for non-invasive cancer monitoring.ConclusionBy integrating molecular mechanisms with clinical perspectives, this review charts a roadmap for targeting the epitranscriptomic-long non-coding RNA circuit in precision oncology.

Background

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), once considered transcriptional “noise,” are now recognized as pivotal regulators of gene expression, influencing virtually every cellular process, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and immune responses [1–4]. Their dysregulation is a hallmark of numerous pathologies [5–12], including cancer [13, 14]. Concurrently, N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant internal chemical modification on eukaryotic messenger RNA (mRNA), has emerged as a central layer of post-transcriptional regulation, dynamically controlling RNA fate [15, 16]. A transformative shift in our understanding has been the discovery of intricate crosstalk between these two systems, in which lncRNAs can be both targets of m6A modification and regulators of the m6A machinery, creating a sophisticated, bidirectional regulatory network [17, 18].

Existing research has begun to delineate this synergy. For instance, m6A modification can directly dictate the lifecycle of lncRNAs, stabilizing transcripts like THOR to promote cancer cell survival or destabilizing others like pncRNA-D to facilitate cell cycle arrest [15, 16]. Conversely, lncRNAs can act as scaffolds, guides, or decoys to modulate the expression or function of m6A “writers”, “erasers”, and “readers”. Studies reveal that lncRNAs such as NEAT1 and XIST engage with methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), and YTH domain-containing 1 (YTHDC1) to influence m6A deposition on critical mRNA targets, thereby steering outcomes in processes from stem cell maintenance to tumor progression and immune evasion [19–22]. This interplay extends beyond cancer to metabolic, inflammatory, and degenerative diseases [23–25], highlighting its fundamental role in cellular homeostasis.

Understanding the physiological state of epitranscriptomic regulation provides essential baseline knowledge for interpreting its pathological rewiring in cancer. In normal tissues, m6A modification and lncRNA expression are tightly regulated to maintain cellular homeostasis. During aging, however, significant dysregulation occurs as RNA methylation patterns shift and lncRNA expression profiles become altered, contributing to physiological decline. For example, in the aging eye, microRNAs (miRNAs) and other non-coding RNAs exhibit age-dependent expression changes that contribute to age-related ocular diseases [26]. This age-related dysregulation creates a permissive landscape where oncogenic insults can more readily drive malignant transformation. miRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that transcriptionally and post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression [27–30] and are frequently dysregulated in cancer, functioning as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors [31, 32]. Their biogenesis and function are regulated by m6A modification, as the “reader” HNRNPA2B1 recognizes m6A marks on primary miRNAs and facilitates their processing by recruiting the microprocessor component DGCR8. Moreover, lncRNAs can act as competing endogenous RNAs that sponge miRNAs, modulating their availability to target mRNAs. Together, the contrast between the tightly controlled homeostatic state and the dysregulated cancer state, along with the intricate interplay among miRNAs, lncRNAs, and m6A modification, underscores the lncRNA-m6A axis as a critical node in tumorigenesis.

In this review, we synthesize current knowledge to present a coherent model of the lncRNA-m6A regulatory axis, moving beyond a catalog of interactions to discuss governing principles. We critically evaluate the functional impact of this synergy on key oncogenic pathways and the tumor microenvironment (TME). Furthermore, we highlight cutting-edge strategies, including small molecules targeting m6A enzymes, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) against oncogenic lncRNAs, and combination therapies, that exploit this axis for cancer intervention. By integrating molecular insights with translational perspectives, this review seeks to chart a roadmap for transforming our understanding of epitranscriptomic-lncRNA crosstalk into novel diagnostic biomarkers and effective therapeutic strategies for cancer.

The m6A modification machinery

The discovery of m6A began with its chemical detection (Fig. 1). In a foundational 1974 study, Desrosiers et al. first identified methylated nucleosides, including m6A, within mRNA from Novikoff hepatoma cells, establishing its existence as a physiological RNA modification [33]. For nearly two decades, the enzymatic machinery responsible for depositing this mark remained elusive. This crucial gap began to close in the 1990s with the pioneering work of Rottman’s group. Bokar et al. first characterized and partially purified an mRNA N6-adenosine methyltransferase complex (MTC) from HeLa cell nuclei, demonstrating its multisubunit nature [34]. Subsequently, they achieved a major breakthrough by purifying and cloning the complementary DNA (cDNA) for the S-adenosylmethionine (AdoMet)-binding subunit of this human methyltransferase, which would later be known as METTL3, thereby identifying the first core “writer” protein [35].

A true paradigm shift occurred in the 2010s with the discovery of reversible RNA methylation, transforming the m6A mark from a static mark into a dynamic “epitranscriptomic” layer akin to DNA and histone modifications. In 2011, fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) was identified as the first m6A “eraser,” revealing its demethylase activity and linking RNA modification to metabolism [36]. Soon after, its homolog ALKBH5 was characterized as another mammalian RNA demethylase [37]. Concurrently, the identification of specific “reader” proteins that selectively recognize the m6A mark unveiled its functional mechanisms. Landmark studies demonstrated that YTH domain-containing proteins like YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 2 (YTHDF2) and YTHDC1 mediated m6A-dependent RNA decay and splicing, respectively [38–40]. Other “readers”, such as heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein C (HNRNPC), heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2/B1 (HNRNPA2B1), and the insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein (IGF2BP) family, were shown to influence alternative splicing of target mRNAs, microRNA (miRNA) processing and enhance mRNA stability and translation [41–44]. This era also saw the elucidation of the core “writer” complex, with METTL3, methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) and Wilms tumor 1-associated protein (WTAP) identified as crucial regulatory subunits and KIAA1429 and methyltransferase-like 16 (METTL16) characterized as methyltransferases for distinct substrates [45–50]. Together, these discoveries established the core triumvirate of “writers” (METTL3/METTL14/WTAP), “erasers” (FTO/ALKBH5), and “readers” (YTHDF, YTHDC, IGF2BP families) that govern the dynamic m6A landscape, setting the stage for exploring its profound role in biology and disease.

m6A-mediated lncRNA stability

The stability of lncRNAs represents a fundamental layer of post-transcriptional regulation, with m6A modification acting as a central switch that governs this process in cancer. The ultimate fate of an m6A-modified lncRNA, whether it is rapidly degraded or markedly stabilized, is dictated by the specific “reader” proteins recruited to the methylation site (Fig. 2). A critical functional distinction exists among “reader” families. YTHDF proteins primarily function in the cytoplasm, where they mediate RNA decay, whereas IGF2BP proteins serve as potent stabilizers. In contrast, YTHDC proteins operate mainly in the nucleus, where they influence splicing and nuclear export.

YTHDF family “readers”

The cytoplasmic YTHDF proteins are key effectors that interpret the m6A code, with each member directing distinct functional outcomes.

YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1), traditionally a translational enhancer, plays a significant role in lncRNA-mediated oncogenic networks. It often stabilizes lncRNAs or facilitates their function within regulatory axes. For example, in colorectal cancer (CRC), YTHDF1 regulates the LINC01106/miR-449b-5p/VEGFA axis to promote angiogenesis [51]. It cooperates with METTL3 to modulate FOXD2-AS1 in esophageal cancer [52] and contributes to the upregulation of CHASERR in glioma [53]. Notably, YTHDF1 is integral to establishing powerful feedforward loops, such as the one between the glycolytic lncRNA SLC2A1-DT and the MYC oncogene in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [54], and it stabilizes oncogenic transcripts like LINC01315 to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [55].

YTHDF2 is the primary driver of m6A modification-mediated RNA degradation, making it a crucial suppressor of tumor-regulatory lncRNAs. It directs numerous tumor-suppressive lncRNAs for decay, including FENDRR in endometrial carcinoma (EC) [56], FGF14-AS2 in breast cancer (BC) [57], and AC026691.1 in gastric cancer (GC) [58]. This pathway can be activated by upstream signals; for instance, TNF-α induces FTO-mediated demethylation and subsequent YTHDF2-dependent degradation of the oncogenic lncRNA THRIL in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [59]. “Writers” like METTL3, METTL14, METTL16, WTAP, and RBM15/B can mark oncogenic lncRNAs such as XIST in CRC [60], NEAT1 in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [61], TIALD in HCC [62], MEG3 in ovarian cancer (OC) [63], and HAR1A in NSCLC [64], while demethylases like ALKBH5 and FTO protect PVT1 in osteosarcoma (OS) [65], LINC00022 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [66], KCNQ1OT1 in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) [67], and LINC00115 in BC [68] by erasing the decay signal.

YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 3 (YTHDF3) often functions cooperatively, integrating m6A signals into complex regulatory circuits. A hallmark example is a reciprocal feedback loop in pancreatic cancer (PC), where YTHDF3 binds m6A-modified DICER1-AS1, which in turn inhibits miR-5586-5p maturation to drive glycolysis [69]. YTHDF3 also intersects with demethylase pathways, as seen with the ALKBH5-demethylated tumor suppressor lncRNA CARMN [70], highlighting its role in fine-tuning lncRNA stability within dynamic networks.

IGF2BP family “readers”

The IGF2BP family (IGF2BP1/2/3) are m6A “readers” renowned for dramatically enhancing RNA stability, frequently stabilizing key oncogenic drivers.

IGF2BP1 stabilizes a wide array of oncogenic lncRNAs. It maintains THAP7-AS1 in concert with METTL3 in GC [71], and stabilizes critical regulators like lnc-CTHCC [72], LINC00106 [73], and RNF144A-AS1 [74] in HCC. It also stabilizes LINC02551 to promote HCC growth [75] and MIR4435-2HG [76] and NRAV [77] to maintain stemness. Its role extends to other cancers, stabilizing LINC00313 in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) [78], CCAT1 in tongue squamous cell carcinoma [79], PDIA3P1 in ESCC [80], H19 in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) [81], and LINC00662 in CRC [82].

IGF2BP2 is a pivotal stabilizer of lncRNAs driving diverse hallmarks. It stabilizes PCAT6 to promote prostate cancer (PCa) bone spread [83], LINC00941 to facilitate PC metastasis [84], DANCR to enhance glycolysis in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [85], LINC00901 to activate the MYC axis in PC [86], MALAT1 to maintain the proliferative signaling in NSCLC [87], and ABHD11-AS1 to inhibit ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death, in CRC [88]. Operating downstream of m6A “writers” like METTL3 and METTL14, which deposit the essential methylation marks [89–92], IGF2BP2 translates epitranscriptomic modifications into stable lncRNA expression, thereby amplifying oncogenic pathways that control proliferation, stemness, and therapy resistance.

IGF2BP3 similarly exerts stabilizing functions. It stabilizes KCNMB2-AS1 to promote cervical cancer (CC) growth by acting as a competing endogenous RNA [93], and it stabilizes OIP5-AS1 to enhance glycolysis and metastasis in GC by inhibiting hnRNPA1 degradation [94]. In PC, IGF2BP3 stabilizes linc-UROD, which in turn stabilizes glycolytic enzymes ENO1 and PKM to strengthen cancer cell proliferation and migration [95].

Nuclear and alternative “readers”

Beyond the canonical cytoplasmic “readers”, other proteins recognize m6A mark in specific contexts to influence lncRNA fate.

The nuclear m6A “readers” YTHDC1 and YTHDC2 play distinct but crucial roles in regulating lncRNA stability and function, primarily influencing nuclear RNA processing and gene regulation. YTHDC1, by recognizing m6A-modified lncRNAs, facilitates their oncogenic activities; for instance, it mediates the m6A modification of CTBP1-AS2 to accelerate CCA progression [96], promotes LINC00294 upregulation in HCC [97], and potentiates LSCC development through MSC-AS1-mediated repression of ATXN7 transcription [98]. Notably, its function can be modulated by metabolic cues, such as histone lactylation driving YTHDC1 to promote HCC progression via NEAT1-mediated lipid metabolism remodeling [99]. Conversely, YTHDC2 is implicated in regulating lncRNAs that influence tumor progression, as seen with its positive regulation of ZNRD1-AS1 to promote lung cancer malignancy via the miR-942/TNS1 axis [100] and its involvement in pathways regulating lncRNAs like CALML3-AS1 in NSCLC [101], LINC00894 in thyroid cancer [102], and LINC02747 whose decrease suppresses NSCLC [103].

Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (HNRNPs), including HNRNPA2B1 and HNRNPC, function as important m6A “readers” that regulate lncRNA stability and function, contributing significantly to cancer progression. HNRNPA2B1 mediates m6A modification of lncRNAs to influence their fate; for example, it facilitates the tumorigenesis and metastasis of NSCLC by modifying MEG3, which regulates the miR-21-5p/PTEN axis [104]. Its expression and function can itself be regulated by m6A-modified lncRNAs, creating a feedback loop, as seen with lncRNA 1833 promoting NSCLC progression by regulating HNRNPA2B1 expression [105]. Similarly, HNRNPC plays a critical role in stabilizing m6A-modified lncRNAs to drive oncogenic processes. In CRC, HNRNPC stabilizes m6A-modified AC145207.5, which accelerates tumorigenesis by impeding the Nrf2/GPX4 axis and inhibiting ferroptosis [106].

The RNA-binding protein HuR (ELAVL1) plays a critical role in the m6A-mediated stabilization of oncogenic lncRNAs, often acting in concert with the methylation machinery to promote cancer progression. HuR frequently cooperates with the m6A “writer” METTL3; for example, in IDH-wildtype glioma, METTL3 enhances the stability of the lncRNA MALAT1 with HuR’s assistance, leading to NF-κB activation and malignant progression [107]. Similarly, in glioblastoma (GBM), the m6A-modified lncRNA LINREP promotes tumor progression by specifically recruiting a functional complex containing PTBP1 and HuR [108].

A unified model for “reader”-dependent lncRNA fate determination

The relative abundance and binding affinities of different “reader” families determine lncRNA fate. YTHDF2 typically outcompetes IGF2BP proteins for binding to m6A marks located in regions accessible to the decay machinery, leading to rapid degradation. Conversely, stabilization predominates when m6A marks are embedded in structural contexts that favor IGF2BP binding or when IGF2BP proteins are overexpressed [109]. The subcellular localization of the lncRNA also influences “reader” access. Nuclear lncRNAs are more likely to be bound by YTHDC or HNRNP “readers”, whereas cytoplasmic lncRNAs encounter YTHDF and IGF2BP proteins [110]. This contextual “reader” competition explains how the same m6A mark can produce opposing functional outcomes depending on cellular state and cancer type.

m6A-mediated alteration of lncRNA function

While the control of RNA stability is a primary outcome, m6A modification exerts profound and diverse effects on lncRNA function beyond half-life regulation. By altering RNA splicing patterns and secondary structure, subcellular localization and compartmentalization, molecular interactions, and the translation of micropeptides (Fig. 3), m6A modification serves as a dynamic switch that reprograms the functional capacity of lncRNAs, enabling them to drive complex oncogenic processes (Table 1).

Integrative summary of lncRNA-m6A regulatory circuits in cancer

Modulation of RNA processing and structure

m6A modification can directly influence the biogenesis and structural configuration of lncRNAs, creating functional isoforms or facilitating novel structures.

m6A modification can dictate the splicing patterns of lncRNAs, generating isoforms with distinct functions. In glioma, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of LINC00475 promotes its alternative splicing, leading to an isoform that induces mitochondrial fission and tumor progression [111]. In PC, m6A modification of ANRIL is crucial for producing the ANRIL-L isoforms. These isoforms regulate gemcitabine resistance and enhance DNA repair capacity by forming a complex with EZH2 and Ring1B [112].

m6A modification can enable lncRNAs to form specific chromatin-associated structures. In alternative lengthening of telomeres-positive neuroblastoma, METTL3 deposits m6A mark on the TERRA, driving its telomere targeting through m6A-dependent R-loop formation, a process critical for maintaining telomere length in this cancer subtype [113].

Reconfiguration of subcellular localization and compartmentalization

The addition of m6A mark can act as a molecular signal that directs lncRNAs to specific cellular compartments, defining their functional interactome.

The lncRNA MALAT1 localizes to nuclear speckles, and its m6A modification is crucial for this localization. m6A modification alters the structure of MALAT1, enabling it to reshape nuclear speckles and promote cancer metastasis [114]. Conversely, m6A-modified SFTA1P in NSCLC exhibits altered localization associated with its tumor-suppressive function [115].

Regulation of molecular interactions

m6A modification critically influences the ability of lncRNAs to interact with other macromolecules, including mRNA, miRNA, protein, and chromatin, thereby determining their mechanistic role.

In GC, m6A-stabilized LINC00958 targets the GLUT1 mRNA to enhance glycolysis [116], while m6A-regulated LINC00675 in BC exerts its tumor-suppressive effect by sponging miR-513b-5p [117].

m6A modification can also disrupt existing interactions. In glioma, the miR-101/METTL3 axis induces m6A modification on EIF3J-AS1, which interrupts its binding to the transcription factor FOXG1, thereby inducing autophagy [118]. Similarly, m6A mark on Lnc-LSG1 in RCC blocks the interaction between Lnc-LSG1 and the splicing factor ESRP2 [119].

m6A-modified lncRNAs frequently serve as scaffolds to recruit epigenetic regulators to specific genomic loci. For instance, m6A-modified TRERNA1 in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) recruits the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) component EZH2 to silence p21 [120]. Similarly, ALKBH5-demethylated SNHG15 in myeloma recruits the histone methyltransferase SETD2 to increase chromatin accessibility [121]. In HCC, m6A modification of LEAWBIH promotes tumor progression through recruitment of the histone demethylase KDM3B to the CTNNB1 promoter, thereby activating its transcription [122]. An analogous mechanism involves m6A-methylated NUTM2B-AS1, which recruits the histone methyltransferase MLL1 to activate transcription of BMPR1A [123]. The canonical lncRNA HOTAIR requires m6A modification to effectively recruit the SNAIL-EZH2 complex and drive epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [124]. In PCa, m6A-modified NEAT1 acts as a scaffold to recruit CYCLINL1 and CDK19 complex to the promoters of metastasis-associated genes, such as RUNX2, facilitating Pol II ser2 phosphorylation and enhancing RUNX2 transcription [125].

m6A mark on lncRNAs can directly influence their interaction with chromatin to regulate gene expression programs. A single m6A site is essential for regulating HOTAIR function in BC cells, impacting its chromatin-associated activity [126]. m6A-modified FAM111A-DT in HCC promotes tumor growth by epigenetically activating the transcription of its sense gene, FAM111A [127].

Regulation of lncRNA-encoded micropeptides

Emerging evidence reveals that some lncRNAs harbor small open reading frames (smORFs) that can be translated into functional micropeptides, and this translation can be regulated by m6A. The tumorigenic effect of lncRNA AFAP1-AS1 is mediated by a translated peptide named ATMLP. The translation of this micropeptide is controlled by m6A methylation, directly linking epitranscriptomic modification to proteomic output in cancer [128].

LncRNA regulating m6A modification of target RNA

Beyond being substrates of m6A modification, lncRNAs function as active, upstream regulators of the m6A methylome. They act as scaffolds, guides, decoys, and signaling molecules to precisely orchestrate the localization, activity, and specificity of m6A “writer” and “reader” proteins (Fig. 4), thereby programming the epitranscriptomic fate of downstream mRNAs. This lncRNA-directed regulation establishes complex, hierarchical gene networks that drive oncogenesis across cancer types through several key mechanisms (Table 1).

LncRNAs as scaffolds and guides for the m6A MTC

LncRNAs can function as essential scaffolds and guides to direct the localization and activity of the m6A MTC, comprising core proteins like METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP. This role is exemplified by MALAT1, which, as shown in thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), is delocalized in a METTL3-dependent manner to drive oncogenic c-Myc expression, demonstrating how lncRNAs can recruit the MTC to specific genomic contexts [129]. Similarly, in CRC, the lncRNA LOH12CR2 is reported to exert its tumor-suppressive function through METTL14-mediated m6A modification, implying a guided interaction for targeted methylation [130]. Furthermore, lncRNAs such as lncSLERT can facilitate m6A deposition by recruiting auxiliary factors like RBM15, which is known to bridge the complex to specific RNA substrates, thereby regulating downstream targets such as HUNK [131].

LncRNAs modulating the stability, localization, or activity of m6A effectors

LncRNAs can post-transcriptionally modulate the stability, localization, and activity of key m6A effector proteins, thereby fine-tuning the functional outcomes of m6A methylation. For instance, VIM-AS1 directly cooperates with the m6A “reader” IGF2BP1 in HCC, forming a complex that stabilizes EPHA3 and inhibits tumor aggressiveness, effectively enhancing the IGF2BP1’s oncogenic function [132]. Similarly, MNX1-AS1 interact with IGF2BP2 to promote the stability of ITGA6 mRNA, which activates the PI3K/AKT pathway [133]. A more direct stabilizing interaction is demonstrated by TMEM99 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), which physically complexes with IGF2BP2 to enhances p21 mRNA stability, thereby preserving the pro-tumorigenic activity [134]. LncRNAs also regulate the localization and translational efficiency of “reader”-bound mRNAs, as seen with the circZFR/YTHDF3 axis in CC, where this circular RNA facilitates lymph node metastasis by driving the translation of m6A-modified FASN mRNA in concert with the “reader” YTHDF3 [135]. Furthermore, lncRNAs can modulate the activity of m6A erasers; in EC, NORAD regulates ferroptosis by influencing the m6A demethylase FTO-mediated modification of GPX4 mRNA, showcasing how lncRNAs can interface with the “eraser” machinery to alter specific m6A-dependent cellular pathways [136]. These mechanisms highlight a sophisticated layer of regulation where lncRNAs act as pivotal modulators of the m6A interpretation system.

LncRNAs establishing autoregulatory and feedback loops

LncRNAs are pivotal in establishing autoregulatory and feedforward loops that amplify oncogenic signaling. A quintessential example is the LINC00511-YTHDF2-SOX2 feedback loop in CCA, where LINC00511 promotes the degradation of SOX2 mRNA via the m6A “reader” YTHDF2, while SOX2 transcriptionally upregulates LINC00511, creating a self-reinforcing circuit that drives cancer stemness and progression [137]. Similarly, hypoxia-induced RPPH1 in BC initiates a feedforward loop; it stabilizes the IGF2BP2/FGFR2 axis to promote metastasis, and the ensuing signaling likely reinforces the hypoxic TME, potentially further upregulating RPPH1 expression [138]. These loops can also originate from the m6A machinery itself, as seen with FOXD2-AS1 in OS, where the MTC component WTAP stabilizes the lncRNA, which in turn promotes tumor progression via the m6A/FOXM1 axis, effectively creating a positive feedback mechanism that sustains its own expression and oncogenic function [139]. Furthermore, lncRNAs can be both products and drivers of methyltransferase activity, exemplified by DLGAP1-AS2 in NSCLC; its expression is induced by METTL3, and it subsequently promotes tumorigenesis through an m6A/c-Myc-dependent pathway, thereby establishing a feedforward loop that perpetuates the hyperactive metabolic state of cancer cells [140].

LncRNAs influencing m6A modification in metabolism and cell death pathways

LncRNAs critically influence m6A-mediated regulation of cancer metabolism and cell death pathways, such as ferroptosis. In metabolic reprogramming, lncRNA AP001885.4 drives proliferation in ESCC by promoting METTL3-mediated stabilization of c-Myc mRNA, a master transcription factor that upregulates the expression of glycolytic enzymes and biosynthesis-related genes, thereby enhancing glycolytic flux [141]. Similarly, in HCC, LL22NC03-N14H11.1 promotes glycolytic activity by stabilizing the m6A “reader” IGF2BP3, which in turn enhances the stability of MYC mRNA, leading to increased expression of glycolytic enzymes and amplification of the Warburg effect, a form of metabolic flux [142].

Concurrently, lncRNAs are central modulators of ferroptosis. In CC, LOXL1-AS1 suppresses this iron-dependent cell death through IGF2BP2-dependent regulation of TFRC (transferrin receptor), a key iron import protein [143]. This anti-ferroptotic role is echoed by FEZF1-AS1 in multiple myeloma (MM), which operates through KIAA1429-mediated m6A modification to inhibit cell death [144]. Conversely, GDNF-AS1 in glioma inhibits progression by mediating the LHX2/METTL3/NCOA4 axis to promote ferroptosis, demonstrating how lncRNAs can orchestrate m6A wiring to activate this lethal pathway [145].

In summary, lncRNAs serve as master regulators of the m6A epitranscriptome. They function not merely as passive substrates but as active architects that recruit m6A machinery, modulate its components’ activity, and establish powerful feedback loops, thereby representing a central layer of gene regulatory control in cancer pathogenesis (Fig. 5).

The synergy between lncRNA and m6A modification in therapeutic sensitivity

The crosstalk between lncRNAs and m6A modification is a pivotal determinant of therapeutic resistance, acting as a major barrier to successful cancer treatment. This synergy creates robust molecular circuits that allow cancer cells to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy (Table 2), radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. Beyond cell-autonomous mechanisms, emerging evidence highlights how this axis actively remodels the TME to foster an immunosuppressive and therapy-resistant niche.

Chemotherapy resistance

For 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), resistance is fostered by lncRNAs like FOXD1-AS1, which sponges miR-570-3p to upregulate SPP1, enhancing stemness and resistance in PC [146]. In GC, the m6A “reader” hnRNPA2B1 recognizes and stabilizes the lncRNA NEAT1, directly conferring chemoresistance via Wnt/β-catenin pathway [147].

In the context of Anthracyclines (Adriamycin/Doxorubicin), resistance is often mediated by feedback loops. In BC, the m6A-modified lncRNA DLGAP1-AS1 enhances resistance via a miR-299-3p/WTAP axis, creating a feedforward loop [148]. The m6A demethylase ALKBH5 modulates the TUG1 to drive resistance in AML through suppressing SH3BGRL expression [149], while in HCC, METTL3-mediated regulation of TUG1 promotes doxorubicin resistance via miR-9-EIF5A2 signaling axis [150].

The resistance to targeted agents such as the PI3Kα inhibitor alpelisib is coordinated on a broader scale, with m6A methylome reprogramming lncRNA networks (e.g., SENCR, PROX1-AS1, and LINC00892) to upregulate PI3K signaling mediators, including ITGA2, F2R, and COL6A1, thereby driving cross-resistance in AML [151]. For the proteasome inhibitor Bortezomib in MM, resistance is mediated by m6A methylation in adipocyte exosomal lncRNAs LOC606724 and SNHG1 [152] and by m6A-mediated upregulation of H19, which modulates the miR-184/CARM1 axis [153].

Taxane resistance is also under epitranscriptomic control. Docetaxel resistance in BC involves FTO-mediated m6A demethylation and upregulation of LINC01559, which suppresses miR-1343-3p [154]. For Paclitaxel, the lncRNA LINC02489 with m6A mark increases sensitivity by up-regulating PKNOX2 expression in OC [155], whereas others promote resistance, including MNX1-AS1 which promotes the stability of ITGA6 mRNA to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway in BC [133], m6A-modified linc-OIP5 which acts through a DDX5-dependent mechanism [156], and METTL3-induced FGD5-AS1 which contributes to resistance in EC [157].

Gemcitabine resistance, a major hurdle in PC, is orchestrated by multiple lncRNA-m6A circuits. The m6A demethylase ALKBH5 upregulates DDIT4-AS1 to maintain stemness and suppress chemosensitivity via the mTOR pathway in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [158]. Similarly, LINC01547 promotes growth and resistance through the miR-34a-5p/MYH9 axis [159] and m6A-mediated SH3BP5-AS1 upregulation activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by targeting miR-139-5p/CTBP1 axis in PC [160]. Conversely, m6A modification of DBH-AS1 can suppress growth and resistance via the miR-3163/USP44 axis in PC, representing a tumor-suppressive node [161]. In bladder cancer, exosome-transmitted LUCAT1 binds to IGF2BP2 to promote stemness and chemoresistance by upregulating HMGA1 expression [162].

Resistance to Platinum-based drugs is heavily influenced by this axis. For Cisplatin, LINC00942 promotes chemoresistance in GC by enhancing c-Myc mRNA stability [163], while METTL3-mediated m6A modification of RHPN1-AS1 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway in OC [164]. In ESCC, the m6A demethylase FTO stabilizes LINK-A to drive resistance via cell-cycle progression and HIF-1α activation [165] and the ALKBH5-induced lncRNA CASC8 promotes the resistance via hnRNPL [166]. In OS, METTL3-mediated modification of LINC00520 confers glycolysis and chemoresistance by binding to ENO1 to upregulate ENO1 expression [167]. For Carboplatin, ALKBH5 promotes the resistance by demethylating PVT1 to increase FOXM1 levels in OC [168]. Oxaliplatin resistance in CRC involves LINC01615 maintaining survival by binding with hnRNPA1 to facilitate G6PD pre-mRNA splicing [169], while in HCC, m6A-mediated lnc-OXAR promotes resistance by enhancing Ku70 stability [170]. A broader Platinum resistance mechanism in CRC involves the m6A-modified lncRNA FAM83H-AS1 promoting progression via PTBP1 [171].

For tyrosine kinase inhibitors, Erlotinib resistance in LUAD involves m6A-associated suppression of TSUC7, leading to Notch signaling activation by targeting miR-146a [172]. Gefitinib resistance is driven by METTL3-induced lncRNA SNHG17, which epigenetically represses the tumor suppressor LATS2 in LUAD [173]. Lenvatinib resistance in HCC is accelerated by LINC01532, which promotes G6PD pre-mRNA splicing and NADPH synthesis to sustain redox homeostasis [174]. Sorafenib resistance in HCC is orchestrated by a network of m6A-modified lncRNAs. These include DUXAP8 which regulates resistance through the miR-584-5p/MAPK1/ERK pathway [175], LINC01273 which confers resistance by targeting miR-600 to regulate METTL3 [176], and KIF9-AS1 which promotes stemness and resistance by upregulating SHOX2 [177]. Furthermore, decreased HNF4A-AS1 facilitates resistance by increasing DECR1 expression in a YTHDF3-dependent manner [178].

For endocrine and antibody therapies, Tamoxifen resistance in BC is affected by m6A-based regulation of HAGLR [179], and Trastuzumab resistance is promoted by m6A-mediated dysregulation of AGAP2-AS1 via ATG5 [180]. Additionally, PAK5 promotes trastuzumab resistance by increasing HER2 nuclear accumulation [181].

Radiotherapy resistance

Radioresistance remains a formidable challenge in cancer therapy, and emerging evidence highlights a critical cooperative role for lncRNAs and m6A modification in driving this phenotype. This synergy orchestrates radioresistance through diverse mechanisms, including enhanced DNA damage repair, maintenance of cancer stem cells, and modulation of the TME.

A primary mechanism involves the stabilization of key DNA repair proteins. In ESCC, m6A modification-mediated upregulation of LNCAROD confers radioresistance by directly stabilizing PARP1, a crucial enzyme in the DNA damage response and repair pathway, thereby enabling cancer cells to survive radiation-induced DNA damage [182]. Similarly, in NPC, METTL3 stabilizes SUCLG2-AS1, which mediates the formation of a long-range chromatin loop between super-enhancers and the promoter of the stemness gene SOX2. This epigenetic rewiring activates SOX2 expression, driving metastasis and conferring radiosensitivity [183].

The TME, particularly immunosuppressive components, is also regulated by this axis to promote radioresistance. In NSCLC, the m6A “writer” RBM15 facilitates resistance by recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This is achieved through the m6A-IGF2BP3/CBR3-AS1/miR-409-3p/CXCL1 axis, where m6A modification stabilizes the lncRNA CBR3-AS1, which in turn modulates CXCL1 signaling to attract MDSCs, creating an immunosuppressive niche that protects tumors from radiotherapy [184].

Furthermore, the lncRNA-m6A nexus is instrumental in maintaining the stem-like properties of tumor cells that are intrinsically resistant to radiation. In glioma, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of LINC00839 is essential for maintaining glioma stem cells. This modification stabilizes LINC00839, which subsequently activates Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a key pathway for stemness and proliferation, thereby sustaining the radioresistant glioma stem cells population [185].

Immunotherapy resistance

Resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is a major clinical hurdle, and the intricate crosstalk between lncRNAs and m6A modification has emerged as a key epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulator of the immunosuppressive TME and ICI efficacy. This synergy modulates immune evasion by altering the expression of immunomodulatory genes, reprogramming immune cell function, recruiting immunosuppressive cells, and establishing molecular signatures predictive of therapeutic response.

The lncRNA-m6A axis directly controls the expression of immune checkpoint molecules on cancer cells. In HCC, lipopolysaccharide-induced m6A modification of MIR155HG upregulates PD-L1 expression, facilitating immune escape [186]. The m6A-modified lncRNA hsa-LINC02418 dictates anti-PD-L1 efficacy by enhancing Trim21-mediated PD-L1 ubiquitination and degradation, identifying this lncRNA as both a predictive biomarker and a potential therapeutic target [187]. These findings establish a direct link between the epitranscriptome and immune checkpoint regulation.

The lncRNA-m6A axis critically regulates the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. In HCC, a sophisticated, multi-layered regulatory mechanism is driven by the lncRNA L1CAM-AS1. Its oncogenic function and role in immunotherapy resistance are initiated by H3K36me3 histone modification, which guides subsequent m6A modification of the transcript. This dually modified L1CAM-AS1 promotes M2 macrophage polarization, shifting the TME towards an immunosuppressive state that blunts the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy [188]. Similarly, in RCC, IGF2BP3-stabilized AGAP2-AS1 promotes M2 polarization through the miR-9-5p/THBS2/PI3K-Akt pathway [189], and exosomal SLC16A1-AS1 stabilized by IGF2BP3 facilitates M2 polarization in HCC [190]. In gastric cancer, YTHDF2-mediated degradation of AC026691.1 promotes M2 macrophage polarization, contributing to an immunosuppressive TME [58]. Beyond macrophages, the axis regulates MDSC recruitment. In NSCLC, the RBM15/IGF2BP3/CBR3-AS1 axis promotes MDSC infiltration via CXCL1 signaling, creating a protective niche that enables tumor evasion from both radiotherapy and immunotherapy [184].

T cell exhaustion represents a progressive state of CD8 + T cell dysfunction characterized by impaired effector function, sustained expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, and reduced anti-tumor activity [191]. Recent advances have highlighted the critical role of m6A modification and lncRNA-mediated mechanisms, in driving CD8 + T cell exhaustion within the TME [192]. The m6A machinery can directly modulate the expression of key exhaustion-associated transcription factors and immune checkpoint molecules, thereby controlling the differentiation and persistence of exhausted T cells. Moreover, m6A-mediated upregulation of specific lncRNAs, such as AC026356.1 in lung adenocarcinoma, promotes cancer stem cell maintenance and activates Wnt signaling [193]. Given that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is known to suppress T cell infiltration and promote an immunosuppressive TME, such lncRNA-m6A circuits in cancer cells can indirectly induce T cell exhaustion by creating a hostile metabolic and signaling environment.

The lncRNA-m6A axis also intersects with antigen presentation pathways. Reduced expression of MHC class I molecules, a common immune evasion mechanism, can be driven by m6A-regulated lncRNAs that control the expression of antigen processing machinery components [194]. Similarly, interferon signaling, which is critical for anti-tumor immunity, is modulated by this axis. In HCC, the m6A-modified lncRNA MIR155HG suppresses interferon response genes while upregulating PD-L1, creating a coordinated immune evasion program [186].

The axis can directly influence TME composition and predict ICI sensitivity. In glioblastoma, the m6A-modified pseudogene HSPA7 correlates significantly with the TME’s immune landscape. Its expression pattern serves as a biomarker that predicts response to immune checkpoint therapy, linking epitranscriptomic regulation of lncRNAs to the clinical outcome of immunotherapy [195].

The mechanistic insights described above reveal multiple therapeutic opportunities to overcome immunotherapy resistance. Targeting the m6A machinery with small molecule inhibitors (e.g., STM2457 for METTL3) may synergize with ICIs by reducing PD-L1 expression or reprogramming macrophage polarization. Similarly, ASOs against specific lncRNAs involved in immune evasion (e.g., L1CAM-AS1, CBR3-AS1) could be combined with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy to re-sensitize tumors. These combination strategies represent promising avenues for future clinical translation.

Translational implications

The convergence of lncRNAs and m6A modification transcends fundamental biology, yielding significant translational implications for precision oncology. This synergy serves as a rich source of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, fundamentally reshaping prognostic prediction, immune landscape characterization, and treatment strategy development.

Prognostic and diagnostic signatures

By integrating expression profiles of m6A-related lncRNAs, robust multi-gene models can stratify patient risk with high accuracy across numerous cancers, including kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) [196], DLBCL [197], CRC [198], and PC [199]. These signatures consistently outperform traditional markers by encapsulating the functional synergy of the epitranscriptome. Furthermore, specific lncRNAs like LINC00839 in NPC [200] and SNHG7 in GC [201] have been individually validated as potent prognostic indicators, directly linking their m6A-modified status to aggressive tumor behavior.

Immune landscape characterization

This axis provides critical insights into the tumor immune microenvironment and response to immunotherapy. Signatures based on m6A-related lncRNAs are powerful tools for dissecting immune cell infiltration, classifying tumors into “hot” or “cold” immunophenotypes [202], and predicting patient response to ICIs [199, 203]. For example, the lncRNA RP11-552D4.1 is associated with immune status in GBM [204]. These models elucidate how the lncRNA-m6A circuit sculpts an immunosuppressive milieu, identifying patients likely to benefit from immunotherapy and revealing novel combinatory targets to overcome resistance.

Therapeutic targets

Based on mechanistic validation and therapeutic potential, several lncRNA-m6A pathways emerge as high-priority translational targets. Among the most clinically actionable circuits is the METTL3-BANCR axis in PC. Pharmacological inhibition of METTL3 using the small molecule STM2457 suppresses tumor invasion by directly reducing m6A modification of the oncogenic lncRNA BANCR, demonstrating preclinical efficacy in patient-derived xenograft models [205]. Another high-priority target is the NEAT1 locus in RCC, where targeted methylation of the NEAT1 promoter using a dCas9-METTL3 fusion protein suppresses malignancy, providing proof-of-concept for epitranscriptomic editing [206]. In HCC, the m6A-upregulated lncRNA LINC00958 drives lipogenesis and tumor growth, and its therapeutic targeting has been successfully achieved using nanoparticle-based delivery systems [207]. In AML, the WTAP-SUCLG2-AS1-JAK1 axis forms a conserved pathogenic circuit, and RNA interference-mediated silencing of SUCLG2-AS1 restores chemosensitivity [208].

Small molecules vs. RNA-based approaches

Targeting the lncRNA-m6A axis can be approached through two primary strategies, each with distinct advantages and challenges. Small molecule inhibitors targeting m6A “writers” or “erasers” benefit from established drug development pipelines and favorable pharmacokinetic properties. The METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 has shown preclinical efficacy in suppressing pancreatic cancer invasion by downregulating m6A modification of the oncogenic lncRNA BANCR [205]. FTO inhibitors, such as FB23, have also demonstrated therapeutic potential across multiple cancer types, with studies showing that FTO inhibition can overcome osimertinib resistance in lung adenocarcinoma and synergize with other targeted agents in breast cancer [209]. However, small molecule inhibitors face several challenges, including potential systemic toxicity due to the ubiquitous expression of m6A machinery, difficulty achieving selective inhibition of specific “writers” or “erasers” without affecting family members, and the need for high selectivity to avoid off-target effects.

RNA-based therapies, including ASOs, small interfering RNA, and CRISPR-based tools, offer high sequence specificity and the ability to target lncRNAs directly. ASOs have demonstrated clinical success in other diseases, such as nusinersen for spinal muscular atrophy and tofersen for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, both approved by the FDA and EMA [210]. For cancer applications, lipid nanoparticle formulations have been developed to deliver ASOs targeting oncogenic lncRNAs like HOTAIR and MALAT1 [211]. Advanced nanoparticle systems using ionizable lipids have been designed to enable nonviral delivery of ASOs, achieving significant reductions in target lncRNA levels in vitro and tumor volume reduction in vivo [212]. However, RNA-based approaches face substantial delivery challenges, particularly for solid tumors. These include limited stability in circulation requiring chemical modifications, potential immunogenicity, difficulty achieving tissue-specific delivery, inefficient cellular uptake, and challenges in nuclear entry for targeting nuclear-localized lncRNAs.

A promising approach combines both modalities. Small molecule inhibitors of m6A regulators can be paired with ASOs targeting specific lncRNAs to dismantle oncogenic circuits while minimizing compensatory activation of redundant pathways. This combination strategy, though early in development, represents a rational path forward for epitranscriptomic cancer therapy.

Liquid biopsy for non-invasive detection and real-time monitoring

The detection of m6A-modified lncRNAs in liquid biopsy specimens represents a transformative approach for non-invasive cancer management. Circulating tumor cells, exosomes, and cell-free RNA can be isolated from peripheral blood to detect and quantify m6A-modified lncRNAs. This approach has particular utility in gastrointestinal malignancies, where serial biopsies are challenging [213]. Liquid biopsy enables four key applications. First, it allows early detection through identification of cancer-specific lncRNA-m6A signatures. Second, it enables minimal residual disease monitoring after curative-intent therapy. Third, it facilitates real-time assessment of therapeutic response, allowing early detection of emerging resistance. Fourth, it supports adaptive treatment strategies where therapy is modified based on molecular evolution captured in circulating markers. The stability of m6A-modified lncRNAs in exosomes makes them particularly attractive as non-invasive biomarkers.

Challenges, opportunities, and future directions

Translating the complex biology of the lncRNA-m6A axis into clinical applications presents formidable challenges. A primary obstacle is the context-dependent nature of these interactions, where the same molecular player can have opposing roles in different cancers or cellular states, coupled with functional redundancy that makes the network resistant to single-target therapies [214]. Significant technical and translational barriers also persist, including the difficulty of precisely mapping the m6A mark on lncRNAs, developing selective drugs for the m6A machinery, and ensuring the safe delivery of RNA-targeting therapies [215, 216]. Furthermore, integrating complex multi-lncRNA signatures as reliable clinical biomarkers requires validation through large-scale, costly prospective trials [217].

Experimental validation strategies

A critical limitation of many studies in this field is the reliance on correlative expression analyses without rigorous mechanistic validation. Establishing direct causal relationships between m6A modification of a specific lncRNA and its functional consequences requires a multi-faceted experimental toolkit. m6A site mutagenesis remains the gold standard, where mutation of the specific adenosine residue within the m6A consensus motif (RRACH) to a non-methylatable base abrogates the modification, and functional rescue assays comparing wild-type and methylation-deficient constructs can definitively attribute observed phenotypes to the specific m6A site [218]. Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) and m6A-seq provide transcriptome-wide m6A maps, while newer methods like m6A crosslinking immunoprecipitation (m6A-CLIP) and m6A individual nucleotide resolution CLIP (miCLIP) offer higher resolution [219]. CRISPR-dCas13-based epitranscriptomic editing represents an emerging technology for targeted m6A deposition or removal without altering the underlying sequence, enabling gain-of-function and loss-of-function studies with high specificity [220]. RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and crosslinking immunoprecipitation (CLIP) are essential for validating direct binding between m6A “readers” and target lncRNAs, with enhanced CLIP (eCLIP) providing nucleotide-resolution binding maps [221].

Methodological limitations in studying m6A-modified lncRNAs

Several technical limitations complicate the study of m6A-modified lncRNAs and must be critically acknowledged. Antibody-dependent methods (MeRIP-seq, m6A-seq) suffer from batch-to-batch variability, cross-reactivity with other RNA modifications, and low resolution, typically generating 100–200 nucleotide peaks that cannot pinpoint individual m6A sites [222]. This low-resolution peak calling leads to false positives, particularly in repetitive regions where many lncRNAs reside. Reproducibility concerns are significant, as different antibody lots and experimental conditions can yield inconsistent results [223]. Distinguishing direct versus indirect regulatory effects is particularly challenging; the m6A mark on lncRNA may directly recruit a “reader”, or the “reader” may bind indirectly through bridging proteins [214]. Technical challenges specific to lncRNAs include their generally low abundance compared to mRNAs, cell-type-specific expression patterns requiring careful model system selection, and complex secondary structures that can interfere with antibody binding or “reader” recognition [224].

Emerging single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies

The advent of high-resolution, single-cell multi-omics now allows researchers to deconvolute the precise lncRNA-m6A networks operating within specific cell types of the TME, revealing novel, cell-state-specific vulnerabilities [225]. Single-cell m6A sequencing technologies have emerged as transformative tools. Several methods are now available, including scDART-seq (which converts m6A sites to C-to-T mutations), scm6A-seq (which adapts traditional MeRIP-seq to single-cell level), sn-m6A-CT (using CUT&Tag technology), and Scm6A (a computational prediction method) [226]. Spatial transcriptomics technologies, such as 10x Genomics Visium and MERFISH, enable mapping of lncRNA-m6A interactions within the spatial context of the TME, revealing how tumor-stroma interactions influence epitranscriptomic regulation [227]. Recent advances have extended m6A detection to spatial transcriptomics data, allowing prediction and visualization of m6A modification distribution within tissues [228]. These technologies, while still maturing, hold immense promise for identifying cell-type-specific vulnerabilities and therapeutic targets within heterogeneous tumor ecosystems.

Current technical and clinical challenges for therapeutic translation

A balanced perspective on therapeutic translation requires acknowledging several critical limitations. Delivery efficiency remains the foremost challenge for both small molecule and RNA-based therapies [229]. For RNA-based therapeutics, systemic delivery to solid tumors is inefficient, and even advanced lipid nanoparticle formulations struggle with extravasation and penetration into densely packed tumor tissue [230]. For small molecule inhibitors, achieving sufficient tumor concentration without systemic toxicity is challenging [231]. Global inhibition of METTL3 or FTO may cause off-target effects in the bone marrow, immune system, and other proliferative tissues [232]. Context-dependent functions of m6A regulators mean that a drug effective in one cancer type may be ineffective or even deleterious in another [214]. Tumor heterogeneity poses a major barrier, as subclonal populations with different lncRNA-m6A pathway dependencies may escape mono-targeted therapy [233]. Off-target effects of epitranscriptomic inhibitors can be substantial; small molecules may inhibit related methyltransferases or demethylases, while antisense oligonucleotides can have hybridization-dependent off-target effects [234]. Challenges associated with RNA-based therapeutics include limited stability in circulation requiring extensive chemical modification, potential immunogenicity triggering innate immune responses, difficulty achieving tissue-specific delivery, inefficient endosomal escape, and challenges in nuclear entry for targeting nuclear-localized lncRNAs [235].

Opportunities and future directions

Despite these hurdles, the field is ripe with transformative opportunities driven by technological innovation. The advent of high-resolution, single-cell multi-omics now allows researchers to deconvolute the precise lncRNA-m6A networks operating within specific cell types of the TME, revealing novel, cell-state-specific vulnerabilities [225]. Concurrently, breakthroughs in therapeutic modalities, such as next-generation ASOs, CRISPR-based RNA editing, and protein degradation technologies, provide an expanding toolkit to precisely target oncogenic lncRNAs or m6A regulators [236, 237]. A particularly promising strategy lies in exploiting synthetic lethality or designing rational combination therapies that pair epitranscriptomic inhibitors with standard treatments to overcome drug resistance [238].

Looking ahead, future research must pivot from descriptive correlation to establishing definitive causation, using sophisticated genetic models to validate key interactions [214]. A major scientific goal is to decode the “interaction code” that governs how specific m6A “readers” are recruited to modified lncRNAs to produce distinct functional outcomes, such as RNA decay or stabilization [239]. The ultimate vision is personalized epitranscriptomic therapy, where a patient’s tumor is profiled to identify the dominant lncRNA-m6A circuit, enabling a tailored combination of inhibitors, RNA-targeting agents, and immunotherapy [240]. Expanding this paradigm to include the crosstalk between m6A and other RNA modifications will provide a holistic view of the epitranscriptomic landscape, solidifying this axis as a foundational target for the next generation of precision oncology [241].

Conclusion

The synergistic interplay between lncRNAs and m6A modification constitutes a central regulatory network in cancer biology. m6A controls lncRNA fate through competitive “reader” binding, while lncRNAs shape the m6A landscape via feedback circuits that drive tumor progression and therapy resistance. Translating these insights requires rigorous causal validation using m6A mutagenesis, CRISPR editing, and rescue assays. Single-cell and spatial technologies can deconvolute cell-state-specific networks within the tumor microenvironment. Therapeutically, small molecule inhibitors like STM2457 and RNA-based approaches such as ASOs show promise, though delivery and toxicity challenges remain. Decoding the “reader” recruitment code and integrating multi-omics profiling will solidify the lncRNA-m6A axis as a cornerstone of precision oncology.
