A 58-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 1-month history of pain and bluish discoloration of all fingers. He reported a decade-long history of Raynaud phenomenon, affecting both hands, that lasted from minutes to hours and was triggered by exposure to cold environments, particularly at work. He worked as a grocery clerk in a cold room. He had a history of paroxysmal atrial flutter (CHADS-65 score 0). His only regular medication was bisoprolol. He smoked 1.5 packs per day (40 pack-year history) and did not use illicit drugs. He had no history of venous thrombosis, intermittent claudication, skin disease, gastresophageal reflux, or heparin use.

The patient was afebrile, blood pressure was 142/77, and heart rate was 80 beats per minute and regular. Neurologic and cardiovascular examinations were normal. We observed coldness and cyanosis of all fingertips (Figure 1) and cyanosis, ischemic ulcers, and dry gangrene of his toes (Figure 2). His upper extremity pulses were palpable except for the right radial and left ulnar arteries. We felt his femoral and popliteal pulses, but could not feel the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses bilaterally. We observed no sicca, telangiectasias, calcinosis, sclerodactyly, rash, livedo, synovitis, limb weakness, or superficial thrombophlebitis.

The patient’s complete blood count, serum creatinine, glycosylated hemoglobin, lipid panel, liver enzyme levels, prothrombin time with international normalized ratio, and activated partial thromboplastin time were normal except for a platelet count of 506 (normal 150–400) × 109/L. His C-reactive protein level was 64 (normal < 10) mg/L. Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm. The emergency department physician ordered 2 sets of blood cultures and assessment of upper and lower limb arterial Doppler waveforms with segmental blood pressure measurements, including toe pressures (Figure 3). The patient’s wrist-brachial and ankle-brachial indices were normal, Doppler waveforms were triphasic except for monophasic waveforms in the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries bilaterally, and toe pressures were too low to be quantified. We admitted the patient to the general internal medicine service to expedite investigations, and administered acetylsalicylic acid (ASA; 81 mg/d), rosuvastatin (40 mg/d), intravenous unfractionated heparin, and amlodipine (5 mg/d) empirically because the differential diagnosis of digital ischemia was wide at that point.

The answer is (e). Given the wide differential diagnosis and the severity of the clinical presentation, we ordered all of the above in an attempt to determine the cause of the distal arteriopathy. The TTE and TEE showed no sign of endocarditis or other sources of cardiac embolism. Antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti–β2 glycoprotein 1) were within normal limits. JAK 2, CALR, and MPL mutations were negative, making a diagnosis of myeloproliferative neoplasm unlikely. The immunologic panel was normal and blood cultures were negative. Computed tomography angiography showed no significant atherosclerotic disease or aneurysm that could have been a source of emboli, and no proximal arteriopathy in the upper or lower extremities, but severe distal arteriopathy of the lower limbs. We also ordered finger and toe plethysmography (PPG) curves, which were all flat (Figure 4), suggesting microcirculation abnormalities in all digits.

The answer is (c). We ruled out a diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome because the results of the 3 antiphospholipid antibody tests were normal. The absence of calcinosis, sclerodactyly, telangiectasias, gastresophageal reflux, and anticentromere antibodies, in addition to a normal ANA test result, were inconsistent with CREST syndrome. The normal ANA result made systemic lupus erythematous highly unlikely. Similarly, the patient’s normal rheumatoid factor and cryoglobulin levels did not support a diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis or cryoglobulinemia. The normal TTE, TEE, and blood cultures made cardioembolic disease unlikely, and CT angiography showed no proximal source of atheroemboli. In addition, we felt it highly unlikely that cardiac or arterial emboli would cause poor perfusion in all 20 digits, as seen on PPG waveforms.

The combination of normal wrist-brachial and ankle-brachial indices, Doppler waveforms in the upper limbs and proximal lower extremities, and abnormal PPG waveforms in all digits made the most likely diagnosis a distal small vessel occlusive disease such as thromboangiitis obliterans (TAO). Because the distal arteries of the left lower limb were not adequately opacified by CT angiography, we ordered arteriography of this limb, which showed no peripheral artery disease that could benefit from endovascular revascularization (Figure 5). Having found no evidence of thrombotic or embolic disease, we stopped heparin.

The answer is (a). The only reliable way to prevent progression of symptoms and to decrease the risk of amputation in patients with TAO is complete and permanent abstinence from tobacco products of any form. Complete avoidance of cannabis products is also advocated, because cannabis arteritis can clinically mimic TAO.1,2 Smoking cessation programs and pharmacotherapy without nicotine, such as varenicline or bupropion, should be offered to every patient.3

Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers can be used to reduce the frequency of episodes of secondary Raynaud phenomenon. 4 Alprostadil is an intravenous vasodilator that can relieve ischemic pain and help heal ischemic ulcers.5 If ischemic ulcers or gangrene are present, revascularization should be considered. However, diffuse involvement of distal arteries usually makes revascularization not feasible in patients with TAO.6

Within 24 hours of admission, we increased the patient’s amlodipine dose to 10 mg/d and started intravenous alprostadil. We discharged him 1 week later on varenicline (0.5 mg twice a day for 3 days and 1 mg twice a day thereafter) and referred him to a smoking cessation program. We continued alprostadil 3 days a week (8 h/d) as an outpatient, following a local protocol (i.e., gradually increasing intravenous alprostadil until optimal dosage, while monitoring possible adverse effects such as hypotension). One month after admission, autoamputation of the patient’s fifth left toe began, and it was surgically removed shortly thereafter. We continued alprostadil until the ischemic ulcers and fifth toe wound had healed. At each follow-up visit, we assessed the patient’s smoking status and offered counselling. Although the role of ASA and statins are debated for the treatment of patients with TAO, we continued these therapies.

After multiple relapses, the patient completely quit smoking, 4 months after admission. He has not had any new critical limb ischemic events. He changed his job to avoid cold exposure, and the episodes of Raynaud phenomenon have been shorter and less frequent. Plethysmography waveforms have greatly improved in all fingers and toes (Figure 6).

Discussion

Thromboangiitis obliterans is typically diagnosed in male smokers younger than 50 years; it has an annual incidence of 12.6 per 100 000 in the United States.1 It is a nonatherosclerotic and segmental inflammatory disease affecting small- and medium-sized arteries and veins of all extremities. Symptoms include intermittent claudication (particularly in the feet), rest pain, ischemic ulcers, or gangrene of the extremities. Two or more limbs are usually affected. Migratory superficial thrombophlebitis, Raynaud phenomenon, erythematous subcutaneous nodules, and abnormal Allen test are common. 7,8 At more advanced stages, it can present with dry or wet digital gangrene.

The diagnosis of TAO can be challenging because it is a diagnosis of exclusion, based on history, physical examination, and imaging. Thromboangiitis obliterans should be part of the broad differential of digital ischemia; the anatomic location of the arteriopathy can guide investigation and treatment (Table 1). Although arteriographic features can suggest TAO — such as absence of or minimal atherosclerosis, absence of aneurysm as a source of embolism, and presence of segmental occlusions of medium or small vessels with signs of collateralization — the diagnosis remains difficult to make because there is no pathognomonic test.

A definitive diagnosis of TAO can be obtained with a biopsy of an artery, but it is rarely done because it is invasive. It is important to make a timely diagnosis because delays, or the wrong diagnosis, can expose patients to the harms of therapies such as anticoagulation with no established benefit for patients with TAO.

Conclusion

Clinicians should consider the diagnosis of thromboangiitis obliterans in patients who smoke and who present with symptoms or signs of digital ischemia. Investigations to rule out other causes are usually needed. Complete abstinence from all tobacco products is the only way to prevent disease progression, improve symptoms, and reduce the risk of amputation.
