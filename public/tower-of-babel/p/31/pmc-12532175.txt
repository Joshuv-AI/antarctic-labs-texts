Assessment of Nanosized Plastics Exposure in Children: Evaluating the Impact of Daily Interaction with Plastic Items on Saliva Contamination
Authors: Shradha Jalan, R Ramesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNanosized plastics, formed from the breakdown of larger plastic items, pose potential health risks, especially to children who frequently interact with plastic through chewing and hand-to-mouth behaviors. Saliva, as a vital biological fluid, offers a unique medium to detect these particles and evaluate their impact on oral and systemic health, highlighting the need for focused research in this area.AimTo evaluate the presence of nanosized plastics in children's saliva after controlled exposure to plastic items, providing insight into the potential health risks associated with daily plastic interaction.Materials and methodsA total of 31 children aged 4–6 years with high interaction levels with plastic items were included. Saliva samples were collected following controlled plastic exposure and analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). These findings highlight the need for preventive measures and further studies on the long-term effects of nanoplastic exposure.How to cite this articleJalan S, Ramesh R. Introduction

Microplastics, defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm, have emerged as a significant environmental concern in recent years due to their widespread presence in ecosystems and potential threats to human health. These particles, originating from the breakdown of larger plastic items, have been identified in various environmental compartments, such as marine and terrestrial ecosystems, the atmosphere, and even in food and water sources. While much attention has been given to microplastic contamination in these media, there is a growing need to investigate the presence of microplastics in human biological fluids, particularly saliva. Understanding microplastic exposure through bodily fluids is crucial for assessing their potential health impacts, as the long-term effects of microplastic accumulation in the human body remain largely unexplored.1

Children, in particular, are believed to be at a heightened risk of microplastic exposure due to their distinct behavioral patterns, such as frequent hand-to-mouth activity, the tendency to play with plastic objects, and biting habits. Furthermore, the developmental stages of children's bodies—characterized by their smaller size and immature immune systems—may render them more vulnerable to the adverse effects of microplastics. While microplastics have been recognized as a common pollutant in environmental media, limited research has focused on their interaction with the human body, particularly regarding their presence in saliva and children. Saliva, a vital fluid for digestion, oral health, and immune response, could act as an indicator of microplastic exposure, offering insight into how these particles enter and are processed by the human body.2

Since 2020, several studies have investigated the occurrence of microplastics in human biological samples, including saliva.3,4 These studies have aimed to identify the types of microplastics present in saliva, the sources of contamination, and their potential health implications. For children, the detection of microplastics in saliva may suggest exposure through the ingestion of contaminated food, water, or direct contact with plastic items. As this issue gains increasing attention, it is essential to explore the full extent of microplastic exposure in children to inform and develop effective policies aimed at reducing these risks.3,4

Materials and Methods

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. A one-sample t-test with a medium effect size (d = 0.5), a significance level of 0.05, and 85% power was used, which determined a required sample size of 31 participants. This ensured adequate statistical power to detect significant changes in microplastic levels in saliva following controlled plastic exposure (Fig. 1A).

Study Design and Participant Selection

This study aimed to assess the presence of microplastics in the saliva of children after controlled exposure to plastic items. A sample group of children aged 4–6 years was recruited. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC), approval number IHEC/SDC/UG-1905/23/PEDO/200. Children who played with plastic toys for extended periods daily (e.g., over 4 hours a day) were particularly targeted, as they were expected to have a higher risk of exposure to microplastics.

Saliva Sample Collection

Saliva samples were collected at one time point after high-risk proven exposure to plastic items. Children were instructed not to eat or drink anything for at least 30 minutes before sample collection to avoid contamination. Saliva was collected noninvasively using passive drool or oral swabs. The saliva was transferred into sterile containers and kept on ice during transport to the laboratory for processing.

Questionnaire for Evaluating Children's Interaction with Plastic Items

The questionnaire used in the study aimed to evaluate the frequency and nature of children's interactions with plastic items, focusing on their chewing and handling of plastic toys, utensils, and other objects. It included questions about plastic consumption through food and beverages packaged in plastic, as well as exposure to plastic debris in play areas. The survey also assessed hygiene practices such as rinsing the mouth or washing hands after handling plastic items. Additionally, parents were asked about any oral health issues their child had experienced after chewing plastic and their concerns regarding plastic exposure. Responses were recorded on a Likert scale, providing insights into the children's daily exposure to plastics.

Controlled Exposure to Plastic Items and Evaluation Criteria

In this study, exposure to plastic items involved children either chewing on plastic items, such as toys and utensils, or handling plastic items, such as straws and plastic toys, for more than 6 hours a day. The plastics encountered by materials in children's daily lives such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC). After the exposure happened, before children rinsed their mouths with water to remove any residual plastic particles, saliva samples were collected. To evaluate the level of exposure, a questionnaire was administered to parents, assessing the children's daily interactions with plastic items and specifically their habits of chewing on plastic objects. Participants who scored 60 or more on the questionnaire were included in the study.

Microplastic Extraction and Analysis

The saliva samples were subjected to filtration or centrifugation to separate solid particles from the liquid. Afterward, the particles were dried and prepared for analysis. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to capture high-resolution images of the particles, enabling the identification of microplastics based on their size, shape, and surface texture. To facilitate SEM imaging, the particles were coated with a conductive material such as gold or carbon. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was then utilized to analyze the chemical composition of the particles and determine their polymer types by comparing the obtained spectra with reference polymer databases. In addition, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to assess the crystallinity and phase composition of the microplastics. This technique helped to identify any mineral phases and crystalline structures present, further enhancing the understanding of the particles' characteristics. Together, these methods provided a comprehensive analysis of the physical and chemical properties of the microplastics.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

The data obtained from SEM, FTIR, and XRD were used to quantify the number and size distribution of microplastic particles in the saliva samples. SEM was employed to identify and categorize the microplastics based on their size, shape, and surface characteristics, while FTIR analysis provided information on the polymer composition, enabling the identification of specific types of microplastics. XRD was used to assess the crystallinity and phase composition of the particles, helping to identify any crystalline structures and mineral phases present. To evaluate the significance of the observed microplastic levels, statistical analysis was conducted using paired t-tests to compare the pre- and postexposure microplastic levels in saliva samples. Based on power analysis, a sample size of 31 children was determined to ensure statistically significant results with a 95% confidence level and 85% power for the study.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC), approval number IHEC/SDC/UG-1905/23/PEDO/200. Written informed consent was provided by the parents or guardians of all participants, ensuring they fully understood the study's purpose, procedures, and potential risks. Participation in the study was voluntary, with participants able to withdraw at any time without consequence. All data collected was kept confidential and used solely for research purposes.

Limitations of the Study

The study was limited by the variability in individual saliva composition, which may have affected the consistency of results. Additionally, the study only focused on child toys or things children use as plastic items, which may not encompass all potential sources of microplastic exposure in children's daily lives. Differentiating between microplastics from the plastic items used in the study and environmental contaminants present in children's surroundings posed another challenge. Despite these limitations, the study design was robust and aimed to provide valuable insights into the exposure pathways of microplastics in children.

Statistical Analysis

The study employed a comprehensive methodology to analyze the impact of nanosized plastic exposure in children. Descriptive statistics summarized key structural and molecular properties from XRD to FTIR data, while independent t-tests compared group differences. Correlation analysis identified significant associations between variables, and regression analysis used a multi-model approach to explore predictive relationships by integrating XRD, FTIR, and questionnaire data. ANOVA assessed variability within and between items and ensured reliability, supported by Cronbach's Alpha for internal consistency. Advanced XRD and FTIR techniques evaluated crystalline and amorphous phases as well as molecular compositions. Graphical analyses, including histograms and normal P–P plots, validated the normality of residuals and visually presented structural and molecular findings, providing a robust understanding of the study outcomes.

Results

The study confirmed the presence of nanosized plastics in all saliva samples collected from children after controlled exposure to plastic items. SEM images revealed nanoplastic particles adhering to biological matrices, while FTIR and XRD analyses identified their composition, predominantly polyethylene, polypropylene (PP), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC). Statistical analysis demonstrated significant findings, including t(30) = 11.357, p < 0.001 for FTIR peak measurements and t(34) = 14.063, p < 0.001 for XRD peak positions, with high effect sizes (Cohen's d > 2.0). These results showed strong correlations between plastic interaction levels and saliva contamination, emphasizing oral habits as critical exposure pathways and highlighting potential health risks such as microbiome disruption and inflammation (Figs 1B and 2).

Validity and Reliability of Questionnaire

The study analyzed a 15-item questionnaire (Appendix I) to evaluate the impact of daily interaction with plastic items on saliva contamination. Out of 36 questions, 25 (69.4%) were valid, while 11 (30.6%) were excluded due to incomplete responses using listwise deletion. The reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach's alpha of 0.928, indicating excellent internal consistency. This highlights that the questionnaire is a highly reliable tool for assessing nanosized plastic exposure in children.

The ANOVA results shown in Table 5 reveal that there is a significant difference between the items in the questionnaire (F =3.027, p < 0.001), indicating that the items are reliably measuring different aspects of nanosized plastic exposure. The total variation accounted for by the model is 89.867, with a grand mean score of 4.6560. A substantial portion of the variation lies between the items (10.064), while the residual error (79.803) represents within-item variation.

Discussion

Nano- and microplastics (NMPs) pose a growing concern in pediatric dentistry due to children's increased exposure through plastic toys, oral appliances, and dental products. These plastics can affect oral tissues, disrupt the microbiome, cause inflammation, and hinder healing. The presence of NMPs in saliva highlights the risks to oral health. The growing concern about microplastics (MPs) and nanoplastics (NPs) in the environment has raised significant questions regarding their potential impact on human health, especially in vulnerable populations such as children. This study evaluated children's exposure to nanosized plastics through daily interactions with plastic items, utilizing XRD, FTIR, and SEM to detect and characterize contamination in saliva. Preda et al. highlight the importance of their study in focusing on children's health by demonstrating how saliva can serve as a medium for detecting nanoplastics, emphasizing the potential risks, such as disruptions to oral health and long-term effects due to increased exposure from common behaviors such as hand-to-mouth activity. Using techniques such as XRD and FTIR, the study confirmed the detection and characterization of nanoplastics, with SEM images providing visual evidence of these particles adhering to biological matrices like proteins and cellular debris in saliva. The findings emphasized saliva as a viable medium for detecting nanoplastic exposure, highlighting the potential health risks, including microbial disruption, tissue healing interference, and inflammatory responses.5,6

This study focuses on evaluating how daily interactions with plastic items lead to saliva contamination by nanoplastics. By using advanced techniques such as XRD, FTIR, and SEM, the study provides direct evidence of nanoplastic particles adhered to organic matrices in saliva. This contrasts with other studies, such as those by Mummaleti and Kong, which mainly address microplastic contamination in environmental settings such as food chains and household dust, or Jackson et al., which examine the broader environmental impacts of microplastics. While these studies discuss the general health risks associated with microplastics and nanoplastics, this research specifically focuses on the oral health risks for children exposed to these particles through daily activities. This study's emphasis on biological fluids provides new insights compared to existing research, highlighting the importance of understanding how micro- and nanoplastics affect children directly.7–9

Microplastics, ubiquitous environmental pollutants, pose potential health risks due to their ability to absorb harmful chemicals such as heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants, which could be released into the body upon exposure. This could lead to oxidative stress, inflammation, and endocrine disruption, particularly affecting children's developmental and reproductive health. Additionally, their small size allowed microplastics to penetrate biological membranes, potentially causing long-term cellular damage. Detecting microplastics in biological fluids like saliva remained challenging due to their complex composition, necessitating advanced techniques such as FTIR and Raman spectroscopy for effective analysis. These methods had successfully identified polymers, including polyethylene and polystyrene, commonly found in toys and packaging, although further refinement was required to enhance their sensitivity and specificity.10–12

Micro- and nanoplastics pose significant risks to children due to their developmental sensitivity, with exposure through air, dust, and ingestion potentially leading to respiratory inflammation, immune disruption, and interference with growth and hormonal systems. The safety of children's toys requires evaluation to ensure they meet acceptable levels of plastic content, as these materials can release microplastic particles during use, especially when chewed or handled frequently. Studies emphasize that toys made from conventional plastics may contribute to microplastic exposure, with risks linked to the ingestion or inhalation of particles that contain harmful additives such as phthalates or BPA.13,14 Regulatory frameworks should enforce stricter standards on the composition and durability of plastic toys while encouraging safer, biodegradable materials as alternatives.15 Policymakers should implement regulations to reduce microplastic pollution in children's environments.

The growing concern over microplastics (MPs) and nanoplastics (NMPs) is increasingly relevant to human health, particularly for children. These particles, found in everyday products such as plastic toys, pacifiers, and dental appliances, pose significant risks, including potential damage to oral tissues, disruption of the microbiome, and inflammatory responses. Exposure to MPs can occur through ingestion, inhalation, and skin contact, leading to the accumulation of toxic substances in the body, which can cause health issues such as respiratory problems, gastrointestinal distress, and cell damage. Studies have shown that MPs and NMPs can act as carriers for harmful pollutants, adding to their toxicity. In children, whose immune and digestive systems are still developing, the risks are even more pronounced, and the long-term effects of exposure are still not fully understood. Further research, improved detection methods, and the adoption of safer alternatives in everyday products are essential to address these concerns.16–19

Shukla et al. highlight the significant health risks posed by microplastics (MPs) and nanoplastics (NPs), particularly their disruptive effects on biological systems in both aquatic organisms and humans. In pediatric dentistry, these findings are particularly relevant as children are frequently exposed to plastic materials through toys, pacifiers, and dental appliances such as oral screens and preorthodontic trainers. This exposure increases the ingestion of MPs and NPs, which, as noted by Razavi et al., can lead to inflammation and disturb the oral microbiome. These studies highlight the importance of adopting safer, non-plastic materials and reinforcing safety standards for dental appliances and children's products to protect the health and development of growing children.16,20

Sharma et al. highlighted environmental stability of microplastics due to their resistance to degradation, as well as their potential to carry harmful chemicals. This study focused on understanding microplastic exposure in children through toys, dental appliances, and biting habits, emphasizing the urgency to investigate safer material alternatives and explore effective degradation methods to mitigate health risks in pediatric populations.

This study's limitations include a small sample size, which may affect the generalizability of the findings, and a focus on children with extensive plastic interaction, potentially excluding other exposure pathways. Differentiating between microplastics from controlled exposure and environmental sources proved challenging. The short-term nature of the study limited the assessment of long-term health impacts, and the analysis was confined not to specific plastic types, leaving a broader range of polymers unexplored and undifferentiated.

Future research should focus on expanding the study to include a more diverse population of children, encompassing different age groups, socioeconomic backgrounds, and geographic regions, to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, it is essential to investigate other potential sources of nano-sized plastics, such as dietary intake and environmental exposure, to better understand the full spectrum of risk factors. Longitudinal studies are necessary to evaluate the long-term effects of nanosized plastic exposure on children's oral and systemic health, providing a comprehensive view of its implications over time. Furthermore, exploring potential biomarkers in saliva could facilitate the early detection of microplastic-associated health risks, offering a valuable diagnostic tool. Finally, there is a pressing need to develop and implement preventive interventions aimed at reducing children's interaction with plastic items and mitigating the associated health risks, ensuring safe environments for pediatric populations.

Conclusion

The research demonstrates that young children are frequently exposed to nanoplastics due to their routine use of plastic items, as evidenced by the presence of plastic particles in their saliva. Analytical techniques identified polymers such as polyethylene and polyvinyl chloride, indicating that behaviors like biting, licking, and chewing plastic items can lead to ingestion. Saliva proves to be an effective and noninvasive medium for monitoring this exposure. These findings highlight the urgent need to raise public awareness, implement stricter regulations on children's plastic toys, and conduct further research into the potential long-term health effects, especially considering children's heightened vulnerability.
