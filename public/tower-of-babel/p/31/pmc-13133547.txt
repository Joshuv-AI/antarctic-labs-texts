Population pharmacokinetics of remlifanserin (ACP‐204), a serotonin 2A receptor inverse agonist
Authors: Mona Darwish, Nancy Lin, Bryan Dirks, David Jaworowicz, Kelly Maxwell, Sanjeev Pathak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONRemlifanserin, or ACP‐204, is a potent serotonin 2A receptor inverse agonist under investigation for treatment of Alzheimer's disease psychosis and Lewy body dementia psychosis. A population pharmacokinetics (popPK) model for remlifanserin was developed to evaluate the impact of covariates affecting pharmacokinetic variability and assess the clinical relevance of these factors in a virtual population.METHODSA popPK model was developed using remlifanserin plasma concentrations (n = 3935) from 209 participants across seven Phase 1 trials (10 to 180 mg, oral). Data were pooled and analyzed using non‐linear mixed‐effects modeling. Intrinsic (demographics, hepatic/renal function) and extrinsic (food effect) factors were assessed. Model evaluation included goodness‐of‐fit plots and prediction‐corrected visual predictive checks (pcVPCs). Interindividual variability was estimated for key parameters; residual variability was modeled using proportional error. Simulated steady‐state exposures (maximum plasma concentration at steady state [Cmax,ss] and area under the plasma concentration‐time curve over the 24‐h dosing interval at steady state [AUC0–24,ss]) in a virtual population (n = 1000; 60 mg/day) were derived using PK parameters sampled from the final model. Typical parameter estimates included apparent oral clearance (35.4 L/h), apparentoral volume of distribution (V/F) (male, 930 L; female, 799 L), absorption lag time (ALAG1) (fasted, 0.720 h; fed, 0.929 h)and absorption rate constant (Ka) (0.692/h). Statistically significant relationships were found between food and ALAG1 and between sex and V/F (P < 0.001); however, neither factor had a clinically relevant impact on Cmax,ss or AUC0–24,ss.DISCUSSIONThe pharmacokinetics of oral remlifanserin (10 to 180 mg) are well described by dose‐proportional linear kinetics. BACKGROUND

Psychosis, a neuropsychiatric condition marked by delusions and/or hallucinations, is frequently observed during the progression of Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementias (i.e., dementia with Lewy bodies and dementia related to Parkinson's disease). 1 Depending on the type of dementia and the stage of disease progression, approximately 10% to 75% of patients may experience dementia‐related psychosis. 2 Patients with dementia and psychosis experience a lower quality of life, higher rates of nursing home placement, and more rapid cognitive decline compared to those without psychosis. 3 Additionally, the co‐occurrence of dementia and psychosis can significantly increase caregiver burden. 3 Pharmacotherapy for dementia‐related psychosis traditionally involves off‐label use of typical or atypical antipsychotics; however, the use of antipsychotics is limited by modest short‐term benefits and adverse effects, such as worsening cognition, extrapyramidal symptoms, sedation, falls, and metabolic abnormalities. 3 Development of novel pharmacological treatments for Alzheimer's disease psychosis (ADP) and other forms of dementia‐related psychosis includes a focus on selective 5‐hydroxytryptamine (serotonin) subtype 2A (5‐HT2A) receptor inverse agonists without appreciable activity at other receptors. Targeted activity at 5‐HT2A receptors without affecting adrenergic, histaminergic, muscarinic, or dopaminergic systems may considerably reduce the adverse effects typically observed when these receptors are activated, thereby improving tolerability.

Remlifanserin, or ACP‐204 (3‐[4‐cyclopropoxybenzyl]‐1‐[2,4‐difluorobenzyl]‐1‐[1‐methylpiperidin‐4‐yl]urea), is a highly potent and selective inverse agonist of 5‐HT2A receptors. Remlifanserin builds upon the scientific data from pimavanserin, a selective inverse agonist and antagonist of 5‐HT2A receptors, aiming to preserve or exceed its therapeutic efficacy in treating psychosis while enhancing safety. Pimavanserin has been marketed in the United States since 2016 for the treatment of Parkinson's disease psychosis (PDP) 4 and has demonstrated consistent efficacy in psychosis related to both dementia and Parkinson's disease. 3 , 5 It has also shown consistent and clinically meaningful efficacy relative to placebo in the reduction of psychosis symptoms and the prevention of relapse in patients with ADP without negatively affecting cognition or causing new onset or worsening of motor function impairment. 3 , 6 The potential risk of QT interval prolongation was identified during the development of pimavanserin, with significant prolongation observed at double the therapeutic dose for PDP. 7 This risk precluded the use of higher doses, despite the potential for greater efficacy identified with higher exposures in relation to a positive exposure‐response curve in other neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia 8 or ADP and Lewy body dementia psychosis (LBDP). Remlifanserin has substantially lower affinity than pimavanserin for ion channels associated with cardiovascular function, suggesting a lower risk of QT prolongation. Additionally, its potentially shorter half‐life may allow for faster onset of action and greater dosing flexibility (Burstein et al., J Pharmacol Exp Ther, 2026; article in press). Currently, remlifanserin is being evaluated for efficacy in ADP and LBDP.

This analysis aims to develop a population pharmacokinetic (popPK) model for remlifanserin using pooled concentration data from all available Phase 1 studies and to generate individual exposure measures from the final model to assess the clinical relevance of covariate effects. Additionally, this analysis seeks to identify the role of intrinsic and extrinsic factors during early development to guide dose selection for vulnerable populations, such as patients with Alzheimer's disease.

METHODS

Study design and the pharmacokinetic (PK) population

The current popPK analysis is based on a pooled dataset derived from all available Phase 1 clinical studies of remlifanserin conducted to date. These studies included full PK sampling and were carried out under standard Phase 1 controlled conditions. Each study was initially analyzed using non‐compartmental methods to derive preliminary PK parameter estimates, such as apparent oral clearance (CL/F) and apparent oral volume of distribution (V/F), which informed the starting values for model development.

The pooled dataset consisted of 3935 plasma concentration samples collected from 209 healthy adults, including individuals aged 18 to 55 years and healthy older adults aged 65 to 75 years. All doses were administered orally, ranging from 10 to 180 mg. A brief description of each study is provided in Table S1.

All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. The study centers involved in the original clinical trials received approval from local ethics committees, and informed consent was obtained from all volunteers. No new consent forms were required/collected for this analysis, as consent was obtained during the original clinical trials.

PK sampling

Plasma concentrations of remlifanserin were determined using a validated liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry assay. The lower limit of quantitation was 0.10 ng/mL. Plasma concentrations below the lower limit of quantitation (1.3%) were classified as missing and excluded from the analysis.

PopPK analysis and base model development

All exploratory data analyses and presentations of data were performed using SAS (version 9.4) and R (version 4.3.2). The popPK model was developed using the non‐linear mixed‐effects modeling software NONMEM (version 7.5), implementing a first‐order conditional estimation method with interaction, interfaced with KIWI (version 4.0) (see Supplemental Methods for model code). Evaluations included one‐, two‐, and three‐compartment models; the best‐performing model was assessed with different absorption processes (i.e., first‐order absorption with and without lag time). Log‐normal distributions of the parameters were assumed due to biological plausibility. The PK data used to develop the model were obtained exclusively through oral dosing; therefore, the oral bioavailability fraction (F), which represents the fraction of the administered dose that reaches systemic circulation, could not be directly estimated. Accordingly, model estimates utilized apparent values for clearance and volume of distribution. Dose‐normalized relative bioavailability estimations were used when potential dose‐dependent bioavailability was indicated. Interindividual variability (IIV) was examined for key parameters, including CL/F, apparent intercompartmental clearance (Q/F), V/F, apparent oral volume of distribution of the peripheral compartment (VP/F), absorption rate constant (Ka), and absorption lag time (ALAG1). Additive, proportional, log error, and combined additive plus proportional error models were tested for their ability to describe residual variability in remlifanserin plasma concentrations. Goodness‐of‐fit diagnostics, precision of the parameter estimates, reduction in IIV, and residual variability estimates were used to assess different models.

Covariate analysis and final model evaluation

Covariates likely to impact remlifanserin PK were explored for their relationships with key remlifanserin PK parameters (Tables S2 and S3). Intrinsic covariates examined included demographics (age, body weight, body mass index [BMI], race, and sex), renal function (baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], baseline renal function category 9 ), and liver function (baseline liver function category). 10 The extrinsic covariate examined was the effect of food (fed/fasted status). Diagnostic plots illustrating the relationship between covariates and the unexplained IIV parameter were evaluated to identify possible trends and the appropriate functional form for testing. Linear and power functions were tested for continuous variables; additive and proportional shifts were tested for categorical variables.

Covariate effects were evaluated using stepwise forward selection, followed by backward elimination. In each step, covariates contributing at least a 6.64 change in the value of the objective function (VOF; α = 0.01, 1 degree of freedom [df] for χ 2 distribution) and at least a 5% reduction in IIV for the PK parameter of interest were included in the model during the forward selection process. During backward elimination, statistical significance was defined as a 10.83 increase in VOF upon removal of a covariate (α = 0.001, 1 df for χ 2 distribution).

Model validation

Prediction‐corrected visual predictive checks (pcVPCs) with 500 replicates were used to validate the model. The statistics of the 5th, 50th (median), and 95th percentiles of the distributions of simulated and observed remlifanserin concentrations were overlaid and visually compared. This technique facilitates the identification of possible model misspecification when stratified across a large range in dose and influential covariates. 11

PopPK model simulations

To assess the clinical relevance of identified significant covariates on steady‐state exposures of remlifanserin, maximum plasma concentration at steady state (Cmax,ss), and area under the plasma concentration‐time curve over 24 h (dosing interval) at steady state (AUC0–24,ss) were stochastically simulated using the final model (including the contribution of IIV) in a virtual population of 1000 participants receiving the target dose (60 mg/day). Random assignments of virtual participants to fed versus fasted status and to sex (male vs female) were performed in a 1:1 ratio. For each categorical covariate, 500 participants were simulated per group (e.g., 500 males vs 500 females; 500 fasted vs 500 fed). Simulations were conducted by setting typical popPK parameters to parameter estimates of the final model, fixing the variance of IIV in the parameters, and randomly sampling from the variance distribution with a mean of 0. The covariance of highly correlated IIV terms was incorporated into the simulation. Covariate impact was evaluated and presented in a forest plot using geometric mean ratios and 90% confidence intervals (CIs), with clinically relevant bounds set at 0.8 and 1.25. 12

Dose proportionality

To assess the dose proportionality of remlifanserin, PK exposure parameters AUC0–24 and Cmax underwent natural log (ln) transformation using the power model: ln(Y) = intercept + βln(dose), where Y is the PK parameter evaluated, and the estimated exponent (β) is a measure of the dose proportionality. The proportionality relationship between PK parameters and dose was confirmed if the 90% CIs of the slope β were within the predefined range: (1 + [ln(θL)/ln(θr)], 1 + [ln(θH)/ln(θr)]), where θL = 0.8, θH = 1.25, and r is the ratio of the highest dose to the lowest dose. 13

RESULTS

Final popPK model description

The final popPK model for remlifanserin was a one‐compartment model with first‐order absorption with lag time and linear elimination. Exploratory data analysis indicated a primarily mono‐exponential decline in remlifanserin post‐dose concentrations over time, suggesting that a one‐compartment model is preferable for simplicity and could provide a better fit for sparse data in future studies. IIV was estimated for Ka, ALAG1, CL/F, and V/F; covariance was estimated between IIV for CL/F and IIV for V/F. Residual variability was characterized using a proportional error model. Final popPK model estimates

The estimated population mean Ka of 0.692/h describes a first‐order absorption half‐life of approximately 1 h for a typical participant. The estimated population mean for ALAG1 is 0.720 h under the fasted state and is predicted to increase to 0.929 h with food intake. CL/F for a typical participant is estimated to be 35.4 L/h. V/F is predicted to be 930 L for a typical male participant and 799 L for a typical female participant (Table 1).

Safety of remlifanserin

Remlifanserin was well tolerated throughout all studies included in this dataset; no serious treatment‐emergent adverse events or discontinuations due to active treatment were reported. Furthermore, no clinically significant effect of remlifanserin on electrocardiogram parameters or cases of meaningful increase in the corrected QT interval using Fridericia's correction method (QTcF) were noted across the studied doses; specifically, no QTcF prolongation was observed up to 180 mg.

DISCUSSION

Here, we report a pooled popPK analysis of Phase 1 clinical trials in healthy volunteers who received various doses of remlifanserin, a novel 5‐HT2A inverse agonist currently under investigation for patients with ADP and LBDP. Following oral administration of remlifanserin at doses ranging from 10 to 180 mg, the PK of remlifanserin is adequately described by a one‐compartment model with first‐order absorption with lag time and linear elimination kinetics. Additionally, the PK parameters estimated by both the non‐compartmental methods and popPK analysis were consistent, further supporting the selected model structure and parameterization. The goodness‐of‐fit and pcVPC diagnostic plots indicate that the final popPK model sufficiently characterizes the central tendency and magnitude of variability in observed remlifanserin concentrations within the overall analysis population.

During compartmental analysis, the conditional weighted residual versus time plot and the pcVPC using a one‐compartment model indicated a slight underprediction beyond day 8. At this point, concentrations approach the limit of quantification and are not relevant to steady‐state concentrations following once‐daily dosing. Although the two‐compartment model provided a marginally better fit, the added complexity was not justified. The one‐compartment model aligns well with the observed mono‐exponential decline in the data and is expected to provide sufficient predictability for the Phase 3 once‐daily dosing regimen. Therefore, it was retained based on parsimony and alignment with the observed data.

Clinical implications

PopPK analysis supports once‐daily dosing of remlifanserin (60 mg, oral) across all patient demographics investigated, with or without food. The slight shift in Tmax observed following food intake is not expected to affect efficacy, as remlifanserin is intended for chronic use, and a slight delay in Tmax (∼13 min) is not clinically meaningful. Inclusion of PK assessments in the elderly population is essential for determining the appropriate therapeutic dose in ADP and LBDP, which are generally more prevalent in the geriatric population. 1 , 14 Additionally, the once‐daily regimen may be preferable for elderly patients with several comorbid conditions who encounter difficulties adhering to multiple daily medication schedules or already experience substantial pill burden. Importantly, remlifanserin demonstrated good tolerability across all studies in this dataset, including those involving the elderly population. Although additional Phase 2 and 3 studies are needed to confirm these findings in disease populations of interest, such as patients with ADP or LBDP, this analysis aims to maximize potential efficacy while minimizing safety risks in the elderly population.

Comparisons to pimavanserin

At daily doses of 60 mg of remlifanserin, participants achieved steady‐state exposure levels within 6 days. By contrast, pimavanserin, another 5‐HT2A inverse agonist and antagonist, requires approximately 12 days to reach steady‐state levels at its highest clinical once‐daily dose of 34 mg. 4 While the onset of action corresponds to the time required to reach the minimum effective concentration at the site of action, the time to steady state represents the point at which systemic exposure stabilizes. While exposure–response analyses have not been conducted for remlifanserin, supportive evidence is provided by its shared mechanism of action with pimavanserin, for which exposure–response analyses across multiple indications have consistently demonstrated higher clinical responses with increasing exposure. Pimavanserin, which is approved for the treatment of PDP, was evaluated in a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study in patients with ADP. 6 The study achieved its primary endpoint, showing separation from the placebo group at week 6. However, this separation was not observed at earlier time points, possibly due to the longer time required for pimavanserin to reach a steady state. A medication with a faster onset is desirable, and remlifanserin has the potential to offer this benefit by achieving steady‐state concentrations more rapidly. This potential clinical advantage should be rigorously validated in upcoming Phase 2 and 3 studies, particularly by evaluating efficacy at early time points.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The clinical data analyzed were collected from Phase 1 trials in healthy participants, the majority of whom had normal liver and renal function. Additionally, most participants were predominantly young, White male adults. The total number of elderly participants (aged 65 to 75 years) included was small (n = 18). These demographic and clinical characteristics may not fully represent the intended geriatric patient population, potentially limiting the generalizability of the popPK simulations conducted in this analysis. This limitation is especially relevant when considering factors such as advanced age (≥80 years), impaired renal function, polypharmacy, and comorbidities. Nonetheless, the primary purpose of the initial popPK model was to support early dose selection decisions. The model will be continuously updated and refined as PK samples from the target patient population (i.e., ADP and LBDP) become available.

CONCLUSIONS

Remlifanserin PK is well described by dose‐proportional, linear kinetics. Although food effect and sex were identified as statistically significant categorical covariates influencing remlifanserin PK parameters, neither factor demonstrated a clinically relevant impact on steady‐state exposure. At the target therapeutic dose of 60 mg, remlifanserin was well tolerated across populations within this dataset, including the elderly. These findings underscore the necessity for further Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies in the intended patient populations of ADP and LBDP.
