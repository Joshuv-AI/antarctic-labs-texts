Distilling structural reasoning: efficient semantic parsing via chain-of-thought rationalization and contrastive demonstration selection
Authors: Seyed Hossein Ahmadpanah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to use hierarchical semantic parsing, models must be able to generalize to complex, compositional logic structures in addition to comprehending natural language. Although chain-of-thought prompting has produced state-of-the-art results for large language models, two major obstacles to their deployment are structural hallucinations resulting from surface-level demonstration retrieval and prohibitive inference latency. This paper introduces a neuro-symbolic framework called distilled structural reasoning, which uses semantic fragment decoding to overcome these bottlenecks. We present three new contributions: (1) contrastive fragment selection, a retrieval mechanism trained to separate lexical noise from structural intent, allowing zero-shot schema adaptation; (2) trie-based grammar constraints, which remove syntax errors during decoding; and (3) a parser-critic feedback loop for semantic self-correction. Most importantly, we suggest a rationale distillation protocol that converts a 70B-parameter teacher’s structural reasoning traces into a compact 8B-parameter student. Our framework achieves a new state-of-the-art for open-source models (87.42% exact match on TOP), outperforming standard supervised baselines and reducing inference latency by 9.4× when compared to the teacher model, according to extensive experiments on TOP, TOPv2, ATIS, and SNIPS.Keywords: Hierarchical semantic parsing, Chain-of-thought distillation, Neuro-symbolic AI, Contrastive representation learning, Compositional generalizationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Modern virtual assistants and task-oriented dialogue systems rely on hierarchical semantic parsing (HSP) as their fundamental interface to translate natural language queries into executable, nested logical forms1,2. HSP requires models to understand recursive structures where intents and slots are compositionally embedded, in contrast to flat slot-filling tasks. An example of this would be differentiating a destination city from a departure city within a complex, multi-leg flight query. Large language models (LLMs) have recently replaced specialized sequence-to-sequence architectures3,4, and this paradigm shift has produced notable improvements in handling such compositional generalization, mainly through in-context learning (ICL)5.

However, there are two major obstacles to the use of LLMs for semantic parsing in real-time applications: structural hallucinations resulting from poor demonstration selection and prohibitive inference latency. First, surface-level lexical similarity (e.g., BM25 or cosine similarity of sentence embeddings) is usually used by standard ICL approaches to retrieve demonstrations6. These heuristics work well for straightforward queries, but they are unable to capture the structural isomorphism needed for intricate parsing. For example, there is a lot of lexical overlap between the queries “Book a flight to Boston” and “Book a flight from Boston,” but they require essentially different semantic fragment hierarchies. When a retriever disregards these structural differences, it frequently gives the LLM false context, which propagates mistakes into the logical form that is produced.

Second, chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting7 increases the computational cost even though it has been demonstrated to improve reasoning by breaking down difficult tasks into intermediate steps. For latency-sensitive user interfaces, the latency introduced by generating verbose reasoning traces using models with billions of parameters (such as GPT-4 or Llama-2-70B) is intolerable. As a result, there is a dichotomy: large models are too inefficient for scale, while small models lack the reasoning depth to parse intricate recursive structures.

We introduce a novel neuro-symbolic framework that combines rationale distillation and contrastive demonstration selection to close this gap. We contend that decoding semantic fragments–atomic intent-slot units–is the key to robust HSP rather than parsing entire sentences as monolithic strings. In order to ensure that the prompts given to the LLM maximize structural coverage rather than just lexical matching, we introduce a retrieval mechanism trained via contrastive learning to differentiate between semantically similar but structurally distinct fragments.

A structural chain-of-thought (CoT) mechanism that linearizes the parsing tree into a depth-first decoding sequence is also proposed. Importantly, we view this high-resource CoT generation as a rich supervision signal rather than the ultimate inference technique. We transfer the structural reasoning capabilities of a large “Teacher” model into a compact “Student” model (e.g., Llama-3-8B) by using step-by-step distillation. The student successfully internalizes the reasoning process while retaining a high inference speed by learning to predict both the intermediate semantic fragments and the final logical form.

Related work

Supervised grammar-based methods have given way to few-shot generation using large language models (LLMs) in the field of hierarchical semantic parsing (HSP). This section compares these approaches with our suggested neuro-symbolic distillation framework after reviewing their development with an emphasis on demonstration selection and reasoning efficiency.

Semantic parsing and in-context learning

Early methods for semantic parsing mainly relied on recursive insertion-based encoders8 or supervised sequence-to-sequence models with pointer networks2. Although these models work well within their training distribution, they struggle with compositional generalization to unseen semantic structures and require large annotated datasets9.

In-context learning (ICL) emerged as a promising substitute for low-resource parsing with the introduction of large language models (LLMs). LLMs were able to complete tasks with just a few examples in the prompt, as shown by Brown et al.10. In particular, Shin and Van Durme5 used constrained decoding to map natural language to logical forms for semantic parsing. However, the caliber and applicability of the given demonstrations have a significant impact on ICL performance11.

The majority of current demonstration selection techniques rely on unsupervised similarity metrics. The standard baseline12 is K-nearest neighbor (KNN) retrieval using sentence-BERT embeddings13. More sophisticated techniques, like Cover-LS14, try to optimize the coverage of local structural patterns. Nevertheless, these techniques frequently fall short in differentiating between utterances with similar lexical overlap but different hierarchical intent (e.g., “arriving at” vs. “departing from”). In contrast to these methods, our contrastive fragment selection reduces the retrieval of false positives by explicitly learning a metric space where structural differences are amplified.

Chain-of-thought reasoning in parsing

The model is prompted by Standard ICL to directly generate the final logical form (X → Y). Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting was introduced by Wei et al.7, who demonstrated that producing intermediate reasoning steps () greatly enhances performance on complex reasoning tasks. When it comes to parsing, techniques such as least-to-most prompting15 break down complicated queries into smaller ones.

While decomposition improves accuracy, it presents two difficulties for HSP. First, “hallucinated structures” that defy the target formal language’s strict schema may result from unrestricted CoT generation. Second, CoT prompts are unsuitable for real-time latency-sensitive applications due to their verbosity and high computational overhead. Our work ensures that the advantages of structural reasoning are maintained without the inference cost by addressing the former through semantic fragment decoding and the latter through distillation.

Knowledge distillation and rationalization

Traditionally, knowledge distillation (KD) entails training a small “student” model to replicate the output probability distribution (logits) of a large “teacher” model16. However, standard KD frequently fails in tasks that require a lot of reasoning because the student mimics the solution without comprehending the underlying logic.

Training students on the teacher’s reasoning traces (CoT) improves generalization, according to recent developments in rationale distillation or step-by-step distillation17,18. For example, Ho et al.19 refined smaller models on GPT-3-generated reasoning paths for arithmetic tasks. This paradigm is extended to hierarchical semantic parsing in our work. We distill structural reasoning–training the student to predict particular semantic fragments in a depth-first traversal order, in contrast to earlier works that treat reasoning as unstructured text. As a result, the student model becomes a structural reasoner rather than just a pattern matcher.

Nguyen et al.20, who showed that dissecting hierarchical structures into atomic units greatly improves large language model performance, introduced the fundamental idea of semantic fragment decomposition, which our framework builds upon. For demonstration retrieval, however, their CombineSF method depends on heuristic combinations of lexical overlap metrics (like Jaccard and ROUGE scores) and logical token classification. We improve this approach by substituting these static heuristics with contrastive fragment selection, which explicitly learns to separate structural intent from surface-level lexical noise, thereby resolving ambiguities that heuristic methods frequently confuse, like the difference between “flights to” and “flights from”. Additionally, we repurpose these structural reasoning traces as supervision signals for Rationale Distillation, whereas Nguyen et al. primarily use chain-of-thought prompting as an inference-time mechanism, resulting in major computational overhead. By transferring the reasoning skills of a 70B-parameter teacher to an 8B-parameter student, we are able to achieve comparable accuracy with a 9.4× reduction in latency and strictly enforce syntactic validity through our novel trie-based constraints, a safeguard lacking in their probabilistic generation pipeline.

An atomic unit of this tree that consists of a non-terminal node (Intent or Slot) and its corresponding span in x is called a Semantic Fragment .

Learning a mapping function is the goal. This procedure is broken down into an intermediate reasoning step in our framework, producing a series of fragments (the structural rationale) r, so that the model maximizes .

Experimental setup and results

In this section, we assess the proposed framework in three ways: (1) parsing accuracy, which compares our distilled student to the most advanced supervised and few-shot baselines; (2) retrieval efficacy, which isolates the effect of contrastive fragment selection (CFS); and (3) efficiency trade-offs, which measures the latency reduction attained through rationale distillation.

Datasets and metrics

We test our framework on four well-known benchmarks in hierarchical semantic parsing to fully evaluate its robustness and generalizability. The TOP dataset1, which includes about 44,000 queries in the navigation and event domains and is notable for its deep and nested semantic structures, is used for the primary evaluation. We extend our analysis to TOPv222, specifically isolating the Reminder and Weather domains to test the model on unseen schemas, in order to investigate domain adaptability and zero-shot transfer capabilities. We further validate our method using the SNIPS dataset23, which verifies performance on simpler, shallower hierarchical architectures, and the Airline Travel Information Systems (ATIS) dataset24, a rigorous benchmark known for complex constraints within the flight booking domain. The main metric used in quantitative evaluation is Exact Match (EM) accuracy, which imposes a stringent requirement that a prediction is only deemed accurate if the generated semantic tree as a whole is isomorphic to the ground truth. Tree-F1 scores, which offer a detailed assessment of the structural validity of distinct semantic fragments, complement this.

Baselines and model configurations

In order to completely evaluate both performance and efficiency, our comparative analysis places the suggested framework against three different types of baselines. The first category includes conventional fully supervised methods, namely a BART-Base model and the pointer-generator architecture (Seq2Seq-PTR)2, both of which have been refined on the entire training corpus to reflect the state-of-the-art in pre-LLM semantic parsing. The teacher-scale in-context learning (ICL) paradigm is represented by the second category, which uses massive foundation models like Llama-2-70B and GPT-4. These models are used as high-resource performance upper bounds because they are prompted using standard BM25 lexical retrieval and traditional chain-of-thought techniques. Finally, to isolate the specific efficacy of our structural rationale distillation, we evaluate a distilled baseline comprising a Llama-3-8B model fine-tuned via standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on direct input-output pairs, devoid of the intermediate structural reasoning traces used in our proposed method.

Zero-shot domain transfer

We tested our schema-agnostic retrieval on the Weather domain of TOPv2 without fine-tuning on weather samples in order to evaluate its generalization capability. Only structurally similar fragments from other domains (such as Reminder or Navigation) were retrieved by the model. Our approach outperformed the baseline’s fixed-classifier approach (42.3%), which was unable to map unseen slots, with a Zero-Shot EM of 71.4%.

Discussion

An 8B-parameter model can match the performance of a 70B-parameter teacher when structural rationales are incorporated into the training pipeline, as shown by the experimental results in Section "Experimental setup and results". This section discusses the theoretical implications for compositional generalization, examines the mechanistic role of our particular contributions (constrained decoding and contrastive selection), and interprets these results through the lens of neuro-symbolic AI.

From system 2 to system 1: the distillation of reasoning

Whether a lack of capacity or inadequate supervision causes smaller models to fail at complex tasks is a key question in current LLM research. The latter is supported by our results. Targeting the final logical form y, standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) views the parsing process as a black-box mapping x → y. Because of this, the model must implicitly learn the hierarchical decomposition rules within its internal weights–a task that is frequently too difficult for smaller architectures.

We explicitly expose the decomposition algorithm, namely the depth-first search traversal logic, to the student model through training on the Structural chain-of-thought (). This effectively combines the teacher’s “System 2” planned reasoning process with the student’s “System 1” intuitive inference. Our distilled model’s high accuracy (87.42% on TOP) indicates that the student has internalized the procedure of semantic fragment decoding rather than just memorizing the outputs. This is consistent with recent research on “Process Supervision,” which demonstrates that reasoning traces are a more transferable and dense signal than final labels alone.

De-biasing retrieval via contrastive learning

A critical weakness in existing RAG systems is highlighted by the notable performance difference (+5.8% improvement) between our contrastive fragment selection (CFS) and standard Sentence-BERT retrieval: lexical bias. Sentences are grouped by common keywords using standard dense retrievers. However, keywords frequently detract from the structural intent in HSP. For example, “Cancel my flight” and “Cancel my alarm” have a high lexical overlap, but they map to completely different sub-trees ([IN:CANCEL_FLIGHT] vs. [IN:DELETE_ALARM]).

When the underlying schema diverges, this lexical overlap is explicitly penalized by our contrastive loss function. CFS guarantees that the LLM is prompted with demonstrations that are isomorphic to the structure of the query by making the embedding space align with the semantic fragments instead of surface text. The main cause of the lower rates of hallucinations seen in our ablation studies is this structural alignment.

The role of constraints and feedback loops

Two particular novelties–the parser-critic loop (Novelty 4) and trie-based grammar constraints (Novelty 2)–were crucial in resolving the “last mile” issue of valid tree generation, according to our error analysis.

Constraining hallucinations

Unconstrained LLMs sometimes produce non-existent slots (e.g., creating a [SL:AIRLINE_CODE] slot in a domain that only supports [SL:AIRLINE_NAME]]), even though the CoT rationale directs the model toward the correct intent. 100% of these syntax errors were removed with the use of Trie-based logit masking. This implies that pure probabilistic sampling is inadequate for formal language generation; neuro-symbolic constraints are required to ensure execution safety.

Self-correction via reconstruction

Semantic consistency was protected by the parser-critic loop. Because the reconstructed natural language did not match the input query semantic, the Critic rejected the initial parse in about 4% of test cases. For instance, the first parse of the query “Book a flight for three people” failed to include the quantity slot. When the “three” was absent from the reconstruction, the Critic initiated a regeneration. In order to ensure semantic fidelity, this cycle-consistency check turns out to be a computationally efficient substitute for beam search.

Limitations and future directions

Even with these improvements, our framework has drawbacks. First, the computational cost of training is non-trivial; it takes thousands of inference hours on a 70B model to generate the teacher-synthesized dataset, but this is a one-time expense that is spread out over the student’s effective deployment. Second, we discovered that a fixed DFS strategy was adequate for 90% of queries, despite the potential of our RL-driven adaptive traversal (Novelty 3). Only the deepest trees (depth > 4) justified the cost of learning a policy network. Future research will concentrate on Continual Learning, examining whether the Student model can retain the structural reasoning patterns acquired from the original domains while adapting to new semantic schemas (such as a new “Healthcare” domain).

Conclusion

In this work, we tackled the crucial conflict in hierarchical semantic parsing (HSP) between compositional generalization and inference efficiency. Large Language Models have shown impressive reasoning abilities with chain-of-thought prompting, but their use in real-time systems has been limited by their dependence on structurally agnostic retrieval heuristics and prohibitive latency.

We presented Distilled Structural Reasoning, a unified neuro-symbolic framework that essentially redefines parsing. We were able to separate structural intent from surface-level lexical noise by using contrastive fragment selection (CFS) and changing the atomic unit of analysis from complete sentences to Semantic Fragments. This made it possible for us to obtain demonstrations that optimize structural isomorphism, allowing for zero-shot adaptation to previously unseen domains without the need for particular fine-tuning.

Additionally, our structural CoT distillation protocol showed that it is possible to efficiently compress the reasoning power of large foundation models (70B parameters) into small student models (8B parameters). We developed a system that offers a 9.4× reduction in inference latency while maintaining 99.4% of the teacher’s accuracy by teaching the student about the process of decomposition rather than just the final logical form. Structural hallucinations were effectively eliminated by integrating parser-critic loop and trie-based grammar constraints, which further ensured that this efficiency did not come at the expense of validity.

The future of task-oriented AI will be greatly affected by these findings. They propose that smaller, more specialized models can function as reliable, effective neuro-symbolic parsers when they are guided by the rigorous reasoning traces of larger models. In order to enable the student model to dynamically acquire new semantic schemas from user interaction without catastrophic forgetting, future work will expand this framework to multilingual settings and explore continual learning strategies. We hope that this work encourages more investigation into the complementary nature of large language models’ fluid reasoning and structured knowledge representation.
