Background

Continuing medical education (CME) plays a critical role in ensuring that health professionals remain competent, adaptable and able to respond to evolving population health needs. Defined as a lifelong process of professional development, CME supports the maintenance and improvement of knowledge, skills and attitudes essential for effective medical practice [1]. International organizations, including the World Health Organization (WHO), emphasize CME/CPD as a key component of health systems strengthening and an essential mechanism for preparing the health workforce to respond to public health challenges [2, 3]. Modern CME methods increasingly rely on digital and distance learning. Over the last 2 decades, traditional face-to-face lectures have been complemented by online platforms, interactive webinars, simulation-based training and blended learning models [4]. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic accelerated the global shift toward online CME, forcing medical institutions and professional societies to adopt innovative approaches at remarkable speed [5, 6]. In parallel, other health emergencies, such as humanitarian crises and armed conflicts, highlighted the importance of scalable, flexible and context-sensitive CME/CPD that can be delivered despite infrastructure and mobility constraints [7, 8]. Armenia provides an illustrative case study of this transformation. Importantly, the dual-crisis occurred during a critical phase of national CME/CPD legislative reform and contributed to the rapid adoption of digital educational approaches (Fig. 1). During the COVID-19 pandemic and 44-day armed conflict in Nagorno-Karabakh (“Second Nagorno-Karabakh War”) in 2020, the Armenian health system shifted CME activities rapidly into online and hybrid formats. This adaptation allowed for the continuation of professional training under difficult circumstances, ensuring the dissemination of updated clinical guidelines and public health measures. Consequently, this case study analyzed the experience of Armenia in organizing CME/CPD during and after health emergencies, using international experiences as contextual reference points for interpretation and policy reflection.

Case presentation

This study deliberately adopts a single-country retrospective case study design rather than a conventional cross-national comparative framework. This methodological choice is necessitated by the notable, dual-crisis paradigm of the Armenian experience in 2020 (the concurrent management of the COVID-19 pandemic and the 44-day armed conflict), which resists systematic benchmarking against standard healthcare trajectories. While the analysis focuses strictly on the internal adaptive resilience of the national system and the non-traditional mobilization of the global Armenian medical diaspora, relevant international literature and global digital CME trends are integrated systematically to contextualize and validate these national findings. For Armenia, quantitative data on CME/CPD activities (number of trainings, format, and participation rates) were collected from institutional records. International literature was reviewed to contextualize the Armenian experience within broader global developments in CME/CPD during health emergencies. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, while graphical visualizations illustrate trends over time.

It is critical to note that the CME/CPD activities analyzed in this case study were strictly emergency-driven, rapid responses to physical distancing mandates, infrastructural collapses, and simultaneous health shocks. Consequently, these data streams cannot be directly compared or conflated with routine, pre- or post-crisis CME/CPD participation metrics. Due to the introduction of the concurrent CME/CPD legislative reforms and mobility restrictions, these training cohorts represent an isolated, context-specific systemic intervention rather than an evolution of standard professional development schedules. To maintain regulatory validity, our analysis focuses on measuring the internal saturation and structural reach of these emergency programs within the active workforce baseline during the crisis, rather than making historical longitudinal comparisons.

Comparisons with global experiences

The rapid transition to online CME in Armenia mirrors patterns reported worldwide during COVID-19. In multiple countries, digital platforms were leveraged to maintain workforce competence despite lockdowns and disruptions [6]. International experience similarly demonstrated a rapid expansion of online medical education and continuing professional development activities during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, reflecting the need for timely dissemination of emerging clinical guidance [6, 9]. The engagement of diaspora and international experts may have facilitated the rapid expansion of online CME activities during the crisis period.

Regulatory framework in Armenia

Historically, Armenia’s continuous education relied on the traditional post-Soviet model, which mandated rigid, centralized training courses every 5 years specifically, 7-week cycles for physicians and 5-week cycles for nursing staff. To align with European regulatory frameworks and modern quality standards, a landmark shift was initiated through the amendments to the “Law On Medical Care and Services for the Population”, which were enacted in 2015 and officially entered into force in 2016 [10]. This reform structured the transition away from the post-Soviet architecture toward a dynamic, continuous CME/CPD credit-accumulation and periodic healthcare professional certification system. To operationalize this model, a foundational decree was enacted by the Minister of Health in 2019, which has since undergone several rapid incorporations and amendments [11]․

This rapid regulatory evolution provides a compelling example of how acute crises can simultaneously disrupt and accelerate institutional reforms. On one hand, the operational realities of managing a nationwide pandemic alongside an active armed conflict forced the Ministry of Health to repeatedly shift its administrative priorities, officially postponing the final legal deadline for comprehensive healthcare workforce certification from January 2021 to January 2023 [12]. On the other hand, the absolute necessity of physical distancing combined with emergency upskilling needs contributed to accelerated policy adaptation. It prompted the introduction of more flexible legislative and regulatory approaches, to explicitly formalize distance education methodologies, establish criteria for online course accreditation, and integrate distance learning channels into the newly developing national CME/CPD framework.

Despite the expansion of online CME, several systemic gaps remain. Armenia lacks a comprehensive regulatory framework for online CME quality assurance, performance monitoring tools, and ensuring content alignment with the health system [3, 13].

The findings of this study underscore the transformative role of health emergencies—most notably the COVID-19 pandemic and the 44-day armed conflict—in accelerating the transition to online CME/CPD in Armenia. The rapid growth of accredited CME programs since 2020 reflects both an urgent need to strengthen clinical preparedness and the capacity of digital platforms to support large-scale professional learning. This aligns with global evidence demonstrating that crises often act as catalysts for educational innovation, particularly in health systems under acute pressure [6]. At the same time, these developments took place during an important transition in Armenia’s CME/CPD system, as the country was moving toward a certification-based model for healthcare professionals. The large-scale use of online CME during the pandemic and the armed conflict not only ensured continuity of professional education under crisis conditions but also encouraged a broader reassessment of the role of digital learning within the national CME/CPD framework. In a setting where online medical education had previously been limited and not widely integrated into formal CME/CPD practice, the experience of the crisis period contributed to the later development and revision of CME/CPD regulations and implementation approaches related to online learning. This process was operationally regulated by Decree No. 20-N of the Minister of Health of the Republic of Armenia (dated April 26, 2019), which established the formal criteria for credit allocation and CME/CPD activities [11].

The fluctuations in CME/CPD accreditation observed in Armenia closely align with international patterns of professional development during the COVID-19 pandemic. Globally, there was a rapid shift from face-to-face to online and hybrid CME models, with participation in digital formats increasing markedly. A recent cross-sectional study further indicates that, while some professionals continue to favor in-person engagement, many participants—especially in resource-constrained conditions—express a clear preference for virtual or hybrid learning options due to convenience and reduced logistical constraints [13].

In regulatory terms, the concept of time-limited accreditation validity, widely adopted by bodies such as the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME), often sets a finite accreditation period—e.g., 2 years for provisional status—after which re-accreditation is required. This structure likely contributes to the sharp accreditation fluctuations observed in Armenia between 2022 and 2024 [14].

A distinctive feature of Armenia’s CME/CPD response was the integration of both national expertise and diaspora/international contributions. This model ensured contextual adaptation of global standards while facilitating knowledge exchange across borders. Such hybridized approaches have been reported in other conflict-affected or resource-constrained settings, where leveraging international partnerships can mitigate local capacity gaps [15].

Overall, these findings demonstrate how Armenia’s national CME/CPD trajectory aligns with international developments, illustrating that while the institutional response was inherently context-specific, it conceptually reflects broader global adaptations. The initial surge in accredited online CME programs during the pandemic suggests substantial structural changes in CME delivery. Nevertheless, several systemic gaps were identified. Despite the legal framework established in 2019 and subsequently amended to account for distance learning, Armenia lacks a comprehensive national CME/CPD policy that explicitly addresses digital education, quality assurance and outcome evaluation [16, 17]. Current monitoring mechanisms remain limited in their ability to assess pedagogical effectiveness or the impact of CME/CPD on clinical practice. These challenges are consistent with global findings highlighting the need for robust evaluation frameworks in digital health education.

Thematic analysis of training content further revealed an emphasis on infection prevention and control, diagnostics, and specialized care, reflecting immediate pandemic and conflict-related priorities. While this focus met urgent needs, it also demonstrated the reactive nature of CME/CPD under crisis conditions. Moving forward, Armenia’s CME/CPD system would benefit from shifting toward a more proactive and sustainable model, integrating resilience training, mental health support and preparedness for future emergencies into its educational agenda.

The Armenian experience demonstrates that health emergencies can accelerate the adoption of digital innovations in CME/CPD. The COVID-19 pandemic and armed conflict in the region highlighted the critical importance of flexible, accessible and scalable training systems for health workers. Armenia’s rapid shift to online CME enabled the continuity of professional development under extraordinary circumstances and provided valuable lessons for future resilience.

However, the experience also revealed systemic challenges, particularly in regulatory oversight, quality assurance, sustainability, and equitable access. Without stronger governance frameworks, the long-term effectiveness and consistency of expanding online CME may be difficult to sustain.

Armenia’s case illustrates both the opportunities and limitations of emergency-driven educational reform. The lessons learned point toward a hybrid model of CME that balances immediacy with sustainability, local adaptation with global alignment and short-term crisis response with long-term system strengthening. These insights may hold relevance not only for Armenia but also for other countries seeking to build resilient CME/CPD systems capable of responding to health emergencies while advancing CPD in more stable times.

Results

Chronological phases of emergency CME implementation

To fully understand these shifts, the temporal interactions of the dual crisis must be analyzed across three distinct structural phases (as conceptualized in Fig. 1 timeline):

Phase 1: Pure pandemic response (March 2020–September 2020): The sudden onset of COVID-19 necessitated a rapid pivot to distance formats, where content was strictly pandemic-driven, focusing on infection control and critical care.

Phase 2: Acute dual-crisis overlap (September 2020–November 2020): The outbreak of the 44-day armed conflict introduced a simultaneous shock. Educational priorities underwent a drastic tactical pivot; while pandemic protocols remained active, a heavy influx of trauma-focused, conflict-driven education was deployed.

Phase 3: Post-conflict and pandemic recovery (December 2020–2023): Acute trauma training subsided and CME topic areas transitioned toward long-term post-conflict rehabilitation, complex diagnostics, surgical innovations, and mental health support.

Discussion

As a descriptive national case study, the analysis focuses on organizational adaptation, regulatory developments and participation patterns rather than on the direct measurement of educational, clinical or workforce outcomes. During the COVID-19 pandemic and 44-day armed conflict-related disruptions, Armenia experienced a significant transformation of its CME delivery system. Two approaches, synchronous interactive training and asynchronous self-paced learning, formed the foundation of Armenia’s transition to distance CME. Both modalities remain highly attractive to health professionals: the former for its immediacy and interactivity and the latter for its convenience and long-term accessibility.

Together, these two approaches created a blended model of distance CME, combining the immediacy of live interaction with the flexibility of self-directed learning. Studies show that blended learning yields significantly higher knowledge acquisition compared to no-intervention or single-format instruction [19]. Direct comparisons reveal that both synchronous and asynchronous methods significantly improve learning outcomes, with synchronous formats exhibiting lower cognitive load [20, 21].

The substantial engagement of healthcare professionals via distance learning frameworks aligns with localized evaluative data from the same period, which demonstrated high participant satisfaction and a positive perception of educational utility during this digital transition [22]. This correlation suggests that the emergency-driven formats were not only structurally accessible but also operationally effective from the end-user perspective.

Systemic resilience and diaspora integration as a notable crisis model

The rapid structural adaptation of CME/CPD delivery during overlapping crises in Armenia provides critical insights into the broader concepts of health workforce resilience and emergency governance. In modern healthcare management systems, CME/CPD frameworks are increasingly recognized as important components of workforce development, organizational adaptability, and broader health system strengthening efforts [23, 24].

Evaluating Armenia’s digital CME/CPD transition highlights a distinct paradigm of health system resilience. The core differentiator was the synergistic interaction between the state’s internal adaptive mechanisms and the rapid, non-traditional mobilization of the international medical diaspora. During Phase 2, when the health system faced concurrent pandemic and conflict-driven shocks, the diaspora functioned as an external buffer. By organizing international virtual networks in partnership with national institutions, they bypassed physical constraints to deliver urgent, context-specific trauma and battlefield medicine protocols [8, 15].

Furthermore, the digital architecture served as a powerful equity-enabling mechanism. The recorded 62% regional engagement suggests that digital delivery may have improved access to CME/CPD opportunities for healthcare professionals practicing outside the capital, helping to reduce some geographic barriers to participation during the crisis period [25].

The COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a rapid expansion of online learning across all levels of education [6, 26]. It took approximately 2 years for virtual education to transition from the rapid development stage to a more mature and stable phase, marked by greater methodological diversity [27]. Our analysis focused specifically on the rapid development phase of online courses, in which the primary emphasis was on addressing urgent health challenges. The operational realities of managing a nationwide pandemic alongside an active armed conflict forced regulatory flexibility, driving sequential legislative adjustments to formalize distance education methodologies within the national framework.

Strategic transition to sustainable institutional models

It is essential to emphasize that these documented outcomes reflect a specific period of rapid, emergency-driven mobilization rather than a finalized institutional model. While this reactive phase successfully maintained educational continuity under acute duress, its true value lies in serving as a conceptual foundation for future health workforce development.

Transitioning from emergency-driven courses to a structured national architecture requires a deliberate focus on long-term capacity building. Future strategic progress must prioritize the establishment of robust technological infrastructures and the institutionalization of rigorous pedagogical methodologies [3, 28], seamlessly aligning distance learning with broader health system governance and professional standards [24, 25]. Further research is needed to examine the longer term effects of digitally enabled CME/CPD on professional practice, workforce development, and health workforce resilience.

Recommendations

The strategic roadmap outlined in Fig. 8 positions distance CME/CPD not only as a complementary educational modality but also as a potentially important component of national health workforce development. Aligning with these structured recommendations requires focused efforts across key operational areas: strengthening Governance and Regulation to institutionalize digital learning, enriching methodological support to guarantee high-quality instructional design and rigorous peer review and establishing stable institutional funding to maintain the necessary technological infrastructure. Implementation of these interventions may contribute to improved system resilience, ensure equitable access to professional development and support alignment with global accreditation and quality assurance benchmarks. Collectively, these elements may support the development of a more sustainable educational infrastructure, consistent governance oversight and reliable financing channels drives a sustainable educational architecture tailored to Armenia’s public health priorities and regional context.
