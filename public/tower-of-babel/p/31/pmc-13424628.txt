Machine Learning Clustering Identifies Achalasia-Spectrum Phenotypes in Distal Esophageal Spasm
Authors: Ofer Z Fass, John E Pandolfino, Dustin A Carlson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsDistal esophageal spasm (DES) is a rare, heterogeneous esophageal motility disorder with variable treatment responses. Machine learning methods are well suited to distinguish DES phenotypes that could inform therapeutic decisions and outcomes. This exploratory study aims to identify and characterize unique DES phenotypes using unsupervised machine learning clustering.MethodsAdults undergoing high-resolution esophageal manometry (HRM) for esophageal symptoms between 2005-2025 were included. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction of HRM metrics. DES patients were classified as within-cluster or outside-cluster according to density contours. Patient characteristics, manometric parameters, functional lumen imaging probe findings, and treatment outcomes were compared between clusters.ResultsAmong 5360 patients, 42 (0.8%) had DES, 2400 (45%) normal motility, and 107 (2.0%) type III achalasia. UMAP identified 33 DES patients clustering alongside type III achalasia patients, while 9 DES patients remained outside this cluster. The primary differentiating characteristic between clusters was premature supine contraction frequency (median 8 vs 0, P < 0.001). Within-cluster patients demonstrated higher Esophageal Hypervigilance and Anxiety Scale scores (30 vs 20.5, P = 0.010). All 3 within-cluster patients who underwent peroral endoscopic myotomy achieved symptom resolution, compared to partial response in outside-cluster patients.ConclusionsMachine learning clustering successfully identified a DES phenotype characterized by high premature contraction frequency that clusters with type III achalasia. Premature contraction frequency may serve as a clinical marker for selecting DES patients for lower esophageal sphincter-directed therapies. Given the rarity of DES, larger validation studies are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: Esophageal motility disorders; Esophageal spasm, diffuse; Natural orifice endoscopic surgery; Peroral endoscopic myotomy; Unsupervised machine learning

INTRODUCTION

Distal esophageal spasm (DES), defined by Chicago classification version 4.0 (CC v4.0) as ≥ 20% premature contractions without impaired esophagogastric junction (EGJ) relaxation, is a rare esophageal motor disorder with a prevalence of approximately 2% among patients undergoing high-resolution esophageal manometry (HRM) for esophageal symptoms.1,2 DES represents a heterogeneous diagnosis that can exist along a spectrum with type III achalasia or be associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and opioid use.3-6 Different phenotypes are thought to exist; however, traditional analytical methods have been insufficient to reliably identify these phenotypes.7 Furthermore, current treatment approaches show variable success rates and identifying these phenotypes could inform which patients would benefit from invasive interventions versus alternative treatment strategies.8-10

The advent of machine learning has introduced novel analytical approaches for identifying patterns and unique clusters within complex datasets that might not be otherwise apparent through traditional methods. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) is a dimensionality reduction technique that preserves local structure and relationships within complex datasets, thereby positioning it as a unique tool for identifying natural DES clusters.11

This single-center exploratory study aims to identify and characterize unique DES phenotypes using unsupervised machine learning algorithms to better delineate subtypes and inform subtype-specific treatment approaches.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

Adults ages 18-93 years presenting to our academic medical center for esophageal symptom evaluation between June 2005 and May 2025 were consecutively enrolled in an esophageal motility registry. All included subjects underwent HRM. Timed barium esophagram (TBE) and functional lumen imaging probe (FLIP) studies were performed at the treating gastroenterologist’s discretion when clinically indicated. No endoscopic or surgical interventions occurred between studies. Subjects with technically limited HRM studies were excluded. Those with prior foregut surgery or pneumatic dilation were also excluded, as these can cause secondary esophageal motor abnormalities. Given the exploratory nature of this study and to distinguish clinical phenotypes, patients with reflux esophagitis, opioid use, and hiatal hernia were retained, as these conditions are associated with shortened distal latency (DL) on HRM.3,5,6,12 The study protocol was approved by our university’s Institutional Review Board (STU00224876), and retrospective analysis of coded, de-identified motility data was conducted under a waiver of informed consent.

High-resolution Esophageal Manometry

After a minimum 6-hour fast, HRM was performed using a 4.2-mm outer diameter solid-state assembly with 36 circumferential pressure sensors at 1-cm intervals (Medtronic, Shoreview, MN, USA). The catheter was placed transnasally and positioned to record from the hypopharynx to the stomach with approximately 3 intragastric sensors. Following a 2-minute baseline recording, the CC v4.0 protocol was performed: ten 5-mL liquid swallows in the supine position followed by five 5-mL liquid swallows in the upright position.2,13,14

While studies before 2021 were performed using older Chicago Classification protocols, CC v4.0 diagnostic criteria were retrospectively applied to all studies for consistent comparison. Investigators were blinded to clinical characteristics and other test results. Median integrated relaxation pressure (IRP) thresholds were > 15 mmHg for supine swallows and > 12 mmHg for upright swallows. Cases with inconclusive HRM findings (esophagogastric junction outlet obstruction [EGJOO] or absent contractility with borderline IRP) lacking conclusive TBE or FLIP results were excluded due to clinical uncertainty.

Timed Barium Esophagram

For inconclusive HRM findings (EGJOO or absent contractility with borderline IRP), TBE was used for further classification.2 TBE was performed upright with 200 mL low-density barium sulfate, with anteroposterior images obtained at 1, 2, and 5 minutes.15 A 12.5-mm barium tablet was administered if significant retention was absent at 5 minutes. Abnormal TBE was defined as barium column height > 5 cm at 5 minutes or > 5 cm at 1 minute with tablet impaction, indicating conclusive EGJ outflow disorder. Normal TBE indicated normal EGJ outflow.16

Functional Lumen Imaging Probe

FLIP studies were performed during sedated endoscopy using a 16-cm catheter (EndoFlip EF-322N; Medtronic).17-19 Endoscopy was performed in left-lateral decubitus position with midazolam/fentanyl or propofol sedation at the anesthetist’s discretion. Sedation choice does not significantly impact FLIP motility findings relative to HRM diagnoses.20 After endoscope withdrawal and atmospheric pressure calibration, the FLIP catheter was placed transorally and positioned with 1-3 impedance sensors beyond the EGJ. Beginning at 40 mL, stepwise 10-mL distensions were performed to a target volume of 70 mL, with each volume maintained for 30-60 seconds.

FLIP data were analyzed using customized software (available open source at http://www.wklytics.com/nmgi) to generate FLIP Panometry plots.21 EGJ opening analysis excluded areas with dry catheter artifact and included the EGJ distensibility index (DI) at 60 mL and maximum EGJ diameter achieved during 60-mL or 70-mL fill volumes.21,22 Contractile response patterns were classified per the FLIP 2.0 Dallas Consensus.23

Evaluation of Treatments and Outcomes

Treatment, clinical course, and outcomes for all patients with a manometric diagnosis of DES were determined through manual chart review. Treatment modalities included lifestyle modifications, proton pump inhibitor therapy, nitrates, hyoscyamine, muscle relaxants/sildenafil, botulinum toxin injection, endoscopic dilation, peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), and surgical hernia repair.

Treatment outcomes were assessed using pre- and post-treatment patient-reported outcomes (PROs), when available. Patients undergoing motility testing were asked to complete pre-treatment questionnaires including the brief esophageal dysphagia questionnaire, Eckardt score, esophageal hypervigilance and anxiety scale (EHAS), and GERD questionnaire (GERDQ).24-27 Post-treatment questionnaire data was collected when repeat motility testing was performed. Post-treatment Eckardt scores were also sometimes available from clinical visit documentation. When PROs were unavailable, outcomes were assessed based on clinician-documented subjective improvement. Patients without subsequent gastroenterology visits after treatment initiation were considered lost to follow-up.

Clustering Method and Statistical Methods

UMAP was used for unsupervised dimensionality reduction, as it preserves local structure and relationships within complex datasets (Supplementary Method).11 Prior to UMAP application, numeric predictors were normalized, variables with > 40% missing data were removed, and remaining missing data was imputed using bootstrap aggregated decision tree models (Supplementary Figure). 28 HRM data were projected into 2-dimensional space using UMAP, and DES clusters were visually identified using density contours.

HRM metrics, patient-reported outcomes, and treatment outcomes were compared between DES cases within the primary cluster versus those outside the primary cluster. Additional comparisons were made with patients diagnosed with CC v4.0 normal motility and type III achalasia, given the hypothesis that DES may exist along a spectrum with type III achalasia.4

Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons used chi-square tests for categorical variables and parametric tests (t-tests/ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables or non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U/Kruskal-Wallis) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). Data analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.2, with UMAP applied using the tidymodels package.29,30

RESULTS

Subjects

The study cohort comprised 5360 patients with a median age of 56 years (interquartile range, 27 years), 61% of whom were females (Table 1). Within this cohort, 42 patients (0.8%) had DES, 2400 (45%) had normal motility, and 107 (2.0%) had type III achalasia. Patients with DES and type III achalasia demonstrated higher rates of opioid use (12% and 18%, respectively) compared to the remainder of the cohort.

Dysphagia was the most common indication for evaluation across all patients. However, DES patients presented more frequently with chest pain (10%) compared to type III achalasia patients (1.9%) and the overall cohort (5.7%).

Manometric parameters aligned with expected disease patterns. Type III achalasia patients demonstrated elevated median IRP in both supine and upright positions. Median DL was reduced in DES and type III achalasia patients while remaining within normal ranges for patients with normal motility. Correspondingly, the frequency of premature contractions was increased in both DES and type III achalasia groups relative to the normal motility cohort.

Cluster Comparisons

UMAP projection of HRM metrics revealed a central cluster with substantial overlap between DES and type III achalasia patients (Fig. 1). Using DES contour borders to define cluster boundaries, 33 DES, 24 normal motility, and 62 type III achalasia patients were classified as within-cluster, while 9 DES, 2376 normal motility, and 45 type III achalasia patients were classified as outside-cluster (Table 2).

The primary characteristic differentiating within- and outside-cluster observations was the number of premature supine contractions (median 8 vs 0, respectively) (Fig. 2). This difference was statistically significant among DES patients (P < 0.001). DL for both supine and upright swallows was also shorter in within-cluster patients. Among DES patients, no significant differences were observed between cluster groups for opioid use, large hernias (> 3 cm), or IRP, though PPI use trended higher within-cluster (42% vs 22%). Among within-cluster normal motility patients, nearly all had moderate to large hiatal hernias measured during esophageal manometry, with a mean hernia size of 3.6 cm. Within this subgroup, 25% had a hiatal hernia larger than 3 cm compared to 2.7% among outside-cluster normal motility patients. These within-cluster normal motility patients otherwise demonstrated intact peristalsis, normal IRP, normal distal contractile integral, and normal bolus transit, findings consistent with CC v4.0 normal motility despite borderline DL values.2

Pre-treatment PROs showed no significant inter-cluster differences except for EHAS, where within-cluster patients demonstrated higher median total scores (30 vs 20.5; P = 0.010). Post-treatment PRO data were insufficient for inter-cluster comparison.

FLIP testing was completed by 14 within-cluster and 2 outside-cluster DES patients. The small outside-cluster sample precluded statistical comparison, though median values were 2.1 vs 2.4 mm2/mmHg for distensibility index and 15.7 vs 12.2 mm for maximum EGJ diameter, respectively. Type III achalasia patients showed no inter-cluster differences in FLIP parameters. Esophagram data were insufficient for DES cluster comparisons.

Treatments

Among DES patients, PPI was the most common treatment within-cluster, while PPI and nitrates were equally common outside-cluster (Table 3). For lower esophageal sphincter-directed interventions, 5 (15%) within-cluster patients received botulinum toxin injection and 3 (9.1%) underwent POEM, compared to 2 POEM procedures in outside-cluster patients. All 3 within-cluster POEM patients reported complete dysphagia resolution. Among outside-cluster POEM patients, 1 achieved symptom resolution while the other experienced partial improvement with persistent mild chest pressure.

DISCUSSION

In this single-center exploratory study, machine learning clustering demonstrated that DES patients represent a heterogeneous population, with a subset clustering with type III achalasia based on premature contraction frequency. Despite the small sample size inherent to this rare condition, within-cluster patients exhibited higher EHAS scores and complete symptomatic response to POEM.

The clear separation between type III achalasia and normal motility patients on UMAP projection validates our clustering approach. The intermediate positioning of DES cases supports the hypothesis that it may exist along a spectrum with type III achalasia, with higher premature contraction frequencies positioning patients closer to the achalasia phenotype. A subset of normal motility patients clustered near DES and type III achalasia due to the presence of moderate to large hiatal hernias, which can shorten DL by mechanically reducing effective esophageal length.12 This anatomic effect likely positioned them closer to spastic phenotypes in UMAP space despite meeting CC v4.0 criteria for normal motility.2 Together, these findings suggest that premature contraction frequency may serve as a marker to determine DES phenotype and guide treatment selection.

DES is recognized as a heterogeneous diagnosis with variable clinical presentations and manometric patterns.31 DL has previously been examined as a key discriminating variable for DES, with a dichotomous threshold of less than 4.5 seconds used to identify clinically relevant premature contractions.1 This analysis reinforces the importance of DL but differs in both methodology and interpretation. Premature contraction frequency was treated as a continuous physiologic measure within a multidimensional framework that incorporated all HRM metrics. UMAP integrates these variables holistically and identifies the features that most strongly influence local clustering structure. Within this framework, premature contraction frequency emerged as the principal factor distinguishing the DES subset that clustered with type III achalasia. Higher frequencies shifted patients toward an achalasia-like physiologic profile when evaluated alongside other motility measures.

Prior studies report lower POEM response rates in DES compared to achalasia patients.32,33 This likely reflects DES heterogeneity, and improved characterization of achalasia-spectrum patients could yield response rates comparable to confirmed achalasia. While the 100% within-cluster POEM response rate is promising, validation in larger cohorts is needed before informing clinical practice.

Of note, several outside-cluster normal motility patients demonstrated abnormal FLIP contractile responses. This finding is not unexpected because HRM and FLIP assess related but distinct physiologic processes. HRM evaluates primary peristalsis and EGJ relaxation, whereas FLIP measures esophageal distensibility and distension-induced secondary peristalsis. As a result, normal motility on HRM can coexist with abnormal FLIP patterns, an observation described in prior studies.34,35 Such discordance underscores the complementary roles of HRM and FLIP and highlights the need for further work to define the clinical significance of discrepant findings, particularly among patients who fall near cluster boundaries in multidimensional analyses.

The primary limitation of this study is the small DES sample size. This precluded meaningful FLIP and esophagram comparisons due to insufficient outside-cluster patients. Additionally, the retrospective study design resulted in missing data, particularly for post-treatment PROs and long-term treatment responses, with many patients lost to follow-up. Larger, multi-institutional datasets are necessary to further assess how clustering can define DES phenotypes and to overcome these data limitations.

Future research should employ advanced unsupervised clustering methods, including deep learning approaches to generate latent spaces similar to virtual disease landscapes for FLIP, which could provide probabilistic models for classifying achalasia-spectrum versus non-achalasia DES and inform candidacy for lower esophageal sphincter-directed therapies.36 Additionally, clinically applicable algorithms incorporating premature contraction thresholds should be developed to identify optimal treatment candidates. Larger, multi-institutional datasets are necessary to externally validate these findings and better characterize DES phenotypes. Prospective studies are also needed to evaluate treatment outcomes in a standardized and comprehensive manner. The identification of an achalasia-spectrum DES subset defined by number of premature contractions on HRM represents a paradigm shift toward phenotype-driven treatment selection that could improve outcomes for patients with this challenging condition.
