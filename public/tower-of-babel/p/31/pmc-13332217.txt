Predicting global distribution shifts of Tabanus taeniola under different climate change scenarios
Authors: Abdalrahman E. Afifi, Refaat M. Gabre, Areej A. Al-Khalaf, Abeer M. Salem, Fadila Al Salameen, Mohamed G. Nasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change poses a significant threat to global biodiversity, altering species ranges and ecological dynamics. This study investigates the impact of climate change on Tabanus taeniola (Diptera: Tabanidae), a widely distributed horsefly with ecological importance in Africa and South America. Our objective was to model its current habitat suitability and predict future distribution shifts under various climatic scenarios. Using occurrence data from GBIF and and bioclimatic variables from WorldClim (BIO1–BIO19), we screened predictors for multicollinearity and calibrated MaxEnt models using a final subset of five variables. The model showed high accuracy, with an AUC of 0.918. Our findings identify the Minimum Temperature of the Coldest Month (BIO6) and Mean Temperature of Coldest Quarter (BIO11) as the key climatic drivers, with the species thriving in temperatures from 16 °C to 29 °C. Future projections, using the BCC-CSM2-MR and MRI-ESM2-0 models under SSP370 and SSP585 scenarios for 2050–2070, predict significant distributional shifts. We forecast a decline in optimal habitats in lowland tropical regions, with an expansion into temperate zones and higher altitudes in East Africa, South America, and parts of southern Europe. These projections indicate substantial redistribution toward higher elevations and temperate regions under future warming scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55413-z.

Introduction

Climate change presents a significant threat to global biodiversity, environmental stability, and human well-being. The rapid increase in greenhouse gas emissions has led to rising global temperatures and altered weather patterns, causing habitat degradation for many species1. Human activities, particularly the burning of fossil fuels, have drastically increased atmospheric CO₂ concentrations by approximately 40% since the pre-industrial era, exacerbating global climate conditions2. These changes have triggered a cascade of biological consequences, including ocean acidification, rising sea levels, more frequent and severe extreme weather events, and shifts in species distributions3. Insects, in particular, are one of the most affected groups, experiencing substantial population declines due to these environmental pressures4. Recent species distribution modeling studies have demonstrated that climatic warming is already driving poleward and elevational shifts across multiple insect taxa, emphasizing the importance of predictive ecological modeling under future climate scenarios1. Understanding how climatic gradients influence vector species distributions is therefore critical for anticipating ecological and epidemiological responses to ongoing global change.

Tabanus taeniola (Diptera: Tabanidae) is an ecologically and veterinarily significant hematophagous insect. Adult horseflies contribute to pollination by consuming nectar, indirectly supporting the reproductive success of numerous plant species. They also play a crucial role in food web dynamics, acting as both a parasite on mammalian blood and as prey for insectivorous birds, reptiles, and amphibians. Of veterinary importance, T. taeniola is a mechanical vector for several bacterial and protozoan diseases that impact livestock, thereby affecting animal health and agricultural productivity. The species’ ecological relevance and vulnerability to environmental factors make it a key subject for research in biodiversity and disease ecology5,6. Their distribution and abundance are highly influenced by temperature, humidity, and habitat availability. As ectothermic hematophagous Diptera, tabanid species exhibit strong physiological dependence on thermal and moisture regimes, which regulate development rate, adult activity, and host-seeking behavior, making them particularly responsive to climatic variability7. T. taeniola is predominantly found in tropical and subtropical regions, especially in Africa, where it thrives in warm, humid conditions.

The current distribution of Tabanus taeniola spans diverse ecological zones, from arid deserts to wet savannas, with populations concentrated in regions with optimal temperature and precipitation8. However, climate changes have been shown to influence species’ regional distributions, necessitating adaptation, migration, or potential population decline9. Studies indicate that species relying on stable climatic conditions for survival may experience range contractions or expansions as a result of environmental alterations1. Therefore, understanding the climatic parameters that govern the distribution of T. taeniola is essential for predicting future changes and mitigating potential ecological and economic impacts3.

Climate change can significantly affect the life cycle and distribution of Tabanus taeniola by altering temperature and precipitation patterns10. Increased temperatures may impact reproductive success by accelerating development or modifying host availability, while changes in precipitation could impair habitat suitability and larval survival1. Variations in humidity may also affect adult activity patterns, leading to shifts in seasonal population dynamics9. The interaction of these climatic conditions can have cascading effects on species population stability and distribution, influencing their roles as pollinators and vectors6.

Species Distribution Modeling (SDM), particularly when combined with Geographic Information Systems (GIS), provides a quantitative framework for linking occurrence data with environmental predictors to infer ecological niches and forecast potential range shifts under climate change. By integrating species occurrence records with bioclimatic variables, researchers can predict potential changes in habitat suitability across various climate scenarios11. The MaxEnt approach, a widely used machine-learning technique for ecological niche modeling, is particularly effective for predicting species distributions based on environmental variables12. These tools enable scientists to evaluate potential habitat loss or gain, identify climate refugia, and propose conservation policies to mitigate the adverse effects of climate change on biodiversity13. Given its broad geographic range and dependence on climatic factors, Tabanus taeniola is an ideal bioindicator for studying the impacts of climate change on species distributions and vector-borne disease transmission. Its veterinary significance further underscores the importance of this research14,15.

This study aims to evaluate the current and future distribution patterns of Tabanus taeniola using SDM and GIS techniques under different Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios11. By examining species occurrence data and key bioclimatic factors, we seek to assess the species’ sensitivity to climate change and predict future habitat modifications by 2050 and 2070. The results will provide critical insights into the effects of global warming on insect biodiversity, guiding the development of conservation strategies for species with essential ecological roles16,17.

Results

Correlation analysis and bioclim choose

The pairwise correlation analysis conducted in JMP Pro 16 revealed a complex network of intercorrelations among the 19 WorldClim bioclimatic variables extracted from Tabanus taeniola occurrence localities. A large number of variable pairs displayed strong correlations exceeding |r| = 0.75, indicating considerable redundancy within the full bioclimatic dataset. For instance, BIO10 (Mean Temperature of the Warmest Quarter) and BIO11 (Mean Temperature of the Coldest Quarter) showed strong positive correlation with BIO1 (Annual Mean Temperature), with r values of 0.74 and 0.74 respectively, reflecting the expected relationship between seasonal temperature measures and the annual mean. Similarly, BIO12 (Annual Precipitation) was strongly correlated with BIO13 (Precipitation of the Wettest Month) at r = 0.97, and BIO16 (Precipitation of the Wettest Quarter) also mirrored this pattern, suggesting these variables were largely capturing the same underlying precipitation signal.

To address this redundancy, variables were evaluated both statistically and ecologically. From the temperature-related cluster, BIO1 was retained as a general thermal indicator reflecting the overall thermal suitability of a given location for T. taeniola activity and survival. BIO6 (Minimum Temperature of the Coldest Month) was retained separately because it captures thermal stress during the coldest period, which is biologically distinct from annual averages and is known to influence the overwintering survival and population persistence of tabanid flies. BIO10 and BIO11 were additionally retained despite their moderate correlation with BIO1 because they reflect the thermal conditions during the most physiologically demanding seasons — the warmest and coldest quarters respectively — which are critical periods governing adult emergence, blood-feeding activity, and larval development in Tabanus species. From the precipitation-related variables, BIO13 (Precipitation of the Wettest Month) was selected over BIO12 and BIO16 because it more precisely captures peak moisture availability, which is directly relevant to the humid microhabitat conditions required for tabanid oviposition and larval development in moist soils.

The final selected set — BIO1, BIO6, BIO10, BIO11, and BIO13 — collectively represented the key thermal and hydrological gradients shaping the species’ potential distribution, while maintaining sufficiently low intercorrelation among themselves to ensure statistical robustness in subsequent modeling steps. The scatterplot matrix further confirmed that the selected variables displayed meaningful scatter in their pairwise relationships, indicating that each contributed unique informational content to the analysis. This reduced variable set was therefore considered both statistically appropriate and ecologically justified for use in habitat suitability modeling of T. taeniola.

Model assessment and contributions of bioclimatic factors

The MaxEnt model for Tabanus taeniola demonstrated exceptional predictive accuracy, with a high Area under the Curve (AUC) value of 0.918 and a True Skill Statistic (TSS) of 0.87. This affirms the model’s robustness and the validity of the generated maps. The analysis of variable contributions in (Table 1) revealed that extreme cold temperatures are the most critical factors influencing the species’ distribution. The most influential bioclimatic variable was Minimum Temperature of Coldest Month (BIO6), with a permutation importance of 50.5%, indicating that low temperatures are a major constraint on the species’ range (Fig. 1a). This was followed by Mean Temperature of Coldest Quarter (BIO11), which contributed 23.3%. Other factors, such as Isothermality (BIO3, 12.8%), Mean Temperature of Warmest Quarter (BIO10, 11.5%), and Annual Mean Temperature (BIO1, 1.9%), had lesser but still notable impacts (Fig. 1b). These findings, confirmed by a jackknife test and a Pearson correlation test to remove redundant variables, underscore that temperature extremes, particularly cold-related ones, are more critical than annual temperature averages in defining the ecological niche of T. taeniola (Fig. 1c).

The analysis showed a high homogeneity of 89.7% among occurrence data, signifying a strong ecological association with specific temperature and precipitation conditions. This analysis identified Annual Mean Temperature (BIO1) and Annual Precipitation (BIO12) as key determinants of the niche boundaries. The limiting factors map further identified the key bioclimatic constraints on the species’ range. Mean Temperature of the Coldest Quarter (BIO11) was the primary limiting factor in temperate and subtropical regions, while Minimum Temperature of the Coldest Month (BIO6) was crucial in tropical climates with seasonal variations. The results emphasize the importance of thermal stability and temperature extremes in determining species distribution, providing insights necessary for developing conservation strategies.

Predicted current and future distribution

The predicted current distribution model identifies highly suitable habitats for T. taeniola in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (East and Southern Africa) and the Amazon Basin in South America (Fig. 4). Moderate suitability is observed in regions like the Sahel, while low suitability areas include North Africa and arid parts of Australia. Unsuitable regions are predominantly high-latitude areas with severe cold temperatures and hyper-arid deserts. The model accurately aligns with the species’ known biological range, confirming its preference for warm, humid environments.

The future distribution of Tabanus taeniola under climate change scenarios was projected using two climate models (BCC-CSM2-MR (Fig. 5) and MRI-ESM2-0 (Fig. 6)) for 2050 and 2070, with both showing broadly consistent spatial patterns. Results indicate that Central and East Africa, along with parts of South America, will remain the primary refuges, while West Africa, North Africa, and the Arabian Peninsula are expected to face severe habitat loss, especially under the high-emission SSP585 scenario. By 2070, suitable habitats become increasingly fragmented, with limited persistence in the Congo Basin, Southern Africa, and the Amazon Basin, and potential expansion into parts of South and Southeast Asia under moderate scenarios. Overall, the findings highlight the species’ high vulnerability to climate change and emphasize the need for conservation measures to ensure its long-term survival.

Discussion

Climate change is reshaping species distributions by altering thermal regimes, precipitation patterns, and habitat stability, thereby influencing ecological interactions and vector dynamics. In this study, SDM projections revealed that temperature extremes, particularly Minimum Temperature of the Coldest Month (BIO6) and Mean Temperature of the Coldest Quarter (BIO11), were the dominant drivers of habitat suitability for Tabanus taeniola. This finding indicates that cold-season thermal limits strongly constrain the species’ distribution, likely because low temperatures affect survival, development rates, and seasonal activity patterns of hematophagous Diptera. The identified optimal thermal range of approximately 16–29 °C reflects physiological constraints typical of many tabanid species, in which moderate warmth supports adult flight activity, host-seeking behavior, and larval development, whereas colder conditions reduce population persistence. Similar temperature-dependent responses have been documented in related dipteran taxa, supporting the role of thermal thresholds as primary ecological filters shaping distribution patterns18,19.

Spatial variability in limiting factors across environmental gradients further highlights how climatic mechanisms operate differently among regions. Regional surveys and faunistic studies confirm the presence of T. taeniola across sub-Saharan and arid environments, supporting the suitability patterns predicted here20–23. In equatorial zones, relatively stable temperatures allow Annual Mean Temperature (BIO1) to support continuous activity and survival, acting as a baseline climatic requirement. In contrast, high-altitude and semi-arid environments exhibit greater thermal variability, where Isothermality (BIO3) and Mean Temperature of the Warmest Quarter (BIO10) become critical constraints. Low isothermality reflects strong diurnal and seasonal temperature fluctuations that can reduce feeding efficiency, reproductive success, and flight activity, while elevated warm-season temperatures may increase desiccation stress and reduce moisture availability in larval habitats. These mechanisms explain why thermal stability and extreme temperature conditions exert stronger control over habitat suitability in climatically marginal regions than annual temperature averages alone. Empirical studies on tabanid ecology and elevational shifts have similarly emphasized the importance of temperature variability and thermal stress in shaping population dynamics and geographic limits19,24,25.

Projected future scenarios suggest that climate warming will drive directional redistribution of T. taeniola, characterized by contractions in lowland tropical regions and expansions toward higher latitudes and elevations. As warming reduces cold limitations in temperate and high-altitude zones, new areas may become climatically suitable, while regions already near the species’ upper thermal tolerance may experience declining suitability. Such poleward and altitudinal shifts are consistent with broader patterns observed across Diptera and other ectothermic organisms, where climatic envelopes move toward cooler environments under increasing global temperatures18,24. The persistence of refugia in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South America reflects the continued availability of suitable thermal conditions, whereas increased fragmentation under high-emission scenarios indicates that climatic extremes may disrupt habitat continuity and potentially alter dispersal pathways. These changes may have important ecological and veterinary implications, as shifts in vector distribution can modify patterns of host exposure and disease transmission, consistent with field observations linking T. taeniola to trypanosome and leishmania dynamics in African ecosystems26.

BCC-CSM2-MR was selected for its demonstrated skill in simulating tropical precipitation patterns and African monsoon dynamics, which are critical drivers of T. taeniola habitat suitability. MRI-ESM2-0 was chosen for its strong performance in reproducing observed temperature trends across tropical and subtropical regions and its relatively fine spatial resolution among CMIP6 models. Together, these two models originate from independent modeling centers (Beijing Climate Center and the Meteorological Research Institute of Japan), representing structural diversity in parameterization schemes and thereby providing a more robust assessment of projection uncertainty than single-model approaches.

While our projections provide valuable insight into climate-driven redistribution, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The models rely primarily on climatic predictors and do not incorporate land-use change, vegetation structure, host availability, or hydrological dynamics, all of which may influence local population stability. Previous studies have highlighted the role of vegetation cover and host–habitat interactions in shaping tabanid distributions, suggesting that integrating additional ecological variables could refine future predictions27. Furthermore, uncertainties associated with different GCMs and emission pathways mean that the projected range shifts should be interpreted as potential climatic trajectories rather than precise forecasts. Despite these limitations, the consistency of results across independent climate models supports the robustness of the observed patterns and reinforces the importance of temperature-driven processes in determining the future distribution of18,19,24.

Conclusion

This study provides a global-scale assessment of the potential climatic suitability of Tabanus taeniola under present and future environmental conditions, highlighting the central role of temperature extremes in shaping habitat persistence and redistribution. Rather than representing definitive forecasts, the projected patterns illustrate how climate-driven thermal constraints may promote contractions in lowland tropical regions while enabling expansion toward higher elevations and cooler latitudes. These findings reinforce the sensitivity of hematophagous Diptera to thermal variability and emphasize the importance of considering temperature stability alongside mean climatic conditions when evaluating future distributional change.

While model performance metrics indicated strong predictive ability (AUC = 0.918; TSS = 0.87), several sources of uncertainty should be considered when interpreting absolute suitability values. The reliance on bioclimatic predictors excludes non-climatic drivers such as land use, vegetation structure, hydrological dynamics, and host availability, which may influence realized habitat suitability. Spatial sampling bias in occurrence records and the use of coarse-resolution climate layers may further affect local-scale predictions. These limitations highlight the need for future modeling efforts that integrate ecological, environmental, and biological variables to better capture species–environment interactions.

From a broader ecological perspective, the projected redistribution of T. taeniola suggests potential changes in vector ecology and host exposure across multiple regions. Expanding geographic sampling, strengthening long-term entomological monitoring, and integrating climatic suitability models with ecological datasets may improve the capacity to track and interpret climate-driven distributional shifts. Continued development of integrative modeling frameworks that combine climatic, ecological, and epidemiological data will be essential for understanding how vector species respond to ongoing environmental change.

Materials and methods

Occurrence data collection and cleaning

Occurrence records were compiled from multiple sources, including GBIF, iNaturalist, and published literature, to maximize geographic representation and reduce regional data gaps. An initial dataset of 2,083 records was assembled and subsequently subjected to a multi-step quality control and spatial filtering procedure. Records lacking geographic coordinates, exhibiting obvious spatial errors (e.g., coordinates located in oceans), or showing inconsistent taxonomic or metadata information were excluded. Duplicate entries and records falling within the same raster grid cell were removed to reduce pseudo-replication. To minimize spatial sampling bias associated with uneven survey effort, spatial thinning was applied so that densely sampled regions did not disproportionately influence model calibration. Duplicate occurrence records were identified and removed using the ‘Remove Duplicates’ function in GBIF’s built-in data cleaning interface, followed by spatial deduplication using the ‘SDMtoolbox’ plugin in ArcGIS, which retained only one record per 2.5 arc-minute grid cell. Additional occurrence data extracted from peer-reviewed literature were incorporated primarily to improve representation in under-sampled regions such as the Middle East, ensuring broader environmental coverage. After filtering and bias reduction, a final dataset of 390 unique occurrence records was retained for modeling. This preprocessing workflow was designed to balance data quality, geographic coverage, and ecological representativeness, which are critical considerations for presence-only modeling approaches. Because a global calibration extent was used, spatial thinning of occurrence records was applied to reduce sampling bias rather than using an explicit bias file, thereby preventing overrepresentation of densely sampled regions while maintaining broad environmental coverage. The final curated dataset was formatted as a comma-separated values (CSV) file for use in subsequent analyses28–31.

Climatic data

Present-day and future bioclimatic data were sourced from the WorldClim v2.1 database (www.worldclim.org) at a spatial resolution of 2.5 arc-minutes (~ 5 km), which provides a balance between ecological relevance and computational efficiency for wide-ranging insect species. This resolution is appropriate for modeling the broad climatic niche of T. taeniola, whose dispersal ability and host-associated movement occur at regional rather than microhabitat scales32. From the 19 standard bioclimatic variables, a subset was carefully selected to avoid multicollinearity, a known issue that can distort model predictions33. To identify the most informative bioclimatic variables for modeling the distribution of Tabanus taeniola, occurrence records were first obtained from species observation data and processed through DIVA-GIS to extract values for all 19 WorldClim bioclimatic variables (bc1–bc19) at each georeferenced locality (Supplementary data 1). A multivariate correlation analysis was then performed using JMP Pro 16, where a full correlation matrix and scatterplot matrix were generated to examine the pairwise relationships among all bioclimatic variables. Variables showing high intercorrelation (|r| > 0.75) were considered redundant, and from each cluster of strongly correlated variables, only the one with the greatest ecological relevance to insect thermoregulation and seasonal activity was retained. This step was essential to reduce multicollinearity, which can otherwise inflate model complexity and reduce the biological interpretability of the final variable set. A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted, and the following five variables with high ecological relevance to insect distribution were chosen: BIO1 – Annual Mean Temperature, BIO3 – Isothermality, BIO6 – Minimum Temperature of the Coldest Month, BIO10 – Mean Temperature of the Warmest Quarter and BIO11 – Mean Temperature of the Coldest Quarter.

Future climate projections were derived from two CMIP6 General Circulation Models (GCMs), BCC-CSM2-MR and MRI-ESM2-034. The use of multiple GCMs allows assessment of inter-model uncertainty and reduces reliance on a single climate projection, thereby increasing the robustness of species distribution forecasts. Rather than representing fundamentally different climate pathways, the inclusion of two independent models provides a comparative framework to evaluate the consistency of projected habitat suitability patterns under identical emission scenarios. Consistent spatial trends observed across both models were interpreted as increased confidence in relative distributional patterns rather than definitive forecasts. Future climate projections were evaluated under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP370 and SSP585), representing moderate-to-high and high greenhouse-gas emission trajectories within the CMIP6 framework. The selection of these scenarios allows assessment of potential species responses under increasing climatic stress, capturing a range of plausible future environmental conditions rather than a single deterministic outcome. Using multiple scenarios provides a means to explore uncertainty associated with future climate pathways and to compare the consistency of projected suitability patterns across emission trajectories35. The spatial extent used for model calibration was defined at a global scale to capture the full range of climatic conditions potentially accessible to Tabanus taeniola. Rather than restricting the background to a regional mask, a global climatic extent was retained to avoid artificially constraining environmental space and to allow the model to identify broad-scale suitability gradients. This approach is particularly relevant for widely distributed dipteran species with potential long-distance dispersal and host-mediated movement. The geographic mask applied in ArcGIS was therefore used primarily to standardize raster alignment and ensure consistent environmental layers across current and future projections, rather than to restrict the ecological calibration area. Using a global calibration extent also minimizes bias introduced by incomplete knowledge of dispersal barriers and allows consistent comparison among future climate scenarios36.

Species distribution modeling and model evaluation

Species distribution modeling (SDM) was performed using the MaxEnt v3.4.4 algorithm, a machine-learning technique well-suited for presence-only data. MaxEnt utilizes maximum entropy principles to estimate the probability of suitable habitat conditions based on occurrence records and environmental predictors12,37. Continuous MaxEnt logistic outputs were reclassified into four suitability categories to facilitate interpretation: unsuitable (0–0.2), low (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), and high suitability (0.6–1.0). These thresholds were selected to provide a standardized representation of relative habitat suitability and were applied consistently across current and future scenarios.

MaxEnt models were implemented using default feature classes (auto features) with a regularization multiplier of 1 to balance model complexity and generalization. The model used 10,000 background points automatically generated by the software across the calibration extent. Occurrence data were randomly partitioned using a 75% training and 25% testing scheme, and model stability was assessed using 10 replicate runs. Logistic output format was retained to facilitate ecological interpretation of habitat suitability. Response curves and jackknife analyses were enabled to evaluate variable influence and model behavior. To reduce unrealistic projections, extrapolation and clamping options were disabled, allowing predictions to remain within the environmental range represented in the training data. Background prediction outputs were also generated to examine environmental sampling across the study extent. Extrapolation and clamping were disabled to prevent the model from generating suitability estimates in environmental conditions outside the range represented by the training data. This conservative approach reduces the risk of biologically unrealistic predictions when projecting into future climate scenarios.

Model performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) and the True Skill Statistic (TSS), calculated using the maximum training sensitivity plus specificity threshold derived from the MaxEnt output. The occurrence dataset was randomly divided into training (75%) and testing (25%) subsets. Model performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve38. An AUC value above 0.75 is typically considered to indicate strong predictive accuracy39. A Jackknife test was also conducted to determine the relative importance of each bioclimatic variable12,40.

The final predictive maps for current and future distributions were then generated and visualized in ArcGIS. All bioclimatic layers were processed in ArcGIS using the “Extract by Mask” tool to match the study extent. Raster resolution and cell size were maintained without resampling to preserve the original climatic signal of the WorldClim dataset35.

Two-dimensional niche analysis

A two-dimensional niche analysis was performed using DIVA-GIS v7.5 to visually represent the species’ climatic niche [39, 4.]. This analysis focused on two key variables: Annual Mean Temperature (BIO1) and Annual Precipitation (BIO12). By plotting the species’ occurrence points within this climatic space, we were able to define its principal niche boundaries and assess potential range expansions or contractions under future climate scenarios41,42.
