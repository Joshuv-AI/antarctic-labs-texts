Incidence and predictors of delirium in patients admitted to intensive care unit at comprehensive specialised hospitals in Amhara region, northwest Ethiopia: a multicentre prospective observational study

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess the incidence of delirium and its predictors among adult patients admitted to the intensive care units of comprehensive specialised hospitals in the Amhara region of northwest Ethiopia from 18 October 2024 to 20 February 2025.DesignA multicentre prospective observational study was conducted.SettingFour comprehensive specialised hospitals in the Amhara region of northwest Ethiopia, from 18 October 2024 to 20 February 2025.ParticipantsA total of 351 patients were included in the final analysis during the study period.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome measure of this study was the incidence of delirium. Additionally, the study investigated the factors associated with delirium incidence among adult patients admitted to intensive care units.ResultsThe incidence of delirium among adult patients in intensive care units was 42.17% (95% CI: 37.08 to 47.42). Pain (adjusted HR (AHR) = 4.74; 95% CI: 2.38 to 9.44), mechanical ventilation (AHR = 2.96; 95% CI: 1.56 to 5.63), age 65 years or older (AHR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.48 to 3.21) and agitation (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) ≥1) (AHR = 3.26; 95% CI: 2.09 to 5.09) were statistically significant factors associated with delirium.Conclusion and recommendationIn the present study, more than one-third of patients developed delirium. Pain, mechanical ventilation, age 65 or older and agitation (RASS≥1) were significantly associated with delirium occurrence. To reduce the incidence of delirium, the current study recommends treating or preventing pain and agitation. Additionally, special attention should be given to patients receiving mechanical ventilation and those aged 65 or older during care.Keywords: Delirium, Ethiopia, Emergency Departments, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Adult intensive & critical care

Background

The intensive care unit (ICU) creates an environment that can either trigger or exacerbate psychological disorders and neuropsychiatric syndromes.1 Among the various ICU-related disorders, such as post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety and depression, delirium is one of the most common behavioural manifestations following an ICU visit.2,4

Delirium is an acute change in mental state characterised by difficulties with attention, awareness and arousal, along with fluctuating cognition.5 The condition can manifest in various forms, including hyperactive, hypoactive or mixed types.5 6 Psychometric assessments in studies evaluating both hyperactive and hypoactive delirium suggest that they are equally frequent when delirium is present.7 Delirium frequently occurs in the ICU; a meta-analysis found that it affects approximately one-third of ICU patients.8 However, there is significant variation in the reported occurrence rates, which range from 4% to 89%.9,11 Pain, the use of physical restraints, respiratory diseases, sleep deprivation, a history of surgery, age and gender are among the factors that heighten the occurrence of delirium in the ICU.12,14 Additionally, alcohol consumption, hypertension, benzodiazepine exposure and advanced age are factors that contribute to delirium in the ICU.15 16 Delirium assessment in the ICU is crucial because of its high prevalence and significant impact on patient outcomes. Several methods have been developed to accurately diagnose delirium in this setting, with the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) and the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) being the most widely used and validated tools,17 18 and current guidelines recommend the routine screening for delirium in all ICU patients.19

Delirium can be managed through various techniques, including prevention, pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic methods. Non-pharmacological strategies and preventive approaches are the cornerstone of delirium management in the ICU. While low-dose dexmedetomidine has shown some efficacy in preventing delirium, primary prevention strategies focus on identifying and modifying risk factors, employing non-pharmacologic interventions and optimising pharmacologic treatments.15 20 Moderate-certainty evidence suggests that multicomponent interventions addressing various risk factors for delirium have reduced the incidence of ICU delirium by 43%.21

Delirium frequently occurs in the ICU, and patients experiencing it often have extended ICU and hospital stays, which in turn increases healthcare costs. This condition can also lead to the accidental removal of medical lines and devices, increased morbidity and mortality, neurocognitive decline and physical impairment.1 22 23 However, studies on the incidence of delirium and its predictors among adult patients in ICUs in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area, are limited. Therefore, identifying the risk factors is crucial for implementing strategies to reduce the occurrence, severity and adverse outcomes of delirium. This study aims to provide valuable data on the incidence of delirium and its associated factors in adult ICUs. The findings may serve as baseline data for future research.

Methods and materials

Study design, period and settings

A multicentre prospective observational study was conducted from 18 October 2024 to 20 February 2025. The study was conducted at the Comprehensive Specialised Hospitals in the Amhara region of northwest Ethiopia, which includes hospitals: Debre Tabor, University of Gondar, Felege Hiwot and Debre Markos. Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, located in Debre Tabor town, had a mixed medical–surgical ICU with five beds and an average monthly admission of 24 patients. University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, located in Gondar town, has separate medical and surgical ICUs with a total of 19 beds and an average monthly admission of 82 patients. Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, located in Bahirdar town, has mixed medical–surgical ICUs with a total of 10 beds and an average monthly admission of 49 patients. Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, located in Debre Markos town, also operates a mixed medical–surgical ICU with four beds and an average monthly admission of 17 patients. These hospitals provide a wide range of critical care services, supported by diverse healthcare teams.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients aged 18 years and older, who were admitted to the study area during the study period, did not have delirium at the time of ICU admission, had available and complete medical records, had no confirmed diagnosis of cognitive impairment or psychiatric illness and showed no evidence of delirium at the time of ICU admission as assessed using the CAM-ICU were included. Patients were excluded if they had unavailable medical records, were admitted to the ICU more than once during the study, had confirmed delirium at ICU admission, had cognitive impairment or mental illness, had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8 or lower for more than 24 hours, stayed for less than 24 hours, did not develop delirium during their stay and were transferred from other ICUs. The study follows the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research (EQUATOR) guideline, STrengthening the Reports of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).24

Study variables

Dependent variable

Delirium (yes/ no).

Independent variable

Sociodemographic characteristics-related factors (ie, age, sex, height, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), smoking and history of alcohol intake).

Clinical characteristics-related factors (ie, temperature, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, pulse rate, comorbidity, cause of ICU admission, use of physical restraint, infection, sodium level, potassium level, chlorine level, mechanical ventilation, sedation level, pain, opioid, benzodiazepine and neuroleptic medications).

Operational definition

Delirium

A patient is considered to have delirium if features 1 (acute change or fluctuating course of mental status) and 2 (inattention) of the CAM-ICU are present, along with either feature 3 (altered level of consciousness) or feature 4 (disorganised thinking).26

Agitated

A patient is deemed agitated when their score on the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RAAS) is between 1 and 4.27 28

Pain

A patient is considered to have pain when their Behavioural Pain Scale (BPS) score is greater than 3, indicating pain and a score of 5 or higher indicates moderate pain.29

Sodium level

Hypernatremia is defined as a plasma sodium concentration greater than 145 mEq/L, while hyponatraemia is defined as a plasma sodium concentration less than 135 mEq/L.30 31

Potassium level

Hyperkalaemia is defined as a plasma potassium concentration greater than 5.5 mEq/L, while hypokalaemia is defined as a plasma potassium concentration less than 3.5 mEq/L.30

Chloride level

The normal range of serum chloride concentrations is 96–106 mEq/L. Hyperchloraemia is defined as plasma chloride concentrations above 106 mEq/L, while hypochloraemia is defined as plasma chloride concentrations below 96 mEq/L.32 33

Diagnosed infection

An infection that has been identified and confirmed through clinical assessment, laboratory tests or medical records.34

Censored

Was considered after patients admitted to ICU did not develop the outcome during the follow-up time (including transfer to other units, death and those who still did not develop delirium at the end of the follow-up period).

Time to event

The time interval (in a day) between ICU admission and the occurrence of delirium.

Follow-up time

The follow-up time was started immediately as the patient had been admitted to the ICU until 5 days postadmission.

Event

Participants who had developed delirium within the follow-up time.

Data collection tools and procedures

A semistructured questionnaire, developed from previous literature,911,14 19 20 25 35 was used to collect data. The CAM-ICU was used to diagnose delirium. This tool involves assessing four main features of delirium: an acute change or fluctuating course of mental status (feature 1); inattention (feature 2); an altered level of consciousness (feature 3) and disorganised thinking (feature 4). The CAM-ICU has high inter-rater reliability (Cohen κ, 0.96; 95% CI: 0.87 to 1.00).36

When using the CAM-ICU to diagnose delirium, the first step is to assess the arousal level with the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS), which ranges from +4 (overtly combative, immediate danger to staff) to −5 (no response to any stimulation). In deeply sedated patients (RASS scores of −4 and −5), the presence of delirium cannot be established, and the test was repeated later. In responsive and awake patients (RASS scores of −3 to +4), the second step involves diagnosing delirium, which is classified as positive when both features 1 and 2 are present, along with either feature 3 or feature 4. If these conditions are not met, the patient is classified as CAM-ICU negative. The outcome variable was assessed every 12 hours since admission.

Demographic data, such as the patient’s age, sex and history of comorbidity, along with variables including electrolyte levels, temperature, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, pulse rate, cause of ICU admission and medications, were recorded from the patient’s chart. Information on the presence of catheters and the use of restraints was collected through observation. Height was measured, and ideal body weight was calculated accordingly. Additionally, variables such as pain and delirium were recorded every 4 hours using the Behavioural Pain Scale and CAM-ICU, respectively.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, a pretest was conducted on 5% (18) of patients in the adult ICU at Woldiya Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, which was not included in the main study. This pretest was conducted ahead of actual data collection, and the contents were modified accordingly. Data collectors and supervisors received 12 hours of training on the study’s objectives and data collection methods from the principal investigator to ensure familiarity with the study. Nurses with BSc degrees collected the data, while assigned anaesthetists supervised the data collection process. The data were then checked for completeness and clarity.

Data processing and analysis

The data were entered into Epi-Data V.4.6 and subsequently transferred to STATA V.17.0 for analysis. Descriptive results were presented in the form of text, tables and figures. The distribution of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; normally distributed data were reported as means with SD, while non-normally distributed data were reported as medians with IQRs. Survival data were summarised using the Kaplan-Meier curve and analysed with the log-rank test. The Kaplan-Meier failure curve illustrated the time it took for patients to develop delirium after being admitted to the ICU. The log-rank test was employed to compare the time to develop delirium across categories of each explanatory variable.

The proportional hazard (PH) assumption of the model was verified using both graphical tests (the log–log survival probability plot and the Schoenfeld residual plot) and the Schoenfeld residual PH test. The assumption states that if any of the variables have a p-value≤0.05, then the PHs assumption is violated. The Cox-Snell residual plot (a graphical representation) and the likelihood ratio (LR) test (a statistical method) were employed to assess the model’s goodness of fit (GOF). Both bivariable and multivariable Cox regression models were fitted to identify predictors of delirium occurrence. Variables in the bivariable model with a p-value<0.2 were included in the multivariable cox-regression model and those variables with a p<0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered significant predictors. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarise the categorical variables. Finally, tables, graphs and texts were used to present the data.

Missing data handling

Missing data were assessed for all variables before analysis. Variables with missing data were reviewed for patterns and proportions. For variables with less than 5% missingness, complete case analysis was performed. No imputation was conducted, as the proportion of missing data was minimal and considered unlikely to bias the results. Patients with missing key covariates required for multivariable modelling were excluded from the final analysis.

Patient and public involvement in this study

The study participants or the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study

Outcome status of study participant

The study participants were followed for a minimum of 1 day and a maximum of 5 days after their admission to the ICU, with a median follow-up time of 3 days (IQR: 2–4). The total observation time (person-time) was 1141 days. Among the study participants, 148 (42.17%) (95% CI: 37.08 to 47.41) exhibited delirium during the follow-up time (figure 2) with an incidence density of 12.97 per 100 person-days of observation, while the remaining 203 (57.83%) were censored (figure 3). Of the censored patients, 44 (21.67%) were followed for the entire follow-up period without developing delirium. Additionally, 62 (30.54%) of the patients died, and 97 (47.78%) were transferred to another ward before completing the follow-up period.

Kaplan-Meier failure status of predictor variables

The incidence of delirium among adult ICU-admitted patients varied across the categories of each independent variable. For example, patients experiencing pain had a higher incidence of delirium compared with those without pain. Participants aged 65 and older exhibited a higher incidence of delirium than those younger than 65. Additionally, participants on mechanical ventilators had a higher incidence of delirium than their counterparts. Furthermore, patients who developed agitation also showed a higher incidence of delirium compared with those who did not (figure 4).

Test of proportional hazard assumption

In this study, each variable in the bivariable analysis met the assumptions of PHs (ie, p-value>0.05). The overall p-value from the global test for the multivariable Cox PH model was 0.2203, which is greater than 0.05, thus satisfying the PH assumption.

Testing the goodness of model fitness

The GOF test for the Cox PH regression model was assessed statistically using the LR test, which demonstrated a good fit (LR χ2=134.58, p-value<0.0001) and graphically through the Cox-Snell residual plot, which was adequate.

Predictor variables of delirium

Variables such as history of smoking, history of alcohol intake, pain, mechanical ventilation, infection, use of restraints, benzodiazepines, opioids, age and sedation status had a p-value of less than 0.2 in the bivariable Cox regression and were included in the multivariable analysis. However, only pain, mechanical ventilation, age and sedation statuses were statistically significant in the final multivariable Cox regression analysis.

Participants with pain were 4.74 times more likely to develop delirium compared with those without pain (AHR=4.74; 95% CI: 2.38 to 9.44). Participants on mechanical ventilators were 2.96 times more likely to experience delirium than their counterparts (AHR=2.96; 95% CI: 1.56 to 5.63). The study findings also indicated that the hazard of developing delirium was 2.18 times higher for participants aged 65 years and older compared with those younger than 65 years (AHR=2.18; 95% CI: 1.48 to 3.21). Furthermore, the hazard of developing delirium was 3.26 times higher in agitated participants compared with those who were sedated or calm (AHR=3.26; 95% CI: 2.09 to 5.09) (table 3).

Discussion

Delirium in the ICU predisposes patients to prolonged ICU and hospital stays, as well as an increased risk of death both during their ICU stay and after discharge. This study aims to determine the incidence of delirium and its predictors.

The incidence of delirium in this study in the adult ICU was 42.17% (CI: 37.08 to 47.41).

The finding is in line with a study conducted in Brazil37 with an incidence of 46.3%.

This study reports a higher incidence of delirium compared with studies conducted in Tunisia35 at 19%, Saudi Arabia38 at 14.5%, Turkey39 at 31.8%, Pakistan40 at 21.8% and India11 at 24.4%. The discrepancy in results may be due to the smaller sample sizes of the aforementioned studies compared with the current ones. Smaller sample sizes can lead to greater variability in outcomes, potentially causing the observed percentage to misrepresent the true percentage in the population. Additionally, a reduced sample size diminishes the study’s power, making it more challenging to detect genuine effects or differences and increasing the risk that the participants do not accurately represent the broader population.41

Furthermore, in the studies conducted in Tunisia and India, the outcome variable was assessed once daily, whereas the current study assessed it every 12 hours. This significant difference in assessment frequency may result in missed true outcome results. Moreover, in the study conducted in Pakistan, the assessment tool was the ICDSC, which underestimates the occurrence of delirium compared with the current study’s use of the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU). The ICDSC has sensitivity for detecting delirium ranging from 60% to 80%, while the CAM-ICU has sensitivity above 80%. Additionally, the ICDSC employs a score-based system (≥4 points out of 8 suggests delirium), meaning that mild cases may not reach the cut-off and could be missed. In contrast, the CAM-ICU is a binary tool (yes/no for delirium diagnosis), which may capture more cases.42

On the contrary, this study reports a lower incidence of delirium compared with a study done in Uganda43 at 50.94% and Italy44 at 55.8%. This may be related to a study conducted in Uganda that focused on individuals under mechanical ventilation. Mechanical ventilation can cause fluctuations in oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, impairing brain function. Hypoxia and hypercapnia may lead to cerebral vasodilation and inflammation, resulting in cognitive dysfunction and delirium.45 46 Patients on mechanical ventilation often receive sedatives and opioids to tolerate intubation. Benzodiazepines, in particular, are a well-known risk factor for ICU delirium due to their effects on the GABAergic system, which can disrupt neurotransmission and cognitive function.47 48 In the Italy study, the sample size was small compared with the current study. With a small sample, random chance has a greater influence on the results. If this sample includes a higher proportion of high-risk patients, it may lead to an overestimation of the incidence of delirium.49

In the current study, pain, mechanical ventilation, age 65 and older and agitation were identified as statistically significant factors associated with the incidence of delirium.

In our study, pain had a significant association with delirium. The hazards of developing delirium in patients with pain were more than four and a half times higher than in their counterparts. The finding is supported by studies conducted in Pakistan40 and the USA.50 That may be due to pain triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines (eg, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) that can cross the blood–brain barrier and induce neuroinflammation. This inflammation disrupts neurotransmitter balance, particularly affecting acetylcholine, dopamine and glutamate, which are essential for cognitive function.51 Additionally, pain activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, resulting in excessive cortisol production.52 Chronic stress and elevated cortisol levels impair hippocampal function, further contributing to cognitive dysfunction and delirium. In ICU patients with uncontrolled pain, fragmented sleep disrupts circadian rhythms, leading to poor sleep quality and cognitive impairment, both of which are hallmark symptoms of delirium.53 Severe pain can also trigger sympathetic overactivation, increasing heart rate and causing vasoconstriction, which may reduce cerebral perfusion and oxygenation, impairing brain function and potentially triggering delirium. Moreover, ICU patients experiencing severe pain often face heightened anxiety, fear and emotional distress, increasing their vulnerability to delirium. These interconnected factors contribute to hyperactive delirium, characterised by agitation and restlessness.51 52 54

In this study, being on a mechanical ventilator was identified as an independent risk factor for delirium; the hazard of developing delirium in adult patients on mechanical ventilators was approximately three times higher than in those who were not on mechanical ventilators. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Uganda43 and China.55 This might be because mechanical ventilation can cause fluctuations in oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, impairing brain function. Hypoxia and hypercapnia may result in cerebral vasodilation and inflammation, leading to cognitive dysfunction and delirium.45 46 Patients on mechanical ventilation often receive sedatives and opioids to tolerate intubation. Benzodiazepines, in particular, are a well-known risk factor for ICU delirium due to their effects on the GABAergic system, which can disrupt neurotransmission and cognitive function.47 48 Furthermore, ventilated patients are frequently immobilised, resulting in muscle atrophy, weakness and reduced sensory input. The loss of normal sensory experiences, such as the inability to speak and dependence on machines, may contribute to delirium and ICU psychosis.56 The distress of being unable to breathe independently can induce panic, fear and anxiety. This psychological stress, combined with the inability to communicate due to intubation, can further trigger delirium.57 It is also important to consider the potential confounding effect of sedation on CAM-ICU assessments. Deep or moderate sedation, particularly at RASS scores of −2 or lower, can impair a patient’s ability to participate in the assessment, potentially resulting in an artificial increase in CAM-ICU positive findings. Since mechanically ventilated patients are more likely to receive continuous sedative infusions, the observed association between mechanical ventilation and delirium may be influenced by this assessment bias.58

Patients aged 65 and older had a significant association with delirium during ICU admissions; the hazards of developing delirium in this age group were more than two times higher compared with patients younger than 65. This finding is supported by two studies done in China.59 60 That might be due to older adults having reduced cognitive reserve, making them more susceptible to acute changes in mental status.61 In the ICU, patients experience sensory deprivation due to isolation, lack of natural light and noise disturbances, all of which can trigger delirium in the elderly.56 Frequent sleep disturbances disrupt circadian rhythms, further exacerbating confusion.62 Additionally, many older adults might be on multiple medications in ICU care, which increases the risk of drug interactions and side effects. Medications, such as benzodiazepines, opioids, anticholinergics and corticosteroids, are known triggers for delirium.63 64 Furthermore, older adults often suffer from multisystem diseases, such as sepsis, kidney failure, heart disease and respiratory failure, which increase the likelihood of delirium.65

In this study, agitation (RASS≥1) had a significant positive influence on delirium development; participants who had agitation were two and a half times more likely to develop delirium compared with those who had no agitation. The result is supported by the study conducted in Uganda.43 The reason behind this might be agitation triggering an overdrive of the sympathetic nervous system, leading to increased catecholamines, which disrupt cognitive function.66 Agitated patients may require benzodiazepines, opioids or physical restraints, all of which are recognised risk factors for delirium.63 Additionally, agitation can indicate underlying issues such as hypoxia, electrolyte disturbances and infections, which further contribute to the development of delirium.67 68

Strengths and limitations of the study

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to determine the incidence and predictors of delirium among adult ICU patients in the study area. Standardised and validated tools (CAM-ICU, RASS and BPS) were used to assess key clinical outcomes and reduce measurement bias. Additionally, the study was multicentre. However, while this study identifies the incidence and predictors of delirium, it does not evaluate patient outcomes or management practices for delirium. Additionally, hypoxia was diagnosed solely through pulse oximetry, which may not fully substitute for arterial blood gas analysis, as this test was unavailable in our setting. Consecutive sampling may introduce selection bias due to the lack of randomisation. Delirium assessments were limited to a 5 day follow-up, which could potentially underestimate late-onset cases. Furthermore, non-clinical factors such as visitation and environmental reassurance, which affect the incidence of delirium, were not included in the study. Additionally, the CAM-ICU assessment tool is influenced by the patient’s level of sedation; particularly at a RASS of −2, this significantly impacts CAM-ICU assessments and can lead to an overestimation of the incidence of delirium.69

Conclusion and recommendation

In the present study, more than one-third of patients developed delirium. Pain, mechanical ventilation, age 65 or older and agitation (RASS≥1) were significantly associated with delirium occurrence. To reduce the incidence of delirium, the current study recommends treating or preventing pain and agitation. Additionally, special attention should be given to patients receiving mechanical ventilation and those aged 65 or older during care.

For future researchers, this study recommends conducting a longitudinal study to assess the short-term and long-term impact of delirium on the outcome of patients in adult ICUs. Moreover, the current study recommends investigating some variables extrapolated from arterial blood gas analysis results, which were not tested whether they had an association with delirium in the current study.
