Improving Measurement Accuracy of the Degree of Carotid In‐Stent Stenosis Using CT Angiography: A Comparison With DSA
Authors: Ikuko Uwano, Yoshiyuki Watanabe, Kyo Noguchi, Makoto Sasaki, Hiroshi Yamagami, Nobuyuki Sakai, for the CAS‐CARE Trial Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComputed tomography angiography (CTA) tends to overestimate restenosis after cervical carotid stenting because of the substantial blooming artifacts from metallic stent materials. Hence, we propose a new measurement method to address this issue and assess the accuracy and reliability of evaluating the degree of in‐stent stenosis (ISS) compared with digital subtraction angiography (DSA) as a post‐hoc analysis of the CAS‐CARE (Carotid Artery Stenting with the Cilostazol Addition for Restenosis) trial.MethodsAmong the image data registered for the trial, 197 paired data sets of poststenting images obtained using both CTA and DSA were used. We measured the ISS degree according to the NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial) criteria with the conventional and new methods using the inner edge and midportion of the stent materials as a boundary to the lumen and compared the stenotic severities with those obtained from DSA.ResultsThe ISS degrees measured using CTA with the conventional method were remarkably higher than those measured using CTA with the new method and DSA (median, 49.5% versus 11.3% and 9.8%; P<0.001). The agreements of the ISS degree between CTA and DSA were significantly improved when using the new measurement method (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.67 [95% CI, 0.59–0.74]) when compared with the conventional method (0.24 [95% CI, −0.08 to 0.57]). In addition, there were no significant systemic biases against DSA in the new method, whereas there were significant biases (mean difference, 39.3%; P<0.001; slope, 0.14; P = 0.034) in the conventional method, although correlations with DSA were comparable between the 2 methods (r = 0.68 [95% CI, 0.59–0.75] and 0.65 [95% CI, 0.56–0.72], respectively).ConclusionMeasurement using CTA with the midportion of the stent materials significantly improved the accuracy of the ISS degree, which was comparable to that using DSA.Keywords: artifacts, carotid artery stenosis, carotid stent, computed tomography angiography, digital subtraction angiography

In patients with substantial stenosis of the cervical carotid arteries, carotid endarterectomy or carotid artery stenting (CAS) can be performed to reduce the risk of stroke. CAS is widely accepted as a less‐invasive alternative to carotid endarterectomy, particularly in high‐risk patients. However, postoperative in‐stent stenosis (ISS) of the cervical internal carotid artery is widely known as a major concern of CAS because it can increase the risk of recurrent stroke. 1 , 2 , 3 Therefore, periodic follow‐up angiographic examinations are needed to detect ISS in patients who have undergone CAS.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for assessing the pre/postoperative severity of cervical carotid stenosis, defined according to the NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial) criteria. 4 However, DSA is an invasive and time‐consuming procedure that includes risks for procedure‐related neurological complications. 5 , 6 Thus, the use of minimally invasive methods such as carotid ultrasonography (CUS), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and computed tomography angiography (CTA) is desirable for this purpose, particularly in postoperative follow‐up examinations. CUS is a noninvasive and convenient method. However, we can measure the stenotic degrees only by an indirect method using the peak systolic velocity (PSV). 7 MRA, either no‐contrast or contrast enhanced, has relatively low spatial resolution and can be strongly affected by susceptibility artifacts from metallic materials. In contrast, CTA can provide accurate vascular anatomy with rapid data acquisition and enables the assessment of stenoses using the NASCET criteria. However, radiation exposure and bolus injection of contrast media are needed. 8 One of the major issues of CTA is blooming artifacts caused by calcific or metallic materials, which appear larger than their actual size. In patients after CAS, blooming artifacts caused by stent struts are known to cause substantial overestimation of luminal stenosis. Previous in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that artificial luminal narrowing ranges from 18% to 60% and 16% to 25%, respectively, depending on the stent type and image reconstruction algorithms used. 9 , 10 , 11 These results indicate that the assessment of ISS after CAS using CTA includes considerable biases.

Thus, in this study, we proposed a new CTA‐based method for accurate measurement of the ISS degree and examined whether the method could overcome the errors caused by the blooming artifacts when compared with DSA in patients who underwent CAS procedures in the CAS‐CARE (Carotid Artery Stenting with Cilostazol Addition for Restenosis) Trial. 12

Methods

This is a post‐hoc substudy of the CAS‐CARE trial, which is a multicenter prospective randomized open‐label blinded endpoint clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01261234; UMIN clinical trial ID: UMIN000004705). The CAS‐CARE trial was approved by the local ethics committees or institutional review boards at each participating center, and written informed consent was obtained from patients or their relatives. Deidentified data collected and presented in this study will be made available upon reasonable request after publication of this article, following approval by regulatory authorities. Data can be requested by contacting the corresponding author.

The details of the trial design and protocols have been previously published. 12 In brief, patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis of ≥50% or asymptomatic carotid stenosis of ≥80% according to the NASCET criteria, who were aged 45 to80 years and scheduled for CAS within 30 days, were randomly assigned to receive cilostazol or other antiplatelet agents. Patients were enrolled for 2 years and followed up for 2 years regarding the occurrence of ISS after CAS procedures using CUS and DSA, as well as nonmandatory CTA. In total, 707 patients were enrolled, and 699 underwent randomization.

Subjects

From these patients, 197 data sets of 152 patients who underwent both DSA and CTA from 20 institutions were collected (immediately after CAS, 141 cases; 2 years after CAS, 52 cases; when ISS was determined by CUS, 4 cases; 133 male patients and 19 female patients; age range, 47–79 years [median, 70 years]; 68 symptomatic and 84 asymptomatic). We excluded cases with violation of the imaging protocols in terms of the intervals between DSA and CTA examinations (baseline, > 7 days; follow‐up, > ± 2 months). The stent devices used were 2 types of open‐cell nickel‐titanium alloy (diameter: 6–10 mm) in 118 cases (Precise, Cordis, Miami, FL, USA, 110 cases; Protégé, Medtronic, Plymouth, MN, USA, 8 cases) and 1 type of closed‐cell cobalt‐chrome alloy with a tantalum core (diameter: 6–10 mm) in 79 cases (Carotid Wallstent, Boston Scientific, Natick, MA, USA). In addition, we collected PSV value data measured by CUS at each participating institution for the comparison.

Imaging Protocols and Postprocessing

The baseline and follow‐up examinations using DSA and CTA were performed using identical equipment in the same participating institutions where the CAS procedures were performed. The DSA acquisition protocols were as follows: contrast media ≥ 300 mg/dL; frame rate ≥ 3 frame/s; matrix size ≥ 512 × 512; anteroposterior and lateral images, as well as nonmandatory oblique images. The CTA acquisition protocols were as follows: scanners of ≥ 4 detector‐row; contrast media of ≥ 300 mg/dL; injection volume (rate), 60 to80 mL (3–4 mL/s) using automated injectors; scan coverage, including C1‐C7; tube voltage, 120 kVp; tube current, optimized in terms of radiation exposure and image quality; slice thickness, 0.5 to1.0 mm; field of view, 160 mm; matrix, 512 × 512; and reconstruction filter, soft tissue kernel. All data were collected from the individual institutions to the imaging data center as anonymized digital images in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format. The data involved in this study included the DSA systems of 5 vendors (Canon, 26 cases; GE, 15 cases; Philips, 86 cases; Shimadzu, 24 cases; Siemens, 46 cases) and CT scanners from 4 vendors (Canon, 43 cases; GE, 125 cases; Philips, 6 cases; Siemens, 23 cases).

In the imaging data center, one of the authors (I.U.) semiautomatically generated curved planar reformation (CPR) images rotating along the long axis of the carotid arteries at 30° intervals from the CTA source images using a workstation (Ziostation2; Ziosoft, Japan) with a field of view of 140 mm, matrix size of 512 × 512, and window level/width of 200/1500 HU.

Image Measurements

All images were sent to a dedicated medical cloud system with an online Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine viewer (LUCID, e‐Site Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) for further measurements of the stented carotid arteries. The ISS degree of in‐stent stenosis was blindly assessed by 2 neuroradiologists (Y.W. and K.N.) independently on DSA and CTA, without patient information such as treatment allocation, outcomes, and participating centers.

On the images showing that the in‐stent lumen is narrowest, diameters of the narrowest in‐stent lumen and those of the normal‐appearing cervical internal carotid artery lumen distal to the stent were measured using a linear cursor under the identical window level/width of 200/1500, and the ISS degrees were calculated using the following formula: stenosis (%) = (1‐[diameter of narrowest in‐stent lumen/diameter of normal distal lumen]) × 100. For CTA, the diameter of the in‐stent lumen was measured on the CPR images by the conventional method using the inner edge of the stent strut (CTA_edge), as well as by the new method we proposed using the midportion of the stent strut (CTA_mid) as the landmark for the lumen‐stent boundary (Figure 1, Figure 2). When the stent was thickened or indistinguishable from the adjacent calcification at the narrowest lumen, we used a virtual line with a consistent distance between the inner edge and midportion as the landmark (Figure 1C, Figure 2C). In addition, to evaluate intrarater and interrater agreements, a second measurement was performed for 50 randomly selected cases with an interval of more than 3 months.

Statistical Analysis

The average values of ISS degree measured by the 2 observers were compared among CTA_edge, CTA_mid, and DSA using a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Correlations and agreements of ISS degrees between CTA_edge or CTA_mid and DSA were examined using the Pearson correlation coefficient and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), respectively. Correlations of PSV values with ISS degrees by DSA were also examined. In addition, a Bland–Altman analysis was performed to elucidate systematic biases. Furthermore, these values were compared between the open‐ and closed‐cell stent groups. The correlations between CTA and DSA were also examined within the subgroup with a close interval within 3 days. Frequencies of ≥ 50% ISS at follow‐up, including 2 years after CAS and when ISS was determined, were also compared between CTA_edge, CTA_mid, CUS (defined as PSV of ≥ 175 cm/s), and DSA using McNemar's test. The intrarater and interrater agreement of the measurements was evaluated using ICC. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, https://www.r‐project.org/) with an alpha value of 0.05. All authors have full access to all the data in the study and take responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis.

Results

One patient was excluded because of severe motion artifacts. The remaining 196 data sets of 151 patients (immediately after CAS, 141 cases; follow‐up, 55 cases; 132 male patients and 19 female patients; age range, 47–79 years [median, 70 years]; 67 symptomatic and 84 asymptomatic) were eligible for further analysis. Among the follow‐up cases, 3 (5.5%) were selected at each participating institution to show ISS by using CUS and DSA and underwent retreatment of angioplasty or restenting. The intervals between DSA and CTA examinations were as follows: immediately after CAS, 1 to7 days (median, 4 days); and follow‐up, 0 to58 days (median, 1 day). The examinations were performed within 7 days in 177 of 196 data sets (90.3%) and within 3 days in 78 data sets (39.8%). The ISS degree of CTA_edge was remarkably larger than that of CTA_mid and DSA (median, 49.5% [interquartile range (IQR), 33.6%–64.8%], 11.3% [IQR, −1.3% to 25.7%], and 9.8% [IQR, −4.9% to 20.1%], respectively; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, P<0.001), whereas there was no significant difference between CTA_mid and DSA (P = 0.104) (Figure 3). There were significant correlations between the ISS degree of CTA_edge and CTA_mid with DSA (r = 0.65 [95% CI, 0.56–0.72] and 0.68 [95% CI, 0.59–0.75], respectively; Pearson correlation coefficient, P<0.001) (Figure 4), which were significantly higher than those of the PSV values with DSA (r = 0.40 [95% CI, 0.27–0.51]; Pearson correlation coefficient, P<0.001). However, CTA_mid showed significantly higher agreement with DSA than CTA_edge (ICC = 0.67 [95% CI, 0.59–0.74] versus 0.24 [95% CI, −0.08 to 0.57], P<0.001) (Figure 4). In addition, Bland–Altman analysis showed that there were significant constant and proportional biases against DSA in CTA_edge (mean difference, 39.3% [95% CI, 36.7%–41.8%], P<0.001 and slope, 0.14, P = 0.034, respectively), whereas there were no biases in CTA_mid (Figure 5).

There were no significant differences between CTA_mid and DSA (P = 0.104–0.356). These tendencies were observed in both groups with open‐ and closed‐cell stents. The CTA_mid and CTA_edge methods both showed good correlations between in‐stent stenosis degree and DSA (r = 0.68 [95% CI, 0.59–0.75] and 0.65 [95% CI, 0.56–0.72]) respectively) (A, B). However, the CTA_mid method achieved a significant improvement in agreement with DSA when compared with the CTA_edge method (ICC = 0.67 [95% CI, 0.59–0.74] and 0.24 [95% CI, −0.08 to 0.57], respectively). Similar tendencies were observed in the open‐ and closed‐cell stent groups (C–F). Among these, the improvement of the agreements in CTA_mid against CTA_edge was significant in the closed‐cell group (ICC = 0.62 [95% CI, 0.46–0.74] and 0.12 [95% CI, −0.05 to 0.39], respectively) (E, F). The subgroup analyses revealed that the open‐ and closed‐cell stent groups both showed the same tendencies: the ISS degrees of CTA_edge (median, 40.8% [IQR, 21.5%–54.4%] and 58.1% [IQR, 48.8%–73.3%], respectively) were significantly larger than those of CTA_mid (5.7% [IQR, −9.6% to 20.7%] and 20.2% [IQR, 5.8%–28.8%], respectively) or DSA (4.8% [−11.5% to 15.3%] and 15.5% [6.6%–26.5%], respectively) (P<0.001), whereas those of CTA_mid and DSA showed no significant differences (P = 0.17–0.36) (Figure 3). In addition, the correlation coefficients and ICCs of ISS degree in CTA_edge and CTA_mid against DSA were comparable between the 2 groups (Figure 4). Among these, CTA_mid in the closed‐cell stent group showed significantly higher agreement with DSA than CTA_edge (ICC = 0.62 [95% CI, 0.46–0.74] versus 0.12 [95% CI, −0.05 to 0.39]; Figure 4). Furthermore, the Bland–Altman analysis showed that the constant bias of CTA_edge against DSA in the closed‐cell stent group was significantly higher than that in the open‐cell stent group (mean difference, 44.3% [95% CI, 40.7%–47.9%] versus 35.8% [95% CI, 32.4%–39.3%]; Figure 5).

In another subgroup analysis using the short‐examination intervals data set within 3 days, we found that the CTA_mid correlation and agreement with DSA was higher than that of the CTA_edge (r = 0.66 and 0.57, respectively; ICC = 0.65 and 0.20, respectively). In addition, Bland–Altman analysis showed a significant constant bias against DSA only in the CTA_edge (mean difference, 40.0%).

Among the 55 follow‐up cases, the number of patients with ISS of ≥50% determined by CTA_edge (19 cases [34.5%]) was significantly larger than that determined by CTA_mid, DSA, and CUS (2 cases [3.6%], 3 cases [5.5%], and 3 cases [5.5%], respectively) (McNemar's test, P<0.001).

Regarding the interrater agreements of the measurements for CTA_edge, CTA_mid, and DSA, the ICC values were 0.84 (95% CI, 0.74–0.91), 0.73 (95% CI, 0.48–0.86), and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.65–0.87), respectively. In addition, the intrarater agreements were as follows: rater #1, 0.64 (95% CI, 0.44–0.78), 0.60 (95% CI, 0.39–0.75), and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.65–0.87), respectively; rater #2, 0.79 (95% CI, 0.66–0.87), 0.72 (95% CI, 0.55–0.83), and 0.70 (95% CI, 0.53–0.82), respectively. These results indicate that there were good interrater and intrarater agreements of the measurements and no significant differences among the methods.

Discussion

In this study, using data sets of CTA and DSA images collected in the CAS‐CARE trial, we revealed that the conventional CTA measurement using the inner edges of the stent struts as a boundary to the lumen (CTA_edge) remarkably overestimated ISS degree of cervical carotid arteries because of the blooming artifacts, which are comparable to the results of previous studies. 9 , 10 , 11 In contrast, the measurement using midportions of the stent struts (CTA_mid), which we introduced, showed good agreement against DSA without significant systematic biases, regardless of the stent type. These results suggest that CTA can be used to assess ISS degree after CAS as a less invasive substitute for DSA when the CTA_mid method is adopted.

On CTA, blooming artifacts caused by metallic stents exaggerate the size of the stent struts and result in spatial deviation of the apparent boundaries between the stent and lumen, which can cause overestimation of in‐stent luminal narrowing after CAS. In addition, the severity of blooming artifacts depends on the size, material, and design of the stents, as well as reconstruction filters and display conditions, that is, the window level/width, in CT. 9 , 10 , 11 , 13 , 14 Although previous studies attempted to minimize the effects of the blooming artifact by standardizing reconstruction algorithms and display conditions, overestimation of ISS has not been eliminated. 10 , 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Hence, we need a new imaging landmark insensitive to the blooming artifacts to determine the stent lumen boundaries for the measurement of ISS using CTA. For this purpose, we adopted the midportion of the stent struts as the landmark because it can be readily identified and is independent of blooming artifacts, regardless of stent type or image quality. The results of this study indicate that the midportion of the stent struts is an appropriate landmark for estimating the stent lumen boundaries on CTA and can overcome the overestimation of luminal narrowing after CAS.

In this study, the overestimation of ISS degree when using the CTA_edge method was more evident in closed‐cell stents than in open‐cell stents. Blooming artifacts depend on stent diameter, strut thickness, strut material, and mesh design. 9 , 10 , 17 Among these, no apparent difference was observed in stent diameter between open‐ and closed‐cell stents in this study. Regarding strut thickness, a previous study 18 reported that the closed‐cell stent (0.11 mm) was thinner than the open‐cell stents (0.18–0.20 mm), which does not explain the stronger artifacts seen in the former. In this study, the strut materials differed between the open‐ and closed‐cell stents. Generally, blooming artifacts depend on the atomic number of the metallic materials, that is, titanium 22, cobalt 27, nickel 28, and tantalum 73. 19 , 20 The closed‐cell stents we used consisted of a cobalt‐chrome alloy with a radiopaque tantalum core. 21 Thus, the tantalum in the closed‐cell stents is considered to cause profound blooming artifacts compared with the nickel‐titanium alloy in the open‐cell stents in this study. In contrast, a previous in vitro study showed no significant differences between several kinds of open‐cell and closed‐cell stents, which consisted of a nickel‐titanium alloy and a cobalt‐chrome alloy with tantalum, respectively. 9 This discrepancy with our results can be partly explained by the different stent design, size, and/or vendor. The variation in the mesh design may have contributed to the artifacts observed in this study, although a previous study demonstrated that mesh designs had minimal effects on the artifacts. 22 Further investigation is needed to clarify the effects of different stent types on the assessment of ISS.

For luminal measurement after CAS with CTA, previous studies used cross‐sectional images perpendicular to the long axis of the internal carotid arterys, which were generated using a multiplanar reformation (MPR) method. 7 , 23 In general, cross‐sectional MPR images can be generated by a simple postprocessing technique and can accurately depict luminal shape and size. However, the MPR images include substantial difficulties in obtaining a longitudinal overview of the target vessel and calculating the stenotic degree using the NASCET method. 24 Therefore, we decided to use CPR images to overcome the issue of the MPR. However, the CPR method requires advanced postprocessing software, is relatively time consuming, particularly in tortuous vessels, and is somewhat operator dependent, which may cause some biases in luminal depiction. 24 Nevertheless, this technique can provide longitudinal views similar to DSA images, which appear appropriate for simple and straightforward measurements using the NASCET method. Although we did not compare CPR and MPR images, our results indicate that CPR images are feasible for the accurate assessment of post‐CAS ISS by CTA in centers where CPR‐capable software is available.

CUS and MRA are less invasive methods for the periodic assessment of postoperative ISS. However, CUS is operator dependent and includes issues related to accuracy and reproducibility. The reported cutoff values of PSV for ISS of ≥50% and ≥70% have substantially varied from 125 to 240 cm/s and 250 to 450 cm/s, respectively. 7 , 25 Our results showed that the correlation of PSV with DSA was significantly lower than that with CTA. In general, the image quality of standard MRA techniques can be severely impaired by strong susceptibility artifacts induced by metallic stents, resulting in intraluminal signal reduction or loss. Although recently introduced MRA techniques using an ultra‐short echo time have been reported to improve the visibility of the stent lumen, 26 , 27 these techniques can be applied only in limited institutes. In contrast, CTA in combination with the CTA_mid method is widely available in clinical practice, suggesting that it is the most promising technique for assessing post‐CAS restenosis. Further comparative studies are needed to establish the advantages of the CTA_mid method over CUS and MRA.

This study has several limitations. First, we could not evaluate the diagnostic performance of the CTA_mid method for substantial ISS of ≥50% because there were few patients with substantial ISS determined by DSA, as the majority of those patients underwent no CTA examination and were excluded from this study. This preliminary study had a limited number of ISS cases and lacked sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences between the CTA_edge and CTA_mid methods. In addition, the CTA_mid method was applied only in the post‐hoc central measurement. Hence, it remains unclear whether CTA_mid can accurately detect substantial ISS and effectively change clinical management as an alternative to DSA. It is also unclear whether the method can be used to evaluate differences in ISS among stent types, which previous studies have demonstrated. 28 , 29 Second, there were several factors that could have affected the results of this study. The intervals between CTA and DSA varied, particularly during the 2‐year follow‐up examinations, although they were within the study protocol. This limitation did not exclude the possibility of temporal changes in in‐stent conditions between the 2 modalities. However, the variability between CTA and DSA observed during the 2 years following CAS could be insignificant, as previous studies have reported that restenosis occurs mostly within the first year after CAS. 30 The image quality regarding the spatial resolution and signal‐to‐noise ratio varied due to the use of CT scanners of various vendors and types from various institutions were used, although the imaging protocols, including the tube voltage, field of view, and reconstruction filter were standardized. In addition, the projection directions of the CPR images were not always identical to those of the DSA images because the images of the measurements were conducted on the images where the in‐stent lumen appeared narrowest. Furthermore, the manual measurement method we adopted was subjected to operator‐dependent variability, which could be reduced by using automatic measurement techniques, as previously reported in coronary stent studies. 15 If these factors are optimized, the accuracy of the CTA_mid method could be improved further. Third, we did not perform comparative studies between CPR and MPR images or between CTA and MRA images because these issues were beyond the scope of this study. In addition, we did not examine whether the CTA_mid method could be applied to other stented vessels, such as the coronary arteries. Fourth, carotid CTAs sometimes include substantial motion artifacts, caused in particular by swallowing during the scan, which may be a potential limitation of CTA in real‐world settings, although only 1 patient with severe motion artifacts was identified in this study. Further studies are needed to evaluate these issues and establish the feasibility and advantages of the CTA_mid method.

Conclusions

To address the issue of CTA overestimating post‐CAS ISS of carotid arteries due to blooming artifacts, we proposed a new measurement method using the midportion of the stent strut as a boundary to the lumen. We revealed that the proposed method accurately evaluated stenotic severities both in open‐cell and closed‐cell types of stents when compared with DSA.
