Predicting pathological complete response after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in rectal cancer: beyond morphologic MRI
Authors: Semra Demirli Atici, Aras Emre Canda, Mustafa Cem Terzi
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

We read with interest the article by Li et al. [1], which investigated the predictive value of MRI variables for pathological complete response (pCR) in locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT). Their large single-center analysis of 585 patients and construction of a nomogram to estimate pCR probability is an important contribution to the growing field of treatment personalization in rectal cancer. We congratulate the authors for addressing this clinically relevant question. However, we would like to emphasize certain concerns that deserve additional attention.

pCR (pT0N0) is frequently employed as a surrogate endpoint in rectal cancer research; nevertheless, its reliability remains debated. While patients achieving pCR often exhibit favorable survival, pooled analyses and multicenter randomized trials demonstrate that pCR does not consistently correlate with long-term survival, particularly in the context of immunotherapy and total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) [2]. Therefore, reliance on pCR alone may inflate the clinical significance of predictive models. Additional endpoints such as disease-free survival (DFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and organ preservation should be incorporated to provide a more comprehensive assessment of treatment effectiveness.

Li et al. [1] relied exclusively on morphologic MRI variables, including T stage, tumor size, and extramural vascular invasion. While valuable, these parameters do not fully capture tumor biology. Functional MRI sequences such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and perfusion imaging provide complementary insights. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values have been associated with tumor cellularity; studies show that lower baseline ADC and greater post-treatment ADC increase predict pCR [3]. Similarly, perfusion-derived parameters such as K^trans (volume transfer constant) and blood flow metrics can differentiate responders from non-responders [4]. These methods are technically feasible with 1.5–3 T MRI scanners, though standardization remains a challenge.

Radiomics and artificial intelligence further enhance predictive performance. Radiomics models have demonstrated strong predictive performance, with pooled AUC values of approximately 0.81 confirmed in recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses [5]. Although promising, radiomics remains largely a research tool; clinical adoption requires standardized image acquisition, robust feature harmonization, external validation, and integration into radiology workflows.

In addition, incorporation of molecular biomarkers such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and immune-related signatures can provide powerful complementary predictors. Prospective studies have demonstrated that serial ctDNA dynamics correlate with tumor regression and early relapse risk [6]. Integrating molecular and imaging biomarkers into predictive frameworks may allow for more personalized and biologically informed treatment strategies.

The presented nomogram underwent internal validation, demonstrating a favorable concordance (C-index 0.778). Nonetheless, the absence of external, multicenter validation constrains its applicability to various clinical environments. Experience from prior predictive models highlights that performance often declines across centers due to differences in imaging protocols, treatment regimens, and patient populations [6]. External validation with harmonized imaging standards is imperative before clinical implementation.

In conclusion, Li and colleagues [1] deserve commendation for their efforts to enhance patient selection for organ-preserving strategies using MRI-based predictors of pCR. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when interpreting pCR as an isolated endpoint. Future predictive models should combine morphologic and functional MRI with radiomics, validated molecular biomarkers, and undergo multicenter validation to ensure robustness and clinical utility. Such integration will help translate predictive modeling from research innovation to routine practice.
