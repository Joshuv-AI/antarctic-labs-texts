Secular Trends in Lipid Management Among U.S. Veterans With Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease, 2003 to 2020
Authors: Patrick Benjamin, Helen Marucci-Wellman, Rachel Ward, David Gagnon, Michael J Gaziano, Luc Djoussé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent guidelines for patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) have explicitly highlighted the direct correlation of reduced low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) with a subsequent risk reduction in further ischemic events. Little is known about secular trends of lipid management among U.S. Veterans with ASCVD over the past 2 decades.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to determine the trend in the proportion of Veterans with ASCVD who are receiving lipid-lowering therapy treated to goal (LDL-C <70 mg/dL) between 2003 and 2020.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study, with annual accounting of treatment information and LDL-C lab results from 2003 to 2020, among U.S. Veterans with prevalent ASCVD who had at least 1 LDL-C lab value in the year before January 1st of the index year. We investigated annual trends in the proportion of Veterans with ASCVD who achieved an LDL-C <70 mg/dL while taking lipid-lowering agents.ResultsThe cohort grew from 642,082 Veterans with ASCVD in 2003 to 1.18 million in 2020, representing an older, high-risk population with substantial comorbidity. At baseline, up to 85.4% of Veterans had LDL-C >70 mg/dL. Over the 17-year period, the proportion achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL rose from 14.5% to 41.6%. These observations were consistent across subgroups, including Veterans >75 years, who experienced marked increases in LDL-C improvement.ConclusionsWe observed a clear trend toward higher prevalence of LDL-C control among Veterans with ASCVD from 2003 to 2020. Despite this progress, most Veterans remain above guideline-recommended LDL-C targets, underscoring the need for continued optimization of lipid-lowering therapy.Key words: atherosclerotic vascular disease, lipid-lowering therapy, statin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading cause of death globally, accounting for substantial morbidity, mortality, and health care costs.1 Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) plays a central role in the pathogenesis of ASCVD, and decades of randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that lowering LDL-C reduces the risk of recurrent ischemic events.2,3 Statins, ezetimibe, and proprotein convertase subtilisin/Kexin type 9 inhibitors have all shown incremental benefit in reducing LDL-C and improving cardiovascular outcomes.4

Current 2026 American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidelines recommend intensive lipid-lowering therapy (LLT), with treatment goals targeting LDL-C <70 mg/dL in patients with established ASCVD and 55 mg/dL for individuals with ASCVD at very high risk.5 Interpretation of temporal trends in LDL-C control requires consideration of the evolving paradigm of lipid management, particularly the tension between target-based and intensity-based treatment strategies. Earlier approaches adopted by the Department of Veterans’ Affairs (VA)/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) emphasized achieving specific LDL-C thresholds (eg, <100 mg/dL, with optional <70 mg/dL for very high-risk ASCVD) reinforcing a treat-to-target framework. Over the past 2 decades, however, accumulating evidence supporting a “lower is better” approach to LDL-C reduction has driven a paradigm shift toward more aggressive lipid management.6,7 Within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), this transition was formalized with the 2014 VA/DoD Dyslipidemia Guideline and reaffirmed in subsequent updates, which moved away from fixed LDL-C targets in favor of statin intensity–based risk-guided therapy.8,9 In practice, these paradigms have overlapped, as clinicians often continue to use LDL-C thresholds to guide treatment escalation, particularly with the expanding availability of nonstatin therapies. Accordingly, secular improvements in LDL-C levels likely reflect the combined influence of increased use of higher-intensity therapies, improved adherence, and residual goal-directed care. Nevertheless, substantial gaps in LDL-C control persist in real-world practice, with many patients continuing to exceed recommended AHA/ACC thresholds despite the availability of guideline-directed therapies.10, 11, 12

Veterans represent a unique population with a high burden of ASCVD and comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, and obesity.9,13 The VHA provides a large, integrated health care system with standardized access to LLTs, making it an ideal setting to evaluate long-term trends in LDL-C control.9,14 However, secular trends in lipid management within this population have not been well characterized, particularly in relation to evolving guideline recommendations.

Understanding these trends is critical for several reasons. First, they provide insight into the effectiveness of health system interventions and guideline dissemination over time.15 Second, they highlight potential gaps in care that may inform future quality improvement initiatives.16 Finally, they offer a window into how real-world practice aligns with clinical trial evidence and guideline recommendations.17

We conducted a large retrospective cohort study of U.S. Veterans with ASCVD from 2003 to 2020 to examine annual trends in the proportion achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL and the corresponding patterns of statin use over this period.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective cohort study of patients identified through the VHA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) with prevalent ASCVD between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2019. ASCVD was defined as coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, cerebral vascular disease, or peripheral arterial disease. At least 2 outpatient or 1 inpatient diagnosis or 1 procedure using International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th revision codes, and Current Procedural Terminology codes were required for inclusion. The first date of diagnosis was used to assign the prevalence of ASCVD for each year.

We extracted all directly measured LDL-C laboratory values from the CDW for Veterans with ASCVD between 2002 and 2020. LDL-C levels were calculated when triglyceride levels were <400 mg/dL, using the Friedwald equation (LDL-C = total cholesterol − high-density lipoprotein cholesterol − triglycerides/5). For triglyceride levels ≥400 mg/dL, LDL-C was directly measured. Veterans with ASCVD and at least 1 LDL-C value during a given year were considered VA health care users and included in the denominator for that year. The most recent lab closest to the index date for each year (eg, the lab dated before January 1, 2020, for 2019 labs) was used as the reference LDL-C for that year. Those with a current LDL-C below 70 mg/dL were considered “at goal” for that year. Analyses were restricted to veterans with ASCVD and available LDL-C measurements within each study year; no imputation was performed, as the objective was to describe observed population-level trends. Information on specific LDL-C assay methods was not available; however, laboratory measurements within the VA system are standardized and subject to quality control procedures.

All LLTs were also extracted from the CDW for every Veteran with ASCVD any time between 2002 and 2020. Each Veteran with prevalent ASCVD and an LDL-C in each year was then flagged as on any LLT or on a statin LLT if they picked up at least 1 prescription covering time in that year (i.e. for index year 2020 there had to be at least 1 prescription covering time in 2019). Any LLT included any of the following: statin, fibrate, resin, ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/Kexin type 9 inhibitor, niacin, and omega 3 fatty acids. We found very few Veterans on nonstatin LLT (1% to 3%) over the 18-year period, therefore this paper is focused on statin use only.

Covariates

Age, sex, race, ethnicity, and body mass index (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) at the ASCVD-index date were included. Self-reported race and ethnicity were obtained from responses to fixed categories on enrollment in the VHA. Diagnosis and procedure codes, with dates, were used to determine the prevalence of all comorbid conditions on or before January 1st of each reference year.

Ethical approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the VA, with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective analysis of deidentified data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the proportion of Veterans with prevalent ASCVD who had at least 1 LDL-C measurement in the year before January 1 of each index year and who filled at least 1 statin prescription with coverage during that same period. Similarly, we assessed the proportion of Veterans whose LDL-C measurement closest to the index date was below 70 mg/dL, categorizing them as treated to goal. These analyses were performed overall and stratified by age group (≤75 years and >75 years at index).

Continuous demographic and covariate variables were summarized as mean +/- SD, whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages. To evaluate temporal trends in LDL-C goal attainment over the study period, age- and sex-adjusted multivariable logistic regression models were constructed with index year as the primary independent variable. Least-squares means derived from the logistic models were used to estimate adjusted predicted probabilities of LDL-C <70 mg/dL for each calendar year with corresponding 95% CIs. Temporal trends were assessed using the overall effect of index year in the logistic regression models, and results were displayed graphically as adjusted predicted probabilities over time.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide (version 8.3; SAS Institute Inc.). A 2-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

At the beginning of the study in 2003, the cohort comprised 642,082 Veterans with prevalent ASCVD. By 2020, the final analytic cohort included 1,182,657 Veterans with ASCVD. At baseline (2003), the mean age was 72.5 years and 97% were males. The mean body mass index was 29.8 kg/m2, and the prevalence of hypertension was 78%, whereas 42% had diabetes mellitus and 18% had chronic kidney disease (Table 1, Supplemental Table 2).

Primary analysis: secular trends in LDL-C goal attainment

Over the 17-year study period, the proportion of Veterans with ASCVD who achieved LDL-C <70 mg/dL increased substantially. Among individuals receiving at least 1 LLT, LDL-C goal attainment rose from 14.5% in 2003% to 41.6% in 2020. The most pronounced gains occurred between 2003 and 2012, with continued but slower increases thereafter. Annual trends in LDL-C control are shown in Figure 1 and Supplemental Figure 1. In multivariable logistic regression analyses, the adjusted predicted probability of achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL increased progressively from 11.6% (95% CI: 11.5% to 11.7%) in 2003% to 34.7% (95% CI: 34.6% to 34.8%) in 2020 (P-trend <0.001). Although LDL-C goal attainment continued to improve after the 2013 ACC/AHA cholesterol guideline transition, the rate of improvement appeared more gradual in later study years (Figure 2).

Secondary analyses: mean LDL-C levels and statin utilization

In secondary analyses, we examined secular trends in the mean LDL-C values and statin use. At baseline (2003), 554,954 Veterans (86.4%) had LDL-C ≥70 mg/dL, with a mean LDL-C of 101.1 mg/dL (SD 31.4). By 2020, mean LDL-C had declined to 84.7 mg/dL (SD 33.8), representing a 16 mg/dL reduction over the study period.

Statin utilization also demonstrated notable temporal variation. The use increased from 72.3% in 2003 to a peak of 77.5% in 2007, followed by a gradual decline to 72.0% in 2020 (Figure 1).

Subgroup analyses

Improvements in LDL-C control were consistent across major demographic and clinical subgroups. Age-stratified analyses revealed similar improvements across younger (≤75 years) and older (>75 years) Veterans (Supplemental Table 2). In the older groups, the proportion of veterans with ASCVD on at least o1ne lipid-lowering agent with LDL-C at target increased substantially, rising from 16.6% to 47.3% over the study period (Supplemental Figure 2). By 2020, the mean LDL-C levels were lower in the older subgroup (80.3 mg/dL) compared with the general population (84.7 mg/dL). Subgroup analyses are presented in Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure 2.

Discussion

In this large national cohort of Veterans with ASCVD, there was evidence of increasing proportion of Veterans reaching target LDL-C level over nearly 2 decades. The proportion of patients achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL rose steadily from 14.5% in 2003 to 41.6% in 2020 Central Illustration (Supplemental Table 1). The most rapid gains occurred between 2003 and 2012, a period that aligned with major guideline updates (National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III) and broader uptake focusing on treating hyperlipidemia to a strict target and performance measurement.4,18 Although progress continued through 2020, the rate of LDL-C improvement slowed thereafter roughly after the release of the ACC/AHA lipid guideline in 2013, which is consistent with national trends.2,6

The mean LDL-C in our study decreased by approximately 16 mg/dL over a period of 2 decades. This LDL-C lowering effect was concurrent with an increased uptake in statin use, from 72.3% in 2003 to a peak of 77.5% in 2007 Central Illustration. During the 2000s and early 2010s, statin use expanded rapidly as evidence for their secondary-prevention benefits became widely accepted, producing substantial population-level reductions in LDL-C. National data sets similarly show sharper declines in LDL-C and higher control rates during this earlier period.19,20 The observed discordance between relatively stable statin utilization and substantial improvement in LDL-C control suggests that factors beyond statin initiation contributed to these trends. The early period of rapid LDL-C reduction (2003-2012) likely reflects a combination of increased statin uptake, progressive dose intensification, and improved adherence within a predominantly target-driven treatment paradigm. In contrast, the plateau observed thereafter may represent a ceiling effect in achievable LDL-C reduction at the population level, potentially influenced by evolving guideline recommendations, including the 2014 VA/DoD shift toward intensity-based therapy and de-emphasis of fixed LDL-C targets.9 Although causal inference is limited, this temporal pattern suggests that both therapeutic optimization and shifts in treatment philosophy may have shaped longitudinal trends in lipid control.

Although the comorbidity burden rose markedly—with increases in stroke (17.5% to 41.3%), peripheral artery disease (6.7% to 18.9%), and myocardial infarction (6.9% to 26.4%) (Table 1), statin utilization remained essentially unchanged. This discordance highlights a mismatch between escalating cardiovascular risk and treatment intensification and suggests that factors beyond statin initiation alone contributed to improvements in lipid control. Within an integrated health care system such as the VHA, this pattern may reflect a system-level efficiency curve, whereby early gains in treatment uptake are followed by diminishing incremental improvements despite increasing clinical need.21 Collectively, these findings suggest that future gains in lipid control will depend less on expanding treatment eligibility and more on optimizing system-level implementation and therapeutic intensification.

Subgroup analyses in our cohort showed consistent improvement in LDL-C control across age groups, with older Veterans achieving the lowest mean LDL-C levels by 2020. The observation shown in Figure 3 that Veterans older than 75 years consistently achieved higher rates of LDL-C goal attainment than their younger counterparts, despite lower statin use, represents an important and unexpected paradox. This pattern suggests that factors beyond statin prescribing alone are influencing lipid control in this population. Older adults may demonstrate better medication adherence,22,23 more regular engagement with the health system, or more stable long-term therapy patterns, all of which could contribute to superior LDL-C outcomes. Conversely, younger Veterans may face greater challenges with adherence,24 lifestyle-related risk factors, or therapeutic inertia following suboptimal LDL-C results. This apparent paradox may reflect selection bias, as older individuals with available LDL-C measurements may represent a healthier or more engaged subset of the population. Survivor effects may also contribute, whereby individuals who reach older age may have more favorable baseline risk profiles or greater treatment responsiveness.25

The divergence between statin use and LDL-C control across age groups underscores the need for targeted interventions to improve adherence and follow-up among younger patients while maintaining effective management strategies in older adults. This age-related pattern also highlights the importance of individualized lipid management approaches within the VHA, ensuring that both younger and older Veterans receive the support necessary to achieve guideline-recommended LDL-C targets.

The documented improvements herein constitute substantial progress in secondary prevention; however, it is still less than the possible statin use rate of 95% reported in controlled secondary preventive trials.24,26 Nevertheless, the plateau in LDL-C reduction and statin usage among statin-eligible Veterans after 2007 indicate the necessity of additional strategies to maintain momentum. The VA has implemented interventions aimed at enhancing patient education, ensuring treatment adherence, and addressing systemic barriers. These interventions have demonstrated promising outcomes and are already yielding significant impacts. Quality improvement initiatives such as the Quality Improvement and Personalization for Statins program and the Veterans Affairs Lipid Optimization Reimagined Quality Improvement program—which incorporate health coaching and structured system supports—have demonstrated success in further lowering LDL-C among Veterans.27,28 Wider implementation of such programs, combined with guideline-driven therapy intensification, could help maintain and enhance optimal LDL-C control in this high-risk population.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. Reliance on routinely collected clinical data introduces the potential for misclassification of comorbidities and medication use, and incomplete availability of LDL-C measurements across all years may contribute to selection bias. Residual bias due to missing LDL-C measurements and potential temporal variation in assay methods cannot be excluded, although such effects are unlikely to fully explain the observed trends.

The cohort was predominantly males, reflecting the demographic composition of the VHA, which may limit generalizability to broader populations. In addition, statin intensity and medication adherence could not be assessed, and the observational design precludes causal inference regarding LLT and LDL-C outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. The exceptionally large, nationally representative cohort of Veterans with ASCVD and the extended 17-year observation period provide a robust platform for evaluating long-term secular trends in lipid management across multiple guideline eras. The use of real-world data from an integrated health system ensures consistent laboratory measurement, reliable medication capture, and comprehensive documentation of comorbidities, enabling precise characterization of lipid control patterns in this high-risk population.

Conclusions

In this large national cohort of Veterans with ASCVD, we observed a marked and sustained increase in the proportion achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL between 2003 and 2020, reflecting substantial progress in lipid control across multiple guideline eras. Although mean LDL-C levels declined and statin utilization patterns evolved over time, these observations reinforce the central finding that LDL-C goal attainment has become increasingly prevalent within the VHA. Continued efforts to optimize LLT remain essential, as most Veterans still do not reach recommended LDL-C targets despite these long-term gains.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Among more than 1 million U.S. Veterans with established ASCVD followed from 2003 through 2020, LDL-C levels declined substantially, with the proportion achieving LDL-C <70 mg/dL increasing from 14.5% to 41.6%. These gains occurred despite the transition from LDL target–based strategies to risk-based statin intensity recommendations. Notably, statin utilization remained essentially unchanged over time and was consistently lower among adults ≥75 years, even as comorbidity burden increased, highlighting a persistent disconnect between rising clinical risk and treatment uptake.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The discordance between improving LDL-C control and stable statin utilization suggests that factors beyond prescribing—such as adherence, care delivery processes, and access to adjunctive therapies—may be driving observed gains. Persistent undertreatment in high-risk subgroups, particularly older adults, represents a measurable quality gap. Future efforts should focus on system-level interventions, including performance metrics tied to LDL-C threshold attainment, structured treatment intensification pathways, and adherence-focused strategies, to reduce therapeutic inertia and ensure consistent implementation of evidence-based secondary prevention.
