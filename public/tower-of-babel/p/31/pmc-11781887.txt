Introduction

The last decade has seen growing interest in leadless cardiac pacing systems. The appeal of leadless pacemaker (LP) implantation is potentially avoiding complications associated with conventional transvenous systems, such as infection, lead fracture, and tricuspid valve (TV) dysfunction.1 However, evidence to date has provided mixed results as to whether leadless systems do, in fact, reduce the risk of TV dysfunction.2,3 Furthermore, there are limited data regarding the feasibility and challenges associated with LP extraction. We present a case of a 67-year-old man who had an LP inadvertently implanted into the TV subvalvular apparatus, leading to severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and ultimately requiring removal 3 years later. We sought to highlight the following 2 important observations: (1) leadless devices do, in fact, retain a risk of TV dysfunction, albeit through a different mechanism from conventional lead-based systems and (2) the fibrous encapsulation of LP that occurs over prolonged dwell times poses a significant challenge for device extraction, even with a direct surgical approach.

Case report

A 67-year-old man presented to another institution with intermittent presyncope and was found to have intermittent 2:1 atrioventricular block on Holter monitoring. His transthoracic echocardiogram was normal. Given his active lifestyle, including competitive rowing, he underwent LP implantation (Micra-AV; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) at the outside institution. Due to poor atrial sensing, the device was programmed VVI with a lower rate limit of 40 beats/min. There was no improvement in his intermittent presyncope. Pacemaker interrogation revealed <1% pacing at most recent follow-up.

The patient presented 3 years later with dyspnea on exertion. Coronary angiography revealed severe multivessel disease, and he was referred for coronary artery bypass grafting. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated normal biventricular size and function and no significant valvular dysfunction, although poor acoustic windows were noted. Transesophageal echocardiography, however, revealed mild segmental left ventricular (LV) dysfunction and severe eccentric TR due to interference from the Micra-AV device, with the subvalvular apparatus causing tethering of the septal TV leaflet (Figure 1A and 1B).

B: Color flow Doppler revealing broad jet eccentric tricuspid regurgitation with large proximal flow convergence zone. C: Computed tomography demonstrating septal placement of Micra-AV. D: Post–transvenous device implantation chest radiograph demonstrating right ventricular lead in apicoseptum, atrial lead in the right atrial appendage, and epicardial lead connected to cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator.

Following successful coronary artery bypass grafting, the right ventricle was approached via the right atrium to extract the Micra-AV under direct visualization. The device was tightly adherent to the right ventricular (RV) septum, owing to fibrotic encasement of the distal portion of the device, as well as the fixation tines. Simultaneous blunt dissection, traction, and counter-traction were performed to ultimately extract the device (see Supplemental Video 1). The patient then underwent successful TV annuloplasty with satisfactory valve function post procedure.

During his cardiac surgery, a bipolar suture-on lead (4968 CapSure Epi; Medtronic) was placed on the RV epicardium and tunneled up to the left pectoral area in anticipation of requiring permanent dual-chamber pacing prior to discharge. However, during his postoperative period, he experienced multiple episodes of rapid nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. Given his ongoing mild segmental LV dysfunction, the decision was made to proceed with a transvenous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. A dual-coil defibrillator lead (6947M Sprint Quattro; Medtronic) was implanted in the RV apicoseptum and a bipolar lead (5076; Medtronic) was implanted into the right atrial appendage. To minimize the risk of TV injury, the RV lead was placed by prolapsing the lead through the TV repair.4 The leads were then connected to a cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator (Cobalt XT HF; Medtronic), with the epicardial RV lead (4968; Medtronic) connected to an IS-1 LV lead port (with the LV lead programmed off). The device was programmed AAIR-DDDR 60–130 beats/min.

Discussion

TR is increasingly recognized as a clinically important complication of implantable cardiac devices occurring in approximately 20% of patients.5 In conventional transvenous devices, this is thought to occur primarily as a result of mechanical interference between the endocardial lead and the tricuspid apparatus.6 Importantly, lead-related TR has been associated with an increased risk of mortality and heart failure.7 Although it was initially thought that LPs were exempt from this complication, numerous observational studies have since reported comparable rates of TR in patients undergoing LP implantation, ranging from 20% to 40%.8,9 Several possible mechanisms have been reported.2,8 First, as highlighted in this case, is mechanical impingement from the LP with the tricuspid and sub-valvular apparatus. This was further demonstrated by Hai and colleagues,9 who found that the distance between the LP and TV annulus was an independent predictor for development of TR.9 Similarly, Beurskens and colleagues2 found a more septal, compared with apical, position was associated with a 5-fold increased risk of worsening TR.2 Taken together, this suggests that an apical position may be more favorable in terms of long-term TR risk, albeit at an increased short-term risk of pericardial effusion.10 Second, injury may occur to the TV at the time of the index procedure. Third, as is the case for both leadless and transvenous devices, RV pacing may result in mechanical dyssynchrony and long-term RV remodeling, giving rise to development or worsening of TR.11 The impact on TV function from emerging dual-chamber leadless systems has yet to be established. The minimization of RV pacing and preservation of atrioventricular synchrony from dual-chamber systems may indeed lead to less TR. Furthermore, intracardiac and transesophageal echocardiography–guided LP implantation may aid with more accurate positioning and, therefore, in reducing the risk of long-term TV dysfunction.12,13

Despite the expanded use of LP, there remains a paucity of data to guide device extraction. Although many devices include design features to allow extraction via percutaneous snaring, this is often challenging, owing to fibrous encapsulation of the device following prolonged dwell times.14, 15, 16 As described in an autopsy study by Kypta and colleagues,15 this encapsulation may occur as early as 1-year post implantation. This adhesion renders extraction difficult, even with a direct surgical approach, as highlighted by this case. Nonetheless, Kiani and colleagues17 and Callahan and Wilkoff14 described successful percutaneous extractions of Medtronic LPs at 4 and 5 years, respectively, interestingly with the use of alternate manufacturer’s snare and extraction tools. Further studies are needed to gain a better understanding of the risks and outcomes associated with late LP extraction. In addition, the advent of specialized manufacturer-endorsed tools may also be important to minimize procedural risks.

This case also highlights the challenges associated with identifying TR on transthoracic echocardiography in patients with an LP. Underestimation of TR from implantable devices is common, and may occur due to acoustic shadowing, as well as highly eccentric regurgitant jets.8 As emphasized by Chang and colleagues,6 a high level of suspicion for TR is required, and a thorough examination of the TV is recommended, including routine Doppler assessment of the hepatic vein for systolic flow reversal. Examinations with increased sensitivity for TR, such as 3-dimensional and transesophageal echocardiography, should also be considered.6

Conclusion

LPs may be complicated by inadvertent TV injury and dysfunction. The LP may be tightly adherent to the TV apparatus and may be difficult to remove, even with a direct surgical approach. Furthermore, LP-associated TR requires careful attention, as the jet may be very eccentric and can be underappreciated on transthoracic echocardiography.
