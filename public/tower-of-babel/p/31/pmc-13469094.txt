A multi-fidelity tabular prior-data fitted network model for accurate prediction and uncertainty quantification
Authors: Yan Shi, Cheng Liu, Aodi Yu, Zhenzhou Lu, Said Elias, Kai Cheng, Jiaqing Kou, Xin Chen, Yu Liu, Hong-Zhong Huang, Michael Beer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of unknown labels from feature-label datasets using machine learning is critical for applications spanning drug discovery, disease diagnostics, and climate science. However, challenges persist with limited data, high-dimensional inputs, and multi-fidelity scenarios. We developed multi-fidelity tabular prior-data fitted network (MFTabPFN), a general-purpose multi-fidelity model integrating low- and high-fidelity data through a hierarchical transformer architecture to enhance prediction accuracy and uncertainty quantification (UQ). MFTabPFN captures cross-fidelity correlations while seamlessly adapting to single-fidelity data. An active learning framework further enhances scalability by prioritizing high-value data for model refinement, minimizing resource demands in resource-intensive tasks. Evaluated on various tasks such as forest fire burned area prediction, wine quality assessment, and computational fluid dynamics, MFTabPFN outperforms state-of-the-art methods, achieving varying degrees of prediction accuracy improvement. Its versatility and robust prediction and UQ capabilities across single- and multi-fidelity datasets position MFTabPFN as a promising tool for data-driven discovery in diverse applications.Subject terms: Computational science, Statistics, Aerospace engineering, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative force across diverse disciplines, including pathology1, climate science2, and materials design3, fundamentally reshaping scientific inquiry and societal applications. As a critical branch of AI, machine learning (ML) models, such as large language models4, trained on extensive datasets, have achieved remarkable advancements in a wide range of tasks. However, in many real-world applications, acquiring large-scale datasets is often infeasible, with only limited data available, such as those derived from labor- or resource-intensive experiments5 or computationally demanding simulations6. Consequently, developing advanced ML models capable of effectively handling tasks with limited data availability represents a critical research priority.

Supervised learning, a cornerstone of ML, involves predicting unknown labels from feature-label datasets and is pivotal for applications ranging from drug discovery7, disease diagnostics8, to biomedical research9. To achieve high prediction accuracy for unseen input features, training datasets should comprehensively span the feature space, enabling ML models to capture the underlying characteristics of the problem. Conversely, limited training data often impairs the ability of ML models to achieve high prediction accuracy. Thus, developing accurate ML models for tasks with constrained data availability remains a significant challenge. At present, several ML models have been developed for supervised learning, including linear models (e.g., ridge regression)10, FastAI MLP (FastaiMLP)11, Torch MLP (TorchMLP)11, RealMLP12, TabM13, ModernNCA14, and gradient-boosted decision trees such as XGBoost15, CatBoost16, LightGBM17, extremely randomized trees (ExtraTrees)18, and explainable boosting machine (EBM)19,20, etc. These models have been widely applied to supervised learning tasks, including those with limited data, across diverse disciplines, significantly advancing ML development. To further enhance the problem-solving capabilities of ML models, ensemble learning21 has emerged as a powerful approach, leveraging the strengths of multiple models rather than relying on a single model. A notable example is AutoGluon11, an open-source automated machine learning (AutoML) framework developed by Amazon, designed to streamline and accelerate the development of high-accuracy ML models. Unlike traditional AutoML frameworks that primarily focus on model/hyperparameter selection, AutoGluon excels by ensembling multiple models and stacking them across multiple layers.

Recent advances in ML, particularly transformer-based architectures22–24, have transformed the modeling of complex data structures. The tabular prior-data fitted network (TabPFN)25, a transformer-based in-context learning26 model designed for tabular data (i.e., the foundational format of most real-world datasets), has emerged as a powerful tool for predictive tasks in low-data regimes. This model introduces a two-way attention mechanism, comprising feature attention and sample attention, to capture relationships among features and among samples, respectively. By pre-training on synthetic datasets derived from probabilistic priors, TabPFN encodes rich prior knowledge, enabling rapid adaptation to new tasks with minimal data. A key feature of TabPFN is its use of a piecewise constant output distribution for regression tasks, which enables the model to predict a probability distribution of target values rather than a single point estimate, thereby facilitating the quantification of prediction confidence and uncertainty. Experimental results demonstrate that TabPFN substantially outperforms previous methods on tabular datasets containing up to 500 features and 10,000 samples. The exceptional performance of TabPFN in addressing small- to medium-sized tabular data challenges highlights its potential for extension to other disciplines with limited data availability. Another effective transformer-based in-context learning approach for tabular data is the tabular discriminative pre-trained transformer (TabDPT)27. Unlike conventional pre-training strategies that rely on synthetic data, TabDPT incorporates real tabular data during pre-training, enabling substantially faster convergence and superior generalization to unseen datasets. It employs a row-wise transformer encoder to facilitate in-context learning, leverages self-supervised objectives to augment the pre-training corpus, and adopts retrieval-based in-context example selection during both training and inference. Notably, TabDPT achieves competitive performance across diverse tabular tasks without requiring task-specific fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter optimization. In parallel, large language models with transformer architectures28–30 have also been adapted to tabular problems, often through careful prompt engineering or auxiliary linear probes. These advances collectively represent a transformative shift in ML for tabular data and are rapidly closing, and in some cases surpassing, the long-standing performance advantage historically held by gradient-boosted decision trees.

The aforementioned ML models10–20,25,27–30 are typically designed for single-fidelity datasets, which comprise data generated or collected at a uniform level of accuracy, resolution, or computational cost. However, these models are often unsuitable for direct application to multi-fidelity datasets, which integrate data from multiple sources or methods with varying levels of accuracy, resolution, or computational cost. Multi-fidelity problems typically involve high-fidelity data (e.g., from resource-intensive simulations or experiments) and low-fidelity data (e.g., from less accurate, cost-effective models), and are prevalent in real-world applications. For example, in molecular property prediction31, low-fidelity data typically consist of molecular energies computed using fast semi-empirical methods such as GFN-xTB, whereas high-fidelity data comprise the same properties evaluated at considerably more accurate, yet computationally intensive, density-functional theory. Integrating high- and low-fidelity data offers a promising approach to balance accuracy and computational efficiency. Multi-fidelity ML models32,33 leverage these diverse information sources to achieve superior performance at equivalent or reduced cost compared to single-fidelity approaches. Among these, multi-fidelity Gaussian process regression (MFGPR)34,35, also known as Co-Kriging, has been widely adopted. For example, MFGPR has served as an effective surrogate for expensive black-box functions, with leave-one-out cross-validation employed as an active learning acquisition criterion to iteratively refine the model36, in which the active learning37 refers to a family of techniques designed to intelligently select informative training samples, thereby accelerating convergence and enhancing accuracy under constrained data budgets. To identify multiple, diverse samples with a high value of an objective function, a multi-fidelity active learning framework integrating generative flow networks as the sampler and MFGPR as the surrogate has been proposed38. Similarly, a nonlinear extension of MFGPR (NMFGPR)39, paired with a weighted expected-improvement active learning acquisition function, has been developed to tackle constrained optimization of analog circuit synthesis40. These MFGPR- or NMFGPR-based strategies36,38,40 consistently achieve target performance at a fraction of the budget required by single-fidelity baselines. Nevertheless, MFGPR and NMFGPR often struggle with high-dimensional representations. Such challenges are common in various fields like climate modeling, computational biology, and aerospace engineering, where high-dimensional input spaces govern complex system behaviors driven by numerous interacting variables. Beyond Gaussian-process methodologies, alternative multi-fidelity architectures have emerged, including online multi-fidelity data aggregation via hierarchical neural network (MAHNN) with progressive sampling41, multi-fidelity enhanced few-shot time-series forecasting for structural dynamics42, Fourier neural operator-based multi-fidelity model (FNO-MFM) for physical field prediction43, and other multi-fidelity operator-learning approaches44–48. Despite these advances, efficiently capturing highly nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional, data-scarce multi-fidelity settings remains a formidable challenge, with profound implications for both fundamental research and real-world engineering applications.

Additionally, uncertainties49 are pervasive across diverse fields and significantly influence their applications. For instance, stock market trends are shaped by multiple factors, including corporate profitability, macroeconomic policies, investor sentiment, and many others, all of which are inherently variable and uncertain. Uncertainties are generally classified into aleatoric and epistemic types50. Aleatoric uncertainty, arising from inherent randomness or measurement noise, is irreducible, whereas epistemic uncertainty, stemming from limited knowledge or data, can be mitigated through additional information or improved models. For example, weather forecasting exhibits aleatoric uncertainty due to chaotic atmospheric dynamics, while suboptimal weather models have epistemic uncertainty that can lead to inaccurate predictions, which may be improved with better training data or methods51. Accounting for these uncertainties is critical in various applications. Uncertainty quantification (UQ)52 is the science of quantitatively characterizing and estimating uncertainties in computational and real-world systems. It evaluates how uncertainties in model inputs and parameters affect outputs, enabling predictions of complex systems with associated confidence intervals. ML-based UQ has achieved notable success in fields such as digital histopathology53, molecular science54, and engineering systems55. However, accurate UQ remains challenging in scenarios involving limited data availability, high-dimensional inputs, and multi-fidelity data.

In this study, we propose multi-fidelity TabPFN (MFTabPFN), a general-purpose multi-fidelity model extending the TabPFN to address the challenges of high-dimensional representations and UQ with limited data availability. MFTabPFN employs a hierarchical transformer architecture that dynamically integrates low- and high-fidelity data, capturing cross-fidelity correlations while retaining the strengths of the original TabPFN. This enables effective handling of input spaces with hundreds of dimensions. To enhance scalability for resource-intensive experiments/simulations, we developed an active learning framework that prioritizes the most informative data for model enhancement. Notably, MFTabPFN not only excels in processing multi-fidelity data but also demonstrates improved accuracy on single-fidelity data, broadening its applicability across diverse data types and applications. We evaluated MFTabPFN on a diverse set of problems, including well-established benchmark datasets such as forest fire burned area prediction, wine quality assessment, geographical origin analysis of music, and many others, as well as resource-intensive experiments/simulations in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) for aviation. Our results show that MFTabPFN achieves superior predictive accuracy compared to state-of-the-art methods. Remarkably, MFTabPFN performs well in scenarios with sparse high-fidelity data, a prevalent challenge in scientific computing. By bridging transformer-based ML with multi-fidelity scientific computing, MFTabPFN provides a versatile framework with significant implications for data-driven discovery and advancing research in biology, chemistry, physics, engineering, and beyond.

Results

Construction of MFTabPFN model

Figure 1 provides an overview of the proposed MFTabPFN model, which integrates low- and high-fidelity data through a hierarchical transformer architecture comprising distinct low- and high-fidelity models. The pre-trained TabPFN model25 serves as the low-fidelity model, as shown in Fig. 1a, directly processing low-fidelity data. As the primary objective of the ML model is accurate prediction of high-fidelity data, two sources of error arise when applying TabPFN to low-fidelity data: (i) prediction errors between the low-fidelity data and TabPFN’s outputs, and (ii) discrepancies between low- and high-fidelity data. These errors are mitigated by the high-fidelity model as illustrated in Fig. 1b. To construct the high-fidelity model, we introduce a lightweight encoder module appended to the pre-trained TabPFN model. This encoder transforms raw input features into a latent space that aligns the model’s prior with the downstream data distribution, enhancing predictive accuracy. To optimize the MFTabPFN model, we employ a fine-tuning strategy56 that adjusts the parameters of the lightweight encoder while keeping the pre-trained TabPFN parameters fixed in both the low- and high-fidelity models. This approach offers dual benefits: fine-tuning the encoder enhances the model’s adaptability to specific downstream tasks, while freezing the TabPFN parameters preserves its robust generalization capabilities, derived from pre-training on synthetic datasets. Notably, any advanced feature extractor capable of capturing input feature information can serve as the encoder module. In this study, we develop a residual convolutional neural network (RCNN) for this purpose. The RCNN provides two key advantages: (i) its convolutional architecture effectively handles high-dimensional inputs, and (ii) its residual framework mitigates gradient vanishing, facilitating efficient fine-tuning of the encoder module. Additionally, the multi-layered convolutional structure captures intricate feature information and inter-feature correlations, facilitating robust adaptation to downstream tasks.

The high-fidelity model aims to approximate the discrepancy between high-fidelity labels and scaled predictions from the low-fidelity model, defined as YTr-High−αZLow(XTr-High), where XTr-High and YTr-High represent high-fidelity training features and labels, respectively, ZLow(XTr-High) denotes predictions from the low-fidelity model, and α is a trainable scaling factor optimized alongside the RCNN parameters for downstream tasks. The scaling factor explicitly controls the relative contribution of the low-fidelity and high-fidelity predictions when forming the final combined prediction. where ZLow(XTe) and ZHigh(XTe) are predictions from the low- and high-fidelity models, respectively. Both models incorporate the TabPFN architecture, which generates multiple prediction values per instance, enabling the formation of predictive distributions characterized by probability density functions (PDFs)57 for each model. The final predictive distribution is derived from Eq. (1) by employing the multiple prediction samples from low- and high-fidelity models. Specifically, we form paired combinations by taking one sample from the low-fidelity set and one from the high-fidelity set. For each such pair, we scale the low-fidelity sample by the scaling factor α and add it to the corresponding high-fidelity sample. The resulting value constitutes one sample from the combined multi-fidelity predictive distribution, as defined in Eq. (1). By repeating this weighted addition across many (or all) such pairs, we obtain a large collection of combined samples. This collection collectively approximates the target multi-fidelity predictive distribution, from which prediction mean, prediction variance, and other statistics are readily derived. This distribution reflects epistemic uncertainty in the predicted label, as discussed in the Introduction. In practice, the prediction mean serves as the predicted label, while the prediction variance or confidence interval quantifies prediction uncertainty. A smaller variance or narrower confidence interval indicates higher prediction accuracy. Unlike several traditional ML models that yield a single prediction per instance, the MFTabPFN model’s ability to provide a predictive distribution offers richer information, enhancing decision-making in real-world applications. Notably, the prediction mean of MFTabPFN can also be obtained by scaling the prediction mean of the low-fidelity model by the scaling factor α and adding it to the prediction mean of the corresponding high-fidelity model, according to Eq. (1).

The MFTabPFN model operates in two stages: training and inference. During training, the scaling factor α and the lightweight encoder (e.g., RCNN) parameters are optimized/fine-tuned using low- and high-fidelity datasets, while the pre-trained TabPFN parameters in both models remain fixed. In the inference stage, all parameters are fixed, enabling direct application to diverse tasks. Notably, the MFTabPFN model is also adaptable to single-fidelity data applications. Here, the same dataset replaces both low- and high-fidelity inputs, with the high-fidelity model approximating the discrepancy between labels and scaled predictions from the low-fidelity model, thereby correcting prediction errors between the real data and TabPFN’s outputs. The procedure otherwise remains unchanged. In this study, the scaling factor α is set to 1 for single-fidelity data applications during training to enhance efficiency, and optimized for multi-fidelity data applications to improve model adaptability to complex multi-fidelity datasets. For low-dimensional problems, e.g., the feature dimension n less than 100, a simple multilayer perceptron (MLP) can be employed to replace RCNN as the encoder module in MFTabPFN to enhance training efficiency. Further details on the MFTabPFN model are provided in the “Methods” section.

Accurate prediction and uncertainty quantification on single-fidelity data

We first assessed the predictive performance of MFTabPFN model on single-fidelity data. To this end, we curated 23 regression datasets from diverse sources. From the well-established OpenML-CTR23 benchmarking suite58, we selected 18 datasets that satisfy the input constraints of the original TabPFN model (i.e., up to 10,000 samples and 500 features) and are naturally formulated as regression problems (i.e., we excluded datasets more appropriate for multi-class classification). These datasets cover a broad range of application domains, including forest fire burned area prediction, wine quality assessment, and geographical origin analysis of music. Additionally, to assess MFTabPFN’s performance across varying training data sizes and UQ capabilities, we generated 5 synthetic high-dimensional regression datasets using well-established engineering performance functions (PFs)59,60 (or their direct extensions). The feature dimension of these datasets ranged from 100 to 500 in increments of 100. These PFs model feature-label relationships in engineering systems, enabling evaluation of MFTabPFN’s ability to capture high-dimensional representations in such contexts. The inputs/features to these functions are uniform variables, reflecting aleatoric uncertainty. For each synthetic dataset, the total data size was fixed at three times the feature dimension n. A detailed description of the datasets is provided in the “Methods” section.

We benchmarked MFTabPFN against state-of-the-art methods, including FastaiMLP11, TorchMLP11, RealMLP12, TabM13, ModernNCA14, TabDPT27, gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost15, CatBoost16, LightGBM17, ExtraTrees18, EBM19,20), AutoGluon11, and TabPFN25. We evaluated each method under three regimes: (i) default hyperparameters (D), (ii) tuned hyperparameters (T) obtained via hyperparameter optimization, and (iii) tuned + ensembled configurations (T+E) using post-hoc ensembling of best configurations61. For TabDPT, only the default setting is reported, as the original paper and implementation do not describe or provide hyperparameter optimization protocols. Hyperparameter optimization was performed under a fixed set of 100 random hyperparameter configurations from the search spaces defined in TabArena62 (for all methods except TabPFN), with a fixed maximum computational budget of 1 h per task to enable fair comparison of tuning/ensembling pipelines. For TabPFN, we adopted the search space and protocol recommended in its original publication and official repository. We constructed T and T+E variants of MFTabPFN by replacing the low-fidelity model with the corresponding T and T+E versions of TabPFN, respectively. AutoGluon (version 1.4, with the best_quality preset and 1 h training limit) served as the principal reference ensemble pipeline. Following the hardware recommendations in TabArena62, TabM, ModernNCA, TabDPT, and TabPFN (including the developed MFTabPFN) were executed on the GPU, while all remaining models and AutoGluon were run on the CPU. All experiments were conducted on a workstation running Ubuntu 22.04.4 LTS, equipped with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 5975WX CPU (32 of 64 logical threads allocated), an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48GB GDDR6), and 512GB system RAM.

For each dataset, model performance was assessed using threefold cross-validation, each fold employing a distinct random seed for model running to ensure robust statistical evaluation. We report both raw and normalized performance metrics: root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient of determination (R2), as well as normalized variants of the negative error measures (NNRMSE and NNMAE). For raw RMSE and MAE, the smaller the value, the better the model’s predictive performance. For NNRMSE and NNMAE, the raw negative RMSE and negative MAE of each method are linearly rescaled per dataset such that the best-performing baseline receives a score of 1.0 and the worst receives 0.0. This normalization facilitates direct cross-method and cross-dataset comparison of relative predictive accuracy. For R2, the conventional interpretation applies: a value of 1.0 indicates perfect prediction, 0.0 corresponds to performance equivalent to the mean prediction, and negative values reflect predictions worse than the mean prediction.

Figure 2 compares the predictive performance of MFTabPFN and state-of-the-art baselines across the 23 single-fidelity regression datasets. Under each hyperparameter regime (D, T, and T+E), MFTabPFN consistently achieves the best scores across all raw and normalized metrics. Remarkably, MFTabPFN in its D configuration outperforms nearly all competing methods in all metrics, including those using their T and T+E configurations. This result underscores the advantage provided by the developed multi-fidelity architecture. Averaged across datasets, MFTabPFN in D, T, and T+E configurations attains NNRMSE values of 0.9328, 0.9364, and 0.9369, respectively. These values exceed those of TabPFN (0.9188, 0.9235, 0.9243), AutoGluon (0.9227), TabM (0.9163, 0.9209, 0.9210), and RealMLP (0.9158, 0.9193, 0.9195). For R2, MFTabPFN yields 0.7594 (D), 0.7674 (T), and 0.7680 (T+E), which are substantially higher than the corresponding scores of TabPFN (0.6641, 0.6851, 0.6880), AutoGluon (0.6795), TabM (0.6290, 0.6670, 0.6678), and RealMLP (0.6332, 0.6417, 0.6430). On raw MAE, MFTabPFN records 121.4487 (D), 117.3244 (T), and 115.3845 (T+E), compared with TabPFN (152.2556, 141.0006, 138.1768), AutoGluon (135.8771), TabM (135.1523, 131.2269, 131.1545), and RealMLP (163.0618, 132.8182, 132.6569). These findings establish MFTabPFN as a leading method for single-fidelity regression tasks, offering superior accuracy with considerably lower tuning and ensembling requirements than existing baselines. As introduced earlier in this subsection, the inputs/features to the high-dimensional PFs are random variables, reflecting aleatoric uncertainty, resulting in output labels that also exhibit aleatoric uncertainty, characterized by PDFs. Estimating these PDFs is a critical task in the UQ field. Here, we evaluate MFTabPFN’s capability in UQ tasks by comparing its predicted PDFs against those from state-of-the-art methods, TabPFN and AutoGluon. Using models trained on datasets with a training data size of 5n, we predicted labels for testing sets comprising 5000 samples per PF. The predicted PDFs were derived from 5000 predicted labels using kernel density estimation63, with reference PDFs computed from the actual labels. The kernel bandwidth was estimated automatically using Scott’s rule, as implemented in SciPy, with no manual adjustment applied. The corresponding cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) can be obtained by integrating the PDFs.

Figure 6 compares the UQ performance of MFTabPFN, TabPFN, and AutoGluon across the five high-dimensional PFs. Across all five cases, MFTabPFN produced the most accurate PDFs and CDFs. To quantify UQ performance, we computed the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence64 between predicted and reference PDFs, and the Wasserstein-1 (Earth Mover’s) distance65 between the corresponding CDFs. Both metrics measure distributional dissimilarity, with lower values indicating superior alignment between predicted and observed distributions. As shown in Fig. 6a, b, MFTabPFN outperforms both baselines in all five high-dimensional UQ tasks. Even in the least favourable case (feature dimension of 300), MFTabPFN achieved substantially lower JS divergence (0.0158) and Wasserstein-1 distance (338.3385) than TabPFN (0.1725, 961.3654) and AutoGluon (0.1288, 808.2313). Across the five cases, MFTabPFN attained a mean JS divergence of 0.0052 and a mean Wasserstein-1 distance of 68.1720, corresponding to more than 20-fold and more than twofold improvements over TabPFN (0.1421, 195.2607) and AutoGluon (0.1101, 164.0557), respectively. The point-prediction accuracy shown in Fig. 6c is consistent with these UQ results. MFTabPFN yields the most accurate predictions, outperforming both baselines. In particular, for the 100-dimensional case, MFTabPFN produced accurate predictions, whereas both TabPFN and AutoGluon exhibit large systematic deviations, highlighting the multi-fidelity architecture’s advantage in capturing complex high-dimensional input-output relationships under limited data regimes. These findings establish MFTabPFN as superior for high-dimensional UQ tasks. Results of these metrics and extended UQ metrics including negative log-likelihood (NLL) and continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), together with performance comparisons for inputs drawn from normal, lognormal, and hybrid distributions, are provided in Supplementary Section S1.5.

Active learning framework for model enhancement

The applications discussed above involve pre-existing datasets used to train ML models for predicting labels of unseen features, following a single-step sampling approach. However, in many real-world scenarios, datasets must be designed under stringent constraints, such as limited resources for costly experiments/simulations. The selection of experimental/simulation conditions to generate these datasets is critical, as it directly impacts the prediction accuracy of ML models and, consequently, mission outcomes. To address this, active learning70, which iteratively prioritizes the most informative data for model enhancement, offers a robust solution. By integrating prediction uncertainty to select high-value data in each iteration, active learning enhances prediction accuracy and has been successfully applied to fields such as protein design71.

To enhance scalability for resource-intensive experiments/simulations, we developed an active learning framework for MFTabPFN to improve model performance with multi-fidelity data. Active learning relies on an acquisition function to identify high-value data for model refinement. As described in the MFTabPFN model construction subsection, both low- and high-fidelity models provide predictive distributions per instance, where the prediction mean serves as the predicted label and the prediction variance quantifies epistemic uncertainty, with lower variance indicating higher prediction accuracy. To improve MFTabPFN’s accuracy, a direct way is to use prediction variance as the acquisition function, prioritizing data with high variance for inclusion in the training dataset through iterative retraining. For multi-fidelity applications, the acquisition function should distinguish between low- and high-fidelity data.

We implemented a two-stage active learning procedure, each with specific acquisition functions for low- and high-fidelity data. In the first stage, targeting initial iterations with limited training data and sparse input feature space coverage, we prioritize data from unexplored regions, at the same time considering the prediction uncertainty of obtaining labels. We benchmarked AMFTabPFN against established multi-fidelity models, including MFGPR, NMFGPR, MAHNN, TLFNO, and the baseline MFTabPFN without active learning. The same counts of low- and high-fidelity samples was then used to train all competing methods. Moreover, since MFGPR and NMFGPR naturally provide prediction variance suitable for measuring prediction uncertainty, we further implemented and compared their active-learning variants (AMFGPR and ANMFGPR). In each iteration, the fidelity level selected for updating in AMFGPR and ANMFGPR was matched to the fidelity level chosen by AMFTabPFN in the corresponding iteration, and the new training point was acquired by selecting the candidate that maximized prediction variance under the currently updated model at that fidelity. This protocol guarantees that, at every iteration, AMFTabPFN, AMFGPR, and ANMFGPR utilize identical numbers of low- and high-fidelity data, enabling rigorous head-to-head comparison of active-learning accuracy. All models were assessed over three independent repetitions, with each repetition using a distinct random seed for initial training-set selection and subsequent model training and prediction.

In the first stage, 25 iterations were performed to establish an initial multi-fidelity surrogate. The second stage then commenced, continuing until one of the predefined convergence criteria was satisfied.

Discussion

High-dimensional representations and UQ are critical in fields such as biology, chemistry, and engineering, yet remain challenging with limited single- or multi-fidelity data. We developed MFTabPFN, a general-purpose multi-fidelity model designed for multi-fidelity data, which can be seamlessly adapted to single-fidelity scenarios. MFTabPFN integrates the pre-trained TabPFN model25 as the low-fidelity component and appends a lightweight encoder module with another TabPFN model, trained as the high-fidelity model, to transform raw input features into a latent space aligned with downstream data distributions, enhancing predictive accuracy. A fine-tuning strategy56 adjusts the encoder’s parameters while preserving TabPFN’s fixed parameters, balancing task-specific adaptability with robust generalization. Leveraging TabPFN’s uncertainty estimation, MFTabPFN generates a predictive distribution per instance, quantifying epistemic uncertainty to support decision-making and enabling an active learning framework with two-stage acquisition functions to select high-value data for model enhancement, thus improving scalability for resource-intensive experiments/simulations.

We evaluated MFTabPFN on 23 single-fidelity datasets involving 18 real-world applications (e.g., forest fire burned area prediction, wine quality assessment, music geographical origin analysis), and 5 synthetic cases (engineering systems) with feature dimensions ranging from 100 to 500. Across all three evaluation regimes (D, T, and T+E configurations), MFTabPFN consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, including FastaiMLP11, TorchMLP11, RealMLP12, TabM13, ModernNCA14, TabDPT27, XGBoost15, CatBoost16, LightGBM17, ExtraTrees18, EBM19,20, AutoGluon11, and TabPFN25. Notably, MFTabPFN in its D configuration outperforms nearly all competing methods across all performance metrics, including those using their T and T+E configurations. One-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed that MFTabPFN achieved the highest number of datasets on which it significantly outperformed competitors (p < 0.05) within the same hyperparameter regime, while simultaneously recording the lowest number of datasets on which it was significantly outperformed. These findings establish MFTabPFN as a consistently superior performer on single-fidelity regression tasks relative to compared state-of-the-art methods, under both default and optimized hyperparameter settings. Although MFTabPFN was not the most computationally efficient overall, its median total runtime remained faster than approximately half of the baselines in the T+E regime.

Given that MFTabPFN in its default configuration outperformed nearly all baselines across all three configurations, we adopted it for investigating performance across varying dataset sizes, high-dimensional UQ tasks on single-fidelity data, and multi-fidelity applications. The results highlighted MFTabPFN’s robustness and superiority over leading baselines (AutoGluon and TabPFN) at diverse data scales. For inputs with aleatoric uncertainty modelled by uniform random variables, MFTabPFN outperformed both baselines across all five high-dimensional UQ tasks. Averaged over these cases, MFTabPFN achieved more than 20-fold improvement in mean JS divergence (for PDFs) and more than twofold improvement in mean Wasserstein-1 distance (for CDFs) relative to TabPFN and AutoGluon, together with varying degrees of improvement in mean NLL and mean CRPS (Supplementary Table S10). We additionally assessed the UQ performance of MFTabPFN when input features were drawn from three distinct distributional families, including normal, lognormal, and hybrid mixtures (Supplementary Section S1.5.2). Across all four metrics, MFTabPFN exhibited consistent improvements over TabPFN and AutoGluon in each of the three distributional settings. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MFTabPFN delivers robust and exceptional performance in high-dimensional UQ tasks with single-fidelity data, even when the input features follow markedly different distributional families. In addition, calibration analysis (Supplementary Section S1.6) shows that MFTabPFN yields well-calibrated predictive uncertainty across all confidence levels by combining with a simple yet effective post-hoc scaling strategy, demonstrating its applicability in settings where precise calibration is required.

MFTabPFN was evaluated for predicting pressure coefficient distributions of the DLR-F4 wing-body configuration66 using multi-fidelity data, comprising low-fidelity simulations and high-fidelity experimental measurements. MFTabPFN produced accurate pressure coefficient predictions across all testing experimental data and achieved the best overall performance among established multi-fidelity surrogate modelling approaches, including MFGPR34, NMFGPR39, MAHNN41, and TLFNO. It also outperformed single-fidelity baselines trained solely on high-fidelity data (TabPFN-H and AutoGluon-H) as well as models trained on concatenated low- and high-fidelity data (TabPFN-M and AutoGluon-M). Notably, TabPFN-H and AutoGluon-H exhibited the poorest performance across nearly all metrics, confirming that high-fidelity data alone are insufficient for this task. Although AutoGluon-M showed strong predictive results, i.e., surpassing the representative multi-fidelity model NMFGPR in R2, MFTabPFN remained superior in all metrics. Correlation analysis further revealed that MFTabPFN and NMFGPR more effectively captured cross-fidelity corrections than the other multi-fidelity methods (MFGPR, MAHNN and TLFNO). In terms of computational efficiency, MFTabPFN demonstrated intermediate performance among the evaluated approaches, with a median total runtime faster than half the baselines and approximately 1/3 that of MFGPR, 1/60 that of TLFNO, 1/94 that of AutoGluon-H, and 1/137 that of AutoGluon-M. Additional experiments with varying ratios of high-fidelity training data (Supplementary Section S2.2) confirmed MFTabPFN’s robust superiority, underscoring its effectiveness in multi-fidelity regimes where high-fidelity data are scarce.

A developed active learning framework with two-stage acquisition functions enhances MFTabPFN’s scalability for resource-intensive experiments/simulations. The first stage prioritizes data from unexplored feature spaces, while the second minimizes prediction uncertainty. Applied to high-dimensional UQ for the ONERA M6 wing72, active learning-enhanced MFTabPFN (AMFTabPFN) iteratively selects high-value data, progressively improving prediction accuracy and yielding a precise PDF for drag coefficient, outperforming most evaluated baselines, including standard MFTabPFN (without active learning), except for TLFNO. TLFNO produces the most accurate UQ results according to standard metrics in this case. However, it suffers from the key limitation of prediction overflow that violates the fundamental physical requirement that drag coefficients remain strictly positive. Furthermore, TLFNO exhibits markedly lower predictive performance on the DLR-F4 wing-body configuration, whereas MFTabPFN delivers the highest accuracy for that application. Both MFTabPFN and AMFTabPFN achieve strong performance on the ONERA M6 wing, highlighting the effectiveness of the multi-fidelity architecture and the added benefit of the proposed active learning strategy. In terms of computational cost, AutoGluon-M records the longest median total runtime, followed by AMFTabPFN. Nevertheless, AMFTabPFN provides substantially more accurate UQ than AutoGluon-M for the ONERA M6 wing. Compared with full high-fidelity simulation, AMFTabPFN reduces the median runtime to approximately 1/7, corresponding to a major decrease in the computational expense of UQ. While designed for accurate prediction and UQ, the active learning framework based on predictive distributions could be extended for tasks like design optimization, to be explored in future work.

Experiments under shifts on benchmark functions and DLR-F4 wing-body configuration (Supplementary Section S4) indicate that no single method achieves uniformly superior generalization across all scenarios. For the benchmark functions, MFTabPFN, TLFNO, and NMFGPR rank among the strongest performers overall, and MFTabPFN consistently outperforms all single-fidelity baselines, including TabPFN-H, AutoGluon-H, TabPFN-M, and AutoGluon-M, under both distribution shift and correlation shift conditions. For the DLR-F4 wing-body configuration, the single-fidelity AutoGluon-H achieves the best performance. In addition, MFTabPFN consistently outperforms the other multi-fidelity baselines and its single-fidelity counterpart TabPFN, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed multi-fidelity architecture in addressing distribution shift in this setting. Systematic analysis of shifts and the enhancement of model performance under shifts represent important research directions. We leave a detailed investigation of these aspects to future work aimed at further improving the performance of MFTabPFN under shift conditions.

Each dataset in this study contains up to 10,000 samples, consistent with TabPFN’s original settings25, as large-scale datasets are beyond the scope of this work. We focused on accurate prediction and UQ with limited single- or multi-fidelity data. TabPFN achieves regression predictions in 4.8 s on average, outperforming baselines25. MFTabPFN, with a lightweight encoder and fine-tuning strategy, increases runtime due to task-specific training but maintains a balance between accuracy and efficiency. For resource-intensive applications, such as UQ for turbulent transport in magnetic confinement tokamak devices requiring each high-fidelity simulation averaging over 8000 core-hours6, MFTabPFN’s training time is negligible compared to simulation costs. Notably, the present work has focused on two-fidelity scenarios, which remain the most common setting across a wide range of multi-fidelity applications31. Nevertheless, MFTabPFN is conceptually extensible to problems involving more than two fidelity levels. Predictions obtained with the extended MFTabPFN on three-fidelity benchmark functions (Supplementary Section S5) show that it achieves superior performance compared with three-level variants of MFGPR and NMFGPR, as well as single-fidelity baselines, thereby demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed extension for handling three-fidelity problems. Future work will investigate more efficient architectures and training strategies to enhance scalability and performance in settings with three or more fidelity levels, thereby broadening the applicability of MFTabPFN to increasingly complex multi-fidelity problems in science and engineering. In conclusion, MFTabPFN offers a versatile framework with significant potential for data-driven discovery across biology, chemistry, physics, engineering, and beyond.

Methods

Data preparation and preprocessing

For prediction tasks on single-fidelity data, we used 23 regression datasets: 18 real-world datasets spanning applications such as forest fire burned area prediction, wine quality assessment, and music geographical origin analysis, and 5 synthetic datasets representing high-dimensional engineering systems. The real-world datasets were sourced directly from the well-established OpenML-CTR23 benchmarking suite58. We selected those that satisfy the strict input constraints of the original TabPFN model (i.e., up to 10,000 samples and 500 features) and are inherently formulated as regression tasks (i.e., datasets better suited to multi-class classification were excluded). The synthetic datasets were generated using the well-established high-dimensional engineering PFs59,60 (or their direct extensions), with the feature dimensions ranging from 100 to 500 in increments of 100. These functions, which characterize feature-label relationships in engineering systems, enable evaluation of MFTabPFN’s ability to capture high-dimensional representations. Input features follow uniform distributions in [0, 1], reflecting aleatoric uncertainty. To generate training and testing data, we sampled feature data from this distribution via random simulation and computed corresponding labels using these PFs, which allow rapid label generation due to their explicit expressions. For assessing MFTabPFN’s performance across varying training data sizes and its high-dimensional UQ capabilities, the same process was applied, adjusting only the data sizes. Dataset identifiers (IDs) and the mathematical definitions of the performance functions are provided in Supplementary Section S6.

For pressure coefficient distribution prediction of the DLR-F4 wing-body configuration66 on multi-fidelity data, we used 141 high-fidelity experimental data points from the ONERA wind tunnel68 and 660 low-fidelity simulation data points generated using the RANS solver in SU2 software67 at a Mach number of 0.75, angle of attack of 0.18°, and Reynolds number of 21.127 million, with standard air properties (specific heat ratio = 1.4, specific gas constant = 287.058 J/kg·K). The low-fidelity simulation employed the Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model, a direct solver, JST convective scheme, implicit Euler time discretization, and monitored drag convergence (Cauchy criterion, ε = 10−6). Boundary conditions included an adiabatic wing surface, far-field, and symmetry plane, with 10,000 iterations, Green-Gauss gradient computation, and FGMRES linear solver with ILU preconditioning. A hybrid unstructured mesh with 2,561,128 cells was generated for the DLR-F4 wing-body geometry. The surface mesh was primarily structured, with unstructured elements restricted to complex regions (nose, tail, wingtips, and wing-body junctions). Grid refinement was applied along the wing leading and trailing edges and at wing-body junctions. The first off-wall grid spacing was chosen to ensure y+ <1, with a normal growth rate of 1.2. Further details on the configuration, including the SU2 input file (turb_DLR_F4.cfg) and mesh file (DLR_F4_mesh.su2), are provided in the Supplementary Data.

For high-dimensional UQ analysis of the ONERA M6 wing72 on multi-fidelity data, low- and high-fidelity data were generated using Euler solver in SU267, at a Mach number of 0.8395, angle of attack of 3.06°, and Reynolds number of 11.72 million, with standard air properties (freestream pressure = 101,325.0 N/m², temperature = 288.15 K). Input features comprised displacements of 198 control points in the perpendicular-to-mid-axis direction, following uniform distributions in [−0.02, 0.02] m. Feature data were sampled via random simulation, and corresponding lift and drag coefficients were computed using SU2 solver for specific wing shapes. A steady inviscid flow simulation (no turbulence model employed) was performed using the JST convective scheme, implicit Euler time discretization, and monitored residuals for convergence (low-fidelity: Cauchy criterion, ε = 10−5, residual threshold = 10−6; high-fidelity: ε = 10−10, threshold = 10−12). Boundary conditions included Euler walls for wing surfaces, far-field, and a symmetry plane, with 2000 iterations, Green-Gauss gradient computation, and FGMRES linear solver with ILU preconditioning. A hybrid unstructured mesh with 582,752 tetrahedral cells was generated for the ONERA M6 wing geometry. The volume mesh consists entirely of tetrahedra, with the surface triangulation primarily unstructured and featuring reasonable refinement near the leading and trailing edges, wing tip, and root section to capture transonic flow features such as shocks and pressure gradients. The wing surface is treated as an Euler wall with approximately 36,454 triangular faces (combining UPPER_SIDE, LOWER_SIDE, and TIP markers), complemented by a symmetry plane at the root and far-field boundaries. More details, including the SU2 input files (inv_ONERAM6_adv_high.cfg for high-fidelity simulation and inv_ONERAM6_adv_low.cfg for low-fidelity simulation) and mesh file (mesh_ONERAM6_inv_FFD.su2), are provided in the Supplementary Data.

All datasets underwent a standardized preprocessing pipeline prior to model training. Feature preprocessing was performed using a custom data preprocessing pipeline implemented via ColumnTransformer in scikit-learn, which robustly handles mixed-type tabular data in a single, reusable transformer. Numeric columns were automatically identified and processed as follows: missing values were imputed using column-wise medians (robust to outliers), followed by application of RobustScaler, which centers the data at the median and scales it by the interquartile range, thereby minimizing the influence of extreme values. Categorical columns were imputed with the most frequent category and subsequently one-hot encoded. This procedure yields a clean, scaled, and fully numeric feature matrix optimized for downstream ML algorithms. Target (label) values were separately standardized to zero mean and unit variance (standard normal distribution) prior to training. Full implementation details are provided in the Supplementary Code and Data.

Notably, the choice of the convolutional architecture for the high-fidelity encoder is deliberate but indeed introduces a locality bias, namely, that features (columns) appearing close to each other in the input matrix may exhibit local correlations that are worth capturing. This bias is reasonable and empirically justified in many datasets arising from scientific and engineering domains. For example, physical simulation outputs (CFD fields, material properties, sensor arrays) frequently exhibit spatial or parametric locality: adjacent columns often correspond to nearby spatial locations, time steps, or correlated physical quantities (e.g., pressure at neighboring grid points, or features derived from the same simulation parameter sweep). Nevertheless, any advanced feature extractor capable of capturing input feature information can serve as the encoder module in the developed multi-fidelity architecture. In this study, MFTabPFN employs an adaptive hybrid strategy: an MLP encoder for datasets less than 100 features (to improve training efficiency) and an RCNN encoder otherwise.

Model training strategy

In MFTabPFN, the low-fidelity model is trained with low-fidelity data to predict high-fidelity labels. The high-fidelity model, comprising an RCNN encoder (or a MLP for low-dimensional applications) appended to a pre-trained TabPFN, approximates the discrepancy between high-fidelity labels and scaled low-fidelity predictions. The scaling factor acts as a fidelity trade-off parameter: it allows the model to dynamically balance computational cost, data availability, and predictive accuracy according to the specific characteristics of the problem and dataset. This design is particularly valuable in real-world multi-fidelity scenarios where high-fidelity data is scarce or costly, while low-fidelity data is abundant.

During training, the parameters of the pre-trained TabPFN models in both the low- and high-fidelity modules are fully frozen. The encoder parameters (RCNN or MLP) and the scaling factor α are jointly optimized in an end-to-end back-propagation process using the loss function defined in Eq. (9). No staged or sequential training is performed, and the encoder weights and scaling factor α are updated simultaneously. When the scaling factor α is fixed a priori (e.g., α=1), only the encoder parameters are optimized.

We employ the AdamW optimizer73 to minimize the MSE loss, enhancing generalization through decoupled weight decay. It uses a learning rate of 10−3, a weight decay coefficient of 10−3. Training runs for a maximum of 100 epochs, with early stopping triggered when the loss falls below 10−4 to prevent overfitting. These strategies ensure stable and efficient fine-tuning of MFTabPFN.

A series of ablation studies of varying architectural and training conditions, including the scaling factor, removal of the encoder from the high-fidelity module, encoder architecture, encoder network depth, activation function type, training epoch number, and training learning rate, are provided in Supplementary Section S1.3. The ablation studies reveal four principal findings. First, the scaling factor α exerts a substantial effect on predictive performance, with the optimal value varying across datasets and proving difficult to determine a priori. Accordingly, we fix α=1 during training for single-fidelity applications to enhance efficiency, while jointly optimizing α together with the encoder parameters for multi-fidelity applications to enhance adaptability to heterogeneous multi-fidelity regimes. Second, ablating the encoder from the high-fidelity module markedly impairs performance, underscoring its essential role in effective feature extraction within the high-fidelity module. Third, the MFTabPFN model and the fixed-RCNN variant (MFTabPFN-RCNN) yield comparable accuracy, both outperforming the fixed-MLP variant (MFTabPFN-MLP), confirming the effectiveness of the RCNN encoder for accuracy improvement of MFTabPFN. Finally, variations in encoder depth, activation function, number of training epochs, and learning rate produce only modest changes in performance, indicating that MFTabPFN is generally robust to these hyperparameters.

Notably, the high-fidelity module in MFTabPFN is designed to model the discrepancy between the high-fidelity ground-truth labels and the predictions generated by the low-fidelity TabPFN model. When this discrepancy is sufficiently small, fine-tuning the MFTabPFN architecture provides negligible benefit. In such cases, the original TabPFN model is used directly for inference, yielding substantial computational savings. The magnitude of this discrepancy can be quantified using the MSE between the true high-fidelity labels and the low-fidelity TabPFN predictions. For single-fidelity tasks, the discrepancy is measured as the MSE between the true low-fidelity labels and the predictions produced by the low-fidelity TabPFN itself. Whenever this MSE falls below a predefined threshold, e.g., 0.01, MFTabPFN is replaced by the original TabPFN model re-trained on concatenated low- and high-fidelity data. This adaptive model-selection mechanism can be found in Fig. 3a, where the pure white squares in the three columns corresponding to TabPFN indicate those datasets in which the discrepancy was sufficiently small that MFTabPFN was replaced by the standard TabPFN model during inference.
