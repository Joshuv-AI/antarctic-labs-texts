Advancing trauma scoring through large language models: automated estimation of injury severity
Authors: Chun-Hsiung Ouyang, Chih-Chi Chen, Pang-Chun Liao, Kuan-Yueh Fang, Albert Jow, Ying-Jia Lin, Chi-Tung Cheng, Jen-Fu Huang, Chien-Hung Liao, Fausto Catena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAccurate Injury Severity Score (ISS) estimation is crucial for trauma care quality improvement but relies on labor-intensive manual review by trauma team and registrars. Large Language Models (LLMs) offer automation potential, yet their validation in trauma scoring is limited.MethodsThis retrospective study from a Level I trauma center evaluated GPT-4, Grok-3, and Perplexity for automated ISS estimation. We used iterative prompt engineering to refine LLM and input the discharge diagnosis of trauma patients. Then we compared the LLMs-estimated ISS against human-assigned scores. A separate validation cohort was used to assess agreement via weighted Kappa statistics, Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman plots (LoA).ResultsThe validation cohort had an ISS range of 1–41 (median: 9.0, IQR:12.0; mean ± SD: 11.71 ± 8.64). Categorical agreement was high in Grok-3 (weighted Kappa = 0.91), and GPT-4 (weighted Kappa = 0.88), but adequate in Perplexity (weighted Keppa = 0.54). All LLMs showed strong concordance with human-assigned ISS. ICCs were 0.97 (GPT-4, CI 95% = 0.94–0.98), 0.92 (Grok-3, CI 95% = 0.86–0.95), and 0.84 (Perplexity, CI 95% = 0.73–0.91), demonstrating near-expert-level consistency. Mean biases were small positive, with a LoA of ± 5.2 (GPT-4), ± 7.0 (Grok-3), and ± 9.7 (Perplexity). Performance decreased for severe injuries (ISS ≥ 16), with wider LoA and lower ICCs, likely due to less granular narrative documentation.ConclusionLLMs demonstrate strong agreement with human-assigned ISS for automated estimation from clinical diagnoses. While complex severe injuries pose challenges, LLMs can significantly enhance efficiency and consistency in trauma registry workflows. Further validation and integration with richer data sources are recommended for clinical deployment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01512-z.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly transforming practices across many fields, including optimizing traffic [1] autonomous manufacturing [2] personalized education platforms and automated grading systems [3]. These advancements are largely due to significant improvements in computational hardware, which have made complex AI architectures more accessible. The emergence of large language models (LLMs), in particular, is now poised to transform administrative systems, especially within healthcare [4, 5].

Trauma remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide. In recent decades, however, advancements in our understanding of injury mechanisms, post-injury physiology, and therapeutic procedures have dramatically improved patient outcomes. A key component of this progress has been the development and widespread use of standardized severity scales, such as the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) [6] and Injury Severity Score (ISS) [7]. These scores enable trauma programs to assess patient outcomes, identify preventable deaths, and benchmark hospital performance [8–11]. As core components of trauma registries, they support review conferences, audit filters, and quality improvement initiatives, collectively enhancing the overall quality of trauma care.

An effective trauma registry relies on the precise data extraction from health records, precise diagnosis coding, and reliable scoring. Traditionally, these tasks require highly trained specialists or registrars [12–14], as inaccurate scoring can lead to flawed outcome triage and suboptimal clinical decisions [15, 16]. Recently, automated grading and scoring systems leveraging LLMs have been developed and successfully applied in this specialized domain, improving efficiency and accuracy [3]. However, the application and validation in trauma scoring remain limited. This study investigates the clinical utility and accuracy of three different LLMs in automatically estimating trauma severity scores. By extracting diagnoses from a trauma registry, we compare the performance of these models to traditional methods, aiming to determine their potential for improving the efficiency and reliability of trauma care.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This retrospective analysis utilized data from a Level I trauma center’s registry in Taiwan. Comprehensive patient information—including demographics, injury mechanisms, prehospital interventions, hospital management, surgical procedures, hospitalization course, complications, and follow-up data—was systematically and prospectively collected and documented within an electronic trauma database. Each entry underwent thorough review by trained trauma registrars who cross-referenced prehospital documentation, clinical records, electronic health records, and final diagnoses. Injury severity was classified using the AIS (Version 2015) and ISS, following standardized coding protocols. A monthly review process ensured data accuracy, with any discrepancies resolved through registrar consensus. The de-identification process was performed by removing all personal identifiers, including names, identification numbers, and other identifiable labels, prior to data retrieval. In this study, only the final diagnoses, patient age, and human-assigned ISS were extracted for analysis. This study was approved by the institutional research ethics board (approval no. 202500438B0) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Training dataset and model training process

The training dataset was randomly derived from databank with computer-generated randomization procedure of trauma patients admitted to our hospital in 2023. After de-identification, only the ISS, patient age, gender and discharge diagnoses were retrieved for this study. For this study, we evaluated three pre-trained LLMs—GPT-4, Grok-3, and Perplexity—for their ability to perform automated ISS scoring through prompt engineering with iterative feedback.

We designed structured prompts to guide the LLMs in understanding trauma classification principles, AIS coding logic, and ISS computation rules. These prompts essentially encoded the detailed instructions and guidelines that a human registrar would follow.

By the sample size calculation, we assume the minimum acceptable reliability (ICCρ0) = 0.6, the expected reliability (ICC ρ1) = 0.8, α = 0.05 with two tail and power = 0.8, number of rater is 2 then the sample size is 49. We randomly selected subset of 52 trauma cases from the 2023 dataset to perform iterative feedback. For each of these cases, the LLM-estimated ISS scores were directly compared against the human-assigned ISS scores from the trauma registry. In instances of discrepancy, corrective prompts were provided to the models. This feedback included detailed explanations of the misclassification or miscalculation, effectively “teaching” the LLM how to refine its output based on the specific errors. For example, if an LLM assigned an incorrect AIS code for a particular injury, the corrective prompt would clarify the correct code and the reasoning behind it, thereby refining the model’s internal “understanding” of that injury’s severity. All the prompts we used in the training phase were listed in the Appendix I. This iterative refinement continued until the models consistently demonstrated internal logic and consistency in their ISS estimations, after which they proceeded to the validation phase.

Validation dataset formation and process

A separate validation cohort of 52 trauma patients, excluded from the training dataset, was randomly selected from the 2023 trauma registry. The discharge diagnoses were input into the LLMs as prompt manually by independent researchers who are blind to the human-assigned ISS, to prevent detection bias. All the analytic period was between January to March 2025 and the generated ISS scores were recorded. These scores were compared against human-assigned ISS values from the trauma registry to assess the models’ accuracy and reliability in automated injury severity scoring.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python (version 3.12.3) with the SciPy and Statsmodels libraries. Agreement and reliability between LLM-estimated ISS and human-assigned ISS were evaluated using the Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with a significance level of p < 0.05. Additionally, a Bland-Altman plot (B-A) plot was used to compare point-to-point differences between the ISS calculated by LLM and that determined by human registrars and evaluate the limitation of agreement (LOA).

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of three LLMs for automated ISS scoring in trauma patients. All models demonstrated moderate to high accuracy in interpreting injury descriptions and estimating severity scores. The ICCs ranged from 0.84 to 0.97, indicating strong agreement with human-assigned ISS scores. GPT-4 exhibited the highest concordance, followed by Grok 3 and Perplexity. Although all models showed slight overestimation of ISS, the mean bias was less than one point, suggesting near-expert-level consistency.

A notable finding was the reduced performance in severe injury cases (ISS ≥ 16). For this subgroup, the ICC values decreased and LoA widened, indicating lower accuracy and wider variability. This is likely due to the inherent complexity of high-severity cases, which often involve multiple injuries across different body regions. The misclassification of a single AIS can significantly impact the final ISS calculation, particularly in complex trauma presentations. In contrast, mild injuries, typically involving a single, well-defined trauma, were scored with higher accuracy with less risk of compound errors.

The accuracy of LLMs depends highly on the quality and specificity of input text [17, 18]. If the clinical diagnoses are documented with limited detail, the LLMs are prone to underestimate the true severity of injury [19, 20]. For instance, a subdural hematoma may be assigned an AIS of 3, 4, or 5 depending on its thickness and the presence of midline shift—critical parameters not always included in the final diagnostic statement.In such cases, the model’s reliance on incomplete or ambiguous language can result in the failure to escalate AIS appropriately, leading to systematic underestimation of ISS. This phenomenon was evident in our Bland-Altman analysis (Figs. 1 and 3), which revealed increasing negative bias as the reference ISS value rose, suggesting that the LLM mispredicted ISS in high-severity cases.

These findings reveal a critical limitation of relying on text-based LLMs: their dependence on narrative documentation, which often lacks the imaging, physiological, or operative data essential for accurate injury scoring [21, 22]. To enhance their predictive reliability, these models must be augmented with multimodal data inputs, such as structured clinical information or medical imaging. This approach is similar to prior work with PelviXNet—an image-based AI model that uses human annotations to improve fracture detection [23]. By similarly incorporating structured data or imaging inputs alongside textual records, and then fine-tuning the models, we can significantly refine the accuracy of LLM-based scoring systems and enable them to transcend purely narrative analysis by integrating a broader spectrum of objective, clinically relevant information.

AI and machine learning are increasingly transforming trauma care by enhancing triage efficiency [24, 25], improving imaging interpretation [26, 27], advancing predictive analytics [28, 29], and supporting real-time clinical decision-making [30]. This study demonstrates the feasibility of deploying LLM-based tools for automated injury scoring with satisfactory performance across multiple severity levels. With the support of LLMs, trauma registrars and review committees may accelerate coding and streamline registry workflows [31]. Incorporating these models could substantially reduce human workload and enhance consistency across trauma data systems. Furthermore, integration with other AI applications—such as diagnostic assistance tools [32] has the potential to fully automate both injury identification and severity scoring.

Beyond ISS, trauma care involves the use of several other scoring systems that also demand timely documentation and interpretation [33, 34]. These may similarly benefit from LLM assistance in the near future. However, the effective deployment of such tools depends on comprehensive training for trauma teams and registrars. Users must understand the limitations of the system, accurately interpret model outputs, and remain vigilant for possible errors or discrepancies.

To maximize the practical utility of this study for trauma centers with varying technical resources, we evaluated these LLMs using their standard interfaces rather than specialized API-driven pipelines. Furthermore, the models were not provided with a proprietary AIS dictionary; instead, they relied on their native ability to retrieve and apply scoring logic from their pre-trained parameters. Our findings suggest that while these models possess high baseline capabilities for data reconstruction, an iterative ‘tuning’ phase through prompt engineering is essential to achieve the level of precision required for trauma registry documentation. Close collaboration between clinicians and AI developers will be critical in refining prompt designs, optimizing user interfaces, and defining integration pathways to ensure the LLM complements rather than disrupts existing workflows.

Limitation

Although our study showed promising performance of LLMs for ISS estimation. There were still some limitations. First, a reliable and structured data pipeline is essential for LLM application. Inputting the diagnosis into LLM tools is also time-consuming, therefore, future development of secure application programming interface portals and tighter EHR integration may further automate the process and reduce the reliance on manual entry. Second, due to token length limitations, the amount of diagnostic information that can be processed by the large language model at one time is constrained. As the number of cases increases, the model may gradually lose contextual recall of the initial system prompt, necessitating re-input of the training instructions. Based on this limitation, we estimated that the practical upper limit for a single analysis would be approximately 54 cases and therefore selected 52 cases for the present study. Although the sample size is relatively limited, statistical power analysis indicated that it was sufficient to demonstrate significant agreement, supporting the validity of our findings. Third, the computational burden associated with deploying LLMs in real-time clinical settings is still high. Running a state-of-the-art model within a hospital environment may necessitate specialized infrastructure or secure cloud services, introducing challenges related to latency, scalability, and cost. Forth, ethical considerations are also paramount [31]. If cloud-based LLMs are used, transmitting patient’s data to external servers could raise significant privacy concerns and potentially violate data protection regulations. While open-source healthcare-specific LLMs might offer a privacy-conscious alternative, they currently do not match the performance of commercial models. In addition, the proportion of patients with severe injuries in our cohort was relatively high (33%), which may not fully reflect the distribution seen in routine clinical settings and could introduce selection bias. Furthermore, due to our inclusion criteria, pediatric patients were not included in this study. Future studies with broader inclusion criteria and larger, more diverse populations are therefore warranted to improve generalizability. Finally, LLM-based ISS estimation is promising but not yet ready for unsupervised clinical deployment. Further validation in real-world environments is required. A prospective study evaluating the reproducibility, and clinical impact of LLM-derived ISS calculations will be the next step in establishing their role in trauma care.

Conclusion

This study offered the feasibility of using LLMs to automate ISS estimation from clinical diagnoses. LLMs demonstrated strong agreement with human-assigned ISS, with high ICC values and good categorical classification performance. The findings support the potential of LLMs to enhance efficiency and consistency in trauma registry workflows.
