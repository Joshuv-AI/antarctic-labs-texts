Chromosome-level genome of butterflyfish unveils genomic features of unique colour patterns and morphological traits
Authors: Suyu Zhang, Yue Song, Meiru Liu, Zengbao Yuan, Mengqi Zhang, He Zhang, Inge Seim, Guangyi Fan, Shanshan Liu, Xin Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractChaetodontidae, known as butterflyfishes, are typical fish in coral ecosystems, exhibiting remarkable interspecific differences including body colour patterns and feeding ecology. In this study, we report genomes of three butterflyfish species (Chelmon rostratus, Chaetodon trifasciatus and Chaetodon auriga) and a closely related species from the Pomacanthidae family, Centropyge bicolour, with an average genome size of 65,611 Mb. Chelmon rostratus, comprising 24 chromosomes assembled to the chromosome level, could be served as a reference genome for butterflyfish. By conducting a collinearity analysis between butterflyfishes and several fishes, we elucidated the specific and conserved genomic features of butterflyfish, with particular emphasis on novel genes arising from tandem duplications and their potential functions. In addition to the two melanocyte-specific tyr genes commonly found in fish, we found the gene tyrp3, a new tyrosinase-related proteins gene in the reef fish, including butterflyfish and clownfish, implicating their involvement in the pigmentation diversity of fish. Additionally, we observed a tandem duplication expansion of three copies of nell1 gene in C. rostratus genome, which likely contribute to its unique jaw development and distinctive morphology of its sharp mouth. These results provided valuable genomic resources for further investigations into the genetic diversity and evolutionary adaptations of reef fish.

1. Background and summary

Chaetodontidae (Butterflyfish) comprised 12 genera and 131 species,1 representing typical reef fishes.2 The interspecific morphology of Chaetodontidae exhibited significant variations, characterized by vibrant colour patterns including yellow, black and white.3 As a result, they were a natural model for studying the pigmentation of fishes and were highly sought-after as ornamental fishes. The unique colour pattern was accompanied by an ongoing aggressive evolution of pigmentation genes4,5 and was an important research direction for neural spine cell migration and pigment cell growth and differentiation.6 Despite previous studies reported the extensive morphological and biogeographic of butterflyfish,7,8 genomic investigations of butterflyfish were limited, with Chaetodon austriacus, for instance, only having a draft assembly and annotation.9 Genomic studies on butterflyfish could be beneficial to further enrich the insights on fish pigmentation genes and provided useful genomic resources for ornamental fish breeding.

In addition to differences in colour patterns and morphological characteristics, the feeding specialization of butterflyfishes was another hot focus for researches.10 Recent studies have indicated that one of the key factors in reef fish diversification was trophic evolution.11 Extreme trophic conditions significantly contributed to the evolution of functional traits in reef fishes.12 Butterflyfishes exhibited notable interspecific variations in food habits and jaw shape.7,13 Most butterflyfishes, such as Chaetodon, possessed short and robust jaws adapted for feeding on corals and other attached organisms.14 Long-jawed butterflyfishes exhibited a rare forward protrusion of the lower jaw among bony fishes, enabling them to feed on invertebrates such as tubeworms and sea anemones.14 Chaetodon species typically experienced increased intramandibular flexion in the intramandibular joint, while Chelmon developed elongated jaws for a different biting strategy.7 The genomic characteristics underlying these special features with elongated jaws remained unknown. Exploring molecular evidence to understand the evolutionary differences among several clades within the Chaetodontidae family was an important research direction.

Physiological and morphological phenotypic diversity in the past was driven by genomic innovations, including gene gain and lost.15–17 Genes underwent duplication through various mechanisms, such as tandem duplication, segmental duplication and whole-genome duplication.18,19 The expansion of gene numbers likely facilitated morphological innovation.20 Specifically, pigmentation genes were inclined to be positively and specifically retained, providing a genetic basis for the evolution and occurrence of a greater chromatophore cell types in teleosts.5 Previous studies have demonstrated the duplication and retention of colour-related genes, such as tyr, pax3, tyrp1 and sox10, following fish-specific genome duplication.4 Howerer, the genomic features associated with pigmentation genes in later-evolving multi-coloured reef fish were not yet clearly understood.

To explore how genomic changes in butterflyfish contribute to phenotypic diversity, we present four newly sequenced assemblies encompassing three Chaetodontidae species (Chelmon rostratus, Chaetodon trifasciatus and Chaetodon auriga) and one Pomacanthidae species (Centropyge bicolor). Subsequently, the comparative genomics analysis was performed between four new genomes and other non-reef fish genomes. By collinearity analysis, we found genomic features that were special and conserved in butterflyfish. Then, we proposed the potential clues linked the duplicated genes and specific morphological characteristics of butterflyfish, which facilitated further elucidation of key molecules important in the formation of pigment patterns and morphological features.

2. Materials and methods

2.1 Sample collection, library construction and sequencing

Adult specimens of C. rostratus, C. trifasciatus, C. Auriga and C. bicolor were collected from the sea area of Qingdao, China, and their muscle tissues were immediately stored in liquid nitrogen. Then, high-quality DNA was extracted from the muscle tissue using a modified protocol for DNA extraction from vertebrate tissues.21 The extracted DNA was fragmented and the MGIEasy single tube Long Fragment Read (stLFR)22 Library preparation kit (PN:1000005622) was employed to construct the stLFR library, following the stLFR protocol. For Hi-C library sequencing of C. rostratus, approximately 1 g living muscle tissue was utilized for DNA extraction and library contraction, according to Wang’s method.22 Sequencing was performed on a BGISEQ-500 sequencer, generating 286.82 Gb raw data (including 134.18 Gb of raw Hi-C data) (Supplementary Table S1). Subsequently, data filtering was conducted using SOAPnuke software (version 1.5)23 with the default parameters, thus low-quality reads (more than 40% bases with quality score lower than 8), PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) duplications, adaptors and reads with high proportion (higher than 10%) of ambiguous bases (Ns) were filtered. Following data filtering, 192.71 Gb of ‘clean data’ (including 133.17 Gb of ‘clean Hi-C data’) of C. rostratus, for instance, was obtained for the further assembly (Supplementary Table S1).

2.2 Genome survey

To characterize the four newly sequenced genomes, we applied k-mer frequency distribution analysis to estimate its genome size and genome complexity (heterozygosity or/and repetition).24 For example, we randomly selected 59.54 Gb (~ 90X) clean reads of C. rostratus and carried out a 17mer analysis using KMERFREQ_AR (version 2.0.4).25 The genome size of C. rostratus, C. trifasciatus, C. auriga and C. bicolor were thus estimated to be 711.39 Mb, 698.52 Mb, 681.72 Mb and 660.85 Mb (Supplementary Table S2). Observing the distribution of 17mer frequency (Supplementary Fig. S1), we anticipated the genomes to be diploid species with slight heterozygosity. In order to get the exact content of heterozygosity, GCE software24 was used to calculation with 59.54 Gb clean reads and resulted that the content of heterozygosity was 0.72%.

2.3 Genome assembly

Four new sequenced species were assembled using Supernova (version 2.1)26 with default parameters for clean stLFR data. Before the process, stLFR reads were first preprocessed to adapt to Supernova by the stLFR2Supernova pipeline (https://github.com/BGI-Qingdao/stlfr2supernova_pipeline). After that, Gapcloser software25 was used to fill gaps within the assembly with default parameters.

To generate a chromosomal-level genome assembly of C. rostratus, high-quality Hi-C data was used to further assembly. Firstly, we used HiC-Pro software (version2.8.0_devel)27 with default parameters to get valid sequencing data. Then, Juicer (version 1.5, an open source tool for analysing Hi-C datasets),28 and the 3D de novo assembly pipeline29 were used to connect the scaffolds to chromosomes. To evaluate the genome assembly of C. rostratus, we aligned sequencing data filtered previously using SOAPaligner (version 2.2).25 We also calculated its GC depth to rule out possible biases during sequencing or possible contaminations. Then, the genome completeness assessment was estimated with Benchmarking Universal Single-copy Orthologs (BUSCO, version 3.0.1) (Supplementary Table S3).30

2.4 Gene prediction and annotation

For newly assembled genomes, genome annotation were carried out to seek protein-coding genes with two ways: (i) the ab initio gene prediction and (ii) the homology-based annotation.31 For ab initio gene prediction approaches, Augustus32 and GlimmerHMM33 were used with Danio rerio as the species of HMM model to predict gene models; For homology-based annotation, four homolog species including Pundamilia nyererei,34Maylandia zebra,34Astatotilapia calliptera35 and Perca flavescens36 were aligned against the genome assembly using BLAT software (version 0.36)37 and GeneWise software (version 2.4.1).38 Protein-coding genes were obtained by combining the different evidences using Glean software (version 1.0).39 In the final gene models, the average length was 16,183.81 bp, with an average of 10 exons. The average length of coding sequences, exons and introns were 1,789.82 bp, 179.10 bp and 1,599.48 bp, respectively, similar to that of the other released fish genomes, such as A. calliptera, M. zebra, P. flavescens and P. nyererei (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S2). Functions of the annotated protein-coding genes were inferred by searching homologues in the databases (KEGG,40 COG,41 NR,42 Swissprot43 and Interpro44) (Supplementary Table S5). To validate the quality of predicted gene sets, we also assessed the completeness using BUSCO (version 3.0.1) with the fish core gene database (actinopterygii_odb9).30 We found that about 90.20% of core gene were annotated in gene set of C. rostratus with 4,000 were single-copy gene and 132 were duplicated (Supplementary Table S6).

2.5 Repeat annotation

For the subsequent comparative genomics analysis, we selected 14 species including Lepisosteus oculatus, Danio rerio, Astyanax mexicanus, Lythrypnus dalli, Hippocampus comes, Cynoglossus semilaevis, Poecilia reticulata, Oryzias latipes, Oreochromis niloticus, Amphiprion percula, Gasterosteus aculeatus, Takifugu rubripes, Larimichthys crocea and Homo sapiens as outgroup (Supplementary Table S7). To ensure the continuity and comprehensiveness of the fish lineage, all published species selected here preformed high assembly quality and extensive phylogenetic relationships. These above genomes and the four newly assembled genomes were annotated with the same conditions to ensure comparability of subsequent analysis (Supplementary Table S8). We annotated the two major types of repetitive sequences (tandem repeats, TRFs and transposable elements, TEs) in genomes. According to the method of previously research,45–47 TRFs were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (version 4.04).48 TEs were identified by a combination of homology-based and de novo approaches. Briefly, for homology-based annotation, known repeats in the database (Repbase16.02)49 were aligned against the genome assembly using RepeatMasker (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) and RepeatProteinMask (version 3.2.9)50 at both the DNA and protein levels. For de novo annotation, RepeatModeler (version 1.1.0.4)51 was employed to build a de novo non-redundant repeat library and then the repeat library was searched against the genome using RepeatMasker.50 Besides, we collected gene annotation published and performed a round of repetitive sequences annotation for the 14 published genomes (Supplementary Table S9).

2.6 Gene family and phylogenetic analysis

The coding sequences of all 18 species undergoing phylogenetic analysis were collected and more than 150 bp sequences were retained. Promoters and terminators were checked to ensure reliable sequences were retained and translated into proteins, ensuring that protein sequences and coding sequences matched one-to-one, and the all-versus-all BLAST search was performed later. The blast matches were then de-redundantly clustered into 20,090 gene families using the hcluster_sg tool (https://sourceforge.net/p/treesoft/code/HEAD/tree/branches/lh3/). Multiple sequence alignment of each single-copy gene family was performed using MUSCLE (version 3.8.31).52 The concatenated sequence alignments of single-copy genes shared by the eighteen species were performed to reveal the phylogenetic relationships. We calculated the best-fit nucleic acid substitution model as GTR + F + R3 by ModelFinder from IQ-TREE (version 1.6.12).53 The PhyML software (version 3.0),54 based on the method of maximum likelihood, was used to construct the phylogenetic tree with nucleotide data type and GTR (General time reversible) model. The divergence time was estimated by PAML MCMCtree55 with the calibration time points based on the TimeTree database (http://www.timetree.org)56 (Supplementary Table S10).

The expansion and contraction of the gene family analysis may reveal the evolutionary dynamics of gene families thus provide the clues for understanding the diversity of different species. It was often inferred from the number of genes in the gene family and the phylogenetic tree. As a result, a total of 13,890 gene families from the most recent common ancestor have been identified (Supplementary Fig. S3). In our study, we used the CAFÉ (version 2.1)57 software to analyse the expansion and contraction of clustered gene families (Supplementary Fig. S4).

2.7 Collinearity analysis

The genome synteny blocks were identified for all-versus-C. rostratus by MCScan software implemented in Python (JCVI v1.2.7) (https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi/wiki/MCscan-(Python-version)). The synteny blocks with five or more gene pairs were retained, and a total of 17,861 genes were synteny related to at least one species, accounting for 86.86% of the total number of C. rostratus genes, indicating a strong conserved relative position among genes (Supplementary Fig. S5).

According to the statistics of synteny blocks, the number of synteny blocks showed an opposite trend to the total number of genes compared with the species (P = 0.011) and the branching species of Ovalantaria and Eupercaria showed good uniform collinearity. The average number of synteny blocks was 642.81 and the average total number of synteny genes was 14,006 (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table S11). C. trifasciatus-versus-C. rostratus synteny blocks had the lowest number of synteny blocks (291) and the highest total number of synteny genes (15,837).

Using the chromosomal position of C. rostratus as a reference, genes within synteny blocks of all species were aligned, and more genes on the same position anchored to C. rostratus indicated that the position was highly conserved in the evolution. Conversely, some genes were only existed in the synteny blocks of reef fish, which may signify unique genetic characteristics of reef fishes. Using the synteny block dataset, we could also identify genes only found in C. rostratus, including specific duplications. GO enrichment analysis was performed by the Gene Ontology (GO) tool Metascape (http://metascape.org/)58 and similarity searches were performed by Diamond (v2.1.8).59

3. Results and discussion

3.1 Genome assembly and annotation

We obtained the assemblies of C. trifasciatus (667.69 Mb), C. auriga (636.77 Mb), C. bicolor (681.28 Mb) and C. rostratus (638.70 Mb), respectively. The scaffold N50 of these four genomes range from 2.61 Mb to 9.96 Mb and the BUSCO completeness were from 95.90% to 97.3% (Table 1), suggesting the good continuity and integrity of these genome assemblies. Among of them, 133.17 Gb high-quality Hi-C data of C. rostratus was used to anchor the scaffolds into 24 chromosomes, which covered 94.40% of total assembled genome (Fig. 1A). The average GC content (or guanine-cytosine content) of 24 chromosomes was 42.58% with length from 14.13 Mb to 33.19 Mb (Table 2 and Fig. 1B).

3.2 Genome evolution analysis

To investigate the potential evolutionary progress of increased biodiversity of reef fish species, we constructed phylogenetic tree and estimated the species divergence times. The protein-coding genes of the total eighteen species were clustered into 20,091 gene families using TreeFam.61–63 Compared to the other species, it was similar to the gene family numbers of C. trifasciatus (13,465), C. Auriga (13,454), C. bicolor (13,467), A. mexicanus (13,448) and G. aculeatus (12,446), but was quite different from those of L. crocea (14,724), H. sapiens (14,578) and L. dalli (8,875) (Supplementary Table S12 and Fig. 2A). Among the butterflyfish in this study, only C. rostratus was non-corallivory.7 To study whether there are specific gene family changes in the C. rostratus, we identified 58 expanded gene families (P-value < 0.05) and 79 contracted gene families (P-value < 0.05). The enrichment results of GO showed that the expanded gene family functions as a phosphate-containing compound metabolic process (GO: 006796), which could help digestion of crustaceans (Supplementary Fig. S4A).64 The contracted gene families were enriched in olfactory receptor activity (GO: 0004984), it may be related to its floating life history in childhood (Supplementary Fig. S4B).65 Its eggs floated away with the ocean current after hatching 2 days, and may return to the birth coral reef through smell after having the ability to swim.66{Gerlach, 2007 #163;Dixson, 2011 #164}

About 1,202 single-copy gene families from 18 species were used to construct the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2A and B), which revealed that Chaetodontidae was the sister with Pomacanthidae.67 Divergence time between C. rostratus and C. bicolor was estimated to be ~65.2 million years ago (Mya) in the Paleocene (Fig. 2C). During this period and subsequent Eocene (66–34 Mya), most modern reef fishes including Chaetodontidae and Pomacanthidae were formed, but large-scale lineage diversification had not yet occurred.2C. trifasciatus and C. auriga, belonged to species-rich Chaetodon genus, separating 15.8 Mya. The Chaetodon diverged from C. rostratus of Chelmon genus at about 35.5 Mya (Fig. 2C). These results showed that in the Oligocene and Miocene (34–5.3 Mya), the biodiversity of reef fish species increased rapidly in the Tethys Sea with a warm climate and increasing reef area,68 which could be an important factor in the outbreak of butterflyfishes.

3.3 Specific and conserved genomic features in butterflyfish

To identify genomic traits of reef fishes, we excluded the synteny genes compared in non-reef fish to construct a potential reef fish-specific collection of 163 genes. A total of 10 genes were found to be shared by more than three reef fishes, including slc25a12, ugt1a6, atp1a3, rora, gpatch, b3gnt3, spast, gpr54, ccno and tyr. Notably, tyr is known to be associated with melanin development. Consequently, further investigation was conducted to explore its specific role.

Using synteny block datasets, we identified 2,711 genes only found in C. rostratus comparing to other 16 species. Of the 2,711 C. rostratus-specific genes, 1,643 genes from the ab initio gene prediction, but lack of functional annotation. Of the remaining 878 annotated genes, there were 298 C. rostratus-specific single-copy genes and 580 C. rostratus-specific duplication, containing 210 C. rostratus-specific tandem duplication (CroTD) events, 40% (210/519) of the genome-wide TD events in C. rostratus (Supplementary Table S13). The proportion of tandem duplications in C. rostratus-specific genes (7.75%) was significantly higher than the proportion of genome-wide tandem duplications (2.52%). We further examined the effects of CroTD and GO enrichment analysis were performed (Supplementary Fig. S7 and Supplementary Table S14). The significant GO terms included cellular component morphogenesis (GO:0032989) and nervous system process (GO:0050877), both were associated with the development of pigment cells, inspired by previous reports that neural crest cells were a class of transitional multipotent cells involved in the formation of the peripheral nervous system and also had a variety of cellular differentiation potential, including multiple pigment cells.69 The other enriched terms including sensory perception of sound (GO:0007605) and ossification (GO:0001503) were also pointed to the unique characters of butterflyfishes, and the related multi-copied genes such as pi4kb of two specific copies and cldn4 of four specific copies in the C. rostratus, which provided potential genetic clues for follow-up studies.

Based on the synteny block dataset, there were 1,522 genes with synteny relationships among 17 species including H. sapiens, called the extreme conserved gene sets. Terms of GO enrichment were focus on forebrain development (GO:0030900), heart valve formation (GO:0003188), kidney rudiment formation (GO:0072003), embryonic organ development (GO:0048568) and eye morphogenesis (GO:0048592), and all functions critical to vertebrate survival (Supplementary Fig. S8 and Supplementary Table S15).

3.4 A new potential colour-determining mechanism of colourful fishes

Tyrosinase-related proteins (TRP) gene family included three members (tyr, tyrp1 and dct), which were the most important enzymes in the initial stage of melanogenesis.70 Tyrosine was hydroxylated to produce l-tyrosine toward l-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (l-DOPA), the latter was further hydroxylated by tyrosinase to hydroxylation (2S)-2-amino-3-(3,4-dioxocyclohexa-1,5-dien-1-yl) propanoic acid (DOPAquinone). These were the first two rate-limiting steps of tyrosinase-catalysed melanin synthesis. Two copies of tyr genes (tyr1 and tyr2) were presented in the vast majority of the teleost genome including butterflyfishes (Supplementary Fig. S8). Collinearity analysis of butterflyfish and other fish genomes, revealed that butterflyfish gained a unique tyrp3 gene located in a block between mrpl16 and txnl4b (Fig. 3A). Amino acid sequence comparison revealed a conserved copper–binding domain Cu (A) and Cu (B) in tyrp3, which were consistent with tyrosinase typical signatures, could be classified as a new member of TRP gene families (Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, both the coral colour-rich fish C. bicolor and the clownfish A. percula also possessed this gene in the same location, which may be a genetic innovation related multi-coloured environmental adaptation. To explore the relationship between tyr1, tyr2, and tyrp3, we performed phylogeny construction of related gene sequences of all 17 species and take human as the outgroup, in which tyr1 and tyr2 were divided into two major branches, which were paralogous homologues of each other, and five tyrp3 genes were clustered into one branch, which was sister group with tyr1 of D. rerio (Supplementary Fig. S10). In order to explore the source of the tyrp3 gene, we examined the exons within gene of all five species. Each gene had four exons that showed high collinearity, possibly from the same ancestral gene and not formed by retrotransposons. The similarity between tyrp3 and other proteins of all species also showed highly identity within five reef fishes (Supplementary Table S16).

The maturation process of tyrosinase had been extensively studied and these steps include protein processing, covalent modification, chaperone binding, oligomerization and transport, disruption of any of these steps leading to extensive pigmentation disorders.71 During oligomerization, the tyrosinase must fold properly, which was stabilized with the assistance of the melanocyte-specific proteins tyrp1 and dopachrome tautomerase dct, forming a melanogenic complex, capable of increasing tyrosinase activity.72,73 However, the maturation efficiency of wild-type tyrosinase was only 50%,74 and the melanocyte-related proteins tyrp3 may facilitated the activity of higher order oligomers of tyrosinase. In conclusion, the finding of tyrp3 genes in butterflyfish and clownfish were rare, but its function and role in tyrosinase expression and pigmentation increase remained unknown.

3.5 The genes promoting osteogenic differentiation in C. rostratus

Short- and long-jawed butterflyfish were also distributed in different phylogenetic positions, and skull biomechanics was an important topic in this family, among which the evolution of long-jawed genera Forcipiger and Chelmon were particularly of interest.67 The distinctive mouth shape of C. rostratus was notably different from the other two butterflyfish species and may be responsible for the dietary differences.10,14C. rostratus relied on its pointed mouth to capture food from coral skeletons or stones. NEL-like protein 1 (nell1) gene had been reported to regulate human jaw development and induced bone regeneration and cartilage regeneration.75 We detected three tandem copies in C. rostratus genome (Fig. 4),76 which is notable compared to other two butterflyfish species with only one copy. The nell1 gene exhibited high conservation from fish to humans and located between Low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (lrp4) and Sodium- and chloride-dependent glycine transporter 2 (slc6a5). Nell1 was tightly interlocked with slc6a5, prmt3, htatip2 and dbx1a, and its position was aligned in most species of teleost, which was involved in the positive regulation of bone mineralization (GO:0030501). Nell1 gene was a growth factor that promotes osteoblast differentiation and late mineralization. Thus, integrated with multiple genes from CroTD were reported to be related to ossification, the C. rostratus-specific multi-copies may play an important function in mouth shape diversification and provide potential clues on the evolution of fish mouth shape.

4. Conclusion

In this study, we first reported assemblies of three Chaetodontidae species and a Pomacanthidae species. Notably, we generated a high-quality chromosome-level assembly of the C. rostratus genome based on Hi-C data, which can serve as a reference genome of Chaetodontidae family for further comparative genomics analysis. The adverse effects of reduced or absent tyrosinase on fish had been widely discussed previously, such as reduced melanogenesis and reduced visual function.77,78 Furthermore, we identified a reef fish species-specific gene, tyrp3, which likely plays a crucial regulatory role in melanin synthesis. However, the precise function of tyrp3 and its effect in colour pattern formation require further investigation, such as knockdown or overexpression experiments. Through collinearity analysis, we identified specific and conserved genomic features in butterflyfishes. In addition, we found that the nell1 gene, which was associated with jaw development, owned specific tandem duplication in C. rostratus differed from other short mouth butterflyfishes. Additionally, we reported several specific tandem duplications in C. rostratus associated with ossification. These findings offered a novel genomic perspective for unravelling the evolutionary history of vertebrate mandibular development.
