Population Pharmacokinetic Analysis of Vixarelimab in Healthy Volunteers and Patients With Chronic Pruritic Conditions
Authors: Fei Tang, Steve Dang, Audrey Arjomandi, Lin Pan, Joy Hsu, Smita Kshirsagar, Nastya Kassir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVixarelimab is a first‐in‐class fully human monoclonal antibody targeting oncostatin M (OSM) receptor beta (OSMRβ). It has been evaluated in Phase 1 and 2 studies in healthy volunteers and patients with chronic pruritic conditions and demonstrated anti‐pruritic efficacy in a Phase 2 study in prurigo nodularis, but detailed pharmacokinetic analysis has not been previously reported. In this study, a population pharmacokinetic analysis was performed to characterize the pharmacokinetics of vixarelimab and identify covariates affecting exposure. The analysis dataset contained 4032 measurements from 274 participants from three Phase 1 and 2 studies. A two‐compartment target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) model with a quasi‐steady‐state (QSS) approximation satisfactorily described the observed concentration‐time data across studies and various dose levels. The model estimated linear clearance (CL), central volume (V c ), and peripheral volume (V p ) at 0.00649 L/h, 3.04 L, and 1.74 L, respectively, in line with values for a typical monoclonal antibody. Subcutaneous bioavailability and the absorption rate constant were estimated to be 56.6% and 0.0126 h−1, respectively. Body weight was identified as the only statistically significant covariate, positively correlating with CL, V c , and V p . Model‐based simulations showed that the clinical impact of body weight on vixarelimab exposure was minor, suggesting that dose adjustment based on weight is not warranted. The developed model provides a thorough understanding of vixarelimab pharmacokinetics and the impact of covariates on exposure to support future clinical development.

Introduction

Vixarelimab (also known as KPL‐716) is a first‐in‐class fully human monoclonal antibody targeting oncostatin M (OSM) receptor beta (OSMRβ), which is a cell surface cytokine receptor subunit that heterodimerizes with interleukin‐31 (IL‐31) receptor α (IL‐31RA) or glycoprotein 130 to form the IL‐31 receptor or the OSM type II receptor, respectively [1]. The role of IL‐31 in the symptomatology of pruritus is well established, with elevated IL‐31 expression levels reported in various chronic pruritic conditions [2]. Nemolizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL‐31 RA, has been shown to be effective in treating moderate‐to‐severe atopic dermatitis (AD) and prurigo nodularis (PN) [3, 4], and recently been approved by the Food and Drug Administration for both indications. On the other hand, OSM, a member of the IL‐6 family of cytokines, has been shown to be involved in inflammation, epidermal integrity, and fibrosis [5]. Therefore, by targeting OSMRβ, vixarelimab simultaneously inhibits the OSM‐ and IL‐31‐mediated signaling pathways implicated in pruritus, inflammation, and fibrosis [1, 6]. In a Phase 2 proof‐of‐concept study in patients with moderate‐to‐severe PN, vixarelimab demonstrated anti‐pruritic efficacy and was well tolerated with no major safety signals identified to date [1].

Vixarelimab binds to both the membrane‐bound form of OSMR and the soluble form of OSMR generated by alternative splicing [7], and such binding is responsible for the target‐mediated clearance of vixarelimab. The pharmacokinetics (PK) of vixarelimab have been evaluated in a Phase 1 study in healthy volunteers and patients with AD following single intravenous (IV) dosing or single and multiple subcutaneous (SC) dosing, as well as in Phase 2 studies in patients with chronic pruritic conditions following multiple SC dosing. However, detailed PK analysis for vixarelimab has not been previously reported. In this work, we aimed to develop a population PK (popPK) model by pooling data from Phase 1 and 2 trials in healthy volunteers and patients with chronic pruritic conditions, to characterize the PK of vixarelimab and to evaluate the impact of covariates on vixarelimab exposure.

KPL‐716‐C001 was a four‐part randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled first‐in‐human study of vixarelimab in healthy volunteers and patients with AD. Part 1 and Part 3 evaluated the safety, tolerability, PK, and immunogenicity of single ascending doses in patients with moderate–severe AD and healthy volunteers, respectively, at dose levels detailed in Table 1. For both Parts 1 and 3, subjects received a single dose of study drug via IV or SC route on Day 0 based on their assigned dose group. Sentinel dosing was implemented in each dose group of Parts 1 and 3, and escalation to the next cohort was based on 14‐day post‐dose safety and tolerability data reviewed by the safety data review committee. Part 2 of the study, originally planned for patients on hemodialysis with uremic pruritus, did not enroll any subject due to lack of feasibility. Part 4 evaluated the safety, tolerability, PK, immunogenicity, and exploratory efficacy of repeated dosing of vixarelimab in patients with moderate–severe AD. Patients were randomized to receive 12 weekly SC administrations of vixarelimab 360 mg or placebo from Day 0 on Week 0 to Day 77 on Week 11.

KPL‐716‐C201 (NCT03816891) was a Phase 2a/b randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study that investigated the efficacy, safety, tolerability, PK, and immunogenicity of vixarelimab in patients with moderate to severe PN and in PN patients with severe pruritus. For the Phase 2a proof‐of‐concept portion [1], the initial treatment duration was 16 weeks, corresponding to one 720 mg SC loading dose followed by 15 maintenance doses of 360 mg SC or placebo weekly. After implementation of Protocol Version 3, the treatment duration was shortened to 8 weeks, corresponding to one 720 mg SC loading dose followed by 7 maintenance doses of 360 mg SC or placebo weekly. For the Phase 2b dose‐ranging portion, patients received vixarelimab 540 mg SC or 360 mg SC or 120 mg SC or placebo every 4 weeks for 16 weeks, followed by open‐label vixarelimab 360 mg every 2 weeks up to Week 48.

KPL‐716‐C202 (NCT03858634) was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled Phase 2 study that evaluated the efficacy, safety, tolerability, PK, and immunogenicity of vixarelimab in five different pruritic diseases characterized by chronic pruritus (chronic idiopathic pruritus, chronic idiopathic urticaria, lichen planus, lichen simplex chronicus, plaque psoriasis). Patients received a loading dose of vixarelimab 720 mg SC followed by 360 mg SC or placebo weekly for 7 maintenance doses.

NONMEM dataset was constructed by pooling data from these studies using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). Concentration data were excluded if (1) the result was missing, (2) the collection date/time was missing, (3) there were duplicate samples associated with the same collection date and time, (4) the sample was collected prior to the first dose but the result was quantifiable.

Assay Methodology

For Study KPL‐716‐C001 Part 1 and Part 3, vixarelimab serum concentrations were measured using a validated ELISA method, with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 400 ng/mL. For KPL‐716‐C001 Part 4, KPL‐716‐C201 Phase 2a/b, and KPL‐716‐C202, vixarelimab serum concentrations were measured using a validated electrochemiluminescent immunoassay (ECLA), with a LLOQ of 150 ng/mL. The assays did not detect vixarelimab when both binding sites are bound.

Modeling Software

Population PK modeling with log‐transformed vixarelimab concentration data was performed using the first‐order conditional estimation method (FOCE) with interaction method in NONMEM (version 7.5.1, ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD) and Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (version 5.3.1, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden). R statistical software (version 4.2 or higher, R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria) was used for generating statistical summaries, graphical analyses, and model diagnostics.

Population PK Model Development

The development of a base model consisted of a structural model, an interindividual variability (IIV) model, and a residual error model. Based on visual inspection of the PK observations, which indicated two‐compartment disposition and dose‐dependent nonlinear PK characteristic of target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD), a two‐compartment TMDD model with quasi‐steady state (QSS) approximation was evaluated as a structural model. The QSS approximation was considered more general than the quasi‐equilibrium (QE) approximation and could avoid the overparameterization with the full TMDD model [8]. The structural model contained the central compartment and the peripheral compartment, with the assumption that the binding of vixarelimab to the target (OSMR) occurs in the central compartment. The QSS approximation was parameterized by equilibrium constant (K ss), target degradation rate constant (K deg), the drug‐target complex internalization rate constant (K int), and baseline target level (R 0). In addition, to describe the SC absorption, a depot compartment was linked to the central compartment with a first‐order absorption process, with the absorption rate constant (K a ) and bioavailability (F 1, parameterized on logit scale) estimated from the model.

Interindividual variability (IIV) of parameters was assumed to follow a lognormal distribution with a mean of zero and variance of ω2: (1) θi=θTypicaleηi where θ i represents the parameter estimate for individual i, θ Typical represents the population parameter estimate, and η i is the interindividual random effect. IIV was examined on PK parameters that describe the linear processes (central volume [V c ], linear clearance [CL], intercompartment clearance [Q], peripheral volume [V p ], K a , and F 1) and R 0. Residual variability was modeled using a log‐additive error model.

The base model was evaluated based on parameter precision (relative standard error [RSE]), condition number, and visual inspection of the goodness‐of‐fit plots. The influence of the outliers (identified by |CWRES| > 6) on parameter estimates was also examined. If there was a change of ≥ 20% in one or more of the fixed‐effects parameters, then the outliers were considered influential and removed before covariate model development.

Covariate analysis was performed using stepwise covariate model building. Continuous variables were tested on the parameters using a power model centered to the median of the covariate value, and categorical variables were tested using a linear model. As the first step, baseline weight on CL and V c was incorporated as a structural covariate. Subsequently, covariates were assessed with forward inclusion at p = 0.01 and backward elimination at p = 0.001 based on the objective function value (OFV) to derive the covariate model. The list of covariates to be evaluated was pre‐specified based on clinical and mechanistic plausibility, which included age, body weight, sex, immunogenicity status, disease status (healthy volunteers vs. patients with chronic pruritic conditions), and race (Asian versus others) (Table S2).

Following the covariate model building step, model refinement of the covariate model was done to optimize the IIV parameterization as needed to derive the final model. In addition, as a sensitivity analysis, the final model was refitted to the whole dataset (i.e., including the outliers previously excluded) to evaluate the influence of the outliers on the final parameter estimates.

Model Evaluation

The final model was evaluated through routine goodness‐of‐fit plots (dependent variable [DV] vs. population predicted value [PRED], DV vs. individual predicted value [IPRED], conditional weighted residuals [CWRES] vs. time after first dose, and CWRES vs. PRED). Visual predictive check (VPC) was performed with 500 simulations stratified on unique combinations of dose level and study, and the results were plotted using “xpose” package in R to assess the predictive performance of the final model. To evaluate the effect of covariates included in the final model on vixarelimab exposure, the fold change in steady‐state maximum concentration (C max) and AUC0‐tau (area under the concentration‐time curve during a dosing interval) following repeated 360 mg weekly SC dosing was simulated with 500 sets of parameters to account for parameter uncertainty, using the “mrgsolve” package in R [9]. The visualization of the covariate effects evaluation was performed using the “coveffectsplot” package in R [10].

Results

Overview of Patient Characteristics and PK Data

The popPK dataset contains 4032 vixarelimab serum concentrations from 274 participants. Excluding 260 samples collected prior to the first dose, 3328 samples out of the remaining 3772 samples were quantifiable and were used for popPK modeling.

Baseline characteristics of the 274 participants in the popPK dataset are shown in Table 2. Most participants were white (68%) and female (58%). The median (range) age and weight were 52 (18–79) years and 82 (48–158) kg, respectively. The vast majority (86%) of the participants had skin disorders. 18% of the participants had at least one positive post‐baseline immunogenicity result.

Population PK Model Development

Model runs are summarized in Table S3. The structural two‐compartment QSS model was first fitted to the dataset, followed by incorporation of diagonal IIV terms, which yielded a stable model (condition number < 1000) with adequate parameter precision (RSE for fixed‐effects parameters < 25%). Subsequently, 11 outliers were identified based on CWRES. Removing these outliers resulted in similar estimates for the PK parameters related to the linear part of the model but led to significantly different estimates that described the nonlinear elimination, including R 0, K deg and K int (Table S4). Therefore, these outliers were considered influential and were removed to yield the base model used for covariate model development. Diagnostic plots for the base model show good concordance between model predictions and observations and absence of obvious bias (Figure S3).

In covariate model building, the incorporation of body weight on CL and V c as structural covariates led to a statistically significant decrease of OFV by 133.5 points. In forward selection, body weight on V p was first incorporated (∆OFV = −16.5), followed by incorporation of age on V c (∆OFV = −10.2). In backward elimination, age on V c was removed, while body weight on CL, V c , and V p was retained. In the covariate model, the estimated IIV on V p approached 0 and was poorly estimated. Fixing the IIV on V p to 0 had no change in the OFV and yielded the final model. As a sensitivity analysis, previously removed outliers were re‐incorporated, which resulted in an increase in condition number from 190 to 2087, an increase in OFV of 1025 points, and significant changes in the estimates for R 0, K deg, and K int (Table S5). Therefore, the two‐compartment TMDD model based on QSS approximation without the previously identified outliers, with IIV estimated on CL, V c , V p , K a , F 1, and R 0, incorporating body weight effect on CL, V c , and V p , was considered the final model.

Model Evaluation

The final model has satisfactory parameter precision (Table 3). The estimated nonspecific clearance was 0.00649 L/h (0.156 L/day). The central volume and peripheral volume were estimated to be 3.04 and 1.74 L, respectively, consistent with limited extravascular distribution of monoclonal antibodies. Subcutaneous bioavailability and the absorption rate constant were estimated to be 57% and 0.0126 h−1, respectively. Routine diagnostic plots showed alignment between observations and model predictions without obvious bias or misspecification (Figure 2). VPCs stratified by dose level and study showed good predictive performance of the model. The model‐predicted medians and 95% confidence intervals were generally aligned with the observations (Figures 3, S4, and S5). As shown in Figure 4, the effect of body weight on vixarelimab PK was minor, with C max and AUC0‐tau ratios and their 90% confidence intervals for weight at the 25th–75th percentiles with respect to the reference subject lying within the bioequivalence limits of 0.8–1.25. At the 5th and 95th percentiles of body weight, the median C max and AUC0‐tau ratios with respect to the reference subject were 1.4 and 0.7, respectively. Overall, the change in exposure due to low or high body weight was not considered to be clinically relevant.

Discussion

In this work, we developed a TMDD population PK model for vixarelimab using data pooled from Phase 1 and Phase 2 studies in healthy volunteers and patients with chronic pruritic conditions. The pooled dataset contained observations after single (IV or SC) and multiple SC dosing, with the single doses tested spanning from 0.3 to 20 mg/kg IV. This two‐compartment TMDD model with QSS approximation satisfactorily described the observed PK data across different studies and different dose levels tested.

In covariate model development, given the relatively high shrinkage estimates for IIV parameters, plots of individual random effect values versus covariate values were not used to guide covariate analysis. Based on pre‐specified significance levels for stepwise covariate model building, body weight was identified as the only statistically significant covariate, and it positively correlated with CL, V c , and V p . Based on simulation results, the clinical impact of body weight on vixarelimab exposure was expected to be minor. Skin disease was not identified as a significant covariate, consistent with the finding that PK profiles were similar between healthy volunteers and patients with atopic dermatitis. No statistically significant effect of immunogenicity was observed on the CL. Similarly, age, sex, and race did not have a statistically significant impact on CL or V c .

Various mathematical models have been developed to characterize the TMDD phenomenon [11]. To avoid the overparameterization with the full TMDD model, simpler models based on approximations, such as the QE approximation, the QSS approximation, and the Michaelis–Menten approximation, have been proposed [8, 12]. Vixarelimab binds to both free membrane‐bound OSMR (mOSMR) and soluble OSMR (sOSMR), with the latter present in the circulation at significant levels (~100–400 ng/mL, equivalent to ~1.4–5.7 nM) [7, 13]. Michaelis–Menten approximation assumes that the target concentration is low relative to the free drug concentration [8], which is not consistent with the presence of significant levels of circulating sOSMR. On the other hand, the QSS approximation is more general than the QE approximation and was therefore used as the mathematical model to describe the PK of vixarelimab. In our case, a validated assay for sOSMR was not available when the clinical studies were conducted, which is a limitation of this study. As the current modeling effort relies on PK alone, the nonlinear part of the QSS model may still be overparameterized without the target level data. This could potentially lead to bias in some parameter estimates related to the QSS approximation, as suggested by the sensitivity in these parameters to outliers. However, the estimate for R 0 in the final model was considered plausible, as it is in a similar range as the reported sOSMR levels. Parameter estimates related to the linear part of the model, including nonspecific clearance and volumes, were shown to be robust to outliers and have values consistent with those reported for a typical monoclonal antibody [14]. As the parameters describing the linear process are the determinant for drug exposure at therapeutic doses that saturate the target‐mediated elimination, the current model was considered suitable for the evaluation of covariate effect at clinically relevant exposures. Overall, the current model provided a satisfactory fit to the PK observations across the range of tested dose levels among different studies, and it was well qualified using the routine diagnostic plots and the VPC approach.

Based on the final model, profiles of vixarelimab serum PK and OSMR levels can be simulated at the tested or alternative dose levels. For example, among the dose levels tested in the double‐blinded portion of the KPL‐716‐C201 Ph2b study, at 360 mg SC or 540 mg SC every 4 weeks, the levels of free receptors are predicted to be below 10% of the baseline level throughout the dosing interval, while 120 mg SC every 4 weeks is not expected to maintain sufficient target engagement. An important caveat here is that given the structure of the model, the predicted receptor occupancy is evaluated from the central compartment, and no distinction can be made between the soluble form of the receptor and the membrane‐bound form of the receptor in the tissues of interest that the drug needs to bind to exert therapeutic effect. To better predict the occupancy of the membrane‐bound OSMR, incorporation of target levels in future studies will allow the application of a two‐target QSS model that accounts for binding to both soluble and membrane‐bound targets, to provide more mechanistic understanding of the nonlinear PK of vixarelimab [15, 16].

In summary, a two‐compartment TMDD model with QSS approximation satisfactorily described the observed vixarelimab concentration‐time data in healthy volunteers and patients with chronic pruritic diseases. Among all evaluated covariates, body weight was the only statistically significant covariate included in the model. The clinical impact of body weight on vixarelimab is expected to be minor, and therefore dose adjustment for weight is not warranted. The developed model provides a thorough understanding of vixarelimab pharmacokinetics and the impact of covariates to support future clinical development.
