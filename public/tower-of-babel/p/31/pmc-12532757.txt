Endophytic bacterial diversity of Vicatia thibetica collected from Xizang and the association with flavonoid accumulation
Authors: Weihua Zhang, Chao Yang, Kexin Zhao, Manfei Jin, Kezhen Han, Ying Wang, Zhao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVicatia thibetica de Boiss is a unique medicinal and edible plant endemic to Xizang, China. It is one of the five primary root medicines in Tibetan medicine due to its high content of flavonoids. However, the community composition of endophytic bacteria in its various tissues and their potential role in flavonoid accumulation remain unclear. We employed high-throughput sequencing to compare the diversity of endophytic bacteria in the rhizosphere soil and various tissues of V. thibetica collected from three sampling sites in Nyingchi, Xizang. Concurrently, we assessed the types and concentrations of flavonoids present in the roots. Finally, we investigated the relationship between root endophytic bacteria and flavonoid accumulation through correlation analysis. The results indicated that the diversity and abundance of bacterial communities in the rhizosphere soil exceeded those of the endophytic bacterial communities of V. thibetica. Proteobacteria is the dominant phylum, and Sphingomonas is the dominant genus. Each tissue of a plant exhibits its dominant genus. PICRUSt predictive analysis revealed that RNA processing and modification were the predominant functions among related species. Targeted metabolomics analysis has revealed that the roots of the plants contain 14 flavonoid compounds. Correlation analysis revealed that the concentrations of flavonoids in the roots, including apigenin, rutin, astragalin, quercetin 3-glucoside, L-epicatechin, kaempferol, and luteolin, are associated with the distribution and abundance of specific bacterial genera, such as Lactobacillus, Kurthia, Bradyrhizobium, Phenylobacterium, Novosphingobium, and Mycobacterium, among others. This finding suggests that these bacterial genera may directly influence the production and accumulation of flavonoids in the plant. Our findings will enhance the understanding of plant-microbe interactions and provide crucial insights into the role of endophytes in the production of V. thibetica and its significant secondary metabolites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02610-4.Keywords: Vicatia thibetica, Endophytic bacteria, 16S metabarcoding, Flavonoids, Culture-independent techniques, Targeted metabolomics

Introduction

Research indicates that the growth of traditional Chinese medicinal materials and the accumulation of their active compounds are influenced not only by the intrinsic characteristics of the plant and environmental factors but also by the community structure of endophytic bacteria associated with these plants [1]. These bacteria can inhabit the roots, stems, or leaves, facilitating the growth of medicinal materials, enhancing stress resistance, and inducing or promoting the synthesis and accumulation of active compounds. Some endophytic bacteria may even produce active compounds identical or similar to those found in the medicinal materials themselves [2, 3]. Song et al. reported that Bacillus altitudinis, isolated from Panax ginseng, can enhance the accumulation of ginsenosides [4]. Additionally, endophytic bacteria isolated from Scurrula atropurpurea have been found to produce quercetin [5]. Pestalotiopsin A and B, isolated from the endophytic fungus Pestalotiopsis leucoths derived from Tripterygium wilfordii by Kumar, exhibit immunosuppressive effects on humans [6]. Furthermore, endophytic fungi isolated from Catharanthus roseus, Podophyllum hexandrum, and Maytenus hookeri are capable of producing antitumor medicinal compounds such as vinblastine, podophyllotoxin, and maytansine [7–9]. However, the composition of endophytes is influenced by factors such as plant species, plant parts, and growth stages [10]. Analyzing the diversity and composition of endophytes across different plant parts is crucial for enhancing plant growth and elucidating the role of these microorganisms.

V. thibetica, commonly called wild angelica or Xigui in Xizang, belongs to the Apiaceae family. This plant is primarily found in the southwestern regions of China, including Sichuan, Yunnan, and Xizang [11], where it thrives on high-altitude slopes (ranging from 2700 to 4200 m) in forested or grassland areas [12]. V. thibetica is one of the five principal root medicines in traditional Tibetan medicine, alongside Polygonatum sibiricum, Asparagus cochinchinensis, Mirabilis himalaica, and Tribulus terrestris. Its therapeutic properties are highly esteemed. Within the framework of traditional Tibetan medicine, this material serves as a crucial medicinal component, incorporated into numerous Tibetan medicinal formulations. The practical medicinal component of V. thibetica is its root, which is abundant in flavonoids. Li et al. employed ultraviolet spectrophotometry to quantify the total flavonoid content in V. thibetica, revealing that it exceeds 7.74 mg/g [13]. Flavonoids exhibit a variety of functions, including anti-inflammatory, analgesic, immune-regulatory, anti-aging, and lipid-lowering effects [14]. Consequently, they serve as a crucial medicinal resource in Tibetan medicine for addressing a range of health issues. However, this plant is a perennial species that requires four years to mature, and its yield is insufficient to meet the demand for medicinal use. Therefore, shortening the growth cycle or identifying alternative methods to obtain its medicinal substances has become an urgent issue that requires attention.

Currently, numerous studies have focused on the pharmacological effects of this plant and the isolation of its active compounds [15, 16]. However, the types and concentrations of flavonoids present in the roots of V. thibetica remain unidentified, and there have been limited investigations into the influence of the internal environment on medicinal plants. Specifically, endophytes and medicinal plants form a microecosystem within the internal environment of these plants, which may elucidate the mechanisms underlying the accumulation of practical medicinal components from a novel perspective.

We employed a culture-independent technique to analyze the diversity of endophytic bacterial colonies, elucidating the community structure and dominant bacterial groups in V. thibetica across varying altitudes and different tissues. This analysis was subsequently integrated with targeted metabolomics to investigate the relationship between flavonoid content and microbial composition. This study aims to elucidate the diversity of bacterial communities in various tissues of V. thibetica at different altitudes, while also exploring the endophytic microbiota associated with flavonoid accumulation. The findings provide insights for the subsequent isolation of microbial communities capable of producing flavonoids and lay a foundation for expanding the knowledge on plant-microbe relationships and improving the quality of V. thibetica.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials

V. thibetica was collected from three artificial planting areas in Nyingchi City, Xizang, at 2940 to 3674 m. The plant samples have a growth period of 3 years. At each sampling site, five plant samples with heights ranging from 1.0 to 1.1 m will be selected within a 1 m2 area located at the diagonal intersection of the medicinal field. From these samples, three will be chosen based on relatively uniform root sizes to serve as replicates for the sampling site. Both plant and rhizosphere soil samples will be collected from each selected specimen. The details regarding the sampling site and sample morphology are illustrated in Fig. 1. The rhizosphere soil was placed in sterile 50-mL centrifuge tubes, while the plants were transported back to the laboratory in sterile bags maintained at 4 °C. Following the separation of the roots and stems, the plant samples were rinsed with tap water and washed three times with distilled water. Surface disinfection of the plants was conducted by soaking the roots and stems in 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 min, followed by treatment with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 6 min and 2.5% sterile sodium thiosulfate for 10 min. After being washed four times with sterile water, the samples were soaked in 70% ethanol for 6 min and then washed five times with sterile water to completely remove residual reagents [17]. The samples were then stored at −80 °C for DNA extraction. For the rhizosphere soil, 5 g of soil was measured into a petri dish lined with filter paper and air-dried at room temperature for 1 week before undergoing dilution treatment for DNA extraction [18].

DNA Extraction, PCR Amplification, and Sequencing

Total DNA from microbial communities was extracted from various root and stem sample points using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were then measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene from both plant endophytic bacteria and rhizosphere soil was conducted using primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [19]. The amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Each sample was repeated three times [20]. After recovering the PCR products, purification was performed using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA), and the purified products were quantified using a Quantus Fluorometer (Promega, USA). Following quantification, library preparation was performed using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-seq Kit, and the samples were sent to Shanghai Meiji Biological Co., Ltd. for sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform.

Analysis of Bacterial Diversity in V. thibetica

The raw paired-end sequencing data underwent quality control using Fastp software (httpss://bcb.umd.edu/software/flash) [21] prior to assembly with FLASH (https://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash, version 1.2.11) [22]. Following quality control, the assembled sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on 97% similarity using UPARSE software (https://drive5.com/uparse/, version 1) [23], and chimeras were subsequently removed. The RDP classifier (https://rdp.cme.msu.edu/, version 2.11) was then employed to compare the sequences against the Silva (version 138.2) 16S rRNA gene database. The community composition of each sample was statistically analyzed at various taxonomic levels. Alpha diversity analysis was conducted using the mothur software (https://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators) [24], incorporating the Sobs, Chao1, Shannon, and Ace indices. Duncan’s multiple range test was utilized to evaluate inter-group differences in alpha diversity. Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed based on species annotation results to assess similarities and differences in species composition between groups and samples. Community bar plots and phylogenetic tree analyses were integrated to examine species composition, abundance information, and trends across different groups. Finally, the distribution proportions and relationships of microbial communities across samples were elucidated using heatmap plots. Additionally, BugBase phenotype predictions were performed utilizing the BugBase software (httpss://bugbase.cs.umn.edu/index.html). Furthermore, the COG database predicted the community functions of endophytic bacteria using PICRUSt (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy) [25].

Analysis of Flavonoid Compound Content in the Roots

The roots of V. thibetica were meticulously cleaned and disinfected, with three replicates taken from each sample site. A precise weight of 100 mg of root was placed into a 2-mL EP tube, followed by the addition of 600 µL of methanol and 100 mg of glass beads. This mixture was then subjected to a high-throughput tissue grinder and shaken at 60 Hz for 1 min, a process repeated twice. Following this, ultrasonic treatment was applied at room temperature for 15 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant obtained was filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane and transferred to a detection bottle. Concurrently, 35 flavonoid compounds (refer to Table 1) were selected, and a single standard stock solution was prepared using 80% methanol in formic acid. This stock solution was measured and combined to create a mixed standard, which was then diluted with 80% methanol to achieve the desired concentration for the standard solution [26].

The parameters for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis were established with the following chromatographic conditions: an ACQUITY UPLC® BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters Corporation, USA), an injection volume of 5 µL, and a column temperature maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of A: 0.1% formic acid and B: methanol, with a flow rate set at 0.25 mL/min. The gradient elution conditions were as follows: 0 to 1 min, 10% B; 1 to 3 min, 10% to 33% B; 3 to 10 min, 33% B; 10 to 15 min, 33% to 50% B; 15 to 20 min, 50% to 90% B; 20 to 21 min, 90% B; 21 to 22 min, 90% to 10% B; and 22 to 25 min, 10% B [27]. Mass spectrometry was conducted using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in negative ionization mode. The ion source temperature was set to 500 °C, the ion source voltage to −4500 V, the collision gas to 6 psi, the curtain gas to 30 psi, and the nebulizer and auxiliary gases to 50 psi [28]. Multiple reaction monitoring was employed for scanning. Quantitative analysis was conducted on all samples following the established sample pretreatment and analytical methods. The peak area of the analytes was utilized in a linear equation to derive the concentration results. The data were analyzed using SPSS 16.0 software, employing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05) to assess variance.

Correlation Analysis

To investigate the correlation between endophytic bacteria in V. thibetica and flavonoid levels, we constructed a dual matrix that integrated the colony structures of the bacteria from the roots with their corresponding flavonoid content. The flavonoid content data served as the response variable, while the diversity of endophytic bacteria was treated as the explanatory variable. We conducted redundancy analysis (RDA) to elucidate the association between the community structure (relative abundance) of endophytic bacteria and flavonoid content. Additionally, we calculated Spearman correlation coefficients between environmental factors and the top 50 species based on relative abundance. This calculation resulted in a numerical matrix, which was visually represented through a heatmap, illustrating the correlation between the community structure of endophytic bacteria and flavonoid content [29]. The data were analyzed using SPSS 16.0 software, employing one-way ANOVA and Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05) to assess variance.

Results

Analysis of Sequencing Data and Alpha, Beta Diversity

A total of 1,489,777 high-quality sequences and 3228 OTUs were obtained from the rhizosphere soil, roots, and stems of V. thibetica. Detailed information can be found in Table S1. The dilution curve (Fig. 2A) illustrates that as sequencing depth increases, the species richness—measured by the Shannon index—of samples from various parts of V. thibetica tends to stabilize. This stabilization suggests that the sequencing data obtained from the samples are sufficient. Specifically, LZ-1 contains 2521 OTUs, of which 455 are unique. It shares 140 OTUs with LZ-2 and 165 OTUs with LZ-3. In contrast, LZ-2 comprises 2478 OTUs, including 121 unique OTUs, and shares 456 OTUs with LZ-3. LZ-3, on the other hand, has 2512 OTUs. Among LZ-1, LZ-2, and LZ-3, a total of 1761 OTUs are shared, ranked from highest to lowest as LZ-1, LZ-3, and LZ-2 (Fig. 2B). The rhizosphere soil of V. thibetica contains 2492 OTUs, which include 648 unique OTUs. It shares 539 OTUs with the roots and 288 OTUs with the stems. The roots comprise 2024 OTUs, with 209 unique OTUs, and share 259 OTUs with the stems. The stems contain 1832 OTUs, with 268 unique OTUs, and share 1017 OTUs with the roots and rhizosphere soil. The total number of OTUs across various organizations is generally highest in rhizosphere soil, followed by roots, with stems exhibiting the lowest count (Fig. 2C).

Sequencing dilution curves (A) and Venn diagram showing bacterial OTUs of V. thibetica at different sample points and tissue sites (B, C)

The results of the Alpha diversity analysis (Fig. 3) reveal that all samples possess coverage values exceeding 0.98, indicating sufficient sample coverage. At the same altitude, the Sobs, Chao1, Shannon, and Ace indices for the rhizosphere bacteria associated with V. thibetica and the endophytic bacteria in its roots and stems gradually decline. This trend suggests that the rhizosphere bacteria of V. thibetica at the same altitude demonstrate greater richness and diversity compared to the endophytic bacteria found within the plant tissues, with statistically significant differences noted. Additionally, while the roots display greater diversity than the stems, the difference between the two is not statistically significant.

Alpha diversity analysis of different samples. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01,***p < 0.001

Beta diversity analysis reveals significant differences in microbial communities across various parts of V. thibetica (Fig. 4A, p = 0.001 < 0.05). Additionally, notable variations in endophytic bacterial communities are observed in the roots and stems, as well as in the rhizosphere soil bacteria of V. thibetica at different altitude gradients (Fig. 4B–D, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the variations in endophytic bacterial communities within the stems are more pronounced than those between the rhizosphere soil bacteria and the endophytic bacteria found in the roots. Specifically, endophytic bacteria from low-altitude environments represent 86.85% of the diversity as determined by PC1 and PC2, while those from medium-altitude and high-altitude environments account for 74.24% and 76.80% of the diversity, respectively.

Species Composition Analysis

Thirty-six phyla, 106 classes, 254 orders, 437 families, 840 genera, and 1558 species were identified across all samples. At the phylum level (Fig. 5A and B), the species composition of V. thibetica exhibits minor variations across different altitudes and plant parts. The predominant bacterial phyla identified are Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes. Proteobacteria display the highest relative abundance in the stem, followed by the root, with the lowest levels observed in the rhizosphere soil. In contrast, Actinobacteria are most abundant in the rhizosphere soil, followed by the stem, and have the lowest abundance in the root. Firmicutes show relatively high abundance in the roots at low and medium altitudes, followed by the stem, and the lowest in the rhizosphere soil. At high altitudes, however, the relative abundance of Firmicutes significantly differs from that at lower altitudes, being highest in the rhizosphere soil, followed by the stem, and lowest in the root. The figure illustrates the top 50 abundant groups at the genus level (Fig. 5A and C). The dominant genera include Sphingomonas, Bradyrhizobium, Lactobacillus, Variovorax, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Escherichia, Shigella, and Pantoea. However, each organization comprises its own dominant groups. In the rhizosphere soil, the relative abundances of Bacillus, Solirubrobacter, and Paenibacillus are notably high at 76.7%, 71.9%, and 46.1%, respectively. Conversely, in the roots, Novosphingobium (96.3%), Actinoplanes (93.7%), and Steroidobacter (89.5%) demonstrate greater relative abundances. In the stems, Sphingomonas (99.0%), Pseudomonas (97.4%), and Variovorax (92.0%) are the predominant genera. Detailed results can be observed in the pie chart in Fig. 5A. Among the top 50 dominant genera, Actinobacteria account for 22 genera, Proteobacteria for 18, and Firmicutes for 6. Notably, although the abundance of dominant genera within Actinobacteriota is lower, the number of dominant groups surpasses that of the other two phyla.

The community heatmap (Fig. 6) illustrates that the dominant bacterial taxa in the rhizosphere soil differ from those present in the plant roots and stems among the top 50 bacterial taxa based on relative abundance. The primary bacterial genera identified in the rhizosphere soil include Solirubrobacter, Bacillus, Paenibacillus, Gaiella, and Massilia, among others. In contrast, the endophytic bacteria in the roots are predominantly represented by Novosphingobium, Actinoplanes, Steroidobacter, Aeromicrobium, Bradyrhizobium, and others. Furthermore, the dominant genera of endophytic bacteria in the stems include Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Variovorax, Pantoea, and Curtobacterium, among others. Importantly, the medicinal parts of V. thibetica harbor a group of bacterial taxa with relative abundances that exceed those found in the rhizosphere soil and stems. Notably, these include Aeromicrobium, Mycobacterium, Nocardioides, Devosia, Pseudonocardia, Bradyrhizobium, Actinoplanes, Phenylobacterium, Novosphingobium, Actinocorallia, Steroidobacter, Acidibacter, Streptomyces, and others. These taxa exhibited significant variation across different tissues (Fig. 6).

Prediction and Analysis of Bacterial Community Functions

Using PICRUSt, based on the Cluster of Orthologous Groups of Proteins (COG) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/research/cog-project/), we predicted the functions of bacterial communities in various samples of V. thibetica. The results identified several functions, including RNA processing and modification, energy production and conversion, nucleotide transport and metabolism, intracellular transport, secretion and vesicular transport, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and catabolism. Notably, RNA processing and modification emerged as the predominant function in the samples, with relative abundances ranging from 9.51% to 10.96% (Fig. 7A). Functional predictions of bacteria across various plant parts indicate that anaerobic bacteria are more abundant in the endophytic bacteria found in the roots than in the rhizosphere soil and stems (p < 0.05). In contrast, bacteria that form biofilms and contain mobile genetic elements are more prevalent in the stems compared to the rhizosphere soil and roots (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the distribution of stress-tolerant bacterial strains follows the order: stems > roots > rhizosphere soil (Fig. 7B).

Targeted Metabolomic Analysis of the Roots of V. thibetica

Through targeted metabolomic sequencing of the medicinal roots of V. thibetica, it was determined that these roots contain 14 distinct types of flavonoids, including apigenin, kaempferol, luteolin, L-epicatechin, kaempferide, quercetin, myricetin, isovitexin, vitexin, astragalin, daidzin, quercetin 3-glucoside, diosmin, and rutin (Fig. 8). Notably, myricetin was exclusively identified in sample LZ-2 and was absent in both LZ-1 and LZ-3. In contrast, isovitexin and diosmin were present in samples LZ-1 and LZ-3 but were nearly undetectable in LZ-2. Additionally, daidzin was found solely in LZ-3. Moreover, the concentrations of L-epicatechin and astragalin in the roots of V. thibetica significantly exceed those of the other flavonoids, with L-epicatechin and astragalin both surpassing 0.010 μg/g in all three samples, and astragalin reaching a concentration of 0.046 µg/g in LZ-3.

Analysis of the Association Between Endophytic Bacteria and Metabolites

The RDA analysis in Fig. 9A indicates that RDA1 and RDA2 collectively accounted for 57.31% of the observed variability. The findings from the RDA analysis demonstrate positive correlations among the flavonoids apigenin, kaempferide, luteolin, and quercetin. Additionally, astragalin showed a positive correlation with quercetin3-glucoside, while rutin showed a positive correlation with L-epicatechin. Correlation analysis further revealed that Lactobacillus, Aeromicrobium, and Mycobacterium were positively correlated with the flavonoids rutin and L-epicatechin, with Lactobacillus exhibiting the strongest correlation with both flavonoids. Moreover, the bacterial groups Kurthia and Actinocorallia were positively correlated with the accumulation of the four flavonoids: apigenin, quercetin, luteolin, and kaempferide. Lastly, the groups Novosphingobium, Phenylobacterium, Bradyrhizobium, and Sphingomonas were positively correlated with the accumulation of astragalin and quercetin3-glucoside. The correlation heatmap analysis of the top 50 species by total abundance revealed several significant relationships (Fig. 9B). Rutin exhibited a strong positive correlation with Bacillus and Romboutsia (p < 0.01). In addition, the accumulation of quercetin 3-glucoside was positively associated with Devosia (p < 0.05). L-epicatechin significantly correlated with Chryseobacterium, Cellvibrio, and Bdellovibrio (p < 0.01). Apigenin showed a positive correlation with Actinocorallia and Gaiellales (p < 0.05), while kaempferol was positively associated with Streptomyces and Rhizobacter (p < 0.05). Luteolin and quercetin also exhibited significant positive correlations with Actinomadura and Streptomyces (p < 0.05). These findings highlight the important roles of the bacterial groups Bacillus, Romboutsia, Devosia, Rhizobacter, Streptomyces, Actinomadura, Actinocorallia, Bdellovibrio, Cellvibrio, Sphingomonas, and Chryseobacterium in the accumulation of the seven flavonoids: rutin, quercetin 3-glucoside, L-epicatechin, apigenin, kaempferol, luteolin, and quercetin.

Discussion

In this study, the diversity analysis revealed a gradual decline in the species composition of the microbial community from the bottom to the top, corresponding to the ecological niche distribution of V. thibetica tissues. The rhizosphere soil exhibited the highest bacterial diversity, followed by the roots, while the stems showed the lowest diversity. This phenomenon has been corroborated by multiple studies [30, 31]. The bacterial diversity in the rhizosphere soil surpasses that in the rhizome, likely due to the abundant nutrient resources and microbial interactions present in the rhizosphere soil [32]. Although the bacterial diversity in the roots is lower than that in the rhizosphere soil, it remains significantly higher than that in the stems, with the bacterial community structure in the roots resembling that in the rhizosphere soil. This similarity indicates a close microbial exchange between the roots and the rhizosphere soil. The stems exhibit the lowest bacterial diversity among plant tissues, which may be attributed to the relatively homogeneous environmental conditions within the stems [30].

Species abundance analysis revealed that the predominant bacterial phyla identified across all samples are Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes. However, significant differences in microbial diversity were observed among various plant tissues across different sampling sites, a phenomenon potentially driven by multiple environmental factors. Among these factors, altitude is considered a key variable [33]. Research indicates that changes in altitude, by influencing microenvironmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, and light intensity, profoundly affect the structure and diversity of microbial communities in different plant tissues, including the rhizosphere, phyllosphere, and caulosphere [34]. Further analysis revealed that the differences in microbial communities among various plant tissues are not only reflected in species composition but also in the distribution of functional groups and interaction networks. This study found that anaerobic bacterial taxa were most abundant in the roots, while bacteria that form biofilms and contain mobile genetic elements were more prevalent in the stems compared to the rhizosphere soil and roots. This difference is attributed not only to environmental factors but also to the distinct microenvironments of various tissues and the specific roles played by the microbiota [32, 35].

Extensive research has demonstrated that alterations in plant endophytic microbiota can significantly influence the accumulation of secondary metabolites, particularly the synthesis and regulation of flavonoids [36]. Flavonoids, a class of secondary metabolites prevalent in plants, exhibit various biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial properties. Their accumulation is crucial not only for the plant’s stress resistance and growth development but also holds significant application value in the pharmaceutical and food industries [36]. In this study, we employed LC-MS/MS to detect flavonoids in plant roots collected from different sampling sites. Our findings indicate that the roots of V. thibetica contain 14 types of flavonoids, including apigenin, rutin, astragalin, and others, with varying concentrations across different sites. Additionally, the roots host a group of bacterial taxa that exhibit a relative abundance significantly higher than that found in the rhizosphere soil and stem. These taxa display distinct differences from those in other tissues, potentially linked to metabolite accumulation. Correlation analysis between metabolites and endophytic bacteria revealed that several flavonoids, such as apigenin, rutin, astragalin, quercetin 3-glycoside, L-epicatechin, kaempferol, and luteolin, showed significant correlations with the specific distribution and abundance of endophytic bacteria present in the roots, including Bacillus, Romboutsia, Devosia, Rhizobacter, Streptomyces, Sphingomonas, Actinomadura, Actinocorallia, Bdellovibrio, Cellvibrio, Chryseobacterium, Kurthia, Bradyrhizobium, Novosphingobium, Phenylobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Aeromicrobium (Fig. 9). These bacterial groups may be associated with the accumulation of flavonoids. Extensive research has demonstrated that endophytic microbiota regulate the synthesis of flavonoids through various mechanisms, including direct participation in metabolic pathways, modulation of host gene expression, and induction of plant defense responses via the secretion of signaling molecules [37, 38]. Huang et al. isolated a strain of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens LD5 from Gentiana nubigena, which is capable of synthesizing flavonoids [39]. Moreover, in the study of endophytic bacteria in Passiflora caerulea and Scurrula atropurpurea, it was found that these endophytic bacteria exhibit biosynthetic functions originally belonging to the host plants, demonstrating potential capabilities for synthesizing flavonoids [5]. Furthermore, endophytic bacteria can regulate the synthesis of flavonoids in plants through various mechanisms, such as influencing the supply of precursor substances, modulating metabolic pathways, or activating signal transduction [40].

It is noteworthy that the impact of endophytic microbiota on the accumulation of flavonoids is closely related to their species and functional diversity. Different types of endophytic microbiota may regulate flavonoid synthesis through distinct mechanisms. In the subsequent phases of our research, we will isolate and culture the relevant strains using pure culture methods. We aim to verify the functions of these endophytes and their associated mechanisms by detecting metabolites and conducting co-cultivation experiments with plants. Furthermore, the current study has employed a limited number of altitude gradients and has not examined the influence of soil nutrients at different sampling sites on bacterial distribution. In our future experiments, we will conduct a comprehensive analysis of how soil nutrients affect the accumulation of plant flavonoids by influencing the structure of the endophytic microbiota.

Conclusions

This study found that the endophytic bacterial communities in the roots and stems of V. thibetica, as well as the rhizosphere soil bacterial communities, are primarily composed of three phyla: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes. The rhizosphere soil exhibited the highest bacterial diversity, followed by the roots, while the stems showed the lowest diversity. Moreover, different tissue sections possess their own dominant bacterial communities, and the differences among them are significant. By conducting a combined analysis with root flavonoid metabolic data, it was discovered that the bacterial genera Bacillus, Romboutsia, Devosia, Rhizobacter, Streptomyces, Sphingomonas, Actinomadura, Actinocorallia, Bdellovibrio, Cellvibrio, Chryseobacterium, Kurthia, Bradyrhizobium, Novosphingobium, Phenylobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Aeromicrobium are significantly correlated with the accumulation of root flavonoids, including apigenin, rutin, astragalin, quercetin 3-glycoside, L-epicatechin, kaempferol, and luteolin. These findings lay the foundation for the isolation and cultivation of flavonoid-producing strains in subsequent stages, providing relevant data to support further understanding of the relationship between plants and microorganisms, as well as guiding the scientific cultivation of V. thibetica.
