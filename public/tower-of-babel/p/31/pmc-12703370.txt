Association of pancreas fat fraction with adverse kidney and cardiovascular outcomes
Authors: Philip Thomas, Martina Salud, Celestin Missikpode, Omar Garcia Martinez, Ashish Verma, Runqi Zhao, Sophie E Claudel, Insa M Schmidt, Daniel H van Raalte, Petter Bjornstad, Brian T Layden, James P Lash, Sushrut S Waikar, Anand Srivastava
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPancreas fat accumulation has been associated with impaired glucose metabolism and stimulation of inflammation. The associations of pancreatic fat fraction (PFF) with adverse outcomes remain understudied.MethodsWe estimated baseline predicted PFF (pPFF) from abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in 29 447 UK Biobank participants. Since the initial imaging visit occurred 9.3 [interquartile range (IQR) 8.2–10.2] years after baseline visit, we calculated annual change in PFF using linear regression in 337 participants who underwent repeat imaging 2.8 (IQR 2.2–4.8) years after the initial scan. We back-calculated pPFF by adding this change to each participant based on the time between baseline visit and initial MRI visit assuming linear change in PFF over time. Multivariable-adjusted proportional hazards models tested associations of pPFF with incident chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiovascular disease (CVD) and death.ResultsParticipants had a mean ± standard deviation age of 56 ± 8 years, mean eGFR of 98 ± 13 mL/min/1.73 m2, median (IQR) albuminuria of 5.7 (3.5–9.2) mg/g and median pPFF of 4.7 (1.6–10.3)%. During median follow-up of 13.8 years, there were 572 incident CKD, 1048 incident CVD and 468 death events. In final adjusted models, participants in the 4th, 3rd and 2nd quartiles had 1.58-, 1.42- and 1.76-fold higher risks of incident CKD compared with the 1st quartile, respectively. Participants in the 4th quartile had 1.27-fold higher risk of incident CVD compared with the lowest quartile. The associations of pPFF with incident CKD and CVD were each non-linear (each P < .001). pPFF was not associated with death.ConclusionsHigher pPFF levels are associated with higher risk of incident CKD and CVD but not death. Future studies should investigate whether pancreas fat depots should serve as a target of current or emerging therapies.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, CKD, ectopic fat, magnetic resonance imaging, pancreas fat

INTRODUCTION

The pancreas has a vital role in both endocrine and exocrine function in the body. The majority of the pancreas consists of exocrine tissue (>95%) which produces digestive enzymes to break down carbohydrates, proteins and fats [1]. The endocrine tissue, which is <5% of the pancreas, produces enzymes to maintain homeostasis for blood sugar levels and glandular secretion [1]. Deposition of fat in the pancreas may occur in the setting of metabolic syndrome and/or obesity that may result in pancreatic acinar cell death and replacement by adipocytes [2, 3]. These changes may ultimately lead to pancreatic steatosis which impairs pancreatic function through multiple mechanisms including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation and β-cell apoptosis. Enhanced understanding of the associations of pancreas fat with adverse clinical outcomes may help determine whether pancreas fat depots should serve as a target of current or emerging therapies.

Prior studies demonstrated that greater ectopic organ fat accumulation is associated with adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [4]. Recent reports suggest that greater levels of pancreas fat are associated with the development of impaired glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity, metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes mellitus [2, 5]. While prior studies have suggested that greater levels of pancreas fat, as assessed by non-invasive imaging, may be associated with the development or worsening of cardiometabolic disease, there are limited data that incorporate robust imaging techniques to quantify pancreas fat in prospective cohort studies [6]. To address this, we evaluated the associations of pancreas fat fraction (PFF), as assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with subsequent adverse clinical outcomes in participants from the UK Biobank.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Source population

The UK Biobank is a large, population-based prospective cohort study, established to investigate the genetic and non-genetic determinants of disease in middle and older aged adults. The UK Biobank recruited over 500 000 participants aged 40–69 years from England, Scotland and Wales between

2006 and 2010 [7, 8]. The UK Biobank imaging substudy is an ongoing investigation to scan the brains, hearts, bones and abdomens of 100 000 of the UK Biobank participants [9]. UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee and obtained written informed consent from all participants prior to the study. Data were extracted under access application 88543.

Study population

Of the UK Biobank participants, 29 464 participants had PFF measurements available. Of these participants that had PFF measurements, we excluded 17 participants who had pancreatic procedures that may have changed the size of the pancreas that resulted in 29 447 participants. These participants were further divided into separate analytic cohorts for each outcome of all-cause mortality, incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and incident chronic kidney disease (CKD) (Supplementary data, Fig. S1). We used data on 29 447 participants (analytic cohort 1) for the all-cause mortality outcome. We then divided these participants into separate analytic cohorts for each outcome. Analytic cohort 2 excluded 573 participants with prevalent CVD, which resulted in 28 874 participants for the incident CVD outcome analysis. Analytic cohort 3 excluded 3683 participants who had prevalent CKD or were missing data for estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) at baseline, which resulted in 25 764 participants for the incident CKD outcome analysis.

Abdominal MRI protocol and analysis

UK Biobank participants were recruited by email or letter from the National Health Service. Participants were scanned at four different imaging centers in Great Britain with a Siemens Aera 1.5T scanner (Syngo MRI D13, Simens, Erlangen, Germany), which used a dual-echo protocol that acquired overlapping images in six stations covering the body from the neck to knee within about 6 min [9, 10]. Dixon data were assembled into a single 3-dimension volume for each participant using an automated fat–water swap detection and correction procedure. Proton density fat fraction was estimated from the single-slice multi-echo data for the pancreas. The methods for calculation of PFF have been previously described [5, 9, 11].

Exposure

The primary exposure was predicted PFF (pPFF) at baseline. Since the initial abdominal MRI visit occurred 9.0 (IQR 7.5–10.1) years after the baseline visit, we calculated the change in PFF per year between abdominal MRI visits in 337 participants who returned for a repeat abdominal MRI. We back-calculated pPFF by adding this change for each participant based on the time between the baseline visit and the initial MRI visit assuming a linear change in PFF over time.

Outcomes

In the prospective analyses, the primary outcomes were all-cause mortality, incident CVD and incident CKD. Incident CVD was defined as incident myocardial infarction, heart failure or stroke, whichever occurred first. Secondary outcomes were the individual components of incident CVD. Incident outcomes were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes in any primary care, hospital admission records and death register records or office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures-version 4 (OPCS-4) codes in hospital inpatient data (Supplementary data, Table S1). Outcomes were assessed from the baseline visit to the date of the outcome of interest.

Measurements and assessment of baseline covariates

Data obtained at the baseline visit included sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, lifestyle behaviors, medications, standardized blood pressure measurements, anthropometric measures and laboratory measurements. Sex, race and ethnicity were self-reported. Comorbid conditions were defined using self-reported medical conditions or ICD-10 codes in primary care and hospital inpatient data. Blood and urine samples were collected and analyzed at the central laboratory. Serum and urine creatinine were measured using an isotope dilution mass spectrometry-traceable method. Urine albumin was calculated using an immunoturbidometric method. eGFR was calculated using the race-free creatinine- and cystatin C–based Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 equation. UACR was calculated as spot urine albumin concentration divided by spot urine creatinine concentration from urine samples. If the urine albumin concentration was lower than the limit of the assay, the value was replaced by half of the minimum observed value and then divided by the urine creatinine concentration.

Statistical analysis

To calculate baseline pPFF, we calculated the change in PFF per year using linear regression with time (in years) as the exposure variable and the outcome as difference in PFF. We added the change in PFF per year to each participant based on their respective time between the baseline visit and the initial abdominal MRI visit. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile range (IQR) and categorical variables were presented as count with percentages. For skewed data distributions, we performed logarithmic transformation as appropriate. To assess differences in frequency distributions of categorical variables, normally distributed continuous variables and non-normally distributed continuous variables by quartiles of pPFF, we used chi-squared, analysis of variance and Kruskal–Wallis tests, respectively. We evaluated clinical predictors of pPFF using unadjusted and multivariable linear regression models.

We performed time-to-event analyses to examine the risks for the outcomes, evaluating pPFF as a continuous variable (per doubling) and as quartiles. We used cause-specific Cox proportional hazards regression to investigate the multivariable-adjusted associations between pPFF and adverse clinical outcomes. Subdistribution hazard competing risk models were not used because the goal was to understand the biological associations of pPFF with each outcome. Our multivariable adjustment strategy was hierarchical and based on biological and clinical plausibility of baseline covariates as potential confounders of the association between pPFF and outcomes. Model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol use, multiple indices of deprivation, body mass index (BMI), waist to hip ratio (WHR) and body surface area (BSA); model 3 included model 2 and further adjusted for diabetes status, history of cancer, history of heart failure, history of myocardial infarction (MI), history of stroke, use of anti-hypertensive medications, systolic blood pressure (SBP), total cholesterol, serum albumin, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), C-reactive protein (CRP), eGFR and UACR. We examined the possibly nonlinear relationship between pPFF and outcomes with restricted cubic splines. Tests for nonlinearity used the likelihood ratio test, comparing the model with only the linear term to the model with the linear and cubic-spline terms [12]. We tested for statistical interaction between age (<55 vs ≥55 years), sex (male vs female) and diabetes status (yes vs no) in Cox models through multiplicative interaction terms. Since there were 29.2% missing covariate data, we used multiple imputation in the primary analysis. In sensitivity analyses, we performed the primary analysis using pPFF data without multiple imputation. We also repeated the primary analysis by using the initial MRI visit PFF data at baseline with multiple imputation of the missing covariate data. We adjusted for type of diabetes (type 1 or type 2), use of oral anti-diabetic medications, frequency of alcohol intake or insulin resistance as assessed by the triglyceride to high density lipoprotein (TG/HDL) ratio to determine whether these variables changed our primary results. We confirmed no violations of the proportional hazards assumption using the Kolmogorov-type supremum test and visual inspection by checking martingale residuals. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered significant.

Factors associated with pPFF

Table 2 shows predictors of pPFF in unadjusted and multivariable adjusted linear regression models. Of the demographic and socioeconomic factors, adjusted pPFF values were 1.05% higher per SD increase in age [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.95 to 1.16], 0.7% lower in females vs males (95% CI –1.01 to –0.41), 4.36% lower in Black compared with White individuals (95% CI –5.36 to –3.28), 0.53% higher in current smokers (95% CI 0.18 to 0.86) and 0.28% higher in previous smokers (95% CI 0.10 to 0.46) compared with never smokers, 0.72% lower in current alcohol drinkers vs never alcohol drinkers (95% CI –1.24 to –0.20) and 0.20% higher per SD increase in deprivation index (95% CI 0.12 to 0.28). Of the comorbid conditions, adjusted pPFF was 1.09% higher in individuals with a history of MI compared with those without history of MI (95% CI 0.34 to 1.84) and 1.91% higher in individuals with a history of stroke compared with those without history of stroke (95% CI 0.93 to 2.89). Of the anthropometric measures, adjusted pPFF was 1.63% higher per SD increase in BMI (95% CI 1.50 to 1.76), 1.68% higher per SD increase in WHR (95% CI 1.55 to 1.81) and 0.38% higher per SD increase in BSA (95% CI 0.23 to 0.53). The laboratory variables most strongly associated with adjusted pPFF were higher HbA1c [0.21% higher per SD increase (95% CI 0.11 to 0.32)], higher serum albumin [0.18% lower per SD increase (95% CI –0.28 to –0.09)], higher CRP [0.27% higher per SD increase (95% CI 0.18 to 0.35)], higher eGFR [0.39% lower per SD increase (95% CI –0.48 to –0.29)] and higher natural log transformed UACR [0.17% lower per SD increase (95% CI –0.26 to –0.08)].

Association of pPFF with incident death

During a median follow-up time of 13.8 (13.1–14.5) years, 468 participants died. Table 3 shows the unadjusted and multivariable adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs according to baseline pPFF as a continuous variable and by quartiles. In unadjusted models, greater levels of pPFF were statistically significantly associated with death as a continuous variable and in quartiles. Adjustment for demographics, socioeconomic factors, anthropometric measures, comorbid conditions and laboratory variables attenuated the association between pPFF and death, which was no longer statistically significant. We found no evidence of effect modification by age (P = .25), sex (P = .61) or diabetes status (P = .55) for the association between pPFF and death (Supplementary data, Table S3). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear association between pPFF and death (P < .001, Fig. 1A).

Association of pPFF with incident CVD

During a median follow-up time of 13.8 (13.1–14.5) years, 1048 participants experienced a CVD event. In unadjusted models, participants with higher levels of pPFF, as a continuous variable and in quartiles compared with the reference group, were at statistically significantly higher risks of incident CVD (Table 3). These results were attenuated after multivariable adjustment, and only participants in the highest quartile were at 1.27-fold higher risk of incident CVD compared with the lowest quartile. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear association between pPFF and incident CVD (P < .001, Fig. 1B). Supplementary data, Table S4 shows the unadjusted and multivariable adjusted associations of pPFF with each individual component of the composite CVD outcome. In the fully adjusted model, participants in quartile 4 and quartile 3 of pPFF were at 1.54-fold and 1.45-fold higher risks of incident MI compared with the lowest quartile. Participants with higher levels of pPFF were at nominally higher risks of incident heart failure, but this did not reach statistical significance after multivariable adjustment. There was no statistically significant association between pPFF and incident stroke after multivariable adjustment. We found no evidence of effect modification by age (P = .09), sex (P = .17) or diabetes status (P = .85) for the association between pPFF and incident CVD (Supplementary data, Table S5).

Association of pPFF with incident CKD

During a median follow-up time of 13.8 (13.1–14.5) years, 572 participants developed CKD. Participants in quartile 4, quartile 3 and quartile 2 were at 1.58-, 1.42- and 1.76-fold higher risks of incident CKD compared with the lowest quartile of pPFF in the fully adjusted model (Table 3). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear association between pPFF and incident CKD (P < .001, Fig. 1C). We found no evidence of effect modification by age (P = .20), sex (P = .15) or diabetes status (P = .37) for the association between pPFF and incident CVD (Supplementary data, Table S6).

Sensitivity analyses

Since we used multiple imputation for missing covariate data, we repeated the primary analysis as a complete case analysis. The associations of pPFF with death, incident CVD and incident CKD were qualitatively unchanged (Supplementary data, Table S7). Because we estimated PFF at baseline (pPFF) assuming a linear change over time, we repeated the primary analysis by placing the initial MRI visit PFF at baseline, which did not qualitatively change the results (Supplementary data, Table S8). Further adjustment for type of diabetes, alcohol intake frequency and TG/HDL ratio did not qualitatively change our results (Supplementary data, Table S9).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective study of over 29 000 middle and older aged adults, we found that higher levels of pPFF, as assessed by abdominal MRI, were independently associated with a higher risk of experiencing a CVD event and developing CKD but not all-cause mortality. We also identified several demographic, socioeconomic, anthropometric, comorbid and laboratory variables significantly associated with pPFF in cross-section. Importantly, our prospective findings remained statistically significant in multivariable models that accounted for these covariates. Collectively, our findings should stimulate future studies to determine the pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying these findings and whether reducing pPFF decreases the risks of adverse clinical outcomes.

Fat deposition in the pancreas may lead to injury and subsequent inflammation and apoptosis of pancreatic cells [3]. Aside from lipid storage, adipocytes may secrete metabolites, cytokines, chemokines and adipokines that may adversely affect islet cell function through endocrine effects [13]. Production of local cytokines that cause inflammation may lead to systemic subclinical inflammation marked by increased circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, similar to what is observed in metabolic syndrome [14]. While it seems plausible that pancreatic fat deposition may impair insulin secretion, prior research in this space is conflicting [2], and it is unclear whether pancreatic fat impairs insulin secretion unless there is heightened genetic risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus [15]. Nonetheless, the inflammatory milieu may lead to various forms of cell death and whether this leads to subclinical or overt β-cell dysfunction and impaired insulin secretion requires further investigation. We speculate that higher levels of PFF may contribute to subclinical exocrine and endocrine dysfunction and local inflammation that leads to greater systemic inflammation, which contributes to adverse health outcomes. Future research is warranted to fully elucidate the mechanisms responsible for adverse clinical outcomes in patients with higher levels of PFF.

Prior cross-sectional studies identified demographic factors such as older age and male sex as predictors of greater PFF [2, 16, 17]. Not surprisingly, risk factors seen in obese individuals, such as higher BMI and waist circumference, were also associated with higher levels of PFF in this study [17, 18]. In addition, participants with clinical phenotypes and diagnoses such as alcohol use, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and metabolic syndrome were all associated with higher levels of PFF [2, 18–21]. Interestingly, factors such as SBP and blood pressure medication use were no longer statistically significant in the multivariable model that included all factors in our study, which suggests that the other clinical factors may explain more of the associations with PFF. Taken together, our findings are largely consistent with the prior literature and provide more granularity for the strength of the associations between clinical risk factors and PFF to consider in future epidemiologic studies.

Prior prospective observational studies identified associations of higher PFF and the development of chronic pancreatitis [22], pancreatic cancer [2] and type 2 diabetes mellitus [5, 23, 24]. Despite the possible role that PFF may play in the development of adverse clinical outcomes, prospective studies to evaluate risks of adverse cardiovascular and kidney outcomes have been limited by relatively small sample sizes and follow-up time. For instance, two prior studies, which had sample sizes of 1478 and 631 participants, found that higher PFF was associated with future development of type 2 diabetes [23, 24]. By leveraging the large sample size of the UK Biobank that incorporated a neck-to-knee MRI protocol that captured abdominal MRI-derived fat quantification with a follow-up time that enabled accrual of a substantial number of events, we were able to test the associations of pPFF with adverse clinical outcomes. Our study also adds information about change in PFF over time in a large population of middle to older aged adults, which has been limited in the prior published literature [2, 16, 25]. In participants that returned for an abdominal MRI scan, PFF increased ∼0.36% per year. Since this change was relatively small, we assumed a linear change over time to back-calculate baseline pPFF levels. Our prospective findings add to the prior literature that higher levels of baseline pPFF are associated with higher risks of a future CVD event or development of CKD. Importantly, our findings are independent of a number of relevant clinical risk factors that include anthropometric measures of body size and fat distribution, which suggests that ectopic fat depots may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of chronic diseases [4]. While prior studies suggested that inflammation and insulin resistance may contribute to pancreatic fat deposition and both are related to subsequent adverse outcomes [14, 15], our results were similar even after adjustment for surrogate measures of systemic inflammation and insulin resistance in our sensitivity analysis. Given the recent strong randomized clinical trial evidence that support the use of glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) to reduce the risks of adverse cardiovascular and kidney outcomes [26, 27], future studies should evaluate whether GLP-1RA reduce ectopic fat depots in the pancreas to mediate some of the cardio- and kidney-protective benefits.

Strengths of this study include the use of a large and well-characterized cohort of individuals at risk of adverse clinical outcomes with quantification of PFF by abdominal MRI and detailed covariate data that allowed for comprehensive multivariable adjustment. This study has limitations. While the UK Biobank is a large population-based cohort with robust imaging measures of PFF, >95% of the study participants are Caucasian and our findings require replication in cohorts with greater racial and ethnic diversity. Although our follow-up time was adequate to generate many events, ascertainment of incident CVD and CKD relied on administrative codes. While histopathologic evidence of PFF may be the gold standard assessment, this approach is less feasible in a large cohort study and prior work suggested a strong correlation between abdominal MRI-derived assessment of PFF and histopathologic correlates of PFF [28]. We estimated PFF at baseline by using PFF measured at two time points assuming a linear change in PFF over time, but our analyses remained robust in our sensitivity analyses that used the raw PFF value. Although we used multiple imputation to reduce missingness among covariate data, our results remained similar in a complete case sensitivity analysis. Despite our comprehensive multivariable adjustment, there may be residual confounding from unmeasured confounders due to the observational design.

In conclusion, we found that higher levels of pPFF are associated with higher risks of experiencing a CVD event and development of CKD independent of the number of clinical risk factors. These findings underscore the prognostic value of ectopic fat accumulation in the pancreas, and future studies should determine whether current or new therapies are able to reduce fat deposition in the pancreas to improve adverse cardiovascular and kidney outcomes.
