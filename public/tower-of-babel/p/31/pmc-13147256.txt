Physical activity in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: from proscription to prescription
Authors: Pier Luigi Temporelli, Marco Zuin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent preclinical and clinical data suggest that moderate physical activity may be safe and beneficial in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Evidence regarding a safe dose of high-intensity exercise remains limited; however, the marked heterogeneity in the morphology and pathophysiology of HCM implies that some individuals are capable of performing even vigorous exercise, including competitive high-intensity sports. Current data indicate that participation in intense exercise and competitive athletics may be considered in a carefully selected subset of predominantly adult patients with a low-risk profile. Studies evaluating the impact of vigorous exercise or moderate-to-high intensity competitive sports on the natural history of HCM remain few and are underpowered to definitively resolve this issue. Nonetheless, emerging evidence now supports a more liberal approach whereby—after appropriate selection, expert clinical evaluation, and shared decision-making—individuals with a low-risk profile may engage in high-intensity physical activity and competitive sports, leading active and fulfilling lives while minimizing risk.Keywords: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Physical activity, Sudden cardiac death

In 1958, a London pathologist described the hearts of eight patients with probable hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, seven of whom had died suddenly.1 A few years later, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction was reported,2 and this large subgroup of patients with HCM is now defined as having obstructive HCM. Obstruction is typically caused by a combination of basal interventricular septal hypertrophy and systolic anterior motion of the anterior mitral leaflet. Some patients may exhibit left ventricular outflow tract obstruction without septal hypertrophy but with abnormalities of the mitral valve or papillary muscles.3

Estimating the true prevalence of HCM remains challenging due to the intrinsic heterogeneity of the disease. Echocardiographic prevalence studies have estimated a global prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy (defined as wall thickness >15 mm) of approximately 1 in 500 individuals (0.2%). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, with its greater sensitivity for detecting left ventricular hypertrophy, has reported a prevalence as high as 1 in 74 individuals (1.35%) in cohorts aged 45–84 years. Women remain underrepresented in population studies, accounting for only 30–45% of HCM cases—a phenomenon that has remained unchanged for three decades.4

Today, HCM is recognized as the most common inherited cardiac disease encountered in clinical practice,5 and until recently, it was considered one of the leading causes of exercise-related sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young individuals.6 Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is characterized by primary myocardial hypertrophy and an increased vulnerability to ventricular arrhythmias, including rare but potentially lethal events occurring at rest or during exertion.

Before the advent of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), the annual incidence of SCD—primarily due to ventricular fibrillation—was approximately 1% in patients with HCM.3 Prognosis has markedly improved in recent years due to evidence-based therapies, including β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, cardiac myosin inhibitors, septal reduction therapy, heart transplantation, and especially ICD implantation. The latter has driven considerable efforts to identify patients at the highest risk of SCD.7,8

Patients at particularly high risk—those with prior ventricular fibrillation, cardiac arrest, or sustained ventricular tachycardia—are candidates for ICD implantation. Major primary prevention risk factors include a family history of SCD, maximal left ventricular wall thickness ≥30 mm, an apical left ventricular aneurysm, unexplained syncope, multiple prolonged episodes of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, and a left ventricular ejection fraction <50%. Use of ICDs in high-risk patients with HCM has reduced the incidence of sudden cardiac death to <0.5% per year.

The rationale behind the proscription of physical exercise

In HCM, left ventricular outflow obstruction is dynamic and varies inversely with left ventricular volume, increasing with exertion, physical activity, and the administration of inotropic agents. Approximately 70% of patients with HCM exhibit subaortic muscular obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract, which can be provoked or exacerbated by exercise or by other stimuli that enhance myocardial contractility. Consequently, until recently, exercise recommendations adopted a conservative stance, limiting affected individuals to low-intensity activity regimens that effectively promoted a predominantly sedentary lifestyle.9,10

In the first autopsy study from 1980 dedicated to sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young athletes, HCM was identified as the leading pathological cause.11 This study—and subsequent confirmatory investigations—cemented the association between HCM and an increased risk of exercise-related SCD in competitive sports. As a result, for many years, exercise recommendations were highly restrictive: patients with HCM were advised to limit themselves to low-intensity physical activity and were discouraged from engaging in sports, while competitive athletics were categorically prohibited for all individuals diagnosed with HCM.

The recommendation to avoid sports originated largely from autopsy studies of young athletes who died suddenly and were found to have a high prevalence of HCM at post-mortem examination.12 Later studies, however, challenged this notion, emphasizing that SCD in athletes may arise from a wide spectrum of cardiac disorders and that HCM may not be the most common cause.13

Despite growing evidence suggesting that systematically denying patients an active lifestyle is misguided, the medical community has been slow to change its stance—partly due to inertia, but also because of methodological limitations in studies assessing structured exercise training programmes.

Today, unfortunately, 55% of patients with HCM fail to meet even the minimum recommended levels of physical activity. A sedentary lifestyle promotes obesity, increases cardiovascular risk, and has a substantial negative psychological impact, particularly in younger individuals.

The paradigm shift

Most patients with HCM have a favourable clinical course, with mild or absent symptoms and severe manifestations occurring only later in life. Arrhythmic events are uncommon and are generally not triggered by physical exercise. Young patients, in particular, often display excellent athletic performance prior to diagnosis and perceive being ‘disqualified’ from physical activity as deeply traumatic when no alternative options are provided. Today, a growing body of evidence supports the benefits and safety of various forms of exercise in HCM.

Following the widespread adoption of echocardiography in the 1990s, studies in unselected HCM populations began to reveal a milder disease presentation, demonstrating that SCD in the absence of major risk factors was rare, occurring at a rate of approximately 0.6% per year. Importantly, advances in understanding the clinical course of HCM have led to the development of robust, evidence-based SCD risk stratification models.7,8 In addition, recognition of the pivotal role of physical activity in psychological and physiological well-being has prompted the design and implementation of clinical studies aimed at strengthening the available evidence base. These investigations have cast an increasingly favourable light on outcomes among patients with HCM who participate in tailored exercise programmes, including vigorous training.

In a recent prospective study involving 1660 individuals with HCM, those engaging in vigorous physical activity did not experience higher rates of death or potentially life-threatening arrhythmias compared with individuals performing moderate exercise or who were sedentary.14 Likewise, the small randomized trial RESET-HCM, conducted in the United States between 2010 and 2015, assigned 136 patients with HCM to 16 weeks of moderate-intensity exercise training or usual care. The investigators demonstrated a modest but clinically relevant improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness without any adverse events, including arrhythmias. Moderate exercise proved safe, producing a small increase in exercise capacity at 16 weeks (1.35 mL/kg/min vs. 0.08 mL/kg/min).14

The more recent study by Basu et al. represents an additional step forward. The authors randomized 80 patients with HCM to a 12-week supervised exercise programme or to standard care. The primary endpoint was safety—specifically, arrhythmia-related events—while exploratory outcomes included changes in cardiorespiratory fitness, cardiovascular risk profile, and patient-reported quality of life. Of the 80 participants, 67 (84%) completed the study, including 34 in the exercise group. Safety outcomes were comparable between groups, whereas patients in the exercise arm showed improvements in peak VO₂ (+4.1 mL/kg/min) and VO2 at the anaerobic threshold (+2.3 mL/kg/min) relative to baseline, alongside a more favourable cardiovascular risk profile and greater improvements in anxiety and depression scores. The study provided valuable insight—not only regarding safety—but also regarding the feasibility, adherence, and clinical value of individualized training in HCM.15

Overall, the heterogeneity of HCM morphology and pathophysiology allows some individuals to safely engage in vigorous physical activity, including high-intensity competitive sports. Recent guidelines have adopted a more liberal stance, supporting shared decision-making in the absence of clinical markers indicating high risk. This shift is critical, as the patient’s perception of risk can be influenced by numerous factors.7,8

Notably, the most recent American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Joint Committee on Clinical Practice Guidelines includes, for the first time in the history of HCM, a Class III recommendation stating that ‘for most patients with HCM, universal restriction from vigorous physical activity or competitive sports is not indicated.’8

It is time to prescribe ‘tailored’ exercise in individuals with HCM

By adapting training programmes to individual needs and ensuring continuous monitoring, clinicians can now enable patients with HCM to lead active and fulfilling lives while minimizing risk. Following a comprehensive clinical assessment—including a detailed history of prior athletic activity, a stress echocardiogram, and a full cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET)—a well-structured exercise programme can be recommended.16

Several essential requirements must, however, be met. Exercise intensity must be tailored to the individual’s functional capacity, symptoms, and risk profile. Intensity should be determined using tools such as the Borg scale, percentage of maximum heart rate, and peak VO₂—parameters obtained through CPET. The frequency, duration, and intensity of training sessions should be progressively increased based on age, individual response, changes in medical therapy, and clinical course.

Annual re-evaluation is strongly recommended—more frequently if clinically indicated—and should include assessment of symptoms, resting and/or stress echocardiography, an exercise test (preferably CPET), and arrhythmic monitoring during a training session. Ideally, the formulation of a personalized exercise prescription for individuals with HCM would involve a multidisciplinary team of experts, though such resources are unfortunately not universally available.16

Conclusions

Historically, individuals with HCM were systematically excluded from vigorous physical activity and competitive sports due to concerns about an increased risk of SCD. However, emerging evidence has challenged this paradigm. Observational and randomized studies demonstrate improvements in functional capacity, quality of life, and both cardiovascular and psychological well-being through personalized exercise programmes in patients with HCM, including competitive sports in appropriately selected low-risk individuals.
