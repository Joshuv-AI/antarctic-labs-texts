Cardiovascular Involvement in Erdheim–Chester Disease Presenting as Pericardial Tamponade: A Case Report and Multimodality Imaging Review
Authors: Francesco Di Spigno, Andrea Faggiano, Alfredo Ricchiuto, Andrea Tedeschi, Federico Breviario, Luigi Gerra, Piergiorgio Turchio, Daniela Aschieri, Marco Sebastiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Erdheim–Chester Disease (ECD) constitutes a rare and clinically heterogeneous non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis, characterized by the systemic infiltration of tissues by foamy, lipid-laden histiocytes. These cells typically exhibit an immunophenotypic profile positive for CD68 and negative for CD1a. The disease’s multifaceted presentation, which can span from isolated bone lesions to fulminant multi-organ failure, frequently results in considerable diagnostic delay. In this case-based review, we describe the case of a 58-year-old who presented with a primary complaint of exertional dyspnoea and fatigue. The initial diagnostic evaluation revealed a hemodynamically significant circumferential pericardial effusion and imaging findings suggestive of aortitis. Clinical presentation of ECD depends on the organs and tissues involved, and may range from bone pain to neurological symptoms, endocrine dysfunction, and cardiac involvement. Cardiovascular involvement occurs in at least 40% of ECD patients, although it is frequently underdiagnosed. Cardiac ECD is heterogeneous and may mimic many alternative aetiologies. The infiltration of the right atrioventricular sulcus, right atrial walls, or interatrial septum is one of the most typical cardiac manifestations of ECD. Recognition of pseudo-tumour intra-atrial mass, pericardial involvement, as well as the circumferential encasement of the entire aorta, the so-called coated aorta, are other frequent findings. Diagnosis often requires a multimodal approach, in particular when cardiac symptoms represent the onset of clinical manifestation of ECD. The combined use of computed tomography, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, dedicated cardiac and abdominal magnetic resonance imaging, and X-ray of long bones can collectively reveal a constellation of findings diagnostic of ECD.

1. Introduction

Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is an uncommon histiocytic neoplasm characterized by tissue infiltration by foamy, lipid-laden histiocytes and by recurrent molecular alterations involving the MAPK signalling pathway, including BRAF V600E and other kinase-pathway mutations [1,2,3,4]. The disorder usually affects adults in midlife [5] and may involve the skeleton, retroperitoneum, cardiovascular system, lungs, central nervous system, skin, and endocrine organs [6,7,8].

The cardiovascular phenotype of ECD is particularly relevant because it may remain clinically silent until advanced disease or may mimic more common conditions such as idiopathic or viral pericarditis, large-vessel vasculitis, IgG4-related disease, lymphoma, metastatic disease, and infectious aortitis [9,10,11,12,13,14]. Characteristic manifestations include pericardial disease, peri-aortic fibrosis with a “coated aorta” appearance, right atrial or atrioventricular groove infiltration, and pericoronary involvement [9,13,14,15,16].

In this case-based review, we revised the cardiovascular manifestations of ECD, focusing mainly on the diagnostic pattern recognition, the role of multimodality imaging, and the limitations that may arise when immunohistochemical and molecular characterizations are incomplete.

2. Case Presentation

A 58-year-old man with only remote mild tobacco exposure presented with constitutional symptoms that had evolved over approximately three months. He reported malaise, low-grade fever, night sweats, non-productive cough, and progressive fatigue. Empirical outpatient treatment with antibiotics followed by a short course of oral glucocorticoids produced only partial and transient improvement.

During the following weeks, he developed progressive exertional dyspnoea, worsening asthenia, unintentional weight loss, and bilateral lower-limb edema, prompting hospital admission. On presentation, he was clinically unstable, with sinus tachycardia, borderline hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure, pulsus paradoxus, and bilateral pitting edema. Laboratory tests showed neutrophilic leucocytosis, mild normocytic anemia, and markedly increased inflammatory markers. Renal function was substantially preserved, cardiac biomarkers were not significantly raised, and the initial autoimmune and infectious workup was unrevealing.

The 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with low-voltage QRS complexes and no relevant conduction or repolarization abnormalities. Continuous telemetry and 24 h Holter monitoring did not document clinically significant arrhythmias.

Bedside transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a large circumferential pericardial effusion with echocardiographic signs of impending tamponade, including right atrial systolic collapse and a dilated inferior vena cava with reduced respiratory variation (Figure 1). Left ventricular systolic function was preserved. Because of the hemodynamic picture, urgent ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis was performed; approximately 700 mL of serosanguineous fluid was drained, with rapid clinical and hemodynamic improvement.

Pericardial fluid analysis was consistent with an inflammatory exudate. Cytology showed reactive inflammatory cells, including histiocytes and lymphocytes, without malignant cells. Microbiological tests were negative. The absence of a clear infectious or neoplastic explanation, together with persistent systemic inflammation, prompted further imaging.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) demonstrated diffuse pericardial late gadolinium enhancement and T2-weighted hyperintensity, in keeping with active pericardial inflammation (Figure 2). No focal myocardial late gadolinium enhancement was identified. CMR also raised suspicion for right-sided periatrial/interatrial septal involvement (Figure 3).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis confirmed circumferential peri-aortic soft-tissue infiltration involving the thoracic aorta, compatible with the “coated aorta” pattern (Figure 4). The same examination showed bilateral perirenal soft-tissue encasement, corresponding to the “hairy kidney” sign, and an infiltrative solid lesion involving the lower pole of the right kidney.

Whole-body FDG-PET/CT demonstrated increased metabolic activity involving the thoracic and abdominal aorta, the right atrial septal/periatrial region, bilateral perirenal tissues, and the long bones of the lower limbs (Figure 5). The skeletal uptake was bilateral and symmetric, involving the metaphyseal and diaphyseal regions of the femur and tibiae. Conventional radiography of the long bones showed corresponding osteosclerotic changes (Figure 6).

Given the suspicion of a multisystem infiltrative disorder and the radiological concern raised by the right renal lesion, the patient underwent partial nephrectomy. Histopathological examination showed infiltration by foamy histiocyte-derived cells with Touton giant cells, fibrosis, and scattered inflammatory infiltrates.

Immunohistochemistry demonstrated positivity for CD68, CD14, and CD4. Weak focal CD1a immunoreactivity was also observed. Although the classic ECD immunophenotype is CD68-positive and CD1a-negative, weak or focal CD1a staining has occasionally been reported [17]. In this patient, the overall clinicopathological and radiological pattern was considered most consistent with ECD. The lesional cells were negative for PAX8, cytokeratin 7, broad-spectrum cytokeratins, carbonic anhydrase IX, HMB-45, Melan-A, tyrosinase, and other epithelial or melanocytic markers. Additional immunohistochemical markers, including CD163, langerin/CD207, factor XIIIa, S100, and IgG4, were not available.

Molecular testing did not detect the BRAF V600E mutation. Extended sequencing for other MAPK pathway alterations was not performed. The diagnosis of multisystem ECD with cardiovascular, retroperitoneal, renal, and skeletal involvement was therefore established by integrating the clinical course, characteristic imaging distribution, histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular data.

After multidisciplinary discussion, pegylated interferon-alpha was selected for systemic treatment because the patient had symptomatic multisystem disease, BRAF V600E was not detected, and broader molecular profiling and targeted therapies were not routinely available at our institution at the time of diagnosis. Short-term follow-up showed clinical improvement after pericardiocentesis and systemic treatment, with no early recurrence of clinically significant pericardial effusion. The above-described case report highlights the importance of multimodality imaging in the differential diagnosis of some rare diseases. In particular, cardiac involvement is a rare onset manifestation of ECD, and a correct diagnostic definition can be difficult, especially when individual histological or molecular findings are incomplete or atypical [18]. The main limitations of this report are the incomplete ancillary immunohistochemical panel, the absence of extended molecular profiling beyond BRAF V600E, and the short duration of follow-up. These points do not negate the diagnostic value of the case, but they limit conclusions about long-term response and about the optimal systemic therapy for this patient.

3. Systemic Clinical Manifestations of ECD

ECD is now regarded as a clonal histiocytic neoplasm within the spectrum of macrophage-dendritic cell lineage disorders [6,9,14]. Its presentation is broad, and isolated symptoms such as fever, bone pain, dyspnoea, edema, renal abnormalities, or constitutional complaints are rarely diagnostic by themselves [6,7,8]. The combination of circumferential peri-aortic infiltration, perirenal encasement, and symmetric long-bone involvement remains one of the most useful imaging constellations for recognizing the disease [9,11,18,19].

Clinical history and onset presentation of ECD are often unpredictable. In most cases, it behaves as a smouldering neoplasm, but it can also be fatal when untreated. Unlike in Langerhans cell histiocytosis, histiocytes accumulate in the affected organs in ECD and rarely undergo regression [6].

Osteosclerosis of the long bones occurs in about 80–95% of patients [7,8], with bone pain in approximately half of cases [14,18]. In these patients, FDG-PET shows an increased uptake in the involved areas in approximately 95% of cases. Moreover, this technique also has a role in follow-up to monitor the metabolic activity of histiocytosis and especially to evaluate therapeutic response [6,19].

Retroperitoneal involvement, sometimes with bilateral ureteral extension and consequent hydronephrosis, is observed in 30% of ECD patients [20]. When present, the periaortic infiltrate is uniformly distributed in the coronal plane, with concentric fibrosis, unlike other forms of retroperitoneal fibrosis [9].

Pulmonary involvement is observed in a percentage between 30% and 50% of patients [7,8,21] and may consist of pleural or parenchymal infiltration with a pattern suggestive of interstitial lung disease. Cysts are usually absent, suggesting other histiocytoses [22].

Endocrine abnormalities are common in ECD, although their prevalence and clinical features remain only partially characterized [23]. Patients may present with GH deficiency in nearly 80% of cases, and hyperprolactinemia in 44%. Diabetes insipidus is found in approximately 25% of ECD patients and is often the first manifestation of the disease [24].

Neurologic involvement may consist of degenerative processes or proliferation with mass effect. The involvement may remain completely asymptomatic, or it may be characterized by cerebellar and pyramidal syndromes [25]. At MRI, central nervous system involvement may be characterized by enhancing masses or areas of high signal intensity [25,26].

4. Cardiovascular Manifestations of ECD

Cardiovascular involvement occurs in at least 30% of ECD patients, although it is frequently underdiagnosed [13,14,27]. It is reported in a substantial proportion of patients with ECD and is clinically relevant because it may affect prognosis and treatment decisions [9,13,14,28]. In large imaging series, pericardial effusion, pericardial thickening or enhancement, right atrial or atrioventricular groove infiltration, peri-aortic fibrosis, and pericoronary involvement are among the most characteristic findings [13,14,15,16].

Cardiac involvement in ECD may mimic a range of alternative aetiologies; conversely, competing diagnostic hypotheses should be carefully considered before establishing a diagnosis of ECD. Although cardiac involvement can represent the first clinical manifestation of the disease, the prevalence of such a condition is unknown.

5. Pericardial Disease

Pericardium is the structure most commonly affected, up to 23–29% of patients [8,14], and may consist of pericarditis or pericardial effusion up to tamponade, which may be fatal [9].

Pericardial involvement is frequently observed in patients who underwent cardiac MRI; among these, 24% had pericardial effusion, 14% had pericardial late enhancement, and 33% had leaflet thickening [29]. ECD should be considered in patients with recurrent pericarditis or otherwise unexplained pericardial effusion, especially when accompanied by tamponade physiology, conduction disturbances, or clinically significant arrhythmia [30]. Cardiac ultrasound represents the first-line imaging method for diagnosis and monitoring of pericarditis, but may show non-specific findings shared by both more common and rarer conditions, including idiopathic/viral pericarditis, neoplastic infiltration, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, and IgG4-related disease [29].

6. Coated Aorta/Periaortic Disease

Circumferential encasement of the entire aorta (sheathing), known as coated aorta, is one of the most typical manifestations of ECD. Peri-aortic infiltration may be confined to the thoracic segment or extended to the abdominal segment, possibly involving the main branches as well [7,8,9,11]. This involvement is usually asymptomatic and not associated with complications such as dilation, stenosis, dissection, or aneurysms.

Differential diagnosis of periaortic thickening includes cancer (mainly lymphoma), inflammatory systemic diseases, such as large-vessel vasculitis and IgG4-related disease, and infectious diseases, including syphilis and mycobacterial infections [31].

Idiopathic periaortitis typically involves the abdominal aorta, with extension into the retroperitoneal tissue and encasement of adjacent structures such as ureters, and only rarely extends to the thoracic aorta [11].

In IgG4-related disease, fibroinflammatory aortitis may occur, characterized by dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration of the thoracic and/or abdominal aorta and associated with a non-negligible risk of aortic dissection. Fibrosclerotic involvement may also extend to the retroperitoneum and other organ systems [10].

7. Right Atrial/Atrioventricular Sulcus Pseudotumor

Right atrioventricular sulcus pseudo-tumoral masses are one of the most common cardiac manifestations in ECD [13,15,16,32]. A recent retrospective study in 205 ECD patients reported abnormal results in 101 (49%) of them, including 72 with an impaired right ventricular atrioventricular sulcus and 65 with an impaired right atrial enclosure [32]. Pseudo-tumour can also appear as an infiltration of the right atrioventricular sulcus and may surround or infiltrate the right coronary artery [16].

Cardiac MRI is the best method for detecting right atrial pseudotumor [7,8,9,33] while peri-aortic infiltrate is better identified by CT [13]. No imaging finding is pathognomonic for ECD, and the diagnostic workup should integrate additional imaging features and relevant clinical history. The principal mimickers include atrial myxoma, intracavitary thrombi, metastatic disease, lymphomatous involvement, and primary cardiac sarcomas [34,35,36].

Both ECD-related right atrial pseudo-tumour and cardiac myxoma may present on ultrasound and cardiac MRI as right atrial masses. However, myxoma is typically a well-circumscribed intracavitary lesion, usually pedunculated, with mobility throughout the cardiac cycle, while ECD pseudotumor usually appears as a non-mobile mural infiltrative process [37].

On cardiac MRI, myxoma is generally hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, with potentially heterogeneous signal intensity due to intratumoral hemorrhage or calcification [34,35,36]. Cardiac sarcoma may closely mimic the imaging of an ECD-related right atrial pseudotumor and therefore represents a key differential diagnosis. In particular, angiosarcoma, the most common primary cardiac sarcoma, shows a marked predilection for the right atrium and may present as an infiltrative mass involving the atrial wall. In chronic forms, intracavitary thrombosis, which may exhibit limited mobility within the cardiac chamber, should also be excluded.

8. Myocardial Involvement

ECD may present with focal myocardial infiltration; however, similar findings may also arise from other infiltrative pathological processes, including sarcoidosis. Moreover, in the setting of coronary branch encasement, ECD may induce ischemic manifestations with abnormalities of myocardial contraction and relaxation suggestive of coronary artery disease [14,15].

Direct infiltrative involvement of myocardial tissue in ECD may lead to conduction disturbances and arrhythmias, similar to what is observed in ischemic injury secondary to atherosclerotic disease or other immune-mediated disorders with direct myocardial involvement, such as sarcoidosis and amyloidosis. Unfortunately, in ECD patients with myocardial infiltration, ECG abnormalities could be completely nonspecific and include short PR interval, sinus bradycardia, left ventricular hypertrophy, ST-T tract abnormalities, and even Q waves despite no anamnesis for acute coronary syndrome [13,30].

9. Pericoronary Involvement

Pericoronary involvement is another, but rare, clinical manifestation in ECD, found in approximately 23% of patients [8]. Pericoronary infiltration has been described as fibrosis or tissue inflammatory proliferation around the vessel. It may be confined to a section of one of the two coronary arteries [38], or it may affect the entire artery or both coronary arteries [9]. This clinical involvement could cause chest pain, angina, or myocardial infarction.

10. Role of Multimodality Imaging

Multimodality imaging played a pivotal role in the diagnostic workup of this patient by progressively revealing a characteristic multisystem distribution pattern suggestive of Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD). Importantly, the diagnosis did not rely on a single pathognomonic finding, but rather on the integration of echocardiography, CMR, CT, FDG-PET/CT, and skeletal radiography.

Transthoracic echocardiography was essential for the initial assessment and rapidly identified the clinically relevant finding of severe circumferential pericardial effusion with echocardiographic signs of impending cardiac tamponade, including right atrial systolic collapse and inferior vena cava dilatation with reduced respiratory variation (Figure 1). Although nonspecific, these findings established the urgent hemodynamic indication for pericardiocentesis.

CMR subsequently provided further tissue characterization by demonstrating diffuse pericardial late gadolinium enhancement and T2-weighted hyperintensity, supporting the presence of active pericardial inflammation rather than isolated fluid accumulation (Figure 2). In addition, CMR raised suspicion for right-sided periatrial/interatrial septal soft-tissue infiltration (Figure 3), a finding increasingly recognized in cardiovascular ECD [13,14,16,32]. Compared with echocardiography, CMR offered superior characterization of infiltrative cardiac involvement and improved assessment of pericardial and periatrial disease extension.

Contrast-enhanced CT allowed comprehensive anatomical evaluation of the systemic disease burden. In particular, the smooth circumferential peri-aortic soft-tissue infiltration involving the thoracic aorta produced the classic “coated aorta” appearance (Figure 4), while bilateral perirenal soft-tissue encasement corresponded to the typical “hairy kidney” sign. The same examination also identified the infiltrative right renal lesion that ultimately led to tissue diagnosis. The combination of peri-aortic and retroperitoneal infiltration represented an important radiological clue supporting a systemic infiltrative disorder.

Whole-body FDG-PET/CT further strengthened diagnostic suspicion by demonstrating metabolically active disease involving the thoracic and abdominal aorta, periatrial region, bilateral perirenal tissues, and long bones of the lower limbs (Figure 5). Particularly relevant was the bilateral and symmetric metaphyseal–diaphyseal uptake pattern involving the femora and tibiae, which is considered highly suggestive of ECD [18,19]. PET/CT also helped demonstrate the systemic coherence of apparently unrelated findings and supported the selection of the most informative tissue site for histopathological evaluation.

The imaging findings also contributed substantially to the differential diagnosis. Peri-aortic and retroperitoneal infiltration may mimic IgG4-related disease, retroperitoneal fibrosis, lymphoma, large-vessel vasculitis, or infectious aortitis [10,11,12] while periatrial soft-tissue involvement may resemble thrombus, myxoma, metastatic disease, or primary cardiac tumours [34,35,36]. However, the coexistence of inflammatory pericardial disease, coated aorta, hairy kidney sign, periatrial infiltration, and bilateral symmetric long-bone involvement created a highly characteristic imaging constellation strongly favouring ECD.

11. Histopathology, Molecular Testing, and Differential Diagnosis

Histopathological confirmation remains important because imaging findings, even when characteristic, are not by themselves definitive. The renal lesion specimen showed foamy histiocyte-derived cells with Touton giant cells and fibrosis, a pattern compatible with ECD when placed in the appropriate clinical and radiological context [2,6,39]. The immunophenotype was supportive, with CD68, CD14, and CD4 positivity. Weak focal CD1a staining represented an interpretive caveat; although CD1a negativity is typical of ECD, focal expression has been described and should be weighed against the entire phenotype rather than interpreted in isolation [17].

Several differential diagnoses deserve explicit consideration. Langerhans cell histiocytosis is usually supported by CD1a and langerin/CD207 expression and by a different clinical–radiological pattern. Rosai–Dorfman disease may show S100 positivity and emperipolesis. IgG4-related disease can produce peri-aortitis and retroperitoneal fibrosis, but would require compatible histology and IgG4 staining. Renal epithelial or melanocytic neoplasms were considered less likely because the lesional cells were negative for PAX8, cytokeratins, carbonic anhydrase IX, HMB-45, Melan-A, and tyrosinase.

BRAF V600E was not detected. This result does not exclude ECD, but it has practical consequences because treatment selection in contemporary practice increasingly depends on molecular characterization [1,3,4,39,40,41,42]. Extended profiling for other MAPK pathway alterations was not available in this case, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the therapeutic pathway.

12. Prognosis

Although ECD-related cardiac involvement seems to increase morbidity and worsen progression-free survival, it is not an independent predictor of worse prognosis in a multicentre registry involving 200 patients with a median follow-up of 5.5 years [14]. On the contrary, data on 104 patients from the Mayo cohort showed that cardiac involvement reduced 5-year progression-free survival from 70.5% to 28.3% [28].

13. Treatment Considerations and Limitations of This Report

Treatment of cardiovascular ECD generally follows the approach to systemic disease. The 2020 recommendations suggested treatment for most ECD patients, except for some selected cases of asymptomatic minimal burden disease, which should be closely monitored [39]. BRAF inhibitors are central in BRAF V600E-positive disease, particularly when cardiac, neurological, or other organ-threatening involvement is present [39]. The use of BRAFV600E inhibitors is suggested by the frequent mutation of the BRAF gene. In this regard, a biopsy of lesional tissue is strongly recommended to determine BRAF mutation status, and treatment with BRAF inhibitors should be considered in all patients with BRAF-V600E mutation [39,43]. However, subsequent real-world evidence suggested that, despite high response rates, a high prevalence of adverse effects leads to dose reduction and drug discontinuation [44].

MEK inhibition has become an important option for selected patients with BRAF-negative or non-BRAF MAPK-altered histiocytic neoplasms [39,40]. Interferon-alpha and pegylated interferon-alpha retain a role in selected contexts, especially when targeted treatment is unavailable or when clinical circumstances favour this approach [6,39].

Finally, systemic treatment with glucocorticoids should be used only as part of the treatment strategy [39], while interferon (IFN)-α, pegylated IFN-α, or cladribine may be considered in patients with high-burden disease when available targeted therapies are absent.

14. Conclusions

Cardiac involvement occurs in about 30% of ECD patients [28] and can represent a challenge for the diagnosis, particularly in the initial manifestation of the disease. ECD should be suspected in patients with unexplained inflammatory pericardial effusion, particularly when pericardial disease is accompanied by peri-aortic soft-tissue infiltration, retroperitoneal or perirenal involvement, renal lesions, or symmetric long-bone abnormalities [18]. In this patient, the diagnosis emerged from the convergence of echocardiography, CMR, CT, FDG-PET/CT, skeletal imaging, and tissue examination. A cautious integrated approach is essential, especially when individual histological or molecular findings are incomplete or atypical.
