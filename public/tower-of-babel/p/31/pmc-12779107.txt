Measurement invariance of the perceived discrimination scale across race/ethnicity and sex: Findings from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) study
Authors: Shu Xu, Keith F. Widaman, Cindy Patippe, Luchang Cui, Xinnuo Shi, Danielle M. Krobath, Adolfo Cuevas, Virginia W. Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Perceived racial discrimination during childhood and adolescence is a critical social determinant of health disparities. However, few scales measure perceived racial discrimination in these age groups, and even fewer are validated with robust psychometric properties or demonstrate measurement invariance across racial and ethnic or sex groups.Objective:This study examines the psychometric properties of the Perceived Discrimination (PD) scale, a seven-item questionnaire designed to assess societal rejection based on racial or ethnic background among youth.Methods:Using data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, we conducted exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses to examine youth-reported perceived discrimination across racial/ethnic groups (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and NH Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander) and sex groups (male and female). These analyses drew on two data sets: one based on youth’s racial/ethnicity reported by parents (Data Set 1, n = 9967) and the other based on self-reported race/ethnicity (Data Set 2, n = 7645).Results:Analyses identified a well-fitting bi-factor model, comprising a general perceived discrimination factor and a specific perceived social exclusion factor, demonstrating high reliability. The general perceived discrimination factor exhibited strong predictive validity, effectively forecasting changes in mental health outcomes over a one-year interval. Results from multi-group confirmatory factor analysis support the configural, weak, and strong measurement invariance of the PD scale across racial/ethnic and sex groups. Sensitivity analyses affirmed the robustness of these findings, showing consistency across reporting types (parent-vs. self-reporting for youth race/ethnicity) and coding strategies (three-vs. five-point rating scales).Conclusion:Overall, this study provides compelling evidence for the high reliability and predictive validity of the PD scale, underscoring its utility in assessing perceived racial discrimination among diverse youth populations in the US.

Perceived racial discrimination (commonly referred to as perceived discrimination) is one’s subjective experience of being treated unfairly or unjustly based on one’s race, ethnicity, or other characteristic (Williams, 1999). In children and adolescents, perceived discrimination is consistently associated with worse health outcomes, such as heightened depressive and anxiety symptoms (Weeks and Sullivan, 2019; Walker et al., 2022), increased stress, reduced self-esteem (Madubata et al., 2019), and poorer physical health (e.g., obesity; Cuevas et al., 2023). These associations have been observed in both cross-sectional (Priest et al., 2013) and longitudinal studies (Cave et al., 2020). Discrimination is also implicated as a social determinant of existing health disparities as racially minoritized groups report disproportionate exposure to discrimination compared to White individuals (Churchwell et al., 2020; Williams et al., 2018).

Numerous measures of perceived discrimination have been developed to capture the nature of this phenomenon. The majority of these tools, however, have been validated and applied primarily among adult populations (Atkins, 2014; Wizentier et al., 2023). Far fewer measures have focused on assessing perceived discrimination in children and adolescents (Braddock et al., 2021), despite growing recognition of the short- and long-term effects of discrimination in early life (Priest et al., 2013; Cave et al., 2020). Even among the limited tools available for younger populations, few have rigorously tested for measurement invariance across racial and ethnic groups. Specifically, measurement invariance implies that the underlying concept is assessed in a consistent way across groups. If the measurement invariance of a scale is supported, properties of the scale, including how they are interpreted and their influence on the scale items, remain stable across groups. For example, studies using the Everyday Discrimination Scale (Williams et al., 1997), a widely used scale on the perception of discrimination in general, have revealed a lack of measurement equivalence across racial and ethnic groups among adults (Bastos and Harnois, 2020; Harnois et al., 2019). This finding suggests that, because scores are not comparable across groups, observed group mean differences or associations between discrimination and health outcomes may be misleading or even artificial. Using measures that lack equivalence across groups may mischaracterize the association between discrimination and health and misconstrue existing disparities. Given these concerns, tests of measurement invariance are necessary for tools assessing perceived discrimination in children and adolescents, ensuring that comparisons are both meaningful and accurate.

An increasing number of studies administered the Perceived Discrimination scale (Phinney et al., 1998) for use with children and adolescents. This trend is partially driven by the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, the largest ongoing long-term study of brain development and child health in the US (Volkow et al., 2018). The PD scale is a seven-item questionnaire, originally developed based on a sample of first- and second-generation immigrant adolescents aged 14–19 years, from Armenia, Mexico, and Vietnam. Participants rated the frequency of item occurrences on a five-point rating scale (from 1, almost never, to 5, very often.) The PD scale has been used to assess children’s and adolescents’ perceptions of societal rejection due to their racial and ethnic background, along with its psychological and health impacts. Using the PD scale to measure perceived discrimination among racially diverse US children and adolescents may encounter challenges. First, studies are prone to bias due to inconsistencies in racial/ethnic background and age group between the original immigrant sample used to develop the PD scale and those from a racially diverse US population. Second, despite accumulating studies (e.g., Nagata et al., 2021; Cuevas et al., 2023) using the PD scale, its psychometric properties (e.g., factorial structure) were not established in the original publication and have been poorly reported in subsequent research. Researchers often treat the PD items as continuous variables and compute the mean of all items to create a composite score for subsequent analyses (e.g., Nagata et al., 2021) and report good to excellent internal reliabilities (Cronbach’s Alpha ranged from .81 to .94; Phinney et al., 1998; Gonzales-Backen et al., 2018). This approach assumes that items reflect a single common factor without further investigating or verifying the dimensional structure. Under a similar assumption, a few others built a one-factor model of the PD scale (e.g., Oshri et al., 2024). Third, to compare perceived discrimination across groups, it is essential to validate measurement invariance to ensure that the scale functions equivalently and reliably across different populations. However, such validation has yet to be conducted for the PD scale. Fourth, one study indicated that the distributions of the PD items were highly skewed (Argabright et al., 2022), raising concerns about treating them as continuous with a normal distribution rather than as ordered categorical. The potential bias of using highly skewed ordered categorical variables as continuous is well-documented in the structural equation modeling literature (e.g., Rhemtulla et al., 2012).

To fill these gaps, we examined the factorial construct of the PD scale in children and adolescents and tested its measurement invariance across different race/ethnicity and sex groups, respectively. First, we conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to examine the factorial structure of the PD scale and established it through confirmation factor analyses (CFA). Based on the optimal model, we conducted a series of multi-group confirmatory factor analyses (MG-CFA) to evaluate measurement invariance across race/ethnicity and sex groups. Lastly, we tested the reliability and validity of the PD scale.

Methods

Transparency and openness

This study involved analysis of existing data and its design and analysis were not preregistered. Data in this study comes from the ABCD data Release 5.0 (DOI: 10.15154/8873-zj65.). We report all data exclusions and all measures in the study. All data, research materials, and analysis code for this study are available by emailing the corresponding author. Mplus version 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2021), SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, 2013), and the Omega (Watkins, 2013) software packages were used for data analyses.

Data source and study sample

We analyzed the data from the ABCD Study (Release 5.0; Volkow et al., 2018). As the largest long-term study of brain development and child health in the US, this study aims to assess the influence of individual, social, and familial contextual variables on health, well-being, and health-related behaviors. The ABCD Study assessments cover seven domains: substance use, mental health, physical health and bio-specimens, neurocognition, gender identity and sexual health, culture and environment, and brain imaging. This study ensures racial/ethnic and sex representation during recruitment and includes multiple informants, including youth, caregivers, and teachers. The ABCD Study recruited 11835 youth aged 9 to 10 at Baseline (2016–2018) from 21 sites across the US to follow participants for approximately ten years. The study design, participant recruitment, and sampling strategy of the ABCD Study are presented elsewhere (Karcher and Barch, 2021). All procedures involving human participants in this study were carried out in accordance with the ethical standards approved by the relevant institutional and/or national research committee (Clark et al., 2018).

We used the data of 11117 children’s reports on their perceived discrimination at Follow-Up 1 (2017–2019). We focused on the measurement invariance test on four types of mutually exclusive, single-race groups: Non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black, Hispanic, and NH Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander (API). These groups are referred to as White, Black, Hispanic, and API hereafter. We also conducted measurement invariance tests on sex groups (i.e., male and female).

In the ABCD Study, parents and children reported data on the child’s race and ethnicity separately. Parent-reported child race/ethnicity was collected at Baseline (2016–2018) in the demographic survey and child-reported self-identified race/ethnicity was collected at Follow-Up 2 (2018–2020) using the Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure (MEIM) survey (Phinney, 1992). Accordingly, we prepared two datasets based on the reporting type of race-ethnicity data (parent-vs. self-reporting for youth race/ethnicity) and conducted parallel analyses.

In Data Set 1 (parent-reported child race/ethnicity), 9968 children (89.7 %) reported at least one item on the PD scale. Among them, 5936 children were identified by their parents as White, 1572 as Black, 2201 as Hispanic, and 259 as API. We excluded 1149 children from Data Set 1: 1005 whose parents identified them as multiracial, 34 identified as Non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native, and 110 with missing parent-reported race/ethnicity data (i.e., survey left blank or marked with “Don’t know”).

In Data Set 2 (child’s self-reported race/ethnicity), 7845 children (70.6 %) reported at least one item on the PD scale. Among them, 4919 self-identified as White, 1401 as Black, 1224 as Hispanic, and 301 as API. We excluded 3272 children from Data Set 2: 685 who self-identified as multiracial, 92 as Non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native, and 2495 with missing self-reported race/ethnicity data (i.e., participants lost to follow up, survey left blank or marked with “Don’t know”).

Measures

Perceived discrimination

The PD scale contains four items asking children about how often they were treated unfairly or negatively because of their ethnic background by peers, teachers, and other adults; and three items about their feeling unaccepted in society because of their race/ethnicity (detailed in Appendix 1). Children responded on a five-point rating scale (1 = Almost never, 2 = Seldom, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Very often).

We used two coding strategies for the PD scale. White, Black, and Hispanic children endorsed all five response categories for the PD items. In contrast, few children in the API group endorsed Categories 3 or 4, and none endorsed Category 5 (Very often), limiting their inclusion in measurement invariance tests due to a lack of responses in some categories (Rutkowski et al., 2019). Therefore, we conducted analyses with a five-point rating scale for the White, Hispanic, and Black groups, and a three-point rating scale (combining Categories 3, 4, and 5) for parallel analyses including the White, Hispanic, Black, and API groups.

Race/ethnicity

Race and ethnicity were assessed using both parent-reported (Data Set 1) and child self-reported (Data Set 2) items.

In Data Set 1, parents were asked at Baseline, “What race do you consider the child to be? Please check all that apply,” allowing for the selection of multiple racial categories, and separately asked whether the child was Hispanic/Latino (yes/no). In Data Set 2, children self-reported their race/ethnicity at Follow-Up 2 as part of the MEIM, a validated scale that assesses ethnic identity exploration and salience across diverse groups and has demonstrated strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = .81 to .90; Phinney, 1992). As part of the MEIM, children were asked, “In terms of ethnic group, I consider myself to be:” and instructed to select a single response from a predefined list of racial/ethnic categories. The response to this item served as the primary child-reported race/ethnicity measure. A full list of response options for both parent- and child-reported items is available in Appendix 2.

Consistent with the 2024 decision by the US Census Bureau mandating race and ethnicity data collection and reporting with a single combined question (Office of Management and Budget, 2024), we assessed race and ethnicity as a single construct with two categorical variables based on parent-reported measures and one variable based on child self-reported measure (in Appendix 2). This reflects the fact that in the US, ethnic and racial identity are both associated with racialized experiences that are deeply interconnected due to colorism, racism, and conflated definitions. Therefore, children were characterized into 4 mutually exclusive racial/ethnic categories: White, Black, Hispanic, and API.

Sex.

This binary information (male and female) was self-reported by parents at the baseline of the ABCD Study.

Covariates.

The following baseline sample characteristics were reported by parents: child’s age (years), annual household income (less than $25,000, $25,000–49,999, $50,000 – $74,999, $75,000 – $99,999, $100,000 – $200,000, and $200,000 or greater), parent education (high school diploma or less, some college, a Bachelor’s or Associate’s degree, and a Graduate or higher degree), and nativity status (US-born and foreign-born).

In addition, we included mental health measures from the 2007 Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) to assess the predictive validity of the PD factors. The 2007 CBCL is a validated survey of emotional, behavioral, and social problems in youth, as reported by the primary caregivers (Ivanova & Achenbach, 2007). Subscales of CBCL have a good-to-excellent model fit in the measurement invariance test using the ABCD sample (Stewart et al., 2024). This study used the ABCD study-derived T-scores of five CBCL subscales, including Anxiety Disorder, Depression, Stress, Somatic Complaints, and Internalizing Problems, which are consistent with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. Details about these mental health measures are available elsewhere (Barch et al., 2018).

Data analysis

We examined the distributions of PD items to decide on analytical strategies. Histograms for each item revealed severely positively-skewed distributions with 77.1 %–95.7 % of participants endorsing “Almost never (1).” Therefore, we conducted subsequent factor analyses using the weighted least square mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, which is appropriate for ordered categorical data with skewed distributions (Muthén and Kaplan, 1985; Li, 2016).

We conducted four sets of analyses (4 = 2 reporting types [parent-vs. self-reporting] for race/ethnicity × 2 coding strategies for the PD scale [3- vs. 5-point rating scale]). The primary analyses used PD items coded on a three-point rating scale, with children’s race/ethnicity reported by parents (Data Set 1), covering four race/ethnicity groups. We then conducted three additional sets of sensitivity analyses to evaluate how the findings vary with reporting types and coding strategies. To provide an overview, we conducted descriptive statistics for the whole sample and by group (Step 1). Next, for each analysis set, we explored the factorial structure and conducted factorial invariance tests across race/ethnicity groups and sex groups, respectively (Steps 2–4). Lastly, the reliability and validity of the resulting PD factors were evaluated in Step 5.

In Step 1, we conducted descriptive statistics to summarize the characteristics of the study samples, both for the entire sample and by groups (i.e., by race/ethnicity and sex, respectively). We also reported the polychoric correlation matrix for the PD items which presents correlations between the latent continuous variables underlying the categorical items.

In Step 2, we conducted EFAs to explore the underlying structure of the PD scale, in each group, respectively. Given the PD scale includes seven items, we tested one- and two-factor models with the varimax rotation. The optimal factor structure from the EFAs was selected based on the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA =< .05 indicating a good model fit; Steiger and Lind, 1980) and standardized Root Mean Square Residual (RMSR =< .08 indicating a good model fit; Asparouhov and Muthén, 2018) as suggested in Preacher et al. (2013).

In Step 3, we conducted CFAs to verify the model structure and model fit (i.e., RMSEA =< .05, Tucker-Lewis Index [TLI] > .95; Tucker and Lewis, 1973; and Comparative Fit Index [CFI] >.95 indicating a good model fit; Bentler and Bonett, 1980) for each group. We adjusted the scale construct to achieve a balance between good model fit and high model interpretability. Using the optimal model from Step 3, we subsequently conducted a series of nested multi-group confirmatory factorial analyses (MG-CFA).

In Step 4, using the data containing all groups, we progressively added constraints, moving from the least restrictive to the most restrictive models, to assess the equivalence of the dimensional structures across groups (Millsap and Yun-Tein, 2004; Widaman and Olivera-Aguilar, 2023) following modeling described by Widaman and Reise (1997). We used theta parameterization, which allows testing for group differences in factor parameters, such as factor means and variances.

Specifically, we first tested for configural invariance (Model 1) where the same dimensional structure is applied to each group, though formal parameters including factor loadings (the strength of the relationship of the test with the underlying factor) and item thresholds are all free to be estimated in each group. To ensure model identification, factor means were fixed at 0, factor variances at 1, and item residuals at 1 in the first group. For each factor, we also set the loading and the corresponding thresholds of the first item to be equal across groups. In Model 2 (weak invariance), we additionally constrained factor loadings to be equal across groups. In Model 3 (strong invariance), we further constrained the corresponding thresholds to be equal across groups. In Model 4 (strong invariance with equal factor covariance matrix), we tested for an equal variance matrix of factors. In Model 5 (strong invariance with equal factor covariance matrix and factor means), we tested for equal factor means.

To address the robustness of our findings given the unbalanced race/ethnicity group sizes, we conducted Models 1 through 5 using equal sample sizes across 300 iterations, following the approach of Yoon and Lai (2018). In each iteration, random subsamples were drawn from the larger groups to achieve 1:1 matching with the sample size of the smallest group. We reported the average results across all iterations. We did not apply this approach to the sex groups, as their sample sizes were approximately equal.

We used a series of tests with conventional criteria to justify a model with a good fit including RMSEA, TLI, and CFI. Additionally, we selected the optimal model from nested models by computing RMSEA associated with the difference test (RMSEAD; Browne and Du Toit, 1992; Dudgeon, 2004). RMSEAD is obtained by substituting the chi-square difference test statistic and its degrees of freedom into the RMSEA equation. RMSEAD is recommended for testing nested models because it has the conventional interpretation for RMSEA as the amount of additional misfit introduced by additional constraints and on the same metrics of RMSEA. However, unlike the traditional RMSEA, it does not depend on the degrees of freedom of the models under comparison, hence it is more sensitive to model mis-specifications (e.g., constraints, Savalei et al., 2023). We computed RMSEAD and its 90 % confidence interval (CI) for nested models. If the higher limit of its CI is below .05, we reject the hypothesis that RMSEAD > .05, indicating that the more restrictive model is the model with better fit. We also reported the results of likelihood ratio tests for comparison; however, in the context of large sample sizes (e.g., sample size of a group >300), these tests become sensitive to even trivial differences between models.

In Step 5, we tested psychometric properties for the resulting PD construct. We calculated a range of reliability coefficients for each factor, including the reliability coefficient ω, the factor determinacy index (FDI), and the percentage of uncontaminated correlations (PUC). The ω coefficient reflects the variance accounted for by all constructs underlying a scale score, with an ω > .70 indicating high reliability. The FDI index indicates how well factor scores estimate the underlying factor. FDI values ≥ .90 are desired (Rodriguez et al., 2016a; 2016b). The PUC index indicates the bias that could result from forcing multidimensional data (e.g., a bi-factor factorial structure) into a unidimensional model (e. g., a one-factor model). If PUC >.80 then the scale can be interpreted as primarily unidimensional (Reise et al., 2013). We also examined the predictive validity by regressing each CBCL subscale score at Follow-Up 2 (i.e., Anxious, Stress, Depressed, Somatic Complaints, Internalizing Syndrome) on the PD factors, adjusting for the prior CBCL subscale score, and covariates.

Missing data.

The proportion of missing values on the parent-reported race/ethnicity of their child was ignorable (.9 %), but many more children did not self-report race/ethnicity (22.4 %). Therefore, we compared our study participants with those who did not report their race/ethnicity terms on demographics, perceived discrimination, and mental health. Given the large sample size, we reported the Spearman’s rho for categorical variables and standardized mean differences for continuous variables.

To achieve study aims, we analyzed data from Data Set 1 and Data Set 2 with non-missing data on race/ethnicity. Given the small proportions of missing values on PD items (Data Set 1: 2.8 %–5.9 %; Data Set 2: 2.4 %–5.3 %), we considered the missing data to be inconsequential, suggesting minimal impact on study conclusions (Schafer, 1999).

Software.

EFA, CFA, MG-CFA, and predictive validity analyses were conducted with Mplus version 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2021). RMSEAD was calculated with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, 2013). Reliabilities were computed using Omega (Watkins, 2013), a stand-alone package.

Results

Primary analyses

We conducted the primary analyses using the seven PD items coded on a three-point rating scale. The children’s race/ethnicity was reported by their parents.

Descriptive statistics

In Data Set 1, the sample consisted of 52.3 % boys, and the majority (97.1 %) were born in the US. Nearly 85 % of the parents had attained some college or higher degrees and the annual family income of 72.1 % of the families was above $50000. Descriptive statistics by group are presented in Table 1.

The polychronic correlation matrices (Table 2) for the White group showed medium to high correlations between items, ranging from .51 to .85. Specifically, the correlations between PD1 through PD4 ranged from .58 to .75; and the correlations between PD5 through PD7 were higher, ranging from .82 to .85. Similar correlation patterns were observed for the remaining race/ethnicity groups. Similarly, among boys (Table S2d), the polychronic correlations between PD1 through PD4 ranged from .54 to .71; and the correlations between PD5 through PD7 ranged from .73 to .80. These correlations were slightly higher among girls.

EFA and CFA

EFA results are presented in Table S3a (by race/ethnicity) and S3b (by sex), and CFA results presented in Table S4a (by race/ethnicity) and S4b (by sex), respectively.

Across race and ethnicity groups.

RMSRs consistently indicated that a one-factor model did not provide a close fit to the data (RMSRs ranging between .086 and .104), whereas a two-factor factor model did (RMSRs ranging between .023 and .060). Additionally, RMSEAs for a two-factor model were also below .05. We proceeded with a two-factor model, with items PD1 through PD4 loading on the first factor and items PD5 through PD7 on the second. All factor loadings were greater than .40.

We conducted CFA to validate the two-factor solution for each group and achieved similar findings across groups (Table S4a and S4b). Despite satisfactory RMSEA (all ≤ .05) and CFI and TLI values (all ≥ .98) for two-factor CFA models, the results indicated a high correlation between the two factors (r > .80), suggesting a large factor underlying all PD items. To address this, we proceeded with a bi-factor CFA model (Jöreskog, 1971).

In a bi-factor CFA model (Fig. S1), all seven items (items PD1 – PD7) loaded onto a general factor, Perceived Discrimination (F1), indicating experiences and perceptions of interpersonal discrimination and exclusion across various social contexts, while only the last three items (items PD5 – PD7) loaded onto a narrower factor, Perceived Social Exclusion (F2), which captures perceptions of societal exclusion and marginalization of one’s ethnic group within broader US society. Given the general factor explains shared variance across all items, and the second factor captures only the residual covariance among the last three items, F1 and F2 factors are independent. Compared to the two-factor CFA results, a bi-factor CFA model achieved a lower chi-square value, similar model fit statistics (RMSEAs <.05, CFIs >.98, and TLIs >.97), and more interpretable findings across groups. Based on these results (Table S4a), we aggregated the data for each group and proceeded to measurement invariance test using bi-factor models.

Across sex groups.

Similarly, model fit statistics from the EFA consistently favored a two-factor solution over a one-factor solution. Although the two-factor CFA models demonstrated satisfactory fit - RMSEA (all ≤ .05) and CFI and TLI values (all ≥ .98) – the factors were moderately to strongly correlated (male: r = .62; female: r = .57), suggesting the presence of a general factor underlying all PD items. Consequently, a bi-factor model was deemed more appropriate.

MG-CFA test for measurement invariance

Results for primary MG-CFAs are presented in Table 3.

Invariance testing across race/ethnicity groups.

We evaluated measurement invariance with a sequence of multiple-group confirmatory factor analysis (MG-CFA), with each model being a bi-factor CFA (Table 3). Starting from the least restrictive model and progressively applying more constraints, we assessed whether parameters met cross-race/ethnicity invariance constraints. In Model 1, configural invariance was established. Fit statistics for the configural model fell within specified ranges (χ2 = 203.04, df = 50; RMSEA = .035, 95 % CI = .030–.040; CFI = .993, TLI = .988) indicating the same factorial pattern across groups was tenable. In Model 2, a metric invariance model was tested wherein factor loadings were constrained to equality across groups. The model had adequate fit based on the aforementioned fit criteria (i.e., RMSEA <.05, CFI >.95, TLI >.95) with a better fit relative to Model 1 (RMSEAD = .009, 90 % CI = .004–.013). Therefore, the metric invariance was supported. In Model 3, a threshold invariance model was tested wherein thresholds were constrained to equality across groups, and strong invariance was supported (RMSEAD = .027, 90 % CI = .024–.030). To further explore why responses vary across groups, we tested the invariance in factor parameters. Model 4 has a better fit relative to Model 3 and we concluded that invariance in the latent factor covariance matrix was supported (Model 4; RMSEAD = .011, 90 % CI = .002–.019). In contrast, Model 5 did not have better fit relative to Model 4 so invariance in factor means was not established (RMSEAD = .097, 90 % CI = .090–.104). After accounting for the unbalanced sample sizes among race and ethnicity groups, we obtained similar results based on 300 iterations following the Yoon and Lai (2018) approach.

The MG-CFA model suggested unequal factor means across race/ethnicity groups. We further conducted pairwise comparisons by calculating the 95 % CI with Bonferroni-adjusted alpha to control for type I error. As illustrated in Fig. S2a, for the Perceived Discrimination factor (F1), the Black group had the highest factor means compared to the other three groups. The Hispanic group also had higher factor means compared to the White group, while there was no significant difference between Hispanic and API groups or between the White and API groups. For the Perceived Social Exclusion factor (F2), while the Black and Hispanic groups had higher factor means compared to the White group, differences in factor means among other groups were not substantial.

Invariance testing across sex groups.

Similar results were obtained in the invariance testing across sex groups. The results indicated strong factorial invariance with equal factor variances but unequal factor means. As illustrated in Fig. S2b, males had a higher mean on F1, while females had a higher mean on F2.

Reliability and predictive validity

As shown in Table S6, the reliability coefficients (ω) for both F1 and F2 exceeded .90, indicating a high level of consistency. The high FDI for F1 (above .956) suggested the factor scores were reliable indicators of the underlying construct, whereas the FDI for F2 (.749) did not meet this threshold, raising concerns about the precision of the score for F2. The PUC value was below .80, suggesting that bias would result from forcing a bi-factor model into a one-factor model (had F2 been ignored). Additionally, F1 demonstrated good predictive validity, with higher F1 scores consistently associated with a greater increase in the Anxiety, Depression, Internalizing Symptoms, Somatic, and Stressed subscale scores between Follow-Ups 1 and 2 (see Table S7). These associations were not observed between F2 and the CBCL subscales.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted three additional sets of analyses to test whether the results for primary analyses were sensitive to reporting type for children’s race/ethnicity (using Data Set 2) and to coding strategy for the PD items (on a five-point rating scale for the White, Black, and Hispanic groups). While Data Set 1 contains ignorable missing data on parent-reported race/ethnicity for children, Data Set 2 has a substantially higher proportion of missing data (22.4 %), as a greater number of missing values on self-reported child race/ethnicity. A comparison between children without and with missing values on race/ethnicity revealed no differences in sex (rho = .02) or nationality (rho = .01). However, children with missing values on race/ethnicity tended to have parents with fewer years of education (rho = .07), lower household income (rho = .10), and slightly higher levels of perceived discrimination (rho ranging from .01 to .05). Differences in child age and mental health subscale scores were not substantial, with all absolute standardized mean differences less than 0.2.

The sample characteristics in Data Set 2 were similar to those in Data Set 1. Table S1 presents the descriptive statistics for the whole study sample as well as by children’s race/ethnicity group. Tables S2a, S2b, and S2c present the polychoric correlation matrix of PD items across race/ethnicity and Tables S2d, S2e, S2f, and S2g across sex groups.

We followed the same sequential procedures: first constructing a two-factor EFA model (results in Table S3a and S3b), then confirming a bi-factor model (results in Table S4a and S4b). The MG-CFA analyses demonstrated strong invariance with equal factor covariance matrix but unequal factor means (results in Table S5a and S5b) across both race/ethnicity and sex groups. Results using the Yoon and Lai (2018) approach for 3-point rating scale analyses arrived at the same conclusions. This approach was not applicable to 5-point rating scale analyses because the matched groups often contained no responses to high levels of discrimination (Categories “4” or “5”; Rutkowski et al., 2019).

As shown in Fig. S2a, for the analyses using the PD items, regarding Perceived Discrimination (F1), both the Black and Hispanic groups had higher means than the White group, the Black group also had a higher mean than the Hispanic Group. For Perceived Social Exclusion (F2), while the Black and Hispanic groups had higher factor means compared to the White, and the Black group had higher factor means compared to the Hispanic group, differences among other groups were not substantial. As shown in Fig. S2b, males consistently had a higher mean on F1, while females had a higher mean on F2.

Lastly, results (Table S6) confirmed high factor reliability (ω), PUC below .80, and high FDI for F1 but not for F2. Predictive validity (see Table S7) for F1 was indicated by higher F1 scores consistently associated with greater increase in the Depressed, Internalizing Symptoms, Somatic, and Stressed subscale scores between Follow-Ups 1 and 2. However, F1 was not associated with greater increase in Anxiety. No significant associations were observed between F2 and the CBCL subscales.

Discussion

This study established the factorial structure of the PD scale and tested its measurement invariance for perceived discrimination in a large and diverse sample of US youth. Using both EFA and CFA across four race/ethnicity groups (White, Black, Hispanic, and API) and two sex groups (male and female), we identified a well-fitting bi-factor model applicable to all groups. This multi-dimensional bi-factor model includes a Perceived Discrimination factor (F1) that represents the general perceived discrimination across all scale items. It also contains a Perceived Social Exclusion factor (F2), which captures perceived social exclusion that specifically influences PD items 5–7. Results of a sequence of MG-CFA tests of measurement invariance indicated that configural invariance, weak invariance, and strong invariance with equal factor loading were tenable. The high reliability coefficients for both factors suggest high consistency among the PD items for the underlying structure. Over one year, higher scores on the Perceived Discrimination factor (F1) were associated with significantly worse mental health (i.e., Depression, Stress, Somatic Complaints, and Internalizing Syndrome), suggesting good longitudinal predictive validity. However, this association was not observed for the Perceived Social Exclusion factor (F2). Results of sensitivity analyses indicated that these results were consistent across reporting types (i.e., parent-vs. child-reporting for child’s race/ethnicity groups) and coding strategies (three-vs. five-point rating scale).

Our findings are the first to support a bi-factor structural model for the PD scale using a large and diverse sample of US youth. Bi-factor models are increasingly used in social sciences (Li and Savalei, 2025) to capture constructs that include a strong unidimensional component (i. e., the general factor), while also accounting for residual correlations among clusters of items explained by additional, specific factors (i.e., the Perceived Social Exclusion factor). This modeling approach is particularly suitable to constructs like perceived racial discrimination, which are widely understood as multi-dimensional (e.g., a bi-factor structure for the Perceived Ethnic Discrimination Questionnaire-Community Version Brief scale, Keum et al., 2018) and is aligned with Jone’s conceptualization of racism as operating at multiple levels: institutionalized, personally mediated, and internalized (Jones, 2000). This framework offers a clear theoretical foundation for modeling perceived discrimination in ways that reflect its layered and context-dependent nature. In this framework, the general factor may correspond to personally mediated racism, which captures the everyday interpersonal experiences of disrespect, suspicion, and marginalization. The Perceived Social Exclusion factor may reflect experiences more aligned with institutionalized racism, such as exclusion from opportunities, resources, or spaces based on membership of a racial group. This factor has high reliability and weaker predictive validity in our sample, and its factor scores exhibited relatively lower reliability. We interpret this as a reflection of meaningful variation in how structural dimensions of discrimination are experienced and reported, rather than a lack of theoretical significance.

Establishing measurement invariance is essential for maintaining quality in health disparities research by ensuring that the measurement tools used to quantify the effect of discrimination are interpretable and function the same way across racial/ethnic and sex groups. Measurement invariance ensures any observed differences in discrimination scores reflect true variations in the experience of discrimination, not biases in how different groups perceive or respond to survey items or bias in the constructs underlying the survey items. Our results indicate that the construct underlying the PD scale is consistent across the White, Black, Hispanic, and API groups. Any differences in the observed PD values can be explained by differences in factor means, primarily the factor means of the perceived discrimination factor (F1). Perceived discrimination (F1) is the highest among the Black group, followed by the Hispanic group. Although the API group reported higher perceived discrimination than the White group, the difference was not statistically significant as indicated by small mean differences relative to the standard errors. Users of the PD scale, particularly those utilizing the ABCD Study data, can be reassured that associations between perceived discrimination and mental health outcomes are reliable with consistent interpretation across groups. In addition, our results indicate that, while females generally report lower overall levels of general discrimination, they tend to report experiencing more discrimination within broader social contexts.

This study highlights the importance of using proper estimation methods that align with the distributions of items on the PD scale. Rating-type scales are commonly used in social and health studies. It is common to use an item on a five- or greater-point rating scale as a continuous variable in empirical studies when its distribution is approximately normal. However, simulations show that maximum likelihood and chi-square tests tend to obtain values that are too large for variables that are severely skewed and the standard errors are biased downwards (Muthén and Kaplan, 1985). The weighted least square estimator (e.g., WLSMV), on the other hand, was specifically designed for ordered categorical data. WLSMV makes no distributional assumptions about the observed values, a normal distribution underlying each observed categorical variable is instead assumed (Li, 2016). Our results supported a bi-factor structure for the PD scale. A mis-specification of the structure as a single-factor model would be subject to biased estimates as suggested by its low PUC value. Researchers are therefore advised to use the categorical measures of the PD items with the WLSMV estimator when investigating or verifying the scale structure or estimating factor scores.

Our findings make a critical contribution to the perceived discrimination literature, as few measures have been designed specifically to assess this construct in children and adolescents. Many widely used measures—such as the Everyday Discrimination Scale—were originally developed for adults and may not align with the social contexts, or interpretive frameworks of younger populations. Items in these adult-oriented measures often reference experiences, such as unfair treatment in service encounters, that are less salient for children. In contrast, during childhood and adolescence, experiences and understandings of race and inequity are shaped by emerging cognitive capacities, teacher and peer dynamics, and the specific institutional settings they navigate (Hughes et al., 2023; Tatum, 2000). Our results indicate that the PD scale captures these experiences in a psychometrically sound way across groups in children and adolescents.

Constraints on Generality.

This study excluded the API group from measurement invariance analysis when PD items on the five-point rating scale were used. The API group, including Non-Hispanic Asians, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islanders, did not report high levels of discrimination on any of the PD items, making equivalence testing with the other three groups impossible. Similar patterns of underreporting have also been noted in other studies (Elenwo et al., 2022), and a few factors may explain the reasons. First, there might be a tendency among the API group members to label discriminatory experiences or perceive negative events as non-discriminatory (Garrett and Pichette, 2000). For example, Asians may be less likely to label certain negative experiences as discrimination as a way of coping. Second, participants may not always recognize subtle forms of discrimination, such as microaggressions, or they may attribute negative experiences to other factors (e.g., personal failure) rather than discrimination. Further qualitative and quantitative research is needed to refine and enhance the scale to ensure it is inclusive and robust for assessing perceived discrimination and its relationship to health (Bastos and Harnois, 2020).

Additionally, we did not consider multiracial children in this study. We reasoned that the multiracial children in the sample present a challenge for clear classification in terms of racial and ethnic identification, which complicates both the interpretation of findings and their generalizability to other studies. The multiracial category includes individuals with a diverse array of racial identity combinations and lived experiences. Treating them as a single analytic group risks biasing the applicability of the results to other multiracial populations.

Our study had several key strengths.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first analyses to examine the factorial structure of the PD scale in children and adolescents by treating its items as ordered categorical, consistent with how they are measured. We carefully selected model estimation methods that are appropriate to the data distribution and scale of measurement. Second, we used sample data from a national survey study with large numbers of White, Black, and Hispanic youth participants, and both male and female youth. Third, we conducted rigorous analyses to test the robustness of our findings across various reporting types for children’s race/ethnicity status (i.e., both parent-reported race of child and child self-reported race) and coding strategies.

The present study is not without limitations.

First, the values of PD scales were from self-report data which are susceptible to random errors and recall bias. Second, we focused only on participants who reported a single race/ethnicity, excluding those identifying as multiracial due to the heterogeneity within that group. Third, we acknowledge the diversity within Asian, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander sub-populations. These groups were combined as “API” in our analysis due to the small sample number of participants included. Future studies with larger samples of Asian, Native Hawaiian, or Pacific Islander participants are needed to test the robustness of our findings. This limitation also echoes the broader issue of “Asian and Asian American invisibility” due to the fact that nationally representative data sets often lack sufficient numbers of Asian Americans for meaningful analyses (Yip et al., 2021). Fourth, perceived discrimination may evolve as children grow older. Studies with longitudinal data are needed to examine measurement invariance of the PD scale factor structure over time and across various age groups. Fifth, while our findings support measurement invariance when grouping all White, Black, and Hispanic children together, our grouping approach may still obscure important within-group diversity that can shape how discrimination is perceived and reported. Future research is needed to assess whether measurement invariance holds when accounting for the heterogeneity of racialized experiences within each group. Sixth, due to the design of the ABCD study, the race and ethnicity measures differed between the child- and parent-reports in terms of timing, measurement, and format. These differences may have contributed to discrepancies or missing data across the two datasets. Lastly, about one-third of the youth had missing values on their race/ethnicity. Therefore, while sensitivity analyses indicate that results are robust across reporting types, findings should still be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study established strong measurement invariance of the PD scale among a national sample of White, Black, and Hispanic children, supporting its reliability and validity for use with pre-adolescent populations. Researchers are encouraged to use the WLSMV estimator when modeling the bi-factor structure of the PD scale. They can confidently compare scores across these groups and ensure the observed patterns reflect true differences rather than bias. Depending on the coding strategy, this conclusion may also extend to children of the API group. Similar findings were also established across sex groups. Further research is needed to replicate our findings. Our results pave the way for more rigorous and equitable research on health disparities in children and adolescents by demonstrating that racism influences health differently depending on how the individual is racialized in society.
