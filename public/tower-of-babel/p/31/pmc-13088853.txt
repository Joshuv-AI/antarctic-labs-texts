Vitamin D and melanoma: an umbrella meta-analysis of serum levels, dietary intake, and VDR polymorphisms
Authors: Bin Jiang, Yaling Li, Weilong Zhong, Yanfen Zou, Bancheng Chen, Bo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin D regulates immune function, cell proliferation, and differentiation, and may inhibit carcinogenesis. Both serum vitamin D levels and genetic variations in the vitamin D receptor (VDR) gene have been linked to melanoma, but findings are inconsistent. This umbrella meta-analysis critically appraised existing evidence on vitamin D intake, serum concentration, and six VDR polymorphisms (FokI, BsmI, TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, Cdx2) in relation to melanoma risk.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched to November 2025 for meta-analyses examining vitamin D intake, serum concentration, or VDR polymorphisms and melanoma risk in adults. Eligible studies reported relative risks (RR) or odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR 2. Pooled effect sizes were calculated using a random-effects model.ResultsNine eligible meta-analyses comprising 63 datasets were included. The FokI polymorphism was significantly associated with increased melanoma risk (RR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.10–1.18), whereas the BsmI polymorphism was associated with decreased risk (RR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81–0.93). No significant associations were observed for TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, or Cdx2 polymorphisms. No significant associations were observed for serum vitamin D concentration or dietary vitamin D intake in relation to melanoma risk. Study quality ranged from critically low to high, with only two meta-analyses meeting high-quality criteria.ConclusionThis umbrella meta-analysis suggests that the VDR FokI polymorphism is associated with an increased risk of melanoma, whereas the BsmI polymorphism may confer a protective association. No significant associations were observed for TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, or Cdx2 polymorphisms. However, the overall certainty of the evidence is limited by the generally low methodological quality of the included meta-analyses, necessitating cautious interpretation of these findings. Further well-designed, large-scale studies across diverse populations are required to validate these associations and clarify their clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07789-9.Keywords: Melanoma, Vitamin D receptor polymorphism, Umbrella meta-analysis, Genetic susceptibility, Vitamin D

Introduction

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble prohormone that is biologically inactive until metabolically activated [1]. Endogenously, it is synthesized in the skin when 7-dehydrocholesterol is photoconverted by ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation (wavelengths approximately 290–315 nm) to previtamin D₃, which is subsequently converted to vitamin D3 [2]. Additional sources include dietary intake from oily fish, cod-liver oil, fortified foods, and supplements [3]. After absorption or synthesis, vitamin D undergoes sequential hydroxylation, first in the liver to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D]—the principal circulating marker of vitamin D status—and then primarily in the kidney to produce 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)₂D], the biologically active form that exerts its effects through the vitamin D receptor [4].

Vitamin D exerts diverse biological effects through the vitamin D receptor (VDR), a ligand-dependent nuclear transcription factor expressed in numerous cell types [5, 6]. Upon binding the active metabolite 1,25(OH)₂D, the VDR regulates gene transcription involved in calcium homeostasis, immune modulation, and control of cell proliferation and differentiation [7]. More than 25 VDR gene polymorphisms have been identified, several of which are linked to altered receptor activity and cancer susceptibility [8, 9]. Individuals with comparable vitamin D intake or serum levels may therefore experience differing cancer risks depending on VDR functionality. Among the polymorphisms most frequently investigated in melanoma are FokI, BsmI, TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, and Cdx2 [10]. Experimental evidence suggests that VDR activation can inhibit tumor progression by reducing cell growth and adhesion, promoting apoptosis, and modulating immune responses [11–13].

Low serum 25(OH)D concentrations have been linked to adverse health outcomes, including an elevated risk of various cancers [14]. A meta-analysis demonstrated an inverse association between circulating 25(OH)D levels and colorectal cancer risk [15]. In the United States, skin cancer is the most prevalent malignancy, with over one million new cases annually, and its incidence—both melanoma and non-melanoma—has risen markedly over the past four decades [16, 17]. This increase is largely attributed to greater ultraviolet radiation exposure and inadequate sun-protection practices [18].

Previous umbrella reviews have examined vitamin D and skin cancer but were limited in scope. Seretis et al. [19] focused only on non-melanoma skin cancer, combining serum status, intake, and VDR polymorphisms in a largely narrative synthesis with high study overlap and limited quality appraisal. Bounas and Seretis [20] addressed melanoma alone, again merging heterogeneous exposures, with few interventional data and similar overlap issues. Melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers differ substantially in their biological behavior, patterns of ultraviolet exposure, vitamin D metabolism, and VDR-mediated signaling pathways. Pooling these entities may obscure true associations and limit translational relevance. Therefore, to enhance etiological specificity and methodological rigor, the present umbrella meta-analysis was restricted to meta-analyses examining melanoma exclusively.

In contrast, the present umbrella meta-analysis simultaneously evaluates melanoma, focuses primarily on specific VDR polymorphisms (FokI, BsmI, TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, Cdx2), treats serum vitamin D and dietary intake as secondary exposures, applies AMSTAR-2 quality assessment, and incorporates evidence up to November 2025, providing a more comprehensive and methodologically robust synthesis. Therefore, the relationship between vitamin D and melanoma remains unclear due to inconsistent findings and limitations in previous umbrella reviews.

Methods

This study was reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [21].

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus, which together provide broad and complementary coverage of biomedical, epidemiological, and genetic research up to November 2025. These databases are commonly used in umbrella reviews and are considered sufficient to capture the vast majority of relevant meta-analyses in this field.

The search strategy was built based on relevant keywords as follows: ((melanoma[MeSH Terms]) AND ((((((“Vitamin D“[Mesh] OR “Ergocalciferols“[Mesh] OR “Vitamin D Deficiency“[Mesh] OR “Cholecalciferol“[Mesh] OR “25-Hydroxyvitamin D 2“[Mesh] OR “Calcifediol“[Mesh] OR “vitamin D receptor“[Mesh] OR (Vitamin D [tiab] OR Ergocalciferols [tiab] OR Vitamin D Deficiency [tiab] OR Cholecalciferol [tiab] OR 25-Hydroxyvitamin D 2 [tiab] OR Calcifediol [tiab] OR 25-Hydroxyvitamin D [tiab]))))) AND (((systematic review) OR (meta-analysis)) OR (meta-analyses)). Search strategies incorporated a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to vitamin D, vitamin D receptor polymorphisms, and melanoma, and were tailored to the indexing structure of each database. Moreover, our search was restricted to articles published in the English language only. The application of the wild-card term “*” was employed to improve the sensitivity of the search strategy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study’s main objective was to examine the association between vitamin D intake, serum vitamin D concentration, VDR polymorphism and risk of melanoma cancer. Pertinent studies were selected using the PICO (population: adults / exposure: VDR polymorphism, serum vitamin D concentration and vitamin D intake /comparison: control group/outcome: melanoma risk) paradigm. Two authors separately chose meta-analysis studies based on specific criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: meta-analyses studies examining the association between intake vitamin D, serum vitamin D concentration, VDR polymorphism and risk of cancer in adults and reporting risk ratio (RR) or odds ratio (OR) along with their accompanying confidence intervals (CI). The current analysis eliminated findings from studies on children and pregnant and lactating women, original experimental studies, case reports, and in vitro, ex-vivo, and animal studies. Also, Meta-analyses that exclusively evaluated non-melanoma skin cancers or combined melanoma and non-melanoma outcomes without separable estimates were excluded.

Study screening, selection, and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the papers, adopting the predefined eligibility criteria. In the first step, the reviewers considered the titles and abstracts. A comprehensive review of the full texts of relevant papers was conducted to confirm the appropriateness for inclusion in the umbrella review. All conflicts have been settled through agreement with the involvement of a third reviewer. The data collected from the chosen publications included information such as the publication year, number of participants, outcome, polymorphism or cancer type, effect size (only melanoma-specific effect sizes were extracted), together with their corresponding 95% CIs. Mixed-outcome meta-analyses were excluded even if melanoma dominated.

Quality assessment

The independent evaluation of the methodological quality of the meta-analysis was carried out by two reviewers utilizing the AMSTAR 2 questionnaire, designed for assessing multiple systematic studies. The AMSTAR 2 questionnaire includes 16 questions that require respondents to respond by marking their replies as either ‘Yes’, ‘Partial Yes,’ ‘No,’ or ‘No Meta-analysis.’ following the findings of AMSTAR 2, the assessment framework recognizes four distinct levels of quality, namely “critically low quality,” “low quality,” “moderate quality,” and “high quality” (AMSTAR 2) [22].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The pooled effect size and its 95% CIs were calculated using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method [23]. Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across included meta-analyses—including differences in populations, study designs, exposure assessment, and genetic models—a random-effects model was considered appropriate. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using the REML method, which provides less biased variance estimates and superior statistical performance compared with traditional estimators, particularly in the presence of moderate-to-high heterogeneity and unequal study sizes. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed by the Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistics, in which I2 values greater than 50% or p < 0.1 were considered significant heterogeneity [24]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for all outcomes using a leave-one-out approach, whereby each meta-analysis was sequentially excluded to assess the robustness of pooled effect estimates. The consistency of the results across these analyses was used to evaluate the influence of individual meta-analyses on the overall findings. In addition to pooled effect estimates, 95% prediction intervals were calculated for random-effects models to reflect the expected range of effects in future studies. Also, Egger’s regression asymmetry test [25] was performed to examine the small-study effect. Visual inspection of funnel plots was also performed to investigate publication bias. If publication bias was observed, it was corrected via the trim and fill method. Statistical analyses were completed using STATA version 16 software (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA).

In accordance with established umbrella review methodology, we evaluated multiple dimensions of evidence robustness, including between-study heterogeneity, small-study effects, sensitivity analyses, and overlap of primary studies across meta-analyses. Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s regression test, and visual inspection of funnel plots for each outcome. Overlap was quantified using the corrected covered area (CCA) metric and considered during interpretation. Formal grading of evidence certainty using GRADE was not undertaken, as the included evidence consisted exclusively of observational meta-analyses; instead, overall credibility was interpreted based on consistency of findings, magnitude of associations, presence of bias, and methodological quality assessed by AMSTAR-2.

To assess redundancy across the included meta-analyses, we quantified the overlap of primary studies using the CCA index, as described by Pieper et al. and implemented in the GROOVE (Graphical Representation of Overlap for OVErviews) tool [26, 27].

Results

Literature review

First, we retrieved 139 articles by searching the database. Second, after deduplication, 54 records were remained relevant to the association between polymorphism of vitamin D receptor, serum vitamin D concentration, or vitamin D intake and melanoma risk. Then, after evaluating the titles and abstracts, 36 records were excluded. Next, 9 meta-analyses were also excluded after the full-text screening. Finally, 9 meta-analyses with 63 data sets were regarded as eligible for the meta-analysis. The selection process and reasons for exclusion are presented in a flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Association between FokI polymorphism and melanoma risk

The results of the analysis showed that the risk of melanoma was significantly higher in people with FokI polymorphism of the vitamin D receptor (RR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.18; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Also, the results of the analysis did not show significant results regarding heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%, P-heterogeneity = 0.59). No statistically significant difference was observed upon excluding a single study using sensitivity analysis. No evidence of small-study effects was detected using Egger’s (p = 0.92) test. Visual inspection of funnel plots indicated asymmetry for FokI polymorphism (Fig. 2B).

(A) Forest plot detailing risk ratio and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between FokI polymorphism and melanoma; (B) Funnel plot for the association between FokI polymorphism and melanoma

Association between BsmI polymorphism and melanoma risk

The findings from the examination of 14 datasets revealed a statistically significant decrease of 13% in the risk of melanoma among individuals with the BsmI polymorphism (RR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81 to 0.93; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the analysis findings demonstrated statistically significant results concerning heterogeneity (I2 = 58.7%, P-heterogeneity = 0.003). Following conducting sensitivity analysis, it was shown that removing a single study did not result in any statistically significant change. A statistically non-significant small-study effect was observed based on the results of Egger’s (p = 0.76) test. However, visual inspection of funnel plots indicated asymmetry for BsmI (Fig. 3B); therefore, the trim-and-fill method was applied to assess the potential impact of publication bias. The pooled effect estimate remained significantly (RR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.83 to 0.90), indicating that the main findings were robust to potential small-study effects.

(A) Forest plot detailing risk ratio and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between BsmI polymorphism and melanoma; (B) Funnel plot for the association between BsmI polymorphism and melanoma

Association between TaqI polymorphism and melanoma risk

The examination of 10 datasets indicated no statistically significant correlation between TaqI polymorphism and increased risk of melanoma (RR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.94 to 1.06; p = 0.89) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the investigation findings did not reveal any statistically significant outcomes about heterogeneity (I2 = 0.00%, P-heterogeneity = 0.92). The removal of a single study during sensitivity analysis failed to find any statistically significant differences. A statistically non-significant small-study effect was not observed based on the results of Egger’s test (p = 0.66). A visual examination of the funnel plot showed that the meta-analyses were evenly distributed (Fig. 4B).

(A) Forest plot detailing risk ratio and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between TaqI polymorphism and melanoma; (B) Funnel plot for the association between TaqI polymorphism and melanoma

Association between ApaI polymorphism and melanoma risk

The research results from the examination of three articles, each containing 9 datasets, indicate that there is no statistically significant correlation between ApaI polymorphism and risk of melanoma (RR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.09; p = 0.65) (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the analysis findings resulted in no statistically significant results to heterogeneity (I2 = 14.3%, P-heterogeneity = 0.31). Furthermore, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the overall findings remained unchanged after the exclusion of any individual study. A statistically non-significant small-study effect was observed based on the results of Egger’s test (p = 0.59). Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated a symmetrical and even distribution of the included meta-analyses (Fig. 5B).

Association between EcoRV polymorphism and melanoma risk

The analysis results of 7 datasets showed that EcoRV polymorphism has no significant association with the risk of melanoma (RR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.11; p = 0.20) (Fig. 6A). Also, the results of the analysis did not show significant results regarding heterogeneity (I2 = 0.00%, P-heterogeneity = 0.93). No statistically significant difference was observed upon excluding a single study using sensitivity analysis.

Association between Cdx2 polymorphism and melanoma risk

Our results from the analysis of six datasets indicate that there is not a statistically significant association between the occurrence of Cdx2 polymorphism and a risk of developing melanoma (RR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.89 to 1.04; p = 0.31) (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the analysis findings did not demonstrate statistically significant results concerning heterogeneity (I2 = 0.00%, P-heterogeneity = 0.99). Upon conducting sensitivity analysis, it was shown that removing a single study failed to produce any statistically significant change.

Association between serum vitamin D concentration and melanoma risk

According to the obtained results, there is not a statistically significant association between the serum levels of vitamin D and melanoma risk, (RR: 1.25; 95% CI: 0.71 to 2.21; p = 0.44) (Fig. 7A). Also, the results did not show significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0.00%, P-heterogeneity = 0.65). No statistically significant difference was observed upon excluding a single study using sensitivity analysis.

(A) Forest plot detailing risk ratio and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between serum vitamin D concentration and melanoma; (B) Forest plot detailing risk ratio and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between vitamin D intake and melanoma

Association between vitamin D intake and risk of melanoma

Study results from the investigation of three datasets indicate no statistically significant association between vitamin D intake and the risk of melanoma (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.81 to 1.25; p = 0.94) (Fig. 7B). In addition, the findings did not show statistically significant heterogeneity (I2 = 12.30%, P-heterogeneity = 0.32). By conducting sensitivity analysis, it was revealed that the removal of a single study did not yield any statistically significant change.

Overlap of primary studies across meta-analyses

The CCA was calculated separately for each outcome of interest, and the detailed graphical outputs are presented in supplementary file.

The degree of overlap among primary studies varied across outcomes, as assessed using the CCA calculated with the GROOVE tool. For the FokI polymorphism, the corrected covered area was 13.89%, indicating very high overlap among included meta-analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1). For the BsmI polymorphism, the corrected covered area was 9.72%, corresponding to moderate overlap (Supplementary Fig. 2). The overlap for TaqI was 2.08%, classified as slight overlap (Supplementary Fig. 3). Similarly, analyses of the ApaI and EcoRV and Cdx2 polymorphism showed a slight overlap CCA (Supplementary Fig. 4, 5, 6).

Regarding vitamin D exposure outcomes, analyses of serum vitamin D concentration demonstrated a corrected covered area of 37.50%, indicating very high overlap (Supplementary Figure S7). Also, vitamin D intake showed a corrected covered area of 27.27%, corresponding to very high overlap (Supplementary Figure S8).

Discussion

This umbrella meta-analysis synthesized evidence from published meta-analyses to examine associations between VDR gene polymorphisms, serum vitamin D concentration, dietary vitamin D intake, and the risk of melanoma. Restricting analyses to melanoma-only outcomes reduced biological and etiological heterogeneity associated with pooling melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers. The findings indicate that the VDR FokI polymorphism is associated with an increased risk of melanoma, whereas the BsmI polymorphism shows a modest protective association. In contrast, no significant associations were observed for TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, or Cdx2 polymorphisms, nor for serum vitamin D levels or dietary vitamin D intake. These results should be interpreted cautiously, as the evidence is derived predominantly from observational studies, limiting causal inference and leaving potential residual confounding. Moreover, most included meta-analyses were conducted in Asian and European populations, which may restrict generalizability.

An important limitation affecting the external validity of the present umbrella meta-analysis is that the majority of included evidence originates from Asian and European populations, with minimal representation of African, Hispanic, or other underrepresented ethnic groups. This limits the generalizability of the observed associations to populations with distinct genetic architectures, skin phototypes, and environmental exposures. Vitamin D synthesis is strongly influenced by skin pigmentation, latitude, ultraviolet radiation intensity, and lifestyle factors, all of which vary substantially across ethnic groups and geographic regions [40]. Individuals with darker skin pigmentation, who are overrepresented in African and some Hispanic populations, generally exhibit reduced cutaneous vitamin D synthesis under equivalent ultraviolet exposure, potentially modifying both baseline vitamin D status and downstream vitamin D receptor–mediated signaling pathways [41].

Moreover, allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium patterns of VDR polymorphisms differ markedly across populations, which may alter the functional consequences of variants such as FokI and BsmI [42]. These population-specific genetic differences, when combined with environmental factors such as sun exposure behavior, occupational ultraviolet exposure, dietary vitamin D intake, and socioeconomic determinants, may result in distinct gene–environment interactions influencing melanoma susceptibility [43]. Consequently, the magnitude and even direction of associations observed in predominantly Asian and European cohorts may not be directly transferable to African, Hispanic, or other ancestrally diverse populations. Future large-scale genetic epidemiology studies incorporating diverse populations and explicitly modeling gene–environment interactions are therefore essential to clarify the role of VDR polymorphisms and vitamin D exposure in melanoma risk across global populations. Although FokI and BsmI polymorphisms were associated with increased and reduced melanoma risk, respectively, these associations require confirmation in large, high-quality studies across more diverse populations. While Egger’s test did not indicate significant small-study effects for most outcomes, some funnel plot asymmetry was observed for BsmI; however, trim-and-fill analyses did not materially alter the pooled estimates, suggesting that publication bias is unlikely to have substantially influenced the conclusions, although selective reporting cannot be entirely excluded.

Because all included meta-analyses were based on observational study designs, the findings primarily reflect associations rather than causal effects.

An important consideration when interpreting these findings is the degree of overlap among primary studies and the methodological quality of the included meta-analyses. Several outcomes, particularly those related to serum vitamin D concentration and vitamin D intake, exhibited very high overlap across meta-analyses, as indicated by elevated CCA values. Such overlap may inflate the apparent precision of pooled estimates and increase the risk of redundant evidence. In parallel, the methodological quality of the included meta-analyses was variable, with most rated as critically low or low according to AMSTAR-2, reflecting limitations such as incomplete risk-of-bias assessment, lack of protocol registration, and insufficient consideration of bias when interpreting results. Although high-quality meta-analyses were identified for selected outcomes, these factors collectively underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the observed associations and highlight the importance of future well-designed, methodologically rigorous evidence syntheses.

Regarding overall certainty of evidence, the observed associations were interpreted within an umbrella-review framework that integrates methodological quality, consistency of effect estimates, heterogeneity, and risk of bias. Although statistically significant associations were observed for selected VDR polymorphisms, most outcomes were supported by meta-analyses of observational studies with variable methodological quality, and in some cases substantial overlap of primary studies. Consequently, the strength of evidence should be considered limited to moderate at best, and causal inferences cannot be established. This approach is consistent with published umbrella reviews that prioritize transparent appraisal of evidence robustness over formal grading systems when randomized evidence is unavailable.

Our findings both align with and extend those of previous umbrella reviews in this field. Seretis et al. [19] reported an increased risk of non-melanoma skin cancer associated with higher serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations, but observed no consistent associations for vitamin D intake or most VDR polymorphisms; however, their conclusions were limited by substantial overlap of primary studies and the pooling of biologically distinct skin cancer types. Similarly, Bounas and Seretis [20] reported conflicting evidence regarding the association between serum vitamin D status and melanoma risk, with suggestive prognostic implications but no definitive evidence for a causal relationship. Importantly, both reviews combined heterogeneous exposures and outcomes, which may have obscured melanoma-specific associations. In contrast, the present umbrella meta-analysis, restricted exclusively to melanoma outcomes, identified a robust association between the VDR FokI polymorphism and increased melanoma risk, alongside a protective association for the BsmI variant, while finding no significant associations for null polymorphisms. Notably, when analyses were limited to melanoma-only outcomes, no statistically significant association was observed between serum vitamin D concentration or dietary vitamin D intake and melanoma risk. By focusing on etiologically homogeneous outcomes and applying rigorous AMSTAR-2 quality assessment, our study provides a more methodologically robust synthesis that helps clarify inconsistencies reported in earlier umbrella reviews.

Compared with previous umbrella reviews in this field [19, 20], the present study provides broader quantitative coverage of both exposure types and genetic variants. Specifically, this umbrella meta-analysis synthesized evidence from nine meta-analyses encompassing more than 120,000 participants across multiple populations, whereas earlier umbrella reviews typically included fewer meta-analyses and focused on a narrower set of exposures. In addition, prior reviews often examined either serum vitamin D status or selected VDR polymorphisms in isolation, while the present study concurrently evaluated serum vitamin D concentration, dietary vitamin D intake, and six major VDR polymorphisms (FokI, BsmI, TaqI, ApaI, EcoRV, and Cdx2). This expanded scope allows for a more comprehensive assessment of vitamin D–related pathways in melanoma and facilitates more consistent interpretation across exposure domains.

Intense, short-term exposure to UV radiation is the primary risk factor for melanoma [44]. Conversely, majority of vitamin D is synthesized in the skin through sun’s action. Hence deficiency of vitamin D was proposed to be linked with different cancers [45]. A key methodological distinction of the present umbrella meta-analysis is the exclusive focus on melanoma, which addresses an important limitation of prior reviews that pooled melanoma with non-melanoma skin cancers. Given the profound differences in tumour biology, patterns of ultraviolet exposure, vitamin D synthesis, and VDR signalling pathways between these entities, pooled analyses may obscure true melanoma-specific associations. The attenuation of the previously significant association between serum vitamin D and skin cancer risk following restriction to melanoma-only outcomes highlights the impact of outcome misclassification and supports the need for etiologically homogeneous analyses.

Consistent with the serum analyses, dietary vitamin D intake was not significantly associated with the risk of melanoma. This finding aligns with melanoma-focused meta-analyses suggesting that dietary sources of vitamin D contribute minimally to systemic vitamin D status compared with endogenous synthesis via ultraviolet exposure [46]. Moreover, dietary intake may inadequately capture biologically relevant vitamin D exposure in melanoma pathogenesis. Future studies incorporating genotype-specific analyses and precise exposure assessment are needed to clarify whether subgroups may derive differential risk or benefit.

The observed associations between specific VDR polymorphisms and melanoma risk are biologically plausible given the functional consequences of these variants on vitamin D signalling. The FokI polymorphism is a start codon variant that results in a shorter VDR protein with higher transcriptional activity in the absence of the f allele; carriers of the f allele produce a longer, less active VDR isoform, which has been associated with reduced regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation [47]. Impaired VDR signalling may therefore weaken the anti-proliferative, pro-differentiation, and pro-apoptotic effects of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D in melanocytes, potentially facilitating melanoma development. In contrast, the BsmI polymorphism, located in the 3′ untranslated region of the VDR gene, is thought to influence mRNA stability and VDR expression rather than protein structure. The protective association observed for BsmI may reflect enhanced VDR expression or more efficient downstream signalling, thereby potentially preserving vitamin D–mediated immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory functions in the skin [48]. Given the established role of vitamin D in modulating immune responses, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways, variation in VDR activity represents a credible mechanistic link between host genetics and melanoma susceptibility.

Although the associations observed for the FokI and BsmI polymorphisms are biologically plausible, direct experimental evidence linking these variants specifically to melanoma pathogenesis remains limited. Functional studies have demonstrated that the FokI polymorphism alters the translational start site of the VDR gene, resulting in a longer VDR protein with reduced transcriptional activity in carriers of the f allele. Reduced VDR activity has been shown to impair vitamin D–mediated regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and immune surveillance—mechanisms that are central to melanocyte transformation and melanoma progression [49]. Experimental studies in melanoma and other cancer cell lines have shown that activation of VDR signaling suppresses tumor cell growth, promotes apoptosis, and modulates inflammatory and immune pathways relevant to melanoma biology [13].

In contrast, the BsmI polymorphism is located in the 3′ untranslated region of the VDR gene and is thought to influence mRNA stability and receptor expression rather than protein structure [50]. Functional data suggest that BsmI-linked haplotypes may affect VDR expression levels and downstream signaling efficiency, although direct melanoma-specific experimental validation is sparse [51]. Importantly, most available functional and mechanistic studies have not been designed to directly compare melanoma risk across specific VDR genotypes, and evidence is often extrapolated from other cancer models or non-melanoma skin contexts. Therefore, while existing experimental data support a biologically credible role for altered VDR signaling in melanoma susceptibility, direct genotype-specific functional studies in melanoma models remain an important gap in the literature and should be a priority for future research.

Several previous meta-analyses reported associations between the FokI polymorphism and overall skin cancer risk, including non-melanoma outcomes [52]; however, our melanoma-specific findings suggest that this association extends to melanoma, reinforcing a potential role for FokI in melanoma susceptibility. The study found that there was a 14% increased risk of malignant melanoma with FokI polymorphism. Studies have demonstrated that f allele of FokI gene was the risk factor for melanoma [53]. The presence of ff genotype in FokI gene polymorphism increases 10% the risk of basal cell carcinoma [54]. Another meta-analysis reported that both VDR FokI and BsmI polymorphisms were associated with melanoma [55]. But our study results showed that BsmI polymorphism was associated with a statistically significant decrease of 12% risk of melanoma. A meta-analysis observed that B allele present in BsmI was responsible for the protective effect in melanoma. Previous meta-analyses have reported associations between VDR polymorphisms and susceptibility to several malignancies, including breast, prostate, renal, and colorectal cancers; [56–60] however, these associations are heterogeneous and often cancer-specific. Such variability underscores that the functional consequences of individual VDR polymorphisms may differ depending on tissue context, environmental exposures, and genetic background. Consequently, melanoma-specific analyses remain essential to avoid misleading inferences derived from pooled cancer outcomes. Collectively, these findings suggest that VDR gene polymorphisms may be involved in cancer susceptibility in a context- and tissue-specific manner; however, the direction and magnitude of these associations appear to vary substantially across cancer types, populations, and polymorphic loci. Therefore, extrapolation of associations observed in non-melanoma malignancies to melanoma should be approached with caution. However, the precise mechanism of association among melanoma and polymorphisms of VDR gene is not explicit. The conflicting results obtained in our study indicate that same gene polymorphism may have varying genetic effects, depending on the cancer type.

Lee et al. in his meta-analysis reported that Polymorphism of BsmI was associated with risk of melanoma in Europeans, but no association was demonstrated between polymorphisms of TaqI, ApaI and EcoRV with risk of melanoma [51]. But our results indicate a decrease in risk of melanoma with BsmI polymorphism. The direction of the association observed for the BsmI polymorphism warrants careful interpretation in light of population-specific genetic and environmental contexts. Although several studies conducted predominantly in European populations have reported a positive association between BsmI and overall skin cancer risk, the present umbrella meta-analysis—restricted to melanoma and including diverse populations—suggests a neutral or potentially protective association. This apparent discrepancy may reflect differences in linkage disequilibrium patterns between BsmI and other functional VDR variants across populations, as BsmI itself is a non-coding polymorphism located in the 3′ untranslated region and is thought to influence VDR mRNA stability rather than protein structure. Moreover, population-specific differences in ultraviolet exposure patterns, vitamin D status, skin phototype, and gene–environment interactions may modify the phenotypic effects of BsmI. Therefore, the association of the BsmI polymorphism with a melanoma risk is likely context-dependent, underscoring the importance of cancer-specific and population-stratified analyses in genetic association studies.

With regard to EcoRV and Cdx2, we did not find any association with melanoma. Similar result was obtained in a meta-analysis conducted by Hou et al. [61]. Small sample size would have been the reason for this non-association or due to lack of expression of Cdx2 by the cells of the skin. The results of our study indicate that the variants of EcoRv and Cdx2 are not susceptible factors for melanoma. Taken together, these findings support a cautious, melanoma-specific interpretation of VDR-related genetic susceptibility, without overstating the clinical or causal implications.

Beyond VDR polymorphisms and circulating vitamin D exposure, other components of the vitamin D metabolic pathway may also contribute to melanoma susceptibility. Enzymes regulating vitamin D activation and degradation, such as CYP27B1 and CYP24A1, influence local availability of the active metabolite 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D in the skin and may thereby affect VDR-mediated control of cell proliferation, differentiation, immune modulation, and tumor progression [62].

In addition, genetic variation in vitamin D binding protein may indirectly modify melanoma risk by altering vitamin D transport, bioavailability, and tissue delivery, independent of VDR genotype [63]. Although emerging experimental and observational data suggest a potential role for these pathway-related genes, evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, and melanoma-specific meta-analytic data are currently insufficient for quantitative synthesis [64]. Consequently, while VDR polymorphisms remain the most extensively studied genetic component of the vitamin D pathway in melanoma, future integrative studies examining multiple pathway genes simultaneously are warranted.

Strengths and limitations

This critical umbrella review evaluated the association between vitamin D and melanoma across six VDR gene polymorphisms. A major strength of this umbrella meta-analysis is the restriction of outcomes to melanoma, which substantially reduces biological heterogeneity and enhances the etiological specificity and interpretability of the findings. Although heterogeneity is an inherent concern in umbrella reviews, our analyses showed low heterogeneity for several outcomes, although heterogeneity was moderate for BsmI. A key limitation is the small number of eligible meta-analyses, particularly for EcoRV and Cdx2, which may have reduced statistical power. Potential links between VDR polymorphisms and cancer severity could not be explored due to limited data. Most included meta-analyses were rated as critically low quality by AMSTAR-2, limiting the reliability of their pooled estimates. Additional issues include overlapping primary studies that may have inflated precision, inconsistent assessment of vitamin D exposure leading to possible misclassification, and reliance on observational data vulnerable to confounding, reverse causation, and publication bias. Collectively, these factors restrict causal inference and may bias effect estimates; hence, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Implications for prevention, risk stratification, and clinical translation

The present findings have potential implications for understanding melanoma susceptibility, but their immediate clinical applicability remains limited. Although the observed associations between VDR FokI and BsmI polymorphisms and melanoma risk suggest a possible role for host genetic susceptibility, current evidence does not support the routine use of VDR polymorphism screening for melanoma prevention, risk stratification, or treatment decision-making. The effect sizes observed are modest, and the underlying evidence is derived predominantly from observational studies with variable methodological quality and limited ethnic diversity.

Nevertheless, these findings may inform future research directions. In particular, VDR polymorphisms could contribute to multivariable risk prediction models when combined with established melanoma risk factors such as ultraviolet exposure patterns, skin phototype, family history, and nevus burden. From a preventive perspective, genotype-specific susceptibility may help explain interindividual variability in melanoma risk under similar environmental exposures, although this hypothesis requires validation in prospective, multi-ethnic cohorts. With respect to treatment and intervention, current evidence is insufficient to justify genotype-specific vitamin D supplementation or targeted interventions in melanoma patients. However, the consistent association observed for FokI, together with its known functional effects on VDR activity, supports further investigation into whether VDR genotype may modify biological responses to vitamin D signaling, immune modulation, or adjunctive therapies in experimental and clinical settings. Overall, while the findings highlight biologically relevant pathways, translation into clinical practice should await robust evidence from well-designed prospective and functional studies.

Conclusion

This umbrella meta-analysis indicates that specific VDR gene polymorphisms may influence susceptibility to melanoma. The FokI polymorphism was associated with an increased melanoma risk, whereas the BsmI polymorphism appeared to confer a protective association. No significant associations were identified for the null polymorphisms. Importantly, neither serum vitamin D concentration nor dietary vitamin D intake was significantly associated with melanoma risk when analyses were restricted to melanoma-only outcomes. These findings emphasize the importance of etiologically homogeneous outcome definitions and suggest that previously reported vitamin D associations may reflect heterogeneity introduced by pooling melanoma with non-melanoma skin cancers. Further large-scale, high-quality studies across diverse populations are required to elucidate the functional relevance of VDR polymorphisms and clarify the role of vitamin D in melanoma pathogenesis.
