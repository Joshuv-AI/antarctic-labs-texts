Nonheparin-bonded stent relining in a case of subacute in-stent thrombosis secondary to heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
Authors: Aaron Litvak, Joel Kruger, Alaska Pendleton, Michael Stoner, Karina Newhall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 62-year-old man with incidentally discovered bilateral popliteal artery aneurysms presented with acute critical limb ischemia with a thrombosed popliteal stent, thrombocytopenia, and positive platelet factor 4 antibodies suggesting a diagnosis of type II heparin-induced thrombocytopenia 2 weeks after popliteal artery aneurysm stenting. He was treated with urgent thrombolysis, bivalirudin, and fondaparinux followed by re-intervention with relining of the popliteal artery stents with nonheparin-bonded Viabahn covered stents. At the 6-month follow-up, his platelet count remained above 100,000. He has been successfully transitioned to a direct oral anticoagulant without further complications.Keywords: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, HIT, In-stent thrombosis

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a serious complication of heparin exposure in susceptible patients, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. It is characterized by thrombocytopenia with an associated hypercoagulable state presenting a high risk for thromboembolic events.1 Discontinuation of heparin products and initiation of a different anticoagulation strategy is currently the first line of treatment.1 Heparin-coated stents are commonplace in endovascular interventions because they improve long-term patency and decrease the risk of in-stent thrombosis.2 There is mixed evidence regarding an increased risk of HIT in susceptible patients with the use of these stents, with evidence suggesting no increased risk of thrombosis in patients with a history of HIT. Additionally, stent relining has been only described in few cases3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 in patients with an unclear HIT etiology. Cases of in-stent thrombosis, including in the superficial femoral artery (SFA) and popliteal artery, Viabahn endoprostheses have been shown to be an effective treatment with relining an already present stent showing patency and freedom from re-intervention at 1 year.11, 12, 13

Case report

The patient is a 62-year-old man who was referred to vascular surgery with asymptomatic bilateral popliteal artery aneurysms. Past medical history noted hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus on noninsulin medications, and a 37.5 pack-year smoking history; he has a height of 198 cm (6′6″). Notably, he previously underwent nonvascular surgery with use of subcutaneous heparin and no complications. Workup included popliteal aneurysms, measured by ultrasound at 3.77 cm and 4.03 cm on the right and left, respectively. Subsequent computed tomography angiography confirmed size, noting thrombus within both aneurysms and three-vessel runoff (Fig 1, A). Appropriate landing zones were identified for bilateral intervention; after appropriate counseling, the patient was consented for elective left popliteal artery stenting with a plan for contralateral intervention at a later date.

Computed tomography angiography demonstrating bilateral popliteal aneurysms with associated measurements (A). Left popliteal artery angiogram post Viabahn heparin-bonded stent placement (B).

The patient was placed in a supine position and left SFA access was obtained. A 9 × 10 cm and a 10 × 10 cm Viabahn stent were deployed and postdilated in an antegrade fashion (Fig 1, B) complicated by closure device failure and pseudoaneurysm formation, which was treated with coverage with 13 × 5 cm Viabahn stent via right common femoral artery access. Completion angiography showed pseudoaneurysm resolution and intact blood flow to the foot. Notably, the procedure was overall technically challenging given the patient's height. He was monitored overnight and discharged the following day with dual antiplatelet therapy.

Two weeks later, the patient presented to the emergency department with 6 hours of left lower extremity pain, paresthesia, coldness, and discoloration extending from the left mid-calf to the lower calf, consistent with Rutherford class IIa acute critical limb ischemia. Computed tomography angiography showed an occluded left popliteal artery stent with thrombus extending distally and without arterial reconstitution (Fig 2, A) and a new thrombocytopenia of 29,000. The patient was empirically started on bivalirudin for suspicion of HIT, with a total HIT score of 4, indicating a high probability. Confirmatory positive platelet factor 4 testing later confirmed this clinical diagnosis.

Computed tomography angiography demonstrating occlusion of left popliteal stent and persistent aneurysm formation bilaterally although with preserved flow in right popliteal artery (A). Left popliteal artery completion angiogram after catheter-directed thrombolysis and stent relining with nonheparin bonded stent (B).

Owing to acute limb ischemia, the patient underwent catheter-directed thrombolysis with tissue plasminogen activator. The following day, angiogram demonstrated successful thrombolysis of the popliteal stent with anterior tibial artery- and posterior tibial artery-dominant runoff to the left leg. Owing to strong suspicion of HIT given the patient's presentation timeline, it was decided to reline the heparin bonded stents using a 9 mm × 150 mm and a 10 mm × 100 mm nonheparin-bonded Viabahn covered stent, with additional stent relining of more proximal SFA access where there had previously been a pseudoaneurysm from the index procedure. Completion angiogram demonstrated a patent SFA and popliteal stents, with no residual thrombus and preserved anterior tibial artery- and posterior tibial artery-dominant runoff.

After the procedure, the patient was transitioned to fondaparinux, discharged home with plan for a direct oral anticoagulant once his platelet count increased to ≥100,000. The patient has remained asymptomatic including no symptoms of acute limb ischemia or claudication at the 6-month follow-up and patency was confirmed via ankle brachial-index and arterial Doppler ultrasound examination. The patient's baseline platelet count returned to >100,000, but owing to a new diagnosis of cirrhosis, elected to defer repair of the right-sided aneurysm. Given stabilization of the patient's platelet count on medical management, there was a low suspicion that cirrhosis was the primary driver of his thrombocytopenia.

Discussion

HIT presents in 0.1% to 7.0% of patients exposed to heparin products and can be a major source of morbidity and mortality, with 50% experiencing thromboembolic events and mortality rates reported of ≤30%.1,14 This patient experienced acute limb-threatening ischemia requiring emergent thrombolysis secondary to exposure to heparin products after heparin-bonded stent placement for a popliteal artery aneurysm.

There are several reports of subacute HIT presenting after heparin-bonded stent placement with peripheral in-stent thrombosis.4,8 There are also several examples of similar occurrences in coronary artery stenting.3,7,9,10 As opposed to the immediate and often mild type I HIT, these cases often represent type II HIT, where antibodies form over the course of several weeks and manifest as a type II hypersensitivity reaction upon re-exposure to heparin products.1 Query of the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience database from January 2015 to January 2025 of heparin-bonded stents most frequently used in vascular surgery including, Gore Viabahn, Bard and Angiomed Fluency, and Bard Flair, did not yield any reports like this patient's experience. Given the delayed nature of presentation in this patient and strongly positive platelet factor 4 antibody, type II HIT with continuous, low-level re-exposure occurring over several weeks from the heparin-bonded stents is the most likely etiology of his presentation.

Frequently these patients are treated with medical management using direct thrombin inhibitors or direct oral anticoagulants,9,15 as well as interventions such as balloon angioplasty, thrombolysis, and thrombectomy3,5,6,8 or stent explantation.4,10,16 However, treatment with nonheparin-bonded stent relining for the treatment of HIT is not well-described and represents a possible pathway for decreasing persistent heparin exposure from heparin-bonded stents. At our institution, nonheparin-bonded stents are ordered directly from the company and are not routinely stocked. Several studies describe successful use of Viabahn stents for the treatment of in-stent restenosis,11,12 with further evidence that relining thrombosed stents represents a feasible, durable treatment strategy.13 Given the high morbidity and mortality associated with HIT, more study is needed to evaluate the use of nonheparin-bonded stent relining in cases of endovascular heparin-bonded stent-induced HIT.

Conclusions

This case described successful endovascular treatment of type II HIT possibly secondary to a heparin-bonded stent. This treatment offers a less invasive alternative to complete stent explant, especially in patients who are thrombocytopenic or at very high risk of thrombotic events.
