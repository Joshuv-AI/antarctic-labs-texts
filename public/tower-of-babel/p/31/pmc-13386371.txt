Global Burden of Non-Communicable Diseases from E‑Cigarette Exposure: Trend Analysis and 2050 Projections
Authors: Xiaoxing Zhang, Xinmo Ma, Dan Yu, Zengliang Ruan, Jinfang Sun, Ting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global rise in e-cigarette use has sparked significant public health concerns, particularly regarding its potential association with major non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including chronic respiratory conditions, cardiovascular diseases, and mental health disorders. A systematic evaluation of the NCD burden attributable to e-cigarettes is critically needed to inform policy. Therefore, this study aims to quantify the burden of major NCDs attributable to e-cigarette use between 2010 and 2021 and project future trends through 2050. This study used deidentified 2010–2021 NCD data from GBD 2021. We applied a comparative risk assessment framework to estimate the burden of NCDs attributable to e-cigarette use and used a generalized additive model to project future trends. We found that between 2010 and 2021, global prevalence of current e-cigarette use increased from 0.90% (95% CI, 0.46–1.48) to 8.73% (95% CI, 5.24–12.99). During this period, prevalent cases and DALYs for major NCDs showed consistent upward trends worldwide. Notably, NCD DALYs attributable to e-cigarette use increased from 2010 to 2021. Projections indicate that this concerning trend will continue, with marked increases in e-cigarette-attributable NCD burden anticipated through 2050. This study demonstrates the urgent need to incorporate electronic cigarette control measures into NCD prevention strategies, particularly targeting adolescent populations where usage rates are rising most rapidly.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), also known as chronic diseases, are characterized by prolonged duration and multifactorial etiology involving genetic, physiological alterations, environmental exposures, and behavioral risk factors. Globally, NCDs account for 74% of all deaths, with cardiovascular diseases, cancer, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes, and mental/neurological disorders representing the leading contributors. Among these, chronic respiratory diseasesparticularly asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)disproportionately affect low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where they account for over 40% of NCD-related disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the economic burden is substantial. Even in a developed country like the UK, respiratory diseases accounted for £11.1 billion in direct healthcare costs in 2014, with indirect costs from productivity losses further exacerbating the societal impact. , Cardiovascular disease remains the foremost global cause of mortality, being responsible for 17.9 million deaths annually. Approximately 80% of deaths occur in LMICs, driven largely by ischemic heart disease and stroke. The economic impact is equally profound, including $147 billion in annual productivity losses in the US labor market. Concurrently, mental health disorders have emerged as the fastest-growing NCD category, with depression and anxiety disorders alone reducing workforce productivity by 2.9% of GDP in Singapore.

Electronic cigarettes (ECIGs), also referred to as electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) or e-cigarettes, are devices that vaporize a nicotine solution combined with liquid flavors instead of burning tobacco leaves. Since the emergence of e-cigarettes in 2004, the prevalence of e-cigarette use has increased exponentially globally. This trend is evident in certain European and North American countries, where e-cigarettes have rapidly gained popularity. A survey conducted in the United States in 2019 displayed that around 4.10 million high school students and 1.20 million middle school students are currently using e-cigarettes. The widespread adoption of e-cigarettes has been accompanied by a range of health concerns. Epidemiological researches present that e-cigarettes are considered an independent risk factor for respiratory diseases, cardiovascular disease, and mental health problems. By analyzing the saliva samples of the research subjects, it was found that African Americans are more susceptible to the oral health hazards caused by e-cigarettes. Laboratory studies have demonstrated that e-cigarettes can exhibit respiratory toxicity, cardiovascular toxicity, and neurotoxicity, potentially leading to damage to the lungs, cardiovascular system, and nervous system in individuals. In 2019, the United States reported acute respiratory failure in patients after using e-cigarette products containing tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), suggesting that e-cigarettes can induce acute lung injury. Animal experiments demonstrated that e-cigarette aerosols induce pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting the circadian rhythm gene per1 and promoting IL6+ macrophage infiltration. This constellation of risks positions e-cigarettes as an emerging public health challenge requiring coordinated surveillance, research, and policy responses.

This study quantifies population-level exposure to e-cigarettes and their attributable burden for major non-communicable diseases from 2010 to 2021, with projections through 2050 using Global Burden of Disease methodologies. By examining cardiopulmonary, metabolic, and neurodevelopmental outcomes across key demographic groups, we provide evidence to inform age-specific prevention strategies, product regulation based on harm profiles, and monitoring frameworks for emerging nicotine productsaddressing critical gaps in understanding the long-term population health impacts of e-cigarette use.

Methods

Overview

The data sources for the GBD database are primarily obtained through systematic reviews and data extraction, as well as leveraging data shared by national collaborators and the World Health Organization. GBD 2021 estimated 369 diseases and injuries and 87 risk factors for each of the 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. For most diseases and injuries, the processed data were modeled using the cause of death ensemble model (CODEm), spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression (ST-GPR), and DisMod-MR to assess estimates of mortality, incidence, and prevalence. GBD used the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system of WHO and made adjustments based on it, mainly including maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases (CMNN diseases), non-communicable diseases (NCDs), and injuries. The data of this study were mainly derived from GBD 2021 and meta-analysis. This study extracted data from GBD 2021 to assess the global burden of NCDs, including asthma, COPD, CVD, IHD, depressive disorders, and anxiety disorders, from 2010 to 2021, employing metrics such as prevalent cases, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs. Due to the fact that few psychiatric disorders were recorded as causes of death, this study concentrated on DALYs to evaluate the disease burden of mental health disorders. The GBD data visualization tools can be found online (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The current prevalence of e-cigarette use and relative risks for specific diseases were obtained through meta-analysis. The population attributable fraction (PAF) was estimated by counterfactual analysis based on the theoretical framework of the comparative risk assessment (CRA) methodology. The burden of NCDs attributable to e-cigarette use was estimated combined with PAF for different diseases. To project the temporal trends of the attributable burden through 2050, generalized additive models were employed.

Estimating the Prevalence of Current e-Cigarette Use and Associated RR for NCDs

Meta-analysis was utilized to estimate the current prevalence of e-cigarette use and combined odds ratio (OR) values between e-cigarette use and major NCDs. In the meta-analysis, when the heterogeneity test results of the included studies indicated statistical significance (I 2 ≥ 50% or P ≤ 0.10 for the Q test), a random effects model was utilized. In contrast, when heterogeneity was not statistically significant (I 2 ≤ 50% or P ≥ 0.10 for the Q test), a fixed effects model was employed.

We defined current prevalence of e-cigarette use as at least 1 day of e-cigarette use in the past 30 days. The study searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for articles published from the establishment of databases to June 23, 2024, utilizing terms such as “vaping”, “e-cigarette”, “electronic nicotine delivery systems”, “epidemiology”, “cross-sectional studies”, and “cohort studies”. Detailed search strategies are shown in Table S1. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed according to the PICOS principles to minimize the influence of confounding factors. We strictly limited the exposure status of the included population and included only studies that focused on exclusive e-cigarette users (defined as individuals who had no history of active smoking, no concurrent smoking and e-cigarette use, and no experience of switching from smoking to e-cigarette use). For detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, refer to Table S2. According to this, the literature was subjected to a screening process, which is illustrated in Figure S1. During data extraction, two researchers independently extracted and cross-validated the data; any discrepancies were adjudicated by a third researcher to reach a consensus. The cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the 11 criteria recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), while the cohort studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Two reviewers independently conducted the assessments and cross-verified the results, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer. The cross-sectional study that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria was evaluated using 11 items recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). When a single item is evaluated as “yes”, it will get 1 point, and when it is evaluated as “no” and “unclear”, it will get 0 point. The total score of low quality is 0–3 points, medium quality is 4–7 points, and high quality is 8–11 points. The cohort study used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assessed eight aspects of each study in three areas (choice, comparability, and outcome). NOS contains 8 items, the total score of low quality is 0–3 points, the medium quality is 4–6 points, and the high quality is 7–9 points. The quality of the final included literature was assessed, and the total score was higher than four as the inclusion criteria.

Then, the study conducted a comprehensive search of the above three databases utilizing keywords related to e-cigarettes, including “vaping”, “electronic cigarette”, and “electronic nicotine delivery systems” and outcomes of interest such as “asthma”, “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”, “cardiovascular disease”, “ischemic heart disease”, “depressive disorders”, and “anxiety disorders”. Similarly, the specific search strategies, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and literature screening process are detailed in Tables S3–S8 and Figures S2–S4.

The relative risk (RR) can be derived from the formula OR(1−P0)+P0×OR(P0>0.05) , where P 0 represents the incidence of outcome indicators in the control group, and the odds ratio (OR) serves as the risk estimate for the disease. When the incidence is low, the OR approximates the RR.

Calculating PAF and Attributable Burden

Comparative risk assessment (CRA) is a systematic assessment framework in the study of the global burden of disease (GBD). The core of CRA is to compare the current distribution of population exposure to a risk factor with the hypothesized exposure distribution (not a single no-exposure reference level). The comparative risk assessment method was used to estimate the burden of NCDs from e-cigarette use. The basic statistic derived from this approach is the population attributable fraction (PAF), which is defined as the proportional reduction in disease or death that would occur if the exposure level were reduced to zero (or another specified baseline value). The PAF is a measure used to estimate the impact of risk factors on the incidence of disease in a population. PAF is associated with the prevalence of risk factors and their associated relative risk (RR). The calculation formula is PAF=Pe×(RR−1)Pe×(RR−1)+1 , where P e is the prevalence of the risk factor in the population and RR is defined as the risk of developing a disease in an exposed population relative to the risk of developing the disease in an unexposed population.

The study of attributable disease burden represents the disaggregation of the overall disease burden based on various causes and risk factors, thereby facilitating an analysis of the impact of each component on health outcomes. The formula is AB = PAF × B, where B is the total burden of disease from a specific cause or group of causes affected by the risk factor, and its indicators include disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years of life lost (YLLs), and years lived with disability (YLDs).

Predicting Attributable Burden

The generalized additive model (GAM) describes any relationship between independent and response variables by using nonparametric smooth functions, and its basic form is Y = f 1(X 1) + f 2(X 2) + ... + f i (X i ) + ε, where f i (X i ) represents the smooth function, X i is the independent variable, and ε represents the error term. When the relationship between variables is expected to be complex and not easy to fit with linear models, GAMs can flexibly capture the nonlinear relationship between variables, thereby realizing the fitting of smooth relationships between two or more variables. , Separate generalized additive models were developed to project the burden of NCDs attributed to e-cigarettes and traditional cigarettes to 2050. The analysis was completed based on R version 4.3.2, and the software package “Multivariate Generalized Cross Validation” (mgcv) was used for GAM model fitting, and the ggplot2 package was used for visualization. For each GAM, thin-plate regression splines were chosen as the smoothing functions. The performance of each GAM was evaluated by several performance metrics, including R2, deviance, mean square error (MSE), root-mean-square error (RMSE), Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC).

Results

Global Current Prevalence of e-Cigarette Use

The characteristics of the literature included in the meta-analysis and the results of the literature quality evaluation are detailed in Tables S9–S11. The results revealed a significant increase in the current prevalence of e-cigarette use, rising from 0.90% (0.46–1.48) in 2010 to 8.73% (5.24–12.99) in 2021 (Figures and S5). In this study, adolescents were defined as individuals aged 10 to 18 years, while adults were classified as those aged 18 years and older. The current prevalence of e-cigarette use was estimated to be 6.42% (4.89–8.14) among adolescents and 4.46% (3.67–5.32) among adults from 2010 to 2021 (Figures and S6). During that period, we assessed prevalence of current e-cigarette use in 12 different countries, with the highest prevalence observed in Kuwait at 27.88% (26.02–29.78). In Poland, the United States, Italy, and Thailand, the current prevalence of e-cigarette use ranged from 5% to 10%, while in all other countries, it was less than 5% (Figures and S7).

Global Burden of NCDs from 2010 to 2021

Prevalent cases of asthma exhibited a decline from 2010 to 2015, followed by a consistent increase beginning in 2016. DALYs of asthma initially rose in 2010, experienced a decrease from 2012 to 2014, and subsequently increased to 21.42 million (16.96–26.89) in 2021. During 2010 to 2021, YLDs demonstrated a pattern of initial reduction followed by an increase, whereas YLLs exhibited an inverse trend. Specifically, YLDs decreased from 9.98 million (6.43–14.83) in 2010 to 9.62 million (6.14–14.23) in 2015 and subsequently rose annually to reach 10.19 million (6.51–15.03) in 2021. Conversely, YLLs increased from 11.02 million (9.11–14.06) in 2010 to 11.40 million (9.50–14.66) in 2018, followed by a gradual decline to 11.23 million (9.41–14.18) in 2021 (Figure A and Table S12).

Prevalent cases of COPD increased from 163.89 million (149.97–178.89) in 2010 to 213.39 million (194.87–233.98) in 2021, and the number of DALYs steadily increased from 67.03 million (62.80–70.56) to 79.78 million (74.03–86.01). Additionally, YLLs rose from 55.52 million (51.97–58.61) in 2010 to 64.92 million (59.33–71.14) in 2021, with YLDs escalating from 11.51 million (9.72–13.23) to 14.86 million (12.41–17.13) (Figure B and Table S12).

From 2010 to 2021, prevalent cases of CVD rose from 458.61 million (431.11–481.87) to 612.06 million (570.32–649.81), and DALYs increased from 374.59 million (359.09–387.20) to 428.33 million (403.68–453.71). The global trends for YLDs and YLLs of CVD increased continually during that period (Figure C and Table S12).

The prevalent cases due to IHD rose steadily since 2010, reaching 254.28 million (221.45–295.49) cases and 188.36 million (177.04–198.15) DALYs in 2021. Over the same period, there were upward trends in the YLDs and YLLs of IHD (Figure D and Table S12).

The global trend for prevalent cases and DALYs associated with depressive and anxiety disorders increased annually from 2010 to 2021, with a notable surge beginning in 2019. Prevalent cases of depressive disorders rose from 248.33 million (225.38–275.95) in 2010 to 332.41 million (297.74–376.10) in 2021, and anxiety disorders increased from 267.44 million (230.86–310.83) to 359.21 million (307.19–419.97) (Figure E and Table S12).

Discussion

Our longitudinal analysis reveals critical findings regarding electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) and non-communicable disease (NCD) burden; global ENDS use prevalence increased markedly from 2010 to 2021, particularly in high-income countries like Kuwait where prevalence reached 27.88% (26.02–29.78). Population attributable fraction analyses identified significant ENDS-associated risks for multiple NCDs, including asthma, COPD, cardiovascular diseases, and mental health disorders, contributing to a 9-fold increase in attributable DALYs since 2010. While current ENDS-attributable DALYs remain lower than those of combustible cigarettes, our projection models indicate that these burden differentials may converge by 2050 due to increasing youth adoption, prolonged exposure durations, and synergistic interactions with other risk factors. Following 2015, the emergence of specific e-cigarette productssuch as JUULwith characteristics including high portability, elevated nicotine concentrations, and appealing flavor profiles, contributed to a rapid and substantial increase in youth e-cigarette use globally, particularly in the United States. This stands in stark contrast to the initial premise between 2010 and 2014 that e-cigarettes would serve primarily as a smoking cessation aid for adult tobacco users. Long-term health risks from this trend are likely to contribute to the projected burden of NCDs in 2050. If individuals who initiated e-cigarette use during adolescence continue using nicotine products or transition to traditional cigarettes in adulthood, it will lead to a significant increase in the burden of NCDs, such as respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, in the coming decades. In 2010, projections based on e-cigarettes primarily as a smoking cessation tool for adults may underestimate the future burden.

Between 2010 and 2021, global current prevalence of e-cigarette use had increased from 0.90% to 8.73%. In countries with high prevalence of e-cigarette use, such as the United States and New Zealand, the significant rise in the prevalence of e-cigarettes may be related to the decline in the global smoking rate. The prevalence of e-cigarette use has risen dramatically among adolescents over the past decade. We found that among current users, adolescent e-cigarette use was 6.42% higher than that of adults at 4.46%. Kuwait had the highest current e-cigarette use, at 27.88%. This may be due to the lack of regulations and policies related to e-cigarette marketing in Kuwait. Attitudes and policies regarding the regulation of e-cigarettes vary widely across countries globally, ranging from outright bans to incentives as harm-reduction tools. This divergence complicates the prediction of the global or regional disease burden. Our analysis revealed a significant increase in prevalence rates of major non-communicable diseasesasthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, ischemic heart disease, depressive disorders, and anxiety disordersfrom 2010 to 2021. Asthma is recognized as the most prevalent chronic respiratory disease, and DALYs of asthma exhibited an overall upward trend from 2010 to 2021. During this time, the burden of COPD also risen significantly. CVD accounts for about half of NCD deaths, and the number of CVD DALYs increased annually during 2010–2021. IHD remains the leading cause of mortality among all cardiovascular diseases, and the trend in DALYs of IHD has also demonstrated an annual increase throughout this period. The number of depressive and anxiety disorder DALYs increased annually between 2010 and 2021, with a notable escalation observed after 2019, potentially linked to the COVID-19 pandemic that emerged in 2020. The direct impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on population psychology may exacerbate the global burden of common mental disorders. COVID-19 pandemic may impact on e-cigarette use behaviors, access to health care services, lifestyle patterns, and direct association with infections. These factors may have led to higher predicted values of NCD burden than pre-pandemic estimates based on a single factor.

Our analysis demonstrates that e-cigarette use is significantly associated with increased risks of asthma development. This risk profile arises from inhalation of ENDS-generated aerosols containing particulate matter (<2.5 μm), volatile organic compounds, and heavy metals, which may induce both direct pulmonary injury and systemic inflammatory responses. In vitro and in vivo evidence has confirmed that electronic cigarette aerosols are capable of inducing acute lung inflammation, and the presence of nicotine and flavorings is found to potentiate this inflammatory reaction. E-cigarettes have been shown to influence the global DALYs of asthma and COPD, accounting for 3.46% and 5.84% in 2021, respectively. Combining the e-cigarette population attributable fraction for asthma and COPD to estimate the attributable burden, this study revealed a general increase in the number of DALYs associated with e-cigarettes from 2010 to 2021. The DALYs of asthma and COPD attributable to traditional cigarettes were found to be higher than those of e-cigarettes. This implies that traditional cigarettes play a more critical role in the DALYs of asthma and COPD. Smoking is recognized as a primary risk factor for asthma and COPD, emphasizing the necessity of reducing tobacco consumption to moderate the risks associated with asthma and COPD. The number of asthma DAYLs due to e-cigarettes is expected to rise consistently until 2050. The forecast results demonstrate that the DALYs and YLLs of asthma due to traditional cigarettes have exhibited a consistent annual decrease through 2050, while the YLDs have demonstrated an increasing trend. Projections indicate that the number of DALYs related to COPD attributable to electronic and traditional cigarettes is anticipated to increase annually until 2050.

Similarly, e-cigarette exposure was associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. This could be attributed to the fact that most e-liquids contain nicotine components, which have a significant impact on the cardiovascular system. The nicotine present in e-cigarettes could stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in increased heart rate, blood pressure, stroke volume, and cardiac output, as well as enhanced coronary blood flow. The number of DALYs of CVD and IHD attributable to electronic and traditional cigarettes from 2010 to 2021 presented an overall increase. However, the burden of disease associated with traditional cigarettes surpassed that attributed to e-cigarettes, indicating the greater impact of traditional smoking on the global burden of CVD and IHD. There is already evidence that traditional cigarette use is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Furthermore, smoking cessation can reduce the incidence of CVD, emphasizing the importance of reducing tobacco use to lower cardiovascular risk. The number of DALYs of CVD and IHD attributable to electronic and traditional cigarettes is expected to increase annually until 2050.

Increased risk of depressive and anxiety disorders was associated with e-cigarette use. Nicotine has a serious impact on the neurodevelopment of adolescents, elevating the risk of alterations in emotional and cognitive behaviors. This might be explained by the fact that nicotine exposure in e-cigarettes leads to abnormal activation of neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, thereby influencing neuronal signal transduction and subsequently affecting adolescent behaviors such as addiction, cognition, and emotional regulation. A general upward trend in the DALYs attributable to e-cigarettes for depressive and anxiety disorders was observed from 2010 to 2021. While GBD 2021 does not estimate the impact of smoking on mental disorders, existing literature has established a strong correlation between tobacco use and various psychiatric disorders. The number of DALYs of depressive and anxiety disorders attributable to e-cigarettes is expected to increase until 2050.

This study advances the field through comprehensive analysis of e-cigarette-attributable NCD burdens using Global Burden of Disease 2021 data, establishing longitudinal trends (2010–2021) and 2050 projections, while providing comparative risk assessments against combustible cigarettes. However, several limitations must be acknowledged: the post-2010 prevalence data precludes the assessment of earlier exposure effects; current epidemiological evidence remains insufficient to establish causal relationships for specific end points like stroke; and data limitations restricted the use of advanced predictive modeling. Particularly concerning is the evidence gap regarding adolescent health impacts, compounded by frequent dual use with combustible cigarettes, a critical confounder requiring future study. These findings nonetheless highlight actionable opportunities: tobacco-related NCD burdens remain preventable through targeted interventions informed by ongoing surveillance of evolving e-cigarette products and usage patterns. Specifically, enterprises are required to disclose the content of harmful components, e.g., acrolein and heavy metals, in e-cigarette aerosols, and standards should be established to limit irritant components closely associated with the incidence of asthma and COPD. Additionally, e-cigarette use prevalence should be integrated into the public health surveillance system to provide data support for setting early warning thresholds. Regarding prevention strategies, targeted population interventions should be implemented. For adolescents, interventions should prioritize exposure control and cognitive guidance. Specifically, e-cigarettes should be incorporated into the adolescent tobacco purchase restriction system, with strict age verification enforced for e-cigarette purchases through online–offline collaborative supervision. Meanwhile, all forms of e-cigarette advertising should be prohibited. Additionally, targeted school–family collaborative health education programs are imperative to clarify the health risks of e-cigarette use; peer-assisted cessation groups should be established, and efforts should be made to mitigate the inducing effects of peer influence on adolescent e-cigarette initiation. For adults, the intervention focus should lie in prevention and harm reduction. For never smoking adults, the priority is to prevent e-cigarette exposure and avert the transition to regular e-cigarette use. This can be achieved via community-based health literacy campaigns to raise awareness of e-cigarette-related health hazards and the designation of e-cigarette-free zones in public areas to reduce environmental triggers. For ever-smoking adults, the core goal is to reduce e-cigarette consumption frequency. Hospitals’ smoking cessation clinics should provide personalized cessation counseling and follow-up interventions.

Conclusions

Our analysis demonstrates that e-cigarettes constitute an increasingly significant contributor to the global burden of non-communicable diseases, with substantiated associations for asthma, COPD, cardiovascular diseases, and mental health disorders during 2010–2021. Projection models indicate that these ENDS-attributable burdens will continue their upward trajectory through 2050. Collectively, these findings establish an evidence triad for public health interventionquantitative risk assessment to inform product regulation, demographic-specific prevention strategies for vulnerable populations, and adaptive monitoring systems responsive to evolving e-cigarette designs and use behaviors. These findings provide three critical evidence bases for public health action, including quantitative risk assessment for product regulation, targeted prevention strategies for high-risk demographics, and monitoring frameworks accounting for evolving product characteristics and usage patterns.
