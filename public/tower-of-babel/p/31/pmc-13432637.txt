Active Cycle of Breathing Technique Versus Oscillating Positive Expiratory Pressure Therapy Versus Exercise With Huffing During an Exacerbation of Bronchiectasis: A Randomised, Controlled Trial
Authors: Jennifer Phillips, Rodney Pope, Wayne Hing, Ashleigh Canov, Nicole Harley, Annemarie L Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveThe relative effectiveness of airway clearance techniques (ACTs) during an exacerbation of bronchiectasis is unknown. This study aimed to compare the effects of three ACTs on sputum expectoration, health‐related quality‐of‐life (HRQOL), and exacerbation rates in adults hospitalised with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis.MethodsA randomised controlled trial of active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT), oscillating positive expiratory pressure (OPEP) therapy and walking with huffing. Sputum wet weight was collected during ACT sessions, 1‐h post‐session and the following 23 h on day 2 and day of discharge. HRQOL was assessed using the Quality of Life‐Bronchiectasis (QoL‐B) questionnaire and Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) on day 2 and day of discharge. Time to first exacerbation was explored for 6 months following discharge.ResultsFifty‐five participants were recruited. ACBT was associated with smaller sputum wet weight 1‐h post session on day of discharge compared to walking with huffing (mean difference −2.58 g); no other significant differences between groups in sputum weights were observed. ACBT and walking with huffing significantly improved selected measures of HRQOL (LCQ total score and multiple QOL‐B domains) at hospital discharge while OPEP therapy produced minimal changes. ACBT (158 days, 95% CI 139–177) resulted in a longer time to first exacerbation than walking with huffing (118 days, 95% CI 83–154) and OPEP therapy (89 days, 95% CI 69–111) but this finding should be interpreted with caution due to loss to follow‐up.ConclusionWalking with huffing and ACBT gave similar improvements in sputum expectoration and HRQOL during hospitalisation in adults with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis.Trial RegistrationThe study was registered on the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12621000428864) on 16‐04‐2021 Keywords: airway clearance, airway clearance techniques, bronchiectasis, exacerbation, physiotherapy

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is a chronic respiratory disease characterised by persistent cough, sputum production, recurrent exacerbations and decreased exercise tolerance [1, 2, 3]. International guidelines recommend airway clearance techniques (ACTs) for adults with bronchiectasis [4, 5, 6], to facilitate sputum expectoration and reduce symptom burden [7, 8]. Undertaking ACTs during an exacerbation of bronchiectasis is necessary due to increased sputum volume and viscosity [6, 9, 10, 11].

Most studies of ACTs in bronchiectasis have been completed when individuals were clinically stable [12, 13, 14]. Among those involving people with an exacerbation, several techniques have been found safe, but there appears limited knowledge regarding clinical efficacy [14]. Two commonly implemented ACTs during exacerbations of bronchiectasis are the active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT) and oscillating positive expiratory pressure (OPEP) therapy [9, 15]. Each has demonstrated short‐term benefit for sputum mobilisation, gas exchange and pulmonary function [14, 16, 17]; however, longer‐term effects on health‐related quality‐of‐life (HRQOL) and exacerbations are unknown. Surveyed physiotherapists managing individuals with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis reported physical exercise is routinely used for airway clearance [15]. This technique has been applied in people with cystic fibrosis (CF) for airway clearance and found effective when combined with huffing [18]. However, no studies have examined immediate or longer‐term effects of exercise as an airway clearance technique (ACT) for individuals with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis.

With recurrent exacerbations a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality in this population [3, 19, 20], further insight into the effectiveness of ACTs will inform physiotherapy practice. The primary aim of this study was to compare the effects of the ACBT, OPEP therapy and walking with huffing on sputum expectoration in adults hospitalised with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis. Secondary aims were to compare the effects of these three interventions on HRQOL, exercise tolerance and times to first exacerbation and first hospital admission in the 6 months post hospital discharge following an exacerbation.

Methods

A multi‐site, stratified, prospective, parallel groups, randomised controlled trial was conducted at two private hospitals in Brisbane, Australia. Participant registration took place from May 2021 to August 2023. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol has been previously published [21]. Methods and results are reported in accordance with the CONSORT statement [22]. No changes to the study methodology occurred once data collection had commenced. No participant involvement occurred in trial planning.

Participants and Demographic Features

Participants were recruited within 48 h of hospital admission for an exacerbation of bronchiectasis. Exacerbation and exclusion criteria have been previously outlined [10, 21]. Bronchiectasis severity was scored on admission according to the Bronchiectasis Severity Index (BSI) [23].

Allocation

Baseline outcome measures were completed. Participants were then randomly assigned to groups using computer‐generated random sequencing. Allocation was stratified by self‐reported usual sputum volume (low volume (< 1 teaspoon sputum per day when well) or high volume (> 1 teaspoon sputum per day when well)) to ensure a balance between groups for this characteristic [24, 25]. Further information regarding randomisation and allocation is detailed in the protocol [21].

Usual Care

All participants received usual care in addition to their allocated ACT, with the description outlined in the protocol [21].

Instructions for each ACT were informed by practice guidelines [26] and previous studies [12, 15]. In hospital, participants were prescribed their allocated ACT three times daily, once supervised by a physiotherapist, and twice independently. Upon discharge, participants were instructed to continue allocated ACTs independently twice daily. See the protocol for instructions for each group [21]. Compliance was encouraged and collected via self‐report during inpatient daily interviews and monthly post‐discharge phone calls.

Outcomes

Assessors were blinded to group allocation. Participants were assessed on three occasions during admission, and monthly for 6 months following discharge. Outcomes during hospitalisation were assessed upon admission (within 48 h), day six, and day of discharge (within 24‐h of hospital discharge). If a participant was discharged from hospital day three, they only completed day two outcome measures. If a participant was discharged from hospital on days four‐to‐seven, they only completed day two and hospital discharge outcomes. Further information is outlined in the protocol [21].

The primary outcome of sputum volume, measured as wet weight of sputum (grams) as a proxy, was assessed three times each inpatient occasion (during treatment session, one‐hour following treatment session, and during the following 23‐h). Twenty‐four‐hour sputum wet weight was then calculated for each day by summing all three measures. Ward physiotherapists collected sputum containers at each time point to prevent confusion about which container to use. A senior physiotherapist, blinded to group allocation, weighed the containers on calibrated scales. The goal of ACTs is to facilitate secretion removal; therefore, a greater sputum wet weight during the session or during the first hour post‐session, or a greater value for the sum of the wet weights from those two initial periods as a percentage of 24‐h sputum wet weight was interpreted as improvement in sputum clearance. A greater reduction in total sputum wet weight in a 24‐h period at hospital discharge, compared to hospital admission, was also interpreted as indicating greater effectiveness in sputum clearance [7, 27, 28, 29].

Secondary outcomes assessed were disease‐specific HRQOL (Bronchiectasis Health Questionnaire (BHQ) [30]; Quality of Life‐Bronchiectasis (QoL‐B) questionnaire [31]), cough‐related quality‐of‐life (QOL) (Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) [32]); and exercise tolerance (six‐minute walk test distance (6MWD) [33]). On all three questionnaires, higher scores indicate improvement in HRQOL.

Time to first exacerbation and hospital admission were collected for 6 months from date of hospital discharge through monthly phone calls and participants' daily completion of the Bronchiectasis Exacerbations and Symptoms Tool (BEST) [34]. Exacerbation of bronchiectasis was defined as an increase in BEST score of four or more points, for more than 48 h [34].

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size calculations were completed a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6) to ensure the study was adequately powered, with 51 participants required. The previously established sputum volume minimal clinical important difference (MCID) was 15 mL over 24 h [27], assuming a standard deviation of 15 mL, power of 0.80 and alpha value of 0.05; 17 participants per group were required [27]. Further details are provided in the protocol [21]. This sample size calculation was based on sputum volume (mL) rather than wet weight (g), as that was the information available during study design, and volume is commonly used when analysing sputum expectoration in bronchiectasis [29, 35, 36, 37, 38]. However, sputum wet weight may be considered more accurate than sputum volume, as measurement does not depend on graduated scales in containers and assessor interpretation [28], so wet weight was used as a proxy for volume. Subsequent to our power calculations, the MCID for sputum wet weight has been determined as a reduction of 6.4 g over 24‐h, or a 17% reduction from baseline in 24‐h sputum for individuals with stable bronchiectasis [28]. This information has informed interpretation of results.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS V.29 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Data were analysed using intention‐to‐treat principles, with alpha set at 0.05 for inferential analyses. Participant demographics were descriptively analysed.

Between‐group differences for sputum wet weight over 24‐h were assessed using a generalised linear mixed model, with treatment group as the fixed variable, contrast group set as the walking with huffing group, and the participant set as a random effect to allow for the repeated measures. The same procedure was repeated for each sputum measure, as well as each secondary outcome. The change over time value for each sputum wet weight measure and secondary outcomes was assessed using a generalised linear mixed model with the covariate set as the day 2 value for the respective measure to adjust for baseline scores.

Between‐group differences in within‐group change scores for all sputum wet weight measures and secondary outcome measures were assessed using mixed‐effect linear regression, with time of measure and treatment group as fixed variables, and participant as a random effect. The estimated means and 95% confidence intervals for the within‐group change scores are presented for each group, along with the mean between‐group differences and associated 95% confidence intervals. The confidence interval adjustment used in these analyses was the least significant difference. Time‐to‐event outcomes, including time to first exacerbation recurrence following discharge, were recorded in days from hospital discharge. Participants who did not experience an exacerbation during follow‐up were censored at the date of last follow‐up (6 months); censoring was also applied to participants who withdrew or were lost to follow‐up. Kaplan Meier survival curves were generated and survival distributions assessed using the log‐rank (Mantel‐Cox) test. To quantify the relative hazard of experiencing an event, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted using walking with huffing as the reference group, and model significance was assessed using the Omnibus tests of model coefficients, with data from follow‐up phone calls.

QoL–B Questionnaire

There were no significant between‐group differences in change of QoL‐B domain scores during admission (Table 3, Table S4). During follow‐up, there was significant improvement in the social domain with walking with huffing compared to OPEP therapy and significant improvement in the health perception domain with ACBT compared to walking with huffing (Table S5).

When examining within‐group changes during hospital admission (Table S3): walking with huffing demonstrated significant improvements in five domains of the QoL‐B—respiratory symptoms, physical functioning, vitality, emotional functioning, and health perceptions; ACBT demonstrated significant improvements in four domains—respiratory symptoms, physical functioning, vitality, and social functioning; and OPEP therapy demonstrated significant improvement in one domain—emotional functioning.

Leicester Cough Questionnaire

During admission, walking with huffing demonstrated a significantly greater improvement than OPEP therapy in change in LCQ total score, and psychological and social scores (Table 3, Table S4). There was no difference in LCQ change scores between walking with huffing and the ACBT during hospitalisation or outpatient follow‐up, or between OPEP therapy and walking with huffing during follow‐up (Table 3, Tables S4 and S5).

When examining within‐group changes during hospitalisation (Table S4), walking with huffing demonstrated significant improvements in the LCQ total score and all domains. The ACBT demonstrated significant improvements in the LCQ total score, physical domain, and psychological domains. OPEP therapy did not significantly change LCQ domains or total scores.

6MWD

There was no significant difference between groups, or within groups change in 6MWD during hospitalisation (Table 3). Due to large amounts of missing data from outpatient follow‐up, differences in changes in 6MWD between groups are not reported. Reasons for non‐completion of 6MWD during hospital admission are in Table S6.

Outpatient Exacerbations and Hospitalisations

Due to significant amounts of missing data, BEST scores were not analysed to determine time to first exacerbation or hospitalisation. Twenty‐two participants completed the BEST (seven, ten, five in Group 1, 2, 3, respectively). Among participants with complete data from the BEST and phone call follow‐up, numbers of exacerbations identified via BEST data compared to phone data were higher in twelve, the same in five, and lower in two participants (Table S7).

Twenty‐four exacerbations, with a diagnosis from a medical professional, were identified from phone call follow‐up during the outpatient period (seven, five, twelve in Group 1, 2, 3, respectively), and 49 participants were included in the final Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses. (Figure S3).

The ACBT group had a longer mean time to first exacerbation following discharge (158 days, 95% CI 139–177) compared to walking with huffing (118 days, 95% CI 83–154) and OPEP therapy (89 days, 95% CI 69–111, Figure 3). Exacerbation survival curves differed significantly across the groups (Χ2(2) = 13.126, p = 0.001). Compared to walking and huffing, the ACBT had a lower hazard rate for experiencing exacerbation although this difference did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.426, p = 0.146). This represents a 57.4% risk reduction. In contrast, compared to walking and huffing, OPEP therapy had a higher hazard rate for experiencing an exacerbation (HR 2.493, p = 0.061), representing a 149% higher hazard of exacerbation.

Kaplan Meier Survival Curves for time to first exacerbation with a diagnosis from a medical professional, by airway clearance group.

Thirteen participants experienced a hospital admission due to exacerbation during follow‐up (two, six, five in Group 1, 2, 3, respectively). All groups had similar mean times to first hospitalisation: walking with huffing (167 days, 95% CI 147–188), OPEP therapy (164 days, 95% CI 147–182) and the ACBT group (161 days, 95% CI 142–180). Kaplan Meier survival curves for admissions during follow‐up (Figure S4) indicated no clinically or statistically significant differences in admission rates between groups (x2 (2) = 1.345, p = 0.511).

Discussion

To the authors knowledge, this is the first study investigating walking with huffing as a stand‐alone ACT for individuals with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis. While walking with huffing increased sputum volume in the one‐hour post ACT session on day of discharge compared to the ACBT, this is unlikely to be clinically significant, and there were no other significant differences between ACTs in sputum wet weight measures. Only the ACBT reached the MCID in change of 24‐h sputum [28]. Both walking with huffing and the ACBT significantly improved multiple HRQOL domains at hospital discharge; OPEP therapy only improved in emotional functioning. The ACBT group demonstrated longer time to first exacerbation in the 6 months post discharge compared to OPEP therapy and walking with huffing, but the limited sample size and loss to follow‐up may have impacted findings.

Twenty‐four hour sputum has been explored in one other study during an exacerbation of bronchiectasis [29]. Tsang and Jones [29] reported the effects of three ACTs (breathing and coughing, postural drainage with breathing and coughing, and OPEP therapy) on 24‐h sputum wet weights, with total grams (26–45 admission, 14–35 discharge) higher than in the current study (maximum 17 admission, 12 discharge). One potential explanation is inclusion criteria; Tsang and Jones [29] included participants with symptoms of persistent cough and sputum production in addition to exacerbation of bronchiectasis, whereas the current study included only participants admitted with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis. In this study, only the ACBT group experienced a reduction in 24‐h expectorated sputum wet weight that exceeded the MCID [28]. This indicates the ACBT may be slightly more effective than the other two ACTs in improving sputum expectoration during hospital admission, despite the difference not being significant.

Both walking with huffing and the ACBT improved HRQOL during hospitalisation, with OPEP therapy achieving limited effects. All three ACTs demonstrated a change in score at hospital discharge that exceeded the MCID for the QoL‐B respiratory symptoms domain, with the smallest change in the OPEP therapy group (12 points) larger than the MCIDs established in clinically stable patients, ranging from 6.8 [39], to 8.6 [40], and 7–10 [31]. Only one previous study explored the changes in LCQ‐derived cough‐related QOL during an exacerbation of bronchiectasis [41], with the ACBT significantly more effective than postural drainage with percussion [41]. In the current study, both walking with huffing and the ACBT were associated with improvements in LCQ total scores beyond the MCID of 1.3 points at hospital discharge [42]. The lack of significant improvement with OPEP therapy in most domains of HRQOL suggests walking with huffing and ACBT prescription are potentially more beneficial to improving HRQOL for individuals hospitalised with an exacerbation of bronchiectasis.

Exercise as an ACT has been examined in people with CF. [18, 43] The physiological rationale is to increase tidal volume and peak expiratory flow (PEF), helping move sputum toward central airways for expectoration [43, 44]. Walking may also reduce sputum mechanical impedance through shearing forces in the lung parenchyma [18, 43, 44]. A recent systematic review on adults with CF concluded that exercise may improve sputum clearance compared to rest, and be comparable to other ACTs when a huff is included [18]. Both treadmill walking (intensity 60% of peak oxygen consumption) and OPEP therapy combined with huffing have demonstrated single session significant improvements in PEF and reduced sputum mechanical impedance in adults with stable CF. [45] These findings support the finding of the current study that walking with huffing may be as effective as OPEP therapy for short‐term sputum clearance. Future research is required to establish whether physiological mechanisms observed in CF also apply during bronchiectasis exacerbations.

Strengths of this study include recruitment of the largest number of participants to date with a bronchiectasis exacerbation, and being the first to examine walking with huffing as a stand‐alone ACT. Participant demographics were similar to those previously published and Australian Bronchiectasis Registry data, suggesting results may be broadly applicable to individuals with exacerbations of bronchiectasis [14, 46]. Adverse events were few and minor. Interventions applied aligned with guidelines and current practice [4, 15, 29, 41], and participants were monitored daily during hospitalisation to maximise compliance. Randomisation was stratified based on usual sputum volume to control for any effects of this factor on findings.

There were several limitations, one being the use of sputum expectoration as a primary outcome, which depends on a patient's ability to expectorate secretions and to remember to expectorate all sputum for 24‐h into a collection container [47]. Recruitment was affected by Coronavirus‐19, with its impact on hospital admissions [48], but participant numbers required for statistical power were successfully recruited. Recruiting from two private hospitals limits generalisability, and it is unclear whether findings apply to community managed exacerbations. No analysis examined whether ACT suitability varied by disease severity or demographics, and group allocation relied on self‐reported usual sputum volume, which may be inaccurate. Recruitment within 48‐h of hospitalisation was difficult, with many patients reporting feeling too unwell to be recruited. While this implies a potential bias toward recruitment of individuals who were feeling ‘more well’, a range of bronchiectasis severities were represented. Not all participants completed every outcome measure, and the absence of imaging prevents assessment of sputum plugging. Usual medical care was not standardised, and medication use was not recorded which may impact on selected study outcomes. Drop‐outs during the 6‐month follow‐up period were evident across all groups and may have biased findings, which must therefore be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution.

Future research should continue to ensure studies are adequately powered to detect meaningful effects, explore the responses of specific bronchiectasis phenotypes and severities to different ACTs, and compare walking with huffing with a range of ACTs, both when stable and during an exacerbation [27, 49].

This research supports the use of walking with huffing as a stand‐alone ACT for adults hospitalised with an exacerbation.
