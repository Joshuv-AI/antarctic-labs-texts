Unmet need for biomarkers in chronic cough: a review of current challenges and future directions
Authors: Wafa Hassan, Feng Wei, Ruchong Chen, Imran Satia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The diagnosis, investigation and management of chronic cough remains a challenge for patients and physicians. Patients with chronic cough can spend many years being investigated for possible underlying conditions and have numerous therapies before a diagnosis of refractory and unexplained chronic cough (RCC/UCC) is made. Recognising that RCC/UCC is a distinct disease with underlying neuro-pathological features which commonly present with clinical features of cough hypersensitivity syndrome would be key to improved management of these patients. Biomarkers are used across various specialities to aid diagnosis, direct treatment and monitor treatment response; however, at present, no such biomarkers exist for RCC/UCC. Biomarkers are needed for RCC/UCC that could potentially be used to identify and predict treatment response. Advances in the mechanisms and therapies for refractory chronic cough targeting the peripheral nerves expressing the P2X3 receptor, and opioid pathways in the central nervous system have provided hope for developing novel biomarkers. However, the development of biomarkers remains a challenging process and there is a need for ongoing research to address the lack of evidence in this area.

Introduction

Chronic cough is a common presentation in both primary and secondary care settings which can be challenging to investigate and manage. Chronic cough is defined as a cough lasting for 8 weeks or longer, and has a global prevalence of between 2% and 18% due to large regional variations [1]. The prevalence in western countries is twice as high in women, often peaking in the 50–60-year age group, but in China and South Korea, the sex distribution is more balanced, and more prevalent in younger individuals [2–8]. The consequences for the patients’ health are significant both physically, socially and psychologically. Patients can suffer from embarrassing symptoms such as stress incontinence, experience chest discomfort and in extreme cases suffer from cough syncope and rib fractures. The stigma of having a chronic cough leads to social isolation and consequently can have a negative impact on a person psychologically. The combination of these factors leads to an overall poor quality of life [9].

The journey to investigate and manage chronic cough, culminating in a diagnosis of refractory chronic cough, is often prolonged, challenging, and fraught with treatment failures and inconclusive investigations [10]. There are multiple potential treatable traits that can cause chronic cough. These include asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), neutrophilic or eosinophilic inflammation, and vocal cord dysfunction (VCD)/inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO) [9, 11–13]. Despite investigations, if no underlying condition is identified or if there is inadequate resolution of chronic cough, then a diagnosis of refractory chronic cough (RCC)/unexplained chronic cough (UCC) is made [9]. Based on the current definition, because RCC/UCC is a diagnosis of exclusion, there is a prolonged duration until the diagnosis is reached, and patients often go through multiple investigations and have tried various treatments with limited relief. By the time they see a specialist, these patients are frustrated, anxious about the cause of their coughing and can feel hopeless about future therapeutic options [13, 14]. In an ideal clinical pathway, if there was a biomarker that would support the diagnosis of RCC/UCC, then the patient journey could potentially be streamlined, with a faster time to diagnosis, reduced burden on healthcare utilisation related to investigations and unnecessary trials of treatment.

Biomarkers are widely used in various disease processes as they can be used to diagnose, evaluate severity of a disease, identify disease phenotypes, predict treatment response and also predict prognosis [15]. Biomarkers are effectively used in many specialties, across both the acute and chronic setting, but at present, no such biomarkers exist for RCC/UCC.

Although biomarkers hold promise for improving the diagnosis and management of chronic cough, they should not be viewed as a substitute for a detailed clinical history. The initial assessment remains pivotal in distinguishing primary from secondary causes of chronic cough, identifying patients with coexisting conditions. However, in cases of RCC/UCC, clinical history alone may be insufficient to guide therapy. Given that many patients have a history of failed treatments and normal standard investigations, biomarkers may provide an objective tool to confirm the diagnosis and predict response to novel therapies. Thus, rather than replacing clinical assessment, biomarkers should be integrated into a structured diagnostic framework that enhances rather than overrides clinical reasoning.

The aim of this article is to explore and justify the need for biomarkers in chronic cough. We provide a brief overview of the different types of biomarkers, question why such biomarkers are needed, and discuss possible biomarkers and the challenges that we may encounter in the future in the development and use of biomarkers in chronic cough.

Advancing refractory and unexplained chronic cough as a disease

While chronic cough is a heterogeneous condition with multiple underlying causes, the landscape of research is shifting towards the recognition of chronic cough as a distinct entity with shared pathophysiological mechanisms across various phenotypes, and a treatable trait approach is used in clinical practice [9, 13]. The concept of cough hypersensitivity syndrome (CHS) has been instrumental in understanding why patients with RCC/UCC fail to respond to traditional therapies. A critical unmet need in the field is the identification of biomarkers that can stratify patients into specific phenotypes or endotypes, allowing for more precise targeted therapeutic approaches. Moving beyond the conventional multiple traits model, future research should integrate emerging biomarkers that reflect neuronal dysfunction, airway inflammation and sensory processing abnormalities in the central nervous system. Incorporating neuroimaging techniques, such as functional MRI (fMRI) studies, and peripheral markers of neurogenic inflammation or epithelial injury could advance the paradigm from one of diagnostic exclusion to precision medicine.

Cough frequency and sound analysis

24-h cough monitoring, used mainly in a clinical trial setting, measures 24-h cough frequency, and daytime and night-time cough frequency and potentially serves as an objective biomarker that tracks the severity of cough. Cough frequency is an important end-point that has recently been identified as a predictive biomarker [17]. The frequency of cough can be used as a surrogate marker to predict response to treatment, i.e., the higher the cough frequency, the better the potential drug efficacy. This has been demonstrated in some recent phase 2 clinical trials [16, 18]. At present the interpretability, variability, daily usability and optimum end-points for continuous cough monitoring over longer periods are being studied in clinical trials [19]. This could be used as a diagnostic or predictive biomarker but more evidence is required for this.

Cough is typically measured as number of coughs per hour over a 24-h period; patients often cannot perceive or remember individual coughs but often can recall the frequency and duration of cough bouts as most impactful [20, 21]. Cough bouts are multiple coughs clustered together, separated by a short interval, and appear as a single cough episode [2]. Holt et al. [22] have recently attempted to define which duration of cough bouts (ranging from 0.5-s to 10-s intervals) best correlates with cough severity visual analogue scale (VAS). When isolated single coughs were excluded and only multi-cough bouts analysed, there was a stronger correlation with cough severity VAS scores when a time window of ≤3 s is used [22]. As a result, cough bouts may be used as a possible diagnostic and predictive biomarker; however, there is still no consensus on the definition of cough bout, and it has only been used as an end-point in a few studies when compared to cough frequency [23, 24].

The acoustic characteristics of cough sounds are increasingly being investigated as potential diagnostic biomarkers for differentiating acute and chronic diseases such as COPD, asthma, interstitial lung disease (ILD) and bronchiectasis, and even heart failure [25]. Advanced machine learning algorithms may be capable of discerning subtle acoustic alterations in cough sounds that elude the human auditory perception. In the future, it may be possible to distinguish a cough that is caused by asthma from one that is due to cough hypersensitivity [26].

Molecular biomarkers

Genetic biomarkers have been considered; however, no specific gene abnormality has as yet been discovered, and it is unlikely that RCC/UCC is a unique monogenic disease due to a single gene mutation. The only exception to this is cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS), an inherited autosomal recessive disease in which chronic cough is a common symptom. If there is suspicion of this underlying condition, then genetic testing for expansions of an intronic AAGGG repeat in the RFC1 gene can be done and serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker in a select few [27].

It is possible that epigenetic factors or multiple gene variants may combine to increase the risk of RCC. Recent examples of this are the polygenic risk scores identified for asthma, ILD and COPD [28, 29]. Coley et al. [30] have recently published a genome-wide association study (GWAS) and found chronic dry cough and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors-associated cough to be associated with seven novel variants which mapped to 10 novel genes, many of which implicated neuronal functions (CTNNA1, KCNA10, MAPKAP1, OR4C12, OR4C13, SIL1) [30]. This study also identified those people who are genetically more prone to cough to have higher risk of comorbid conditions such as fibromyalgia, urinary incontinence, migraine and irritable bowel syndrome.

Reflex sensitivity: capsaicin, ATP and hypo-osmolar challenges

Cough challenges such as the capsaicin, hypo-osmolar, and citric acid challenges are mainly used as a research tool to study mechanisms of action and target engagement [31], but may have the potential to be used as a possible predictive or diagnostic biomarker. Lower capsaicin cough reflex sensitivity, which is suggestive of more sensitive airway nerves, was associated with COPD hospitalisation [32], poor asthma control [33] and exacerbations of asthma [34]. Cough challenges have also shown evidence of distinct neuro-phenotypes in airway disease which showed varying sensitivities of different airway diseases to different inhaled challenges [35]. This study found that patients with asthma and chronic cough were more likely to have a heightened cough sensitivity to both capsaicin and citric acid as compared to patients with COPD who are sensitive to capsaicin only. This highlights the importance of different neuro-phenotypes that can be demonstrated by cough challenges. This suggests that cough challenge could be used as a diagnostic test for phenotypes of chronic cough and also allow development of targeted therapies based on underling neurophenotype [33, 35].

It was hoped that demonstrating this heightened and exaggerated coughing to inhaled stimuli like capsaicin and citric acid would then lead to suppression of spontaneous objective coughing, when the specific ion channel, e.g., TRPV1 and TRPA1, was blocked. However, to date, double-blind randomised placebo-controlled studies blocking TRPV1 and TRPA1 have failed to improve 24-h cough frequency [36–39]. This is despite one study demonstrating significant reduction in capsaicin-evoked coughs meaning that there was target engagement [36]. These negative studies thus questioned the utility of performing cough challenges to identify novel potential targets for drug development. However, the counter-argument was that perhaps these ion channels are not important in the pathology of RCC, and hence unsurprising that cough challenge targeting those ion channels failed to improve coughing. Supporters of this would argue that recent cough challenge data from ATP-evoked coughing and suppression with gefapixant corroborates this.

Gefapixant and camlipixant are P2X3 antagonists which reduce cough by blocking ATP from binding to the P2X3 receptor [40]. As such, one might speculate that ATP-evoked coughs could predict patients who might benefit more from P2X3 antagonism. However, there have been recent reports of adverse events with use of ATP due to the development of bronchoconstriction and hypoxia in a few participants at high doses [40–42]. An alternative cough challenge might be hypo-osmolar cough challenge, which in a recent study did not cause bronchoconstriction and did differentiate well between RCC and healthy volunteers, and potentially indirectly induce the release of ATP via activation of TRPV4 and release of intracellular ATP via the pannexin channel [41, 43].

Notwithstanding the scientific arguments for and against performing cough challenges, there are also operational issues in conducting cough challenges. These include sourcing capsaicin, citric acid and pH buffered hypo-osmolar solution that is produced to Good Manufacturing Practices standards and supplied with a certificate of analysis that demonstrates no bacterial or fungal contamination to European pharmacopoeia (Ph.Eur) standards. Some countries require regulatory approval for inhalational challenges whilst others may only require research ethics board approval. Second, delivering a specific dose/concentration of solution requires availability of a nebuliser system (including nebuliser pots) which is approved in the respective countries. The previously used devilbiss 646 nebuliser (fixed baffle with custom inspiratory flow-limiting valve pots) with KoKo dosimeter is no longer available, so alternatives that can calibrate the dose delivered to the lungs will need to be used. Third, there are differences in methodology where some centres are using tidal breathing compared to deep inhalation to total lung capacity, or single breath at each dose compared to four breaths at each dose with 30-s intervals. Fourth, there is still some ongoing debate about what should be the optimum end-point for cough challenge: either the concentration/dose causing two coughs (C2) or five coughs (C5), or maximum evoked coughs (Emax) or dose evoking half the Emax (ED50) [44]. The C2/C5 end-points demonstrate considerable variability and thus do not discriminate well between diseases, but the challenge can be stopped when C2 or C5 is reached, so the duration of the test is shorter. In contrast, going to Emax (or maximum tolerated dose) can take up to 30 min. It should be noted that performing the full dose response cough challenge does allow extraction of C2/C5 values. Finally, recently there have been studies conducted using mannitol dry powder to demonstrate cough hypersensitivity, and many authors will use mannitol cough dose ratio (CDR/100 mg) by adding up all the coughs during the mannitol challenge, then dividing by the cumulative dose delivered and multiplying by 100 [45].

The measurement of ATP and the ectonucleotide enzymes CD39L and CD73 in the blood and airways has recently been performed [46]. Measuring ATP is challenging as it is rapidly broken down extracellularly, making it difficult to quantify, and somewhat counter-intuitively, lower levels of ATP but higher levels of CD39L were found in RCC patients compared to healthy controls [46, 47]. The elevated enzymes detected are likely to be a compensatory mechanism to tightly regulate extracellular ATP concentration. In the future, it would be worthwhile investigating whether levels of ATP, CD39L or CD76 would be predictive of a better response to P2X3 antagonists [40].

Neuroimaging

fMRI could be used as a predictive biomarker to identify those who might benefit most from centrally acting neuromodulators for RCC/UCC. fMRI studies using capsaicin to induce urge to cough sensations suggest amplification of signals in the brainstem or reduced activity in the pre-frontal cortex suggestive of impaired inhibitory controls [48]. More recently, resting steady state fMRI without any inhalation of capsaicin has demonstrated reduced activity in the sensorimotor network compared to healthy controls [49]. Notwithstanding the costs and accessibility of performing fMRI, the question remains whether fMRI can be used to identify patients with specific areas of high or low activity in RCC which can predict response to opioids or if there are changes in signal activity after administration of peripheral blockers like P2X3 antagonists [50]. However, this would require performing fMRI at resting steady state and also after treatment with central or peripheral antagonists.

Quality of life and trigger burden

Quality of life questionnaires such as the Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) assess the impact on the quality of life and severity of symptoms for those with chronic cough; they are widely used in chronic cough studies to monitor treatment response. These could be transitioned to act as a prognostic biomarker in clinical practice to assess severity of cough and monitor treatment response, as they are readily available [51].

Cough triggers can be used as an indicator of cough hypersensitivity. A study has shown an association between non-respiratory, non-mental symptoms (MNNS) and cough hypersensitivity. MNNS includes conditions such as chest pain on exertion, constipation, back pain and aching joints to name a few. A higher number of cough triggers was associated with the presence of these symptoms [52]. The coexistence of these symptoms in patients with chronic cough could indicate a higher prevalence of cough hypersensitivity, and as a result centrally targeted medications could be used earlier on in the management [52, 53].

Biomarkers to differentiate from comorbid diseases

Biomarkers to differentiate comorbid diseases associated with RCC/UCC are also needed. The use of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) as a serum biomarker in chronic cough has previously been explored, alongside eosinophils, exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FENO) and to a lesser extent concentration of alveolar nitric oxide (CANO). These biomarkers are used to help diagnose comorbidities such asthma, cough variant asthma (CVA) and GORD. MMP-9 activity has been associated with increased activity in the blood, induced sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and bronchial biopsies of patients with uncontrolled asthma [54]. FENO is a marker of eosinophilic inflammation and is useful in the diagnosis of asthma. It has recently been endorsed by the British Thoracic Society in a recent clinical statement on chronic cough as part of the diagnostic work up for chronic cough; however, the European Respiratory Society task force do not routinely recommend this. Both societies recognise the lack of evidence for this biomarker in chronic cough [2, 9]; a systematic review demonstrated that FENO has moderate diagnostic accuracy for predicting CVA, nonasthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis and an improvement in cough response to inhaled corticosteroids [55]. In addition, CANO helps assess peripheral airway inflammation in GORD patients, and high CANO indicates potential micro-aspiration and may predict the need for enhanced anti-reflux therapy [56]. Despite the good availability of these biomarkers, their use in clinical practice has had limited success [57].

GORD is another common comorbidity associated with RCC/UCC. In some cases, cough may present as the primary symptom, without the typical reflux-associated symptoms such as heartburn, dyspepsia or regurgitation, making it difficult to exclude GORD as a potential cause of RCC/UCC based on history alone [58]. GORD can be treated empirically if heartburn symptoms are present, and diagnostic tests such as 24-h pH monitoring with high-resolution oesophageal manometry may provide valuable insight [2, 59]. There are emerging studies attempting to identify specific biomarkers in the management of GORD-related symptoms [60].

Biomarkers that predict prognosis have yet to be identified, and a potential reason could be the lack of available data on the natural history of this disease. There are data that suggest that productive cough is associated with a higher risk of death [61].

Challenges in biomarker development for chronic cough

There are many factors to consider when developing a biomarker for RCC/UCC (figure 4). One of the primary challenges is the heterogeneity of chronic cough. There is an array of conditions that present with cough as their primary symptom, including, but not limited to, asthma, COPD, ILD, bronchiectasis, tuberculosis and heart failure. Thus RCC/UCC is a diagnosis of exclusion, and a detailed history and examination alongside relevant investigations are needed prior to reaching a diagnosis [9].

Challenges in identifying biomarkers for chronic cough. Different types of biomarkers need to be sampled at various stages of the diagnostic and longitudinal journey. Diagnostic biomarkers distinguish between refractory chronic cough/unexplained chronic cough (RCC/UCC), resolved/explained cough and healthy individuals. Predictive biomarkers are essential for identifying patients who will respond to current treatments for chronic cough, allowing us to avoid administering ineffective treatments. Prognostic biomarkers help track the natural progression of the disease, providing patients with valuable information and reassurance about their prognosis.

The pathophysiology of cough also poses challenges to the development of biomarkers. It is unlikely that a single receptor or one pathway is heightened or upregulated that can be targeted in patients with RCC/UCC. Multiple pathways involving both the central and peripheral nervous systems probably exist that contribute to cough hypersensitivity making it difficult to target a single pathway or develop an all-encompassing biomarker.

The absence of licensed treatments for UCC/RCC significantly limits our ability to identify biomarkers which predict improvement. To be able to identify a predictive or prognostic biomarker we need a licensed treatment to be effective, and longitudinal data need to be collected from prospective trials to identify responders and non-responders. Lack of availability of such data is a challenge in the development of predictive or prognostic biomarkers.

Biomarkers also need to comply to standardised methodologies, uniform standards and protocols, which are essential for ensuring data comparability and reliability [19]. A delicate balance between the invasiveness of sample collection methods and their feasibility is also crucial. While invasive procedures such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [62] or lung biopsy provide direct access to pulmonary biomarkers, their associated risks and costs render them unsuitable for all chronic cough patients. Consequently, the advancement of noninvasive sampling techniques is imperative for chronic cough biomarker research. Such approaches not only mitigate patient discomfort and risk, but also facilitate longitudinal studies, thereby enhancing the representativeness and reliability of research outcomes.

Future directions

A pivotal focus of future research is the development and validation of noninvasive biomarkers, which are more accessible, better accepted by patients, and reduce the risks and discomfort associated with sample collection. Volatile organic compounds in exhaled breath condensate and proteins in saliva or sputum are potential sources of noninvasive biomarkers. The application of AI and machine learning techniques represents another significant direction in the study of biomarkers for chronic cough. These advanced technologies can process and analyse vast amounts of biomedical data, including genomics, proteomics and metabolomics data, to identify new biomarkers associated with chronic cough. Through pattern recognition and the establishment of predictive models, machine learning helps to unravel the complex relationships between biomarkers and disease and optimises the selection and validation process of biomarkers.

Conclusion

Biomarker research holds significant importance for patient stratification and personalised treatment in chronic cough, yet the demand for such biomarkers remains largely unmet, constraining a deeper understanding of the disease mechanisms and optimisation of therapeutic outcomes.

The challenge is to identify and validate biomarkers for chronic cough with high specificity and sensitivity. Researchers are exploring a variety of biomarkers, including inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress markers, proteins and gene expression profiles [63, 64], in the hope of finding indicators that accurately reflect the pathophysiological changes associated with chronic cough. Furthermore, the role of biomarkers in patient stratification is increasingly recognised [65]. Detection of biomarkers allows for the classification of patients into distinct subtypes, enabling the provision of more precise treatment regimens tailored to each subtype.

In conclusion, research into biomarkers for chronic cough is moving towards less invasive, precise and intelligent approaches. By combining multi-omics data with clinical information and advanced analytical methods, future studies are expected to identify biomarkers with greater clinical relevance. These advancements will enhance the accuracy of diagnosis, guide more effective treatments and improve prognostic assessments, ultimately supporting more personalised and precise therapeutic strategies for chronic cough.
