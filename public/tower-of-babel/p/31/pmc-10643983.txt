INTRODUCTION

Alopecia areata (AA), trichotillomania (TM), nevus sebaceous (NS), and linear scleroderma en coup de sabre (LSCS) in children can all present with a patch of linear alopecia on the scalp, and a definitive differential diagnosis can be difficult to obtain with the naked eye. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Here, we describe for the first time an assessment of children with linear alopecia using a combination of reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) and dermoscopy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Children with linear alopecia treated at the Department of Dermatology at Tianjin Children's Hospital between 2017 and 2022 were included in this retrospective study. A total of 36 patients (19 males and 17 females, age range: 1 to 8 years) were enrolled, of which 14 had AA, seven had TM, nine had NS, and six had LSCS (Figure 1. A1,B1,C1,D1). A definitive diagnosis was made by two independent, clinically experienced dermatologists according to clinical presentations and RCM and dermoscopy findings. All patients included in the study were examined using a commercially available Vivascope 1500 (Lucid Inc., Rochester, NY, USA) and the Dermoscopy‐II desktop dermatoscope from Beijing Demetricom. No participants received systemic or topical treatment before the examination, and all patients signed an informed consent form. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

AA

RCM revealed a normal honeycomb pattern and dermal papillary rings, and the size and density of the follicular openings were decreased. Some miniaturized hair follicles showed round to oval cyst‐like structures containing medium‐ to low‐refractive keratinoid amorphous substances and round, bright cell infiltration around the hair follicles (Figure 1. A3,A4). Dermoscopy detected decreased hair density, yellow dots, black dots, broken hairs, short vellus hairs, exclamation mark hairs, and pigtail hairs（Figure 1. A2).

TM

RCM revealed a generally normal epidermal structure. The size and density of the follicular openings were normal, and in some cases, there was hair in the follicles (Figure 1. B3,B4). Dermoscopy detected irregularly broken hairs of different lengths, irregularly coiled or hooked hairs, branched hair, black dots, and blood spots (Figure 1. B2).

NS

In the epidermis, a round to polygonal honeycomb pattern was revealed by RCM. Scans of deeper skin layers revealed some petal‐like structures, round cyst‐like structures with low refractivity in the center and large bright oval‐shaped structures radially distributed from the central area. Some expanded annular structures densely packed with low‐refractive small matter appeared as frogspawn‐like structures scattered throughout the dermis (Figure 1. C3,C4). Dermoscopy revealed yellowish or light red alopecia, whitish round structures, and bright yellow dots lacking hair follicles (Figure 1. C2).

LSCS

A typical honeycomb pattern was observed in the epidermis by RCM, and the number of scan layers was markedly decreased (epidermal atrophy). The normal dermal papillary rings of the dermal papillae were partially or completely missing at the dermoepidermal junction. In the dermis, miniaturized sebaceous glands and follicle microstructures were detected, and a large quantity of diffuse, disorganized, medium‐refractive fibrous material was present, some of which was gathered in the fascicle (Figure 1. D3,D4). Dermoscopy showed the absence of follicular openings, a whitish skin surface, an atrophic area with white patches, and short thick linear branching tortuous vessels on or at the periphery of the lesion (Figure 1. D2).

DISCUSSION

Pediatric alopecia patients often present with localized alopecic foci on the scalp, which often indicate diseases different from those that occur in adults. These diseases in children, including AA, TM, NS, and LSCS, can be congenital or acquired, and the variability in their clinical appearances complicates the diagnosis. 5 , 6 Scalp biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosis but is not readily accepted in the pediatric population. RCM and dermoscopy can be considered an intermediate step between clinical and histological analysis in the evaluation of alopecia diseases and can provide clues for diagnosis. 7 , 8

RCM visualizes horizontal planes from the epidermis to the upper dermis at a cellular resolution, which can reveal the skin and skin appendage structures, including hair follicles and perifollicular epidermis, whereas dermoscopy amplifies the optical observation of skin lesions. 9 AA, TM, NS, and LSCS all showed normal honeycomb patterns in the epidermis, and the key feature for differential diagnosis was the dermal. In AA, the hair follicular density was decreased, and miniaturized hair follicles showed round to oval cyst‐like structures. The size and density of the follicular openings were normal in TM. In NS, the major features were petal‐like and frogspawn‐like structures. In LSCS, dermal papillary rings, sebaceous glands, and follicles were partially or completely missing, and abundant fibrous material was distributed in the dermis. Dermoscopy revealed alopecia and decreased hair density in all four conditions. AA patients demonstrated yellow dots, black dots, and exclamation mark hair. TM patients presented with irregularly broken hairs and blood spots. NS and LSCS patients showed an absence of follicular openings; NS patients demonstrated whitish and yellowish round structures, while an atrophic area with white patches, linear vessels and no yellow or black dots were observed in LSCS patients. CONCLUSION

Accurate early diagnosis is important for guiding optimal treatment and follow‐up in pediatric alopecia patients; however, each examination method has limitations. Therefore, we suggest that RCM combined with dermoscopy could provide more information on the status of the disease, significantly improving the success of noninvasive assessments for alopecia in pediatrics.
