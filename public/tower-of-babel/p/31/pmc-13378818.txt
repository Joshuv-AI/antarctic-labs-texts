The scavenger hunt game: An innovative way to augment peer-assisted learning among medical undergraduates for competency attainment
Authors: Ishita Manral, Deepanjan Dey, Manu Chopra, M. Sarala, C. S. Makhani, R. N. Khan, Adarsh Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Medical education is undergoing rapid reform, especially with the introduction of Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) and the emergence of Gen Z learners who prefer interactive, and self-directed learning environments. Traditional lectures, particularly for fact-heavy topics like Environmental Toxicology, often fail to engage students. Gamification, such as scavenger hunts, may offer a more engaging and effective alternative. This study evaluates the impact of gamification versus didactic teaching on cognitive learning outcomes and student engagement.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A randomized controlled study was conducted among 44 Phase III Part I MBBS students, equally divided into two groups. One group received a traditional lecture on Environmental Toxicology, while the other engaged in a gamified scavenger hunt covering the same content. Pre- and post-tests assessed knowledge gain, and feedback was collected via Likert-scale items and open-ended questions. The gamified activity was collaboratively designed by departments of Forensic Medicine, Respiratory Medicine, and Medical Education, aligned with CBME competencies (FM13.1).RESULTS:Both groups showed significant improvement in post-test scores. The gamification group improved from a mean score of 1.86 to 6.09 (mean gain: +4.23; Cohen’s d = 2.99), while the lecture group improved from 2.32 to 4.00 (mean gain: +1.68; Cohen’s d = 1.27). Thematic analysis of feedback revealed high engagement, preference for interactive formats, and enhanced teamwork. Students appreciated the novelty and peer-learning aspects of the scavenger hunt, though they also suggested improvements in time management and instruction clarity.CONCLUSION:Gamification, specifically scavenger hunts, significantly enhances cognitive outcomes and student engagement in undergraduate medical education compared to didactic lectures. While not a replacement, gamified methods serve as powerful adjuncts—particularly for content-heavy, low-engagement topics. Future implementations should focus on optimizing design and addressing logistical challenges to maximize educational impact.

Introduction

The evolving landscape of medical education is significantly influenced by curriculum changes and the unique profile of its learners—predominantly Gen Z students born between the mid-1990s and the early 2010s. Digital fluency is a hallmark of Gen Z learners, who favor visual and interactive content, require quick feedback, and excel in active, self-directed, and collaborative learning settings.[123]

Since its 2019 debut, CBME has revolutionized medical education by shifting from a teacher-centered, schedule-driven model to a student-focused, outcome-based system that prioritizes clinically relevant, integrated learning and core competencies like communication and professionalism.[45] Success, however, depends on more than just curriculum changes; it requires a complete rethinking of teaching methods to meet the diverse learning styles of today’s students.

Research shows traditional lectures are ineffective for Gen Z, particularly in subjects like Environmental Toxicology, which often results in poor student engagement and knowledge retention.[6] The excitement of gamified activities, such as scavenger hunts, boosts active learning, strengthens understanding, and fosters collaboration.[278] Gamification’s popularity notwithstanding, critics point to inconsistent outcomes, heightened competitive stress, and the potential for distraction from the main subject matter as significant drawbacks. This study investigates the relative effectiveness of didactic lectures versus gamification in undergraduate medical education. By examining pre- and post-test results and student feedback, the study measures how each method affects students’ learning and engagement. This study also explores whether a gamified scavenger hunt would be a practical and acceptable teaching method within Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME).

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A randomised controlled study was conducted among undergraduate medical students to compare two pedagogical approaches: traditional didactic lectures and gamification (in the form of a scavenger hunt). The study’s data included both quantitative measures (pre- and post-test scores) and qualitative student feedback as summarised in Figure 1 and Table 1.

Study participants and settings

Forty-four undergraduate medical students from the first part of their third year voluntarily participated in the study. Researchers randomly allocated the participants into two equal groups (n = 22 each). Participants were randomly assigned into two equal groups (n = 22 each) using a computer-generated random sequence (or random number table/drawing of lots). This method ensured that each student had an equal chance of being placed in either the control group or the intervention group, thereby reducing allocation bias. All Phase III (Part 1) undergraduate MBBS students enrolled during the study block who were had not yet received formal teaching in sociomedical toxicology, were available for the full session, and provided written informed consent were eligible for inclusion. Students were excluded if they declined consent, were absent on the day of the intervention.

The control group received a standard lecture, whereas the test group engaged in a gamified scavenger hunt covering identical material. The sheer amount of factual information in environmental toxicology would have made a didactic lecture on the subject tedious. To make it more engaging, we gamified the learning process. As shown in Table 2, the group leader selected one chit from those placed on the table. The group then identified the corresponding table set up in the classroom, solved the questions there as in Figure 2, and proceeded to the next table. The top three teams in the hunt each received a small prize. With open-book access, the activity concluded with a debrief to solidify key concepts.

Quantitative findings

A total of 44 undergraduate students were randomly assigned to two groups (22 each). Pre- and post-test comparisons showed significant knowledge gains in both groups. The gamification group improved from a mean score of 1.86 to 6.09 (mean gain: +4.23, Cohen’s d = 2.99), while the didactic group improved from 2.32 to 4.00 (mean gain: +1.68, Cohen’s d = 1.27). Both gains were statistically significant (P < 0.001), with a markedly larger effect size in the gamification group.

Results from a paired samples t-test showed that post-test scores significantly improved in both the scavenger hunt and didactic groups, proving both teaching methods effective. The scavenger hunt group demonstrated a markedly greater improvement (t = 14.04, df = 21, P < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.99), suggesting a very strong educational impact. The Didactic group also showed significant score enhancement (t = 5.96, df = 21, P < 0.001), though with a smaller effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.27), indicating a moderate to large effect. Prior to conducting the t-tests, the assumption of normality of the difference scores was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The results confirmed that the score differences were approximately normally distributed in both groups (Scavenger Hunt: P = 0.220; Didactic: P = 0.063), thus validating the appropriateness of the paired samples t-test for this analysis as in Table 5.

Discussion

Engaging the digital-native Gen Z learner remains a significant challenge in medical education.[9] This study investigated the effectiveness of a gamified scavenger hunt approach to teaching environmental toxicology, a subject typically taught via traditional lectures. Compared to traditional methods, our research indicates that gamification substantially boosts cognitive learning and student engagement.

A randomized controlled trial, including pre- and post-test assessments, allowed for an objective comparison of learning gains between the gamified and didactic groups. The gamification cohort demonstrated a substantially greater improvement in test scores (mean gain +4.23) relative to the didactic group (+1.68), with a very large effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.99). These results corroborate existing evidence supporting gamification’s effectiveness in improving knowledge retention.[10111213]

We gained valuable insights into the learner experience through qualitative feedback. According to a thematic analysis of student responses, the scavenger hunt was deemed interactive, unique, and memorable. These findings align with previous studies, such as those by Burgess et al., which reported learner preference for active over passive instructional formats.[14] Similarly, Huang et al. emphasized gamified environments’ capacity to foster deeper engagement and promote peer learning. Nevertheless, consistent with other studies observations., participants highlighted the need for clearer instructions and better time allocation, underscoring the importance of careful instructional design in gamification.[15] Importantly, learners also suggested that gamified methods should complement rather than replace traditional teaching.[1213141516171819]

Consistent with previous literature,[1113] students reported high levels of engagement and enjoyment, describing the gamified session as a refreshing alternative to routine lectures. However, not all learners favoured gamification unequivocally; some questioned its academic rigor and preferred conventional lectures for theoretical content, viewing gamification as more suited to application-heavy or repetitive material.[12131415]

Collaboration and teamwork emerged as key benefits, with students learning “with and from peers” through group problem-solving.[111213141516] Motivation and self-direction were also prominent, as students expressed personal investment in puzzle-solving, which enhanced information retention and curiosity-driven learning.[514] Such intrinsic motivation is central to CBME, which prioritizes learner-centred education in both policy and practice.

Students recommended minor logistical improvements, notably clearer instructions and more time per puzzle, which should be addressed to optimize future implementations.[14] Students recommended minor logistical improvements, notably clearer instructions and more time per puzzle, which should be addressed to optimize future implementations.[14] Despite these suggestions, gamified learning experiences, when well designed, provide highly engaging and emotionally resonant educational encounters. The interplay of competition, collaboration, and curiosity appears to enhance both knowledge retention and learner satisfaction.

A randomized controlled design and the use of both quantitative and qualitative data are key strengths of this study, leading to a complete evaluation of gamification’s impact on education. A notable feature is the use of a multidisciplinary team—comprising experts from forensic medicine, respiratory medicine, and medical education—to design and implement the intervention. Applying game mechanics to the teaching of Environmental Toxicology, a traditionally didactic and non-clinical subject, offers new perspectives and broadens the range of interactive teaching methods in medical education. The mixed-methods approach reveals both quantifiable learning improvements and the valuable qualitative insights into student experiences, thus guiding instructional improvements.

Limitation and recommendation

However, it’s important to acknowledge certain constraints. The study was conducted within a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Moreover, short-term learning outcome assessments don’t evaluate long-term retention and practical clinical use. While the qualitative feedback offered valuable insights, further research using focus groups or interviews would provide a deeper understanding of learner needs and enhance gamification design.

Gamification, such as scavenger hunts, should be incorporated as an adjunct to traditional lectures for fact-heavy CBME topics to enhance engagement and learning. Clearer instructions, structured timing, and debrief sessions are recommended to optimize outcomes. Larger, multi-institutional studies are needed to assess long-term retention and broader applicability.

Conclusion

This research adds to the mounting evidence that gamified learning, especially using scavenger hunts, significantly improves learning and engagement in undergraduate medical education. Compared to traditional didactic lectures, the gamified approach not only led to higher post-test scores but also fostered greater student motivation, collaboration, and active participation. The method’s effectiveness in transforming passive learners into active problem-solvers, along with its creativity and acceptability, was highlighted in the thematic analysis of student feedback. Importantly, by applying gamification to a non-clinical, public health topic like environmental toxicology, this study shows its versatility and value across diverse domains of the medical curriculum. The continued reshaping of medical education in India by CBME necessitates the increasing integration of such innovative, student-centered strategies to meet modern learners’ evolving needs.
