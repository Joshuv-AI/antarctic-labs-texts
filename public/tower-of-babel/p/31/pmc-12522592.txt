Association of metabolic score for insulin resistance and preeclampsia: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Qiong Li, Jing Yan, Chenyang Zhao, Meng Li, Ying Gu, Bingshun Wang, Yangjing Pang, Chaoyan Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) is a novel indicator used to assess insulin resistance. Previous studies have shown that it is associated with an increased risk of hypertension. However, its relationship with preeclampsia (PE) remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the association between METS-IR and PE.MethodsA total of 39,693 singleton pregnant women from three hospitals in China between January 2018 and June 2024 were included in the study. Data were collected through the hospital information system, and METS-IR was calculated using the following formula: ln([HDL-C (mg/dL)] × [2 × fasting glucose (mg/dL)] + TG (mg/dL) × BMI (kg/m2)). ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of METS-IR and identify an optimal threshold. Participants were grouped based on METS-IR quartiles and this threshold value. Smooth curve fitting, multivariable logistic regression models, and subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between METS-IR and PE.ResultsThe results showed a nonlinear dose–response relationship between METS-IR and PE. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.649, with an optimal cutoff value of 38.644. After adjusting for potential confounders, higher METS-IR levels were significantly associated with increased risk of PE. Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the odds ratios (ORs) for PE in the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups were 1.19 (95% CI: 1.00–1.42), 1.75 (95% CI: 1.48–2.05), and 2.44 (95% CI: 2.09–2.84), respectively. When stratified by the threshold, METS-IR ≥ 38.6 was significantly associated with both PE (OR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.72–2.11) and preterm birth (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.0–1.27) compared to METS-IR < 38.6. Subgroup analyses indicated that the association remained consistent across different age, test week, and parity.ConclusionMETS-IR is significantly and positively associated with the risk of PE and demonstrates moderate predictive ability. It may serve as a useful tool for early identification of pregnant women at high risk for PE in clinical practice.Keywords: METS-IR, Insulin resistance, Preeclampsia, Cohort study

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a serious complication of pregnancy, affecting approximately 5% to 8% of pregnancies [1, 2]. It is primarily characterized by the onset of hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation [3]. PE can lead to multi-organ dysfunction in pregnant women, preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, and neonatal asphyxia, with potentially life-threatening consequences for both mother and infant [4, 5]. Moreover, PE has been associated with an increased long-term risk of cardiovascular diseases in both mothers and their offspring. As a placenta-related disorder, the pathological changes underlying PE begin early in pregnancy, often before the appearance of clinical symptoms. Studies have shown that metabolic abnormalities such as increased insulin resistance (IR) can be detected months before the clinical manifestation of PE [6, 7]. Therefore, identifying reliable markers of early pregnancy IR and enabling early risk stratification could play a critical role in the prevention of PE and improvement of perinatal outcomes.

The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (EHC) technique is considered the gold standard for assessing IR. However, its invasive nature and technical complexity limit its use in routine clinical screening [8]. The metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), developed by Bello-Chavolla et al., is a novel cardiometabolic risk assessment model that incorporates commonly available clinical parameters, including fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and body mass index (BMI) [9]. Researches have demonstrated that METS-IR outperforms EHC in evaluating insulin sensitivity [9], with higher reproducibility and ease of calculation, making it a promising tool for assessing IR. Recent cohort studies have consistently linked elevated METS-IR with an increased risk of hypertension [10–14]. However, there is currently no consensus on the effect size of METS-IR on the risk of developing hypertension, with reported odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 1.58 to 3.89. Morever, no study has yet investigated the relationship between METS-IR and PE.

This study aims to evaluate the association between METS-IR and the risk of PE and to explore its potential as a predictive tool for PE. We utilized a multicenter pregnancy cohort and applied smoothed fitting curves and multivariate logistic regression to evaluate the independent effect of METS-IR on the risk of developing PE. Subgroup analyses were conducted to determine whether age, test week of METS-IR, and parity had any interactive effects. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to explore its potential as a predictive tool for PE. The findings may provide clinicians with a simple and practical method for early identification of high-risk pregnancies.

Methods

Study design

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted between January 2019 and June 2024. The study population was drawn from three medical centers: the huangpu branch (Center 1) and yangpu branch (Center 2) of the obstetrics and gynecology hospital affiliated with fudan university, and the first people’ hospital of chenzhou (Center 3). A total of 39,693 singleton pregnancies were included, among which 2,185 women were diagnosed with PE, and 37,508 had uncomplicated pregnancies. Baseline clinical data and laboratory tests were collected during the first antenatal visit. All data were extracted from the hospital information system (HIS) and laboratory information system (LIS) of each participating institution.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) singleton pregnancy; and (3) delivery at any of the participating hospitals. Exclusion criteria included: (1) first measurement of FPG, TG, or HDL-C after 20 weeks of gestation; (2) multiple gestations; (3) pre-existing diabetes or other endocrine disorders (e.g., type 2 diabetes or thyroid dysfunction); (4) chronic hypertension, cardiovascular disease, renal disease, or respiratory disease before pregnancy; (5) incomplete maternal or neonatal clinical records; and (6) individuals who did not sign the informed consent form. The participant selection process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of study participants

The study was approved by the ethics committees of the obstetrics and gynecology hospital affiliated with fudan university and the first people’ hospital of chenzhou. All participants provided broad informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the declaration of Helsinki.

Variables and measurements

The primary exposure was the METS-IR. Demographic and clinical data were collected by trained healthcare professionals and included maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol consumption), history of chronic diseases (e.g., hypertension and diabetes), and educational level. Laboratory measurements were obtained from the first prenatal examination before 20 weeks of gestation. Participants fasted for at least 8 h before blood samples were drawn, and biochemical parameters including FPG (mmol/L), TG (mmol/L), HDL-C (mmol/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), and creatinine (Cr, μmol/L) were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer. METS-IR was calculated using the formula: ln([HDL-C (mg/dL)] × [2 × fasting glucose (mg/dL)] + TG (mg/dL) × BMI (kg/m2)), where BMI was calculated as pre-pregnancy weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) [9]. In this study, METS-IR scores ranged from 21.580 to 58.431 and were categorized into quartiles: Q1 (21.580–32.182), Q2 (32.182–36.101), Q3 (36.101–40.910), and Q4 (40.910–58.431). Postpartum maternal and neonatal clinical data, including gestational age at delivery and birth weight, were also collected.

Based on prior literature and clinical relevance, the following covariates were considered: age, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), gestational week at sampling, Cr, ALT, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, adverse pregnancy history, parity, smoking status, alcohol use, in vitro fertilization (IVF), and education level. Age was dichotomized as < 35 and ≥ 35 years according to World Health Organization guidelines [15]. Adverse pregnancy history was defined as a history of spontaneous abortion or other major obstetric complications. Educational level was classified into five categories: postgraduate or above, undergraduate, associate degree, senior high school, and junior high school or below.

Outcomes and measurements

The primary outcome was PE. PE was defined as new-onset hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg on two occasions at least 4 h apart) after 20 weeks of gestation in previously normotensive women, accompanied by either proteinuria (urinary dipstick ≥ 2 + or 24-h urine protein > 300 mg) or evidence of end-organ dysfunction, including thrombocytopenia (< 100 × 109/L), hepatic injury (serum transaminases > twice the upper limit of normal), renal impairment (serum creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL or twice the upper limit of normal), pulmonary edema, or new-onset headache or visual disturbances not attributable to other causes [1].

The secondary outcome was preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation [16]. For women with regular menstrual cycles, gestational age was calculated based on the last menstrual period. For those with irregular cycles, gestational age was estimated using early ultrasound findings.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to the presence or absence of PE. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), while skewed variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Group comparisons were performed using weighted t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

First, a generalized additive model (GAM) with smoothing splines was used to explore the nonlinear relationship between METS-IR and the risk of PE. Second, ROC curves were constructed to assess the predictive performance of METS-IR for PE, and the area under the curve (AUC) and optimal cutoff value were determined. METS-IR was then categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. Additionally, METS-IR was dichotomized based on the ROC-derived cutoff, with the lower group as the reference. Two models were built according to the STROBE statement [17]: Model I was unadjusted, and Model II was adjusted for potential confounders including age, BBP, DBP, test week, Cr, ALT, hypertension history, diabetes history, adverse pregnancy history, parity, tobacco, alcohol, IVF, and education levels. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate the association between METS-IR and the risks of PE and preterm birth, with results expressed as ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses were conducted stratifying by age (< 35 vs. ≥ 35 years), test week (< 14 vs. ≥ 14 weeks), and parity (primipara vs. multipara) to examine potential effect modification. Interaction terms were tested using likelihood ratio tests. A P-value > 0.05 indicated no significant interaction, while P < 0.05 suggested possible heterogeneity across subgroups.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 21.0, Armonk, NY) and R software (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two-sided tests were applied throughout, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

ROC analysis

ROC analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of METS-IR for PE. The AUC was 0.649 (95% CI: 0.636–0.662) (Fig. 3). Using the Youden index, the optimal cutoff value for METS-IR was determined to be 38.644, with a specificity of 65.89% and sensitivity of 56.41%.

ROC curve for METS-IR to predict the risk of preecalmpsia in all participants

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Table 2 summarizes the associations between METS-IR and the risks of PE and preterm birth. Participants were categorized into quartiles based on METS-IR, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. In unadjusted model I, individuals in the higher METS-IR quartiles showed significantly increased odds of developing PE compared to those in Q1 (P < 0.05). After adjusting for potential confounders in model II, all higher quartiles remained significantly associated with increased PE risk. Compared to Q1, the adjusted ORs for PE were 1.19 (95% CI: 1.00–1.42) for Q2, 1.75 (95% CI: 1.48–2.05) for Q3, and 2.44 (95% CI: 2.09–2.84) for Q4. When stratified by the ROC-derived cutoff value of 38.6, METS-IR ≥ 38.6 was significantly associated with higher PE risk. In model I, the OR was 1.48 (95% CI: 1.27–1.72), and in model II, it was 1.90 (95% CI: 1.72–2.11).

Subgroup analysis

To further assess the robustness of the association between METS-IR and PE, subgroup analyses were conducted according to age, test week of METS-IR, and parity. Results are summarized in Table 3. Stratification by age showed that the association between METS-IR and PE was significant in both women aged < 35 years and ≥ 35 years. In the < 35 years group, the highest quartile (Q4) was associated with a 2.37-fold increase in PE risk compared to Q1 (OR = 2.37, 95% CI: 2.00–2.81, P < 0.0001). In the ≥ 35 years group, the corresponding OR was 2.84 (95% CI: 1.92–4.21, P < 0.0001). When stratified by test week of METS-IR (< 14 vs. ≥ 14 weeks), the association remained significant in both subgroups. For < 14 weeks, the OR for Q4 versus Q1 was 2.34 (95% CI: 1.99–2.75, P < 0.0001); for ≥ 14 weeks, the OR was 2.63 (95% CI: 1.48–4.67, P < 0.0001). Stratification by parity also confirmed the consistency of the association. In nulliparous women, the OR for Q4 versus Q1 was 2.50 (95% CI: 2.12–2.95, P < 0.0001). In multiparous women, the OR was 2.07 (95% CI: 1.35–3.17, P < 0.0001). No statistically significant interactions were observed across any subgroups (all interaction P > 0.05), suggesting that the positive association between METS-IR and PE risk was consistent regardless of age, test week, or parity.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study involving 39,693 pregnant women from three hospitals in China, we investigated the association between METS-IR and the risk of PE. Our findings revealed an approximately linear dose–response relationship between METS-IR and PE risk. The AUC for METS-IR in predicting PE was 0.649. After adjusting for multiple confounders, elevated METS-IR remained significantly associated with increased PE risk, indicating its potential as an independent predictor. Subgroup analyses consistently showed a positive association across different age, test week, and parity (interaction P > 0.05). These results suggest that METS-IR, a simple and non-invasive metabolic assessment tool, holds promise for early risk stratification and identification of women at high risk for PE.

Emerging evidence indicates that women with PE exhibit significant metabolic disturbances, including elevated levels of biomarkers related to IR compared to those with normal pregnancies [18]. Further studies have confirmed that enhanced IR during early and mid-pregnancy may contribute to the development of PE [6, 7]. Although the EHC is considered the gold standard for assessing IR, its clinical application is limited due to its invasive nature and technical complexity. Several surrogate indices, such as QUICKI, HOMA-IR, and TyG index, have been explored for their associations with PE [2, 7, 19], but further evidence is needed to validate their predictive performance. METS-IR is a novel composite index integrating FPG, TG, HDL-C, and BMI. Studies have shown that METS-IR outperforms EHC in evaluating insulin sensitivity [9] and has been widely applied in cardiovascular and hypertension risk prediction [13, 20–23]. However, no previous study has specifically examined the relationship between METS-IR and PE. Our results demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in PE risk with higher METS-IR scores. Compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), the highest quartile (Q4) was associated with a 144% increased risk of PE. This finding aligns with prior studies showing elevated maternal HOMA-IR levels in women with PE, particularly during early pregnancy [7, 24, 25]. IR is also a central mechanism underlying gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), which is recognized as an independent risk factor for PE. A large retrospective study found that GDM increases the risk of PE by 29% [26], while systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported that pre-existing diabetes increases PE risk by approximately 3.56-fold [27]. Moreover, improving insulin sensitivity may reduce the risk of PE. For example, metformin use in obese non-diabetic pregnant women has been associated with reduced PE incidence [28, 29]. Collectively, these findings support the utility of METS-IR as a practical and reproducible tool for identifying women at high risk for PE. To further evaluate its predictive value, ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.649 (95% CI: 0.636–0.662), with a specificity of 65.89% and sensitivity of 56.41%. Although the AUC is less than 0.7, METS-IR still demonstrates moderate predictive capacity, suggesting it could serve as a useful tool for early risk stratification of PE in clinical practice.

We further analyzed the association between METS-IR and preterm birth. The results showed that the quartiles of METS-IR were not significantly associated with preterm birth. However, when METS-IR was ≥ 38.6 (the cutoff value), the risk of preterm birth increased (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.27). Previous studies on the relationship between IR and preterm birth have yielded inconsistent findings. Some have reported an increased risk of preterm birth with higher IR [30–32], while others found no significant association [33]. Notably, some research even suggests that preterm birth may predispose offspring to future IR and metabolic disorders [34]. This inconsistency may stem from the multifaceted role of IR in pregnancy. On one hand, excessive IR can lead to complications requiring medical intervention, thereby increasing the likelihood of iatrogenic preterm birth. On the other hand, preterm birth itself is influenced by numerous factors, and differences in IR assessment methods, study populations, and confounding control may contribute to conflicting results. Therefore, the causal relationship between IR and preterm birth warrants further prospective investigation.

Interestingly, in this study, the preterm birth rate among women with PE was 15.24%, while the term birth rate was 84.76%, indicating that the majority of PE cases in our cohort were term-onset (delivery ≥ 37 weeks). This indirectly suggests that the association between METS and PE may primarily pertain to late-onset PE, defined as PE diagnosed and delivered at or after 34 weeks of gestation. In clinical practice, PE occurring before 34 weeks is classified as early-onset, whereas PE occurring at or after 34 weeks is considered late-onset [35]. Previous studies have indicated that early-onset and late-onset PE, although sharing similar clinical features, represent distinct pathophysiological entities, with metabolic imbalance and insulin resistance more likely contributing to the development of late-onset PE [36, 37]. Low-dose aspirin initiated before 16 weeks of gestation has been shown to effectively reduce the risk of early-onset preeclampsia, but its preventive effect on late-onset PE appears to be limited [38]. Conversely, managing gestational weight gain [39] and treating insulin resistance [40] may offer more targeted preventive strategies for preeclampsia, particularly the late-onset form. Therefore, further research is warranted to explore the relationship between metabolic markers such as METS and late-onset PE, as well as their potential predictive value in the onset and progression of this subtype.

The association between METS-IR and PE likely involves complex pathophysiological mechanisms. As a marker of IR, elevated METS-IR reflects impaired insulin sensitivity. In normal pregnancy, physiological IR develops gradually to meet increased maternal and fetal energy demands [41]. However, when IR becomes excessive, it may trigger metabolic dysregulation and adverse pregnancy outcomes [42]. IR may contribute to PE through several pathways. For instance, it can reduce nitric oxide (NO) production and increase endothelin-1 secretion, leading to endothelial dysfunction [43]. Systemic inflammation is another key mediator. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 inhibit lipoprotein lipase activity, promoting triglyceride accumulation and exacerbating IR [44]. C-reactive protein (CRP) may further impair insulin signaling by inducing inflammatory responses [45, 46]. Notably, METS-IR has been shown to correlate positively with CRP and IL-6 levels in previous studies [47]. Dyslipidemia is a hallmark of PE, characterized by elevated triglycerides, free fatty acids, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), along with reduced HDL-C levels [48–50]. Given that METS-IR incorporates TG and HDL-C, it may partially reflect lipid metabolism status. Oxidative stress also plays a role by activating the NF-κB pathway, which impairs insulin signaling and endothelial function, thereby worsening insulin resistance [51]. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as dietary patterns and physical activity levels influence insulin sensitivity and may modulate PE risk. Taken together, the association between METS-IR and PE may result from the interplay of IR, inflammation, lipid dysregulation, and oxidative stress.

The main strength of this study lies in its multicenter design and large sample size, which minimized bias due to small sample limitations and enhanced statistical power, thereby improving the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the exposure variable, METS-IR, is a simple, non-invasive metabolic score that can be easily calculated using routinely collected clinical data, making it highly applicable in clinical practice and suitable for widespread use. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Firstly, as this was a retrospective study, we were unable to accurately classify cases into early-onset and late-onset preeclampsia due to limitations in determining the exact time of onset from the medical records. This restricts our ability to further analyze the association between METS and each PE subtype. Future prospective studies are needed to better elucidate the relationship between metabolic markers and late-onset preeclampsia. Secondly, selection bias may have arisen from the inclusion and exclusion criteria used during recruitment, which could limit the generalizability of the results to broader or more diverse populations. Thirdly, although we adjusted for several potential confounding factors in our analysis, there may still be unmeasured or residual confounders that could influence the observed associations. For instance, lifestyle-related factors such as dietary patterns and levels of physical activity were not fully accounted for in this study, which may partially affect the interpretation of our findings. The absence of detailed information on these variables limits our ability to fully disentangle their independent effects from those of the primary exposures under investigation. Therefore, future studies with more comprehensive data on such confounding factors are needed to further validate our results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate a positive association between elevated METS-IR scores and increased risk of PE, suggesting that METS-IR has potential as a predictive marker for PE. As a novel, simple, and readily available indicator of IR, METS-IR offers a practical tool for early risk stratification and timely intervention in clinical practice, with promising applicability in obstetric care.
