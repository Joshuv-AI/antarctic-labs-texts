Trends in the homogenization of macromoth assemblages (2016–2023) in a Seoul City park
Authors: Sei-Woong Choi, Sung-Soo Kim, Jae-Young Lee, Jaesang Noh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a Long-Term Ecological Network project in Korea, we conducted monthly macromoth sampling from May to October, 2016–2023, in Namsan Park, Seoul, at three permanent plots representing dominant tree communities: Quercus mongolica, Robinia pseudoacacia, and Pinus densiflora forests. Over eight years, we recorded 3,486 individuals from 283 species in 11 families. In total, 351 macromoth species from 13 families were documented in the city park, including 212 newly discovered species and 68 previously recorded species now disappearing. Species richness and abundance varied among the three forest types, with the Q. mongolica forest showing the highest values in both species and individuals. Overall species richness showed a nonsignificant decline, and auto regressive integrated moving average models predicted ongoing fluctuations in richness and a rising trend in abundance in the coming years. Macromoth assemblages in Namsan exhibited distinct clustering by forest type, with consistent distribution of body size groups emphasized throughout the study. Furthermore, relatively high faunal turnover within forests, combined with minimal annual variation, suggests a trend toward homogenization of macromoth assemblages across forest types. This pattern appears to be influenced by the city park’s isolation, which may limit species dispersal and contribute to localized faunal differentiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18373-4.

Introduction

Globally, urbanization drives the growth of cities and towns. This expansion has resulted in extensive, fragmented landscapes characterized by high-density built-up areas interspersed with lower-density rural and natural regions1. In Korea, urbanization has reached nearly 90%, with forest area in Seoul decreasing by ~ 57.1% from 1996 to 20162. Consequently, natural areas with high conservation value have been markedly reduced to small, scattered urban remnants3 leading to the decline or extinction of various species, including insects4,5. The conservation of urban remnant ecosystems is urgently needed because (1) these remnants often exhibit high beta diversity and serve as refuge habitats for many species, (2) they provide ecosystem services that enhance the urban environment and improve quality of life for city residents, and (3) they serve as key links between nature and many urban populations6.

Since the late 14th century, Seoul has been the capital city of Korea, functioning as its central urban hub. Today, it remains a megacity with a population of 10 million. Mount Namsan (hereafter, referred to as “Namsan”) is a natural park located in central Seoul (latitude: 39° 32′ 07˝N–37° 33′ 21 ˝N; longitude: 126° 58′ 53 ˝E–127° 00′ 21 ˝E; Fig. 1). Since the Korean War (1950–1953), the park area has been degraded due to residential construction, road and tunnel development, the planting of afforestation species [such as black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia L.), poplar (Populus × tomentigladulosa T.B. Lee ex M. Kim), and various pines (Pinus densiflora Siebold & Zucc. (Pd), Pinus rigida Mill. (Pr), and Pinus koraiensis Siebold & Zucc.)), air pollution, soil acidification, and pest infestations (e.g., oak wilt)7. Although natural succession has not occurred owing to ongoing disturbances, Namsan remains a critical green space in Seoul’s north–south green axis, acting as an essential ecological hub within the urban landscape8.

Namsan consists of four dominant tree communities, namely Pd, Pr, Robinia pseudoacacia (Rp), and Quercus mongolica Fisch. Ex Ledeb. (Qm), which have declined due to vegetation changes7,8. The Pd forest is being outcompeted by deciduous broad-leaved species, leading to homogenization of overall vegetation8. The Qm forest, which once covered 23.5–24.4% of Namsan’s forest area, has declined due to logging triggered by oak wilt disease. This reduction allowed for the growth of various shrub species well-adapted to urban conditions and open canopies, promoting the spread of species such as Quercus serrata Thunb. ex Murray, Rp, Styrax japonica Siebold & Zucc., Sorbus alnifolia (Siebold & Zucc.) K. Koch, and Fraxinus rhynchophylla Hance7. The Rp forest area has also declined due to selective logging since 1990, although Rp trees have now colonized and expanded into Qm and Pd forests7.

Lepidopterans are among the few insect groups that are readily identifiable and taxonomically well documented9. They play important ecological roles as herbivores, pollinators, and prey for birds and mammals9. Therefore, lepidopterans are frequently used as surrogates for ecological indicators in tropical and temperate ecosystems10,11. Despite Namsan’s central location in Seoul, studies on insect fauna, including Lepidoptera, have been limited. This is largely due to the park’s small size, which restricts opportunities for systematic ecological research, and a general lack of interest in conducting detailed studies in urban green spaces. Lepidopterans found at Namsan include 139 species in 12 families12,13. An ecological study on moths in Namsan, initiated in 2005, was part of a long-term ecological research project14. This study investigated moth assemblages in Qm and Pd forests, finding no differences in species richness (number of species) and abundance (number of individuals) between the two forest types; however, both richness and abundance exhibited notable monthly variation14.

This study aimed to monitor the diversity, composition, and turnover of macromoth assemblages across forest types during 2016–2023. Additionally, changes in body size were examined, as urbanization has disproportionately affected macromoths of different sizes15,16.

Results

Diversity of macromoth assemblages at Namsan

Over eight years (2016–2023), 3,486 individuals were identified, representing 283 species across 11 families (Tables 1 and 2). Including previously documented records from Namsan12,13 we compiled 351 species from 13 families. Among these, Erebidae was the most diverse family (110 species), followed by Noctuidae (91), Geometridae (86), and Notodontidae (17) (Appendix 1). These four families collectively accounted for 86.6% of all recorded species. Notably, six families (Thyrididae, Lasiocampidae, Limacodidae, Euteliidae, Sphindidae, and Bombycidae) experienced a decline in species richness. Of the documented species, 71 were observed in both past and present studies, 68 previously recorded species were absent in the current survey, and 212 species were newly recorded in the local fauna. Additionally, two Lasiocampidae species, Dendrolimus spectabilis and Gastropacha populifolia, along with six Sphingidae species, were not sampled in the present study.

Among the three forest types, the Qm forest exhibited the highest species richness and abundance (212 species and 1,601 individuals), followed by the Rp (162 species and 1,118 individuals) and Pd (146 species and 767 individuals) forests (Table 1). The singleton rates of the Qm (36.3%) and Rp (37.0%) forests were comparable, whereas the Pd forest exhibited a higher singleton rate at 43.2%. Rarefaction and extrapolation sampling curves based on Hill numbers indicated that species richness (q = 0) was highest in the Qm forest, followed by the Pd and Rp forests. However, the Shannon (q = 1) and Simpson (q = 2) diversity indices did not differ among forest types (Fig. 2). Based on eight-year monitoring, the estimated total species numbers (Chao 1 with 95% confidence intervals) in Namsan and within the Qm, Rp, and Pd forests were 378.7 (338.4–448.2), 293.2 (257.4–357.3), 232.7 (198.5–299), and 224 (186.8–295.2), respectively (Table 1).

Temporal changes of macromoth assemblages in Namsan

Overall species richness in Namsan declined over time, but the trend was not statistically significant (Table 3). Similarly, species richness in the Rp and Qm forests declined nonsignificantly, whereas that in the Pd forest showed nonsignificant increases (Table 3; Fig. 3A). Total abundance decreased, although not significantly (Table 3). Abundance trends in each forest mirrored those of species except Rp (Fig. 3B).

Macromoth assemblage pattern in Namsan

We conducted nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination for macromoth assemblages across the three sites and two variables, identifying two axes (final stress = 14.82 after 70 iterations). The first and second axes explained 31.1% and 22.6% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 7). Based on forest type, macromoth assemblages in Namsan revealed that forest composition was a significant factor in sample clustering across eight years [multi-response permutation procedure (MRPP): A = 0.04, P = 0.002].

A set of 283 species (slope = − 0.07, intercept = 1.16, R2 = 0.65, P = 0.016) and a subset of 80 species with > 5 individuals (slope = − 0.06, intercept = 1.22, R2 = 0.38, P = 0.10) showed decreasing average distances among the three forest types, indicating a trend toward homogeneous macromoth assemblages (Fig. 6). However, assemblages with larger numbers of species exhibited a less pronounced homogenization pattern (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Overall, we documented 351 macromoth species across 13 families based on previous studies12,13 and the present research. The estimated species richness (Chao1: 378.7; 95% confidence interval: 338.4–448.2) suggests that overall Lepidoptera diversity in Namsan is likely higher, especially considering the exclusion of microlepidopteran families, such as Crambidae, Pyralidae, and Tortricidae. Compared with previous studies, 68 species were not observed during the past eight years (2016–2023). Among them, the pine caterpillar D. spectabilis was observed until 200912,13, with no specimens found since 2016. This species, once a major forest pest in Korea (from the 1950 s to the early 1970 s), has markedly declined on the mainland in recent decades17.

Larger-bodied moths, particularly those in the family Sphingidae, were also absent from the recent survey. Large moth species are known to be more sensitive to habitat fragmentation owing to their higher resource demands16. Similar declines have been reported in Borneo18 and the northeastern United States19 with causes including habitat disturbance18, introduced biological control agents, and forest cover and compositional change19. The global decline in sphingid populations is increasingly evident, and their loss may have cascading effects on ecological networks due to their role in food webs.

At Namsan, macromoth assemblages exhibited divergent trends in species richness and abundance. In recent years, species richness has declined, whereas abundance has increased. The ARIMA models for both datasets confirmed their seasonal and stationary natures, predicting continued fluctuations in species richness and a consistent increase in abundance (Fig. 4). Declines in biodiversity are well-documented across animal taxa and biomes20–22 primarily attributed to human disturbances, including land-use changes, invasive species, climate change, and nitrogen deposition21. Areas dominated by human activity have experienced greater losses in local diversity compared to regions with more natural vegetation. Although abundance has also declined, the decrease has been less severe than that of species richness23. However, the shifts in insect populations associated with increasing urbanization have not markedly affected certain groups, such as polyphagous pests and generalist pest control agents24. The macromoth assemblages in Namsan, located in the center of Seoul, are likely strongly influenced by habitat changes. In particular, secondary succession involving vegetation adapted to urban environments may shape the diversity of primary consumers, including moths. Understanding the structural complexity of vegetation in herbaceous, ruderal urban ecosystems can provide valuable insights into the factors driving insect population changes24. Conversely, moth abundance may follow a different trajectory due to the reduced presence of natural enemies and the ample resources supplied by a few urban-resistant plant species in this island-like city park24–26.

The macromoth assemblages in Namsan showed that forest type was the significant factor determining the clustering of sample sites across the eight-year period (Fig. 5). Among the three forest types, Qm forest had the highest observed and estimated number of species, followed by Rp and Pd forests. Although changes in macromoth composition within each forest was not statistically significant, the number of species and individuals in the Qm forest declined from 2016 to 2023, whereas both measures increased in the Rp and Pd forests.

Oak trees are known to be one of the major food plants supporting lepidopteran diversity across temperate regions27–30 and this pattern was reflected in Namsan, where the Qm forest showed the highest species richness. However, the total area of Qm vegetation declined between 2009 and 2016 due to logging and fumigation measures implemented after the spread of oak wilt in 20127. In contrast, tree species more tolerant of urban environments, such as Styrax japonicus, S. alnifolia, and Acer palmatum, were maintained or increased in the subtree layer. In addition, the shrub layer experienced a marked increase in plant species due to canopy opening7. Although pine trees in the upper layer remained largely unchanged, their overall area decreased, and other species, including S. japonicus, Stephanandra incisa var. incisa, and Prunus sargentii expanded in the Pd forest31. As a result, plant species richness increased in both the Pd and Rp forest communities31.

Over the past eight years, the macromoth assemblages across the three forest types in Namsan have shown a trend toward homogenization, reflected in increased faunal similarity among sites (Fig. 6). This trend was less evident among assemblages composed of species represented by > 5 individuals. Several factors may explain this pattern. One likely explanation is the close relationship between moths and their host plants, as moths serve as important primary consumers. The decline of dominant tree species, along with the regrowth of urban-adapted vegetation in Namsan, may have led to changes in moth communities, contributing to observed homogenization despite the persistence of distinct tree communities.

In contrast to the relatively high proportion of specialist species found within individual forests in Namsan (Pd: 17.1%; Rp: 11.7%; Qm: 20.8%), the average annual rate of change in specialist species within each forest over the eight-year period was comparatively low (Pd: 8.03%; Rp: 3.78%; Qm: 8.09%). This suggests notable spatial variation in faunal composition among the forests but relatively limited year-to-year turnover. Rare species are commonly observed in many ecosystems32,33 and a high proportion of singletons is often interpreted as evidence of true forest specialists that occupy narrow ecological niches at low densities or as indicators of active colonization during early successional stages34. Additional factors that may contribute to elevated singleton rates include the rarity of host plants for caterpillars, narrow environmental tolerance, pressure from natural enemies, interspecific competition, edge-of-range distributions, and insufficient sampling effort35.

In the Pd forest, 25 species were recorded as habitat specialists, with host plants known for 21 of these species, none of which feed on coniferous trees, such as pines. In the Rp forest, 19 habitat specialists species were observed, and the host plants of 16 of these species are known: 3 feed on legumes and 1 is polyphagous, feeding on various deciduous trees. The Qm forest had 44 habitat specialists, of which 38 have known host plants. Notably, eight of these feed on oak tree species, indicating a higher degree of host fidelity compared with the other forests. Despite these patterns, correlations among forests were weak, indicating that many active species are shared across sites but vary in abundance. However, the limited temporal change in specialist composition suggests minimal colonization from neighboring areas. The observed negative relationship between species richness and patch isolation is likely driven by reduced immigration across larger distances to other urban habitat patches36–38. In this context, Namsan functions much like an ecological island, with low levels of macromoth immigration. Although butterflies and moths are proficient fliers, their responses to urban fragmentation differ. Butterflies can navigate through urban landscapes using stepping stones, such as small green spaces4. In contrast, moth dispersal is more susceptible to disruption from artificial city lights, limiting their ability to colonize isolated green spaces in urban areas.

Among all specimens collected across the three forests in Namsan, the species Alcis angulifera and Naarda maculifera were dominant, accounting for 9.04% and 8.66% of the total individuals, respectively. Alcis angulifera, a geometrid moth species, is polyphagous, whereas the host plant of N. maculifera remains unknown. These findings contrast markedly with those reported two decades ago14. In 2010, Yi and Kim14 identified Diarsia deparca and Xestia c-nigrum as dominant species in Pd and Qm forests. Both species are polyphagous: D. deparca is active in early spring and autumn, whereas X. nigrum is a summer flier. In the present monitoring, only a single D. deparca specimen was recorded in the Qm forest while 11 X. c-nigrum individuals were found across all three forests. This marked shift in species dominance over the past 20 years underscores the importance of long-term ecological monitoring in urban parks.

The eight-year monitoring of macromoths in Namsan clearly demonstrates that vegetation changes in urban parks have a substantial impact on macromoth assemblages. This research is expected to continue in the future. Although sampling sites have been restricted to permanent plots within each forest, future studies may expand to additional locations and incorporate a broader range of taxonomic groups, such as birds and ground beetles. This will allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how shifts in urban forest composition influence multiple trophic levels, including both herbivores and their predators.

Methods

Field survey

The field survey was conducted in three forest types in Namsan, Seoul, Korea: Pd, Rp, and Qm (Fig. 1). Surveys were conducted from 2016 to 2023, with regular monthly sampling performed between May and October each year.

Moths were collected using ultraviolet light bucket traps, each equipped with a 22-W circular ultraviolet tube (BioQuip Co., USA) powered by a 12-V battery. Sampling was conducted for 5 h after dusk, and traps were operated simultaneously across all three sites to minimize sampling bias. Specimens classified as macromoths were identified to the species level using taxonomic references39–42. Ecological trait data, including wingspan and larval food plants, were gathered from the authors records and published literature43–46.

Collection permission

Sampling within the city park was conducted with the permission from the Seoul Metropolitan Office, coordinated through the National Institute of Ecology, Korea.

Statistical analysis

Species diversity across the three forest types was assessed using alpha diversity metrics (species richness and abundance). sample-size-based rarefaction and extrapolation curves with 95% confidence intervals for macromoth species were generated for each forest type using Hill numbers at three orders: species richness (q = 0), Shannon diversity (q = 1), and Simpson diversity (q = 2)47.

Trends in species richness and abundances from 2016 to 2023 were analyzed using linear regression models with lm function in R48. Monthly time series data (species richness and abundance) were further analyzed using ARIMA models, with modeling conducted via the auto.arima function in the R package “forecast”48,49. These models incorporate an autoregressive component (parameter p, representing the number of lag observations), a moving average component (parameter q, indicating the size of the moving average window), and an integration/differencing component (parameter d, denoting the number of times the data must to be differenced to achieve stationarity)49.

The auto.arima function chooses whether to include each component (and how many terms are included) using AICc49. We used these models to forecast macromoth trends for the following five years based on existing data.

Macromoths were categorized into size classes based on wingspan: small (< 20 mm), medium (21–40 mm), and large (> 40 mm). Proportional changes over time for each class were evaluated using the Mann–Kendall trend test in R48.

To compare macromoth community compositions across Pd, Rp, and Qm forests, NMDS was performed. NMDS reduces complex multispecies data into lower-dimensional ordination axes, minimizing stress while preserving ecological distances among sites50. A matrix of 24 sites and 283 species over eight years was initially used, employing Sørensen dissimilarity as the ordination distance measure. To reduce noise, species with fewer than five individuals were excluded, resulting in a refined matrix of 80 species across 24 samplings (sites × years). A secondary matrix was created to incorporate environmental variables: sampling year and forest type. In total, 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations were used to evaluate the significance of the final ordination axes.

Following ordination, study sites were plotted in a two-dimensional NMDS space. The significance of observed clusters was determined using the MRPP, which tests the hypothesis of no difference between two a priori defined entity groups, in PC-ORD v7.0851.
