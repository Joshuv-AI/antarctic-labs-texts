Interviewer and Respondent Sociodemographic Characteristics, Rapport, and Their Joint Impact on Data Quality in the NESDA Study
Authors: Melany Horsfall, Adriaan W Hoogendoorn, Stasja Draisma, Merijn Eikelenboom, Brenda W J H Penninx
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesData quality in face‐to‐face interviews may be affected by sociodemographic characteristics of interviewers and respondents (age, education, and gender) as well as their (dis)similarity. Similarity in these characteristics may foster rapport, potentially enhancing cooperation and improving data quality. This study examined the associations between interviewer and respondent sociodemographics, their similarity, and data quality, and whether rapport mediates these relationships in a sample of respondents with and without a depressive or anxiety disorder.MethodsBaseline data from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety were analyzed. Data quality was measured using item nonresponse and socially desirable responses to sensitive questions; rapport was assessed via post‐interview evaluations from both parties. Multilevel regression and mediation analyses were conducted.ResultsRapport was lower among older respondents but higher with older interviewers. More dissimilarity in age was negatively associated with rapport sum score and dissimilarity in education was negatively associated with rapport scores from interviewers. Furthermore, older respondents had higher item nonresponse and, along with male respondents and those with higher education, gave more socially desirable responses. Rapport partially mediated the effect of respondent age on data quality. Additionally, having a depressive or anxiety disorder was negatively associated with rapport. Experiencing a depression was also linked to more item nonresponse, in contrast, having a depressive disorder or anxiety leads to less socially desirable responses.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of interviewer training in rapport‐building, particularly with older respondents and respondents who suffer from a psychiatric disorder, to enhance data quality.Keywords: anxiety, data quality, depression, item nonresponse, rapport, socially desirable responses, sociodemographics

Introduction

In psychiatric research, face‐to‐face interviews are often used to collect data that are too complex to obtain through self‐administered questionnaires. Answering questions regarding own mental states can be burdensome and distressing, especially for respondents with mental health issues. This is why the use of interviewers who are able to gain trust and can guide and motivate respondents is of utmost importance. Ideally, response variation should reflect true differences between respondents, but face‐to‐face data collection is susceptible to interviewer and respondent effects that can reduce data quality (Fowler and Mangione 1990; Groves 2005). Standardized interviewing aims to reduce interviewer and respondent effects by enforcing strict protocols (Fowler and Mangione 1990), yet answer distributions may still be influenced by sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education) of both interviewers and respondents. This will be addressed in the following paragraphs.

With respect to respondent characteristics, research indicates that women tend to show higher acquiescence, reflecting greater passive agreement with questionnaire items (Hox et al. 1991). Older respondents are more likely to exhibit item nonresponse, require additional clarification, misunderstand questions, and take longer to complete interviews (Blumenberg et al. 2018; Hox et al. 1991). However, they also provide more extensive responses to open‐ended questions and demonstrate lower acquiescence (Beullens et al. 2019; Hox et al. 1991). Lower educational attainment is also linked to increased item nonresponse (Blumenberg et al. 2018; Kupek 1998).

Findings regarding interviewer characteristics are inconsistent. West and Blom (2017) reported that, across 23 studies, nine found that female interviewers obtained higher quality data, four found the opposite, and 10 found no gender effect. Results for interviewer age are similarly mixed, with studies reporting positive, negative, or no association with data quality. Silber et al. (2021) found that older interviewer age and greater age differences between interviewer and respondent were associated with increased “don't know” responses. Interviewer education and gender did not significantly affect the likelihood of “don't know” answers or refusals. Interviewer age, gender, and education had no effect on response refusals.

Given the interpersonal nature of face‐to‐face interviews, the sociodemographic characteristics of both interviewer and respondent may interact to influence data quality. Research on whether similarity in these characteristics affects item nonresponse yields mixed results. Gender‐matching has sometimes been linked to reduced item nonresponse, particularly among male pairs, while female pairs may show increased nonresponse (Bittmann 2020; Catania et al. 1996; Vercruyssen et al. 2017). Evidence for age similarity is also inconsistent, with some studies reporting reduced item nonresponse and others finding no effect or only subgroup‐specific effects (Bittmann 2020, 2022; Grönemann 2024; Silber et al. 2021; Vercruyssen et al. 2017). Additionally, older interviewers may elicit fewer “don't know” responses, but those with higher education than respondents may elicit more (Tu and Liao 2007).

Socially desirable responding to sensitive questions is another important indicator of data quality. Several studies suggest that gender similarity between interviewer and respondent can influence responses to sensitive topics, with higher reporting of sensitive sexual behaviors in same‐gender pairs (Catania et al. 1996; Kianersi et al. 2020). However, exceptions exist, such as women reporting certain issues more frequently to male interviewers (Fuchs 2009; Kianersi et al. 2020).

Sociodemographic similarity may enhance data quality by fostering interpersonal liking (McPherson et al. 2001), which promotes rapport—mutual interest, support, and understanding (Kahn and Cannell 1957). Good rapport might impact data quality when it improves cooperation and response behavior (Groves et al. 1992). Key elements for building rapport include attentiveness, common ground, courtesy, connection, and information sharing (Webb and Barrett 2014). Attentiveness and positivity are crucial initially, while coordination becomes more important as rapport develops (Tickle‐Degnen and Rosenthal 1990). When focusing on standardized interviews, rapport involves interviewer responsiveness and respondent engagement (Garbarski et al. 2016). Interviewers play an important role in establishing rapport. Assuming that a pleasant interview experience is a reflection of rapport, Schmiedeberg and Schröder (2024) show that interviewers significantly influence how pleasant respondents perceive assessments. Furthermore, assessments conducted by female interviewers are experienced as more pleasant.

Empirical evidence on rapport's effect on data quality is mixed. West and Blom (2017) found in their review that most studies reported either no effect or a negative impact, while Gabbert et al. (2021) reported that positive rapport generally enhances information sharing. Foucault Welles et al. (2022) showed that interviewer nonverbal behaviors, such as smiling and nodding, increase participant response, likely by enhancing rapport. Sun et al. (2021) found higher rapport increased disclosure on sensitive questions but found no effect on item nonresponse, whereas Horsfall et al. (2021) observed lower item nonresponse but higher socially desirable responding with high rapport. Nonstandardized interviewer behaviors can influence rapport and consequently compromise data quality (Bell et al. 2016). Rapport may mediate the relationship between interviewer–respondent similarity and data quality, with greater similarity potentially enhancing rapport and reducing item nonresponse.

The relationship between interviewer and respondent sociodemographic characteristics, their similarities, and data quality remains inconclusive. Rapport has not yet been systematically examined as a potential explanatory factor in this context. By investigating this topic in a sample including respondents with psychiatric disorders, we aim to extend survey methodology to psychiatric research. According to the World Health Organization (World Health Organization 2026), 4% of the global population currently experiences a major depressive disorder and 4.4% an anxiety disorder. Research into the etiology and consequences of these high‐burden mental disorders remains a priority. As such research largely relies on self‐reported interview data, ensuring high data quality is essential. However, the presence of depression or anxiety disorders may affect both the interviewer–respondent relationship and the data quality. Depression and anxiety are associated with cognitive difficulties, such as impaired concentration and memory (Cambridge et al. 2018; Marazziti et al. 2010; Rock et al. 2014), which may hinder respondents' ability to recall information and make response decisions. Additionally, overall functioning, including social functioning, can be impaired in individuals with these disorders (DSM‐5, American Psychiatric Association 2013), potentially making it more difficult to establish good rapport. Therefore, it is important to investigate factors influencing rapport and data quality in samples including both psychiatric patients and healthy controls.

This study examines whether interviewer and respondent sociodemographic characteristics, and their similarity, are associated with data quality in the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (Penninx et al. 2021), and whether rapport mediates these associations. Based on previous research, we expect higher respondent age and lower education to be linked to increased item nonresponse due to greater cognitive difficulty (Blumenberg et al. 2018; Hox et al. 1991; Kupek 1998). However, drawing on the theory of liking (McPherson et al. 2001), we anticipate that greater sociodemographic similarity will be more strongly associated with both lower item nonresponse and higher socially desirable responding, as similarity fosters liking and rapport. This may enhance mutual trust and promote helping behaviors, such as clarifying questions and motivating respondents, thereby reducing item nonresponse. Conversely, strong rapport may also lead to more socially desirable responses, as interviewer–respondent pairs may avoid giving undesirable answers to protect their relationship. This is consistent with previous findings (Horsfall et al. 2021) showing that good rapport is linked to less item nonresponse but more socially desirable answers. In summary, we hypothesize that interviewer–respondent pairs with greater similarity in age, education, and gender will show less item nonresponse and more socially desirable responding, with rapport partially mediating these effects.

Method

Sample

Data from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA) baseline assessment (2007) (Penninx et al. 2021) were used for analysis. NESDA is an ongoing longitudinal study of adults aged 18–65, including those with current or past depression and/or anxiety disorders (n = 1701), subthreshold symptoms (n = 907), and healthy controls (n = 373). Participants were recruited from the general population, primary care, and specialized mental health care across three Dutch regions between 2004 and 2007. Ethical approval was obtained, and all participants provided informed consent. Data access requires NESDA Board approval and an analysis plan (see www.nesda.nl). For this study, only interviewers who conducted at least 10 interviews (N = 38) (Olson and Peytchev 2007) and respondents who completed the evaluation form were included, resulting in 2923 interviews. Interviewers were predominantly female (91%), between 21 and 59 years old and their education ranged from vocational (various staff members had a nursing training) to university (quite a few staff members had a psychology master). Respondents were randomly assigned to interviewers for the assessment.

Baseline Assessment

Since rapport was a key variable, baseline data were used to measure rapport without potential biasing effects from prior interactions between interviewer and respondent during previous interviews. For example, it might be more difficult to build rapport during a follow‐up assessment with a respondent who had a negative rapport experience on a previous assessment. Respondents are not routinely interviewed by the same interviewer at follow‐up assessments; some may be interviewed by the same interviewer, while others may have a different interviewer at each wave. As a result, rapport may be experienced differently by respondents who are interviewed multiple times by the same interviewer compared to those who are interviewed by different interviewers at each assessment. To avoid this difference in rapport experience only baseline data was used for analyses. Respondents attended a four‐hour face‐to‐face assessment at a Dutch clinical site, including a CAPI on psychopathology, demographics, functioning, medical measurements, computer tasks, and interview evaluations. They also completed two self‐administered questionnaires at home.

Recruitment and Training of Interviewers

Interviewers were recruited based on education, social skills, and relevant experience, and received extensive training focused on standardized assessment procedures. Interviews were audio‐recorded, with early recordings reviewed for feedback to ensure protocol adherence and data quality. Regular meetings addressed assessment issues and further standardized interviewer behavior.

Rapport

To assess the dyadic nature of rapport, both respondents and interviewers completed an evaluation form about their interview experience at the end of the NESDA assessment. Given that the NESDA interview is lengthy and focuses on serious topics such as psychiatric disorders and physical health, we consider a positive evaluation to primarily reflect the quality of the interaction—serving as an indicator of rapport. A pleasant experience was interpreted as high rapport. Respondents rated the interview on a 4‐point scale from 0 = “unpleasant” to 3 = “pleasant,” while interviewers used a 6‐point scale ranging from 0 = “very unpleasant” to 5 = “very pleasant.” To ensure equal weighting, respondent scores were multiplied by 5/3 before combining with interviewer scores. Rapport will be examined in two steps. In the first step, a sum score is used in which the rapport ratings of the participant and the interviewer are added together. The sum of both ratings produced a rapport score ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater rapport.

Although combining interviewer and respondent scores provides the best overall impression of rapport, interpreting the rapport sum score is also challenging, as it remains unclear to what extent the interviewer's and respondent's scores contribute to the effect. Therefore, subsequently, we examine associations with rapport ratings of the respondent and the interviewer in separate analyses.

Analyses included only cases with complete rapport data (N = 2923).

Data Quality

Two indicators were created.

Item nonresponses (N = 2923). To measure item nonresponse a sum score was calculated from 678 mandatory questions on topics such as demography, health, and trauma. Higher item nonresponse scores indicated lower data quality. A standardized Computer Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) was used for data collection. The CAPI was programmed to prevent respondents from skipping any questions. To circumvent this restriction when necessary, interviewers were required to deliberately enter a specific code in the program to record a refusal or “don't know” response. As a result of this data collection method, the overall number of item nonresponses was generally low.

Covariates

Presence of a current (6‐month recency) depression (major depressive disorder or dysthymia) or anxiety disorder (social anxiety, panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, agoraphobia) was measured with the Composite International Diagnostic Instrument (CIDI DSM‐IV) (Wittchen 1994). Lastly, number of conducted interviews per interviewer, ranging between 10 and 242, was added as covariate to control for a difference in interview experience between the interviewers.

Statistical Analyses

Summary statistics were calculated for all variables. Multilevel linear regression was used to examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics, rapport, and data quality, accounting for respondents nested within interviewers. Multilevel negative binomial regression, which adjusts for overdispersion, was applied to analyze relationships between sociodemographic predictors and count‐based data quality outcomes. This technique uses an additional dispersion parameter to account for overdispersion thereby providing more accurate estimates of regression coefficients and standard error (Nguyen 2020). All analyses were conducted using unweighted data.

First, we examined the association between the sociodemographic characteristics and rapport. A separate model for each rapport variable (rapport sum score, rapport rating respondents, rapport rating interviewers) was created. Second, we studied the association between sociodemographic variables and respectively item nonresponse and socially desirable responses. These models distinguish between all combinations of interviewer and respondent sociodemographics (age, education and gender), explicitly modeling differences such as “male interviewer–female respondent” versus “female interviewer–male respondent” through separate indicator variables. Next, we examined the relationship between rapport and the data quality variables, again, running a separate model for each rapport variable (rapport sum score, rapport rating respondents, rapport rating interviewers) taking multicollinearity between the variables into account.

In the last step we studied the mediating effect of rapport on the relationship between sociodemographic characteristics and data quality (see Figure 1) by performing mediation analyses in SPSS 28 (IBM Corp 2023) using the PROCCESS V4.2 Macro model 4 (Hayes 2017). This method uses bootstrap resampling procedures to evaluate the indirect effects, setting the number of bootstrap samples to 5000. The indirect effect would be considered statistically significant when the 95% bootstrap percentile confidence interval (CI) did not include zero.

Sociodemographic Variables and Rapport

Respondent and interviewer age were both significantly associated with the rapport sum score, which combined the rapport ratings of interviewer and respondent (Table 2). Rapport was rated lower when respondent age increased and in contrast, was rated higher when interviewer age increased. In line with prior expectation, as age difference between interviewer and respondent increases, the rapport sum score decreases, so rapport was less positive. Regarding education, an increase in dissimilarity in education between interviewer and respondent was associated with lower rapport ratings from interviewers. Regarding gender neither gender of the interviewer, nor gender of the interviewer, nor the (dis)similarity in gender were significantly associated with rapport. This last result was unexpected since we hypothesized that more similarity leads to higher rapport ratings. Finally, evaluating the role of the covariates across all models in Table 2, we observe that both current depression and current anxiety affect rapport negatively (sum score, interviewer rapport and respondent rapport), while the number of interviews by the interviewer showed a negligible effect. Important to note is the small to moderate but significant variance at the interviewer level (Table 2) indicating differences between interviewers in the experience of rapport. This could not be explained by difference in the number of conducted interviews per interviewer, as this variable was not associated with rapport.

Sociodemographic Characteristics and Data Quality

Item Nonresponse

The top part of Table 3 shows the results of multilevel analyses regressing the number of item nonresponses on sociodemographic variables. Contrary to our expectations similarity in sociodemographic characteristics was not significantly related to item nonresponse. From all the sociodemographic variables of the interviewer and respondent, only age of the respondent was significantly linked to item nonresponse. In line with some previous studies (Blumenberg et al. 2018; Hox et al. 1991) we found that the number of item nonresponses was higher when the respondent was older.

Socially Desirable Responses

The bottom part of Table 3 shows the results of the same analysis for the number of socially desirable responses to sensitive questions. These results reveal statistically significant effects for age, education and gender of the respondent: scores on the socially desirable responses variable were lower (and thus more socially desirable) with older age and more years of education and when the respondent was male. Unexpectedly, sociodemographic characteristics of the interviewer and interviewer‐respondent similarities in sociodemographics were not significantly associated with socially desirable responses.

When examining variance between interviewers with respect to the data quality variables, there are large significant differences in item nonresponse between interviewers that are not explained by sociodemographic characteristics or covariates. For the variable socially desirable responses to sensitive questions, no interviewer variance was observed. Number of conducted interviews per interviewer was not significantly associated with data quality.

Rapport and Data Quality

The findings concerning rapport and data quality are as expected and in line with our previous study about this topic (Horsfall et al. 2021). Analysis revealed that a higher rapport sum score (combining interviewer and respondent ratings) is significantly associated with lower item nonresponse (see Table 4). When examining the individual rapport scores of respondents only or interviewers only, similar results are observed: higher rapport scores are related to lower item nonresponse. Regarding socially desirable responses to sensitive questions, higher rapport scores are associated with more socially desirable answers. This association is evident for the rapport sum score, as well as for the individual rapport scores of both respondents and interviewers.

Discussion

High‐quality data are essential for reliable scientific conclusions. Our study found that depression or anxiety disorders were associated with lower rapport and, for depression, higher item nonresponse. Since we have no influence on the psychiatric status of respondents, identifying modifiable factors—that may impact data quality such as interviewer characteristics and rapport—is crucial. Such information can inform targeted interviewer training or the selection of interviewers with characteristics that enhance rapport and data quality. We examined how sociodemographic characteristics and rapport influence data quality in 2923 respondents (with and without psychiatric disorders) and 38 interviewers.

First, we found that rapport decreased with increasing respondent age but increased with interviewer age, possibly reflecting greater rapport‐building experience among older interviewers. Furthermore, an increase in age difference between interviewer and respondent was linked to a lower rapport rating and a greater gap in educational level was associated with lower rapport as rated by interviewers. These results partly support the theory that sociodemographic similarity between interviewer and respondent leads to higher rapport because people with similar characteristics tend to like each other more.

Next, results showed that age was significantly associated with item nonresponse. Older respondents showed higher item nonresponse, consistent with previous findings (Blumenberg et al. 2018; Hox et al. 1991), possibly due to recall difficulties. This effect was partly mediated by rapport, which may be harder to establish with older respondents while it might be important to motivate respondents to cooperate with data collection.

Socially desirable responding increased with respondent age which was in contrast to our expectations. This result is partially explained by rapport (sum score and interviewer ratings). We expected fewer socially desirable responses among older respondents, especially since lower rapport—more common among older participants—has been linked to fewer socially desirable answers (Horsfall et al. 2021). Higher education of the respondent and male gender of the respondent were also linked to more socially desirable responses. The finding of more socially desirable responses in certain groups of respondents may partly result from the operationalization of this construct. As no dedicated instrument was available, a proxy variable for socially desirable responses was constructed from secondary data by selecting interview questions addressing behaviors generally considered undesirable, such as financial difficulties, legal issues, or suicidal behavior. A limitation of this approach is that these behaviors may genuinely be less prevalent in certain groups (e.g., older individuals, those with higher education, or men), meaning that lower endorsement may not necessarily only reflect social desirability bias, warranting further investigation using dedicated social desirability scales or record verification.

Contrary to expectations and some previous studies (Bittmann 2020; Tu and Liao 2007; Vercruyssen et al. 2017), we found no effects of sociodemographic similarity on data quality. This suggests that matching interviewers and respondents by sociodemographic traits to improve data quality seems unnecessary. However, literature remains inconsistent (West and Blom 2017).

Beyond sociodemographic factors, higher rapport scores—whether sum, respondent, or interviewer—were associated with lower item nonresponse but increased socially desirable responding, consistent with Horsfall et al. (2021). High rapport may motivate interviewers to probe more and support respondents, but may also encourage socially desirable answers to maintain rapport.

Finally, we want to highlight the relationship between psychiatric status, rapport, and data quality. The presence of anxiety or depression was negatively associated with rapport between interviewer and respondent. Additionally, respondents with current depression exhibited higher item nonresponse, while those with current depression or anxiety disorders scored lower on social desirability.

The difficulty in building rapport with respondents who have depression or anxiety disorders may be explained by impaired social but also cognitive functioning (DSM‐5, American Psychiatric Association 2013). When social functioning is compromised, it may be more challenging to demonstrate mutual interest and understanding. Furthermore, impaired cognitive functioning may cause difficulties with recalling answers, selecting responses, or maintaining attention. The relationship between depression and cognitive impairment in for example executive functioning, memory, and attention, has been demonstrated in several reviews and meta‐analyses (Cambridge et al. 2018; Marazzitti et al. 2010; Rock et al. 2014).

To mitigate the negative impact of depression or anxiety disorders on rapport, it is important to devote extra attention to rapport building with these participants. Rapport building techniques from treatment settings, called therapeutic alliance, might give insight into building rapport with depressive or anxious respondents. Literature about therapeutic alliance emphasizes the importance of a supportive, explorative, experiential, affect‐focused, engaged, and active attitude for optimal treatment outcomes (Husain et al. 2016). While some elements of therapeutic alliance overlap with rapport‐building techniques, not all can be applied in standardized interviews. For example, exploration by asking open‐ended questions and allowing participants to elaborate, conflicts with standardized interviewing. However, it would be interesting to explore whether techniques to create therapeutic alliance could be adjusted and incorporated into a research interview. For example, creating space for respondents to speak freely before the interview, during breaks and after the interview might help to build rapport. Incorporating such breaks could also help with the cognitive difficulties that respondents with a depression experience more often.

Several limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the results of this study. While the use of an existing dataset such as NESDA offers access to extensive data, a limitation is the unavailability of certain relevant data. For example, information on interviewer characteristics such as prior interview experience would have been valuable as a covariate, as greater experience may be associated with improved interviewing skills. However, in the current analyses, we did not find differences in rapport between interviewers who conducted large versus small amount of interviews. Furthermore, a proxy for social desirable response was retrospectively based on available questions. This reliance on secondary data may also limit comparability and contribute to the mixed findings in the literature (West and Blom 2017). To address these limitations, it would be advisable for future research to focus specifically on the impact of interviewer–respondent interaction by employing an experimental design and relaxing CAPI constraints to allow observation of natural interviewer behavior, including deviations from standardization and question skipping.

In conclusion, respondent age showed the strongest associations with data quality and rapport. Furthermore, our results confirm that next to respondent age, the presence of depression and anxiety disorders are significant determinants of lower rapport between interviewer and participant. Additionally, current depression was associated with increased item nonresponse. These findings underscore the importance of focusing on data quality and effective rapport building in psychiatric research. Training should focus on evidence‐based techniques such as responsiveness, attentiveness, establishing common ground, courtesy, connection, information sharing, approachability, and personalization to enhance rapport and improve data quality (Gabbert et al. 2021; Garbarski et al. 2016; Webb and Barrett 2014).
