Impact of Flow Restrictors on Aerosol Delivery of the Respimat® Soft Mist Inhaler
Authors: Moritz Fleischhauer, Kai Berkenfeld, David Stadermann, Sitaram Velaga, Igor Gonda, Peter Langguth, Herbert Wachtel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe modification of an inhaler’s air flow resistance influences a patient’s inhalation flow profile, thereby affecting the exit velocity of an aerosol leaving the Respimat® mouthpiece. A slower inhalation maneuver results in reduced plume velocity and thus a decreased oropharyngeal deposition due to reduced impaction. This could not only lead to fewer unwanted side effects associated with inhaled therapies, but also enhance lung deposition.MethodsDevice prototypes with different air flow resistances were designed using custom-made inserts that can be clipped into the Respimat mouthpiece. The consequences on aerosol characteristics, as well as on in vitro deposition, were analyzed. Computational fluid dynamics simulations contributed to a better understanding of the modified aerodynamic conditions.ResultsDifferent insert geometries resulted in modified device resistances. However, an increased flow resistance does not necessarily result in an improved in vitro performance. The flow restrictors critically determine aerosol characteristics such as plume velocity and spray pattern, thereby altering in vitro deposition patterns. Quantitative data on mouth–throat deposition and aerosol characteristics are reported.ConclusionsIntegrating flow restrictors into the Respimat mouthpiece offers a promising approach to enhance patient centricity by promoting slower inhalation, thereby reducing the likelihood of suboptimal use. The use of a porous insert acting as a diffuser demonstrated minimal impact on internal airflow dynamics and in vitro deposition, suggesting that such designs can support correct inhalation technique without compromising aerosol performance. By minimizing the influence of patient-dependent factors, this strategy may help standardize the inhalation process and improve therapeutic outcomes.Video Abstract available for this article. Key Summary Points

The effectiveness of inhaled therapies is influenced by the inhalation technique. For soft mist inhalers, slow and deep inhalation is recommended to optimize drug delivery to the lungs.Modifying the airflow resistance may help guide patients toward a slower inhalation maneuver, potentially improving drug delivery and reducing oropharyngeal deposition.This study investigated how different inserts acting as flow restrictors inside the Respimat mouthpiece affect aerosol characteristics and in vitro deposition.Increased resistance alone did not consistently improve aerosol performance in vitro. Altered aerodynamic conditions inside the mouthpiece triggered by flow restrictors proved to be a key factor influencing both aerosol characteristics and deposition behavior.The use of a porous insert serving as a diffuser demonstrated minimal impact on internal airflow dynamics and in vitro deposition, suggesting that such designs can support correct inhalation technique without compromising aerosol performance.

Introduction

The Respimat® Soft Mist Inhaler (SMI) is a handheld, portable, multidose inhalation device used in the treatment of respiratory conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. SMIs do not require propellants like pressurized metered dose inhalers (pMDIs) for nebulization, making them more environmentally friendly [1]. Furthermore, SMIs do not rely on the patient’s inspiratory flow for effective nebulization of the dispersed drug as is required when using dry powder inhalers (DPIs) [2, 3]. Concerning DPIs, achieving the necessary flow rate can be challenging for children and the elderly [4]. Nebulization using the Respimat is purely mechanical—a spring is compressed when the inhaler is cocked and released once the inhaler is triggered. The inhaler functions by forcing a metered dose of liquid drug solution through a unique nozzle system. Two fine jets converge at a pre-set angle. This collision of the impinging jets generates the soft mist with a high fine particle fraction (FPF) of around 65–80% [5]. This exceeds values known from pMDIs and DPIs [6, 7]. The relatively long spray generation time of 1.2–1.5 s [5, 8, 9] facilitates the coordination of inhalation and actuation—a major problem occurring with pMDIs [10]. These key features, together with the slow velocity of the soft mist, result in a larger amount of drug reaching the lungs and less drug being deposited in the oropharynx [7, 11–13].

Alongside sedimentation and diffusion, impaction is one of the three main mechanisms by which inhaled particles are deposited in the airways. Impaction describes the deposition of particles due to inertia and occurs mainly for particles > 5 µm [14], yet any particle ≥ 0.5 µm can be subject to this mechanism [15, 16]. Inertial impaction can occur throughout the respiratory tract, beginning in the oral cavity [17] and oropharynx [18], extending down to the bronchial bifurcations [19]. Along this pathway, particles encounter regions of abrupt airflow direction changes and high velocities. Fast inhaled particles cannot follow the direction of flow of the air stream. Due to their inertia, the particles remain on their trajectory and collide with the walls. Oropharyngeal impaction is undesirable in inhalation processes and leads to loss of the active ingredient [18]. The drug may not reach the lung and this may increase variability [20]. Furthermore, deposition in the mouth–throat region can contribute to unwanted systemic side effects and may be responsible for local side effects, like oral thrush associated with inhaled corticosteroids [21, 22]. Reduced oropharyngeal impaction is therefore both desirable for the efficiency of drug delivery to the lungs and crucial in terms of therapy adherence.

In addition to particle size, aerosol velocity has a decisive influence on impaction. The exit velocity of an aerosol leaving the Respimat mouthpiece is directly affected by the air flow rate at which a patient inhales [23]. A previous study reported that deviations from the recommended slow and deep inhalation technique can occur during the use of SMIs [24]. Inserting flow restrictors to modify the Respimat mouthpiece and its intrinsic resistance could influence the patient’s inhalation flow profile and guide patients to inhale more slowly, thereby addressing the observed deviation from the recommended technique, which involves a slow and deep inhalation followed by a breath-hold to optimize drug delivery to the lungs [6].

Methods

Inhaler & Production of the Inserts

Respimat (Boehringer Ingelheim Pharma GmbH & Co. KG, Ingelheim am Rhein, Germany) containing an aqueous placebo solution was used to investigate plume velocity, spray pattern, and particle size distribution. The main features of the Respimat SMI are featured in the Introductory Video (see the video in the online/HTML version of the manuscript or follow the digital features link under the abstract).

Inserts were designed using SolidWorks® Premium 2019 (SP4.0 © 1995–2019, Dassault Systèmes, Waltham, MA, USA). Nine different restrictors were tested and compared against the unmodified device without insert (reference). Inserts 1–7 were produced using a Form 4B stereolithographic 3D-printer (Formlabs GmbH, Berlin, Germany). The material used for Inserts 2–6 was BioMed White Resin (Formlabs GmbH, Berlin, Germany). Insert 1 and Insert 7 were made of BioMed Clear Resin (Formlabs GmbH, Berlin, Germany)—a more flexible, opalescent material. Insert 8 and Insert 9 were made from polyester foam with a density of 0.02 g/cm3. The foam was cut to fit the mouthpiece of the Respimat and securely attached to ensure it remained in position at high flow rates. The height of Insert 8 and Insert 9 was approximately 5 mm. Table 1 and Supplement Fig. 1 provide an overview of the inserts and the respective device prototypes. Their resulting intrinsic resistances are shown in Table 1.

The pressure drops were measured at different flow rates in a setup according to USP < 601 > . The device resistance was calculated by plotting the square root of the resulting pressure drops against the respective flow rates (see Supplement Fig. 2). This procedure is similar to the one applied to DPIs [25]. Unless otherwise stated, all flow rates reported in this study refer to the volumetric flow exiting the mouthpiece (patient side). For plume front velocity and spray pattern measurements, airflow was applied from the outside into the inhaler from where it is directed towards the patient side. The plume exits the inhaler at the patient side.

Tracking of the plume front frame by frame using a video-based motion and skill analysis tool

Insert 1 features two small holes positioned right under the baffle on each side, which led to an increased flow resistance of 0.082 kPa0.5/(L/min)—an approximate sevenfold increase compared to the original Respimat with a resistance of 0.012 kPa0.5/(L/min). The highest flow resistance of 0.134 kPa0.5/(L/min) for Insert 2 was primarily attributed to the circular, small air inlets on each side, replacing the Respimat air vents. Additionally, a sealing ring shown in the cross-sectional view in Table 1 (highlighted in red) was incorporated, inhibiting airflow through the device housing. Insert 3 and Insert 4 share an identical internal geometry, characterized by a centrally positioned, horizontally aligned grid. The only difference lies in the size of the air inlets, resulting in a resistance of 0.084 kPa0.5/(L/min) for Insert 3, and a halved resistance of 0.041 kPa0.5/(L/min) for Insert 4 due to the use of two openings of twice the diameter. Insert 5 and Insert 6 were based on the same design principles as Insert 3 and 4, with the key difference being the repositioning of the central grid above the nozzle. The lateral air inlets remain unchanged, resulting in flow resistances that closely matched those of the two previous inserts. Insert 7 features a modified air inlet design with a slightly reduced vent surface area compared to the original Respimat mouthpiece, resulting in a moderately elevated flow resistance of 0.044 kPa0.5/(L/min). Inserts 8 and 9 introduced a new method for modulating flow resistance. Instead of changing the geometry of the air inlets, a porous foam material was used that acted as a diffuser. Insert 8 showed a flow resistance similar to that of Inserts 6 and 7. In contrast, Insert 9 had a higher flow resistance. This was due to the deeper placement of the foam, which caused slight compression. As a result, the material became denser and offered more resistance to airflow.

Insert Design Rationale

The sequence of insert designs followed a chronological development process. Insert 1 represents an early concept patented by Boehringer Ingelheim in 2008, which had not been further pursued at the time but was included in this study for comparison. Inserts 2–6 were developed in collaboration with Kenox and Niconox (both Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), exploring different geometries and airflow modifications. Based on the performance of these inserts, Insert 7 was designed to eliminate small lateral air inlets and grid structures, aiming to guide the airflow along the mouthpiece wall while shielding the aerosol cloud from the air stream. Inserts 8 and 9 were then developed using a porous foam material to address issues such as localized high flow velocities and particle backflow, aiming for a more uniform pressure drop and reduced internal deposition. This stepwise approach allowed for continuous optimization based on experimental findings. The mouthpiece deposition is continuously reduced (Fig. 6A) starting from Insert 1 to Insert 9.

Aerosol Plume Velocity Measurements

For plume velocity measurements, high-speed video recordings (Panasonic® HC-X1, Osaka, Japan) at 120 frames per second were taken in a dark field setting [26]. The arrangement of the equipment was set up identically to the study by Wachtel et al. [23]. In this setup, air was pushed through the inhaler air vents. Measurements were carried out in triplicate at 0 L/min, 15 L/min, 30 L/min, and 60 L/min. The flow rate was set using a breathing simulator (ASL 5000®, IngMar Medical, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Representative aerosol clouds captured during plume velocity measurements are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Image analysis was carried out as follows: First, MAGIX Movie Edit Pro® (MAGIX Software GmbH, Berlin, Germany) was utilized to manually identify the start when the aerosol emerged from the mouthpiece, as well as to adjust the brightness and contrast. Subsequently, a second video with millisecond timestamps was superimposed. SkillSpector (Version 1.3.2, Video4Coach, Aarhus, Denmark), a video motion and skill analysis software, was then employed to analyze the motion by digitizing the leading front of the aerosol plume frame by frame (Fig. 2).

The aerosol plume front was tracked over time, and the velocity 10 cm in front of the mouthpiece was determined using the fit function described by Hochrainer et al. [26], which calculates the velocity as the slope of the regression line of distance versus time. The distance of 10 cm was chosen by Hochrainer et al. as a representation of the oropharynx, where aerosol particles need to follow the curvature of the throat into the lower airways.

Analysis of the Resulting Spray Pattern

By creating a laser sheet (Bosch® PLL5, Bosch, Gerlingen, Germany) 3 cm in front of the nozzle in a dark field setting, the spray pattern was captured (Canon® EOS 760D, Ōta, Japan, 50 mm EF Lens). The experiments were conducted in triplicate without airflow and at 15 L/min, 30 L/min, and 60 L/min. The flow rate was set using the breathing simulator (ASL 5000®, IngMar Medical, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Air was pushed through the air vents of the Respimat. The first five frames after the initial detection of the aerosol cloud were selected for analysis, representing a time span of 0.2 s at a recording rate of 25 frames per second. Evaluation of the generated frames was carried out by the Vision Builder for Automated Inspection software (Version 2012, National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA). To assess the spray pattern, the parameter intensity was used, derived from the Red Avg value in the image analysis software. Higher intensity values reflect a more concentrated spray, while lower values suggest less aerosol or a more diffuse distribution. To assess the shape of the spray pattern, the aspect ratio (AR) was calculated. The Respimat was pre-aligned that only horizontal or vertical elongations of the spray pattern could occur. Using the Caliper function, the maximum horizontal and vertical extents of the spray cloud were measured. The AR was then defined as the ratio of width to height, providing a dimensionless indicator of the cloud’s elongation. Values greater than one indicate a horizontally stretched pattern, values close to one suggest a symmetrical or circular shape, and values below one reflect a vertically elongated spray.

Examination of the Particle Size Distribution

The potential influence of the inserts on aerosol particle size was evaluated using laser diffractometry, providing insight into any changes in the droplet size distribution (HELOS® BF, Sympatec GmbH, Clausthal-Zellerfeld, Germany). The measurements were conducted at three different flow rates (15 L/min, 30 L/min, 60 L/min) with an optical concentration ≥ 1.0% for trigger and a cut-off after 1.2 s. This matches the spray time of the Respimat stated in the literature [8, 9]. Five different Respimat devices were each measured once as a reference to record any fluctuations in particle size within the devices. Each of Inserts 1–7 were measured six times in total, using two different devices with three repetitions per device. Insert 8 and 9 were measured in triplicate. The particle size distribution was characterized by determining the FPF ≤ 5.0 µm, defined as the proportion of the sampled dose with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 5.0 µm. In addition, the x50 value was determined, representing the median particle diameter at which 50% of the aerosol particle volume is composed of particles smaller than this size.

Conducting the In Vitro Deposition Study

An in vitro deposition study was carried out to quantify the effect of the inserts. Methylene Blue (C.I. 52,015, Reag. Ph. Eur., Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used as a marker to allow for photometric quantitative analysis using an UV–VIS spectrophotometer UV-2102 PC (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) at a wavelength of 660 nm. A 2.0% (m/m) aqueous Methylene Blue solution was prepared with distilled water and filled into double-walled, collapsible bags, which are also used in the Respimat cartridges. 70% (m/m) ethanol was prepared from 99.8% denatured ethanol (Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) and distilled water for washing and analyzing three compartments. Measurements were carried out in triplicate at constant flow rates of 15 L/min, 30 L/min, and 60 L/min. Five puffs per run were administered by the Respimat into the setup shown in Fig. 3. The Respimat was connected to the inlet of the Alberta Idealized Throat (AIT). A dose tube was attached to the outlet of the AIT representing the simulated total lung dose (TLD). Connecting the dose tube to a vacuum pump allowed the three flow rates to be set using the TSI mass flow meter 4040 E (TSI Incorporated, Shoreview, MN, USA). The deposition in the three regions was analyzed quantitatively.

Computational Fluid Dynamics Simulation

Meshing and flow simulation was performed with ANSYS® Fluent software (Version 15.0.7, ANSYS, Canonsburg, PA, USA). An air flow rate of 30 L/min was set for all simulations to allow reference to the experimental measurements conducted at 30 L/min. Parameters and conditions used in the simulation were adopted from Longest and Hindle [27]. The density-based solver was chosen due to its ability to handle substantial pressure changes expected to occur at the lateral air inlets of the Respimat mouthpiece. The SST k-ω turbulence model was used to provide turbulence modeling, particularly in areas with complex flow separation and adverse pressure gradients. A porous-jump boundary condition was created for the simulation of Insert 8 and Insert 9, allowing the pressure drop over a surface to be represented.

To save computing power, a quarter of the mouthpiece was analyzed, as indicated by the orange box in Fig. 7. To simulate airflow conditions, a spherical inlet region (green) was attached to the lateral air vents, and an outlet region (red) was added to visualize flow behavior at the mouthpiece exit. These regions are represented by colored boxes in the simulation. Note that these geometries are artificial extensions used for CFD boundary conditions and do not correspond to physical components of the device. The airflow is depicted by velocity contours on the surface, with red zones indicating areas of high velocity. For Insert 1, Insert 4, and Insert 6 the emerging air flow was shown slightly offset on one plane and not on the entire mouthpiece surface to better visualize the flow conditions inside the lateral air inlets.

Statistics

Each insert was compared with the market version (reference) at three different flow rates. First, an F-test was conducted to assess the equality of variances. Depending on the outcome of the F-test, either a Student’s t test (for equal variances) or Welch’s t test (for unequal variances) was applied. Statistical significance was defined as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***). Non-significant results were indicated as “n.s.” (not significant). All values are reported as mean ± one standard deviation.

Ethics

This analysis is an in vitro analysis and did not involve any human participants or animals. Therefore, the study did not require ethical approval.

Results

Aerosol Plume Front Velocity Measurements

Plume velocity was measured three times with the Respimat actuated into quiescent ambient air without any externally applied airflow, in order to verify the analytical method by comparing the values obtained with those previously published. The plume velocity of 0.94 m/s (± 0.03 m/s) without airflow found in this study is close to the value of 0.8 m/s reported for Respimat SMI plume velocity in literature [26], suggesting a reliable analytical method.

Figure 4 illustrates the recorded values at three investigated flow rates. As anticipated, plume velocity increased with higher flow rates for all inserts. At 15 L/min, only Insert 2 and Insert 7 showed a significantly enhanced plume velocity. At 30 L/min, Inserts 2–3, Insert 5 and Insert 7 exhibited a slight increase in plume velocity relative to the reference. At 60 L/min, the aerosol plumes for Inserts 2–8 were accelerated compared to the reference, with Insert 7 achieving the highest recorded values across all flow rates. Notably, Insert 1, Insert 8 and Insert 9 showed plume velocities that revealed no significant deviation from the reference across all investigated flow rates, except the value for Insert 8 at 60 L/min.

Conducting the In Vitro Deposition Study

Deposition inside the mouthpiece (Fig. 6A) increased at 15 L/min for Inserts 1–6, while no significant deviation was observed for Inserts 7–9 compared to the reference. At 30 L/min all inserts showed a significant increase in mouthpiece deposition in vitro. At 60 L/min, increased values were also found for all inserts, except for Insert 7. The lowest mouthpiece deposition among the investigated inserts was measured for Inserts 7–9.

At 15 L/min, an enhanced AIT deposition was found for Inserts 2–4, as well as for Insert 7 and Insert 9 (Fig. 6B). At 30 L/min, all investigated inserts showed a significantly elevated AIT deposition. Deposition values increased at 60 L/min for Insert 2, Insert 4, and Inserts 6–9. The highest recorded values across all flow rates were detected for Insert 7.

With increasing co-flow, the simulated TLD decreased (Fig. 6C) and simultaneously deposition inside the AIT increased for all inserts, as the generated aerosol plume was accelerated more and thus oropharyngeal impaction increased. Among the inserts, the highest simulated TLD values were observed for Insert 8 and Insert 9.

Discussion

Throughout the following section, references to plume velocity (Fig. 4), particle size (Fig. 5), spray intensity and shape (Table 2), and in vitro deposition data (Fig. 6) consistently relate to the figures and table presented in the results section. CFD simulations shown in Fig. 7 illustrate the prevailing aerodynamic conditions that support the explanation of the experimental data.

To evaluate the impact of the insert designs, measurements with the original Respimat (no insert) were included in all experiments and served as a consistent reference for comparison. The plume velocity for the reference increased steadily with increasing flow rate, while spray intensity decreased, likely due to aerosol dilution at higher air velocities. The resulting spray pattern was horizontally elongated, with an AR of ~1.4 at 30 L/min and 60 L/min. In vitro data showed that mouthpiece deposition remained stable across all flow rates. However, AIT deposition increased notably at 60 L/min, which could be attributed to the elevated plume velocity and increased impaction. As a result, simulated TLD in vitro decreased at 60 L/min. CFD simulation revealed high airflow velocities of up to 15 m/s behind the lateral air inlets. At the mouthpiece outlet, air expands in the space above the air vents. This may entrain particles, which motivates the horizontally elongated aerosol plume. The airflow emerging from the nozzle was modeled based on parameters adapted from Longest and Hindle [27], using a simplified jet introduced through the spray orifice to avoid two-way momentum coupling.

Although Insert 1 introduced a substantial increase in flow resistance, plume front velocity remained unchanged across all flow rates, while particle size decreased slightly at 60 L/min. This may also account for the low AIT deposition in vitro, as the influencing factors of impaction—velocity and size of the particle—remained largely unchanged. However, the highest mouthpiece deposition among the inserts, approximately twice that of the reference at all flow rates, and, consequently, a reduced simulated TLD was found. This elevated mouthpiece deposition accounted for the reduction of the aerosol intensity, which decreased to approximately one-fifth at 60 L/min. Both could be attributed to particle backflow within the Respimat mouthpiece. The airflow passes through the holes below the baffle at high speed, as shown in Fig. 7. According to the Bernoulli principle, this creates a low-pressure zone that may pull particles back into the mouthpiece, causing particle loss inside the device. In contrast to the reference, the airflow is more centrally directed and escapes towards the vertical direction. This flow pattern could account for the vertically elongated spray shape shown in Table 2, reflected by an AR of 0.68 at 30 L/min.

As shown in the CFD simulations (Fig. 7), the airflow through Insert 2 is strongly influenced by the narrowed lateral inlets and the angled internal grid structure. The constriction of the lateral air inlets leads to accelerated airflow and thus to the formation of a localized negative pressure zone causing particles to flow back into the mouthpiece (recirculation). This could explain the higher in vitro mouthpiece deposition at all flow rates and the reduced aerosol intensity at 30 L/min and 60 L/min. Furthermore, the lateral airflow emerging from the mouthpiece after passing the angled grid may entrain aerosol particles, and redirect them sideways. This flow behavior likely contributed to the horizontally elongated spray shape indicated by an AR above 1.3, resembling that of the reference.

Despite the differing flow resistances of Insert 3 and Insert 4, both inserts demonstrated very similar aerosol characteristics. A slight increase in plume front velocity was observed for Insert 2 compared to the reference at 30 L/min, with both showing elevated velocities at 60 L/min. A reduction in spray intensity was observed at 30 L/min and 60 L/min, accompanied by a more circular spray shape, as indicated by an AR close to 1. In vitro, both inserts led to a significantly increased mouthpiece deposition, resulting in a reduced simulated TLD. CFD simulations support these findings. The airflow entering through the lateral inlets reaches high velocities—more pronounced in Insert 3 due to the smaller inlet diameter. The resulting pressure gradient leads to a recirculating flow pattern, which may draw particles back into the mouthpiece, explaining the elevated deposition and weakened aerosol signal. In addition to the lateral inlets, the centrally positioned grid appears to play a key role in enhancing mouthpiece deposition. As the airflow passes through the grid, it is locally accelerated and focused, which not only promotes particle impaction within the mouthpiece but also contributes to a more symmetrical, circular spray pattern. This focused flow reduces the relative influence of the lateral inlets on the overall aerosol behavior, explaining the similar deposition characteristics observed for both inserts despite their differing inlet geometries. The airflow passing through the grid remains highly concentrated above the structure, further reinforcing the circularity of the spray. Notably, in the CFD visualization of Insert 4, the velocity contours were slightly shifted within the plane to better illustrate the flow through the narrowed air vents. The characteristic lateral jets, visible as two vertical turquoise streams for Insert 3, also appear with Insert 4 if the velocity contours were displayed across the full surface.

Although Insert 5 and Insert 6 exhibited flow resistances similar to those of Inserts 3 and 4, their aerosol characteristics differed notably. Both inserts exhibited a slightly elevated plume front velocity at 60 L/min, consistent with the behavior of Insert 3 and Insert 4. However, spray intensity varied at 30 L/min: Insert 6 showed intensity levels comparable to the reference, while Insert 5 demonstrated a marked reduction. The spray shape also differed—Insert 5 produced a horizontally elongated plume, shown by an AR of approximately 1.3 similar to the reference, whereas Insert 6 generated a more circular spray pattern. This discrepancy is notable given the similar geometries. One possible explanation lies in the airflow behavior observed in CFD simulations: for Insert 6, the airflow passing through the grid above the nozzle remains centrally aligned and focused, potentially stabilizing the spray and promoting circularity. In contrast, no such central focusing effect was evident in Insert 5, which may account for the elongated shape. In vitro data revealed distinct differences in deposition behavior between these two inserts, too. Insert 5 exhibited higher mouthpiece deposition—nearly 50% of the delivered dose across all flow rates—indicating substantial particle retention and deposition. This effect may be driven by the high local flow velocities at the lateral air inlets, which are known to promote particle backflow and impaction, as observed in previous designs. In addition, droplet impaction may occur on the grid positioned directly above the nozzle, which could intercept particles before they exit the mouthpiece, further increasing particle retention. However, these grid structures avoid a baffle effect because of their large central openings immediately in front of the nozzle. As a result of the recirculation, both inserts led to a reduced simulated TLD, while deposition in the AIT remained at low levels.

Among all tested inserts, Insert 7 produced the highest plume front velocities at both 30 L/min and 60 L/min. This might suggest a link to the increased deposition in the AIT. However, the comparison with Insert 6—which showed only a slightly reduced plume velocity but still low AIT deposition—suggests that velocity alone was unlikely to be the sole contributing factor. FPF showed a slight increase at 30 L/min and 60 L/min, though this variation appeared to be within the range of measurement fluctuations and did not reflect a consistent insert-related effect. The spray pattern was nearly circular, accompanied by a slight reduction in intensity at higher flow rates. Interestingly, mouthpiece deposition did not exceed reference levels, except for a slight increase at 30 L/min, indicating that the reduction in simulated TLD may be attributed to other factors than with previous investigated inserts. CFD simulations provide further insight into the flow dynamics. Although high velocities occur at the lateral air inlets, the insert’s geometry prevents direct particle backflow into the mouthpiece. The sloped insert surface lacks perforations, effectively shielding the negative pressure zone created by an airflow at high velocity through the air inlet. Instead, the lateral airstream exits at the very edge of the mouthpiece and spreads broadly behind. Unlike the more outward-directed lateral jet seen for Insert 2, this flow remains centered and fan-shaped, potentially contributing to the formation of a circular aerosol plume. The pronounced AIT deposition may be linked to this lateral airstream. While no direct turbulence effects are visible in the simulation, it is conceivable that the redirected jet induces subtle flow disturbances or recirculation zones within the AIT, enhancing particle impaction in this area. Further investigation is needed to confirm this hypothesis and clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Despite differences in foam placement and resulting flow resistance, Insert 8 and Insert 9 demonstrated comparable plume front velocities; at all tested flow rates, both inserts consistently matched the plume front velocity of the reference device, with one exception: at 60 L/min, Insert 8 showed a slightly elevated value. Among all investigated inserts, both inserts exhibited the highest spray intensity values at flow rates of 30 L/min and 60 L/min, while simultaneously demonstrating the lowest levels of mouthpiece deposition. Although AIT deposition increased at 60 L/min for both inserts—leading to a slight reduction in simulated TLD at higher flow rates—the values still remained the highest among all tested configurations. CFD simulations revealed flow fields closely resembling those of the reference. Slightly elevated velocities were observed at the air inlets, though not as pronounced as in the original design. The porous foam acts as a homogenizing element, distributing the airflow evenly through the mouthpiece. In contrast to the reference, where an outward-directed jet contributes to a horizontally elongated spray, the foam appeared to center the airflow more efficiently. This may have led to a more focused aerosol plume and the observed circular spray pattern.

While this study provides valuable insights into the altered aerosol performance and aerodynamic conditions using various flow restrictors, some limitations should be considered. The experiments were conducted under controlled in vitro conditions and complemented by CFD simulations. We used the AIT model, which may not fully reflect the variability of real-world inhalation behavior or anatomical differences among patients. Additionally, the study focused on technical performance and did not assess clinical outcomes or user experience. Future research involving in vivo studies and patient-centered evaluations will be important to confirm the clinical relevance and practical applicability of the proposed modifications.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that integrating flow restrictors into the Respimat mouthpiece can significantly influence aerosol performance—not only through the magnitude of the resulting flow resistance, but more importantly through the geometric modifications they introduce to the mouthpiece itself. These geometric changes alter the internal airflow dynamics, which in turn affect plume velocity, the emerging aerosol geometry and intensity, and ultimately the deposition behavior within the mouthpiece and the oropharyngeal region.

While increased flow resistance may guide patients toward slower inhalation and thereby reduce oropharyngeal impaction, increased flow resistance alone is not a reliable predictor of improved performance. Several insert designs altered the aerosol plume by accelerating or deflecting it, leading to increased deposition in non-target regions. In contrast, a flow restrictor consisting of a porous material acting as a diffuser proved to be a promising approach: it increased flow resistance while minimally altering the prevailing aerodynamic conditions. As a result, the aerosol cloud remained centered and stable, and the simulated TLD was largely preserved.

Beyond technical performance, the use of inserts offers a valuable opportunity to improve patient centricity. By encouraging slower inhalation, inserts may help address commonly observed deviations from the recommended inhalation technique with SMIs, such as insufficiently slow and deep inhalation [24]. This can potentially reduce oropharyngeal impaction and enhance drug delivery to the lower respiratory tract. By minimizing the variability introduced by the patient’s inhalation technique, this approach may improve the inhalation process with SMIs, leading to even better therapeutic outcomes and an improved treatment experience.

Future research should explore how different insert designs affect user experience and therapeutic outcomes in clinical settings. Evaluating patient preferences, ease of use, and adherence could help translate technical improvements into meaningful clinical benefits and support the development of more patient-centric inhalation devices.
