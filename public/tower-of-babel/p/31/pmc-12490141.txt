The development of gender- and sex-diverse standardized patients for interprofessional learning: a community-based participatory approach
Authors: Jacob R. Siegel, Scout Silverstein, Natalie Florescu, Jacqueline R. Klevan, Faith Sullivan, Harrison Van Der Kloot, Sarah Bauerle Bass, Jamie L. Garfield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study introduced a novel approach to developing standardized patient (SP) cases for training medical residents and clinical support staff in providing competent and affirming care to gender- and sex-diverse (GSD) individuals. Conducted at a large urban academic medical center, the UNIQUE Project (UNiting in Interprofessional Learning to Serve the QUeer Community Through Education) established the UNIQUE Advisory Board (UAB) to integrate GSD community voices into SP case development. The UAB consisted of GSD community members, Temple University Health Systems faculty and residents, and Lewis Katz School of Medicine faculty and students. GSD community members served as the driving force behind the UAB and collaboratively created four SP cases that addressed experiences impacting care for GSD patients. The UAB employed a community-based participatory research framework to prioritize authenticity, cultural competency, and inclusion in medical training. The LKSOM Simulation Center recruited, trained, and utilized GSD SPs, expanding representation of GSD individuals among the SP actor pool and increasing fidelity of character portrayal. Interprofessional SP encounters invited both medical residents and clinical support staff to develop GSD-focused empathy, communication skills, and cultural sensitivity. The UNIQUE project highlighted the value of interdisciplinary training for enhancing care coordination and providing informed, culturally aware care for GSD patients at all points of a healthcare encounter. The inclusion of community-informed SP cases represented an advancement in bridging gaps in medical education, particularly for clinical support staff who undergo minimal training in GSD-affirming care and often have not been invited to participate in experiential learning like SP encounters. This project emphasized the importance of integrating marginalized voices into medical education and showed how community-informed SP encounters can address systemic biases, improve patient experiences, and foster a more inclusive healthcare environment. These findings contribute to ongoing efforts to standardize GSD-affirming practices across medical education programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07884-3.

Background

Gender and sex diverse healthcare experiences

Gender- and sex-diverse (GSD) individuals, including transgender, nonbinary, intersex, and/or gender-expansive often report negative experiences with healthcare providers and ineffective or harmful treatment practices [1–3]. In one study of thirty GSD adults, patients described healthcare experiences as “traumatic,” “stigmatizing,” “marginalizing,” and “objectifying.” [3] Reported provider behaviors in the healthcare setting included misgendering, pathologizing patient identities, and misinterpreting lab results [2, 3]. Even when patients were correctly gendered in person, misgendering in healthcare records was common [3]. GSD individuals who identify as members of more than one marginalized group, such as trans people of color, may face more negative encounters [1, 4]. These harmful experiences cause GSD individuals to mistrust healthcare systems, reducing utilization and worsening health disparities [5, 6]. In the 2022 United States Transgender Survey, 24% of respondents avoided care due to fear of mistreatment and 48% of respondents reported at least one negative healthcare experience within the past year, up from 33% in years prior [7]. This growing mistreatment has increased healthcare avoidance for GSD individuals [2, 5, 6].

Current gaps in medical education regarding GSD populations

Negative healthcare interactions for GSD patients partly stem from inadequate provider education [8, 9]. Undergraduate and graduate medical education often lacks training on GSD patient care, including case-based learning with GSD standardized patients (SPs), didactic curricula, and/or clinical exposure [8, 9]. Consequently, GSD patients are frequently left to educate their providers, eroding their trust in the medical system [2, 6]. Intersex communities have emphasized the need for increased medical education, specifically about intersex variations, patient perspectives, and iatrogenic harm [2]. Community-based research has emphasized the importance of addressing crucial issues such as poverty, sex work, and non-prescription hormone use [3]. Failure to consider GSD-specific contexts and needs risks further harm, underscoring the importance of informed, inclusive care.

In Temple University Health System’s (TUHS) Internal Medicine Residency program, residents receive GSD training through lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer/questioning, intersex, and asexual (LGBTQIA+) lectures, journal clubs, and patient panels. Residents care for GSD patients in primary care clinic and/or inpatient care. TUHS patient-facing employees are required by Human Resources to complete LGBTQIA + sensitivity training when hired. While some departments offer optional gender-affirming language training, no other trainings for competency or sensitivity are mandated.

Use of SPs to train medical students and residents

SPs simulate real-world scenarios, allowing learners to practice patient interactions in a controlled environment. SPs are trained actors who provide consistent, realistic patient portrayals, supporting the development of technical and interpersonal skills, as well as cultural competency, without risking harm to real patients [8, 10]. However, existing frameworks for producing SP cases typically prioritize consistency at the expense of authenticity and risk of stereotyping [9]. Incorporating patient voices into curricula is essential for enhancing depth, relatability, and sensitivity – core principles of this project [11]. Expanding gender-inclusive curricula must be accompanied by intentional SP hiring practices. Fidelity in case portrayal can be SP-dependent; studies have documented individual variability in communication and affect, which was also evident in our study [12, 13]. However, when GSD SPs portray GSD cases, their diverse lived experiences enhance authenticity, emotional resonance, and potential impact of the learning experience [12, 13].

Aims of the paper

At TUHS, a large academic non-profit urban medical center, where this research was conducted, residents and clinical support staff, such as medical assistants and intake coordinators, who are often the first point of patient contact, encounter GSD individuals in their clinical care. However, with the existing didactic LGBTQIA + curriculum, TUHS residents reported feeling unprepared to provide competent care for GSD individuals. Only 60% of United States and Canadian medical schools teach intersex medical needs, and many of these approaches may not comprehensively address affirming care for intersex individuals [14]. Many medical providers who deliver GSD-affirming care sought education and training outside of medical school curricula [15].

The UNIQUE project was formed to respond to a critical opportunity in healthcare: supporting needs of GSD individuals. Its name—UNiting in Interprofessional Learning to Serve the QUeer Community Through Education (UNIQUE)—reflects its mission. UNIQUE focused on training resident physicians and clinical support staff, as every individual involved in the patient journey requires sensitivity and competence in caring for GSD patients. In addition to interprofessional participation, the foundation of this project’s success lies in the recruitment of the UNIQUE Advisory Board (UAB) and in simulatory, experiential learning. This paper outlines our application of a community-based participatory research (CBPR) framework to develop replicable SP cases for use in graduate medical education training.

Development process

Development of the UNIQUE advisory board

The UNIQUE Project was developed to create SP case simulations, informed by GSD community members, who were invited to serve on the UAB. We tasked the UAB with conceptualizing and authoring four SP cases and evaluation tools. To reinforce a CBPR approach, we synthesized a board that included equal parts external GSD community members and internal TUHS and Lewis Katz School of Medicine (LKSOM) faculty, staff, and students, all of whom are stakeholders in advancing GSD care. The UAB’s design aimed to move beyond traditional focus groups toward a collaborative forum that integrates GSD people into development, refinement, and execution of the learning tools. The internal UNIQUE team recruited community board members with a multifaceted approach, utilizing local LGBTQIA + Facebook groups, flyers at LGBTQIA + health facilities, personal connections, and outreach from LGBTQIA + resource centers. The foundation, rationale, and outcome of SP cases were explained as supporting equitable care for the GSD community. Other information included was time commitment, project duration, and compensation. We offered participants either $35 per hour or authorship on subsequent manuscripts and abstracts. Researchers interviewed respondents to assess interests, motivations, and lived experiences.

Nine individuals external to TUHS were selected as community board members to ensure diverse representation of gender, sex traits, race, ethnicity, occupations, and sexual orientations. For those who chose hourly compensation, funding was provided through the Building Trust Through Diversity grant (#19113028), funded by the Alliance for Academic Internal Medicine, the American Board of Internal Medicine, the American College of Physicians, and the Josiah Macy Jr. Foundation.

Nine additional individuals internal to TUHS were selected for the UAB: the Principal Investigator (PI) and Director of the Internal Medicine Residency LGBTQIA + curriculum, a gender-affirming surgeon, a physician who provides LGBTQIA + primary care, a bioethicist and Doctor of Law, a gender-affirming care navigator and Licensed Social Worker, the Director of the SP program, an LGBTQIA+-identifying internal medicine resident, and both LGBTQIA + student group chairs.

Case development

The UAB convened monthly via video conference over nine months to develop the cases and evaluation tools. Early meetings focused on content generation during which we invited community board members to describe experiences and perspectives related to healthcare and its interactions with the GSD community. To center a CBPR approach, researchers encouraged community board members to shape the structure of meetings in collaboration with the internal UNIQUE team. Insights from early meetings informed discussion prompts for subsequent meetings, allowing for an iterative process. Initially, researchers provided structure by posing questions (e.g., “What contributes to healthcare avoidance?”), after which conversation progressed organically. Community board members had opportunities to speak to the entire board and meet in smaller groups to facilitate deeper, more private conversation. We encouraged breakout sessions led by community board members to serve as listening opportunities for the internal team. We facilitated regular check-ins with community board members, gauging safety and support, understanding that participation sometimes required personal disclosure of negative experiences.

Attendees used a virtual whiteboarding platform to map ideas for case development. Members who preferred asynchronous or anonymous sharing used a virtual bulletin board to answer prompts ranging from how board meetings should be run to issues cases should address. Employing familiarity with SP case utilization, researchers organized and honed contributions into themes such as “misgendering,” “hormone therapy,” and many others. Community board members verified themes for accuracy and relevance to lived experiences. Themes were distilled and distributed among four cases to address a spectrum of identities, histories, anatomies, diagnoses, and barriers to care, as demonstrated in Supplementary Material 1. The UNIQUE team prepared drafts of each case for board members to edit asynchronously, then review as a group. Final drafts incorporated themes into a story of patients, their experiences, and desired impacts of each case (Supplementary Material 1).

Training and implementation

SP recruitment, hiring, and training

We collaborated with the Simulation Center at the LKSOM to recruit and train GSD SPs for the UNIQUE project (Supplementary Materials 2 and 3). LKSOM SPs are part-time employees scheduled on a per-project basis. We distributed an informational email to all 81 LKSOM SPs with project aims, background on SP case development and authoring, and a brief description of patients’ key medical concerns, psychosocial concerns, and gender identity. Interested SPs received additional details. This approach allowed for inclusion of SPs who were unknown to us as GSD and permitted self-identification to their own comfort. Internal recruitment yielded seven GSD SPs. One additional GSD individual was recruited for UNIQUE cases and offered a permanent SP position.

Simulation Center leadership facilitated training in partnership with the PI and a GSD SP who served as consultant and co-trainer. One three-hour training session was held for experienced SPs with an additional hour of paid time. Training included review of cases, role portrayals, learner assessment, and practicing feedback.

We trained SPs to facilitate approximately 5-minute feedback sessions. SPs elicited self-reflection from residents and clinical support staff. SPs are skilled in communicating how verbal and non-verbal communication translates to patient comfort, safety, and satisfaction. Feedback was based on communication and interpersonal skills that impact feelings of comfort and emotional safety. SPs were guided by learning objectives, case content, and learner reflection to highlight salient points. Some feedback discussion points included correct use of pronouns, comfort with direct questioning about sexual practices, and approaching goals for gender-affirming care, which offered a simulated patient perspective and potential impact.

We conducted training in accordance with the Association of SP Educators’ Standards of Best Practice and implemented the standard of Psychological Safety throughout program development [16]. SP limitations and safety were considered throughout case development. Recruitment processes provided clarity, transparency, and the ability to opt in and out as needed. For example, we recruited SPs before cases were completed, and as cases changed, gave the option to switch roles according to comfort with patient presentation. We solicited SP feedback throughout the training and in debriefings, expanding insight and the pool of stakeholders. We offered multiple formats (email, phone, private or group debrief) to talk with staff, the PI, and other SPs. We provided breaks between encounters, a staff lounge, and ability to leave the space as necessary.

Evaluation tools and learner assessment

We invited resident and clinical support staff to provide feedback about their experience, including reflexive feedback on their performance and confidence in navigating the SP encounter. Thirty-four residents and 11 clinical support staff participated. Residents and clinical support staff received feedback from SPs, who provided both affirming comments on effective communication and constructive suggestions for improving care delivery. The feedback was in an open forum format. We obtained consent from participants for video recording of encounters, some of which were brought to the UAB for discussion on outcomes.

Executing and evaluating SP encounters

We conducted encounters over four 4-hour sessions in August 2023 for TUHS Internal Medicine residents and clinical support staff. We obtained informed consent from all participants prior to participating in SP encounters. Sessions required rooms with audio and video recording, two-way mirrors and audio output for learner observation. Orientation guides, door instructions, and consent forms were provided. A brief orientation preceded the sessions, detailed in the UNIQUE Logistics Guide (Supplementary Material 4). The encounters began outside of rooms with participant instructions (Supplementary Material 5). Cases took one of two possible formats: two residents, or one resident and one clinical support staff. Sequential participation permitted opportunities for both individual and group reflection.

Preliminary evaluation

Early qualitative data reflect a mixed but instructive picture of the SP encounters. Residents often demonstrated effective, affirming communication, including active listening, correct pronoun use, modeling inclusive language, empathy, trauma-informed approaches, and clear explanations of sensitive questions. Simultaneously, SP encounters highlighted gaps in medical knowledge, missed diagnosis/identification, and general non-affirming language, often stemming from assumptions, failure to explore key elements of patient identity, or overlooked opportunities to provide psychoeducation. Others flagged uncertainty or awkwardness discussing transition-related care, underscoring the need for deeper clinical integration. These findings reinforce that while the program is supporting growth in affirming care, sustained attention to reflective practice, content knowledge, and structural humility will be essential to maximize long-term impact. For example, many residents framed gender transition as having a linear endpoint, suggesting a need for greater depth in understanding gender trajectories.

These early reflections suggest that while the program is fostering awareness and skill development, it also exposes persistent discomfort and areas of unawareness in provider communication. The combination of SP praise and critique reinforces the importance of continuing to scaffold clinical knowledge with opportunities for reflection, feedback, and practice. Future iterations may benefit from addressing common missteps more explicitly and providing additional support for navigating identity-related conversations with greater fluency and humility.

Discussion

We believe this study represents many firsts. While existing GSD SP simulations span specialties and professions, many involved GSD community members only as reviewers and providers of feedback on practitioner-developed content. Surveys show that GSD SPs continue to advocate for involvement in curricular development [15–17]. We believe our approach is novel in integration of community members prior to development of educational content. These external participants valued the opportunity to articulate often-overlooked nuances of medical encounters and transform negative experiences into teaching opportunities.

To our knowledge, this is the first use of GSD SP cases designed for both residents and clinical support staff, promoting interprofessional accountability and respect. We facilitated interactions and interdisciplinary observations between residents and clinical support staff, furthering understanding of team roles and appreciation of coordination and communication improving patient care. We built on previous studies that highlighted the effectiveness of SP encounters in teaching cultural competency, empathy, and interdisciplinary communication [18, 19]. Nursing programs have integrated SPs to simulate encounters with vulnerable populations, which enhanced student confidence, clinical skills, and cultural sensitivity [20, 21]. Physical therapy programs have used SPs to teach and/or evaluate students on interview skills, musculoskeletal exams, and treatment [22]. While previous authors have noted the impact of interprofessional education on healthcare outcomes, we found that few utilized SP encounters to train clinical support staff, often patients’ first point of contact, in empathy, communication, and trust [23–27]. We recognized that while medical students and residents often learn through SP encounters, this modality has rarely been used to educate clinical support staff. Our methodology provided them with a valuable learning experience.

We advocate standardizing the use of community-informed SP encounters across medical education programs to bridge existing gaps in training and better prepare providers to meet needs of marginalized populations. We designed a methodology for creating encounters by leveraging direct input from local GSD community members, incorporating interprofessional education that can be replicated for any marginalized group for whom healthcare disparities exist and whose voices in medical education are often suppressed. Our approach enhances relevance and authenticity of the training while aligning with broader calls for more inclusive and standardized medical education frameworks that address systemic biases and improve patient care.

Positionality

The UNIQUE research team comprises individuals with diverse identities, including cisgender and GSD perspectives, heterosexual and marginalized sexual orientations, and predominantly white members, with one Asian contributor. The community board members, all holding GSD identities, represented non-binary, transmasculine, transfeminine, and intersex perspectives, and were predominantly white, with two Black participants. Some UAB members had lived experiences of financial and housing insecurity and sex work. Contributors’ ages ranged from 20 s to 50 s and primarily held roles in education or clinical practice. We recognize that our team’s lived experiences and potential blind spots influenced the formulation of SP cases. To address this, we incorporated stories and experiences from peers to inform conceptualization of GSD concerns.

Limitations

Although the UAB helped ensure a culturally attuned and community-driven study design, limitations remain. Considering the wide range of UAB priorities distilled into four SP cases, time constraints (10 to 20 min per encounter) and need for defined learning objectives may have excluded some UAB perspectives. We mitigated this by providing SP case orientation and explanations regarding adaptation of content to meet project aims and best practice in SP methodology. For instance, balancing patient story specificity with the feasibility of finding SPs to portray the role occasionally yielded variation. Recruiting GSD SPs to the UAB could have improved alignment between case detail and performance flexibility. Despite tailoring of cases, the wide range of patient concerns and identities lend to improvisation, and therefore possible script deviation. A longer SP orientation could have allowed GSD SPs to engage deeper in case development, reflect on personal experiences, and portray patients with greater fidelity. Finally, SP feedback is critical for learners to gain insights into interpersonal communication and its impact, but feedback time is typically brief and shared with peers. Extending the debriefing with SPs could foster richer and more personalized discussions regarding meeting case objectives.

Future directions

This paper outlines the UNIQUE project’s approach to developing a community-engaged advisory board, advancement of existing educational resources in medical training, and expansion of the Standardized Patient Center’s diversity, inclusivity, and involvement in case design and implementation.

This research represents advancement in medical pedagogy and provides a framework for inclusive future curricular and training development. The UNIQUE project achieved two key impacts: engagement of SPs’ voices in case development and the move toward greater gender inclusivity in graduate medical education SP curriculum. This increased cases with GSD patients and therefore work opportunities for GSD individuals as SPs. The full impact of this work remains to be demonstrated as these cases evolve and are tested amongst medical trainees and clinical support staff. However, the methodology used to solicit community voices to improve and expand medical education could be used broadly to enrich existing materials and promote issues faced by any marginalized group.

Drawing from the UAB’s experiences, greater integration of community voices could significantly enhance medical education, address systemic biases in medical training, and improve patient experiences and outcomes.
