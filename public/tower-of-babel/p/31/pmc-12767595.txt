The association of relative deprivation, interpersonal relationships, and problematic social media use in 39 countries/regions: Does school contextual inequality matter?
Authors: Zékai (Zachary) Lu, Zelin Liu, Danhua Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media use among adolescents has rapidly expanded, raising concerns about problematic social media use (PSMU). Few studies have examined how relative deprivation (RD) and contextual inequality shape PSMU. We explain whether four key interpersonal relationships (relationships with teachers, classmates, and family, and bullying victimization) mediate the link between RD and PSMU and whether school-level inequality moderates these associations.MethodThe data was from the 2017/2018 Health Behavior in School-aged Children (190,707 adolescents, 5,576 schools, 39 countries/regions). The independent variable was RD, calculated via the Yitzhaki index, and the dependent variable was PSMU. Four interpersonal relationships (teacher, classmate, and family relationships, and bullying victimization) were assessed as mediators. Multilevel mediation models were used to evaluate the mediating roles of these relationships. We then extracted each country/region's indirect effects for cross-national comparisons. The Johnson-Neyman method was used to assess the moderating effect of contextual inequality.ResultsAlthough RD had no direct effect on PSMU, higher RD was associated with poorer teacher, classmate, and family relationships and heightened bullying victimization. These then predicted increased PSMU. Contextual inequality strengthened RD's adverse association with family relationships and magnified the associations of family relationships and bullying on PSMU. Cross-national analyses revealed stable mediating effects of teacher and classmate relationships.ConclusionsWe highlight RD's associations with PSMU primarily through interpersonal pathways, with contextual inequality amplifying them. Targeted interventions that address both socioeconomic disparities and interpersonal dynamics can help mitigate PSMU. These outcomes underscore the need for policies that reduce economic gaps and foster supportive relationships.Keywords: relative deprivation, problematic social media use, interpersonal relationships, contextual inequality, multilevel mediation, cross-national comparison

Introduction

With the popularity of applications like TikTok and Instagram among adolescents, social media has become deeply integrated into their daily lives, serving as a crucial channel for self-expression and establishing social connections (Smahel et al., 2020). According to Vogels et al. (2022), over 90% of U.S. adolescents use at least one social media platform daily. While moderate social media use can facilitate adolescents' social network expansion, information literacy development, and civic engagement (Boer et al., 2020; Skoric, Zhu, Goh, & Pang, 2016), problematic social media use (PSMU) may lead to various psychological and behavioral issues (Boer et al., 2020). Crucially, the motivation behind social media engagement significantly determines its impact; adolescents who use platforms primarily for social connection report higher well-being, whereas use for entertainment is linked to poorer outcomes and greater substance use (Boniel-Nissim et al., 2022).

PSMU is defined as “being overly concerned about social media, to be driven by a strong motivation to log on to or use social media platforms and to devote so much time and effort to social media that it impairs other social activities, studies/job, interpersonal relationships, and/or psychological health and well-being” (Andreassen & Pallesen, 2014). PSMU has been linked to psychological distress, including heightened anxiety, depression (Andreassen et al., 2016), and loneliness (Keles, McCrae, & Grealish, 2020), along with adverse academic outcomes (Zimmer, 2022). PSMU may also trigger identity crises as adolescents increasingly derive social value from social media interactions, potentially leading to distorted self-perceptions (Wang et al., 2021). Furthermore, PSMU seldom occurs in isolation, often clustering with other risk behaviors such as substance use and unhealthy lifestyle habits, with such multi-risk profiles being associated with the poorest mental health outcomes (Walsh et al., 2020).

Digital well-being theory advocates moving beyond binary classifications of social media effects as beneficial or detrimental, emphasizing instead its impact on individual autonomy (Büchi, 2024; Vanden Abeele, 2021). Indeed, considerable debate persists regarding whether PSMU constitutes a behavioral addiction (Boer et al., 2022; Griffiths, 2012). This study employs the PSMU construct rather than social media addiction to avoid pathologizing everyday behaviors (Kardefelt-Winther et al., 2017). PSMU encompasses a range of negative characteristics exhibited during social media engagement, which shares features with behavioral addictions, including compulsive usage patterns, impaired impulse control, and withdrawal symptoms when attempting to reduce use (Boer et al., 2022; Griffiths, 2005). Given the significant adverse effects of PSMU on adolescent well-being, understanding its determinants holds considerable theoretical and practical significance. However, existing research on PSMU antecedents has predominantly focused on individual characteristics (Assunção, Costa, Tagliabue, & Mena Matos, 2017; Bright, Kleiser, & Grau, 2015; Casale, Rugai, & Fioravanti, 2018; Piqueras et al., 2024). For example, Lahti et al. (2024) has identified key individual protective factors, demonstrating that strong family support, school satisfaction, and high health literacy can effectively counteract the development of PSMU. Few studies have investigated PSMU's antecedent factors through the lens of socioeconomic inequality (Lenzi et al., 2023).

The digital inequality framework posits that individuals' relative socioeconomic positions significantly influence their social media engagement patterns (Büchi, 2024; Büchi & Hargittai, 2022). Adolescents, at a critical developmental stage for identity formation and social belonging, demonstrate a heightened sensitivity to peer evaluation and social status, thus making them particularly susceptible to inequality (Dijkstra, Kuyper, van der Werf, Buunk, & van der Zee, 2008). Our study investigates the mechanisms linking RD to PSMU. Relative deprivation theory posits that individuals experience deprivation when social comparisons reveal disparities in resources, status, or opportunities relative to expected levels (Gurr, 2011; Runciman, 1972). As Confucius said, “[people] do not worry about scarcity, but rather about uneven distribution.” RD emerges not from absolute deprivation but from upward social comparisons with peers or societal standards. Wilkinson and Pickett's (2019, 2007, 2009) income inequality hypothesis conceptualizes RD as the micro-level mechanism bridging relationships between socioeconomic inequality and health/behavioral outcomes. They argue that RD operates through psychosocial pathways (distinct from material pathways of absolute deprivation, like socioeconomic status, SES), whereby discontent and resentment undermine social cohesion, social capital, trust, and well-being (Kawachi, Subramanian, & Almeida-Filho, 2002).

Adolescents experiencing intense RD may demonstrate a greater propensity for PSMU, potentially using social media platforms to seek alternative reference groups or aspirational role models (Gustafsson et al., 2025). According to the compensatory internet use theory (Kardefelt-Winther, 2014), individuals may engage in excessive online activities to alleviate negative emotions and compensate for deficits in offline life, such as lack of belongingness, self-esteem, or achievement. RD constitutes a potent source of such deficits by generating pervasive feelings of disadvantage and dissatisfaction. Moreover, for adolescents susceptible to relative deprivation, social media serves a dual role: it provides compensatory outlet for seeking alternative reference groups or aspirational identities, yet simultaneously exacerbates feelings of disadvantage through exposure to curated, idealized representations that foster upward social comparisons (McComb, Vanman, & Tobin, 2023). Existing research examining associations between RD and PSMU (or related constructs like problematic smartphone use) has predominantly relied on subjective measures, convenience sampling, and limited sample sizes, often restricted to specific schools or regions (Gkinopoulos et al., 2023; Lin & Liu, 2020). A recent work established stress and fear of missing out (FoMO) as parallel mediators linking personal RD to increased mobile phone dependence, underscoring the role of negative affective pathways (Quan et al., 2024). To our knowledge, only Lenzi et al. (2023) have examined the RD-PSMU relationship at a cross-national level, confirming that both individual-level RD and school-level economic inequality are positively associated with adolescent PSMU, particularly in contexts of low peer or family support. However, their analysis neither elaborated on underlying mechanisms nor explored cross-national heterogeneity.

This study examines the RD-PSMU relationship through an interpersonal lens. The interpersonal model of addiction emphasizes the centrality of relationships in addiction processes (Gray & Edson, 2025). While recent research has begun exploring interpersonal influences on PSMU (e.g., parent-child relationships and peer influences, see Liu & Kuo, 2007; Marino et al., 2020), limited attention has been directed toward examining how school-based interpersonal dynamics might influence PSMU. Schools, as crucial contexts for adolescent daily life and socialization, create interpersonal environments that profoundly shape adolescents' psychological well-being and behavioral patterns (Dijkstra et al., 2008). School bullying, for instance, represents a significant risk factor for adolescent mental health, with effects persisting into adulthood (Elgar, Craig, Boyce, Morgan, & Vella-Zarb, 2009; Olweus, 2013). Indeed, extensive cross-national evidence shows a strong, dose-dependent relationship between the intensity of social media use and the likelihood of both perpetrating and experiencing cyberbullying (Craig et al., 2020). Moreover, existing research lacks a comprehensive examination of multiple interpersonal relationships simultaneously, potentially introducing confounding effects. This limitation may be particularly salient for PSMU, given its heightened sensitivity to interpersonal factors compared to other forms of electronic use. Integration of RD, diverse interpersonal relationships, and PSMU is essential for understanding how social contexts and interpersonal dynamics influence adolescent well-being through complex mechanisms. RD theory suggests that RD generates negative emotions such as envy and frustration, potentially undermining relationships with family, classmates, and teachers while increasing vulnerability to bullying and interpersonal conflicts (Smith, Pettigrew, Pippin, & Bialosiewicz, 2012). Social displacement theory further posits that when offline social interactions become restricted or deteriorate, individuals may turn to social media to seek compensatory social connections and emotional support (Hall & Liu, 2022). These theoretical frameworks suggest interpersonal relationships are critical mediating variables in the RD-PSMU association. This study evaluates the mediating effects of four interpersonal factors: relationships with teachers, classmates, and parents, and bullying victimization.

Individual-level RD can be aggregated at the school level to obtain contextual inequality. Contextual inequality describes the variation in income within the school, and its impact may be independent of individual RD (Rich & Owens, 2023). According to the income inequality hypothesis, aversive social comparisons are more frequent in groups with higher levels of socioeconomic inequality (Wilkinson & Pickett, 2007, 2009, 2019). Schools with significant wealth gaps create an intense social competition environment, where students may be more sensitive to RD, amplifying the adverse association between RD and interpersonal relationships. Furthermore, schools with high levels of wealth inequality may weaken overall cohesion and trust, making students less confident about real-life interpersonal relationships and more willing to use social media to compensate for losses in real-life interactions. Therefore, high contextual inequality may strengthen the associations between RD, interpersonal relationships, and PSMU. The moderating effect of contextual inequality is vital for understanding how the school environment affects adolescent behavior patterns (Brons et al., 2024; Visser et al., 2021). However, previous studies have often been unable to conduct such analyses due to limited sample size and coverage (Rich & Owens, 2023). Another aim of this study is to evaluate the influence of contextual inequality on the association between RD and PSMU.

With the widespread use of social media, adolescents worldwide face similar technological usage pressures. However, adolescents in different countries/regions may perceive these pressures differently due to varying social backgrounds (Boer et al., 2020). For example, in societies emphasizing individualism and competition, adolescents may be less likely to seek help when experiencing discomfort from RD, as they do not want to be perceived as weak or incompetent. People may also view individuals with high RD as failures, leading to negative evaluations or rejection (see cross-cultural studies by Smith et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2024). This may cause adolescents to turn to social media to seek emotional solace and recognition of self-worth. This suggests that the effects of RD may also exhibit heterogeneity. It is necessary to explain cross-national differences further. Our final aim is to examine the cross-national heterogeneity of the indirect effects between RD and PSMU.

The present study

To address current research gaps, we propose an interpersonal model of PSMU with RD as a social determinant, testing a multilevel mediation model across a large-scale adolescent sample spanning 39 countries. We hypothesize that individual-level RD indirectly influences PSMU through impairments in four specific interpersonal relationships: relationships with parents, relationships with teachers, relationships with classmates, and experiences of bullying victimization. Specifically, higher levels of RD are associated with poorer quality in these key interpersonal domains, which in turn mediate higher levels of PSMU. Furthermore, we posit that school-level contextual inequality amplifies the adverse associations between RD and these interpersonal relationships and between these interpersonal relationships and PSMU. Finally, we examine the heterogeneity of these indirect effects across national contexts. The proposed conceptual model is presented in Fig. 1.

Data and methods

Samples and procedures

Data were drawn from the 2017/2018 wave of the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study, a cross-national research study conducted in collaboration with the World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for Europe (WHO, 2025). Initiated in 1982, the HBSC study is conducted every four years to gain insight into adolescent health and well-being within their social context. Data collection adheres to a standardized international protocol to ensure that school-based surveys yield comparable and nationally representative data across all participating countries and regions (Moor et al., 2020). The survey encompasses representative samples of adolescents aged 11–15 from 47 countries and regions across Europe, North America, and West Asia (including Flanders and Wallonia in Belgium, England, Scotland, and Wales in the United Kingdom). The sampling design employed stratified random cluster sampling, utilizing schools or classes as sampling units. Standardized weights were constructed to ensure representativeness in countries with geographic or ethnic oversampling. Data collection procedures and survey instruments were standardized and strictly adhered to international research protocols, with ethical approval obtained from respective national ethics committees. The HBSC achieved robust response rates at school (median across countries: 89.7%) and individual levels (median across schools: 83.0%).

We followed the protocol outlined in Figure S1 for sample selection. At the national level, we excluded eight countries/regions with all missing data on theoretical model variables (Fig. 1). At the school level, we excluded schools where all students had missing data on theoretical model variables, or enrollment was below 10. At the individual level, we excluded adolescents who had missing wealth data. For the remaining missing values, Little's MCAR test indicated that data were not Missing Completely at Random (χ2(2531) = 29285.64, p < 0.001). Correlation analysis of missing values revealed significant associations among variables, suggesting covariate-dependent missingness (see Figure S2). Therefore, the data can be considered missing at random (MAR, see Buuren, 2018), and we used the MissForest method for imputation (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012). The final analytical sample comprised 190,707 adolescents (Mage = 13.51 ± 1.63, 51.04% Girls) nested within 5,576 schools across 39 countries/regions. Measures

Income inequality

To isolate psychosocial pathways, we controlled for absolute SES, measured through the Family Affluence Scale (FAS, Currie et al., 2008). This scale assesses adolescents' household material assets through six items: family vehicle ownership (none, one, or two or more), private bedroom possession (no or yes), and so on. These items were summed to create a 13-point Family Affluence Index representing absolute SES.

RD was operationalized by transforming the FAS score into a Yitzhaki index within the participant's school (Yitzhaki, 1979). The mathematical form of this index is:

For each individual i, their RD equals the mean wealth difference between themselves and all wealthier schoolmates (j). Thus, the Yitzhaki index represents an “upward-looking” measure of deprivation. We used the participant's school as the reference group to calculate each student's RD; higher scores indicate higher RD. According to Pham-Kanter (2009), individuals tend to compare themselves with people they know in their close networks. Schools are important places for adolescents' daily life and socialization, where they spend much time and frequently interact and socially compare with peers. Therefore, choosing the school as the reference group is reasonable.

Contextual inequality was derived by aggregating RD scores at the school level, with higher scores indicating greater within-school income inequality.

Following Oka's (2021) recommendations, all income inequality variables were normalized to range from 0 (lowest) to 1 (highest).

Interpersonal relationship

We assessed four dimensions of interpersonal relationships, encompassing three positive factors (teacher relationships, classmate relationships, and family relationships) and one negative factor (bullying victimization).

Teacher and classmate relationships were evaluated using parallel three-item scales (Torsheim et al., 2012). The teacher relationship scale assessed trust in teachers, perceived teacher acceptance, and caring. Similarly, the classmate relationship scale measured trust in classmates, classmate friendliness, and happiness with classmates. Both scales employed five-point Likert responses (1 = “strongly agree” to 5 = “strongly disagree”). We reverse-coded and averaged the items, with higher scores indicating better relationships. These two scales' cross-national reliability and validity have been confirmed (Torsheim et al., 2012). In this study, both scales showed good internal reliability (teachers: α = 0.825; classmates: α = 0.803).

Family relationships were assessed using the family subscale of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (Zimet et al., 1988). This four-item measure evaluated family assistance, emotional support availability, problem-discussion capacity, and decision-making support. Responses were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = “low support” to 7 = “high support”). We averaged the items, with higher scores indicating better family relationships. The scale demonstrated excellent internal reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.936).

Finally, bullying victimization was measured using a single item adapted from the Olweus Bullying Questionnaire (Olweus, 2013). Participants reported their frequency of school-based victimization in recent months (“How often have you been bullied at school in the past couple of months?”) using a five-point scale (1 = “never” to 5 = “several times a week”).

Problematic social media use

PSMU was assessed using the Social Media Disorder Scale (van den Eijnden, Lemmens, & Valkenburg, 2016), which measures nine characteristics of PSMU: preoccupation with social media, dissatisfaction with lack of usage time, feeling uneasy when not using, unsuccessful attempts to reduce usage, neglecting other responsibilities to use social media, regularly arguing about social media use, hiding usage from parents or friends, using social media to escape negative emotions, and having severe conflicts with family due to social media use. Participants provided dichotomous responses (yes = 1, no = 0). We averaged the items, with higher scores indicating more severe PSMU. The scale has demonstrated cross-national solid validity and reliability (Boer et al., 2022), with a Cronbach's α of 0.852 in the current study.

Covariates

We controlled for gender, grade, and family structure as individual-level demographic covariates. Additionally, we controlled for online interaction preferences, a recognized risk factor for PSMU (Lee, Ko, & Chou, 2015). This construct was measured using a three-item scale (α = 0.903; Caplan, 2010) assessing adolescents' preference for online versus offline disclosure of personal information (e.g., “On the internet, I talk more easily about secrets than in a face-to-face encounter”), with responses ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5).

At the national level, we controlled for per capita GDP (proxied for national wealth), Gini coefficient (proxied for national income inequality), and percentage of internet users (proxied for cross-national variation in social media accessibility), all measured in 2017. Macro-level data were obtained from the World Bank Economic Database and World Telecommunication/ICT Indicators Database (International Telecommunication Union, 2024; World Bank, 2024). For regions within countries, macro-indicators were matched with their respective nations (e.g., Wales matched with United Kingdom data).

Statistical methods

We initially employed Harman's single-factor test to assess common method bias among variables. Subsequently, we examined Spearman correlations between key variables (see Table S2). Weighted descriptive statistics for study variables are presented in Table 1. Multicollinearity diagnostics (Table S3) revealed maximum Variance inflation factors (VIFs) below three across all models, indicating no severe multicollinearity concerns.

We used two-level random-effects models for the primary analyses. Although the data structure was hierarchical with three levels, current methodological limitations preclude effective mediation analysis beyond two levels (Preacher, 2015). Furthermore, school-level Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were consistently lower than country-level ICCs (except for teacher relationships, see Table S4), indicating greater between-country than between-school variance. Based on these considerations, we fitted random models incorporating only country and individual levels. We first estimated null models and calculated ICC and Design Effects (DEFF) to establish multilevel modeling necessity (Sommet & Morselli, 2021). Then, we extended these models to random slope models incorporating key variables, allowing RD slopes to vary across countries/regions. This approach enabled country-specific effect estimation and improved model fit. Nested model comparisons demonstrated superior performance of random slope models compared to random intercept models (see Table S6), indicating significant cross-national variation in RD-interpersonal relationships-PSMU associations.

We used the causal mediation method recommended by Tingley, Yamamoto, Hirose, Keele, and Imai (2014) to analyze the pure mediation model (model (a) in Fig. 1). This framework offers considerable flexibility and enables examining indirect effects in multilevel models. We allowed mediators to be estimated in parallel to avoid confounding. We extracted country/region-specific indirect effects and plotted coefficient plots to examine cross-national heterogeneity. I2 statistics were calculated to quantify cross-national heterogeneity magnitude (Harrer, Cuijpers, Furukawa, & Ebert, 2021). Lastly, we analyzed moderated mediation effects (model (b) in Fig. 1), incorporating interactions between RD and contextual inequality and between contextual inequality and interpersonal relationships. Significant interactions were probed using Johnson-Neyman (JN) techniques. Indirect effect (Quasi-Bayesian) confidence intervals were calculated using 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations.

All models incorporated sampling weights to ensure representativeness. Analyses were conducted in R, primarily utilizing survey, lme4, and mediation packages (R Core Team, 2024).

Ethics

Either active-consent or passive-consent approaches depending on country and school board requirements were used in HBSC 2017/2018. Appropriate ethical approval was gained in all countries and regions.

Result

Descriptive analysis

Harman's single-factor test yielded six factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1, with the largest factor accounting for 15.7% of the variance (threshold: 40%), indicating slight common method bias. Descriptive results are shown in Table 1. Spearman bivariate correlations revealed small but significant positive associations between RD and PSMU (p < 0.001) and significant negative correlations with interpersonal variables (ps < 0.001). Contextual inequality showed positive correlations with both RD and PSMU (ps < 0.001) and exhibited correlation patterns with interpersonal variables similar to RD. Interpersonal variables had stronger negative correlations with PSMU than RD-PSMU associations (ps < 0.001).

Null models showed ICCs ranging from 0.03 to 0.08 (see Table S5). Although the ICCs were relatively small, the DEFFs in each model were all greater than the threshold of 1.9. According to methodological recommendations, multilevel analysis is still necessary if data are nested and the DEFF exceeds 1.9 (Sommet & Morselli, 2021).

Mediation analysis

The results of multilevel regression are presented in Table 2. Across all four indirect effect models, RD was significantly associated with poorer interpersonal relationships. Specifically, adolescents with higher RD tended to have worse relationships with teachers (b = −0.689, p < 0.001), classmates (b = −0.274, p < 0.001), and family (b = −0.412, p < 0.001) and experienced more bullying victimization (b = 0.206, p < 0.001). However, in the direct effect and total effect models, RD was not significantly associated with PSMU (ps > 0.05). In the direct effect model, poorer interpersonal relationships were significantly positively associated with PSMU (ps < 0.001). Contextual inequality was significantly positively associated with PSMU (b = 0.071 and 0.079, ps < 0.001), indicating that students in schools with greater wealth disparities had higher risks of PSMU. The above analyses suggest that the relationship between RD and PSMU may be more complex and involve mediating mechanisms.

Moderated mediation analysis

We conducted a moderated mediation model to examine the moderating effect of contextual inequality. Interaction results are presented in Table 4, and path coefficients are shown in Panel (b) of Fig. 2. In the family relationship model, contextual inequality moderated the path from RD to the family relationship (b = −0.830, p < 0.05). In the direct effect model, contextual inequality moderated the paths from family relationship to PSMU (b = 0.011, p < 0.001) and from bullying victimization to PSMU (b = 0.025, p < 0.001). The other moderation effects were not significant. The JN analyses are shown in Fig. 4. On the M → Y paths, as contextual inequality increased (panels (a) and (b)), we observed that the positive association between bullying victimization and PSMU strengthened (the risk effect of bullying increased). The negative association between family relationships and PSMU weakened (the protective effect of family relationships decreased). On the X → M path, as contextual inequality increased (panel (c)), the negative association between RD and family relationships strengthened (the risk effect of RD increased). These analyses indicate that contextual inequality had adverse moderating effects on all three paths, enhancing the indirect effect from RD to PSMU.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and tested an integrative interpersonal model of PSMU among a large-scale adolescent sample across 39 countries. We found that RD predicts higher PSMU through interpersonal mediating mechanisms. Moreover, contextual inequality moderated the mediation pathway from RD to PSMU. As the first investigation to concurrently analyze RD, interpersonal factors, and PSMU within a multinational adolescent sample, we observed significant heterogeneity in the mediating effects of family relationships and bullying victimization. This variation likely stems from the diverse cultural norms governing family functioning and school bullying dynamics across European countries. Overall, our findings provided crucial insights that can inform the development of more effective prevention strategies tailored to the specific contexts of different nations.

The mediating effect of interpersonal factors

The most significant finding is the mediating role of interpersonal relationships in the association between RD and PSMU. RD does not directly predict PSMU but indirectly increases PSMU risk through its detrimental effects on adolescents' interpersonal relationships. This suggests that the impact of RD on PSMU operates primarily through psychosocial mechanisms, rather than a direct causal link. This is particularly salient given that PSMU often coexists with other risk factors, forming multi-risk clusters that are profoundly detrimental to adolescent mental health, which underscores the importance of identifying such foundational psychosocial drivers (Walsh et al., 2020). RD, as a subjective experience of disadvantage, exerts a significant psychosocial impact by triggering negative emotions such as frustration, discontent, and social comparison stress. For adolescents, a heightened sensitivity to social comparison amplifies the emotional toll of RD, leading to increased stress and social withdrawal, which hinder their ability to maintain meaningful and supportive connections with peers, teachers, and family (Tian, Deng, Tong, & He, 2025). These compromised relationships, in turn, exacerbate the risk of problematic social media use, as adolescents may turn to online platforms to seek validation, cope with negative emotions, or establish alternative reference groups. This finding supports the interpersonal model of addiction (Liu & Kuo, 2007; Sullivan, 2013), which emphasizes interpersonal difficulties as a core mechanism in the development of addictive behaviors. However, the practical significance of these mediating pathways must be interpreted with caution. Although all paths were statistically significant, the effect sizes were relatively small. This indicates that while the pattern—whereby RD contributes to PSMU via impaired interpersonal relationships—is robust and consistent, it accounts for a limited portion of the variance in PSMU. Thus, our model should be considered an important piece of the complex etiology of PSMU, not an exhaustive explanatory framework.

RD may influence interpersonal relationship quality through multiple psychosocial pathways. From an active perspective, RD theory suggests that RD generates negative emotions such as frustration and anger (Smith et al., 2012), potentially leading adolescents to adopt more defensive behavioral patterns in interpersonal interactions, thereby compromising relationship quality. Additionally, adolescents experiencing high RD may develop shame and inferiority, leading to social withdrawal or difficulties in establishing and maintaining intimate relationships (Xu et al., 2023). RD may also elicit feelings of envy, fostering competitive and hostile interpersonal orientations. From a passive perspective, adolescents with higher RD are more susceptible to negative treatment from others (Feather, 2015). Students' social status in school environments often correlates with their family's economic background. Students experiencing high RD may face marginalization due to limited economic resources for participating in collective activities (e.g., school excursions), reducing opportunities for peer bonding (Dijkstra et al., 2008; Tian et al., 2025). Furthermore, these students may face ridicule or exclusion due to their inability to match peers' consumption levels, potentially increasing their vulnerability to bullying victimization. These psychosocial mechanisms help explain why RD is associated with poorer interpersonal relationships and increased bullying victimization (Cen & Xia, 2024; Li, Zhao, Zhao, Liu, & Yu, 2023). This perspective is further reinforced by complementary findings showing that positive interpersonal factors—namely strong family support and high school satisfaction—act as significant protective buffers that counteract the development of PSMU (Lahti et al., 2024), highlighting the dual role of interpersonal dynamics as both a risk and a protective pathway.

So, how do impaired interpersonal relationships lead to elevated PSMU? According to social displacement theory, when adolescents experience rejection, conflict, or a lack of intimacy in their offline social worlds (e.g., with family, teachers, or classmates), their fundamental needs for belonging and support go unmet (Hall & Liu, 2022). In response, they may increasingly turn to social media platforms as a compensatory space to seek alternative social connections, validation, and a sense of community that is absent in their immediate environment. This compensatory use can become excessive and problematic when online interactions replace rather than supplement offline social efforts (Winstone et al., 2021). Additionally, self-medication theory provides a complementary framework, proposing that the stress, loneliness, and negative affect generated by poor relationships drive adolescents to use social media as a maladaptive coping strategy (Khantzian, 1997). Specifically, immersive and algorithmically curated online content can serve as a potent short-term escape from psychological distress, while the pursuit of likes and comments can become a form of emotion regulation. This pattern is reinforced through operant conditioning: social media use temporarily alleviates negative states (negative reinforcement), and the receipt of online engagement provides fleeting rewards (positive reinforcement, Moretta, Buodo, Santucci, Chen, & Potenza, 2023). Over time, this reliance on digital platforms for coping and connection can disrupt real-world functioning and solidify into patterns of PSMU. These theorized mechanisms—compensatory connection and self-medication theory—coherently explain our empirical finding that deficits across multiple interpersonal domains significantly predict increased PSMU.

Cross-national heterogeneity

Cross-national heterogeneity analyses reveal significant regional variations in the indirect effects of RD. The mediating effects through teacher and classmate relationships showed high consistency across countries, suggesting that school-based interpersonal relationships linking RD to PSMU possess cross-cultural universality. This stability may stem from schools' formal organizational structure and engenders similar interpersonal interaction patterns across national contexts (Dijkstra et al., 2008). In contrast, mediating effects through family relationships and bullying victimization exhibit substantial heterogeneity. This variation likely reflects cultural differences in family functioning and school bullying dynamics. From a cultural theory perspective, these patterns align with Hofstede's individualism-collectivism framework (Hofstede, 2001). For instance, collectivist cultures may provide greater familial emotional support buffering against RD's adverse effects. Similarly, nations emphasizing egalitarianism and collectivism typically implement more comprehensive anti-bullying policies and report lower bullying incidence, potentially attenuating RD-bullying victimization associations. In individualistic cultures, however, adolescents may be more inclined to handle the stress of RD independently (Smith et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2024) and may be more likely to become targets of bullying, as wealth disparities may be seen as symbols of personal ability and value.

Notably, the magnitude of indirect effects followed discernible regional patterns, which align with variations in educational and social policies. For instance, countries characterized by early academic tracking and highly stratified educational systems (a model prevalent in several Central European nations) consistently demonstrated heightened indirect effects across all interpersonal pathways. This pattern may be attributed to the way such systems institutionalize social comparison by allocating students to different school types based on academic performance (Henninges, Traini, & Kleinert, 2019; Moosbrugger & Bacher, 2018). This stratification system may intensify social comparisons among students, making the impact of RD on interpersonal relationships more pronounced. Moreover, the education systems in these Central European countries often emphasize competition and individual achievement, creating a cultural atmosphere that may heighten adolescents' vulnerability to RD effects (Herzog-Punzenberger & Schnell, 2019). This intensifies the strain on interpersonal relationships with teachers, classmates, and even family, as these ties become arenas for status competition or reminders of one's relative position. Consequently, the mediation pathways from RD to PSMU via poorer relationships are significantly strengthened, as observed in our results.

Conversely, nations with comprehensive welfare states and egalitarian educational philosophies (exemplified by the Nordic model) showed consistently smaller and often non-significant indirect effects. The educational systems in these regions emphasize equality and inclusiveness, avoiding early academic tracking. This de-competition educational environment may reduce the negative impact of RD on interpersonal relationships (Federick, 2020). Macro-economically, their extensive social welfare apparatuses are instrumental in reducing material inequality and providing robust institutional support for disadvantaged families and youths (Johnsen, Ortiz-Barreda, Rekkedal, & Iversen, 2017; Kettunen, 2001), which may buffer against the adverse effects of RD and interpersonal relationship problems on PSMU. Psycho-socially, the de-emphasis on intra-group competition in schools fosters a more cooperative and supportive interpersonal environment. This dual action weakens the negative impact of relative deprivation on interpersonal relationships and potentially strengthens the protective quality of relationships, making them less likely to lead to maladaptive coping mechanisms like PSMU.

The moderating effect of contextual inequality

We found that contextual inequality at the school level amplifies RD's association with PSMU through interpersonal relationships. In schools with greater wealth disparities, RD showed stronger negative associations with family relationships, while the effects of family relationships and bullying victimization on PSMU became more pronounced. These findings support the income inequality hypothesis (Wilkinson & Pickett, 2007, 2009, 2019), suggesting that more significant socioeconomic gaps intensify social comparison and status anxiety, magnifying RD's adverse effects.

Contextual inequality first strengthens the adverse association of RD with family relationships. In schools with significant wealth disparities, social comparisons and status competition are more prominent, which may increase the stress and frustration of adolescents with high RD (Park, 2021; Rich & Owens, 2023). When confronted with more evident socioeconomic differences, these adolescents may transfer their frustration to family interactions, exhibiting increased anxiety and rebellious behavior, thereby undermining family relationships (Brons et al., 2024). Parents may also experience anxiety and guilt over their inability to meet their children's material needs in high-inequality environments, potentially affecting parent-child interaction quality (Schenck-Fontaine et al., 2020). Additionally, contextual inequality moderates the association between interpersonal relationships and PSMU. In high-inequality schools, bullying victimization and low-income family relationships more strongly predict PSMU. This may reflect reduced social cohesion and trust levels in high-inequality environments (Kawachi et al., 2002), making it more difficult for adolescents to access adequate social support when facing interpersonal challenges. Consequently, they may be more inclined to seek alternative social connections and emotional support through social media. High-inequality environments may also increase economic barriers to offline social activities, further driving adolescents toward more economically accessible online social interactions (Ahn, 2012). These findings emphasize the importance of addressing school-based economic inequality in preventing adolescent PSMU.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It is the first to examine how RD is associated with PSMU through interpersonal relationships in a large-scale, cross-national adolescent sample. The multilevel mediation analysis of data from 39 countries/regions enhances result generalizability and reveals important cross-national variations. Secondly, we simultaneously examined multiple interpersonal factors, avoiding confounding due to omitting important interpersonal variables. We also distinguished individual-level RD from school-level contextual inequality, which overcomes the limitation of previous studies that often focus on only one type of income inequality. This multilevel analytical framework facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of socioeconomic inequality associated with adolescent PSMU.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. For instance, PSMU may reciprocally influence the quality of interpersonal relationships. That is, PSMU could erode family and peer relationships by displacing face-to-face interaction and fostering conflict, thereby creating a pernicious feedback loop. Future longitudinal research is essential to disentangle these bidirectional effects. Second, our sample predominantly represents European developed nations. As social media use becomes globally ubiquitous, adolescents in developing countries may face different socioeconomic pressures and cultural contexts. Wealth disparities may be more pronounced in some rapidly urbanizing and economically transitioning countries, and traditional social support networks may weaken. This unique social context may affect the pattern of relationships between RD and PSMU.

Furthermore, our measure of RD, based on within-school comparisons, may not capture adolescents' broader social comparisons, particularly those with idealized peers on social media. This could lead to an underestimation of true RD levels. Future research should develop methods to assess these complex, multi-layered digital-era reference groups to better understand their role in shaping subjective relative deprivation. Our operationalization of the Social Media Disorder Scale warrants discussion. We created a continuous score by averaging the nine dichotomous items to capture PSMU severity. While this approach is common, the recent paper also recommends a diagnostic approach based on meeting a cutoff of five or more criteria (Boer et al., 2022). Future research should examine whether using this dichotomous classification alters the observed associations, which would be important for clinical interpretation.

Additionally, our model did not control for key psychological covariates, such as pre-existing symptoms of depression and anxiety. Given that such internalizing symptoms are strongly associated with both impaired interpersonal relationships and a higher propensity for PSMU, their omission may lead to omitted variable bias. Future studies should incorporate these measures to better isolate the effects of RD. Finally, the current study used data from the 2017/18 HBSC survey, so our findings may only partially reflect the latest social media usage patterns. Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly altered adolescents' social media habits, with lockdown measures increasing online social dependency while potentially exacerbating income inequality (Wiederhold, 2020). This major public health crisis may have reshaped the association patterns examined in this study.

Policy implications

Our findings offer significant policy implications for preventing adolescent PSMU. Intervention strategies should simultaneously address economic inequality and interpersonal relationships. At the school level, education authorities should strive to minimize the impact of economic disparities on students' participation in school activities. For example, providing free or subsidized extracurricular activities ensures equal participation in school life, avoiding marginalization due to economic reasons. Additionally, emphasis should be placed on developing teacher-student and classmate relationships, given their stable mediating effects across national contexts. Schools can promote positive interpersonal interactions through team activities and peer mentoring programs (Throuvala, Griffiths, Rennoldson, & Kuss, 2019).

Secondly, policies and interventions must pay special attention to schools with significant wealth disparities. These schools have stronger associations between RD, interpersonal problems, and PSMU. Education administrators might consider allocating resources to high-inequality schools to improve campus environments and implement targeted intervention programs, such as enhanced anti-bullying initiatives and increased psychosocial support for economically disadvantaged students (Rawlings & Stoddard, 2019). Given cross-national variations, policy development must consider local cultural contexts and institutional characteristics. In countries where individualism is dominant, focusing more on enhancing individuals' ability to cope with RD may be necessary.

Conclusion

This study reveals how RD is associated with adolescents' PSMU through multiple interpersonal pathways. We found that RD does not directly lead to PSMU but is indirectly associated with PSMU by undermining adolescents' relationships with teachers, classmates, and family and by increasing the risk of bullying victimization. These patterns exhibit heterogeneity across countries, especially in the mediating effects of family relationships and bullying victimization. Significantly, school-level contextual inequality amplifies these adverse effects, indicating that preventing adolescent PSMU requires interventions at both individual and contextual levels. This research advances the understanding of the social determinants of PSMU while offering important implications for developing more effective prevention strategies.
