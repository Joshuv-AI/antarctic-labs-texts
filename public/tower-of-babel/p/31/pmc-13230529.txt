Neural network modeling of Lassa fever spread and disability effects
Authors: Zulqurnain Sabir, M. A. Abdelkawy, Maros Jakubec, Nora yehia Ibrahim, Ashour M. Ahmed, Mustafa Bayram, Harun Çiçek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lassa fever is one of the viral hemorrhagic illnesses produced through the Lassa virus and may be transmitted to individuals via contact with diseased rodents or their feces and urine. The current study presents the solutions to the nonlinear zoonotic spread of Lassa virus fever leading to disabilities model by designing a supervised neural network. The model is divided into the human (susceptible, severely infected, mildly infected, and recovered) and rodent (susceptible, infected) populations. The dynamic structure of one class to the other performs the zoonotic spread of Lassa virus fever model makes nonlinear. A dataset via Runge-Kutta is obtained, which decreases the mean square error by dividing the training, validation, and testing with 78%, 12% and 10%. The proposed neural network construction is designed with a single hidden layer keeping 20 neurons and sigmoid function, while Bayesian regularization is used for the optimization. The designed neural network scheme’s reliability is obtained using different tests including best training values, correlation performances, state transition, and error histogram. The correctness of the designed neural network solver is observed through the matching of the proposed and Runge-Kutta outputs, and insignificant absolute error calculated as 10− 02 to 10− 03.

Introduction

Human diseases are frequently recognized by different symptoms such as suffering, pain, dysfunction, or mortality. Because they have a chance to produce considerable contagion and death, infectious diseases can be disseminated in either the direct or indirect way by microorganisms that pose serious threats to the world’s health1. A zoonotic illness that is produced or reappeared in several West African nations, such as Sierra Leone, Ghana, Togo, Liberia, Nigeria, Guinea, and Benin is Lassa fever (LF)2. LF is one of the zoonotic diseases, which means it transmits between humans and animals3. LF initially spreads through the contact of diseased rodents, their urine or feces. In the West Africa region, the illness is endemic, and individuals frequently experience contact with infected rodents while conducting foodstuffs that have been polluted with rodent feces or urine4. This zoonotic conduction can lead to severe implications, like respiratory distress, hemorrhagic fever, and even death. The overwhelming consequences of LF are to produce longstanding disabilities. Nigeria recorded 211 laboratory confirmed cases of LF from January 3 to 30, 2022, which resulted in forty-total mortality5. The incidents spread throughout the federal capital region and 14 out of 36 states in Nigeria, which makes up a sizable chunk of the nation. The LF survivors can face enduring harm to their hearing, cognitive function or vision. Some of the reports also indicate that LF produces brain inflammation, leading to tremors, seizures, and other nervous indications6. These disabilities present significant effects on the quality of life, which make it challenging for them to attend school, work, or contribute to regular activities. The zoonotic banquet of LF highlights the essentials through education and awareness about the illness, mainly in rural groups where the danger of spreading is high. By endorsing proper hygiene practices, such as storage of food in rodent-proof vessels and evading interaction with hypothetically diseased rodents, persons can decrease their danger of constricting the illness. Moreover, healthcare suppliers must be qualified to identify the symptoms of LF and deliver rapid action to avoid lasting disabilities and lessen the mortality risk7.

Prior to spreading to other tissues, this viral infection first attacks immune systems in the nasal cavity then moving on to neighboring lymph glands. The illness progresses in a variety of ways after an incubation time frame of one to three weeks. More serious instances may come with a variety of indicators, such as pulmonary edema, low blood pressure, and diarrhea; however, the majority of individuals only have minor signs including low-grade fever, malaise, and headache8. According to Khan et al.9, a little portion might show blood leaking through the genitalia, rectum, or oropharynx. Most prevalent neurological impairment seen in LF individuals is hearing loss. However certain studies contend that this estimate may be overestimated; statistics indicate that this consequence impacts between one quarter and one-third of patients who sustain the disease10. It is important to note that deafness usually happens in the postoperative stage of the sickness, regardless of whether the individual has fully recovered, suggesting that this disease may be caused by an immune process as opposed to having a viral origin11. This hearing loss occurs in just over 50% of instances and may impact one ear. The disabilities based on the infrequent central nervous system are produced by LF, which contain encephalitis, sensorineural deafness, recuperative ataxia, prolonged neuropsychiatric appearances (like psychosis, depression, mania, asthenia, cognitive impairments, and sleep disturbances), meningitis, coma, and seizures. These neurological signs tend to be less frequent than LF’s more prevalent generalized symptoms12,13. Moreover, the mathematical model has been studied in various applications, some of them are Yunus et al.14 provided a fractional mathematical system for understanding and controlling communication dynamics in the management systems of computer viruses. Oqielat et al.15 examined a structure of fractional series explanations for fractional diffusion reaction for the growth system of bacteria through Laplace residual power series scheme. Khan et al.16 provided the nonlinear dynamics based on the piecewise modified ABC fractional leukemia system with symmetric numerical imitations.

The current research investigations provide the numerical solutions to the nonlinear zoonotic spread of LF leading to disabilities by designing a single structure of the hidden layer is used by relating the sigmoid function. The optimization is assessed by the Bayesian regularization neural network (ByRNN), which has never been exploited before for solving the nonlinear zoonotic spread of the LF leading to disabilities model. However the existing investigations that use the conventional compartmental schemes, the designed ByRNN structure capture intricate relations between rodent and human populations. In current decades, the neural network schemes have been applied in a variety of nonlinear biological and stiff applications, like singular 5th nonlinear multi-pantograph differential system17, fractional environmental/economic system18, acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus model19, hard water consumption of kidney function20, and singular two-point nonlinear system based on thermal-explosion theory21. Moreover, the neural network studies is applied in other application, e.g., Anwer et al.22 provided the machine intelligent expedition together with supervised Bayesian adaptive neural networks for solving the stochastic cholera epidemic system in a periodic environment. In another study, Anwar et al.23 studies a stochastic adaptive autoregressive eXogenous networks operative with Grünwald Letnikove fractional finite differences based on the nonlinear fractal differential framework of coffee rust control system. They also applied the neural network structures in the solutions of computer virus model24, and nonlinear measles transmission system with impact of immunization25.

Conclusion

In the current study, the numerical solutions of the nonlinear zoonotic spread of Lassa virus fever leading to disabilities have been performed by designing a stochastic computing infrastructure of a supervised neural network. The nonlinear zoonotic spread of Lassa virus fever system leading to disabilities is divided into the human (susceptible, mildly infected, severely infected, and recovered individuals) and rodent (susceptible, and infected) populations. Some concluding remarks of this work are presented as:
