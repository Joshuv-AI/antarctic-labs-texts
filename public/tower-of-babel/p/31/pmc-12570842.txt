Healthcare providers’ knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding occupational exposure to human papillomaviruses
Authors: Que Wu, Yongfang Li, Linying Chen, Ling Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccupational exposure to human papillomaviruses (HPV) poses a potential health risk to healthcare providers, yet preventive awareness and behaviors remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of healthcare providers toward occupational exposure to HPV.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2023 and February 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College of Shantou University. The participants included healthcare providers in gynecology, dermatology, or coloproctology departments. A self-designed questionnaire was employed to collect demographic characteristics and assess KAP toward occupational exposure to HPV. The exploration of the relationships between KAP was performed through Structural Equation Modeling (SEM).ResultsThis study enrolled 1059 participants with a mean age of 34.76 ± 9.56 years. Most participants were female (77.90%), with a bachelor’s degree (46.55%). The mean KAP scores were 19.96 ± 6.20 (possible range: 0–28), 20.84 ± 2.62 (possible range: 6–30), and 24.43 ± 6.50 (possible range: 8–40). In the SEM analysis, knowledge positively influenced attitude (β = 0.171, P < 0.001), and practice (β = 0.091, P = 0.016). Attitude positively influenced practice (β = 0.254, P = 0.001).ConclusionsHealthcare providers demonstrated a moderate knowledge, attitude, and practice towards occupational HPV exposure. Educational and training activities should be implemented to enhance the KAP of healthcare providers toward occupational HPV exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08059-w.Keywords: Cross-sectional study; Healthcare providers; Human papillomavirus; Knowledge, attitude, practice; Occupational hazard

Background

Human papillomaviruses (HPVs) are small, nonenveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses. More than 200 HPV types have been identified and are classified based on the genetic sequence of the L1 major structural protein, tissue tropism, and oncogenic risk in humans [1, 2]. Most HPV types (approximately 80) infect the cutaneous epithelium (nonmucosal) and cause skin warts on the hands and feet. About 40 types infect the mucosal epithelium, including more than 12 high-risk (oncogenic) types [3–5]. Cutaneous infection is transmitted by contact with viral particles, through direct contact with existing warts or fomites [3, 4]. HPV causes benign skin lesions, but high-risk serotypes can lead to cervical, vaginal, vulvar, anal, penile, and oropharyngeal cancers. Some rarer HPV-induced conditions include recurrent respiratory papillomatosis and warts, hypogammaglobulinemia, recurrent infections, and myelokathexis (WHIM) syndrome [3, 4]. HPV infection can also result in cosmetic issues and decreased quality of life [6]. Healthcare providers, especially those treating HPV-associated conditions, may face occupational exposure to HPV [7]. Some evidence suggests that nasal and oropharyngeal HPV infection can occur following exposure to the smoke generated during the surgery or ablation of HPV-related lesions, particularly affecting healthcare providers in gynecology, dermatology, otolaryngology, and genito-urinary departments [8–11]. Direct contact with HPV-infected tissues or fluids during procedures can also pose a risk [12, 13]. The development of HPV-associated diseases in healthcare providers, including oropharyngeal cancer, has been described, suggesting a potential occupational risk [7]. Vaccination against HPV is a recommended method to prevent occupational HPV exposure [7], as endorsed by the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP) for professionals treating HPV-related lesions [7]. Any professional working with HPV-related lesions should be vaccinated [14]. In addition to vaccination, other risk reduction measures, such as proper smoke evacuation, room ventilation, and compliance with personal protective equipment (PPE), are crucial [15]. The British Association of Dermatologists also advocates using smoke extractors and personal protective equipment [16]. Nevertheless, occupational HPV exposure remains a public health issue. A comprehensive understanding of the potential for occupational HPV exposure, associated precautions, and preventive measures is essential for adopting appropriate behaviors.

Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) studies offer insights into the knowledge gaps, misconceptions, and misunderstandings that may serve as barriers to the accurate execution of specific practices within a given population [17, 18]. While no prior KAP study has been conducted on healthcare providers concerning occupational HPV exposure, a study in China revealed insufficient HPV knowledge within the general population, with less than < 50% of healthcare providers demonstrating adequate knowledge of HPV [19]. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the KAP of healthcare providers toward occupational HPV exposure.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2023 and February 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College of Shantou University. The participants included healthcare providers in gynecology, dermatology, or coloproctology departments, comprising physicians, nurses, and other healthcare staff. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College of Shantou University (No. B-2023-217). Written informed consent was provided by the participants before the survey.

Questionnaire introduction

The questionnaire was designed based on previous studies about occupational HPV exposure [7, 9, 14], and the opinions of two experts (one specializing in obstetrics and gynecology and one in coloproctology) with working experience over 20 years were considered for modifying the survey. A pilot study involving a small sample (57 participants) was conducted, resulting in a Cronbach’s α of 0.812, with subscale reliabilities of Knowledge = 0.810, Attitude = 0.718, and Practice = 0.730, indicating an acceptable internal consistency. Also, the results of Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) indicated satisfactory structural validity, with acceptable model fit indices (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1), thereby providing evidence that the integrated instrument possesses a coherent underlying factor structure suitable for subsequent SEM analysis.

The final questionnaire was in Chinese and encompassed six dimensions: demographic characteristics, the Physician Attitude and Behavior (PAB) Scale [20], Physician Perception of Professional Risks (occupational injury section) [21], knowledge dimension, attitude dimension, and practice dimension.

The PAB Scale is a 17-item validated questionnaire that reflects the attitudes (the first four questions) and behaviors of physicians in their medical practice [20]. The questionnaire has also been validated in medical students. To address concerns regarding the psychometric properties of the scale in our sample, we evaluated its reliability and validity using the data collected in this study. The overall Cronbach’s α was 0.884, with subscale reliabilities of Attitude = 0.853 and Behavior = 0.862, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.955 (P < 0.001), suggesting sampling adequacy for factor analysis. Furthermore, CFA demonstrated good structural validity, with satisfactory model fit indices (Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

The Physician Perception of Professional Risks (only the Occupational Injury section was used in this study) assesses physicians’ perceptions of risks associated with occupational injuries [21].

This scale evaluates physicians’ perceptions of various risks encountered in their work. Each potential risk is assigned a score based on its likelihood and severity. Higher likelihood scores indicate that physicians perceive the risk as more likely to occur to them. Elevated severity scores suggest that physicians would face more serious consequences. The total score for each risk is determined by multiplying the likelihood and severity scores. The overall score is then calculated by summing up individual scores. Higher scores on the scale indicate a heightened sense of risk experienced during work.

The knowledge dimension comprised 14 questions, with scores of 2, 1, and 0 for very familiar, heard about, and unfamiliar, respectively. The total score ranged from 0 to 28 points. The attitude dimension consisted of six questions using a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 6 to 30 points. The practice dimension included five questions and eight items. Questions 1–3 used a 5-point Likert scale (positive statements were scored from always = 5 to never = 1, while negative statements were scored from always = 1 to never = 5), while questions 4–5 were scored 5 points for “yes” and 1 point for “no,” resulting in a total score range of 8 to 40 points (higher scores indicate better practice). Categorization of the KAP dimension scores was based on Bloom’s cutoff points. For each dimension, 80%−100% of the total score was considered good knowledge, positive attitude, and proactive practice, 60%−79% was considered moderate, and < 60% was considered poor [22, 23].

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

The questionnaire was administered through both online and offline approaches. For the online survey, a questionnaire was developed using the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/), and a corresponding QR code was generated and distributed to eligible participants at the hospitals via WeChat. For the offline survey, paper-based questionnaires were delivered directly to participants through face-to-face distribution. Trained research assistants were responsible for administering and collecting the questionnaires, ensuring standardization and minimizing data collection bias. All questions were mandatory to reduce item nonresponse, and participants were instructed to complete the survey independently. To ensure data quality and the credibility of the study results, a series of pre-established quality control criteria were applied: questionnaires were excluded if they were incomplete, exhibited obvious logical inconsistencies, demonstrated abnormal values (e.g., age > 120 years), or showed patterned responses (e.g., selecting the same option for all items). These measures were designed to minimize information bias, enhance internal validity, and ensure the reliability of the dataset.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) were used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared by independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as n (%). The correlations between the KAP dimensions were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. The exploration of the relationships between KAP and physician attitudes and risk perception was performed through Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). Model fit was evaluated using Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 1,114 questionnaires were collected. Among these, 12 were excluded due to participants not providing consent for this study, 1 was excluded because all responses to the knowledge section were marked as “uncertain,” and 42 were excluded for logical inconsistencies (e.g., selecting both a specific department and “other” under occupational type). After these exclusions, 1,059 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. The corresponding response rate was therefore 95.1%. The majority of the cases were submitted through the online methods (92.45%). The participants were 34.76 ± 9.56 years old. The study population included physicians (473, 44.66%), nurses (555, 52.41%), and other healthcare staff (31, 2.93%). Most participants were female (77.90%), with a bachelor’s degree (46.55%), graduated (74.88%), associate senior or senior title (54.49%), working in obstetrics and gynecology (50.14%), participated in training about occupational exposure (75.35%), and had a history of occupational exposure (64.21%) (Table 1).

Characteristics and KAP score of the participants

Knowledge, attitude, practice

The mean knowledge score was 19.96 ± 6.20 (possible range: 0–28), indicating moderate knowledge. The knowledge items with the highest unfamiliar rates were K8 (26.53% unfamiliar; “In surgical procedures for treating gynecological diseases, surgical smoke may also contain HPV DNA.”), K7 (22.19% unfamiliar; “During surgical procedures for treating HPV-related diseases, surgical smoke generated by methods like electrocautery or ultrasound may contain HPV DNA, leading to occupational exposure.”), and K10 (17.56% unfamiliar; “Wearing N95 masks, using smoke evacuators, and maintaining ventilation can effectively prevent the occupational exposure risk associated with surgical smoke.”) (Supplementary Table 3).

The mean attitude score was 20.84 ± 2.62 (possible range: 6–30), indicating a moderate attitude. Less favorable attitude scores were observed for A6 (70.72% favorable; “When treating/caring for patients with HPV-related diseases, I would be concerned about the occupational exposure risk.”) and A4 (72.15% favorable; “I am concerned about the potential risks of occupational exposure.”) (Supplementary Table 4).

The mean practice score was 24.43 ± 6.50 (possible range: 8–40, 64.43%), indicating moderate practice. Most participants had favorable practice about seeking knowledge about occupational exposure (65.53%) and participating in such training (62.61%). However, many professionals regularly fail to perform hand hygiene management (24.55%), medical waste management (25.12%), and personal protective measures during procedures (25.87%), and personal protective measures during surgery (26.53%). Only a little more than half of the participants were vaccinated against HPV (54.11%). Most participants would promptly report an occupational exposure event (83.10%) (Supplementary Table 5).

Correlations analysis

The knowledge scores were correlated to the attitude (r = 0.374, P < 0.001), practice (r = 0.113, P < 0.001), physician attitude (r = 0.478, P < 0.001), and risk perception (r = 0.013, P < 0.001) scores. The attitude scores were correlated to the practice (r = 0.106, P < 0.001), physician attitude (r = 0.435, P < 0.001), and risk perception (r = 0.041, P < 0.001) scores. The practice scores were correlated to the physician attitude (r = 0.071, P = 0.022) and risk perception (r = 0.270, P < 0.001) scores (Table 2).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study suggests that healthcare providers exhibited a moderate KAP level regarding occupational HPV exposure. To enhance the KAP of healthcare providers concerning occupational HPV exposure, it is recommended to implement educational and training activities. These results provide valuable insights into potential strategies for improving the KAP of healthcare providers regarding occupational HPV exposure, ultimately contributing to enhanced workplace safety.

Several studies on the KAP toward occupational exposure to biological agents generally revealed unsatisfactory KAP levels, including among fifth-year dental students in Egypt [24], nursing students in Gaza [25], medical college students in China [26], midwives in China [27], healthcare providers in the United Arab Emirates [28] and China [29], nurses in Qatar [30], and laboratory staff in general (meta-analysis of 34 studies) [31]; those previous studies mainly focused on blood-borne infections or COVID-19. Such infections can be more morbid and lethal than HPV infection, but HPV infection still carries an increased risk of skin and mucous cancers [7, 9, 13]. The present study reported moderate KAP of healthcare providers toward occupational HPV exposure, which was not reported before. Only one previous study in China reported poor knowledge of HPV in the general population, including in the subgroup of participants who were working in healthcare, with < 50% of the healthcare providers having adequate knowledge of HPV [19]. The knowledge scores were higher in the present study. Still, the study enrolled only healthcare providers in departments at risk of HPV exposure, i.e., obstetrics and gynecology, dermatology, gastrointestinal surgery, and clinical laboratory. Still, the knowledge remained moderate despite the higher risk. Females had higher KAP scores, which could be because female physicians display a higher motivation toward learning than male physicians [32]. It was also perhaps because female healthcare workers, once infected with HPV, will face more severe health risks than males; they exhibit a higher enthusiasm and motivation to learn about protection against occupational exposure to HPV. Non-physician-non-nurse professionals had more knowledge of HPV, probably because that category included laboratory technicians. Indeed, professionals working in clinical laboratories showed higher KAP scores, probably because they handle human samples and specimens all day long, and biohazards and biological safety are at the core of their training curriculum. Professionals who were early in their careers displayed higher KAP scores, probably because such professionals work more with patients and hold fewer administrative positions. Of course, participants who underwent training about occupational exposure had higher KAP scores, as well as those who never experienced exposure events, probably because of higher care to prevent such events.

The present study showed that knowledge was positively associated with attitude and practice toward occupational HPV exposure. Therefore, designing and implementing educational interventions should improve the attitude and practice of healthcare providers toward HPV. More specifically, the knowledge about the risk of HPV exposure via surgical smoke and the proper methods to avoid HPV exposure through surgical smoke should be improved. In addition, only half of the participants were vaccinated against HPV, which should be improved since the ASCCP highly recommends HPV vaccination among healthcare providers at risk of HPV exposure [7]. Although most HPV infections are benign, patients undergoing procedures for HPV-related lesions are at a higher likelihood of harboring a high-risk (oncogenic) type [1–5, 13], and some reports suggest that healthcare providers exposed to surgical smoke of HPV-related procedures are at higher risk of nasal and oropharyngeal HPV infection [8–11, 13]. Therefore, it is an important public health issue that must be properly addressed.

The present study had several limitations. The participants were concentrated in several provinces, resulting in a relatively small sample size and limiting the generalizability of the conclusions to a specific geographical area. The investigators designed the questionnaire based on local realities and policies, further restricting the generalizability of the conclusions and the exportability of the questionnaire. The study was cross-sectional, precluding causal analysis. Instead, a SEM analysis was conducted, with causality inferred statistically based on predetermined hypotheses. Additionally, all KAP studies are susceptible to social desirability bias, wherein participants may be inclined to respond in a manner they perceive as socially acceptable rather than accurately reflecting their behaviors [33, 34]. The study used a Chinese-translated version of the PAB scale without expert committee review or pre-testing, which may introduce linguistic and cultural bias.

Conclusions

In conclusion, healthcare providers exhibit a moderate KAP level regarding occupational HPV exposure. To enhance the KAP of healthcare providers in this regard, it is recommended to implement educational and training activities. The subsequent steps involve designing an intervention to address these findings and investigating its practical application.
