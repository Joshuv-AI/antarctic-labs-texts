Predicting the progressive-free survival of ALK-positive lung cancer: machine learning algorithm based on habitat imaging
Authors: Fen Wang, Qinmin Hao, Yizhen Jia, Lincen Zhang, Tongfu Yu, Hai Xu, Mei Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer associated with the harbor oncogenic driver mutations or fusions, such as anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), could benefit from representative clinical first-line treatment. And patients with ALK-positive (ALK+) show longer median survival than ALK-negative patients. This study aimed to develop a machine-learning model based on habitat analysis derived from computed tomography (CT) lung images to predict disease progression and explore its prognostic value for progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with (ALK+) lung cancer.MethodsA total of 79 patients with ALK-positive lung cancer and advanced TNM stage (III–IV) at The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between April 2016 and August 2022 were divided into training (n=63) and testing (n=16) cohorts. Radiomics features were extracted from CT images using a habitat-based method with clustering analysis. Subsequently, the performance of eight machine learning algorithms was compared to build a habitat-radiomics (rad_habitat) model, yielding a rad_habitat_score. Using regression, we developed both a clinical model and a combined model that integrated clinical variables with the rad_habitat_score. Subsequently, these models’ performance were evaluated. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed on clinical variables and the rad_habitat_score.ResultsThree distinct habitats were identified. Among the eight machine learning algorithms, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) model demonstrated superior performance in handling non-linearity, achieving a higher area under the curve (AUC) of 0.836 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.628–1.000] in the testing cohort. The combined model (AUC =0.836, 95% CI: 0.628–1.000) exhibited a higher AUC than the clinical model but a similar AUC to the rad_habitat model in the testing set. Delong test revealed a significant difference in the AUC values between the clinical model and the combined model and between the clinical model and rad_habitat model in the training cohort (P=0.002 and P=0.007, respectively), while the AUC values of the other models were not statistically different. Cox regression analysis identified the rad_habitat_score as the only independent predictor of PFS (P<0.001) for patients with high-risk ALK+ lung cancer.ConclusionsThe habitat-radiomics approach, utilizing three regional habitats, shows promise for accurately and interpretably identifying disease progression in ALK+ lung cancer. The rad_habitat_score, when combined with clinical features, demonstrated improved predictive accuracy for prognostic stratification compared with the clinical model alone.Keywords: Habitat imaging, lung cancer, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), disease progression, machine learning

Key findings

• The radiomics model derived from habitats imaging shows promise in accurately and interpretably identifying the disease progression status in lung cancer with anaplastic lymphoma kinase-positive (ALK+).

What is known and what is new?

• Radiomics features based on habitat images have been confirmed to characterize the heterogeneity of tumors and could be used to identify the disease progression status in lung cancer with ALK+.

• We developed and validated three models to predict the progression-free survival (PFS) in lung cancer patients with ALK +, and the results show that the rad_habitat model could achieve similar results to the combined model. The rad_habitat model and combined model provide greater benefits in predicting prognostic status for patients than the clinical model.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The rad_habitat_score was an independent predictor of PFS of lung cancer with ALK+.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide (1), with adenocarcinoma accounting for 50% of cases. While early-stage lung cancer is often treated with surgery or supplemented with chemotherapy, advanced-stage disease carries a poor prognosis and high mortality rates. Accordingly, focusing on advanced-stage patients represents a critical area of unmet medical need. It is widely acknowledged that malignancies harboring oncogenic driver mutations or fusions, such as anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), reactive oxygen species (ROS) proto-oncogene 1 receptor tyrosine kinase, B-raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) and others, can benefit from targeted first-line treatment (2-4). Rearrangements in the ALK gene have been identified in 2–7% of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (5,6). According to a study conducted by Pan et al. (7), the distribution of metastases in patients with ALK or ROS1-positive NSCLC is as follows: bone metastasis (43%), intrapulmonary lesions (37%), pleura metastasis (30%), and brain metastasis (21%). These four types of metastases are the most observed in NSCLC cases. Brain metastases are frequently observed in advanced lung cancer (30–43%) (8) and in NSCLC (10–30%) (9). Symptomatic brain metastases (BM) can occur during the course of chemoradiotherapy in patients (10,11). Moreover, the majority of BM may be observed within 2 years of diagnosis, although baseline neuroimaging revealed no BM during initial NSCLC staging (12). Regarding overall survival (OS) rates, patients with ALK-positive mutations have a median survival time of 9.8 months, compared to 5.6 months in patients with ALK-negative mutations. These findings highlight the importance of preoperative diagnosis of disease progression in lung cancer.

Radiomics analysis analyzes and extracts features from the whole tumor to characterize tumor heterogeneity. However, it is critical to determine whether this heterogeneity is evenly distributed. Few studies have investigated habitat imaging based on radiomics-based machine learning-derived correlation methods (13-15). Habitat image analysis, as reported in several research, has confirmed the predictive value of these imaging technologies aimed at capturing subtle differences among tumors with different subregions (16,17). Based on the aforementioned background and rationale, our study aimed to determine whether radiomics features extracted from inter-tumoral subregions can facilitate the prediction of progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with ALK-positive lung cancer. We also investigated the associations of subregional imaging biomarkers with clinicopathological factors to determine whether they can provide complementary prognostic information beyond the existing predictive system. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-171/rc).

Methods

Patients

The single-center, retrospective study, which involved anonymized data analysis, fully complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 2023-SRFA-337) with a waiver of written informed consent. All procedures related to participant recruitment, data collection, and data analysis were conducted following relevant guidelines and regulations.

A total of 139 patients with advanced [Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) stage III–IV] ALK-positive lung cancers were enrolled between April 2016 and August 2022. All patients were pathologically confirmed through the percutaneous transthoracic lung biopsies and bronchoscopes lung biopsy. Tumor response was assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and CT at baseline, every 4 or 6 weeks for the first 24 weeks, and every 9 weeks thereafter until radiological disease progression (RECIST v1.1) (18). Follow-up

After undergoing regular and comprehensive imaging follow-up, patients’ PFS was evaluated. PFS was defined as the time from the start of treatment to the initial occurrence of disease progression or death from any cause. During follow-up, participants were classified into progression group if they developed intrapulmonary lesions, bone, brain, pleural metastasis, lymph node, liver, adrenal metastasis, or their follow-up records indicated either “alive with disease” or “dead” (with cause of death being lung cancer) (19), whereas others were classified into non-progression group. Time to metastases referred to the period between ALK-positive lung cancer diagnosis and the first radiographic evidence of metastases. Patients without metastatic events were censored at their last follow-up or imaging assessment.

Image acquisition

The imaging data used in the study were obtained from different sites using various MRI vendors (Siemens System, General Electric System), resulting in heterogeneous image data. All patients underwent brain MRI surveillance at 3.0T using several imaging protocols, including axial T1-weighted post-contrast and T2-weighted scans. Additional imaging protocols included non-enhanced T1-weighted, T2-weighted, T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI).

All patients underwent thoracic computed tomography (CT) with a Siemens Somatom Sensation CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The CT acquisition parameters were as follows: detector collimation widths of 64 mm × 0.6 mm and 128 mm × 0.6 mm; tube voltage, 120 kV; and tube current regulated by an automatic exposure control system (CARE Dose 4D).

Clinical features

Two board-certified thoracic radiologists, QMH with 3 years of experience and FW with 5 years of experience, independently performed retrospective blind evaluations of the imaging data of the enrolled patients. They were blinded to the histopathological information and the original CT and MR imaging reports. Any subjective disagreement between the two readers was resolved by reaching a consensus. The following imaging features were recorded and analyzed: (I) location: upper right lobe (RUL), middle right lobe (RML), lower right lobe (RLL), upper left lobe (LUL), lower left lobe (LLL); (II) maximum diameter; (III) hilar enlargement lymph node; (IV) the enhanced CT value of the lesion after contrast enhancement; (V) pericardial effusion; (VI) lymphangitis carcinomatosis; (VII) pleural indentation; (VIII) pathological type (i. adenocarcinoma; ii. squamous cell carcinoma; iii. adenosquamous carcinoma; iv. neuroendocrine carcinoma; v. mixed carcinoma). For analysis, types ii-v were classified as other types. The baseline characteristics included age, gender, and smoking history. The final values for maximum diameter and enhanced CT value with each lesion were determined by the mean values measured by two investigators. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression (LR) analyses are employed to identify independent predictors of the clinical model.

Data processing

Two board-certified thoracic radiologists, Q.H. with 3 years and L.Z. with 10 years of experience in thoracic radiology, utilized in-house software called Multilabel. This software employs a semi-automatic methodology to delineate the volume of interest (VOI) encompassing the tumor area. Subsequently, the radiologists manually delineated these VOIs. These feature sets analyzed original, morphologic, and textural characteristics. The radiomics features were then extracted using the open-source software package called Pyradiomics, implemented on the Python 3.7 platform (http://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html). In this study, the extracted features are comprehensively documented in the Radiomics library. These features encompass multiple categories, including: first-order features, shape features, gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features, gray level run-length matrix (GLRLM) features, neighbouring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features, and gray level dependency matrix (GLDM) features. For a detailed description of the high-throughput radiomics features extracted in this study, you can refer to the Radiomics library documentation available at http://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io. These features conformed to the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) (20) and facilitate the clustering of pixels within the tumor region based on their local characteristics via Python, which assigns distinct color labels to each cluster, resulting in a clustering label map that provides insight into the global distribution pattern of the internal tumor area (21).

Machine learning with radiomics

A habitat map was constructed wherein the intensity values of voxels were replaced with the previously identified habitat index. The K-means method was used for clustering sub-regions in this study. The Silhouette coefficient used as the criterion in selecting the best number of clusters at the patient population level. We adopted the concept of quantitative imaging features from radiomics studies to measure the spatial heterogeneity between the identified subregions. A total of 4,683 features were extracted from delineated VOI. Radiomics features were extracted from three subregional habitat images, as depicted in Figure S1. All filtered features were normalized using the Z-score method (22), and the mean and variance of each column feature were subsequently calculated.

To screen for radiomics features with good reproducibility and low redundancy, we first performed independent sample t-tests on the radiomics features, removing any features with P values greater than 0.05. Next, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) to assess the relationship between features and retain any pairs of features with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.9. For further analysis, we utilized the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm. Finally, to evaluate the importance of each feature, we generated a histogram of the coefficients selected by the LASSO algorithm. This visualization enabled quantitative evaluation of feature significance within our model. For standardization, each feature column was normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, thereby transforming the data to follow a standard normal distribution.

Subsequently a machine learning classification model was constructed using the scikit learn machine learning library incorporating eight different classifiers (23): LR, support vector machine (SVM) (24), K-nearest neighbor (KNN) (25), random forest (RF) (26), Extra Trees (ET) (27), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) (28), Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) (29) and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) (30). To reduce overfitting, 5-fold cross-validation was performed during the training phase to optimize model parameters, and the corresponding rad_habitat_score was calculated. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values can be adopted to interpret the output of machine learning models. These values facilitate the allocation of each feature’s contribution towards the model’s final prediction. Feature importance ranking using SHAP values can be used to identify the most important features (31). The SHAP Python package provides a comprehensive set of functions and algorithms for this analysis.

Development of the combined model

LR analysis, including both clinical factors and rad_habitat_score, was performed to identify predictors of disease progression in ALK-positive lung cancer. Variables with significant association (P<0.05) with ALK-positive status in the univariate logistic analysis were subsequently included in multivariate LR to develop a combined model, which was visualized as a nomogram.

Efficacy evaluation of the model

The prediction performance of the optimal habitat_radiomics model was assessed by plotting the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to compare the clinical utility of the various predictive models.

PFS analysis

According to the cutoff of the rad_habitat_score, which was determined by the surv_cutpoint function in R package “survminer”, participants were categorized into a high-risk group and a low-risk group (32). The survival curve was generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the difference between the low- and high-risk groups was evaluated by log-rank test.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.0.2, www.Rproject.org), SPSS (Chicago, v.20) and python (version 3.71, https://www.python.org). The statistical significance for all two-tailed tests was set at P<0.05.

The present study included 79 participants who were randomly allocated, with 80% assigned to the training set (positive:negative, 20:43) and the remaining 20% to the testing set (positive:negative, 5:11). Radiomics features selection and rad_habitat model development

The interobserver reliability for VOI measurements, assessed using the inter-class correlation coefficient, yielded an average value of 0.818 across all habitat radiomics features.

Analysis of the Silhouette coefficient identified three as the optimal number of clusters. Thus, the tumor region was clustered into three sub-regions, as shown in Figure S2. A total of 1,561 features were extracted from each CT image across three tumor subregions. Development of the nomogram and evaluation of different models

The visualized nomogram was constructed on the rad_habitat_score and the selected clinical variable (lymphangitis carcinomatosis) using stepwise regression (Figure 5). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test demonstrated adequate calibration of the nomogram, which yielded P values of 0.851 and 0.320 in the training and testing cohorts, respectively. The combined model exhibited higher diagnostic efficiency in the training set (AUC =0.909, 95% CI: 0.882–0.997; accuracy: 0.857, sensitivity: 0.950, specificity: 0.814, F1-score: 0.809) and the testing dataset (AUC =0.836, 95% CI: 0.628–1.000; accuracy: 0.813, sensitivity: 1.000, specificity:0.727, F1-score:0.769). We found that the AUC values for the combined model and the rad_habitat model were similar, but higher than the clinical model (Table 4). A comparison of the ROC curves for these models is depicted in Figure 6A,6B. The DeLong test was used to demonstrate the statistical differences between these models. In the training set, the AUC values for the clinical model vs. the rad_habitat model, as well as for the clinical model vs. the combined model, were statistically significant (P<0.05). However, the AUC values of the other models were not statistically different. Detailed results of the DeLong test between different models are exhibited in Table S1. We further examined an individual patient prediction with a output value of −1.02, which is lower than the base value (–0.85). In this case, the rad_habitat_score acted as a negative contributor to the overall assessment, resulting in the patient being classified into the positive group (Figure S3). The DCA curves for three different models were analyzed in both the training and testing sets (Figure S4). The rad_habitat and combined models showed comparable predictive benefits in testing datasets. Specifically, in the training sets, these two models conferred greater net benefit in predicting the PFS of patients compared to the clinical model at threshold probabilities ranging from 5% to 86%. Furthermore, in the testing set, the rad_habitat and combined models provided greater benefits in predicting progression status for patients than the clinical model at threshold probabilities ranging from 5% to 46%.

PFS analysis

The median follow-up for patients with ALK+ lung cancer (n=79) was 15 months (interquartile range, 10–40 months), with 25 cases (31.6%) of recurrences involving local-regional (n=5) recurrence and distant metastasis (n=20). The median PFS was 11 months (interquartile range, 5.0–12.5 months) in the rad_habitat_score-predicted high-risk group, and 26 months (interquartile range, 15–44 months) in the low-risk group.

Cox-regression was next conducted to predict the progression status in ALK+ lung cancer patients. Eventually, only the rad_habitat_score was identified as an independent predictor for progression during multivariable Cox analysis (P<0.001) (Table 5). The optimal cutoff value for stratifying patients between high- and low-risk groups, calculated based on the rad_habitat_score, was −0.76 using the surv_cutpoint function (Figure 7A). Discussion

The presence of diverse intratumoral spatial patterns is thought to play a role in the tumor’s capacity to adjust and acquire resistance while undergoing cancer treatment in patients (33). This may result in clinical consequences, including accelerated tumor growth and reduced survival (33-35). Accurately comprehending the features of a patient’s specific tumor phenotype is essential for providing personalized treatment guidance. Habitat imaging offers a potential non-invasive approach to clearly pinpoint unique tumor regions or cell subpopulations (21,35).

The previous research related to developing a radiomics model based on pre-treatment CT to predict brain metastasis in stage III/IV ALK-positive NSCLC patients, achieving moderate performance in the test set (AUC =0.642, 95% CI: 0.501–0.783, specificity =60.0%, sensitivity =83.3%) (8), The moderate predictive accuracy may be attribute to the variations in treatment regimens among NSCLC patients, highlighting the potential influence of unaccounted tumor heterogeneity. Our study classified and segmented ALK-positive lung cancer into three subregions using the Silhouette coefficient. Next, numerous radiomics features were extracted from the three subregions, and eight machine learning algorithms were adopted to identify the optimal model. The importance of each feature in the model was ranked by the visualized SHAP value method to determine the features most closely associated with enhanced stability and identify the key characteristics that predict the progression in lung cancer with ALK-positive. As a result, a rad_habitat_score derived from habitat imaging effectively categorized the progression probability in ALK-positive lung cancer, aiding in clinical decision-making for both patients and clinicians. Notably, the radiomics model derived from habitat imaging. model achieved comparable diagnostic efficacy (AUC =0.836 in the testing dataset) and clinical utility to the combined model. This result suggests that the radiomics model based on habitat imaging could be a non-invasive tool to monitor progression status in ALK+ lung cancer. Another study also developed a MR-based radiomic model for survival and intracranial progression stratification in a limited cohort (n=87), reporting strong predictive performance (AUC =0.84 in training set, AUC =0.85 in testing set) (18). Importantly, the radiomics-score significantly differentiated the high- and low-risk groups (P=0.017). In our study, multivariate cox regression further confirmed that the rad_habitat_score was the only independent predictor of PFS (P<0.001) for patients with high-risk ALK+ lung cancer, reinforcing its clinical utility.

Variations in the design of machine learning algorithm models play a key role in reflecting the diverse areas of emphasis in the algorithms and their representation of radiomics features in cancer pathology. LR, a prevalent model that derives parameters directly from data, is essentially a linear model. However, when dealing with high-dimensional and highly correlated radiomics features in CT profile, it may struggle to effectively handle the excessive features. SVM utilizes kernels to operate on reduced and manageable data representations rather than directly on the features. However, the performance of SVM may not exhibit advantages when the sample size is insufficient to support vectors in higher dimensions and determine the key hyperplane. One of the primary challenges in supervised learning is overfitting, with the MLP being particularly prone to this issue (13). In the present study, we applied LASSO to select the features that may contribute to reducing the complexity of the ML classifiers and avoid overfitting (30). The application of LASSO was not adopted in Deng et al.’s study (13), resulting in their study incorporating a larger number of features for model development. In our study, the model developed by MLP showed the best performance compared with the other seven models. Overall, when building a classification model based on habitat-radiomics features, it is essential to comprehensively consider both the patient sample size and disease characteristics. SHAP values were utilized to interpret the top-ranked features. In our study, the top-ranked features included original shape sphericity, aligning with findings from prior research (13).

Irrespective of the clinical model, emphasis was placed on risk predictors for ALK+ lung cancer, which ultimately presented with an adverse survival endpoint. Current oncological research has identified lymphangitis carcinomatosis as a terminal manifestation of metastatic disease associated with poor clinical outcomes. This pathological process involves neoplastic cell invasion of lymphatic channels, resulting in obliterative lymphangitis. The metastatic cascade progresses with tumor cells penetrating vascular endothelial barriers to ultimately colonize perivascular lymphatic spaces. Even though hilar lymph node enlargement was excluded as an independent risk factor, it is well-established that the widespread retrograde permeation and embolization may occur (36).

Cox regression and LR analyses were conducted in our study. Notably, the nomogram exhibited superior performance in predicting progression status than the clinical model, while demonstrating comparable performance with the rad_habitat model. Besides, the rad_habitat_score was regarded as an independent predictor. Notably, high-risk patients experienced a significantly increased risk of progression compared with low-risk patients.

This investigation advances prior research through several methodological innovations. Importantly, a novel habitat imaging quantification approach was developed employing radiomics feature analysis, subsequently examining prognostic correlations between rad_habitat characteristics and survival outcomes. Furthermore, we implemented comparative machine learning analyses to identify the optimal predictive model within our habitat-based framework.

Indeed, there are limitations in this study that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the small sample size of patients presents constraints on the validation of the identified habitats. Secondly, the retrospective nature of the study may lead to selection bias. Furthermore, our machine learning algorithm model was subject to mechanistic constraints, potentially impacting its overall applicability. Regrettably, the limited sample size only enabled internal validation without external validation. Therefore, future research should prioritize increasing the sample size and external multicenter validation to facilitate the development of a more robust and scientifically sound model.

Conclusions

The radiomics model derived from habitat imaging demonstrated superior performance compared to the clinical model. The integration of the rad_habitat_score and clinical variables may offer enhanced clinical utility as a better supporting tool in clinical decision-making for ALK-positive lung cancer patients.
