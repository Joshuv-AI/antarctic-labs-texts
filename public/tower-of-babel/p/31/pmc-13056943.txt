Comprehensive characterization of VSIR reveals dual epigenetic regulation and immune landscape across hematological malignancies
Authors: Zi-jun Xu, Xiao-mei Wu, Ran Chang, Hao-yu Cao, Fei Wang, Xiang-mei Wen, Jing‑dong Zhou, Jiang Lin, Jun Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVISTA (V-domain Immunoglobulin Suppressor of T cell Activation), encoded by VSIR, functions as an inhibitory checkpoint predominantly expressed on myeloid cells. Despite its recognized role in solid tumors, systematic characterization of VSIR regulation and clinical implications in hematological malignancies remains limited. We performed integrative multi-omics analyses of diverse hematological malignancies (> 10,000 transcriptomes) to elucidate VSIR expression patterns, epigenetic regulation, and therapeutic potential. VSIR exhibited preferential upregulation in hematological malignancies, particularly myeloid leukemias. We identified dual epigenetic mechanisms driving VSIR overexpression: promoter hypomethylation progressively intensified from healthy controls through myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) to acute myeloid leukemia (AML), validated by targeted bisulfite sequencing in 130 clinical samples; chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing revealed direct transcriptional activation by KMT2A fusion proteins through enrichment of H3K4me3, H3K79me2, and H3K27ac activating marks at the VSIR promoter. Menin inhibitor treatment substantially reduced KMT2A occupancy and histone modifications, confirming Menin-dependent regulation. Similarly, NPM1 mutations promoted VSIR expression through stabilizing Menin-containing chromatin complexes. Functionally, VSIR-high tumors showed enrichment in immune regulatory pathways and predicted favorable immunotherapy responses. Prognostically, elevated VSIR expression conferred adverse outcomes in AML while predicting improved survival in DLBCL and MM. Computationally, VSIR-high patients showed increased likelihood of benefiting from immune checkpoint blockade, validated in real-world immunotherapy cohorts. The elucidated Menin-VSIR regulatory axis suggests potential for combining Menin inhibitors with VISTA checkpoint blockade in KMT2A-rearranged AML. Our findings suggest that VSIR expression is epigenetically dysregulated in hematological malignancies through dual mechanisms, which could inform biomarker-driven patient selection and rational design of combination immunotherapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41978-2.Keywords: VISTA, immune checkpoint, hematological malignancies, epigenetic regulation, immunotherapy, DNA methylationSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology, Oncology

Introduction

Immune checkpoint molecules play critical roles in maintaining immune homeostasis by regulating the balance between immune activation and tolerance1,2. The therapeutic blockade of checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 has revolutionized cancer treatment in multiple solid tumor types3–5. However, the efficacy of current checkpoint inhibitors remains limited in hematological malignancies, highlighting the urgent need to identify alternative immune regulatory targets6. Recent studies have revealed that distinct checkpoint molecules may dominate immune suppression in different cancer contexts, suggesting that tissue- and lineage-specific checkpoint inhibitors could offer more effective therapeutic strategies7,8.

VISTA (V-domain Immunoglobulin Suppressor of T cell Activation), encoded by the VSIR gene, is a critical negative immune checkpoint regulating both adaptive and innate immunity. As a B7 family member, VISTA is predominantly expressed on myeloid cells, where it suppresses T cell activation and regulates innate immunity by dampening TLR signaling, inhibiting MAPK/AP-1 and NF-κB cascades, thereby limiting inflammatory cytokine production9. VISTA deficiency enhances Th17 differentiation and exacerbates inflammatory conditions10,11. In the tumor microenvironment, VSIR/VISTA functions as an immunomodulatory checkpoint that suppresses anti-tumor immunity12,13. It is expressed on tumor cells and immune cells across multiple solid tumors, where high expression correlates with poorer prognosis and immunosuppressive microenvironments13–15. Mechanistically, VISTA is enriched on myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), enhancing MDSC suppressive activity16,17, and preclinical studies demonstrate that VISTA blockade can reactivate antitumor immunity18.

Extending these observations to hematologic malignancies, VSIR exerts potent immunosuppressive effects with distinct expression patterns and prognostic implications. In AML, VISTA is highly expressed on MDSCs and leukemic blasts, inhibiting T cell activation16. Notably, VSIR overexpression is enriched in AML with DNMT3A and NPM1 mutations, correlating with poor prognosis19–22, and shows preferential expression in myeloid leukemias (AML, CML, JMML)7. Beyond AML, elevated VISTA expression in CML is linked to TKI resistance23, while in NK/T-cell lymphoma, high expression predicts poor prognosis and PD-1 blockade resistance24.

Despite these advances, several gaps remain in understanding VISTA’s role in hematologic malignancies. The epigenetic mechanisms governing VSIR expression across cancer subtypes remain poorly defined25, and comprehensive transcriptional regulation mechanisms require further investigation. Moreover, although transcriptomic analyses have provided insights into VISTA’s prognostic value20, systematic evaluation of its potential as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response across diverse hematological malignancies has yet to be established, and clinical trials remain in early stages. Leveraging our group’s experience in multi-omics characterization of immune regulatory molecules26–31, this study systematically defines the regulatory mechanisms controlling VSIR expression in hematologic malignancies, delineates its role in the immune microenvironment, and evaluates its potential as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response, thereby providing a foundation for precision immunotherapy in hematologic cancers.

Materials and methods

Expression data collection

mRNA expression data for normal tissues and cancer cell lines were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx; https://gtexportal.org) project and the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE; https://www.broadinstitute.org/ccle), respectively. The curated Hemap dataset, comprising 16 hematologic malignancy types and normal hematopoietic cell populations, was defined by Dufva et al. (Synapse ID: syn21991014; DOI: 10.7303/syn21991014)7. Pan-cancer expression data integrating TCGA, TARGET, and GTEx were downloaded from the UCSC Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net). The Pan-Hem-Diff cohort, including 40 datasets with matched tumor and normal samples across 22 hematologic cancer types (n = 9,101), was curated based on our previous work26.

Datasets with survival information for AML, DLBCL, and MM were retrieved from GEO, the GDC data portal, cBioPortal, and PRECOG, as described previously26. VSIR expression patterns were validated in three independent AML cohorts (BeatAML, n = 451; TCGA AML, n = 173; GSE6891, n = 461). Prognostic associations were primarily evaluated in GSE10358 (n = 269) and GSE6891, whereas the TCGA AML cohort served for clinical correlation and mutation landscape analyses. Copy number variation (CNV) and mutation data for hematologic cancers were obtained from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org).

Proteomic data analysis

VSIR protein expression data were obtained from the publicly available AML proteomics database (https://proteomics.leylab.org/), which provides TMT-based quantitative protein abundance measurements for 44 TCGA AML samples32. Clinical annotations and mutational profiles were retrieved from the database along with the proteomic data. Statistical comparisons of VSIR protein levels across clinical subgroups were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis tests (multiple groups). Correlations with continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

DNA methylation data collection

DNA methylation profiles for cancer cell lines were retrieved from the CCLE database to characterize cancer-type-specific epigenetic patterns across hematologic malignancies and solid tumors. Clinical methylation datasets were integrated for validation, including DiseaseMeth (http://diseasemeth.edbc.org/; 271 AML patients and 10 healthy controls), GSE58477 (62 cytogenetically normal AML patients and 10 CD34⁺ controls), and GSE63409 (44 AML patients and 30 controls). Four CpG sites near the VSIR transcription start site were assessed for site-specific hypomethylation. Paired expression and methylation data from the TCGA AML cohort were further analyzed to evaluate the inverse association between VSIR expression and methylation levels.

Targeted bisulfite sequencing

To validate VSIR promoter hypomethylation patterns observed in public datasets, targeted bisulfite sequencing was performed in our clinical cohort. Clinical samples for targeted methylation analysis were derived from a cohort of myeloid malignancy patients enrolled at the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Jiangsu University between 2002 and 2018. The cohort included 103 de novo AML patients, 28 MDS patients, 12 secondary AML patients, and 25 healthy bone marrow donors as controls. All patients received induction and consolidation chemotherapy according to standard protocols. Genomic DNA was extracted from bone marrow mononuclear cells. Four CpG sites within the VSIR promoter region on chromosome 10 (− 989 to − 1148 bp relative to the transcription start site) were interrogated by bisulfite sequencing. Bisulfite conversion was carried out using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research). PCR amplification was performed with bisulfite-specific primers, and amplicons were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform. β values were averaged to represent VSIR promoter methylation. Hypomethylation progression was assessed across healthy controls, MDS, secondary AML, and primary AML. An independent reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) dataset from our previous study33(accession number: PRJNA670308) was used to corroborate VSIR hypomethylation in MDS. The collection and use of all clinical samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Jiangsu University (Approval No. K-20230212-Y), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from hematologic malignancies were retrieved from the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub 2 (TISCH2; http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/home/). The integrated dataset included 15 studies covering AML, ALL, CLL, MM, and PCFCL. VSIR expression was quantified across 13 cellular populations, including malignant, immune effector, and stromal cells. For focused AML single-cell validation, we analyzed two additional AML single-cell datasets: the Galen et al. cohort (15530 cells from AML patients)34 and the FIMM AML cohort (32796 cells)7. Normal bone marrow controls were obtained from the Human Cell Atlas (HCA) dataset (170072 cells). VSIR-high cells were defined as those with expression above the 75th percentile among VSIR-positive cells. For ligand-receptor interaction analysis, we calculated cell-cell communication scores as the product of mean ligand expression in source cells and mean receptor expression in target cells, focusing on VISTA-PSGL1 (VSIR-SELPLG) and VISTA-LRIG1 (VSIR-LRIG1) pairs across myeloid and T cell populations.

Tumor immune microenvironment and immunomodulatory gene analysis

To assess associations between VSIR expression and tumor immune microenvironment (TME) features, 29 functional gene expression signatures described by Bagaev et al.35 were analyzed by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Immune and stromal scores were estimated using ESTIMATE and xCell. Immune cell fractions were further deconvoluted with CIBERSORT and MCP-counter. Cytolytic activity was quantified in the Hemap dataset as described previously7. Spearman correlations between VSIR and immunomodulatory genes were computed across hematologic malignancies.

Immunotherapy response prediction

The predictive potential of VSIR for immunotherapy response was evaluated by combining computational algorithms and clinical datasets. Response predictions for seven hematologic cancers (AML, MDS, ALL, CLL, DLBCL, FL, and MM) were generated with the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm. AML datasets including BeatAML, GSE6891, and TCGA AML were analyzed to compare VSIR expression between predicted responders and non-responders.

For validation in solid tumors, transcriptomic data from immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) cohorts were analyzed, including melanoma treated with anti-CTLA-4 (Snyder et al., NEJM 2014; n = 64; Nathanson et al., Cancer Immunol Res 2017; n = 24)36,37, metastatic melanoma (Liu et al., Nat Med 2019; n = 144, anti-PD-1; Gide et al., Cancer Cell 2019; n = 120, anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4)38,39, and pancreatic cancer (Parikh et al., Nat Cancer 2021; n = 25, radiation + anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1)40. Data and clinical annotations were obtained from GEO, cBioPortal, the TIDE platform, or supplementary files. Treatment response was defined per RECIST, with CR/PR as responders and SD/PD as non-responders.

Gene set enrichment analysis

For bulk transcriptomic analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing the top and bottom 30% of VSIR-expressing samples in the Hemap dataset. Enrichment was performed with the clusterProfiler R package using Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). For single-cell validation, pathway activity scores were calculated in AML scRNA-seq dataset GSE116256 using GSVA. Differential pathway activity was assessed with limma, focusing on immune pathways including TNFα–NF-κB, interferon responses, and IL6–JAK–STAT3 cascades.

Survival and prognostic analysis

Survival endpoints including overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and event-free survival (EFS) were analyzed. Patients were dichotomized into VSIR-high and VSIR-low groups using optimal cutoffs defined by the maxstat algorithm in the Survminer R package. Survival curves were compared by log-rank tests, and hazard ratios were estimated by Cox proportional hazards regression. Meta-analyses across datasets were performed with the survcomp package. To assess added prognostic value, LI24 and LSC17 risk scores were recalculated as described previously41. Patients stratified by these models were further subdivided by VSIR expression to test incremental prognostic discrimination.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to assess the independent prognostic value of VSIR expression after adjusting for clinical and molecular covariates. Variables with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. For GSE10358, the model included age, NPM1 mutation status, and VSIR expression group. For GSE6891, the model incorporated age, NPM1, FLT3-ITD, FLT3-TKD, CEBPA mutation status, EVI1 expression group, cytogenetic risk category, and VSIR expression group. Forest plots were generated to visualize hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

ChIP-seq public data analysis

To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms governing VSIR expression in KMT2A-rearranged AML, we analyzed publicly available chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) datasets targeting KMT2A fusion proteins, Menin, and active histone modifications. ChIP-seq data for MLL-AF9 and associated histone marks (H3K79me2, H3K4me3, H3K27ac) in primary AML samples were obtained from GSE89336 and GSE29130. To assess the effects of Menin inhibition on chromatin occupancy, we utilized datasets from MI-503-treated mouse MA9 cells (GSE186695) and SNDX-5613-treated MV4-11 cells (GSE196036). Additionally, ChIP-seq data from NPM1c degron experiments were retrieved from GSE197381 to evaluate NPM1 mutation effects on Menin recruitment at the VSIR locus.

ChIP-seq datasets were processed following standard pipelines with peak calling performed using MACS2. Genomic tracks were visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) for detailed examination of protein occupancy and histone modification patterns at the VSIR promoter region. For datasets with RPKM-normalized bigWig files, signals were displayed in IGV with group autoscaling applied to normalize ChIP-seq signals across comparable factors. Chromatin occupancy changes following pharmacological or genetic interventions were assessed by comparing signal intensities between treated and control conditions at the VSIR locus and established KMT2A target genes.

Menin inhibitor treatment and RT-qPCR analysis

For Menin inhibitor treatment, OCI-AML3 cells were treated with VTP-50469 (4 µM) or DMSO vehicle control for 48 h before RNA isolation. Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy kit (Qiagen) and reverse transcribed with PrimeScript RT reagent kit. Quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System with the following primers: VSIR forward 5’-ACGCCGTATTCCCTGTATGTC-3’, reverse 5’-TTGTAGAAGGTCACATCGTGC-3’; GAPDH forward 5’-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3’, reverse 5’-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3’. GAPDH served as internal control. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. PCR cycling conditions: 95 °C for 30s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10s, 60 °C for 30s, and 70 °C for 30s.

Statistical analysis and data processing

Expression data were log2-transformed and normalized according to platform requirements. DNA methylation data were processed as β values (Illumina arrays) or percentage methylation (bisulfite sequencing). For targeted bisulfite sequencing, β values were averaged per promoter region. Batch effects were evaluated and corrected using ComBat when necessary. Group comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (continuous variables) and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests (categorical variables). Spearman correlations were computed for associations between continuous variables, including expression–methylation relationships. Differential expression analyses across Pan-Hem-Diff cohorts were meta-analyzed with MetaVolcanoR. Statistical significance was defined using appropriate tests for each dataset, with multiple testing corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) where applicable. A summary of datasets, accession numbers, and applications is provided in Table S1.

Results

Lineage-dependent landscape of vsir expression in normal and malignant contexts

Since the biological functions of immune checkpoint molecules are often shaped by their tissue and lineage distribution7,8, we first assessed VSIR expression using GTEx for normal tissues and the Hemap resource for hematopoietic populations. VSIR was most abundant in immune-privileged and hematopoietic compartments, with enrichment in myeloid lineages such as neutrophils and monocytes, but reduced levels in lymphoid populations (Fig. 1A, B). Analysis of CCLE cell lines and Hemap patient cohorts consistently showed that VSIR expression was highest in myeloid malignancies (AML, CML), progressively lower in lymphoid malignancies and MM, and minimal in solid tumors (Fig. 1C, D), highlighting a lineage-dependent regulatory pattern. Extending to a pan-cancer analysis of 33 tumor types, VSIR expression was most strikingly elevated in AML, with additional upregulation observed in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) and low-grade glioma (LGG), while the majority of other cancer types showed downregulation relative to normal tissues (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these data highlight AML as the context with the most pronounced VSIR dysregulation, underscoring its relevance for mechanistic and clinical investigation.

Genetic alterations contribute minimally to VSIR dysregulation

For differential expression analysis, we leveraged the Pan-Hem-Diff cohort (40 datasets, 22 subtypes, > 9,000 samples) and applied the MetaVolcanoR framework to integrate p-values and effect sizes across cohorts, thereby increasing statistical power (Table S2). This meta-analysis revealed that VSIR expression was most strikingly elevated in AML, with additional upregulation in myeloid malignancies such as CML and PMF, whereas the majority of lymphoid tumors showed downregulation, most notably ALCL and ALL (Fig. 2A, Table S2).

We next examined whether these dysregulated patterns could be attributed to genetic alterations. Across all subtypes, mutations were the predominant alteration type but remained exceedingly rare (highest in DLBCL at ~ 2.1%, followed by MM at ~ 0.5%), while AML and ALL harbored almost no recurrent changes beyond occasional deletions (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that VSIR dysregulation in hematologic malignancies is not genetically encoded but instead likely reflects transcriptional and epigenetic regulation.

Comprehensive methylation profiling reveals cancer-type-specific epigenetic regulation

Given that genetic alterations contributed minimally to VSIR dysregulation, we next examined whether epigenetic mechanisms, in particular DNA methylation, underlie its aberrant expression in hematologic malignancies. Using CCLE data, we performed detailed analysis across three distinct VSIR genomic regions (VSIR_1, VSIR_2, VSIR_3). AML cell lines consistently showed hypomethylation at all three loci, with VSIR_3 displaying the most pronounced reduction relative to other cancer types (Fig. 2C). Similar hypomethylation patterns were observed in additional hematologic cancers, including CML, ALL, CLL, DLBCL, and MM, whereas most solid tumor cell lines retained higher methylation, especially at VSIR_2 and VSIR_3, underscoring cancer-type-specific regulation (Fig. 2C and Fig. S1A).

To validate these findings in clinical samples, we analyzed multiple independent patient datasets. DiseaseMeth data (271 AML patients, 10 controls) revealed significantly lower methylation in AML (p = 0.005; Fig. 2D), and reduced methylation was also observed in DLBCL relative to healthy individuals (Fig. S1B). Consistent results were obtained from two additional datasets (GSE58477 and GSE63409), where specific CpG sites near the VSIR transcription start site (cg24499627_553, cg23282441_589, cg06372475_948, cg11633461_1000) showed significant hypomethylation in AML (Fig. 2E). These sites likely represent critical regulatory elements driving VSIR upregulation in this context.

We further validated these observations using our own cohort. Targeted bisulfite sequencing of 103 AML (65 with evaluable data), 28 MDS, 12 secondary AML (sAML), and 25 controls confirmed significantly reduced VSIR methylation in AML, revealing a progressive hypomethylation pattern from healthy controls through MDS and sAML to primary AML (Fig. 2F, Fig. S1C, and Table S3). Moreover, reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) data from our previous study33 independently corroborated lower methylation in MDS, supporting the stepwise nature of epigenetic alterations during myeloid transformation (Fig. S1D).

Finally, analysis of paired TCGA AML expression and methylation data demonstrated a significant inverse correlation (R = − 0.33, p = 1.3e-05; Fig. S1E), providing mechanistic evidence that hypomethylation directly contributes to elevated VSIR expression. Collectively, these results establish hypomethylation as a cancer-type-specific mechanism underlying VSIR dysregulation, with myeloid malignancies showing the most pronounced epigenetic activation.

Prognostic significance of VSIR in hematologic malignancies

The above analyses established that VSIR exhibits hematologic cancer–specific expression patterns, prompting us to next investigate whether these differences carry prognostic relevance. We therefore evaluated the impact of VSIR expression on overall survival (OS) across three major blood cancer types—AML, DLBCL, and MM. Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups using the Maxstat algorithm, and prognostic effects were assessed by univariable Cox regression followed by cancer type–specific meta-analyses.

This analysis uncovered strikingly context-dependent roles of VSIR across hematologic malignancies. In AML, elevated VSIR expression consistently emerged as an adverse prognostic factor, conferring significantly worse OS across multiple independent cohorts (HR > 1.5, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3A). By contrast, high VSIR expression was associated with favorable outcomes in both DLBCL and MM. In DLBCL, overexpression of VSIR predicted improved survival across diverse datasets (HR < 1, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3B). Most notably, in MM, elevated VSIR expression conferred a pronounced survival benefit, with the meta-analysis revealing a substantial protective effect (HR < 0.5, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3C).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the prognostic significance of VSIR is highly context-dependent: functioning as an adverse biomarker in AML, but serving as a favorable prognostic indicator in lymphoid malignancies. This duality underscores the importance of disease-specific biology in shaping the clinical impact of immune checkpoint molecules.

Correlations between VSIR expression and the tumor microenvironment in hematologic malignancies

Given the emerging role of VSIR as a noncanonical immune checkpoint, we systematically examined its associations with tumor microenvironment (TME) features across hematologic cancers. Analysis of 29 TME signature scores revealed highly context-dependent correlation patterns. In myeloid neoplasms, distinct patterns emerged: MDS and CML showed positive correlations with anti-tumor immune signatures, including NK cells and cytotoxic T cells, whereas AML exhibited stronger associations with pro-tumor macrophage-related programs (Fig. 4A, Table S4). In lymphoid malignancies, CLL showed positive correlations with pro-tumor signatures including macrophages and MDSCs, yet inverse associations with proliferation and EMT programs. Notably, VSIR expression correlated positively with MDSC signatures across myeloid neoplasms and lymphoid leukemias (ALL and CLL) (Fig. 4A, Table S4), consistent with prior reports linking VISTA to MDSC-mediated immunosuppression in hematological malignancies16. Other lymphoid malignancies showed weaker correlations overall. These findings indicate that VSIR plays divergent, context-dependent roles across hematological malignancies, ranging from anti-tumor immunity to pro-tumor microenvironment modulation.

To quantify overall immune and stromal infiltration, we applied ESTIMATE and xCell algorithms. VSIR expression showed strong positive correlations with immune scores across multiple subtypes, including T-ALL, pre-B ALL, PTCL-NOS, MDS, CML, CLL, CHL, and AML (Fig. 4B, C). Stromal correlations were most pronounced in CLL and CML (Fig. 4D). Moreover, cytolytic activity scores revealed positive associations with VSIR in CLL, CML, and MDS, but a striking negative association in MM (Fig. 4E), pointing to disease-specific divergence in effector function. Immune cell deconvolution analyses revealed that VSIR expression aligned with monocyte/macrophage infiltration across most cancer types, while correlating positively with CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells and negatively with CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. S2A). Additionally, correlation analyses with immunomodulatory genes showed broad positive associations in myeloid malignancies and lymphoid leukemias, contrasting with predominantly negative correlations in MM (Fig. S2B).

Based on these correlation patterns, single-cell RNA-seq analysis confirmed preferential VSIR expression in monocytes/macrophages, NK cells, and CD8⁺ T cells within the TME (Fig. 4F). To validate these findings in AML, we analyzed two independent single-cell datasets (Galen et al.34) and FIMM cohorts7. UMAP visualization demonstrated predominant VSIR enrichment in monocyte/macrophage populations (Fig. 4G-H), with 22.6% and 44.3% of monocytes classified as VSIR-high in the respective cohorts (Fig. S3A). Notably, FIMM AML samples exhibited elevated VSIR-high proportions across multiple immune cell types, including NK cells (29.4%) and CD8 + T effector memory cells (22.4%), relative to normal bone marrow (Fig. S3A).

Given the preferential expression in myeloid and T cell populations, we next examined potential VISTA ligand-receptor interactions by analyzing recently identified binding partners PSGL-1 (SELPLG) and LRIG125,42. Co-expression analysis revealed cohort-specific patterns: VSIR and SELPLG were predominantly co-localized in monocytes in the Galen cohort, whereas both SELPLG and LRIG1 co-expressed with VSIR in T cell subsets in the FIMM cohort, particularly CD8 + T effector memory and regulatory T cells (Fig. S3B–D). Cell-cell communication analysis further demonstrated that VISTA+ myeloid cells exhibited high interaction scores with PSGL-1 + CD8+ Tcm and Tregs, and with LRIG1 + CD4+ Tn and Tregs (Fig. S3E, F). Collectively, these data support a model wherein VISTA on myeloid cells engages its ligands on T cells to mediate immunosuppression, with Tregs serving as a key target of VISTA-dependent immune evasion in AML.

Gene set enrichment analysis links VSIR to tumor–immune signaling dynamics

We next explored the biological programs associated with differential VSIR expression using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of Hemap transcriptomes, comparing the top versus bottom 30% of tumors ranked by VSIR levels. Elevated VSIR expression was strongly associated with immune-regulatory pathways, including TNFα–NFκB signaling, interferon-γ response, IL6–JAK–STAT3 signaling, allograft rejection, and the p53 pathway (Fig. 5A, Table S5). These enrichments indicate that VSIR-high tumors engage proinflammatory and antigen-rejection circuits within the tumor microenvironment (TME). By contrast, proliferative and metabolic programs such as MYC and E2F targets, as well as the G2M checkpoint, were negatively associated with VSIR expression, suggesting a trade-off between immunoregulatory activation and proliferative drive. Single-cell RNA-seq data from AML (Galen cohort) corroborated these observations: VSIR-high cells exhibited enrichment of immune-related hallmarks—including complement, interferon signaling, and apoptosis—while displaying reduced activity of canonical oncogenic circuits such as KRAS, WNT/β-catenin, and MYC target pathways (Fig. 5B). Together, these convergent findings across bulk and single-cell platforms highlight VSIR as a context-dependent immunomodulator that amplifies immune signaling programs while constraining oncogenic proliferation, pointing to potential tumor-suppressive functions in specific hematologic settings.

VSIR predicts response to cancer immunotherapy

To evaluate the clinical relevance of VSIR, we next assessed its predictive value for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) responses. Using the TIDE algorithm across seven hematologic malignancies, VSIR expression was consistently higher in predicted responders than in non-responders. The association was particularly striking in myeloid malignancies, including AML (p < 0.001 across three independent cohorts), MDS (p = 5.6 × 10⁻⁵), and MM (p = 0.0002), as well as in lymphoid cancers such as ALL (p = 0.001), CLL (p = 0.003), and FL (p = 0.002) (Fig. 6A and Fig. S4A). In contrast, DLBCL showed no significant association (p = 0.85).

To validate whether these computational predictions translate into clinical benefit, we examined real-world cohorts receiving ICB therapy. In melanoma, confirming the TIDE-based predictions, patients with high VSIR expression demonstrated higher response rates compared to those with low expression in both anti-PD-1 monotherapy or anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4 combination therapy (61% vs. 49%) and anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy (42% vs. 25%) (Fig. 6B). Similar predictive capacity was observed in additional melanoma cohorts and in pancreatic cancer patients treated with combination ICB, where high VSIR consistently correlated with improved clinical responses (Fig. S4B, C).

Critically, these predictive associations translated into survival benefit. In melanoma, elevated VSIR expression was associated with prolonged overall survival (OS) (p = 0.014 and p = 0.012) and progression-free survival (PFS) (p < 0.001 and p = 0.026) across both combination and monotherapy settings (Fig. 6B). Additional cohorts confirmed these associations, including advanced melanoma treated with anti-CTLA-4 (p < 0.001 for OS), metastatic melanoma receiving anti-PD-1 therapy (p = 0.008 for OS), and pancreatic cancer patients undergoing combination ICB (p = 0.030 for OS) (Fig. S4B, C). Collectively, these findings establish VSIR as a robust predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response across hematologic and solid malignancies, demonstrating that computationally predicted therapeutic responses indeed translate into improved overall survival outcomes.

Additional value of VSIR expression in refining AML risk stratification

The strong prognostic effect of VSIR prompted us to test whether it could enhance established risk stratification systems in AML. We first focused on cytogenetically normal AML (CN-AML), a clinically heterogeneous subgroup. In the GSE10358 cohort, VSIR expression effectively dichotomized CN-AML patients into groups with significantly different overall and event-free survival (OS: p = 0.0002; EFS: p = 0.008) (Fig. 7A). These results were validated in an independent cohort (GSE6891), where VSIR expression again stratified CN-AML patients (OS: p = 0.018; Fig. S5A). Notably, in intermediate-risk AML within GSE6891, VSIR expression also provided significant discrimination for both OS and EFS (OS: p = 0.003; EFS: p = 0.006; Fig. S5B).

We next evaluated whether VSIR expression could refine two widely used gene expression–based prognostic models, LI24 and LSC17. In GSE10358, VSIR further stratified outcomes within both high- and low-risk groups defined by these models. For LI24, VSIR expression discriminated OS in high-risk (p = 0.005) and low-risk patients (p = 0.002); for LSC17, it provided even stronger separation (high-risk: p = 0.0009; low-risk: p = 0.0002) (Fig. 7B). Together, these retrospective analyses suggest VSIR as a potential adjunct to existing prognostic frameworks that may enhance risk assessment in both clinically and molecularly defined AML subgroups.

To determine whether VSIR represents an independent prognostic factor, we performed multivariate Cox regression analysis in two independent cohorts adjusting for established clinical and molecular risk parameters. In both GSE10358 and GSE6891 datasets, univariate analysis identified variables with p < 0.2 for inclusion in multivariate models (Table S6). After adjusting for age, genetic mutations (NPM1, FLT3, CEBPA), and cytogenetic risk stratification, high VSIR expression remained an independent adverse prognostic factor in GSE10358 (HR = 2.472, 95% CI 1.645–3.716, p = 1.34 × 10⁻⁵) and GSE6891 (HR = 2.048, 95% CI 1.026–4.089, p = 0.042) (Fig. 7C, D).

VSIR defines high-risk AML and is directly activated by KMT2A fusions

Building on our immunogenomic analyses, we next investigated the clinical and mechanistic basis of VSIR dysregulation in AML. In the TCGA cohort, patients with high VSIR expression displayed distinct clinical and molecular features compared with VSIR-low cases. Clinically, the VSIR-high group had elevated leukocyte counts (p = 0.078) and was enriched in monocytic AML subtypes (FAB M4/M5; p = 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁰), while being underrepresented in cytogenetically defined low-risk groups (p = 0.003) (Fig. 8A). At the molecular level, VSIR-high patients were strongly associated with KMT2A rearrangements (p = 0.007, Fig. 8A) and also showed increased frequencies of NPM1 and DNMT3A mutations, but reduced STAG2 alterations (Fig. 8B). Across three independent datasets (BeatAML, TCGA, GSE6891), VSIR expression was consistently elevated in KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated AML relative to wild-type counterparts (Fig. 8C). To validate our transcript-level findings, we analyzed VSIR protein abundance using quantitative TMT-based proteomics data from 44 TCGA AML samples32. Consistent with mRNA expression patterns, VSIR protein levels recapitulated key clinical associations: positive correlation with WBC counts, elevated expression in monocytic subtypes (FAB M4/M5), and underrepresentation in favorable-risk groups (Fig. S6A-D). Importantly, VSIR protein expression was significantly higher in NPM1-mutated AML compared to NPM1-wildtype cases (p = 0.013) and showed elevated levels in KMT2A-rearranged leukemia (Fig. S6E-G).

To investigate the mechanistic basis for VSIR overexpression in KMT2A-rearranged AML, we analyzed ChIP-seq data from MLL-AF9 AML samples. This analysis revealed strong KMT2A binding at the VSIR promoter, accompanied by marked enrichment of active histone modifications (H3K79me2, H3K4me3, H3K27ac) characteristic of KMT2A fusion targets (Fig. 8D). Pharmacologic inhibition further confirmed Menin dependency: treatment of MA9 mouse cells with MI-503 markedly reduced Menin/KMT2A occupancy and diminished activating histone modifications at Vsir (Fig. 8E). Similar effects were observed in MV4-11 cells with wild-type MEN1, but not in MEN1-M327I mutant cells, validating the specificity of Menin-targeted therapy (Fig. 8F).

Parallel analyses showed that NPM1 mutations also drive VSIR overexpression through Menin-dependent mechanisms. Acute depletion of NPM1c using a degron system led to rapid loss of NPM1c and Menin occupancy at the VSIR locus (Fig. 8G). Consistently, treatment of NPM1-mutated OCI-AML3 cells with the Menin inhibitor MI-503 significantly reduced VSIR expression, while DOT1L inhibition showed a weaker effect (Fig. S7A-B). Independent validation using another Menin inhibitor, VTP-50469, confirmed robust VSIR downregulation in OCI-AML3 cells (p < 0.0001, Fig. S7C), further establishing the Menin dependency of VSIR expression in NPM1-mutated AML. These results indicate that NPM1c promotes VSIR activation by stabilizing Menin-associated chromatin complexes and that VSIR expression remains pharmacologically targetable in this context. Collectively, these findings establish VSIR as a clinically relevant high-risk marker in AML. Its aberrant expression in KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated subtypes is maintained through Menin-dependent chromatin remodeling, highlighting VSIR as both a molecular signature of aggressive disease and a potential therapeutic vulnerability.

Discussion

VISTA is predominantly expressed in myeloid lineages and functions as a critical immune checkpoint in solid tumors13,15, yet its regulation in hematological malignancies remained poorly defined25. Dufva et al. reported preferential VSIR expression in myeloid malignancies7, and Wang et al. demonstrated VISTA-mediated T-cell suppression in AML16, but molecular drivers were unclear. Our comprehensive analyses reveal marked VSIR upregulation in hematological compared with solid malignancies, with highest levels in myeloid lineages mirroring its physiological expression in neutrophils and monocytes, suggesting lineage-specific regulatory mechanisms.

Epigenetic mechanisms governing VSIR expression across cancers have been insufficiently characterized25. Our systematic methylation profiling across multiple datasets-including 168 clinical samples-establishes progressive promoter hypomethylation as a primary driver. This hypomethylation intensified stepwise from healthy controls through MDS to AML, with strong inverse correlation with expression (R = -0.33, p = 1.3e-05). These findings extend epigenetic checkpoint regulation from solid tumors to hematologic malignancies and provide mechanistic insight into lineage-specific VSIR dysregulation.

Building on these epigenetic observations, we further sought to uncover the chromatin-level mechanisms underlying VSIR dysregulation in KMT2A-rearranged AML. ChIP-seq revealed strong enrichment of KMT2A fusion proteins at VSIR promoter regions, accompanied by activating H3K4me3 histone marks, suggesting direct transcriptional regulation by oncogenic chromatin complexes. Importantly, treatment with Menin inhibitors led to substantial reductions in both KMT2A and Menin occupancy at VSIR loci, indicating that VSIR expression is mechanistically dependent on functional Menin–KMT2A complexes. Moreover, targeted degradation of NPM1c using degron systems resulted in coordinated loss of binding at VSIR loci alongside decreased Menin occupancy, establishing a NPM1–Menin–VSIR regulatory axis that extends beyond canonical KMT2A targets such as HOXA9 and MEIS143,44. This observation parallels our prior finding that LILRB4, another immune checkpoint enriched in monocytic AML, is similarly regulated by KMT2A fusion proteins through direct promoter binding and activating histone modifications29. Together, these convergent findings suggest that immune checkpoint genes represent a previously underrecognized class of KMT2A fusion targets beyond classical developmental transcription factors. Collectively, these mechanistic insights elucidate the epigenetic control of VSIR across hematological subtypes, addressing critical gaps previously highlighted in the field25.

Next, we examined whether these mechanistic insights translate into functional immune consequences. Gene set enrichment analysis indicated that VSIR-high tumors were coordinately enriched in immune regulatory programs, including TNFα–NFκB signaling, IFN-γ response pathways, and IL6–JAK–STAT3 cascades. Conversely, VSIR expression negatively correlated with proliferative–metabolic circuits, including MYC and E2F targets, reflecting an immunoregulatory–proliferative trade-off characteristic of immune checkpoint activation. Notably, computational modeling using TIDE algorithms suggested that predicted responders to immune checkpoint blockade consistently exhibited higher VSIR expression relative to non-responders, implying that VSIR may functionally reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment in hematological malignancies. Together, these patterns are consistent with VISTA’s established role in enhancing myeloid-derived suppressor cell function and promoting immune evasion, while highlighting pathway preferences distinct from solid tumors13,15,16. VSIR upregulation in AML likely converges with other myeloid-driven immunosuppressive mechanisms, particularly arginase-1 activity, which restricts T-cell proliferation through L-arginine depletion45. Functional studies indicate that VISTA-high myeloid populations exhibit enhanced suppressive programs that may overlap with arginase-dependent metabolic restriction of T-cell function16, suggesting potential for dual-pathway targeting strategies. However, a significant limitation is the reliance on solid tumor immunotherapy cohorts for validation, as clinical experience with immune checkpoint blockade in hematologic malignancies remains limited. These computational predictions require prospective validation in blood cancer patients receiving VISTA-targeted or combination immunotherapies.

Having established mechanistic and immunogenomic context, we evaluated VSIR’s prognostic significance across multiple independent AML cohorts, with particular focus on cytogenetically normal cases. VSIR expression was strongly associated with inferior overall survival (p = 0.0002) and event-free survival (p = 0.008). Moreover, in retrospective analyses, VSIR expression stratified outcomes within established prognostic models, including LI24 and LSC17 scoring systems, indicating potential for enhancing risk stratification. The association between high VSIR expression and reduced proportions of low cytogenetic risk cases further suggests that VSIR may identify high-risk patients within traditionally intermediate-risk categories, corroborating prior observations linking VSIR expression to DNMT3A and NPM1 mutations and poor prognosis19–21. Collectively, these retrospective findings suggest VSIR’s potential utility for enhancing AML risk stratification, pending prospective validation.

A notable feature emerging from our analyses is the lineage-dependent nature of VSIR regulation and function. Promoter hypomethylation was most pronounced in myeloid malignancies, immune microenvironment associations differed between myeloid and lymphoid contexts, and prognostic impact showed opposing directions in AML (adverse) versus DLBCL/MM (favorable), collectively suggesting fundamentally distinct regulatory and functional mechanisms across hematologic lineages. These observations underscore the importance of disease-specific therapeutic strategies in checkpoint modulation.

Among these lineage-specific mechanisms, the elucidated Menin-VSIR regulatory axis in KMT2A-rearranged AML supports the hypothesis that dual-targeting strategies combining epigenetic modulation with immune checkpoint blockade warrant further investigation. KMT2A-rearranged AML represents a significant unmet clinical need, with five-year overall survival below 25% and limited response to conventional chemotherapy46,47. FDA approval of revumenib for KMT2A-rearranged acute leukemia48, coupled with the advancing clinical pipeline of VISTA checkpoint inhibitors49–51, presents a rational basis to explore combination therapy. Despite FDA approval, Menin inhibitor monotherapy faces significant challenges. The AUGMENT-101 study demonstrated a CR/CRh rate of only 23%, with 37% of patients showing no response48. More critically, acquired resistance mediated by MEN1 mutations emerged rapidly in 38.7% of treated patients, typically within two treatment cycles46,52. These dual challenges—limited initial response rates and rapid resistance development—underscore the urgent need for combinatorial approaches integrating immune checkpoint modulation to overcome both primary and acquired therapeutic resistance.

VSIR distinguishes itself from PD-1/CTLA-4 pathways through three unique mechanisms25,42,49,53: (1) pH-dependent ligand binding optimized for the acidic tumor microenvironment (pH ~ 6.0), (2) direct suppression of tumor-specific CD8 + T cell responses via LRIG1 engagement independent of conventional checkpoint signaling, and (3) predominant expression on myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages that orchestrate immunosuppression in the hematological bone marrow niche. This mechanistic distinctiveness, particularly the targeting of myeloid compartments enriched in KMT2A-rearranged AML, suggests that VISTA blockade may represent a rational complement to Menin inhibition for overcoming both primary and acquired therapeutic resistance, although this hypothesis requires prospective experimental validation. An important consideration is VSIR’s physiological role in maintaining immune quiescence in normal myeloid cells, as experimental models demonstrate that VISTA deficiency can exacerbate inflammatory responses and alter T-cell differentiation10,11. Therapeutic strategies must therefore balance anti-tumor immunity with potential effects on normal hematopoiesis and immune homeostasis, necessitating careful dose optimization and monitoring of bone marrow function in clinical development. Moving forward, clinical development should prioritize biomarker-driven patient selection based on VSIR expression profiles, alongside rigorous safety evaluation of combination regimens integrating both epigenetic and immune-modulatory strategies.

Finally, several methodological considerations warrant discussion. Our study relies predominantly on retrospective datasets with VSIR expression cut-offs determined by optimal algorithms (Maxstat), which require prospective validation with predefined thresholds before clinical implementation. Computational predictions of immunotherapy response require prospective clinical validation in hematological malignancy cohorts, whereas ChIP-seq analyses were primarily conducted in established cell lines rather than primary patient samples, potentially limiting generalizability. Furthermore, while our protein-level validation confirms transcript-protein concordance, spatially resolved analyses-such as imaging mass cytometry or spatial transcriptomics-would provide deeper insights into VSIR expression patterns within the bone marrow microenvironment and cellular interactions at the tissue level. Additionally, the cross-sectional study design precludes evaluation of dynamic VSIR expression during treatment or therapeutic intervention. For Menin-VSIR dual-targeting, prospective studies must validate whether Menin inhibition enhances anti-tumor immunity via VSIR before clinical application. These limitations underscore the need for focused experimental validation, mechanistic characterization of VSIR-mediated immune suppression, and preclinical combination therapy studies to facilitate clinical translation. Collectively, such efforts will be critical to fully delineate VSIR’s role in KMT2A-rearranged AML and optimize therapeutic targeting strategies.
