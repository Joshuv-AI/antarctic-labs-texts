Diagnostic Utility of High-resolution Esophageal Manometry and Its Correlation with Symptoms
Authors: Balaji Musunuri, Vikas Pemmada, Ganesh Bhat, Athish Shetty, Shiran Shetty, Ganesh C. Pai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Primary esophageal motility disorders present with a spectrum of symptoms where manometry plays an important role. We designed this study to evaluate the utility of esophageal manometry among various symptoms.Materials and Methods:This is a single-center observational study conducted over 5 years in a tertiary referral center. A total of 564 patients who underwent high-resolution esophageal manometry (HREM) using a 16-channel water perfusion system were included in the study. Their clinical profile and manometric findings, as per the Chicago classification version 4.0, were recorded, and the diagnostic utility of HREM with respect to symptoms was studied.Results:Motility disorders were identified in 48.8% of patients, the most common being Achalasia cardia (32.4%). Dysphagia (55.5%) was the most common indication of manometry, followed by gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (34.9%), chest pain (6.2%), and symptoms such as belching, globus, and hiccoughs (3.4%). Among those who were re-classified from Chicago classification v3.0 to v4.0, 21.2% had a newer diagnosis. Among the patients with dysphagia, HREM revealed the highest yield of detecting an abnormal esophageal motility test (70.6%); meanwhile, this yield was much lower in those with GERD (21.8%), chest pain (22.8%), and other symptoms (15.7%). The most common finding among those with dysphagia was Achalasia cardia (55.9%), while the study was normal among those with GERD (78.1%), chest pain (77.1%), and other symptoms (84.2%). The sensitivity and specificity of dysphagia for major motility disorders were 65% and 91%, respectively, with a positive predictive value of 90%.Conclusion:HREM has high accuracy and a good diagnostic yield among patients with dysphagia, with the most common finding being Achalasia cardia.

INTRODUCTION

Primary esophageal motility disorders present with a spectrum of esophageal symptoms and consist of various disorders that involve the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) and esophageal body. These disorders are evaluated using various techniques such as barium swallow, esophageal manometry with or without impedance, and Endo flip. High-resolution esophageal manometry (HREM) is an important tool of choice for the evaluation of motility disorders of the esophagus and is indicated primarily for assessing obstructive symptoms such as dysphagia and regurgitation. HREM also plays a role in evaluating typical and atypical gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and behavioral symptoms such as belching and rumination.[1]

Esophageal motility disorders were diagnosed and classified based on findings from esophageal manometry according to Chicago classification version 4.0.[2] Motility disorders of esophagus are classified into disorders affecting LES (such as Achalasia and esophagogastric junction [EGJ] outflow obstruction), and peristaltic disorders (including diffuse esophageal spasm, hypercontractile esophagus, absent contractility, and ineffective esophageal motility [IEM]). The current version provides a more rigorous protocol for performing manometry, incorporating both supine and upright test positions as well as provocative testing, and has more stringent diagnostic criteria for identifying motility disorders. If the diagnosis is inconclusive, it recommends additional supportive tests, including barium swallow, for further characterizing the abnormalities.[2]

The most common esophageal motility disorders varied across different studies in India, which include Achalasia[34] and ineffective motility disorder.[5] In addition, esophageal motility disorders present with various symptoms.[6] Few studies have evaluated the diagnostic impact of HREM among patients with various symptoms. Achalasia was the common motility disorder among patients with dysphagia,[347] while among patients with GERD, esophageal manometric abnormalities include low LES pressures and/or IEM.[89] On this background, we designed the present study to evaluate the symptom profile and manometric profile of the patients undergoing esophageal manometry and its correlation with various esophageal symptoms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This is a retrospective observational study conducted at a tertiary care center in India, where consecutive patients presenting with esophageal symptoms and undergoing HREM in the motility clinic of the department of gastroenterology were included. Those patients having esophageal symptoms of dysphagia, chest pain, reflux, regurgitation, belching, globus, or hiccoughs for at least 1 month duration were included in the study. Patients with age <18 years, uncontrolled diabetes, inability to pass catheter across the LES leading to incomplete evaluation, or those who had a history of prior foregut surgery were excluded from the study. Patients whose clinical details were not available and those taking drugs, including prokinetics and opioids, were also excluded. The clinical records of all patients who underwent HREM at our center between March 2017 and December 2023 were reviewed. Data regarding their demographic profile, clinical symptoms, and esophageal manometry findings were collected using a predesigned questionnaire. Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC) clearance was obtained for the study (IEC: 324/2022).

Esophageal manometry

Esophageal manometry was performed by a trained physician after at least 6 h of fasting using a 16-channel high-resolution water-perfusion manometry system (RMH, Australia). The catheter was passed transnasally into the esophagus in the supine position and then adjusted to identify the LES position and fixed. The procedure was carried out according to the standard protocol by a trained physician.[2] It consisted of a landmark phase for the assessment of basal LES pressure, followed by 10 5 ml water swallows done 30 s apart. Swallows were done in both the supine and upright positions. Provocative tests include rapid multiple swallows, where five 2 ml swallows were administered rapidly, and rapid drink challenge, where 200 ml water was ingested as fast as possible through a straw. The analysis was carried out using the Trace 1.3.3 software program (Hebbard, Australia). For each swallow, distal contractile integral, integral relaxation pressure, peristaltic breaks, and distal latency were calculated. The final esophageal motility diagnosis was made according to Chicago classification version 4.0, and abnormal findings were classified into disorders of EGJ outflow obstruction and peristaltic disorders. Manometric data of patients whose data were analyzed using Chicago classification version 3.0[10] were re-classified and interpreted based on the recent version.[2]

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in the present study were carried out using SPSS for Windows (Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Categorical variables were presented as percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) or as median (interquartile range). Comparisons between proportions were performed using the Chi-square test and continuous variables using the Student’s t-test. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and positive and negative predictive values of various esophageal symptoms were calculated in identifying motility disorders. For all tests, P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 564 patients were included in the analysis, of which 59.9% were male [Table 1]. The mean age was 44.16 ± 14.88 years. The most common indication for esophageal manometry was for the predominant symptom of dysphagia among 55.5% (n = 313), followed by GERD in 34.9% (n = 197) of the patients. Noncardiac chest pain was the predominant symptom among 6.2% (n = 35), and other symptoms, including belching, hiccoughs, and globus, were noted among the rest 3.4% (n = 19) patients [Table 1]. One hundred and seventy-nine patients whose diagnosis was based on Chicago version 3.0 were re-classified by analyzing the manometry based on their clinical profile as per version 4.0. Among these, 38 patients (21.2%) had a change in their manometric diagnosis. Five patients with fragmented peristalsis, 16 patients with IEM, and 17 patients with esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction (EGJOO) were re-diagnosed as normal.

Motility disorders were identified in 48.8% (n = 275) of all patients undergoing manometry. Achalasia cardia was the most common abnormality noted among 32.4% (n = 183) of patients. Type II (17.4%, n = 98) was the most common type of achalasia cardia, followed by type I (8.4%, n = 47) [Table 1].

Motility disorders among various esophageal symptoms

The profile of patients according to various symptoms is depicted in Table 2 and Figure 1. Manometric abnormalities were detected in 70.6% (n = 221) of patients presenting with dysphagia, while they were noted less frequently in groups presenting with GERD (21.6%, n = 43), chest pain (22.8%, n = 8), and other symptoms (15.7%, n = 3). Among patients presenting with dysphagia, the most common abnormality was Achalasia Cardia, seen in 55.9% (n = 175), while IEM was the most common abnormality among patients with GERD (13.7%, n = 27) [Figure 1]. In the chest pain group, IEM (8.5%, n = 3) and DES (8.5%, n = 3) were the most common abnormalities. Among patients with other symptoms, the only manometric abnormality observed was IEM, present in 15.7% (n = 3) of patients.

DISCUSSION

HREM is a part of the armamentarium in the evaluation of esophageal motility and has become a diagnostic tool in patients with esophageal symptoms.[1] The current study evaluates the manometric profile of a large cohort of patients presenting with esophageal symptoms. The most common indication was dysphagia (55.5%), followed by GERD (34.9%), while chest pain (6.2%) and other symptoms, including belching, globus, and hiccoughs (3.4%), constituted the rest.

Our study shows that achalasia was the most common motility disorder overall. This is consistent with other studies done in Northern and Central India[347] and worldwide.[11] However, a recent study from Punjab showed that IEM is the most common abnormality.[5] These could be due to inter-regional differences and referral patterns of various centers in India. Type II was most commonachalasia followed by I, similar to other studies.[6]

Among the patients with dysphagia, HREM revealed the highest yield of detecting an abnormal esophageal motility test (70.6%); meanwhile, this yield was much lower in patients with reflux disease (21.8%), chest pain (22.8%), and others (15.7%) [Table 2]. A study by McClelland et al. reported similar manometric abnormalities among 68.42%, 54.83%, and 38.70% of patients with dysphagia, reflux, and chest pain, respectively.[12] Achalasia was the most common abnormality (55.9%) noted among dysphagia patients, while IEM was noted to be the most common abnormality among reflux patients (13.7%). Our findings are consistent with those of other studies.[1314]

Among the group with dysphagia, peristaltic disorders were noted among 13.1%, while they were 16.7%, 20%, and 15.7%, respectively, among the groups with GERD, chest pain, and the rest. Minor peristaltic disorders, including IEM, were noted even among healthy asymptomatic individuals.[1516] In a study conducted among patients with normal or minor esophageal disorders, a long-term follow-up showed symptomatic improvement without needing any intervention.[1718] As the most common manometric abnormality was IEM in nondysphagia group, the clinical significance of these remains unclear.

Due to the clinical impact of major esophageal motility disorders, we evaluated the diagnostic impact of esophageal symptoms in predicting major findings. Dysphagia had sensitivity and specificity of 65% and 91% in identifying manometric abnormalities using HREM. However, the accuracy of HREM, among other symptoms, was not significant. Dysphagia has a high positive predictive value of 90%. Our findings are in concurrence with other studies that showed a high impact of HREM among patients with dysphagia.[1419]

We noted re-classification to a new diagnosis among 21.2% of the patients using CC 4.0, where five patients with fragmented peristalsis, 16 with IEM, and 17 with EGJOO were re-diagnosed with normal. Recent studies have shown a similar re-classification of 17%–22%, which is similar to our study.[2021] This is due to the latest CC v4.0 offering a more rigorous and expansive manometry protocol that utilizes analysis in supine and upright positions as well as provocative testing, and includes more stringent diagnostic criteria for a few motility disorders like IEM and supportive testing with barium esophagogram and/or endoluminal functional lumen imaging probe (EndoFLIP), especially those with EGJOO or those with inconclusive diagnosis.[2]

A normal study was observed among 29.4% of the patients with dysphagia. These findings of a normal study in patients with dysphagia have varied among various studies from 19.4% to 29%.[519] These groups of patients with dysphagia and normal motility tests could belong to the group of functional esophageal disorders. The Rome IV classification defines functional dysphagia as dysphagia after excluding structural, mucosal, or motor disorders.[22] These disorders of gut–brain interaction are a heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by gastrointestinal symptoms related to multifactorial association with motility disturbance, visceral hypersensitivity, altered mucosal and immune function, altered gut microbiota, and altered central nervous system processing.[22] The recent emergence of EndoFLIP and impedance testing helps bridge some limitations, as it detects major motility disorders in certain ambiguous cases.[23] Recent studies have shown that EndoFLIP helps in the evaluation of the subset of dysphagia patients who have characteristic symptoms of achalasia but have normal LES relaxation on HREM, further aiding in treatment.[24] The diagnostic yield of manometry was only 15.7% among patients presenting with predominant symptoms of belching, globus, and hiccoughs. The limitation of present manometry systems in characterizing the pharyngeal/upper esophageal sphincter disorders could be the reason for this low yield, as those with globus might have abnormalities of the upper esophageal sphincter.[25]

Major strengths of the study include its large sample size. However, limitations of the study include its retrospective and single-center study, which cannot be generalized to other centers as there may be inter-regional variations in the pattern of presentation and referral. Another limitation is that our study was done using a 16-channel water perfusion system. Furthermore, the treatment outcomes have not been included in the present study. Despite the limitations, it draws an important conclusions, showing that HREM has a higher diagnostic yield among patients with dysphagia compared to those without.
