Background

Congenital anomalies are an important cause of infant morbidity, mortality, and long-term disability worldwide, with substantial impact on health systems and families, particularly in low- and middle-income settings [1, 2]. Although global age-standardized incidence rates of congenital birth defects have remained relatively stable, the absolute number of affected births remains high, and temporal patterns vary according to anomaly group, geographic setting, and surveillance quality [1, 2].

The interpretation of congenital anomaly trends is complex because observed prevalence may reflect not only true changes in occurrence, but also improvements in prenatal diagnosis, neonatal screening, and case ascertainment in birth registries. Population-based studies have documented substantial increases in prenatal detection over time, especially after the implementation of more detailed ultrasound protocols and advances in fetal imaging [3, 4]. In Europe, registry-based analyses have also shown that temporal trends differ considerably across anatomical subgroups, reinforcing the importance of system-specific analyses rather than relying only on overall prevalence estimates [5].

Maternal and perinatal factors also play a central role in the occurrence of congenital anomalies. Advanced maternal age, and multiple gestation have all been associated with higher risk, while maternal metabolic conditions such as diabetes and obesity are recognized risk factors for several structural anomalies, particularly congenital heart defects [6–10]. These factors may have changed over time in many populations and could contribute to shifts in congenital anomaly prevalence.

In Brazil, studies based on national data have reported prevalence estimates lower than those observed in many high-income countries, suggesting possible underreporting or differences in diagnostic and surveillance capacity [11]. At the same time, the country has experienced important epidemiological events with direct implications for congenital anomaly patterns. The Zika virus epidemic, particularly during 2015–2016, was strongly associated with an increase in congenital neurological anomalies, especially microcephaly and other manifestations of congenital Zika syndrome [12–15]. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining robust surveillance systems capable of detecting both long-term trends and unusual changes related to emerging public health threats.

Despite the relevance of congenital anomalies as a public health problem, temporal analyses in large Brazilian populations remain limited, particularly those examining both overall prevalence and specific anomaly groups. Understanding these patterns is essential for improving surveillance, identifying priority conditions, and informing maternal and child health policies.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate temporal trends and maternal and perinatal factors associated with congenital anomalies among live births in São Paulo, Brazil, between 2015 and 2023, using population-based registry data.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a population-based retrospective study of live births in the state of São Paulo, Brazil, from 2015 to 2023. The study was based on secondary data obtained from the Brazilian Live Birth Information System (Sistema de Informações sobre Nascidos Vivos, SINASC), which is derived from the Live Birth Certificate (Declaração de Nascido Vivo, DNV). São Paulo is the most populous state in Brazil and has a large and diverse population, making it an important setting for monitoring temporal patterns of congenital anomalies.

The study period was defined from 2015 and 2023 because, at the time of data extraction, records for 2024 were still incomplete and therefore not suitable for stable population-based estimates. The inclusion of incomplete data could have led to underestimation of prevalence and distortion of temporal comparisons.

Data source and study population

All live births registered in SINASC for mothers residing in São Paulo between 2015 and 2023 were eligible for inclusion. The study population consisted of all live births with available information on congenital anomaly status. Births with missing information on the main outcome were excluded from analyses requiring classification of congenital anomalies.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [16] and the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) guidelines [17]. The description of data collection, study population definition, and reporting of routinely collected health data followed the recommendations of international congenital anomaly surveillance systems and methodological guidance documents [18, 19].

Outcome definition

The main outcome was the presence of congenital anomalies among live births, as recorded in SINASC. For descriptive and multivariable analyses, the outcome was treated as a binary variable (yes/no). For temporal analyses by anomaly group, congenital anomalies were classified according to ICD-10 groups recorded in the birth registry. The groups analyzed were: chromosomal abnormalities, circulatory system, cleft lip and cleft palate, digestive system, eye, ear, face and neck, genital organs, musculoskeletal system, nervous system, respiratory system, urinary system, and others.

Study variables

Maternal, pregnancy, and neonatal variables were selected based on data availability in SINASC and their epidemiological relevance. The descriptive analysis included prenatal care (yes/no), adolescent pregnancy, maternal age group, maternal education, marital status, maternal race/skin color, sex of the newborn, type of pregnancy, and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min.

For the adjusted logistic regression, the following variables were included: maternal education, maternal race/ethnicity, sex of the newborn, maternal age, paternal age, type of pregnancy, prenatal care, and previous pregnancies. Maternal and paternal ages were analyzed as continuous variables in the multivariable model, while the remaining variables were entered as categorical variables.

For temporal comparisons by epidemiological period, four periods were defined a priori based on major public health contexts: Zika period (2015–2017), baseline period (2018–2019), COVID-19 period (2020–2022), and post-COVID period (2023).

Statistical analysis

Absolute and relative frequencies were calculated for maternal, pregnancy, and neonatal characteristics according to the presence of congenital anomalies. Differences between groups were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Newborn sex was categorized as male, female, or indeterminate/unknown according to SINASC records. The indeterminate/unknown category represents live births for which sex could not be assigned or was not recorded at the time of notification. Because this category may include newborns with ambiguous genitalia, disorders/differences of sex development (DSD), chromosomal anomalies, or other congenital conditions affecting sex assignment, it was treated as a distinct category rather than as missing data.

Annual prevalence rates of congenital anomalies were calculated as the number of cases divided by the total number of live births in each year, expressed per 10,000 live births. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated using exact Poisson confidence intervals for the number of cases.

To compare rates across predefined epidemiological periods, incidence rate ratios (IRR) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using count regression models with the logarithm of the number of live births included as an offset. The baseline period (2018–2019) was used as the reference category. Poisson regression models were initially fitted; when overdispersion was detected, negative binomial regression was adopted as the main inferential model.

In addition, temporal trends were evaluated using a segmented regression (joinpoint-like) approach. Annual prevalence rates were modeled on the logarithmic scale, and both linear and segmented log-rate models were fitted for the overall prevalence of congenital anomalies and for the major anomaly groups. Segmented models were allowed to identify a single change point in the time series, and model selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). For each selected model, annual percent changes (APC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated to quantify temporal variation across the study period.

To formally assess annual temporal trends according to ICD-10 anomaly group, separate count regression models were fitted for each anomaly group, using year as the independent variable and the logarithm of live births as an offset. Incidence rate ratios per year (IRR) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Poisson models were initially considered, and negative binomial regression was used when overdispersion was identified. An IRR greater than 1 indicated an increasing annual trend, whereas an IRR lower than 1 indicated a decreasing annual trend.

Factors associated with congenital anomalies were evaluated using adjusted logistic regression models. Odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were estimated. Covariates were pre-specified based on epidemiological relevance, previous literature, and availability in the SINASC database; no automated variable selection procedures were applied. The final model included maternal education, maternal race/ethnicity, newborn sex, maternal age, paternal age, type of pregnancy, prenatal care, and previous pregnancies. For analyses evaluating the association between newborn sex and congenital anomalies, records classified as indeterminate/unknown sex were excluded from the estimation of odds ratios for male versus female sex, given the potential overlap between indeterminate sex registration and congenital conditions directly related to sex development.

This study used secondary, de-identified data from a population-based information system, with no direct contact with participants. Because the analyses were based on routinely collected administrative data, no individual informed consent was required.

All data management, statistical analyses, and graph construction were performed using R statistical software version 4.5.2, with the data.table, dplyr, tidyr, stringr, writexl, MASS, segmented, stats, and ggplot2 packages [20].

Results

Between 2015 and 2023, a total of 4,329,485 live births were analyzed in São Paulo, Brazil, of which 53,217 (1.23%) presented congenital anomalies. Prenatal care information was missing or unknown for 911,112 live births (21.04%). Among records with valid prenatal care information, prenatal care coverage was high in both groups. However, the absence of prenatal care was slightly more frequent among mothers of newborns with congenital anomalies than among those without anomalies (1.14% vs. 0.77%; p = 0.001) (Table 1).

Adolescent pregnancy differed between groups (p = 0.007), although the absolute difference was small. Among live births with congenital anomalies, 11.61% occurred among adolescent mothers, compared with 11.99% among those without anomalies.

Maternal age distribution was significantly associated with congenital anomalies (p < 0.001). Among newborns with congenital anomalies, 65.05% were born to mothers aged 20–34 years, 16.80% to mothers aged 35–39 years, 11.61% to mothers aged 10–19 years, and 6.53% to mothers aged ≥ 40 years. Compared with live births without anomalies, the anomaly group showed a higher proportion of advanced maternal age, particularly among mothers aged 35–39 years (16.80% vs. 14.15%) and ≥ 40 years (6.53% vs. 3.61%).

Maternal education was also significantly associated with congenital anomalies (p < 0.001). Among mothers of newborns with congenital anomalies, 60.83% had completed secondary education, 31.86% had higher education, and 7.06% had primary education. Compared with births without anomalies, higher education was more frequent in the anomaly group (31.86% vs. 28.58%), whereas completed secondary education was less frequent (60.83% vs. 64.08%).

Marital status differed significantly according to congenital anomaly status (p < 0.001). Among newborns with congenital anomalies, 41.16% were born to married mothers, 39.34% to single mothers, and 16.89% to mothers in a stable union. Compared with births without anomalies, stable union was more frequent in the anomaly group (16.89% vs. 15.51%).

Regarding maternal race/skin color, significant differences were observed (p < 0.001). Among births with congenital anomalies, 56.52% occurred among White mothers, 34.68% among Brown mothers, and 7.63% among Black mothers. Compared with births without anomalies, Black mothers were proportionally more represented in the anomaly group (7.63% vs. 6.08%), whereas White mothers were slightly less represented (56.52% vs. 57.57%).

Male newborns were more frequent among those with congenital anomalies compared with those without anomalies (55.97% vs. 51.12%; p < 0.001). The category ambiguous/indeterminate/unknown sex represented 1.06% of live births with congenital anomalies and 0.00% of those without anomalies. Singleton pregnancies predominated in both groups, although multiple pregnancies were proportionally more frequent among births with anomalies, including twin pregnancies (3.60% vs. 2.45%) and triplet or higher-order pregnancies (0.13% vs. 0.06%) (p < 0.001).

Neonatal vitality differed markedly according to congenital anomaly status. At 1 min, Apgar scores of 8–10 were less frequent among newborns with anomalies than among those without anomalies (55.42% vs. 68.79%), whereas scores of 3–7 (17.53% vs. 7.80%) and 0–2 (4.40% vs. 0.55%) were more common in the anomaly group (p < 0.001). Similarly, at 5 min, Apgar scores of 8–10 were less frequent among newborns with anomalies (86.32% vs. 96.78%), while scores of 3–7 (7.65% vs. 1.39%) and 0–2 (2.53% vs. 0.18%) were more frequent (p < 0.001). Unknown or missing Apgar information was also more frequent among newborns with congenital anomalies at 5 min (3.50% vs. 1.65%).

A particularly notable pattern was observed for circulatory system anomalies, which showed a progressive increase over time, with a more marked rise during the later years of the series (Figs. 1 and 3; Supplementary Table S1). After relatively high levels already observed between 2016 and 2018, prevalence remained above 21 per 10,000 live births during 2019–2021 and continued to increase thereafter, reaching 26.07 in 2022 and peaking at 31.79 in 2023. This sustained increase contrasts with the more stable patterns observed for most other anomaly groups and represents the most pronounced upward trend among the major congenital anomaly categories.

Among the remaining major groups, nervous system anomalies showed an early peak in 2016 (16.41 per 10,000 live births), followed by a decline and subsequent stabilization between 2020 and 2023, when rates ranged from 10.21 to 11.32 per 10,000 live births (Figs. 1 and 2; Supplementary Table S1). Eye, ear, face and neck anomalies remained relatively stable over the series, varying from 8.38 in 2020 to 11.07 in 2023. Genital organ anomalies also showed limited fluctuation, with rates generally around 8–10 per 10,000 live births across the study period. Digestive system anomalies increased over time, particularly in the final year, rising from 10.19 in 2022 to 16.51 in 2023.

The faceted visualization further highlights the temporal patterns of less frequent anomaly groups (Fig. 2). Cleft lip and cleft palate remained relatively stable, fluctuating between 5.55 and 7.21 per 10,000 live births. Chromosomal abnormalities varied within a narrow range, from 4.41 to 5.91 per 10,000 live births. Other anomalies showed a slight overall decline, from 4.80 in 2015 to 3.95 in 2023. Respiratory system anomalies remained the least frequent group throughout the series, ranging from 1.17 to 2.21 per 10,000 live births, while urinary system anomalies showed low but slightly increasing rates in the final year, reaching 3.42 per 10,000 live births in 2023 (Supplementary Table S1).

When the six groups with the highest mean prevalence were plotted together with the total prevalence, the overall pattern became more evident (Fig. 3). Musculoskeletal anomalies remained the dominant group across all years, whereas circulatory system anomalies exhibited the most consistent and pronounced increase over time. The trajectory of the total prevalence curve closely followed the combined influence of these trends, with a marked rise observed in 2023. Taken together, these patterns suggest that the recent increase in the overall prevalence of congenital anomalies was accompanied mainly by increases in circulatory and digestive anomalies, with a smaller contribution from musculoskeletal anomalies.

The highest rate was observed in 2023, reaching 143.92 per 10,000 live births (Table 3).

In Poisson regression, the Zika period showed a slightly lower rate compared with baseline (IRR = 0.966; 95% CI 0.944–0.988; p = 0.003), whereas 2023 showed a higher rate (IRR = 1.178; 95% CI 1.143–1.215; p < 0.001). However, because overdispersion was detected, negative binomial regression was considered the primary model. In this model, neither the Zika period (IRR = 0.967; 95% CI 0.873–1.072; p = 0.523) nor the COVID-19 period (IRR = 1.003; 95% CI 0.905–1.112; p = 0.950) differed significantly from baseline. The year 2023 was associated with a higher rate compared with baseline in the negative binomial model (IRR = 1.179; 95% CI 1.028–1.353; p = 0.0188), corresponding to an approximate 18% increase. However, because this comparison involved a single year of observation and would not remain statistically significant under a conservative Bonferroni correction for the three period-based comparisons, this finding was interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory.

A segmented trend analysis was performed to evaluate annual changes in congenital anomaly prevalence without imposing predefined epidemiological periods (Table 4). Overall congenital anomaly prevalence showed a significant increasing trend throughout the study period, with an annual percent change (APC) of 2.01% (95% CI 0.17–3.89).

Among specific anomaly groups, circulatory system anomalies exhibited a consistent upward trend over the entire series (APC = 5.78%; 95% CI 3.46–8.15). Musculoskeletal anomalies also showed a modest but significant increase over time (APC = 1.27%; 95% CI 0.12–2.44).

Digestive system anomalies were best described by a segmented model with a change point around 2022. From 2015 to 2021, prevalence remained relatively stable, with a modest increase (APC = 5.77%; 95% CI − 1.36 to 13.41), followed by a marked increase between 2022 and 2023 (APC = 59.92%; 95% CI 4.98–143.62).

Nervous system anomalies also demonstrated a segmented pattern. An initial increase was observed between 2015 and 2016 (APC = 26.62%; 95% CI − 5.62 to 69.87), followed by a significant decline from 2017 onward (APC = − 5.25%; 95% CI − 9.30 to − 1.03). Overall, the segmented analyses showed increasing trends for total congenital anomalies, circulatory system anomalies, and musculoskeletal anomalies, with a recent increase in digestive system anomalies. Complete trend analyses for all anomaly groups are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

To formally assess temporal trends in congenital anomaly groups, count regression models with an offset for the number of live births were fitted for each group, and the results are presented in Table 5. Increasing annual trends were observed for digestive system anomalies, circulatory system anomalies, chromosomal abnormalities, and musculoskeletal system anomalies. Digestive system anomalies showed the steepest increase over the study period, with an average annual increase of 9.8% (IRR = 1.098; 95% CI 1.051–1.149; p < 0.001). Circulatory system anomalies increased by 5.8% per year on average (IRR = 1.058; 95% CI 1.038–1.079; p < 0.001). Musculoskeletal system anomalies showed a smaller annual increase of 1.3% (IRR = 1.013; 95% CI 1.003–1.023; p = 0.013), while chromosomal abnormalities showed a modest increasing trend (IRR = 1.023; 95% CI 1.002–1.044; p = 0.030).

Decreasing annual trends were observed for the group classified as others and for nervous system anomalies. Nervous system anomalies showed a more modest decreasing trend, with an average annual reduction of 3.3% (IRR = 0.967; 95% CI 0.940–0.995; p = 0.021).

No statistically significant temporal trends were observed for respiratory system anomalies (IRR = 1.033; 95% CI 0.994–1.073; p = 0.093), genital organ anomalies (IRR = 1.010; 95% CI 0.993–1.027; p = 0.259), urinary system anomalies (IRR = 1.004; 95% CI 0.976–1.033; p = 0.764), eye, ear, face and neck anomalies (IRR = 1.002; 95% CI 0.978–1.026; p = 0.884), or cleft lip and cleft palate (IRR = 0.994; 95% CI 0.974–1.015; p = 0.605).

To analyze factors associated with congenital anomalies, an adjusted logistic regression model was conducted, and the results are presented in Fig. 4. The model included maternal education, race/ethnicity, newborn sex, maternal and paternal age, type of pregnancy, prenatal care, and previous pregnancies.

Factors associated with congenital anomalies in live births in the state of São Paulo, Brazil

Maternal education was not associated with congenital anomalies. Compared with mothers with no schooling, the odds of congenital anomalies were similar among mothers with primary education (OR = 0.851; 95% CI 0.491–1.475; p = 0.565), secondary education (OR = 0.835; 95% CI 0.483–1.444; p = 0.519), and higher education (OR = 1.007; 95% CI 0.582–1.743; p = 0.980).

Differences were observed according to maternal race/ethnicity. Compared with White mothers, higher odds of congenital anomalies were observed among Asian mothers (OR = 1.283; 95% CI 1.130–1.457; p < 0.001), mothers of mixed race (Pardo) (OR = 1.112; 95% CI 1.079–1.147; p < 0.001), and Black mothers (OR = 1.293; 95% CI 1.222–1.367; p < 0.001). No association was observed among Indigenous mothers (OR = 0.585; 95% CI 0.278–1.230; p = 0.157).

Male newborns had higher odds of congenital anomalies compared with females (OR = 1.294; 95% CI 1.261–1.328; p < 0.001). Maternal age was positively associated with congenital anomalies, with the odds increasing by approximately 2.3% for each additional year of maternal age (OR = 1.023; 95% CI 1.020–1.026; p < 0.001), corresponding to an 11.8% increase for every 5-year increase in maternal age (OR = 1.118; 95% CI 1.102–1.135; p < 0.001). In contrast, paternal age was not associated with congenital anomalies, either per year (OR = 1.002; 95% CI 0.999–1.004; p = 0.149) or per 5-year increase (OR = 1.009; 95% CI 0.997–1.021; p = 0.149).

Regarding type of pregnancy, twin pregnancies showed higher odds compared with singleton pregnancies (OR = 1.295; 95% CI 1.207–1.388; p < 0.001), and triplet or higher-order pregnancies were also associated with higher odds of congenital anomalies (OR = 1.663; 95% CI 1.165–2.374; p = 0.005).

Prenatal care was associated with lower recorded odds of congenital anomalies. Mothers who received prenatal care had reduced odds compared with those who did not receive prenatal care (OR = 0.704; 95% CI 0.592–0.837; p < 0.001). The number of previous pregnancies was not associated with congenital anomalies, either per additional pregnancy (OR = 0.991; 95% CI 0.979–1.002; p = 0.120) or per 2-pregnancy increase (OR = 0.982; 95% CI 0.959–1.005; p = 0.120).

In a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for preterm birth, most associations remained similar to those observed in the main model (Supplementary Table S3). Preterm birth was strongly associated with congenital anomalies (OR = 2.497; 95% CI 2.410–2.587; p < 0.001). However, the association for twin pregnancy changed direction after adjustment for preterm birth, suggesting that gestational age may reflect a complex perinatal pathway rather than acting only as a confounder.

Discussion

This population-based study of 4,329,485 live births in São Paulo, Brazil, between 2015 and 2023 identified 53,217 cases of congenital anomalies (1.23%), with an overall prevalence increase from 103.90 to 143.92 per 10,000 live births. Temporal patterns were heterogeneous across anatomical systems, with increases mainly observed for circulatory and digestive system anomalies, a modest increase in musculoskeletal anomalies, and a decreasing pattern in nervous system anomalies. These findings add epidemiological evidence on congenital anomaly surveillance in a large middle-income setting and reinforce the value of system-specific analyses in birth defect registries.

The segmented trend analysis provided additional insight beyond the predefined epidemiological periods. Overall congenital anomaly prevalence showed an upward trend throughout the study period, with an annual increase of approximately 2%. However, trends differed substantially across anatomical groups. Circulatory system anomalies exhibited a consistent increase over time, while digestive system anomalies showed a more pronounced increase in the most recent years of the series. In contrast, nervous system anomalies declined after an initial increase in the early years of the study period. This heterogeneous pattern reinforces the importance of system-specific surveillance, as overall temporal trends may obscure distinct patterns across congenital anomaly groups [1, 5].

The increase in overall congenital anomaly prevalence observed during the study period is consistent with trends reported by population-based surveillance systems. Analyses from European congenital anomaly registries documented increasing prevalence for several congenital anomaly subgroups over time [5]. A recent Brazilian national study also reported lower prevalence than that usually observed in high-income settings, suggesting that differences in prenatal diagnosis, case ascertainment, and registry completeness may partly explain international variation [11]. Global Burden of Disease analyses indicate that, although age-standardized incidence rates of congenital birth defects have remained relatively stable, the absolute number of affected births remains high, particularly in lower-resource settings [1, 2].

Several factors may have contributed to the pattern observed in São Paulo. Improvements in surveillance quality and reporting completeness may increase case ascertainment over time, particularly as professionals become more familiar with registry completion and information systems mature [5, 11]. Advances in prenatal diagnosis may also contribute to increased detection. A population-based study from Western Australia documented a 5.5-fold increase in prenatal diagnosis prevalence over time, especially for cardiovascular, urogenital, and chromosomal anomalies [3]. Similarly, implementation of more detailed first-trimester anatomical ultrasound protocols has increased detection of major fetal anomalies in large population studies [4]. Demographic shifts, particularly increasing maternal age, may also influence temporal patterns, given the association between advanced maternal age and both chromosomal and non-chromosomal anomalies [6, 7, 21]. In addition, changes in the prevalence of maternal risk factors, including diabetes and obesity, may affect congenital anomaly occurrence [8–10].

Compared with recent national Brazilian studies, the prevalence observed in São Paulo was higher than the national estimates reported for 2011–2020 and 2012–2020 [7, 11]. This difference may reflect regional variation in case ascertainment, diagnostic capacity, and registry completeness rather than a true difference in underlying risk. The predominance of musculoskeletal and circulatory system anomalies was broadly consistent with national findings, supporting the relevance of São Paulo as a large state-level setting for monitoring congenital anomaly patterns.

Circulatory system anomalies showed a consistent annual increase (IRR 1.058, 95% CI 1.038–1.079), corresponding to an average annual increase of approximately 5.8%. Similar patterns have been reported in international surveillance systems. Analyses from the EUROCAT registries documented increasing prevalence for several severe congenital heart defects over time, including atrioventricular septal defects and tetralogy of Fallot [5].

Improved prenatal detection through advances in fetal echocardiography may partly account for this pattern. For example, a nationwide study in the Czech Republic showed that the involvement of pediatric cardiologists in prenatal screening programs significantly improved detection of major congenital heart defects, while overall incidence remained stable [22]. Similarly, a large systematic review demonstrated that reported birth prevalence of congenital heart disease has increased over time largely due to improvements in diagnostic technologies and screening practices [23]. Postnatal detection has also improved through widespread implementation of newborn screening programs for critical congenital heart disease using pulse oximetry. In the United States, mandatory newborn screening has been associated with reduced early infant mortality and fewer emergency hospitalizations [24, 25]. While these programs improve early identification and outcomes, they may also contribute to increased reported prevalence in surveillance systems.

At the same time, changes in underlying risk cannot be excluded. Maternal metabolic conditions such as diabetes and obesity are well-established risk factors for congenital heart defects [5, 8, 9]. A large meta-analysis including more than 80 million births showed that pre-gestational diabetes was associated with a more than threefold increased risk of congenital heart defects, while gestational diabetes was associated with a moderate but significant increase in risk [8]. Maternal obesity has also been associated with increased risk of circulatory system malformations [9]. In addition, a population-based study from Canada identified maternal diabetes, hypertension, and maternal congenital heart disease as significant predictors of congenital heart defects in offspring [26].

Another consideration is the potential role of maternal infections or inflammatory conditions affecting fetal cardiovascular development. However, the temporal relationship between the COVID-19 pandemic and the increase in circulatory system anomalies should be interpreted with caution. Because most structural cardiac anomalies arise during early embryogenesis, live births occurring in 2023 would largely reflect embryonic development during 2022 rather than during the initial pandemic waves. Therefore, the increase observed in 2023 should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct embryotoxic effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Individual-level data on maternal infection, gestational timing, disease severity, vaccination status, and other exposures would be required to evaluate this hypothesis.

Digestive system anomalies showed the largest relative increase among all anomaly groups (IRR 1.098, 95% CI 1.051–1.149), corresponding to an average annual increase of approximately 9.8%. Global analyses have shown that the overall burden of digestive congenital anomalies has declined over recent decades, largely due to improvements in surgical management and survival rather than changes in birth prevalence [27, 28]. A systematic review reported birth prevalence estimates for major digestive congenital anomalies ranging from 0.86 to 3.11 per 10,000 births [29]. In contrast, European surveillance data suggest that fluctuations in digestive anomaly prevalence may partly reflect surveillance artifacts rather than sustained epidemiological changes [5].

Several explanations may account for the increase observed in São Paulo. Improvements in prenatal detection through second-trimester ultrasound may increase identification of gastrointestinal anomalies such as duodenal atresia, esophageal atresia, and abdominal wall defects. Improved postnatal diagnosis and notification may also contribute, particularly for anomalies that are not immediately apparent at birth. In addition, more complete follow-up and improvements in registry practices may increase case ascertainment. Although changes in underlying incidence related to environmental or maternal risk factors cannot be excluded, the evidence supporting such associations remains limited [30].

Musculoskeletal anomalies showed a small increase over time (IRR 1.013, 95% CI 1.003–1.023). Although the relative increase was modest, this group represents the largest share of congenital anomalies in many surveillance systems. Global studies consistently report musculoskeletal anomalies among the most prevalent congenital defects, although they generally contribute less to mortality compared with cardiac or neurological anomalies [31]. European surveillance data also indicate that trends in musculoskeletal anomalies may be influenced by surveillance artifacts or differences in diagnostic classification across registries [5]. In São Paulo, the modest increase observed may reflect improved prenatal detection, changes in diagnostic classification, or demographic shifts such as increasing maternal age, rather than a substantial change in underlying risk.

Nervous system anomalies showed a decreasing annual trend (IRR 0.967, 95% CI 0.940–0.995), corresponding to an average annual reduction of approximately 3.3%. However, because this finding was based on a marginal p-value in the context of multiple group-specific comparisons, it should be interpreted cautiously.

This temporal pattern may be compatible with the epidemiological context of the Zika virus epidemic in Brazil during 2015–2016, which was associated with a temporary increase in congenital neurological anomalies, particularly microcephaly and other manifestations of congenital Zika syndrome [12, 13, 15, 32–34]. Previous studies conducted by our group in the Midwest region of Brazil also documented congenital Zika syndrome cases in a Brazil-Paraguay-Bolivia border region and an increase in central nervous system anomalies during the 2015–2018 Zika epidemic, followed by subsequent decline [14, 35]. More recently, cohort evidence showed that children exposed to ZIKV in utero had higher risks of early and long-term adverse outcomes, particularly when maternal infection occurred during the first trimester [36]. Multiple studies have documented strong associations between maternal Zika infection and microcephaly, neurological abnormalities, and neuroimaging abnormalities in newborns [12, 13, 15, 34]. For example, a prospective cohort study conducted in São Paulo State reported that neonatal Zika RT-PCR positivity was associated with a fivefold increased risk of microcephaly [12].

Thus, the decline observed after the early years of the series may reflect a return toward lower levels of Zika-associated neurological anomalies after the epidemic period. Nevertheless, the present ecological analysis cannot establish a direct causal relationship between Zika virus circulation and the observed trend in nervous system anomalies. In Brazil, the interpretation of trends in nervous system anomalies should also consider long-standing preventive and legal contexts. Mandatory folic acid fortification of wheat and maize flour was introduced by ANVISA through RDC 344/2002, aiming to reduce neural tube defects [37]. In addition, since the 2012 Supreme Federal Court decision on ADPF 54, termination of pregnancy in cases of fetal anencephaly has been legally permitted [38]. These factors may influence the frequency of severe central nervous system anomalies recorded among live births and should be considered when interpreting temporal patterns in registry-based studies.

No significant temporal trends were observed for genital, urinary, eye/ear/face/neck, cleft lip and palate, or respiratory anomalies. This stability is consistent with international surveillance literature showing that many congenital anomaly groups remain relatively stable over time [1, 2, 31]. Stable trends may reflect relatively constant detection practices, unchanged etiological exposures, or well-established prenatal screening protocols. For example, cleft lip and palate have long been targeted in prenatal ultrasound screening programs, which may contribute to stable ascertainment [5].

The multivariable analysis identified several factors associated with congenital anomalies that are consistent with previous epidemiological literature. Male sex, advanced maternal age, and multiple pregnancy were associated with higher odds of congenital anomalies [6, 7, 21, 26]. Multifetal pregnancies have been repeatedly associated with increased risk of structural anomalies [6, 7]. In the present study, sensitivity analysis adjusted for preterm birth suggested that the association between multiple pregnancy and congenital anomalies should be interpreted cautiously, as gestational age may reflect a complex perinatal pathway rather than acting only as a confounder.

Associations with maternal race or ethnicity should be interpreted cautiously and should not be understood as biological differences between groups. Rather, these findings may reflect broader structural and social inequalities, including unequal access to healthcare, differences in living conditions, environmental exposures, and other social determinants that shape maternal and child health outcomes [1, 7].

The association between prenatal care and lower odds of recorded congenital anomalies should be interpreted cautiously. Although prenatal care is essential for early detection and management of maternal and fetal conditions, most structural anomalies originate during embryogenesis, often before prenatal care is initiated. Therefore, this association may reflect differences in healthcare access, record completeness, and ascertainment rather than a direct protective effect. Preventive effects are more biologically plausible for specific preconceptional or periconceptional interventions, such as folic acid supplementation and glycemic control [4, 7, 39].

Maternal metabolic conditions deserve particular attention. Both pre-gestational and gestational diabetes have been associated with increased risk of congenital anomalies overall, especially congenital heart defects [8, 10]. Maternal obesity is also associated with higher risk of congenital heart defects and neural tube defects, with evidence of a dose-response relationship with body mass index [9, 40, 41].

Taken together, the heterogeneous temporal trends across anatomical systems suggest that the overall increase in congenital anomaly prevalence in São Paulo was accompanied mainly by increases in circulatory and digestive system anomalies, while other groups remained stable or declined. These findings highlight the importance of analyzing congenital anomalies by specific anatomical groups rather than relying solely on overall prevalence estimates [1, 5].

These findings have important implications for clinical practice and public health policy. The increasing prevalence of congenital anomalies, particularly cardiac defects, emphasizes the need for robust prenatal screening programs and specialized fetal assessment [4, 24]. Additionally, universal implementation of newborn screening for critical congenital heart disease can substantially reduce infant mortality [24, 25].

The results also reinforce the importance of preconception and prenatal interventions targeting modifiable risk factors, including optimal glycemic control in women with diabetes, healthy body weight before pregnancy, and folic acid supplementation [8, 10, 21]. Furthermore, the experience of the Zika epidemic illustrates the importance of maintaining surveillance systems capable of detecting emerging threats to fetal development [13–15, 32, 35, 36].

This study has several strengths, including its large population-based sample, long time series, detailed classification of congenital anomalies by anatomical system, and the combination of temporal and multivariable analyses.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the observational design precludes causal inference. Registry-based studies are subject to underreporting, misclassification, and changes in data completeness over time, which may partly influence apparent temporal changes in prevalence. In addition, the lack of individual-level data on maternal infections, medication use, environmental exposures, metabolic conditions, and other clinical factors limited the evaluation of specific etiological hypotheses.

Second, the study was restricted to live births recorded in SINASC. Therefore, congenital anomalies resulting in fetal death, stillbirth, or pregnancy termination were not captured, which may underestimate the occurrence of severe anomalies with high fetal or perinatal lethality, particularly major central nervous system malformations, chromosomal abnormalities, and critical congenital heart defects. According to the Brazilian Ministry of Health, analyses based exclusively on live births may underestimate severe congenital anomalies because a proportion of affected pregnancies result in fetal death or are not captured in live birth registries [42]. In contrast, surveillance systems such as EUROCAT include live births, fetal deaths, and terminations of pregnancy for fetal anomaly, allowing broader ascertainment of congenital anomaly occurrence [19].

Third, some newborns were recorded with indeterminate or unknown sex in SINASC. Although these records were treated as a separate category and excluded from sex-specific odds ratio estimation, the database does not provide sufficient clinical information to determine whether they corresponded to differences of sex development, chromosomal anomalies, temporary uncertainty at birth, or administrative recording issues. Thus, residual misclassification cannot be excluded.

Fourth, the study included different inferential levels. Temporal trend analyses were based on aggregated annual data and therefore represent ecological, population-level associations. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that individual mothers or newborns experienced higher or lower risks in specific years. Conversely, the logistic regression analyses used individual-level records, but their observational and cross-sectional nature also precludes causal interpretation. Therefore, caution is required when linking population-level trends with individual-level risk factors.

Finally, data from 2024 and 2025 were not included. At the time of extraction, 2024 registry data were still incomplete, and their inclusion could have led to underestimation of prevalence and distortion of temporal comparisons.

Conclusions

This large population-based study documented an increase in congenital anomaly prevalence among live births in São Paulo, Brazil, from 2015 to 2023, with heterogeneous patterns across anatomical systems. The overall increase was accompanied mainly by rising trends in circulatory and digestive system anomalies, while nervous system anomalies showed a declining pattern after the early years of the series. These findings suggest that improvements in detection, reporting, and registry completeness may have contributed to the observed trends, although changes in maternal risk factors cannot be excluded. The results highlight the importance of system-specific congenital anomaly surveillance, continued strengthening of birth defect registries, and public health strategies targeting modifiable maternal risk factors.
