Attenuation tomography using large-scale seafloor and land network data in northeast Japan
Authors: Yadab P. Dhakal, Ryoichi Nakamura, Takashi Kunugi, Shin Aoi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Existence of a heterogeneous attenuation structure in northeast Japan has long been documented. Despite this, attenuation structure around the plate boundary in the offshore region could not be examined thoroughly owing to a lack of data. After the 2011 Mw 9.1 Tohoku-oki earthquake, a seafloor network of 150 observatories, known as S-net, was established spanning the entire Japan Trench subduction zone to reinforce infrastructure for earthquake and tsunami early warnings. In the present research, we computed S-wave Fourier spectra of accelerograms from the seafloor and land stations and applied the principle of seismic tomography to retrieve three-dimensional attenuation structure (Qs). Our results showed a prominent low-to-moderate Qs zone near the upper surface of the oceanic plate in the seafloor area, most likely indicating the presence of water-rich oceanic crust and subducted sediments. We also found that the Qs structures in both onshore and offshore regimes are moderately to strongly frequency-dependent. A comprehensive understanding of the frequency-dependent behavior of attenuation structure remains elusive. We anticipate that the findings presented here will contribute to resolving pertinent issues in the field of solid earth science and to a deeper understanding of earthquake hazards in subduction zones.

Introduction

When the 2011 Mw 9.1 Tohoku-oki earthquake occurred, only three ocean-bottom seismographs (OBS) and two pressure gauges were in operation within the source region1. Tsunami warnings relied solely on data from land-based seismic stations, leading to a severe underestimation of tsunami heights. Approximately 20,000 lives were lost as a result of the earthquake and ensuing tsunami2. Following the great disaster, a seafloor observation network for earthquakes and tsunamis along the Japan Trench, known as S-net, was launched with the primary goal of enhancing the Japan Meteorological Agency’s earthquake and tsunami early warning capabilities3. The network comprises 150 stations equipped with seismometers and pressure gauges, covering the entire offshore area around the Japan Trench (Fig. 1). In this research, we utilized waveform data from S-net accelerometers3 and K-NET and KiK-net strong motion stations on land4,5 to examine three-dimensional (3-D) attenuation structure for S waves in northeast Japan, with a particular focus on the offshore area. Attenuation structure is generally expressed in terms of quality factors, Qp and Qs, for P and S waves, respectively. Existence of a heterogeneous attenuation structure in northeast Japan has long been recognized, due to the anomalous observation of seismic intensities mostly associated with deep-focus earthquakes in the region6–8. However, the attenuation structure near the plate interface, where large-magnitude earthquakes occur in the Japan Trench region, remains poorly understood. Determining the three-dimensional attenuation structure, especially for S waves, is essential not only for developing ground motion models aimed at reducing earthquake-related hazards, but also for understanding a range of geophysical processes occurring deep within the Earth9–13.

We analyzed the S-wave portions of the accelerometric records to compute Fourier spectral amplitudes from approximately 2700 earthquakes with moment magnitudes ranging from around 4–7. The study area, shown in Fig. 1, was divided into blocks of 0.2° × 0.2° in the horizontal directions and 10 km in the depth direction, extending down to 200 km. One-dimensional velocity model14 was employed to calculate the ray paths and travel times in each block. The recording stations were classified into 10 distinct site categories based on prior research15,16. Next, we estimated Qs values at frequencies between 1 and 10 Hz in 1 Hz increments using an iterative method17. We evaluated the reliability of these estimates through checkerboard resolution test (CRT) and discussed the results with sufficient recovery. The various steps of data analysis are explained in detail in the Method section. Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and 3 depict the site characteristics, convergence rates, and CRT results, respectively.

Discussions

Quality factors are commonly related to the seismic velocity of Earth’s materials, and these relationships are practically valuable since seismic velocity models are typically readily accessible31–33. We begin by examining the relationship between the S-wave velocity (Vs) perturbations34 and the Qs structures identified in the present research. Supplementary Figure S4 presents S-wave velocity perturbations together with the estimated Qs structures for the selected depth intervals. Despite being based on independent datasets, travel times in the previous study and spectral amplitudes in the current one, the low Qs regions in the oceanic area generally showed good agreement with the low Vs regions34. This correspondence is particularly noticeable at depth of 30–40 km (Panels a and d in Figure S4). However, a closer inspection of the Vs perturbation map in Figure S4 Panel a, around latitudes 38°-40° and longitude 141.5°, reveals inconsistencies between the two studies. In this region, we observed relatively high Qs values, whereas the Vs perturbations show large negative values, indicating lower S-wave velocities. This area of contrasting Qs and Vs lies within the fore-arc mantle wedge, which is considered to be composed of cold and stagnant mantle35,36. Previous studies have also reported relatively higher Qs values in the fore-arc mantle wedge8,19. Further investigation is necessary to clarify the cause of this discrepancy between the Qs and Vs in the identified region.

Next, we discuss the relationship between the Qs structure revealed in the oceanic area and the Japan integrated velocity model (JIVSM)37. The JIVSM was developed by merging regional velocity models primarily for the purpose of long-period ground motion simulation. In Supplementary Figure S5 (Panels a, b, c), continuous zones of relatively lower Vs values corresponding to the oceanic crust layers are clearly visible. These low Vs zones in the oceanic area generally align with or are situated near the low Qs zones identified in the present research, as shown in the lower panels of Figure S5. However, the extent of the low Qs zones decreases with depth, potentially indicating a shift in underlying geophysical processes38,39. It has been suggested that the oceanic crustal layers are rich in water and serve as a conduit for water in subduction zones38. The low Qs zone may thus reflect the water-rich oceanic crustal layers. However, it should be noted that the width of the low Qs zone in the seafloor area may not be reliable because the block size used in the present study is somewhat larger. Finer grids with more data in the future may provide further insights.

The study19, using the t* method, reported relatively higher Qp and Qs values in the Pacific plate and forearc mantle wedge. While the overall trends qualitatively agree with our results, some differences were noted offshore of the Tohoku region. That study identified distinct patches of high and low Qs along the plate interface and interpreted them as zones of strong and weak seismic coupling. In contrast, our analysis did not reveal such pronounced patches, likely due to the use of 1-D velocity model, whereas study19 employed a 3-D velocity model. However, they concluded that their results were largely insensitive to the choice between 1-D and 3-D models. If variations in Qs indeed reflect differences in seismic coupling, our results imply that strong and weak coupling may dominate deeper and shallower portions of the plate interface, respectively, with a transition around 30–40 km depth in the offshore region of northeast Japan. Because the resolution of the spectral data is generally lower than that of the travel time data, the Qs structure may be constrained more effectively in future studies with a well-developed 3-D velocity model.

The hypocenter of the 2011 Tohoku-oki earthquake was located within a relatively moderate Qs zone, and significant slip occurred in moderate to low Qs zones in the shallow plate interface. Conversely, the deeper part of the plate interface, characterized by higher Qs values, coincided with regions of stronger high-frequency seismic radiation40. Additionally, the 2003, Mw 8.3 Tokachi-oki earthquake’s hypocenter was located at a depth of approximately 42 km offshore of Hokkaido41. More recently, low velocity anomalies in the subslab asthenosphere have been hypothesized to modulate plate coupling in the source area of megathrust earthquakes in the subduction zones42,43. The above observational results indicate the importance of tomographic studies in delineating changes in the deep Earth’s medium properties. Continued collection and analysis of S-net data are anticipated to offer a greater understanding of earthquake generation and seismic wave propagation in the Japan Trench subduction zone and other comparable tectonic settings.

We perform iterative inversion procedure using the ARTB algorithm17 to determine Qs structure by minimizing residuals between the observed and calculated spectra of ground accelerations. The ARTB algorithm is provided in a supplementary file. We used 225,711 seismograms recorded by the K-NET, KiK-net, and S-net from 2,731 earthquakes that occurred between 15 August 2016 and 31 October 2023. The moment magnitudes of the earthquakes were between approximately 4 and 7. Epicentral distances were restricted to ≤ 100 km for earthquakes occurring at depths of 0–30 km, and to ≤ 500 km for those at depths of 30–200 km. The distance limit of 100 km was to avoid the Lg phases at the land stations for shallow focus earthquakes45. The focal depths of the earthquakes used in this study, as reported by the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA), were all shallower than 200 km. For ray path calculations, we substituted the JMA depths with moment tensor depths provided by F-net, NIED46. These two depth estimates generally agreed, except for events located farther offshore. The stations and epicenters of the earthquakes used in this study are shown in Fig. 1.

Nonlinear site effects on ground motion recordings at soil sites generally become apparent when the peak ground acceleration exceeds approximately 100 cm/s247,48. To minimize such effects, we selected seismograms with the maximum acceleration below 100 cm/s2 at the land stations, whereas a further lower threshold value of 50 cm/s2 was considered at the seafloor stations49. We divided land stations into eight site groups based on average S-wave velocity in the top 20 m of the soil column (AVS20) to take account of the near-surface amplification50,51. The site groups were separated at equal intervals of AVS20 in a logarithmic scale. Unlike land stations, S-wave velocity data were not available for seafloor stations. Additionally, to protect equipment from fishing activities and ensure good coupling with sediments, stations located in shallow waters (depths < 1500 m) were buried about 1 m beneath the seafloor, while those in deeper waters were left unburied3. Using the generalized spectral inversion method, significant site amplifications across a broad frequency range were reported at many S-net stations, with unburied sites landward of the Japan Trench exhibiting relatively stronger amplification at longer periods52. A more recent study16 found that buried stations generally showed lower amplification, whereas unburied stations showed higher amplification, relative to median ground motion prediction equations for both peak ground acceleration and velocity. Therefore, for simplicity and in line with the 1–10 Hz frequency range considered in this study, the seafloor stations were classified into two categories: buried and unburied.

To validate our Qs model, a checkerboard resolution test (CRT)59 was conducted. Artificial Qs of 100 and 400 were alternately assigned to blocks in horizontal and depth directions. The geometrical configuration of the stations and earthquakes was set identical to that of the inversion of the observed data. The ray paths were identical to those calculated for the observed spectra. Source spectra for half of the earthquakes were set to 0.5 × 107 cm/s2, while for the other half, the source spectra were set to 2.0 × 107 cm/s2. Site amplification was considered uniform for simplicity, as the trial calculations showed that the CRT results were insensitive to the number of site categories used in the present analysis. .
