National Beef Quality Audit—2022: Transportation, mobility, and harvest-floor assessments of targeted characteristics that affect quality and value of cattle, carcasses, and by-products from fed steers and heifers
Authors: Trent E Schwartz, Sydni E Borders, Thachary R Mayer, Kerri B Gehring, Davey B Griffin, Christopher R Kerth, Keith E Belk, Lily Edwards-Callaway, John A Scanga, Mahesh N Nair, J Brad Morgan, Jarrett B Douglas, Morgan M Pfeiffer, Gretchen G Mafi, Keayla M Harr, Ty E Lawrence, Travis C Tennant, Loni W Lucherk, Travis G O’Quinn, Erin S Beyer, Phil D Bass, Lyda G Garcia, Benjamin M Bohrer, Jessica A Pempek, Andrea J Garmyn, Robert J Maddock, C Chad Carr, T Dean Pringle, Tracy L Scheffler, Jason M Scheffler, Alexander M Stelzleni, John M Gonzalez, Keith R Underwood, Bailey N Harsh, Crystal M Waters, Jeffrey W Savell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe National Beef Quality Audit (NBQA)-2022 systematically evaluated quality characteristics of cattle, carcasses, and by products generated from the fed steer and heifer sectors. This audit was conducted from September 2021 through November 2022 in 22 beef processing facilities across 11 states. Approximately 7800 live animals were evaluated exiting the trailer and in holding pens, and about 23,200 carcasses were evaluated on the harvest floor for multiple characteristics. Cattle were transported on average 245.3 km for a travel time of 2.9 h, averaging 36 animals per load. On average, trailers had 41.3 m2 of floor surface area, and cattle were housed in 3.7 compartments, allotting 1.2 m2 per animal. Cattle received 91.7% mobility score 1 (walks easily, no stiffness). Cattle identification was observed on 93.3% of the those evaluated with the following frequencies: lot visual tags (61.3%), individual visual tags (58.1%), electronic tags (29.4%), low frequency electronic tags (28.9%), and metal clip tags (11.0%). For hide color/apparent breed type, most were black hided (62.3%), followed by Holstein (12.3%), red (11.3%), tan (4.9%), yellow (2.6%), gray (2.0%), brown (2.0%), non-Holstein dairy (1.7%), and white (1.1%). Most cattle were not branded (70.5%), followed by presence of butt brands (22.4%), side brands (7.0%), and shoulder brands (1.1%). For mud/manure on the hide, 49.6% had none that was visible; when observed, the most common areas were on the legs (38.7%) and belly (31.1%). Most cattle had no horns (84.1%); for those with horns, 4.6% were <2.54 cm in length, 7.9% were 2.54 to 12.7 cm in length (7.9%), and 3.3% were > 12.7 cm in length. For bruises, 47.7% of the carcasses had none, and for those with bruises, the loin (30.0%), rib (23.7%), chuck (19.7%), and round (19.3%) were the most common locations. For dentition, 95.4% had 2 or fewer permanent incisors deemed as less than 30 mo of age. Offal/byproducts condemnations occurred for liver (28.5%), lung (20.9%), viscera (12.5%), head (4.5%), and tongue (1.8%). Compared numerically to NBQA (2016), although we observed an increased area allotted per animal, travel time to the harvest facility was longer and mobility scores were lower (less mobile) exiting the trailer. Fewer cattle were identified via ear tagging, however, there was an increase in the use of electronic tags. Numerically, the percentage of black-hided cattle increased, and percentage of Holsteins declined, branding increased, mud/manure amount found on the hide declined, cattle without horns increased, and cattle 30 mo of age or older increased. Carcasses found with 1 or more bruises increased drastically (13.4% increase compared the NBQA-2016). Metrics observed in this study provide industry updates on improvements and deficiencies found in the fed beef cattle system.Keywords: animal welfare, audit, bruising, hide, offal

Introduction

The National Beef Quality Audit (NBQA) is conducted roughly every 5 yr and has been conducted 7 times, counting this audit, since 1991 (Lorenzen et al 1993; Boleman et al 1998; McKenna et al 2002; Garcia et al 2008; McKeith et al 2012; Eastwood et al 2017). Over time, the audits have become more complex but observations such as cattle brands, presence of horns, bruising, and offal condemnations have been evaluated since the first audit.

Offal condemnations, particularly liver condemnations, remain a problem within the industry (Brown and Lawrence 2010; Amachawadi and Nagaraja 2016; Herrick et al 2022; Lawrence 2022). Animal color/pattern was evaluated starting in the NBQA-2000 (McKenna et al 2002), and remains important because of the demand for certified box beef programs (Tatum 2015). The importance of animal welfare resulted in the introduction of animal transportation and mobility components into the NBQA-2016 audit (Eastwood et al 2017), and it remains important because the known defects associated with poor animal handling and transportation (Schuetze et al 2017).

This portion of the NBQA-2022 reported here was conducted to evaluate transportation parameters, cattle mobility, hide-on characteristics, and carcass and offal conditions of fed steers and heifers. Findings from this phase of NBQA-2022 will continue the collection of data that serves different aspects of the beef industry in its quest to determine management that best improves the quality and value of cattle, carcasses, and by-products.

Materials and methods

Approval from an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee was not necessary for this study. Data on live cattle transportation, mobility assessments, and evaluations were gathered solely through observation conducted on animals both before and after slaughter.

General overview

These observations were assimilated from September 2021 to November 2022, and the average number of animals collected per data type was n = 23,217. A total of 6 harvest floor data types were configured at a strategy workshop, before the beginning of data collection, by the collaborating universities. The beef harvest facilities selected for this portion were chosen to accurately represent the beef harvest capacity in the United States by size and geographical location (Table 1). Data were collected throughout the production of one full day. If the beef harvest facility ran two shifts (A/B), equal sampling occurred in both.

Transportation and mobility of live cattle

Approximately 10% of all truck and trailers that arrived at each beef harvest facility (minimum 5 and n = 216) were sampled. Trailer type was recorded as, bumper pull, gooseneck, single deck, pot belly, or other. Trailer dimensions, width and length, as well as number of compartments used also were recorded. The uses of the front upper pen, rear upper pen, and center gate were recorded to evaluate their usage. A series of time and origin questions were asked including origin of cattle (city, state), date loaded, time loaded, time zone, distance traveled, time unloaded, time traveled, cattle holding overnight, and cattle unloaded during the route. A total number of animals per load was recorded, as well as sex class and mixed pen status. After evaluation of the trailer, each truck driver was asked if they were familiar with the BQA (Beef Quality Assurance) program, and whether they were certified.

Cattle mobility was recorded on all animals (n = 7,817) as they exited the surveyed trailers into the holding pens of the beef harvest facility. Each animal was evaluated using the 4-point scale from Grandin and North American Meat Institute Animal Welfare Committee (2021). An animal designated as 1 was normal, walked easily, and showed no apparent lameness. Animals designated as a 2 exhibited minor stiffness with shortness of stride, a slight limp, but kept up with the normal cattle. With animals designated as a 3 obvious stiffness was present, there was an obvious limp with discomfort, they had difficulty taking steps and they could not keep up with the normal cattle. Animals listed as a 4 were considered extreme, they are very reluctant to move when encouraged, and they stood statue-like.

Harvest floor assessments: before hide removal

Before hide removal, approximately 50% of animals (n = 24,065) within each lot were evaluated for individual identification, knots, horns, and if the head dragged the floor during processing (n = 24,065). The identification that was evaluated included, none, low frequency electronic, ultra-high frequency electronic, barcode, wattles, individual tag, lot tag, metal clip, and other. If the animal possessed more than one identification method, each applicable method was recorded. Presence of horns was evaluated and recorded as none, <2.54 cm, 2.54 to 12.7 cm, and >12.7 cm. If an animal that was evaluated dragged the floor with any part of their head or body, it was recorded. A total of 50% of each lot was also evaluated for individual markings. The animal primary color (black, red, yellow, brown, grey, white, tan, Holstein, non-Holstein dairy) as well as pattern (none, brindle, roan, baldy, spots, other) were recorded (n = 23,557). Mud location (no visible, legs, belly, side, top line, tail region) and mud amount (none, small, moderate, large, and extreme) also were recorded using a pictorial guide (Savell 2016). Presence/absence, size (length × width) and location (butt, side, shoulder) of cattle brands were evaluated. If cattle displayed more than one brand, total area of coverage for each location was calculated.

Harvest floor assessments: after hide removal

After the hide was removed, carcasses (n = 23,157) from approximately 50% of each lot were evaluated for the presence or absence of bruising. Number of bruises, location (round, loin, rib, chuck, brisket, plate, flank), and severity (minimal, major, critical, extreme) were recorded. Bruise severity was recorded using a 10-point scale: 1, 2, 3 = minimal, <0.45 kg of estimated trim loss on surface; 4, 5, 6 = major, 0.45 to 4.54 kg estimated trim loss; 7, 8, 9 = critical, >4.54 kg of estimated trim loss; and 10 = extreme, estimated loss of entire primal. Additionally, the presence/absence of injection-site lesions was evaluated, and location was recorded when observed. The presence of a fetus was recorded if observed at the time of harvest. Beef dentition was evaluated by counting the number of permanent incisors present on approximately 50% of the beef heads in each lot (n = 23,831). Beef heads and tongues (n = 20,421) and offal products (liver, lungs, viscera, and hearts; n = 23,203) were evaluated for wholesomeness by United States Department of Agriculture, Food Safety and Inspection Service (USDA, FSIS) personnel, and condemnations/reasons for condemnations were recorded where applicable (i.e., liver abscess, lung contamination, tongue hair sore, abscessed lymph nodes in the head). Liver abscesses, when present, were recorded by level of severity using the procedures outlined by Elanco (2022).

Statistical analyses

Data were analysed using JMP Pro, Version 16.0.0 Software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 1989 to 2007) and Microsoft Excel 2018 (Microsoft Corporation., Redmond VA). Frequencies, distributions, means, standard deviations, minimums, and maximums were calculated using the Distribution function of JMP.

Results and discussion

Transportation and mobility of live cattle

Simple statistics for transportation factors of time traveled, distance traveled, number of cattle in each load, number of compartments used, trailer dimensions and area allotted per head are reported in Table 2. Mean time traveled was 2.9 h with a minimum time of 0.1 h and a maximum of 23.0 h. Average distance traveled was 245.3 km with a minimum of 3.2 km and a maximum of 2,124.3 km. Average number of animals per load was 36, ranging from 8 to 47. Averages for trailer dimensions were 41.3 m2 and ranged from 9.29 to 66.47 m2. Area allotted per animal was 1.2 m2 and ranged from 0.6 to 2.97 m2. Results were comparable to NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017). When asked, 91.2% of truck drivers (n = 216) claimed they were BQA certified.

Mobility scores of cattle were assessed as they exited the trailers (n = 7,817). Mobility scores were calculated in percentage form based on the total number of head evaluated. Cattle received scores of 1 (normal) = 91.7%; 2 (minor stiffness) = 7.1%; 3 (obvious stiffness) = 1.2%; and 4 (extreme stiffness) = 0.12%. When compared to NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017), the number of 1’s decreased numerically from 96.8% to 91.7%, 2’s increased numerically from 3.0% to 7.1%, 3’s increased numerically from 0.1% to 1.2%, and 4’s increased numerically from 0.0% to 0.1%.

Although we observed an increased area allotted per animal, travel time to the harvest facility was longer and mobility scores were lower (less mobile) exiting the trailer. Mijares et al (2021) reported that transportation time was one of the key factors affecting mobility score. Comparatively, mobility data collected during the COVID-19 pandemic by Mijares et al (2021) also reported a decreased frequency of mobility score 1’s (74.6%), and an increase in mobility score 2’s (24.3%), which they noted were numbers greater than the previous 4-yr average at the same facility. Knowing this information, it is possible that the COVID-19 pandemic caused a shift in the factors affecting transportation, and subsequently the mobility outcomes of the current study.

Animal identification method

Over 93% of animals arriving at beef processing facilities had at least one form of individual identification, excluding hot iron or freeze brands (Table 3). The denominator for computing percentages of differing types of identification was the total number of cattle sampled; for explanation purposes, that amount exceeds 100% because some animals had more than one form of identification. Percentage of animals with no identification was 6.7%; lot visual tags, 61.3%; individual visual tags, 58.1%; electronic tags, 29.4%; low frequency (LF) electronic tags, 28.9%; ultra-high frequency (UHF) electronic tags, 0.5%; metal-clip tags, 11.0%; bar-coded tags, 2.4%; wattles, 0.2%; and other, 4.3%. This is the first NBQA audit to evaluate LF and UHF tags as their own category because of interest in the use of these tags. Comparatively, electronic tag usage experienced a notable increase compared to previous audits: NBQA-2005 (Garcia et al 2008), 3.5%; NBQA-2011 (McKeith et al 2012), 20.1%; NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017), 16.9%; and NBQA-2022, 29.4%. Additionally, there was greater use of barcode tags, which increased about 2.0% from previous years: NBQA-2005 (Garcia et al 2008), 0.3%; NBQA-2011 (McKeith et al 2012), 0.0%; and NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017), 0.05%.

Animal traceability is important for national management of animal health and to export customers. There are an increasing number of world, health, and food safety trade organizations that are focused on improving and implementing more suitable traceability methods (Schroeder and Tonsor 2012). Electronic tagging is one way to improve traceability standards, either via LF or UHF tagging methods. Utilization of electronic tagging, specifically for harvest facilities, could improve traceability and labeling, as well as provide information that could be used for a national database (Barge et al 2013). The use of electronic tags is likely to increase because of the demand for traceability of imports, exports, and domestic markets.

Hide color or apparent breed type

Predominant hide color or apparent breed type of cattle in beef processing facilities evaluated for previous and the present NBQA are reported in Table 4. Hide color was recorded starting in NBQA-2000 (McKenna et al 2002) and has been evaluated in subsequent audits (Garcia et al 2008; McKeith et al 2012; Eastwood et al 2017) to estimate what cattle types are being harvested. The addition of a non-Holstein dairy color pattern as well as tan hided were added for this audit. The predominant hide color evaluated remained black-hided (62.3%), which was the greatest percentage recorded through the audits thus far. Holstein (12.3%), red (11.3%), tan (4.9%), yellow (2.6%), gray (2.0%), brown (2.0%), non-Holstein dairy (1.7%), and white (1.1%) rounded out hide colors observed. The greatest change between the past audit was a relatively large numerical reduction in percentage (approximately an 8.0%-point decrease) of Holstein-patterned animals, from 20.4% to 12.3%. This decrease in Holstein prevalence is likely because of the decrease in the number of facilities willing to harvest them, and the emergence of beef × dairy fed cattle (Foraker et al 2022). Beef × dairy animals are typically half dairy (e.g., Holstein) and half beef breeds (e.g., Angus), and their markings are often a solid color pattern, such as predominantly black-hided. This audit observed the greatest numerical percentage of black-hided animals documented since the audits began, which is likely attributed to the upswing in beef × dairy production.

Hide brand assessment

Branding cattle is a form of identification for the animal. Hides become less valuable when they are branded, especially if done more than once and in a location such as the side that disrupts the hide pattern. Branding data were first collected in NBQA-1991 (Lorenzen et al 1993) and in subsequent audits since then (McKenna et al 2002; Garcia et al 2008; McKeith et al 2012; Eastwood et al 2017). For NBQA-2022 (Table 5) 70.1% of the animals evaluated had no brands, which was a numerical decrease from the previous audit (Eastwood et al 2017). Most of the brands observed were butt brands (22.4%), followed by side brands (7.0%), and shoulder brands (1.1%). In this audit, 4.1% of cattle had multiple brands, which was an increase from 1.6% reported by Eastwood et al (2017). Hide values historically dominated the value of byproducts produced from a single beef animal and branding can negatively impact that value. However, hide are around 50% of their previous value and have been negatively impacted by the recent supply chain disruptions (Brester and Swanser 2021).

Mud or manure evaluation

External mud and manure presence on hide-on carcasses was assessed, revealing that 49.6% of cattle had none visible (Table 6). Eastwood et al (2017) reported that 37.7% of cattle surveyed displayed no mud or manure on the hide. Mud amounts documented included were none (49.6%), small (41.6%), moderate (7.3%), large (1.3%), and extreme (0.3%). All categories of mud amounts were an improvement from the previous audit. However, the previous audit did include a section to record “none,” and animals that would have been categorized as none were tabulated with small. This change partially caused the upwards shift in percentage from extreme to none when compared to the previous audit. Mud location was tabulated as none (49.1%), legs (38.7%), belly (31.1%), side (6.7%), top line (3.1%), and tail region (5.6%). Furthermore, NBQA-2022 decreased in mud and manure amount in all areas when compared to NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017).

Mud/manure on the hide can be a carrier for bacteria as animals enter the processing facility (Fegan et al 2005; Childs et al 2006). Bacteria, specifically STEC Escherichia coli, become more prominent as the seasons change (Cernicchiaro et al 2020). Prevalence and concentration of bacteria can change based on level of mud and manure on the hide (Cernicchiaro et al 2020). It is important to continue monitoring mud/manure on the hide to help safeguard against potential bacteria entering the harvest facility. Additionally, using interventions within the harvest facility combat any seasonal changes that might occur in bacterial load entering the plant (Reagan et al 1996; Castillo et al 1998; Koohmaraie et al 2005; Bosilevac et al 2006).

Horn evaluation

Presence/absence of horns has been evaluated since NBQA-1991 (Lorenzen et al 1993) and the subsequent audits since (Boleman et al 1998; McKenna et al 2002; Garcia et al 2008; McKeith et al 2012; Eastwood et al 2017). Horn presence has steadily decreased since the audits began in 1991; cattle evaluated in this audit (Table 7) displayed the lowest numerical percentage of horns thus far (15.9%). Also, when horns were present, horn length (not shown in tabular form) was determined. Cattle with horns < 2.54 cm occurred for 4.6% of observations, whereas those 2.54 to 12.7 cm were 7.9%, and those >12.7 cm were 3.3%. Presence of horns not only reduces ease of harvest, but they also can cause carcass bruising. Horns are best handled by removing the horn buds when the animal is at a young age (Grandin and Gallo 2007). As producers continue to use genetics to dehorn their herds, and management practices (dehorning) become more efficient, the number of cattle free of horns should continue to increase.

Carcasses dragging floor or equipment

As part of the NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017), frequency of carcasses that dragged on the floor or equipment as they traveled through the harvest facility was assessed. The present audit documented that 2.4% of the carcasses evaluated (n = 19,514) dragged the floor or equipment during harvest (not presented in tabular form). This is a decrease from the frequency observed in NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017) in which 6.3% of the carcasses observed dragged the floor or equipment. It is not known whether modifications were made in these processing plants or the differences in the types of animals processed between these two audits (e.g., fewer purebred Holstein cattle) helped reduce the incidence of carcasses dragging on the floor or equipment.

Carcass bruises

Carcass bruising results in lean and fat losses of the carcass, because in most instances they must be removed. Bruises can be caused during transportation, via animal handling, and from animal-to-animal interaction. Bruise data has been collected since the NBQA-1991 (Lorenzen et al 1993) and has been evaluated in the subsequent audits (Boleman et al 1998; McKenna et al 2002; Garcia et al 2008; McKeith et al 2012; Eastwood et al 2017). NBQA-2022 displayed the greatest incidence of carcass bruising (52.3%) recorded since the audits began (Table 8). Carcasses exhibited 1 bruise (29.7%), 2 bruises (14.9%), 3 bruises (5.9%), and 4 bruises (1.7%). More than 20% of the carcasses observed exhibited more than 1 bruise (22.5%), which is numerically more than any of the previous audits. Most bruises were observed in the loin (30%), followed by the rib (23.7%), chuck (19.7%), round (19.3%), and brisket/plate/flank (7.3%), which was similar to previous years in which the loin, rib, and chuck regularly ranked the greatest in overall bruise percentage.

Bruising found on cattle carcasses can lead to a severe reduction in overall carcass value (Grandin 1980). In some instances, a bruise can cause total loss of a subprimal cut. Bruises are typically trimmed, and depending on the severity and location, may cause a loss in red meat yield (Ferguson and Warner 2008). This audit observed over half of the bruises evaluated came from the loin and rib, which are valuable middle meat cuts. Further steps are needed to identify the cause of increased bruising, and solutions to improve bruising especially over the back, are warranted.

Dentition

Evaluation of dentition in cattle is used to help determine age at the time of harvest. Dentition was first evaluated in NBQA-2005 (Garcia et al 2008) and in subsequent audits (McKeith et al 2012; Eastwood et al 2017). NBQA-2022 observed the lowest percentage (77.5%) of cattle having 0 permanent incisors since these data started being collected (Table 9). This may indicate that cattle are being fed to later stages of maturity. The remaining data for permanent incisors was 1 (3.5%), 2 (14.4%), 3 (0.9%), 4 (2.6%), 5 (0.2%), 6 (0.5%), 7 (0.1%), and 8 (0.3%). The numbers collected for animals containing 3 or more permanent incisors resulted in 4.6% of the cattle being 30 mo of age or older, which is a 1.9% increase from the previous audit. The COVID-19 pandemic may have played a key role in this increase. As beef harvest plants had issues with harvest capacity, this created a severe backlog of cattle on feed, causing them to be kept beyond their optimal marketing condition (Martinez et al 2021). The backlog of cattle ready for harvest created in 2020 by the pandemic most likely carried over into 2021–2022, when these data were collected, causing an increase in age (dentition) at the point of harvest.

Offal and by-product condemnations

Offal evaluation took place after USDA-FSIS personnel had evaluated the products post-evisceration, and the frequencies at which products were condemned were recorded (Table 10). Liver condemnations typically make-up a large percentage of total offal condemnations: liver abscesses, flukes, contamination, and other comprise the liver condemnation category. Liver condemnations decreased numerically (28.5%) when compared to Eastwood et al (2017), which was 30.8%, but remains high when compared to past audits. Lung condemnations, which included lung pneumonia, contamination, and other were condemned at a rate of 20.9%, which increased numerically from Eastwood et al (2017), which was 18.2%. Viscera condemnations were composed of visceral abscesses and other. Visceral condemnations occurred at a rate of 12.5%, which is numerically fewer than Eastwood et al (2017) reported in the previous audit (16.3%). Heart condemnations (not shown in tabular form) were condemned 7.65%, which decreased numerically (11.1%) from NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017). Number of fetuses observed at the time of evisceration was 1.0%.

Rates of liver abscessation (17.5%), was similar to the previous audit (17.7%) (data not presented in tabular form). A new metric to this audit was to evaluate minor vs. major liver abscesses using the Elanco (2022) system for liver abscess classification. Of the liver abscesses observed, numerically more were minor (11.1%) than major (6.42%). This was similar to findings of Grimes et al (2024) in which most of the liver abscesses found were mild (minor). Liver defects such as abscesses cost the beef industry approximately $USD 60 million annually (Herrick et al 2022). Liver abscesses lead to other offal losses, such as the viscera and heart, via condemnation of the entire gut mass (Lawrence 2022). Severe liver abscesses can diminish carcass values from carcass weight lost because of trimming (Brown and Lawrence 2010; Lawrence 2022). Offal products create value for the beef production system, and reducing visceral defects at the point of harvest will help improve production and profitability of carcasses from fed steers and heifers.

Head and tongue condemnations also occurred post inspection by USDA-FSIS. Similar to NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017), the present study documented whether defects observed were able to be trimmed (T) or if they were condemned (C) (data not presented in tabular form). The total percentage of heads and tongues condemned during NBQA-2022 was 4.5% and 1.8%, respectively. Although there was not a large percentage difference in tongue condemnations, the number of head condemnations increased by almost 2% when compared to NBQA-2016 (Eastwood et al 2017). Heads were evaluated for swollen lymph nodes (T–0.3%, C–0.6%), abscesses (T–0.01%, C–0.1%), contamination (T–1.1%, C–2.4%), and other (C–1.5%). Tongues were evaluated for swollen lymph nodes (T–1.1%, C–0.5%), hair sores (T–4.8%, C–0.1%), cactus tongue (T–0.2%, C–0.0%), contamination (T–2.5%, C–0.9%), and other (C–0.3%).

Conclusions

NBQA-2022 provided valuable information about the production characteristics associated with live cattle and their carcasses and by products. Compared to NBQA-2016, this audit observed a numerical decrease in frequency of cattle with normal mobility (score of 1) exiting the trailer. In addition to the highest recorded percentage of black-hided cattle, there was a numerical decrease in purebred Holstein cattle, which may be reflective of increasing numbers of beef × dairy cattle. Producers are using electronic tags at a higher rate than previously reported, and cattle maturity (frequency over 30 mo) as well as rate of cattle bruising exceeded all previous audits. Liver condemnations, although decreased numerically, remain a problem for the beef industry. Although some areas of concern were improved from the last audit, others declined. Findings from the NBQA-2022 will allow the beef industry stakeholders the needed information to address current challenges associated with beef production.
