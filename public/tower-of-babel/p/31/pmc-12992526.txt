Machine learning and Regression–Based models for prediction of postoperative atrial fibrillation following coronary artery bypass grafting: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Pooya Eini, Homa serpoush, Mohammad Rezayee, Milan Kassulke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is a common complication following coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Traditional risk prediction models show limited accuracy, prompting increasing interest in machine learning based approaches. This systematic review and meta analysis aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance, methodological quality, and clinical applicability of machine learning models for predicting POAF after CABG.MethodsA comprehensive literature search identified observational studies developing or validating machine learning or advanced statistical models for POAF prediction after CABG. Diagnostic performance measures were pooled using random effects bivariate models. Risk of bias and applicability were assessed using PROBAST and PROBAST AI. Between study heterogeneity, sensitivity analyses, meta regression, and publication bias were evaluated.ResultsTwelve studies were included in the quantitative synthesis. The pooled sensitivity was 0.73 (95% CI 0.64–0.80) and pooled specificity was 0.83 (95% CI 0.73–0.90). The pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.84 (95% CI 0.80–0.87), indicating moderate discriminative ability. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (generalized I2 = 87.3%). Most studies were judged at high risk of bias, primarily due to limitations in analysis methods and validation strategies, resulting in overall certainty of evidence rated as moderate to low.ConclusionsMachine learning models demonstrate moderate accuracy for predicting POAF after CABG but are limited by heterogeneity, methodological shortcomings, and restricted external validation. Further rigorously designed and prospectively validated studies are needed to support clinical implementation.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Coronary artery bypass grafting, Machine learning

Introduction

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) represents one of the most prevalent and clinically burdensome complications following coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), with incidence rates ranging from 20% to 40% in contemporary cohorts [1]. This arrhythmia typically manifests within the first 2 to 4 days postoperatively, peaking around day 2, and often resolves spontaneously; however, its occurrence is far from benign [2]. POAF is associated with a heightened risk of thromboembolic events, including stroke, as well as hemodynamic instability, which can exacerbate heart failure or lead to organ hypoperfusion. Furthermore, it contributes to prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays, elevated healthcare costs, and increased short- and long-term mortality [3]. Established risk factors for POAF include advanced age, systemic inflammation, atrial structural remodeling, and procedural elements such as cardiopulmonary bypass, which may induce oxidative stress and autonomic imbalance [4]. Despite these insights, preoperative identification of high-risk patients remains challenging, limiting opportunities for targeted prophylaxis and monitoring.

Conventional risk stratification tools, such as the CHA2DS2-VASc score and POAF score have been employed to predict POAF after CABG [5]. These approaches typically rely on a limited set of demographic, clinical, and operative variables, yielding modest discriminative performance [6]. Their limitations include poor generalizability across diverse patient populations, inadequate incorporation of multimodal data (e.g., electrocardiographic parameters, biomarkers, and imaging metrics), and susceptibility to overfitting in smaller datasets [7]. Consequently, these models frequently fail to capture the complex, nonlinear interactions underlying POAF pathogenesis, resulting in suboptimal risk reclassification and hindering clinical utility for preventive strategies, such as beta-blocker therapy or enhanced rhythm monitoring.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for addressing these shortcomings, offering the capacity to integrate high-dimensional, heterogeneous data sources including clinical features, laboratory values, electrocardiograms, echocardiographic indices, and intraoperative metrics, while modeling nonlinear relationships and interactions [8,9]. ML techniques have demonstrated superior performance in related cardiovascular domains, such as arrhythmia detection from wearable devices, risk prediction for acute coronary syndromes, and forecasting surgical outcomes [10]. In the context of POAF after CABG, preliminary studies have explored ML models incorporating preoperative and perioperative variables, achieving improved discrimination compared to traditional methods [11,12]. Nonetheless, challenges persist, including dataset heterogeneity, inconsistent validation protocols, and limited external generalizability, which underscore the need for a synthesis of existing evidence.

Despite growing interest in ML for perioperative risk assessment, no comprehensive systematic review or meta-analysis has yet evaluated the predictive performance, algorithmic diversity, and methodological rigor of ML-based models specifically for POAF following CABG. This gap impedes the translation of these tools into clinical practice. In this review, a broad and pragmatic definition of machine learning was adopted, consistent with contemporary guidance for prediction model research (e.g., PROBAST-AI and TRIPOD-AI). In addition to nonparametric and ensemble algorithms (such as random forests, gradient boosting, support vector machines, and neural networks), we included regression-based prediction models and nomograms that employed advanced statistical learning techniques, including penalized regression (e.g., LASSO), multivariable logistic regression with data-driven feature selection, and internally validated risk scores. By synthesizing outcomes across studies, we aim to identify optimal modeling strategies and highlight avenues for future research, ultimately facilitating enhanced perioperative risk stratification, targeted interventions, and integration into decision-support systems without overstating the maturity of ML in this domain.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [13]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261277621). No amendments were made to the prespecified protocol, ensuring methodological transparency and minimizing bias.

Search strategy

We systematically searched electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and EBSCO, from database inception to November 30, 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (Emtree and MeSH terms) and free-text keywords encompassing the clinical condition ("atrial fibrillation" OR "postoperative atrial fibrillation" OR "POAF"), population and procedure ("coronary artery bypass grafting" OR "CABG" OR "coronary artery bypass surgery"), and methodological terms ("machine learning" OR "artificial intelligence" OR "deep learning" OR "prediction model"). Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to refine the query, with no restrictions on language or publication year. The full search strategy, including database-specific adaptations, is provided in the Supplementary Table 1. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened for additional eligible articles.

Studies involving mixed cardiac surgery cohorts were included if CABG-specific subgroup data were extractable. Exclusion criteria encompassed pediatric populations, non-CABG cardiac surgeries (e.g., isolated valve procedures), or patients with preoperative atrial fibrillation.

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts using Covidence software, with discrepancies resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized, piloted form in Microsoft Excel. Extracted items included study characteristics (e.g., publication year, country, design, sample size), patient demographics (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities), surgical details (e.g., isolated vs. combined CABG, on-pump vs. off-pump), ML model specifics (e.g., algorithm type, input features including clinical, laboratory, ECG, imaging, and intraoperative variables, validation strategy such as cross-validation or hold-out sets), and performance metrics with 95% confidence intervals. Validation strategies were classified a priori into internal validation (e.g., cross-validation, bootstrapping, or split-sample testing within the same cohort), temporal or sequential validation (evaluation on a later, non-overlapping subset from the same institution), and external validation (testing on an independent cohort from a different institution or population). For subgroup and meta-regression analyses, only truly independent cohorts were considered external validation, while internal and temporal validations were analyzed separately to minimize misclassification and optimistic performance estimates. Models were categorized as traditional statistical learning or more complex machine learning approaches. Traditional statistical learning methods included logistic regression–based models and other linear classifiers, primarily emphasizing interpretability and predefined model structure. More complex machine learning approaches encompassed tree-based, kernel-based, ensemble, and neural network models, which allow flexible, non-linear modeling of high-dimensional data and are primarily optimized for predictive performance.

For studies reporting multiple models, the primary or best-performing model (as defined by the authors) was prioritized; sensitivity analyses explored alternatives if applicable. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a senior author.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool with adaptations for AI models (PROBAST-AI) where relevant [14]. Domains evaluated included participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. Studies were rated as low, high, or unclear risk in each domain, with overall risk classified accordingly. The certainty of evidence was appraised using an adapted Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework for prediction models, downgrading for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Discrepancies were adjudicated by a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with packages including metafor, meta, mada, and pROC. The primary synthesis involved random-effects meta-analysis of AUROC values. The Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine transformation was used to stabilize variance and normalize the distribution of AUROC estimates, particularly when values approach boundary limits, a common issue in diagnostic accuracy meta-analyses [15]. For sensitivity and specificity, bivariate random-effects models were employed to account for threshold effects. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 (with thresholds: <25% low, 25-50% moderate, >50% high) and τ2 statistics. Prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by ML type (DL vs. traditional ML), validation strategy (internal vs. external), and data modality (clinical-only vs. multimodal, e.g., including ECG or imaging). Meta-regression explored sources of heterogeneity (e.g., sample size, study quality) if ≥ 10 studies were available. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Deeks' asymmetry test. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out methods and exclusion of high-risk-of-bias studies. Forest plots visualized pooled estimates, and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were generated for overall discriminative performance. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

The systematic literature search identified 318 records across PubMed (n = 46), Scopus (n = 166), Web of Science (n = 55), Embase (n = 36), and EBSCO (n = 15). After removal of 134 duplicate records, 184 unique studies were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 40 articles were deemed potentially eligible and underwent full-text review. Six studies were excluded at the full-text stage due to being study protocols (n = 3), non-English publications (n = 1), review articles (n = 2) and focusing on all of cardiac surgeries (n = 15). Ultimately, 19 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis, of which 12 provided sufficient data for inclusion in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are in Table 1. The included studies were predominantly observational in design, comprising retrospective cohorts, prospective cohorts, and case control analyses conducted across diverse geographic regions, including China, the United States, Europe, and Southeast Asia. Definitions of POAF varied substantially across studies (Supplementary Table 2), particularly with respect to episode duration thresholds and monitoring intensity. Validation strategies varied substantially, ranging from internal validation and internal test sets to external, sequential, and independent validation cohorts, while several studies reported no formal validation. Selected features spanned electrocardiographic parameters, demographic and anthropometric variables, laboratory biomarkers, echocardiographic indices, perioperative and intraoperative factors, genetic markers, and composite risk scores, reflecting wide heterogeneity in feature engineering approaches. Sample sizes ranged from small single center datasets with fewer than 100 participants to large population-based cohorts exceeding 18,000 patients. Mean patient age was generally in the mid to late 60s, with a predominance of male participants across studies, often exceeding 70 percent. Past medical history commonly included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, peripheral vascular disease, and prior myocardial infarction, although the breadth of comorbidities varied by study. Event rates for postoperative atrial fibrillation ranged widely, from approximately 10 percent to nearly 50 percent. Surgical techniques included on pump and off pump CABG, minimal access approaches, and grafting strategies such as left internal mammary artery and saphenous vein grafts, though several studies did not report procedural details. Left ventricular ejection fraction was variably reported, most often preserved or mildly reduced, with some studies providing categorical distributions. Medical therapy was inconsistently described but frequently included beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, statins, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, anticoagulants, and antiplatelet agents. A broad spectrum of machine learning algorithms was evaluated, including logistic regression, support vector machines, random forests, gradient boosting methods, neural networks, ensemble and stacking models, and causal inference approaches, with best performing models differing across studies and no single algorithm consistently dominant.

Quality assessment

During model development, most studies showed low to moderate risk of bias. Participant-related concerns primarily reflected retrospective designs, single-center cohorts, limited sample sizes, and incomplete reporting of inclusion and exclusion criteria, which may restrict representativeness of the full MR spectrum. Predictors were generally well defined and consistently measured, although moderate risk was assigned when preprocessing steps, handling of missing data, or blinding to outcome assessment were insufficiently reported. Outcome definitions were largely based on standardized echocardiographic or guideline-defined criteria and were assessed independently of predictors, resulting in predominantly low risk of bias. Analytical methods represented the main source of concern, with moderate risk arising from limited transparency in model selection, hyperparameter tuning, cross-validation strategies, and inadequate reporting of uncertainty measures, raising the possibility of overfitting.

In the evaluation phase, high risk of bias was frequently observed, driven mainly by participant selection and analytical methods. Many studies relied on internal validation or non-independent datasets, with limited demographic and clinical diversity, leading to potentially optimistic performance estimates. Predictors and outcomes were usually consistent with the development phase, yielding mostly low to moderate risk; however, incomplete reporting of preprocessing pipelines occasionally undermined validity. Analytical bias was common due to the absence of true external validation, selective reporting of performance metrics, and lack of uncertainty estimates or clinically interpretable measures, substantially limiting confidence in reported model performance.

Concerns regarding applicability were also prominent. High concern for participants was assigned when study populations did not reflect real-world clinical settings, such as exclusion of patients with comorbidities or advanced disease. Predictor applicability was generally acceptable, as most models used routinely available echocardiographic and clinical variables, although reliance on specialized imaging protocols or proprietary features increased concern in some studies. Outcome definitions aligned well with established clinical standards, resulting in low applicability concerns. In contrast, analytical applicability was frequently rated as high concern because models often lacked clinically relevant thresholds, subgroup analyses, or assessments of impact on clinical decision-making. Overall, while development quality was generally acceptable, substantial risk of bias during evaluation and high concerns regarding applicability highlight the need for rigorous external validation, transparent analytical reporting, and closer alignment with real-world clinical practice (Fig. 2). detailed domains are available in Supplementary Table 3.

Overall pooled performance

Summary estimates of diagnostic performance across model types are presented in Table 2. Twelve studies were included in the quantitative synthesis. The pooled sensitivity was 0.73 (95% CI 0.64–0.80) and the pooled specificity was 0.83 (95% CI 0.73–0.90) (Fig. 3). The summary positive likelihood ratio was 4.4 (95% CI 2.6–7.3), and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.33 (95% CI 0.24–0.45), indicating a modest to moderate effect on post-test probability. The pooled diagnostic odds ratio was 13 (95% CI 6–28). Overall discriminative performance was moderate, with a pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.84 (95% CI 0.80–0.87) (Fig. 4).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect estimate by sequentially omitting each individual study and recalculating the summary effect. Across all iterations, the pooled effect size remained stable, with point estimates ranging narrowly around the overall summary estimate. In all leave-one-out analyses, the recalculated effect sizes were statistically significant, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals consistently overlapped with that of the full model. No single study exerted a disproportionate influence on the pooled estimate, and omission of any individual study did not materially alter the magnitude or direction of the association. These findings indicate that the overall results were not driven by any single dataset and support the internal robustness of the meta-analytic conclusions, despite the substantial between-study heterogeneity observed elsewhere in the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Subgroup analysis

Studies conducted in China

Seven studies performed in China were included in this subgroup analysis. The pooled sensitivity was 0.67 (95% CI 0.57–0.75), while the pooled specificity reached 0.83 (95% CI 0.67–0.92). Substantial heterogeneity was observed overall (I2 = 94.23%), with particularly high inconsistency for sensitivity (I2 = 90.39%) and specificity (I2 = 96.69%), indicating considerable variability in diagnostic performance across studies conducted in this setting (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Models based on logistic regression

Five studies employing logistic regression models were synthesized. The combined sensitivity was estimated at 0.69 (95% CI 0.51–0.83), and the corresponding specificity was 0.66 (95% CI 0.62–0.70). Overall heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 84.82%), driven mainly by variability in sensitivity estimates (I2 = 93.72%), whereas heterogeneity for specificity was comparatively lower (I2 = 73.51%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Comparison of external and internal validation

Three studies that conducted external validation using independent cohorts were included in this subgroup. The pooled sensitivity was 0.62 (95% CI 0.47–0.75), and the pooled specificity was 0.85 (95% CI 0.57–0.96). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across externally validated studies (overall I2 = 91.91%), with high inconsistency for both sensitivity (I2 = 91.96%) and specificity (I2 = 98.29%) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Seven studies that evaluated model performance on new, previously unseen data from the same source population were included in this subgroup. The pooled sensitivity was 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.79), and the pooled specificity was 0.85 (95% CI 0.73–0.92). Moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity was observed overall (I2 = 76.83%), with moderate inconsistency for sensitivity (I2 = 54.92%) and high heterogeneity for specificity (I2 = 90.42%) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

After exclusion of studies at high risk of bias

Following the removal of studies judged to have a high risk of bias, eight studies remained for analysis. Diagnostic performance showed a pooled sensitivity of 0.71 (95% CI 0.61–0.80) and a pooled specificity of 0.83 (95% CI 0.70–0.92). Despite improved point estimates, heterogeneity remained substantial (overall I2 = 95.38%), particularly for specificity (I2 = 98.13%), suggesting persistent variability even after restricting the analysis to higher-quality studies (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Comparison of traditional statistical learning and more complex ML/DL approaches

Six studies employing more complex machine learning or deep learning approaches were included in this subgroup. The pooled sensitivity was 0.75 (95% CI 0.73–0.77), and the pooled specificity was 0.89 (95% CI 0.81–0.94). Heterogeneity was minimal overall (I2 = 0.02%) and for sensitivity (I2 = 1.40%), while substantial heterogeneity was observed for specificity (I2 = 77.99%) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

In contrast, six studies based on traditional statistical learning methods were analyzed separately. The pooled sensitivity was 0.68 (95% CI 0.53–0.80), and the pooled specificity was 0.76 (95% CI 0.58–0.87). Considerable heterogeneity was present across these studies (overall I2 = 96.65%), with high inconsistency for both sensitivity (I2 = 93.62%) and specificity (I2 = 98.38%) (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Meta-regression analysis

Meta regression analyses were performed to investigate potential sources of between study heterogeneity in pooled diagnostic performance. Prespecified covariates included sample size (log transformed), number of outcome events, model type, and validation strategy. Likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate the contribution of each covariate.

None of the examined parameters showed a statistically significant association with heterogeneity. Sample size was not a significant moderator (LRT χ2 = 3.20, p = 0.20), although it was associated with a partial and non-significant reduction in residual heterogeneity (I2 = 38%). The number of events did not explain between study variability (LRT χ2 = 1.11, p = 0.57), with no residual heterogeneity reduction (I2 = 0%). Similarly, model type was not associated with differences in performance (LRT χ2 = 1.17, p = 0.56), and validation strategy showed no significant moderating effect (LRT χ2 = 3.81, p = 0.15), despite a modest decrease in heterogeneity (I2 = 47%). Overall, these findings indicate that the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies could not be explained by the examined study level characteristics (Supplementary Table 4).

Publication bias

Assessment of publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test did not indicate significant small-study effects (p = 0.99). This finding suggests no statistically detectable evidence of publication bias among the included studies (Fig. 5). However, given the limited number of studies and the substantial between-study heterogeneity, the possibility of residual reporting bias cannot be fully excluded, and this result should be interpreted with caution.

GRADE assessment

Overall certainty of evidence was judged to be moderate to low. The certainty was downgraded for serious risk of bias, primarily driven by PROBAST-AI findings indicating frequent high risk during the model evaluation phase. Common limitations included reliance on retrospective, single-center datasets, predominant use of internal validation without independent external validation, incomplete reporting of analytical pipelines, and selective reporting of performance metrics. These factors raise concern for optimism bias and limit confidence in the reproducibility of reported model performance.

The certainty was also downgraded for serious inconsistency, reflected by substantial between-study heterogeneity in pooled diagnostic estimates (generalized I2 >85%), including high heterogeneity for both sensitivity and specificity. Although leave-one-out sensitivity analyses demonstrated stable pooled estimates and no single influential study, the unexplained variability across study populations, feature sets, model architectures, outcome definitions, and validation strategies reduced confidence in the generalizability of the summary effects.

Indirectness was judged as not serious to moderate. Most studies evaluated clinically relevant CABG populations and used guideline-consistent definitions of postoperative atrial fibrillation; however, variability in rhythm monitoring intensity (continuous telemetry versus intermittent electrocardiography) and AF duration thresholds introduces potential indirectness when extrapolating model performance to real-world postoperative care pathways.

Imprecision was considered not serious to moderate. While pooled estimates of sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, and AUROC were accompanied by relatively narrow confidence intervals, the absence of prespecified clinical decision thresholds and the limited reporting of calibration and net benefit measures constrain interpretation of clinical usefulness at the individual patient level.

No statistically significant evidence of publication bias was detected using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test; however, given the modest number of studies and substantial heterogeneity, residual reporting bias cannot be definitively excluded (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that ML models exhibit moderate to strong discriminative performance in predicting POAF after CABG, with pooled AUROC values ranging from 0.62 to 0.94 across the included studies. Given the consistently high between-study heterogeneity, pooled estimates were interpreted as descriptive summaries of average performance rather than precise benchmarks. Meta-analysis was retained to quantify the extent of variability and provide a structured comparison across studies; however, narrative synthesis and stratified reporting were emphasized to reflect the underlying heterogeneity. This performance often surpasses that of traditional clinical scores, such as the POAF score (AUROC 0.63) or CHA2DS2-VASc (AUROC 0.59), particularly when ML integrates multimodal data including electrocardiographic parameters, biomarkers, and intraoperative metrics. While discrimination metrics such as AUROC indicate a model's ability to rank risk, they do not directly inform clinical decision-making. Few included studies reported calibration measures or decision-curve analyses, limiting assessment of whether predicted risks translate into meaningful net benefit. For example, a model with AUROC ∼0.84 may still be clinically unhelpful if it systematically overestimates risk in older patients or fails to identify actionable thresholds for prophylactic therapy.

Substantial heterogeneity in model architectures, validation strategies, and POAF definitions limits direct comparisons and underscores the need for standardized reporting.

A key strength of the reviewed ML models lies in their ability to handle high-dimensional, nonlinear data, enabling the incorporation of diverse predictors that traditional regression-based approaches often overlook. For instance, models utilizing ECG-derived features, such as P-wave duration and PR interval, demonstrated improved AUROC (0.67–0.89) by capturing subtle atrial remodeling signals that precede POAF, potentially allowing for preoperative risk refinement [34,35]. Similarly, the integration of intraoperative variables like cardiopulmonary bypass time and defibrillation in ensemble methods, such as XGBoost, yielded AUROCs up to 0.94, highlighting ML's capacity to model dynamic procedural risks that static scores cannot [12,36]. This multimodal approach not only enhances discrimination but also supports personalized prophylaxis, such as selective beta-blocker use, which could mitigate POAF-related complications like stroke and prolonged hospitalization. Furthermore, the use of interpretable techniques, including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), in some studies provides clinical insights into feature importance (age and left atrial diameter consistently ranked highly) facilitating model transparency and adoption [37,38].

Despite these strengths, several limitations temper the clinical applicability of these models. High heterogeneity (I2 >50% in pooled analyses) arose from inconsistencies in POAF definitions (duration thresholds varying from 30 s to >5 min) and ascertainment methods (continuous telemetry vs. intermittent ECG), potentially inflating performance estimates in retrospective cohorts where subclinical episodes may be underdetected [39,40]. Many studies were single-center and retrospective, introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability, particularly in underrepresented demographics such as non-Western populations [41,42]. External validation was infrequent, with only a subset of models demonstrating consistent performance across cohorts (AUROC drop from 0.81 to 0.79 in one example), raising concerns about overfitting and real-world robustness [11,43]. Additionally, calibration was underreported, and when assessed, models often showed poor fit in high-risk subgroups, risking over- or underprediction that could lead to inappropriate interventions [10,44]. Interpretability remains a barrier for complex algorithms like deep learning, where "black-box" nature hinders clinical trust, as seen in studies relying solely on AUROC without decision curve analysis [6,45].

Comparisons with select studies reveal both synergies and disparities. For example, Burgos et al. (2020) developed a simple clinical score (NOPAF) with an AUROC of 0.71, emphasizing age and cardiopulmonary bypass as key predictors, which aligns with our pooled findings but lacks the multimodal integration [46] seen in ML models like Karri et al. (2021), where gradient boosting achieved an AUROC of 0.74 by incorporating coagulopathy and valve operations [41]. Similarly, Fan et al. (2022) reported a nomogram with an AUROC of 0.665, outperforming CHA2DS2-VASc, but its reliance on clinical variables alone contrasts with the enhanced performance (AUROC 0.813) in Cao et al. (2025), which added stress hyperglycemia ratio via XGBoost [12,37]. These comparisons highlight ML's strength in data fusion but also underscore limitations in external validation, as evidenced by reduced AUROCs in heterogeneous cohorts [5,38].

Traditional statistical learning and more complex machine learning or deep learning approaches differ fundamentally in their modeling philosophy and practical trade-offs. Traditional statistical methods, such as regression-based models, rely on predefined functional forms and emphasize interpretability, making them well suited for hypothesis testing, clinical transparency, and settings with limited sample sizes. In contrast, more complex ML/DL approaches are designed to maximize predictive performance by automatically capturing non-linear relationships and high-order interactions in high-dimensional data, often with fewer assumptions about data structure. While these flexible models may offer superior discrimination in complex clinical scenarios, they typically require larger datasets, rigorous validation, and careful attention to overfitting, and their reduced interpretability can limit direct clinical insight.

Clinical implication

To translate the reported diagnostic performance metrics into actionable clinical decision thresholds, consider a hypothetical implementation where an individual machine learning model's predicted POAF probability informs prophylactic interventions. Across the reviewed studies, models commonly achieved AUROC values in the range of 0.70–0.95, with many demonstrating sensitivities around 0.70–0.90 and specificities of 0.70–0.90 in their reported validations. For instance, at a commonly considered risk threshold of 20%, a model with sensitivity of approximately 0.73 and specificity of 0.83 could identify a substantial proportion of true high-risk patients for targeted interventions such as beta-blocker titration, amiodarone initiation, or intensified rhythm monitoring, while correctly classifying a majority of low-risk individuals to avoid unnecessary exposure to side effects like bradycardia, hypotension, or drug-related complications. This operating point would typically correspond to a positive likelihood ratio of around 4–5, potentially elevating the post-test probability of POAF from a baseline of 20–40% to 50–70% or higher in screen-positive cases, thereby supporting more selective and resource-efficient postoperative care, such as enhanced ICU telemetry.

However, optimal thresholds must be calibrated to local POAF prevalence, available resources, patient-specific trade-offs (e.g., balancing overtreatment risks against missed opportunities for prevention), and formal net benefit assessments, such as decision curve analysis, since individual models varied in calibration performance and few reported explicit clinical thresholds. Suboptimal calibration in certain studies could result in systematic over- or underprediction, leading to inappropriate prophylaxis or overlooked high-risk cases. Prospective impact studies remain essential to validate and refine these thresholds in real-world settings, confirming that ML-driven risk stratification not only improves discrimination over traditional scores but also translates into measurable reductions in POAF-related adverse outcomes, including stroke, prolonged hospitalization, or increased healthcare costs, without introducing net harm.

Compared with traditional scoring systems, machine learning based models represent a substantive methodological advance in cardiovascular diagnosis and risk stratification. Conventional scores in cardiology are derived from a restricted set of predefined variables combined through fixed thresholds and linear assumptions, prioritizing interpretability and clinical familiarity. While these attributes facilitate adoption and guideline integration, they limit the ability of traditional models to capture complex, nonlinear interactions among anatomical, functional, and clinical parameters, particularly in heterogeneous or borderline cases [8,9]. Multiple studies have shown that such scoring systems exhibit only moderate discriminative performance and are vulnerable to interobserver variability, especially in imaging driven assessments.

In contrast, machine learning models are specifically designed to integrate high dimensional data and to learn nonlinear relationships directly from the data, enabling the extraction of latent patterns that are not readily apparent to human observers [47]. Large scale evaluations in cardiovascular medicine have consistently demonstrated superior discrimination of machine learning approaches compared with established clinical scores [48]. Despite these advantages, important limitations remain. Machine learning models often lack transparency, may function as opaque decision systems, and are sensitive to dataset shift and population bias, raising concerns about generalizability and clinical trust [49]. Traditional scoring systems, although less flexible, benefit from conceptual clarity, standardized implementation, and strong guideline endorsement.

Across studies with internal and external validation, ML approaches consistently integrated multimodal data encompassing clinical variables, electrocardiographic parameters, laboratory biomarkers, and intraoperative metrics to achieve incremental improvements over traditional scores [50]. This capability could facilitate earlier identification of high-risk patients, enabling selective implementation of prophylactic measures such as beta-blockers or amiodarone, while sparing low-risk individuals from unnecessary interventions. In clinical practice, such models might inform enhanced postoperative monitoring protocols, potentially optimizing intensive care unit resource allocation and reducing overall healthcare utilization [51]. Importantly, ML should be viewed as a complementary tool that enhances, rather than supplants, clinical judgment in multidisciplinary perioperative care.

However, methodological heterogeneity across the included studies tempers these implications. Variations in feature selection, POAF definitions (e.g., duration thresholds or inclusion of asymptomatic episodes), and ascertainment methods (e.g., continuous telemetry versus intermittent ECG monitoring) introduce potential biases that may inflate reported performance. For instance, reliance on retrospective data in most cohorts risks annotation inconsistencies, where transient or subclinical AF episodes might be undercaptured, leading to biased model training. Additionally, differences in surgical techniques (e.g., on-pump versus off-pump) and perioperative management protocols further complicate direct comparisons, highlighting the need for standardized benchmarks to ensure reliable application in diverse clinical settings.

Technical limitations also warrant caution in interpreting these results for routine use. ML models demonstrated reduced accuracy in borderline-risk patients or for detecting short-lived AF episodes, potentially due to challenges with missing data and class imbalance in datasets where POAF prevalence is low. Model sensitivity to input data quality such as institutional variations in ECG sampling or biomarker assays, could further degrade performance in real-world deployment. Moreover, complex deep learning architectures, while effective, often lack interpretability, making it difficult for clinicians to understand prediction drivers and integrate them into decision-making workflows.

Concerns regarding clinical applicability and generalizability further limit immediate translation. The predominantly retrospective, single-center nature of the studies, coupled with limited demographic and geographic diversity (e.g., underrepresentation of non-Western populations), raises questions about external validity. Overprediction in certain models could result in overtreatment, exposing patients to adverse effects of prophylactic therapies, while underprediction might overlook vulnerable subgroups. Critically, no included studies provided evidence linking ML-guided predictions to downstream clinical outcomes, such as reductions in stroke incidence, hospital length of stay, mortality, or costs, underscoring a key evidence gap.

Study limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several important limitations. First, the available evidence was restricted to a limited number of eligible studies, most of which were small, single-center, retrospective analyses, thereby constraining statistical power and external validity. Although the initial search yielded 319 records, the majority were excluded due to lack of model validation, absence of extractable performance metrics, or focus on mixed cardiac surgery populations without CABG-specific data. This reflects current reporting limitations in the ML literature rather than restrictive selection criteria. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed, likely driven by variability in machine learning model architectures, feature selection strategies, data sources, and outcome definitions. Third, inconsistent reporting of predictive performance including binary versus time-dependent atrial fibrillation outcomes, limited cross-study comparability and precluded more granular synthesis. While advanced AI methods can process large datasets, they cannot compensate for missing validation, undefined outcomes, or absent performance reporting. Inclusion of such studies would risk biased or uninterpretable synthesis. Fourth, the exclusive reliance on retrospective datasets precludes causal inference and raises concern for overfitting and optimism bias. Finally, the predominance of studies conducted in high-resource academic centers may limit generalizability to broader surgical populations and community settings, where postoperative atrial fibrillation risk stratification remains particularly challenging.

Future directions

Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter prospective studies that adhere to standardized machine learning development and reporting frameworks, such as TRIPOD-AI, to enhance transparency, reproducibility, and comparability across studies [52]. Harmonization of reference standards for postoperative atrial fibrillation, incorporating continuous rhythm monitoring and guideline-consistent diagnostic criteria, is essential to reduce outcome misclassification and annotation bias. Rigorous external validation in diverse surgical populations, including older adults, women, and patients with complex comorbidities, is necessary to establish model generalizability and transportability. Further work should explore parsimonious and interpretable models capable of real-time perioperative deployment, facilitating integration into electronic health record–based decision support systems. Hybrid approaches that combine machine learning–based risk prediction with clinically actionable risk stratification pathways warrant evaluation to guide targeted prophylaxis and monitoring strategies. Ultimately, randomized controlled trials are required to determine whether ML-guided prediction of postoperative atrial fibrillation improves clinical outcomes, reduces resource utilization, and demonstrates cost-effectiveness prior to widespread clinical adoption.

Conclusion

In conclusion, machine learning models demonstrate moderate diagnostic performance for POAF after CABG assessment, with stable pooled estimates but substantial between study heterogeneity. Although these models show promise as adjuncts to conventional echocardiographic evaluation, the overall certainty of evidence remains moderate to low due to frequent methodological limitations, limited external validation, and concerns regarding clinical applicability. Further well designed, prospectively validated studies are required before routine clinical implementation can be supported.
