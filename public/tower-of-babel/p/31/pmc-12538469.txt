Diagnosis and management of toe dislocations: Insights from a case series
Authors: Clara X Quek, Jun Jie Leeu, Daniel Wei Ren Seng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractToe dislocations can lead to chronic pain and impaired function if not promptly diagnosed and treated. In this paper, we describe a series of complex interphalangeal (IP) and metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint dislocations and their subsequent surgical interventions. In Case 1, the patient was a 28-year old lady who sustained a left hallux IP joint dislocation after a fall. Case 2 described a 27-year old male with a missed diagnosis of a left fifth MPJ joint dislocation at initial presentation. Both patients underwent open reduction and Kirshner-wiring with good outcomes. In Case 3, a 33-year old lady had a closed fracture-dislocation of the left second metatarsal head and underwent surgical fixation with an uneventful recovery. By reviewing these cases and the current literature, we highlight important factors in diagnosing and managing these injuries.Keywords: Dislocation, Interphalangeal joint, Metatarsophalangeal joint, Reduction

Introduction

Toe dislocations, though relatively uncommon, can lead to significant morbidity if not promptly diagnosed and managed. The sequelae of chronic dislocation or subluxation can be debilitating, resulting in persistent pain due to instability, early degeneration [1] and recurrent dislocation [2]. Early recognition and intervention for toe dislocations is imperative, as delayed reduction may be challenging due to scarring [3] and contractures. Closed reduction is typically the initial management approach. However, it is often unsuccessful due to interposition of the sesamoid or soft tissue, including the plantar plate, flexor tendons and collateral ligaments [[4], [5], [6]]. Subsequently, the collateral ligaments tighten around the distracted or hyperextended joint, further locking the structures in place [7,8]. In this paper, we present a case series of complex interphalangeal (IP) and metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint dislocations, along with the surgical interventions performed. Through these cases and analysis of the current literature, we discuss key considerations in diagnosing and managing these injuries.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 28-year old female teacher had left big toe pain and deformity after sustaining a fall. She was seen in the emergency department on the same day and physical examination showed dorsal and medial deviation of the distal phalanx of her left big toe. There were no open wounds and neurovasculature was intact. X-ray of her left foot confirmed a dorsomedial dislocation of the left big toe IP joint, and was reported to have a possible fracture fragment within the joint (Fig. 1-A). Manipulation and reduction (M&R) was attempted twice, however post-M&R X-rays showed that the IP joint was still dislocated (Fig. 1-B).

(A) Dorsoplantar and oblique radiographs of the left foot obtained at the emergency department showing a big toe IP joint dislocation at presentation. (B) Repeat radiographs obtained after the attempt at closed manipulation and reduction showed persistent dislocation of the big toe IP joint with a widened joint space and bony interposition.

The patient was taken to the operating theatre on the same day for left big toe IP joint open reduction and Kirschner-wiring (K-wiring) under general anaesthesia. A dorsal incision was made over the left big toe IP joint, and the extensor hallucis tendon retracted medially. Distraction of the joint revealed an incarcerated sesamoid which was freed to allow for reduction of the IP joint. There was also laxity of the lateral collateral ligament post-reduction. After congruent reduction was obtained and confirmed using the Image Intensifier (II), two Kirschner wires (K-wires) were inserted for joint stabilisation (Fig. 2). The joint capsule was tagged, and layered closure was performed. The lateral collateral ligament was not repaired.

Intraoperative image-intensifier images of the IP joint of the big toe after open reduction and K-wiring in Case 1.

She was discharged the next day. The sutures were removed two weeks after surgery and the K-wires were removed at six weeks (Fig. 3). Radiographs showed good alignment with congruent left big toe IP joint. The IP joint was stable to dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, varus and valgus stress. She was able to fully weight-bear and walk without pain.

Clinical picture showing the post-operative alignment of the big toe, and after the K-wires were removed in Case 1.

Case 2

A 27-year old male construction worker fell after missing a step, sustaining an inversion injury to his left foot. He was reviewed in the emergency department, and X-Rays showed a dorsolateral dislocation of his fifth MTP joint (Fig. 4-A). Following the initial attempt at M&R, he was placed in a below knee back-slab. He was reviewed in the Orthopaedic Surgery specialist clinic ten days following his injury, and was found to have persistent MTP joint dislocation on the lateral X-Ray (Fig. 4-B).

(A) Dorsoplantar and oblique radiographs of the left foot obtained at the emergency department showing dislocation of the fifth MTP joint. (B) Lateral radiograph performed in the outpatient clinic showed a dorsal translation of the proximal phalanx over the fifth metatarsal head.

He subsequently underwent left fifth MTP joint open reduction and K-wiring under regional anaesthesia. He was found to have an interposed plantar plate that likely resulted in the failure of closed reduction. Open reduction was achieved through direct visualisation from a dorsal approach. The plantar plate was pushed in the volar direction; joint reduction was confirmed with II guidance and maintained with a longitudinally inserted K-wire (Fig. 5).

Intraoperative image-intensifier images of the fifth MTP joint after open reduction and K-wiring in Case 2.

The patient had an unremarkable postoperative course and was discharged the next day. The sutures were removed at two weeks, and the wound healed completely. The K-wire was removed in the outpatient clinic 40 days post-operatively. Alignment following K-wire removal was satisfactory as confirmed by plain radiographs (Fig. 6). He was able to walk normally with no pain.

Clinical picture showing the post-operative alignment of the fifth MTP joint in Case 2.

Case 3

A 33-year old sales lady tripped over her foot and fell, subsequently had difficulty bearing weight on her left foot (Fig. 7). She presented to the emergency department and X-rays showed a closed fracture-dislocation of the left second metatarsal head (Fig. 8). She was placed on a below knee back-slab and referred to the Orthopaedic Surgery outpatient clinic. She was reviewed five days post-injury and counselled for surgery.

Clinical picture showing the pre-operative alignment of the second proximal phalanx relative to the metatarsal in Case 3.

Dorsoplantar and oblique radiographs obtained at the emergency department showing the fracture-dislocation of the left second metatarsal head.

She underwent surgical fixation of the left second metatarsal head under general anaesthesia 11 days following her initial injury. Open reduction was performed via a dorsal incision, and reduction held with a 1.25 mm K-wire. Internal fixation was performed with two 2.2 mm headless compression screws (Fig. 9), followed by closure in layers.

Intraoperative image-intensifier images of the second metatarsal head after open reduction and surgical fixation with two headless compression screws.

Her recovery was uneventful. She was allowed to heel-weightbear with crutches, and was discharged the next day. Wound healing was satisfactory and sutures were removed two weeks post-surgery (Fig. 10). Serial X-rays were performed at two and six weeks; the screw positions were stable and fracture healing was seen (Fig. 11). At six weeks post-operatively, she was allowed full weight-bearing as tolerated.

Clinical picture showing the post-operative alignment of the second MTP joint in Case 3.

Post-operative dorsoplantar radiograph in Case 3.

Discussion

Complex toe dislocations have varied presentations, including soft tissue interposition, sesamoid incarceration and fracture-dislocations, as presented in our case series. These situations make closed reduction difficult, and if neglected will have significant morbidity and functional impairment. This paper aims to highlight key lessons from our case studies to improve diagnosis and treatment of toe dislocations.

In the emergency department, the most commonly obtained X-ray films for foot injuries are dorsoplantar and oblique views. However, dislocations can still be missed. We propose that orthogonal views should be obtained for suspected toe dislocations. In Case 2, only dorsoplantar and oblique films were obtained in the emergency department. On the dorsoplantar film, the fifth MTP joint appeared congruent, with only mild widening of the lateral joint space. In contrast, the lateral film obtained subsequently in the outpatient clinic revealed an obvious dorsal dislocation of the fifth MTP joint (Fig. 4). In such situations where dislocation is suspected on clinical examination, we recommend the use of lateral radiographs to aid in the diagnosis.

Furthermore, a study by Miki et al. reported that the interphalangeal sesamoid is radiographically invisible in up to 44 % of patients [9]. As such, a Miki Type 1 dislocation can be missed, as the phalanges will appear coaxial with only a subtly widened but clear joint space. Razak et al. also reported a missed diagnosis of an IP joint dislocation on the initial radiographs [10]. The dorsoplantar and oblique radiographs showed an oblique IP joint space of the left great toe, but this was overlooked at the initial presentation. They recommended obtaining both dorsoplantar and true lateral views when an IP joint dislocation is clinically suspected.

In our case studies, attempts at closed reduction failed due to interposition of the plantar plate, sesamoid bone and fracture. Repeated attempts should be avoided as these can worsen swelling, aggravate soft tissue trauma and lead to inadvertent fractures. Therefore, once initial reduction attempts prove ineffective, the next step should be early preparation for open reduction surgery. Various approaches have been described in the literature, such as dorsal open [4,11], dorsal percutaneous reduction under fluoroscopy [12] or ultrasound guidance [13] and plantar open [14]. Our preference is for the dorsal open approach, as it allows direct visualisation and removal of the interposed structures, while avoiding injury to the plantar neurovascular structures. Conversely, while the plantar approach offers direct access to the plantar plate, there is also risk of formation of a sensitive scar on the weight-bearing surface [15]. Repair of the plantar plate is also controversial and may be unnecessary, as previous studies have reported good outcomes regardless [16].

Following reduction, the stability of the affected joint must be assessed. Joint laxity is often increased due to overstretching or injury of the capsule and collateral ligaments [4,17]. In all our cases, intraoperative evaluation revealed capsular rupture. Case 1 also demonstrated lateral collateral ligament laxity, with no end point on stress test. Achieving joint stability is crucial to allow for early rehabilitation and to reduce rates of recurrent subluxation. Various stabilisation methods have been reported in the literature, including K-wiring, buddy strapping [2,10,12,[17], [18], [19]], splinting [4] and immobilisation in a short leg cast [4]. There are no existing studies comparing the efficacy of immobilisation modalities for toe dislocations. In the treatment of bony mallet finger, Thillemann et al. compared subluxation rates with splinting versus extension-block pinning [20]. They reported higher rates of secondary subluxation in the splint group before the six weeks follow-up and suggested that splinting may not be sufficient in preventing secondary subluxation. We opted for K-wiring for the dislocation cases and internal fixation for the fracture-dislocation. as these provided rigid stabilisation, allowing for controlled ligament healing while maintaining joint congruency. Further evaluation is recommended to determine whether K-wire fixation is necessary in all post-reduction cases. It should also be considered if splinting may be an alternative for stabilisation when intraoperative assessment confirms joint stability.

An important consideration is balancing stabilisation with the complications of prolonged immobilisation such as stiffness and pin tract infection. Previous studies have reported varied durations for K-wire retention, typically ranging from four to eight weeks [2,10,12,19]. In our cases, we chose to remove the K-wires at six weeks. Thereafter, patients were allowed prompt return to full weight-bearing, and encouraged to start range-of-motion exercises to facilitate recovery. In a study on K-wire fixation following lesser toe surgery for hammer or claw toe, the authors found that there was higher rate of recurrent malalignment in the 3-week-transfixion group compared to the 6-week-transfixion group at 3 months follow up [21]. There was also a greater amount of sagittal and transverse plane instability in the 3-week-transfixion group, although this did not reach statistical significance. No increase in complication rates for pin tract infection, wire loosening and breakage were observed. Future studies should further evaluate the optimal duration of immobilisation.

With timely and appropriate intervention, patients with toe dislocations generally achieve favourable outcomes, with a high likelihood of returning to pre-injury activity levels. In our cases, all patients returned to pain-free weight-bearing and resumed their pre-injury employment within two to three months. To the best of our knowledge, while no studies have evaluated long-term outcomes, existing case reports have not reported significant morbidity. Long-term follow-up studies assessing objective functional outcomes, recurrence rates, and patient-reported measures of pain and stiffness would further contribute to refining best practices in the management of these injuries.

Conclusion

Complex IP and MTP joint dislocations represent a spectrum of bony soft tissue injury. Early diagnosis is imperative and in cases with a high suspicion of dislocation, lateral-view X-Rays are advised for adequate joint visualisation. If initial closed reduction attempts fail, a dorsal approach for open reduction is recommended for its versatility to address plantar plate interposition, sesamoid entrapment and fracture fixation.
