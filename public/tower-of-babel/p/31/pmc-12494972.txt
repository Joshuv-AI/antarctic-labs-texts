Exploring Nigella Sativa’s medicinal capacity against skin cancer pathways using network pharmacology and molecular docking
Authors: Ahmad M Alamri, Abdullah A Assiri, Amjad Yousuf, Najeeb Ullah Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin cancer is a growing global health concern, marked by high incidence and significant mortality, particularly in aggressive melanoma subtypes. In this study, we employed an integrative network pharmacology and molecular docking approach to evaluate the anticancer potential of Nigella sativa (black seed) against skin cancer. Initially, 13 active compounds were identified from N. sativa based on stringent pharmacokinetic criteria. Target prediction using SwissTargetPrediction, integrated with 9697 skin cancer-associated genes from GeneCards and DisGeNET, revealed 303 overlapping targets implicated in critical oncogenic processes. Subsequent Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses via the DAVID database identified 780 biological processes, 87 cellular components, and 278 molecular functions. Enriched pathways included the positive regulation of the MAPK cascade, EGFR signaling, angiogenesis, and several other pathways central to skin cancer pathogenesis. The compound-target network further underscored the polypharmacological nature of N. sativa, highlighting hub genes. Molecular docking studies were conducted to validate the interactions of select bioactive compounds with key receptors (AR, CDK4, EGFR, MAPK1, and MAPK3). Among the compounds, Gramisterol (CID: 5283640) demonstrated the strongest binding affinities, with energies of − 9.1 kcal/mol for both EGFR and MAPK3, − 9.0 kcal/mol for CDK4, − 8.2 kcal/mol for MAPK1, and − 7.4 kcal/mol for AR. Cycloeucalenol (CID: 101690), Obtusifoliol (CID: 65225) and Lophenol (CID: 160482) also exhibited potent interactions, particularly with EGFR, MAPK1, MAPK3 and CDK4, supporting their potential to disrupt tumor proliferation and survival signaling. Collectively, these findings indicate that N. sativa’s bioactive compounds can modulate multiple cancer-related pathways, offering a promising multi-target strategy for skin cancer therapy. This computational study lays a robust foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo validations and paves the way for the development of novel, less toxic therapeutic regimens against skin cancer. Although the computational discoveries offer a solid conceptual foundation, evidence of their clinical significance is still pending. The next crucial stage of this investigation is to conduct extensive in vitro and in vivo verification investigations in order to close this gap. These initiatives will be crucial to converting our research into practical skin cancer treatment plans.Keywords: Skin cancer, Nigella sativa, Network pharmacology, Molecular docking, MelanomaSubject terms: Biotechnology, Cancer, Drug discovery

Introduction

Skin cancer is a prevalent and potentially life-threatening condition characterized by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal skin cells. Globally, it ranks as the 17th most common cancer, with over 331,722 new cases reported in 20221. In the United States alone, approximately 104,960 new melanomas are projected to be diagnosed in 2025, with an estimated 8430 fatalities2. These statistics underscore the significant public health burden posed by skin cancer, necessitating ongoing research into effective prevention and treatment strategies. The primary types include basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and melanoma. BCC and SCC, collectively referred to as non-melanoma skin cancers, are more common and generally less aggressive3. In contrast, melanoma, though less frequent, is notably more dangerous due to its propensity for rapid progression and metastasis4.

The pathogenesis of skin cancer involves a complex interplay of genetic, molecular, and environmental factors. Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from sunlight is a primary environmental risk factor, inducing DNA damage in skin cells that can lead to mutations and carcinogenesis5. Genetic predispositions also play a critical role; individuals with a family history of melanoma have an increased risk, highlighting the influence of hereditary factors6. Moreover, specific gene mutations, such as those affecting tumor suppressor genes, contribute to the development and progression of various skin cancer types, including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma3.

Understanding the complex mechanisms driving skin cancer progression has prompted the search for innovative therapeutic strategies. One promising avenue involves exploring natural source. Nigella sativa, a plant from the Ranunculaceae family, has a long history in traditional medicine and has recently gained attention for its potential role in combating cancers, including skin cancer7. Its seeds and oil have been employed to treat a myriad of ailments, including inflammatory and autoimmune disorders. The therapeutic potential of N. sativa is largely attributed to its rich composition of bioactive compounds, notably thymoquinone (TQ), which exhibits a spectrum of biological activities8. The anticancer properties of N. sativa have been substantiated through various studies9,10. Research indicates that TQ exerts cytotoxic effects on multiple cancer cell lines, including those of the skin, by modulating apoptotic pathways and inhibiting proliferation11. Studies suggest that N. sativa’s bioactive compounds, such as thymoquinone, may inhibit cancer progression by inducing oxidative stress and promoting the death of abnormal skin cells12. In the context of skin diseases, a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials assessed the efficacy of N. sativa in treating various skin conditions13. The findings suggested that N. sativa essential oil and extract demonstrated therapeutic benefits, highlighting its potential as an alternative treatment for skin disorders.

Despite these promising findings, the precise molecular mechanisms through which N. sativa exerts its anticancer effects remain incompletely understood. This gap in knowledge underscores the need for advanced analytical approaches to systematically investigate the interactions between N. sativa’s bioactive compounds and cancer-related molecular targets. Network pharmacology emerges as a powerful bioinformatics tool to address this complexity14. By constructing networks that map the interactions between drugs, targets, and diseases, network pharmacology facilitates a holistic understanding of the multifaceted effects of bioactive compounds15. This approach aligns with the paradigm shift from the traditional “one drug, one target” model to a more integrative “multi-target” strategy, which is particularly pertinent in the context of complex diseases like cancer. Applying network pharmacology to study N. sativa involves several key steps. Initially, the identification and characterization of its bioactive constituents are essential. Subsequently, potential molecular targets associated with skin cancer are predicted using databases and computational models. The interactions between these compounds and targets are then mapped to construct a comprehensive network, which can be analyzed to identify critical pathways and biological processes influenced by N. sativa. By combining network pharmacology with laboratory validation, researchers can identify specific pathways influenced by N. sativa, paving the way for targeted therapies against aggressive skin cancers.

Moreover, bioinformatics techniques extend beyond network pharmacology, with molecular docking simulations offering predictions about the binding affinity of N. sativa’s compounds to specific protein targets, thereby revealing their potential efficacy and specificity. Additionally, gene expression profiling and pathway enrichment analyses can elucidate the broader impact of these compounds on cellular functions and disease pathways. In conclusion, the convergence of traditional medicine and modern bioinformatics offers a promising avenue for cancer research. N. sativa, with its rich repertoire of bioactive compounds, presents a valuable resource for developing novel anticancer therapies. The application of network pharmacology and other bioinformatics tools enables a systematic exploration of its therapeutic potential, paving the way for evidence-based integration of herbal medicine into modern oncology16 To fully realize the benefits of N. sativa in the fight against skin cancer, future research should concentrate on verifying these computational predictions through clinical trials and experimental studies.

Methods

Identification of active constituents

A thorough literature review was conducted to find out all the active components of the medicinal plant N. sativa by using Google Scholar and PubMed13,17,18. Furthermore, we used the Indian Medicinal Plants, Photochemistry, and Therapeutics (IMPPAT) database19 with N. sativa as a keyword to compile all medicinally important phytochemicals of N. sativa. PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was utilized retrieving SMILES notations and the 3D structures of the active compounds. To identify probable principal active components, we assessed their oral bioavailability (OB) and drug likeness (DL) values. Oral bioavailability refers to how much of a medicine is absorbed into the bloodstream after oral administration20. The drug-likeness concept was utilized to characterize and refine pharmacokinetic and pharmacological features20. We used OB > 30% and DL index ≥ 0.18 as screening indicators for active constituents. The MolSoft database (https://www.molsof.com/mprop/) was implemented to get the DL value, and SwissADME was used to derive the OB value. Active compounds that met both requirements were chosen for additional analysis.

Target gene identification

Target prediction is a crucial step in examining the cellular mechanism behind the pharmacological activities of N. sativa because of the variety of its ingredients, which allow the herb to target a wide range of proteins. Swiss Target Prediction (https://www.swistargetprediction.ch/) databases were utilized to determine gene targets for active components by using canonical SMILES strings from PubChem. Swiss Target Prediction is a well-respected method for forecasting the protein targets of active ingredients21.

Identification of disease targets

Deciphering the molecular processes by which herbal remedies offer medicinal properties for a range of diseases and conditions requires first identifying the genes linked to disease22. The target proteins linked to skin cancer were gathered from two Databases. GeneCards (www.genecards.org) was the initial source. This is a detailed database of human genes that includes gene analysis transcriptomics, proteomics from other databases23. DisGeNET served as the second source. It offers useful details about illnesses, genes, and associated scientific research24. We used the Venn online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) to combine the gene targets acquired from the two datasets and remove any duplicate targets.

GO, KEGG and functional enrichment analyses

The results obtained for the GO and KEGG pathway analysis by the DAVID25 functional annotation tool. This analysis helped predict the roles of the overlapping target genes in three key biological domains: Molecular Function (MF), Biological Process (BP) and Cellular Component (CC). We utilized the intriguing and common disease targets of the active components as input in the DAVID database to perform the analysis. Official gene symbols were used as IDs and with “Homo sapiens” as the organism. The resulting output provided information about N. Sativa’s targets with regards to skin Cancer. GO analysis can provide uniform descriptions and annotations of genes and their products with an in-depth knowledge of biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. Similarly, KEGG26 is a popular database for analyzing gene functions and genomic data. The top 20 Gene Ontology findings and KEGG target pathways with the highest significance (Benjamini’s value < 0.01) were assessed. clusterProfiler27 and ggplot2 libraries in R were used to perform functional enrichment analysis and to generate bubble maps representing KEGG pathways and GO annotations.

Network construction

The next phase is figuring out the cellular mechanism, which is accomplished by creating a compound-target network with Cytoscape software28. The compound-target network helps in understanding and analyzing the process of the component-target interaction, as well as the pathway associated. In this network, nodes represent the active ingredients and targets, while edges depict their interactions29. The network analyzer function was used to determine the network’s basic properties and to calculate the degree that sheds light on the connectedness of the network and the importance of the connections between the genes, active components, and their associated pathways30.

Hub gene identification and PPI network

The development of macromolecular assemblies and a number of biological processes are heavily influenced by protein interactions. As a result, researching these interactions can provide important information about how drugs work, increasing their efficacy and reducing adverse effects20. STRING is a database that contains all observed and projected protein connections, both chemical and biological. The protein–protein interaction of 303 common genes was examined using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), with “Homo sapiens” as the organism31. The network was analyzed using Cytoscape, an effective tool for network analysis. The CytoHubba plugin that uses maximal clique centrality approach, was employed to detect hub genes and nodes with significant levels within the network32. The presence of the highest degree emphasizes the substantial correlations between the genes under consideration.

Molecular docking analysis

Selected top targets were then further validated by molecular docking. The PDB structures selected targets such as AR (PDB: 1E3G) chain A, CDK4 (PDB: 2W9Z) chain B, EGFR (PDB: 2GS6) chain B, MAPK1 (PDB: 4S31) chain A, and MAPK3 (PDB: 2ZOQ) chain A were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/) whereas the 3D structures of the active compounds (-)-Butyrospermol (CID: 12302182), Gramisterol (CID: 5283640), Cycloeucalenol (CID: 101690), Hederagenin (CID: 73299), Lophenol (CID: 160482), Nigellicine (CID: 11402337) and Obtusifoliol (CID: 65252) were obtained from the PubChem database. Morover, positive controls such as Gefitinib and Palbociclib were used as they are well known against cancer 33,34. Based on the targets’ resolution and structural cruciality for this study, every PDB structure was chosen. A low value in Angstrom (Å) indicate that the structure’s resolution is good and indicates consideration for additional study35. The native ligands for all the structures were removed, the active sites of the target proteins were determined through literature study and DoGSiteScorer36 then receptors were prepared via Chimera X37 such as, removal of the ligand, solvent deletion, correction of bond angles, filling in the missing atoms, and adding charges. For AR, selected binding site was X = 27.7, Y = 26.6, Z = 25.6 in angstroms. For CDK4, selected binding site was X = 21.06, Y = 29.9, Z = 21.8 in angstroms. For EGFR, selected binding site was X = 16.74, Y = 18.5, Z = 20.9 in angstroms. For MAPK1, selected binding site was X = − 11.003, Y = 12.88, Z = 40.04 in angstroms. And for MAPK3, selected binding site was X = 24.08, Y = 21.3, Z = 25.8 in angstroms. Molecular docking between active constituents and targets was performed using PyRx tool38. For best binding post analysis, the best docked score with the lowest binding energy and RMSD was chosen. The PyMol39 and Discovery Studio tools40 were used to analyze and visualize the docked complex as well as to study the binding poses and ligand interactions between active compounds and expected proteins or targets41.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is a powerful computational method used to validate molecular docking results and to explore the atomic-level behavior of biological macromolecules42. In this study, MD simulations were conducted for the top-ranked compounds bound to their respective target receptors using the Desmond module from the Schrödinger software suite. The stability of the resulting protein–ligand complexes was assessed through a standardized workflow involving system preprocessing, energy optimization, and minimization. Each simulation was run for 100 ns using the OPLS_2005 force field43. The complexes were placed in an orthorhombic simulation box with a 10 Å buffer and solvated using the TIP3P water model44. The system was equilibrated under an NPT ensemble at a constant temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 atm. Prior to the production run, all systems underwent a relaxation phase to ensure equilibrium. Trajectories were recorded every 50 picoseconds throughout the simulation. Post-simulation analyses included Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) calculations to evaluate the structural stability and dynamic flexibility of both the protein and ligand during the simulation period.

This analysis identified 110 active constituents. The bioactive compounds were initially evaluated based on their pharmacokinetic properties and ADMET analysis. 13 bioactive compounds fulfilled the filtering criterion (DL ≥ 0.18 and OB ≥ 30). These active compounds were thought to be primarily responsible for the medicinal properties of N. Sativa. Table 1 provides detailed information on these 13 compounds. Cycloeucalenol, Lophenol, Nigellicine Obtusifoliol, Butyrospermol, and Hederagenin were among these substances. Additionally, the SwissTargetPrediction database was used to predict each candidate component’s possible targets. This research found a total of 1300 targets connected to active compounds.

Molecular docking analysis

Following the docking process, stringent selection criteria were applied to identify the most promising drug-like compounds, ensuring optimal receptor-ligand interactions. Notably, a lower binding affinity indicates a stronger receptor-ligand complex. The binding affinities of the selected bioactive compounds from N. sativa were evaluated against key skin cancer-related receptors (AR, CDK4, EGFR, MAPK1, and MAPK3) to identify potential therapeutic interactions. The heatmap analysis revealed that Gramisterol exhibited the strongest binding affinities across most receptors, particularly with EGFR (− 9.1 kcal/mol) with multiple alkyl and pi-alkyl bonds formed by the ligand, and MAPK3 (− 9.1 kcal/mol) with multiple alkyl, pi-alkyl and van der Waals bonding interactions, indicating its potential as a highly stable receptor-ligand complex. Similarly, Lophenol and Cycloeucalenol demonstrated strong binding affinity and interactions with multiple receptors including van der Waals, alkyl, pi-alkyl, and pi-sigma and hydrogen bonds (Figs. 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8), including CDK4 (− 8.3 kcal/mol) and MAPK3 (− 9.0 kcal/mol), suggesting their specificity for these targets. EGFR emerged as the most interactive receptor, with multiple compounds showing affinities below − 8.8 kcal/mol, making it a promising therapeutic target. MAPK3 also shows consistently strong binding across compounds, making it a secondary receptor of interest. In contrast, Hederagenin displayed the weakest binding, particularly with AR (− 4.1 kcal/mol) and CDK4 (− 5.8 kcal/mol) given in Fig. 9, except with EGFR, indicating limited potential for AR, CDK4, MAPK1, and MAPK3. Nigellicine and Butyrospermol showed consistent strong binding affinities with all the receptor ranging from − 5.8 to − 9.0 kcal/mol, indicating their stronger and interactive behavior with these receptors just like the two controls as a standard that have been used, but as it was observed that multiple ligands superseded these controls. These findings highlight Hederagenin with EGFR and Gramisterol with MAPK3 and as the most promising bioactive compounds for further investigation in targeting skin cancer through a network pharmacology approach. All interactions for all receptors and ligands went through molecular docking study can be seen in Figs. 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 while binding affinities of ligands including the given Gefitinib and Palbociclib controls with all the receptors can be seen in Fig. 9.

MD simulations

EFGR-Hederagenin

In EGFR-Hederagenin complex’s, ligand’s root-mean square deviation (RMSD) remained within 12–14 Å, indicating a stable binding conformation within the active site. Such stability suggests that the ligand maintains consistent interactions with key residues, reflecting a well-equilibrated system. After 25 ns, it attained stability in this range. At start, a sudden increase in RMSD to approximately 16 Å may correspond to a conformational adjustment or exploration of alternative binding modes. This behavior is not uncommon and can result from the ligand adapting to the dynamic environment of the binding pocket or transiently interacting with flexible regions of the protein. Similar observations have been reported in MD simulations, where ligands exhibit temporary deviations before re-establishing stable interactions. The RMSD stabilizes around 12–13 Å, suggesting that the ligand has settled into a new, albeit slightly different, binding conformation. This plateau indicates that the system has reached a semi-stable state, maintaining consistent interactions in this new configuration (Fig. 10A). The root-mean square fluctuation (RMSF) plots revealed that most of the residues did not show many fluctuations during simulation as the RMSF values were lower than 4 Å, throughout the simulation indicating that the ligands did not exert the fluctuations in the protein. Elevated flexibility is observed at the terminal regions and around residue indices ~ 55 and ~ 240 reaching around 7 Å, which are typically associated with loop or unstructured regions, rather than the active or binding site suggesting increased flexibility in these regions (Fig. 10B). According to the RMSF analysis, the protein–ligand complex exhibited overall stability, as most residues maintained a rigid conformation. The interactions among the atoms of protein and ligands were observed during the simulation and it was observed that the main interactions involved hydrogen bonding, water bridges, and hydrophobic interactions. These contacts play an important role in protein–ligand complex stability during the simulation. The major hydrogen bond forming residues in EGFR-Hederagenin complex were VAL34, SER28, ARG307, and ASP311 (Fig. 10C). Figure 10D shows the 2D interactions of ligand with the receptor EGFR highlighting the interactions of LEU26, PHE31, VAL34, ALA51, LYS53, THR98, LEU100, ALA308, GLN328, GLN329, GLY330, and TYR331 with consistency from to docking analysis to MD simulations.

Such stability suggests that the ligand maintains consistent interactions with key residues, reflecting a well-equilibrated system. After initiation, it attained stability in this range. Similar observations have been reported in MD simulations, where ligands exhibit temporary deviations before re-establishing stable interactions. The RMSD stabilizes around 5 Å, suggesting that the ligand has settled into a new, albeit slightly different, binding conformation and stayed in this state throughout the 100 ns simulation. This plateau indicates that the system has reached a semi-stable state, maintaining consistent interactions in this new configuration (Fig. 11A). RMSF plots revealed that most of the residues did not show many fluctuations during simulation as the RMSF values were lower than 4 Å similar like the EFGR complex, throughout the simulation indicating that the ligands did not exert the fluctuations in the protein. Elevated flexibility is observed at the terminal regions and around residue indices ~ 250 reaching around 5 Å, which are typically associated with loop or unstructured regions, rather than the active or binding site suggesting increased flexibility in these regions (Fig. 11B). According to the RMSF analysis, the protein–ligand complex exhibited overall stability, as most residues maintained a rigid conformation. The interactions among the atoms of protein and ligands were observed during the simulation and it was observed that the main interactions involved mostly in hydrophobic interactions. These contacts play an important role in protein–ligand complex stability during the simulation. The major hydrophobic bond forming residues in MAPK3-Gramisterol complex were ILE51, ALA55, VAL59, ALA72, and LEU176 (Fig. 11C). Figure 11D shows the 2D interactions of ligand with the receptor EGFR highlighting the interactions of ILE51, GLU53, GLY54, ALA55, VAL59, ALA72, LYS74, ILE104, GLN125, ASP126, LE127, MET128, SER173, ASN174, LEU176, and ASP187 with consistency from to docking analysis to MD simulations.

Discussion

Skin cancer remains one of the most pervasive malignancies worldwide, representing a significant public health challenge due to its high incidence and potential lethality. The disease, which primarily manifests as basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma, is driven by a complex interplay of genetic mutations, environmental exposures, and aberrant cellular signaling pathways. Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is well documented to induce DNA damage that can precipitate oncogenic mutations, particularly in genes that regulate cell proliferation and apoptosis5. In recent years, the increasing incidence of skin cancer especially melanoma, which accounts for the majority of skin cancer deaths has spurred a search for more effective, less toxic therapeutic strategies. Traditional treatments, while often effective in early-stage disease, can fall short when dealing with advanced or resistant forms of the malignancy. This scenario has led researchers to explore novel modalities, including the use of bioactive natural products. Among these, Nigella sativa has emerged as a promising candidate due to its long-standing use in traditional medicine and its array of bioactive constituents, notably thymoquinone, which have been reported to exert significant anticancer effects8,9.

The integration of network pharmacology into cancer research has revolutionized our understanding of the multi-targeted effects of natural compounds. Unlike the conventional “one drug, one target” approach, network pharmacology offers a systems-level perspective that captures the complexity of molecular interactions underpinning disease pathogenesis. In the context of N. sativa, this method enables the systematic mapping of its bioactive compounds onto a network of potential protein targets, thereby illuminating the molecular mechanisms through which these compounds may exert their therapeutic effects. Recent studies have demonstrated that network-based approaches can successfully identify critical signaling nodes such as those within the PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and JAK/STAT pathways that are often dysregulated in skin cancer14.

Our study applied a robust computational framework to evaluate the anticancer potential of N. sativa against skin cancer. Through extensive literature mining and database searches, 13 active compounds were identified based on favorable pharmacokinetic parameters, including high oral bioavailability and drug-likeness indices. These compounds were further subjected to target prediction analysis using state-of-the-art bioinformatics tools, resulting in the identification of over 1300 potential targets. Subsequent integration with skin cancer-associated genes compiled from reputable sources such as GeneCards and DisGeNET yielded a subset of 303 intersecting targets. This large-scale target mapping underscores the polypharmacological nature of N. sativa, suggesting that its anticancer activity may be attributed to a synergistic modulation of multiple pathways45. Notably, the protein–protein interaction network analysis revealed several hub genes, including AKT1, EGFR, MAPK3, and CDK4, which are critically involved in the regulation of cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis11. These findings are in line with previous research, which has identified these proteins as key drivers in the progression and metastasis of skin cancers4.

The compound-target interaction network revealed that the most connected bioactive compounds exhibited high degrees of interaction with critical skin cancer-associated targets, indicating their potential therapeutic importance. Hub genes identified through PPI analysis, including AKT1, IL6, SRC, EGFR, MAPK3, MAPK1, PTGS2, CDK4, and AR, play crucial roles in oncogenic signaling pathways associated with skin cancer progression, survival, and metastasis46–49. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that N. sativa’s active compounds modulate essential oncogenic pathways such as PI3K-AKT signaling, JAK-STAT signaling, MAPK signaling, and apoptosis regulation, all of which are implicated in tumorigenesis and cancer cell survival. These findings provide mechanistic insights into how N. sativa exerts its anti-cancer effects through a poly-pharmacological approach50.

The convergence of these computational analyses spanning network pharmacology, GO/KEGG enrichment, and molecular docking offers a comprehensive depiction of how N. sativa compounds may mediate anticancer effects. The multi-target binding profile, especially the strong interactions observed for compound CID: 5283640, supports the hypothesis that N. sativa exerts its therapeutic effects through simultaneous modulation of several pivotal signaling pathways. This poly-pharmacological approach is particularly advantageous in the context of complex diseases such as skin cancer, where single-target therapies often fail due to compensatory mechanisms and resistance.

The selection of AR, CDK4, EGFR, MAPK1, and MAPK3 from the identified hub genes for molecular docking was based on their well-documented roles in skin cancer progression and their high connectivity (degree score) in the PPI network. EGFR is a key player in epidermal proliferation and tumor growth, frequently overexpressed in skin cancers46. MAPK1 and MAPK3 are central to the MAPK/ERK pathway, implicated in melanoma proliferation and survival51. CDK4 is a crucial regulator of the cell cycle, and its dysregulation is a hallmark of melanoma52. AR (androgen receptor), though classically associated with prostate cancer, has been increasingly recognized for its role in melanoma resistance and tumor progression48.

The molecular docking studies further reinforced the multi-targeted potential of N. sativa. Among the seven bioactive compounds evaluated, the docking results provided compelling insights into the interaction dynamics with key skin cancer-related receptors (Fig. 9). For instance, Gramisterol exhibited robust binding with multiple targets, as evidenced by a binding energy of − 7.4 kcal/mol for the androgen receptor (AR), − 9.0 kcal/mol for CDK4, − 9.1 kcal/mol for EGFR, − 8.2 kcal/mol for MAPK1, and − 9.1 kcal/mol for MAPK3. These values indicate a strong and stable receptor–ligand interaction, especially with EGFR and MAPK3, which are critical drivers of tumor proliferation and survival51,53. In contrast, Cycloeucalenol demonstrated binding energies of − 7.2 kcal/mol (AR), − 7.8 kcal/mol (CDK4), − 8.8 kcal/mol (EGFR), − 7.8 kcal/mol (MAPK1), and − 9.0 kcal/mol (MAPK3), suggesting that while it is effective, its overall binding profile is slightly less potent compared to Gramisterol and indicating its potential to interfere with EGFR-driven tumor growth and MAPK3-mediated signaling cascades. Compound Lophenol, with a binding energy of − 6.3 kcal/mol (AR), − 8.3 kcal/mol (CDK4), − 8.8 kcal/mol (EGFR), − 8.0 kcal/mol (MAPK1), and − 9.0 kcal/mol (MAPK3), further corroborates the trend observed with Gramisterol, particularly in terms of MAPK3 interaction. These interactions imply that Lophenol may effectively inhibit CDK4-mediated cell cycle progression and MAPK3-related signaling pathways, both of which are pivotal in skin cancer development52. Interestingly, Hederagenin, despite exhibiting a very strong binding to EGFR at − 9.9 kcal/mol, showed considerably weaker interactions with AR (− 4.1 kcal/mol) and CDK4 (− 5.8 kcal/mol), which may compromise its overall therapeutic utility. Compounds Nigellicine and Butyrospermol demonstrated intermediate binding affinities, with values such as − 7.6 kcal/mol (AR) and − 9.0 kcal/mol (EGFR) for Butyrospermol, highlighting a balanced yet less pronounced interaction profile compared to the top performers. Obtusifoliol demonstrated notable binding affinities across several key protein targets associated with skin cancer. Specifically, it exhibited binding energies of − 7.1 kcal/mol with the Androgen Receptor (AR), − 8.2 kcal/mol with Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 4 (CDK4), − 8.7 kcal/mol with Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), − 7.5 kcal/mol with Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase 1 (MAPK1), and − 8.9 kcal/mol with Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase 3 (MAPK3). These interactions suggest that Obtusifoliol may effectively inhibit multiple signaling pathways critical for skin cancer cell proliferation and survival, positioning it as a promising multi-target therapeutic candidate in skin cancer treatment as reported by studies54. Comparisons with benchmark inhibitors gefitinib and palbociclib underscore the biological relevance of our docking results. Compounds from N. sativa showing comparable or better affinities suggest promising potential for EGFR and MAPK3 targeting.

The MD simulations conducted for the EGFR-Hederagenin and MAPK3-Gramisterol complexes provide critical kinetic validation of the docking and network pharmacology findings, reinforcing the structural and temporal stability of these receptor-ligand interactions. Notably, both systems reached a stable binding conformation during the 100 ns simulations, with MAPK3-Gramisterol demonstrating superior stability as indicated by lower RMSD values (4–6 Å) compared to the EGFR–Hederagenin complex (12–14 Å). The lower and more consistent RMSD of Gramisterol, along with minimal residue fluctuation (RMSF < 4 Å for most regions), emphasizes its robustness in occupying the MAPK3 binding pocket with sustained interactions throughout the simulation. These kinetic properties underscore its potential as a lead compound capable of withstanding dynamic intracellular conditions. Importantly, the stability of key interactions identified in docking particularly hydrophobic contacts in the MAPK3 Gramisterol complex and hydrogen bonding in EGFR–Hederagenin were retained throughout the simulations. Residues such as LEU176, VAL59, and ALA72 in MAPK3 and SER28, VAL34, and ASP311 in EGFR remained persistently involved in these interactions, reflecting the precision and reliability of our earlier in silico predictions. This alignment of docking and MD simulation data serves as a two-layered validation of ligand-receptor compatibility and target engagement, a critical prerequisite for downstream in vitro or in vivo translation.

What distinguishes this study is the convergence of multi-scale computational methods network pharmacology, molecular docking, and MD simulations into a unified workflow that collectively identifies, validates, and deconstructs the molecular mechanisms underlying Nigella sativa’s anticancer effects. The identification of EGFR and MAPK3 as central signaling nodes within our network, followed by their structural confirmation as high-affinity and kinetically favorable binding sites, positions them as viable druggable targets for skin cancer therapy. Notably, these two pathways regulate pivotal processes in melanoma progression, including proliferation, angiogenesis, and apoptosis resistance, and are frequently deregulated in therapy-resistant forms of the disease. Thus, the dynamic stability of EGFR-Hederagenin and MAPK3-Gramisterol complexes not only corroborates their therapeutic potential but also highlights the feasibility of a dual-targeting approach. This may enable the simultaneous disruption of upstream receptor tyrosine kinase signaling (EGFR) and downstream MAPK cascade modulation (MAPK3), a strategy particularly relevant in overcoming compensatory feedback loops often implicated in targeted therapy resistance.

This study integrates network pharmacology and molecular docking with MD simulations in a complementary manner to predict and validate the therapeutic efficacy of N. sativa’s bioactive compounds. The network pharmacology approach helped to identify 303 overlapping gene targets involved in skin cancer, with hub genes such as EGFR, MAPK3, MAPK1, CDK4, and AR standing out based on topological degree and pathway involvement. Molecular docking and MD simulations were then employed to validate these key interactions at the structural level. The observed strong binding affinities of compounds like Gramisterol and Cycloeucalenol to these targets confirm the potential functional relevance of network-derived predictions. Thus, the docking results serve as an orthogonal validation of the predicted compound-target-pathway associations, strengthening the mechanistic insight offered by the network pharmacology framework.

In conclusion, our findings do not merely propose Nigella sativa as a source of therapeutic agents they establish a systems-level blueprint for its application in modern oncology. The observed receptor-specific, time-stable interactions, when viewed against the backdrop of enriched KEGG pathways and hub gene topologies, argue for the ultimate therapeutic potential of N. sativa’s bioactive constituents in managing and potentially reversing skin cancer pathogenesis. In light of these findings, future experimental validation is imperative. In vitro and in vivo studies should be designed to assess the cytotoxicity, pharmacokinetics, and combinatorial efficacy of these compounds with existing chemotherapeutic agents. The promising binding affinities observed in our study provide a strong rationale for such investigations, which could ultimately pave the way for the development of novel, plant-derived therapeutic regimens with improved efficacy and reduced side effects. Moreover, the insights garnered from the GO and KEGG analyses can guide the selection of biomarkers and the design of targeted assays to further elucidate the molecular underpinnings of N. sativa’s anticancer activity.

Conclusion

In summary, our integrative analysis underscores the potential of N. sativa as a multifaceted therapeutic agent against skin cancer. The compelling docking analysis findings, particularly for CID: 5283640, CID: 160482, and CID: 101690 combined with the enriched oncogenic pathways identified through GO and KEGG analyses, highlight a promising multi-targeted approach that could overcome the limitations of conventional therapies. While the computational predictions are robust, the translation of these findings into clinical practice will require rigorous experimental validation and a multidisciplinary effort to optimize formulation and delivery. This study not only reinforces the therapeutic promise of traditional herbal medicine in modern oncology but also lays a solid foundation for future translational research aimed at harnessing the full potential of N. sativa in the fight against skin cancer.
