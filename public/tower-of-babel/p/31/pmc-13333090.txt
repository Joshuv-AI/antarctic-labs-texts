Antibiotic non-susceptibility associated serotypes of invasive pneumococcal disease - a nationwide population study from Switzerland, 2012–2022
Authors: Nicolaj Just, Carlo Casanova, Florent Baty, Werner C Albrich, Markus Hilty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIt has been reported that the introduction of thirteen-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) had an influence on antibiotic resistance rates of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD). Recent data about antibiotic resistance in IPD are scarce. The aim of the current study was to analyse the impact of PCV13 introduction and COVID-19 pandemic on antibiotic resistance in IPD and the effects of antibiotic resistance on mortality. Furthermore, we correlate vaccine and non-vaccine serotypes with antibiotic resistance.MethodsWe included 8747 IPD cases from a Swiss nationwide IPD surveillance database for 2012–2022. Regression analyses were performed to examine significant trends over time and to identify serotypes and patient characteristics associated with antibiotic resistance.ResultsThe proportion of non-susceptibility to erythromycin (IRR: 0.9, p < 0.001) and cotrimoxazole (IRR: 0.9, p < 0.001) decreased from 2012 to 2022. Penicillin non-susceptibility remained constant with a dip in 2022 (2012: 9.2%, 2022: 4.7%). PCV13 serotypes 6B, 9V, 14, 19A and 19F were significantly associated with non-susceptibility while the non-PCV13 serotype 15A, 23B, 24/F and 6C were associated with resistance to at least two antibiotic groups. Antibiotic non-susceptibility was not associated with death but meningitis was associated with penicillin non-susceptibility.ConclusionsAntibiotic resistance in IPD decreased over time without increases during the COVID-19 pandemic, while a strong association between serotypes and antibiotic resistance persists. Introduction of vaccines with extended serotype coverage may further affect antibiotic resistance rates underlining the importance of continued surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41479-026-00203-4.Keywords: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Antibiotic resistance, Penicillin non-susceptibility, Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs)

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) causes severe clinical manifestations such as invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) with a relevant 30-day case fatality risk (CFR) of 8–30% [1, 2]. One of the most important virulence factors is the pneumococcal polysaccharide capsule which determines the currently more than 100 known different serotypes [3].

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) have been introduced globally, which generally led to a decrease in IPD incidence. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the incidence of IPD decreased even more but this transient effect has already been reversed after loosening of containment measures [4, 5]. However, due to the limited coverage among the currently more than 100 known different serotypes, replacement of the introduced vaccines serotype has abolished some of these benefits. Therefore, the pneumococcal incidence is still high and increasingly due non-vaccine serotypes despite the usage of PCVs [6, 7].

A recent study from 2016 to 2019 demonstrated high proportions of antibiotic non-susceptibility in non-bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia and sinusitis in US adults [8]. Antibiotic resistance data limited to sterile-site isolates is often biased towards individuals attending tertiary care facilities and, therefore, numbers may largely differ if compared to community-based studies [9].

Nonetheless, resistance monitoring of IPD requires close monitoring. A systematic review of antimicrobial resistance from IPD in Latin American countries revealed that approximately one-quarter of IPD isolates displayed penicillin resistance, with higher rates in young children [10].

In addition, several resistant vaccine-serotypes such as 6A, 6B, 9V, 14, 19A, 19F and 23F have been detected for many years even prior to the introduction of PCVs [11]. In 2003, an increased proportion of multiple resistance was predicted, especially to beta-lactams and macrolides [12]. However, in Switzerland there was a decrease in proportions of resistance from 2004 to 2014 with more resistant serotypes disappearing simultaneously with the introduction of pediatric pneumococcal vaccination while some new, non-vaccine serotypes associated with antibiotic resistance such as 6C and 15 emerged [13]. Before the introduction of PCVs, patterns of geographic variation of antibiotic resistance in pneumococcus were best explained by the intensity of selection for resistance, which is reflected by differences between the proportions of resistance within individual serotypes, rather than by differences between the frequencies of particular serotypes [12]. These reports underline the importance of continuous follow-up studies.

In Switzerland, PCV13 was introduced as a 2 + 1 schedule for all children under 5 years of age in 2011 [14] and for adults with risk factors for IPD PCV13 replaced PPSV23 as the only recommended vaccine in 2014. No higher-valency PCVs were introduced in Switzerland during the study period. While pediatric coverage has been high and slowly increasing (3 dose coverage for those under 2 years of 89% and at least 1 dose of 93% during 2020–2022 [15]), adult vaccination coverage remains very underutilized and likely creates no serotype selection pressure. Only 4.5% of adults between 18 and 85 years had received a pneumococcal vaccine according to a 2020 nationwide cross-sectional survey without regional differences with 45% having received PCV13 and 46% having received PPSV23 [16].

From 2019 to 2020, i.e. the early COVID-19 pandemic, most European countries reported a decreased IPD incidence but an increase in antibiotic resistance among invasive pneumococcal isolates [17]. It is unclear, whether antibiotic resistance has increased, decreased or remained stable in recent years. Earlier, we reported an ongoing impact after pediatric PCV13 introduction and large but only transient and age-specific effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on IPD epidemiology and high CFR in patients with PCV13 serotypes [18]. Here, we analysed antibiotic non-susceptibility trends within IPD cases, serotype-specific antibiotic resistance and association of clinical risk factors and outcomes with antibiotic resistance in a nationwide study.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, we retrospectively analysed IPD data collected through the national public health surveillance system in Switzerland. IPD is a notifiable disease in Switzerland. Laboratories that detect pneumococcus from normally sterile sites send the pneumococcal isolates to the National Center for Invasive Pneumococci for serotyping and antibiotic resistance testing. All laboratory results are reported to the Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH). In addition, the patient’s clinical information is reported to FOPH by the physicians. The FOPH then combines the laboratory data with the corresponding clinical information. The data used for this study originate from the nationwide IPD database from 2012 to 2022 and was provided anonymised by the FOPH. This analysis uses de-identified public health surveillance data collected under the epidemics act and does not require approval by an ethics board, in accordance with the Federal Act on Research involving Human Beings.

Antibiotic resistance testing of invasive pneumococcal disease isolates

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed by disc diffusion for oxacillin, erythromycin, cotrimoxazole and levofloxacin. Isolates susceptible to oxacillin were interpreted as susceptible to penicillin (all indications including meningitis) and ceftriaxone. For oxacillin non-susceptible isolates, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for penicillin and ceftriaxone was determined by gradient tests. We used meningitis criteria to define penicillin and ceftriaxone non-susceptibility (MIC > 0.06 mg/L and > 0.5 mg/L, respectively). For erythromycin and cotrimoxazole, “intermediate” (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute, CLSI), “susceptible, increased exposure” (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, EUCAST) and “resistant” results were categorized as “non-susceptible”. For levofloxacin, only “resistant” results were categorized non-susceptible, as EUCAST defines wild-type organisms as “susceptible, increased exposure”. During the study period there was a change in the AST procedure. Until 2017 AST was performed and interpreted according to CLSI guidelines, since 2018 according to EUCAST. The overall categoric agreement for 590 isolates tested in parallel with both guidelines was 99.2% (all antibiotics). We can therefore exclude a major influence of the methodology change on the data interpretation. Of note, in 2019 EUCAST changed the interpretive criteria for cotrimoxazole disk diffusion, from 18 mm to 13 mm zone diameter for cotrimoxazole susceptibility.

Data preparation

We received only anonymized data. In this retrospective analysis we focused on antibiotic resistance, clinical manifestation and risk factors i.e. comorbidities combined with serotype related analysis. Serotypes were examined in groups as covered by different pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV13, PCV15, PCV20 and non-vaccine serotypes (NVT), i.e. not covered by PCV20) or individually by serotype. Mutually exclusive serotype groups were PCV13, PCV15-non-PCV13, PCV20-non-PCV15 and NVT. All PCV20 serotypes were used in the single serotype analysis and explored separately except for serotype 15B, which was combined with 15C as 15B/C. If the overall proportion was above 0.5% for a serotype, NVT serotypes were examined individually, otherwise grouped as other (non-typable, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 15 F, 18, 18F, 19 [includes e.g. 19B and 19C but not 19A and 19F)], 2, 21, 22, 25, 27, 28, 29, 33, 33A, 34, 35, 36, 37, 39, 41, 42, 6, 6D, and 9). Non-vaccine serotype groups 16 and 24 also were grouped together, respectively, as separate serotyping started only in 2019 and were named 16/F (16, 16F) and 24/F (24, 24F). The clinical information of the IPD cases includes five different manifestations listed in our dataset (pneumonia, bacteremia without focus, meningitis and arthritis) or a free text field (“other manifestations”), where the exact disease was specified. We categorized all IPD cases into one to four mutually exclusive manifestation groups: pneumonia (irrespective of other organ manifestations, but without meningitis); meningitis (irrespective of other organ manifestations); bacteremia without focus; other (i.e. not included in the first three groups). Information on the following 13 risk factors is routinely collected through the surveillance system: none known, chronic kidney disease, immunosuppression, recurrent lung disease, recurrent otitis, splenectomy, nephrotic syndrome, skull base fracture, chronic lung disease, functional asplenia/hemoglobinopathy, diabetes mellitus, liquor fistula and other. Each of these were reported as “yes” or left blank (if absent or unknown). If information on all risk factors was missing, we interpreted it as no risk factor present. In addition, using principal component analysis (PCA), multi-collinearity between chronic kidney disease and diabetes mellitus, and chronic with recurrent lung disease were identified, and two risk clusters based on the first 2 principal components were created: chronic kidney disease and/or diabetes mellitus; recurrent and/or chronic lung disease. The other risk factors were examined separately. Death was reported with either “yes” or an empty field, which could mean no death or unknown. Serotyping was performed by Quellung reaction using specific antisera [18].

Statistical analysis

We calculated incidence rates of IPD with Swiss population data from the Federal Statistical Office. To analyse trend over time, Poisson regression was used to estimate the incidence rate ratios (IRR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Association analysis was performed using multivariate logistic regression of antibiotic non-susceptibility with certain serotypes (susceptible as reference) or the outcomes of CFR and antibiotic non-susceptibility with certain risk factors (no death or susceptible as reference). Regression was adjusted by age, manifestation and the first principal component (PC1). We calculated PC1 with the PCA-analysis as an approximation of level of comorbidity. Furthermore, we added logistic regression of antibiotic non-susceptibility with serotypes stratified for age groups 17–64 and 65 + years and for time periods 2012–2016 and 2017–2022. Differences in proportions of antibiotic non-susceptibility in different age groups were calculated with 3 × 2 Pearson’s Chi-squared test. All analyses were done using the R statistical software (v. 4.2.1).

Results

Trends in antibiotic non-susceptibility

Overall, 8747 IPD cases were included of which 7833 cases had a reported antibiogram. To answer the question if non-susceptibility has generally decreased over time, we first noted that incidences of both, antibiotic susceptible and non-susceptible IPD declined from 2012 to 2022 (Fig. 1; Table 1). The proportion of non-susceptibility to penicillin remained constant with a dip in 2022 (2012: 9.2%, 2022: 4.7%) (Fig. 1A; Table 1). Only 33 cases fulfilled the non-meningitis criteria for penicillin resistance according to EUCAST 2025 (v.15.0) (MIC > 1.0 mg/L). Importantly, 44 pneumococcal isolates showed MIC > 0.5 mg/L to ceftriaxone (Fig. 1B; Table 1) of which 43 isolates belonged to the PCV20 serotypes. The proportions of non-susceptibility to erythromycin (IRR: 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9-1.0, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C; Table 1) and cotrimoxazole (IRR: 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9–0.9, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1D; Table 1) decreased in the study period. We next analysed the trend in proportion of antibiotic non-susceptibility of isolates by serotype group PCV13, PCV15 and PCV20 serotype groups (Supplementary Table 1). For PCV13 and PCV15 serotype groups, the proportions of non-susceptibility significantly decreased for cotrimoxazole (IRR: 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9-1.0 for PCV13 and IRR: 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9-1.0 for PCV15 serotypes) but not for penicillin, erythromycin and ceftriaxone (Supplementary Table 1). Interestingly, proportions of non-susceptibilities to penicillin, erythromycin and cotrimoxazole decreased significantly for PCV20 serotypes (Supplementary Table 1).

Association between antibiotic non-susceptibility and single serotypes

Next, we performed a multivariate logistic regression to analyse the association between single serotypes and antibiotic non-susceptibility to penicillin, ceftriaxone, erythromycin and cotrimoxazole. Among the PCV13 serotypes, 6B, 9V, 14, 19A and 19F were associated with penicillin non-susceptibility as indicated by the odds ratios for the period 2012–2022 (Fig. 2A; black values). Among these five serotypes, serotypes 14 (OR: 10.8, 95% CI: 4.8–22.0), 19A (OR: 6.5, 95% CI: 3.2–12.5) and 19F (OR: 27.7, 95% CI: 13.7–53.7) were also associated with non-susceptibility to ceftriaxone (Fig. 2B). Serotype 33F was associated with non-susceptibility exclusively to erythromycin (Fig. 2C) (OR: 11.1, 95% CI: 7.4–16.6). Serotypes 18C (OR: 5.6, 95% CI: 3.2–9.5), 23F (OR: 3.8, 95% CI: 2.2–6.4), 11A (OR: 1.8, 95% CI: 1.1–2.7), 12F (OR: 2.6, 95% CI: 1.8–3.7) and 15B/C (OR: 3.7, 95% CI: 2.5–5.2) were associated with cotrimoxazole non-susceptibility (Fig. 2D). The two most frequent serotypes 3 and 8 were susceptible to all tested antibiotics (Fig. 2). PCV13 serotypes 6B, 9V, 14, 19A and 19F were significantly associated with non-susceptibility to three antibiotics i.e. penicillin, cotrimoxazole and erythromycin (OR and 95% CI > 1.0) for the whole study duration (2012–2022) (Fig. 2E).

Among non-PCV20 serotypes, there was no serotype associated with non-susceptibility to all four antibiotic groups but several were associated with at least two groups such as serotype 15A, 23B, 24/F and 6C (Fig. 3). Serotype 23B was associated with non-susceptibility to penicillin (OR: 11.1, 95% CI: 8.2–14.9) (Fig. 3A) and cotrimoxazole (OR: 4.1, 95% CI: 3.0-5.6) (Fig. 3C). Serotypes 15A and 6C were significantly associated with non-susceptibility to penicillin and erythromycin (Fig. 3). Serotype 24/F was associated with multi-non-susceptibility (Fig. 3D). Significant association with exclusively non-susceptibility towards penicillin was found for serotype 35B (OR: 4.1, 95% CI: 2.2–7.1) (Fig. 3A).

As occasional serotype switch events may confound serotype-associated non-susceptibility over a long-time duration, we then redid the analyses using data from 2012 to 2016 as compared to 2017–2022. Interestingly, we found that the analysis of non-susceptibility and serotypes was non-different/consistent with a few exceptions (Figs. 2 and 3). Association of non-susceptibility to the three antibiotics penicillin, erythromycin and cotrimoxazole, with serotype 6B and 9V was higher in the 2017–2022, compared to the 2012–2016 period (Fig. 2A, C and D) with similar trends for serotype 23B (except for erythromycin) and 24F (Fig. 3). Association of cotrimoxazole non-susceptibility with PCV20 serotypes 11A, 12F and 15B/C was lower in the second period (Fig. 2). Also, non-PCV20 serotype 15A had lower association with antibiotic non-susceptibilities in the second period (Fig. 3).

Association of antibiotic non-susceptibility, risk factors and manifestations with death

Overall, antibiotic non-susceptibility was not found significantly associated with death (Supplementary Fig. 3A). The following risk factors were associated with death: chronic kidney disease/diabetes mellitus (OR: 1.3, 95% CI: 1.1–1.5), immunosuppression (OR: 1.5, 95% CI: 1.3–1.8), splenectomy (OR: 1.9, 95% CI: 1.1-3.0), functional asplenia/hemoglobinopathy (OR: 2.7, 95% CI: 1.2-5.0) and other (OR: 2.2, 95% CI: 1.8–2.6) (Supplementary Fig. 3B). None of these risk factors increased in proportion over time (Supplementary Table 2). As for manifestations, bacteremia without focus and meningitis were associated with death, pneumonia (without meningitis) with survival (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Association of antibiotic non-susceptibility with age, risk factors and manifestations

The proportion of antibiotic non-susceptibility was higher in the 0–16 year age groups (Table 2), mainly for penicillin (MIC > 0.06 mg/L; P = 0.06) and cotrimoxazole (P = 0.03). Cotrimoxazole, erythromycin and penicillin were the antibiotics with highest non-susceptibility (Table 2). Antibiotic non-susceptibility was comparable in the 17–64 and 65 + age groups overall (Table 2) and for most serotypes. However, odds ratios of antibiotic non-susceptibility in serotypes were similar in both age groups 17–64 and 65+ (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

Our study had several important findings. The introduction of PCVs has ongoing effects on serotype distribution and is simultaneously beneficial in terms of decreases of antibiotic resistance, which was further lowered during the COVID-19 pandemic. Several PCV13 serotypes but also non-PCV-20 serotypes were associated with antibiotic non-susceptibility. Meningitis was associated with penicillin non-susceptibility. In turn, antibiotic non-susceptibility was not associated with an increased CFR, in contrast to bacteremia without focus and meningitis.

As for the investigated antibiotics, we found that the resistance to cotrimoxazole was most frequently observed followed by erythromycin and penicillin. Similarly, analysing 15 400 cases of IPD in England from 2017 to 2020, resistance to cotrimoxazole was the most frequently observed resistance (n = 2331 [17%]), followed by resistance to tetracycline (n = 1199 [8·7%]) and β-lactams (n = 1149 [8·4%]) [19]. Globally antimicrobial resistance to penicillin, ceftriaxone and cotrimoxazole has significantly decreased after PCV introduction [20], which is confirmed in our Swiss surveillance. We also detected a further decrease in penicillin resistance during 2022.

Ceftriaxone resistance was generally very low in our setting and, therefore, it is of interest to note that it has been found that in patients with meningitis, a ceftriaxone total daily dose of 2 g may be associated with similar outcomes to a 4 g total daily dose, provided that the causative organism is highly susceptible to ceftriaxone [21]. Of note, in 2019 EUCAST changed the interpretive criteria for cotrimoxazole disk diffusion, which could have led to an artificial decrease in the resistance rate as roughly 2.5% of isolates would have been assigned as being resistant using the ‘old guidelines’. However, as the resistance rate did not change from 2019 to 2020, but rather continued to decrease after 2020 the decrease is anticipated to be real and not due to the breakpoint change.

The association between pneumococcal serotypes and antibiotic resistance has been well studied. Pediatric serotypes, responsible for the majority of childhood IPD and, at the same time, being disproportionately associated with antibiotic resistance were chosen to be included in the initial PCV7 [22]. As shown in our dataset these serotypes are still associated with antibiotic resistance in Switzerland. Mathematical models suggested the selection of resistant non-PCV7 serotypes such as serotype 19A after the introduction of pediatric PCVs [23]. This was confirmed repeatedly in real-life data for non-vaccine serotypes after PCV7 and PCV13 introduction [24–27]. Interestingly, increases over time in non-susceptibility after PCV13 introduction were most common in both PCV13 serotypes (6B, 9V, 14, 19F, and 23F), and the non-vaccine serotypes 23B and 24F, as also previously reported, but slightly different from another meta-analysis (which included increases of the non-vaccine serotypes 6A, 6C, 15A, 15B/C, 19A and 35B) [28–30]. As for other non-vaccine serotypes, 15A and 6C were significantly associated with non-susceptibility to all reported antibiotics except cotrimoxazole while 35B was found to be only associated with resistance to penicillin. Similarly, antimicrobial resistance of S. pneumoniae isolated from children in Japan, revealed that the three most common non-PCV13/PCV20 serotypes 15A, 23A, and 35B, exhibited high prevalences of penicillin non-susceptibility (≥ 89.5%) and multidrug resistance (≥ 81.5%) [31]. Important to note, the more resistant non-PCV13 serotypes are generally still less common than the more susceptible serotypes 8 and 22F in our setting [18]. Therefore, the fact that vaccine serotypes are more often associated with antibiotic resistance as compared to non-vaccine serotypes, is probably the main driver for the overall reduction of antibiotic resistance in our study, as recently reported. This is not unique to our country as e.g. declines in antimicrobial resistant IPD were also shown in South Africa eight years after the introduction of PCV13 [32], and reported from Spain and Belgium and by a systematic review of studies reporting antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of paediatric pneumococcal isolates between 2000 and 2020 [20, 33, 34]. Another recent systematic review on invasive and non-invasive pneumococcal disease in adults in Europe identified serotypes 11A, 15B and 33F as frequently antibiotic resistant during 2010 to 2022. Serotype 11A was the only serotype which showed an increasing proportion of penicillin resistance [35] while serotype 33F was particularly resistant against macrolides in both our data and Spanish pediatric IPD [33, 35].

There are only few reports on pneumococcal antibiotic resistance beyond the first COVID-19 pandemic year, yet. In Japan penicillin and cephalosporin resistance decreased [36], while Argentina did not see consistent tends [37]. In Spanish children with IPD, the trend of decreasing incidence and stable proportion of strains with any antibiotic resistance and resistance against individual antibiotics (e.g. penicillin, erythromycin, cefotaxime) was reversed after lifting of pandemic measures with an increase of resistant IPD [33]. These were due to increases of PCV13 serotype 19F, of PCV20 serotype 11A and of non-PCV13 serotypes 24F, 23B, 15A, and 16F with serotypes 19F and 11A demonstrating rising proportions of antibiotic resistance post-pandemic [33]. Very recently serotype 38, which is associated with only little resistance, has emerged in Germany and Poland, but was not frequent in the Netherlands and in Switzerland [18, 38]. In France, during 2009–2021, data collected by the French Regional Pneumococcal Observatories Network highlighted an increase of invasive PNSPs in children and non-vaccine serotypes [39]. A current study from Italy reported increases of serotype 8 as a cause of IPD between 2007 and 2023, which is the second most frequent after serotype 3, albeit with little antibiotic resistance. Most resistance was detected in only a few serotypes (14, 15A and 19A) all of which did not increase in frequency over time [40].

We also included mortality data in our study and found that risk of death was significantly associated with older age, organ manifestations (increased for bacteremia without focus and meningitis) and underlying chronic conditions (immunosuppression, functional asplenia, chronic kidney disease/diabetes) but not with antibiotic resistance, as previously reported [41–43]. The lack of an association with resistance is likely related to low resistance rates and the ability to overcome e.g. penicillin resistance with sufficient beta-lactam doses in mostly low-level resistance. Our observation of an association between cotrimoxazole resistance and immunosuppression and nephrotic syndrome is well explained by these comorbidities being frequent indications for cotrimoxazole prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii. Similarly, the association of splenectomy and macrolide resistance potentially reflects frequent or chronic macrolide treatment for recurrent infections or immunomodulation. Of high clinical importance, we also found a clear association between meningitis and penicillin non-susceptibility. This has also been identified recently in France, which was mainly driven by an increased incidence of the frequently penicillin-resistant serotype 24F responsible for an increase in pediatric meningitis cases [44]. Data from Argentina confirmed the increasing importance of the antibiotic resistant non-PCV20 serotypes 12F and 24F as causes of meningitis [45]. Interestingly, if adjusted for age, serotype 19F, 23B and 35B but not 12F and 24/F were associated with meningitis in our data. Perhaps, the latter non-association is due to low numbers of serotype 12F and 24/F which actually even decreased in the more recent years in our setting.

This study has some major strengths. It includes a very large number of IPD isolates from before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. It covers the period of the PCV-13 era and will allow subsequent analyses of the influence of the new vaccines on antibiotic resistance. In addition, we include a large amount of clinical data from patients like case fatality, risk factors and manifestations into our analyses.

This study has also some limitations. In our study, death was reported with either “yes” or an empty field which may obfuscate certain death-related analyses. However, the clinicians were asked to mark ‘death’ if this happened within 90 days and we assume that if data is missing, the patients did probably not die within 90 days. Within 2012–2022, the CFR from Switzerland has been found to be in the range of 13.6% (2014) and 6.1% (2022) [18] which is comparable with a study from Sweden which reported CFR of 10% among all patients [41].

We did not include genomic data in our analyses. Therefore, the genomic population structure of invasive S. pneumoniae strains remains largely unknown. However, due to the strong association of antibiotic resistance with serotype, we hypothesize that there are distinct lineages circulating in Swiss patients with IPD. Another limitation is the use of gradient diffusion methods as they were found to frequently underestimate benzylpenicillin MIC values by one or more doubling dilutions as compared to broth microdilution susceptibility testing [46]. Therefore, the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) has cautioned against their use for S. pneumoniae. From 2018 onward, isolates should have been tested using broth microdilution in accordance with EUCAST recommendations. Nevertheless, the consistent application of the same methodology throughout the study period enables the assessment of longitudinal trends. Finally, we did not include antibiograms of non-invasive isolates in our study. However, as shown in our earlier study, association of resistance with serotypes is also assumed in carriage isolates [13].

In conclusion, we found that antibiotic resistance prevalence is generally decreasing due to the decrease of more resistant, vaccine serotypes with only limited and presumably transient impact of COVID-19 on resistant IPD. Therefore, the usage of PCVs not only has an effect on IPD incidence overall but also on resistant IPD in particular.
