Spinal epidural hematoma (SEH) is an uncommon but potentially devastating complication following lumbar spine surgery, with a reported incidence of approximately 0.1%–0.2%.1 Although PELD results in minimal soft tissue disruption, SEH may still occur. Early diagnosis and timely decompression are critical for neurological preservation.2

Appropriate evaluation of lumbar disc herniation morphology plays an important role in surgical planning, particularly when considering minimally invasive approaches such as PELD. Disc morphology and migration patterns may influence the risk of residual compression or postoperative complications, underscoring the importance of careful preoperative imaging assessment.

Although SEH is uncommon, several clinical series have emphasized its potential for rapid neurological deterioration if not promptly recognized.3 In addition, postoperative SEH has been reported even after biportal or endoscopic lumbar decompression, underscoring that minimally invasive techniques do not eliminate this risk.4

Essential thrombocythemia (ET) is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by sustained thrombocytosis and qualitative platelet dysfunction. ET patients exhibit a hemostatic paradox: increased bleeding tendency due to platelet dysfunction, yet persistent risk of thrombosis due to hypercoagulability. Cytoreductive therapy discontinuation, such as hydroxyurea, may result in rebound thrombocytosis and dysregulated platelet behavior, increasing the risk of postoperative hematoma formation.5–7 A similar case of acute SEH occurring in a patient with essential thrombocytosis has also been reported.8

Multilevel postoperative SEH remains exceedingly rare, and recurrent SEH despite repeated surgical decompression is even more uncommon.3,4 Here, we report a case in which repeated endoscopic and open decompression failed, and stabilization was achieved only after hematological correction.

Illustrative Case

A 71-year-old man with a medical history of ET, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension was scheduled for percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) at the left L2–3 level (Fig. 1). His ET had been medically controlled with hydroxyurea and anagrelide, but he had discontinued both medications several days prior to surgery. Preoperative laboratory testing (October 29, 2025) revealed a platelet count of 615,000/µL, hemoglobin level of 12.2 g/dL, and white blood cell count 9500/µL. The coagulation studies were within the normal limits.

Sagittal T2-weighted MR image obtained on October 14, 2025, demonstrating a left-sided L2–3 disc herniation (arrow) with significant thecal sac compression.

On October 30, 2025 (postoperative day [POD] 0), the patient underwent PELD at the left L2–3 for lumbar disc herniation. The herniated disc fragment was successfully removed, and no significant intraoperative bleeding was encountered. However, immediately on arrival in the recovery room, the patient developed severe radiating pain in the left lower limb, far exceeding the expected postoperative discomfort.

Given the acute presentation and high suspicion of postoperative hematoma, the patient was returned to the operating room for urgent endoscopic reexploration without obtaining preoperative MRI. A substantial amount of clotted epidural blood was removed through the endoscopic corridor. The decompression was satisfactory, and the patient experienced brief symptomatic relief.

However, within approximately 10 minutes in the recovery room, the severe left leg pain recurred, mirroring the symptoms before reexploration. Owing to persistent neurological deficit, he underwent a third surgery, open decompression at L2–4, later the same day. Intraoperatively, we observed diffuse epidural oozing, fragile microvasculature, and rapid reaccumulation of clot, despite copious irrigation and meticulous hemostasis. These findings raised the suspicion of hematological instability as the underlying etiology. A drain had also been placed during the second endoscopic reexploration but became rapidly occluded due to the viscous epidural clot. During the third open decompression, an epidural drain was again inserted, and with wider exposure and more complete hematoma evacuation, the drain remained patent throughout the postoperative course.

After the third operation, postoperative MRI (POD 1) revealed a massive posterior epidural hematoma extending from L1–2 to L5–S1, with marked compression of the thecal sac, predominantly on the left (Fig. 2). This imaging confirmed that the hematoma had reaccumulated across multiple levels, explaining the recurrent symptoms despite repeated decompressions.

Sagittal T2-weighted MR image obtained on October 31, 2025, revealing a large posterior epidural hematoma extending from L1 to S1(arrows).

A hematology review revealed that the patient’s discontinuation of hydroxyurea and anagrelide likely resulted in uncontrolled thrombocytosis and dysregulated platelet activity. The paradoxical combination of continuous epidural oozing and rapid clot formation was consistent with ET-associated hemostatic dysfunction.

The patient was immediately restarted on hydroxyurea 500 mg twice daily and anagrelide 100 mg daily, along with methasone 5 mg intravenously (IV) every 8 hours for anti-inflammatory reduction of nerve edema, tramadol 100 mg IV every 8 hours for analgesia, and tranexamic acid 1000 mg IV every 8 hours for hemostatic stabilization. Follow-up MRI on POD 14 demonstrated near-complete resolution of the epidural hematoma and restoration of the normal dural contour (Fig. 3).

Sagittal T2-weighted MR image obtained on November 13, 2025, demonstrating near-complete resolution of the previously noted multilevel epidural hematoma.

Neurologically, the patient showed gradual improvement. At 1 week postoperatively, motor strength in the left lower limb was M3, and at 2 weeks, it was M3–4. At the time of writing, he remains hospitalized with improving motor function, no radicular pain, and no sensory deficits. Steroids (methasone) have been discontinued, and he remains on tramadol 100 mg IV daily and tranexamic acid IV every 12 hours, in addition to resumed cytoreductive therapy.

Discussion

Postoperative SEH remains a dangerous complication of spine surgery.1,2 Even with endoscopic techniques, which generally reduce soft tissue damage and bleeding, SEH can still develop.9

Hemostatic Paradox of ET

ET features chronic thrombocytosis, platelet dysfunction, and increased thrombosis risk.5 Interruption of cytoreductive therapy may exacerbate hematological instability, predisposing patients to both hemorrhagic and thrombotic events.5–7 These mechanisms explain the diffuse oozing and rapid clot reformation observed in this case—mirroring earlier reports of ET-related SEH.8 In addition, preoperative platelet function testing, although not performed in this case, may help identify functional platelet abnormalities in ET patients and improve perioperative risk stratification.

Endoscopic Decompression Challenges in Hematological Disorders

Endoscopic discectomy reduces tissue trauma and results in faster recovery,9 but its limited visualization restricts the identification of multilevel hematomas or diffuse bleeding sources. This was evident in our case: endoscopy removed the hematoma at the operative level but failed to address the extensive multilevel hematoma later identified on MRI.

Failure of Repeated Decompression: Hematological Correction as the Key

When repeated decompression fails, surgeons should consider systemic hematological causes rather than inadequate surgical hemostasis.2–4 In this case, the hematoma reaccumulated immediately after two decompressions and stabilized only after the restoration of cytoreductive therapy—highlighting the limitations of surgery when the underlying ET remains uncontrolled.5,6 In patients with ET, careful preoperative risk stratification is essential. Key considerations include reviewing prior bleeding or thrombotic events, ensuring appropriate continuation or adjustment of cytoreductive therapy, and evaluating for extreme thrombocytosis that may destabilize platelet behavior. When clinically indicated, platelet function testing (such as PFA-100 or aggregation studies) may further clarify functional platelet abnormalities. Multidisciplinary coordination with the hematology team is recommended to optimize perioperative stability and reduce the risk of postoperative hematoma.

Comparison With the Literature

Most studies emphasize urgent evacuation for postoperative SEH,2,3,10 but few cases describe repeated failure of decompression due to ET-driven hematoma dynamics. A similar case of SEH associated with ET has been reported, supporting the concept that hematological stabilization may outweigh surgical evacuation.8

Observations

The patient’s clinical deterioration shortly after PELD highlighted the atypical and rapid progression of SEH in the context of essential thrombocytosis. Despite the minimally invasive approach, persistent bleeding occurred due to qualitative platelet dysfunction. Early recurrent neurological symptoms indicated incomplete hematoma control, which was later confirmed intraoperatively. This case also emphasized the diagnostic value of repeated MRI and the importance of heightened vigilance in ET patients, whose platelet function may not correlate with absolute platelet counts.

Lessons

This case illustrates the unique challenges associated with managing postoperative SEH in patients with ET. The combination of excessive intraoperative bleeding and rapid postoperative clot formation reflects the hemostatic paradox inherent to ET.5,6 When systemic instability is the primary driver of recurrent hematoma, repeated surgery may be ineffective. Hematological correction is essential for successful management.1,2,5,9 Spine surgeons must recognize when postoperative deterioration suggests a systemic—not local—etiology.
