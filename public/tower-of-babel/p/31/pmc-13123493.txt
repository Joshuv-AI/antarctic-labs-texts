Dynamic systemic immune-inflammation index predicts survival after neoadjuvant immunotherapy in gastric cancer: A multicenter study
Authors: Hua-Long Zheng, Li-Li Shen, Chen-bin Lv, Jia Lin, Bin-bin Xu, Zhen Xue, Jun Lu, Zhi-Wei Zheng, Chang-Ming Huang, Jia-bin Wang, Chao-Hui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), derived from platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts in peripheral blood, is linked to prognosis in several cancers. We evaluated whether changes in SII during neoadjuvant therapy could predict treatment outcomes in gastric cancer. A total of 410 patients received either chemotherapy alone or chemotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors, including internal and external cohorts. The SII rate (SIIR) was defined as the relative change in SII before and after treatment. Patients with at least a 35% reduction were classified as the SIIR reduction group. In the immunotherapy cohort, patients with SIIR reduction had higher 3-year overall survival and disease-free survival, which was confirmed in the external cohort. Joint model analysis showed that a higher log(SII) was associated with increased mortality risk. No survival differences by SIIR were observed in the chemotherapy-only cohort. These findings suggest SIIR may help identify patients likely to benefit from neoadjuvant immunotherapy.Subject areas: Oncology, Therapeutics

Introduction

Despite remarkable advances in diagnosis and treatment, gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global malignancy, ranking fifth in both incidence and mortality worldwide.1 Particularly in Asia, nearly half of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages III or IV.2 While curative surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, over half of patients eventually experience recurrence despite radical surgery.3,4 Clinical trials, including the MAGIC5 and FFCD studies, established the benefit of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in improving overall survival (OS) compared to surgery alone.

More recently, phase III trials such as CheckMate 6496 and KEYNOTE-8597 have shown that adding PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to chemotherapy as first-line treatment can further improve outcomes in locally advanced GC. However, a considerable proportion of PD-L1-positive patients do not respond to ICIs.8 Given the spatial heterogeneity of tumors, traditional tissue-based biomarkers such as microsatellite instability (MSI) and combined positive score (CPS) have limited predictive power.9,10 Conversely, the systemic immune-inflammatory status reflects the broader interaction between the tumor and host immunity and can offer a more accessible and dynamic approach to treatment monitoring.11 Traditional prognostic systems, including TNM staging and tumor regression grade (TRG), rely on postoperative specimens and fail to provide real-time treatment evaluation. Although imaging can monitor tumor burden, its frequent use may lead to increased patient anxiety, financial burden, and radiation exposure. Moreover, differentiating true progression from pseudo-progression based on radiologic findings remains a significant clinical challenge. Therefore, an ideal biomarker should be minimally invasive, cost-effective, and suitable for repeated measurement.12

Tumor-associated inflammation plays a critical role in promoting cancer progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance.13 Peripheral blood-based indices such as the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), derived from neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts, have demonstrated prognostic value in various malignancies—including GC—by reflecting the balance between pro-tumor inflammation and anti-tumor immunity.14,15,16,17,18,19 Jomrich et al.19 found that elevated baseline SII was associated with poorer survival in advanced gastroesophageal junction cancer. Compared to other inflammatory biomarkers such as NLR, PLR, and LMR, the SII provides a more comprehensive evaluation of the host’s immune and inflammatory status and has demonstrated superior prognostic value across various malignancies.20,21,22 A recent meta-analysis by Tian et al. further showed that SII has greater predictive value for treatment outcomes in digestive system cancers receiving ICIs, compared to its performance in lung cancer.23 However, the majority of existing studies have focused on static SII values measured at a single time point. Mechanistically, neutrophils and platelets can enhance tumor angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune escape,24,25,26,27,28 while tumor-derived exosomes in GC have been shown to prolong tumor-associated neutrophil survival and activate the HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB pathway, promoting metastasis.29 Importantly, immune responses to ICIs treatment are dynamic rather than static. Early in ICI treatment (typically after the first 1–2 cycles), proliferation and clonal expansion of peripheral CD8+ T cells, especially Ki67+ PD-1+ subsets, are closely correlated with treatment response.30 Similarly, expansion of effector memory-like CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood and their subsequent tumor infiltration have been linked to clinical benefit.31,32 Collectively, these observations suggest that the systemic immune landscape evolves continuously during treatment, and these findings highlight SII as an integrative marker reflecting both systemic inflammation and immune suppression. Therefore, a single time-point assessment of inflammatory markers (e.g., baseline SII) may not capture this dynamic process, whereas the rate of SII change (SIIR) could serve as a real-time indicator of treatment-induced immune activation, inflammation resolution, and tumor-host equilibrium.

Apart from baseline peripheral blood biomarkers, previous research in various solid tumors has demonstrated that dynamic changes in inflammatory indices such as NLR, PLR, SII, and LMR are closely associated with immunotherapy efficacy and prognosis.19,33,34,35 Shang et al. reported that elevated post-treatment SII predicted poor outcomes in pancreatic cancer patients receiving ICIs.36 Similarly, Cao et al. found that a reduction in SII and an increase in LMR during ICI treatment were significantly correlated with prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma.37 In metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC), SII elevation after 12 weeks of immunotherapy was also associated with disease progression.38 However, in GC, evidence remains limited and primarily based on small cohorts and static time points.19,39,40 No large-scale study has systematically assessed the longitudinal trajectory of SII during neoadjuvant treatment, nor its prognostic implications across different treatment strategies.

Therefore, this multicenter study aimed to assess the prognostic value of SIIR in predicting survival outcomes among patients undergoing neoadjuvant treatment, and to further explore its predictive performance under different therapeutic strategies (conventional chemotherapy versus chemo-immunotherapy). In addition, we examined whether SIIR could help identify patients who are more likely to benefit from postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. As a low-cost, easily accessible, and reproducible immune-inflammatory biomarker, SIIR holds promise for assisting in optimizing perioperative treatment strategies and refining clinical decision-making.

Results

Clinical characteristics

This study included 410 patients who underwent gastrectomy following NAT at the internal center (283 cases) and external center (127cases) between December 2018 and December 2021. The diagnostic threshold for the SIIR was determined using the median value (−0.35) across all patients. Table 1 displays the clinicopathological data of all patients in the FMUH cohort. Among the patients, 161 were included in the combined ICIs treatment group, and 122 were included in the NAC group. The median age of all participants was 63 years (IQR: 56–69 years), with 207 males and 76 females. The median number of neoadjuvant therapy cycles received by the ICIs cohort was 4 (IQR: 3–4 cycles). Most patients (146 cases, 90.2%) received postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. For the NAC cohort, the median number of neoadjuvant therapy cycles was 4 (IQR: 3–4 cycles). Postoperatively, 110 (90.7%) patients received adjuvant chemotherapy.

The efficacy outcomes of tumor response rates for SIIR

All patients underwent radiological evaluation of changes in lesion size before and after treatment based on the RECIST 1.1 criteria. Boxplots were used to illustrate the association between the SIIR and tumor response to ICIs based on both radiological and pathological evaluation. The results demonstrated that within the ICIs cohort, patients who achieve objective response (ORR: Partial or complete response (PR/CR)) exhibited significantly lower SIIR values compared to those with stable or progressive disease (SD/PD) (−0.31 ± 0.07 vs. −0.11 ± 0.07, p = 0.047). Moreover, the ORR in the SIIR reduction group (62.8% vs. 40.0%, p = 0.004) was significantly higher than that in the SIIR non-reduction group (Figures 1A and 1B). Similarly, in the pathological assessment, patients with TRG1a/1b exhibited a tendency toward lower SIIR values compared with those with TRG2/3 (−0.27 ± 0.09 vs. −0.18 ± 0.06, p = 0.408). Consistently, the SIIR reduction group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with favorable pathological regression (TRG1a/1b: 44.2% vs. 28.0%, p = 0.033) (Figures 1C and 1D).

Long-term survival outcomes

Patients in the SIIR reduction group were associated with improved 3-year OS of 83.7% compared to 72.5% in SIIR non-reduction group (HR 1.70, 95%CI: 1.01–2.84, p = 0.042) and 3-year DFS of 79.4% versus 68.3% (HR 1.65, 95%CI: 1.03–2.63, p = 0.034) (Figures 2A and 2B). This study further evaluated the prognostic significance of the SIIR across various treatment strategies. In the ICIs cohort, those in the SIIR reduction group exhibited significantly superior OS (88.4% vs. 72.0%, HR 2.57, 95%CI: 1.21–5.46, p = 0.011) and DFS (84.9% vs. 65.3%, HR 2.70, 95%CI: 1.39–5.27, p = 0.002) than their SIIR non-reduction counterparts (Figures 2C and 2D). Conversely, in the NAC cohort, the differences in 3-year OS (76.4% vs. 73.1%, HR 1.03, 95%CI: 0.51–2.11, p = 0.930) and DFS (70.9% vs. 71.6%, HR 0.89, 95%CI: 0.46–1.73, p = 0.729) between the SIIR reduction and SIIR non-reduction groups were not significant (Figures 2E and 2F). The results from the external validation cohort indicated that patients treated with ICIs in the SIIR reduction experienced a higher 3-year OS (93.8% vs. 76.9%, HR 4.56, 95%CI: 1.01–21.28, p = 0.034) than those in the SIIR non-reduction group. Additionally, a numerical trend toward a higher 3-year DFS was observed in the SIIR reduction group (84.4% vs. 64.1%, HR 2.52, 95%CI: 0.91–7.00, p = 0.067), although this difference was not statistically significant (Figures S4C and S4D). In the NAC cohort, no significant differences were observed in 3-year OS (69.2% vs. 64.7%, HR 1.21, 95%CI: 0.45–3.22, p = 0.711) or DFS (59.0% vs. 41.2%, HR 1.48, 95%CI: 0.67–3.27, p = 0.321) between the SIIR reduction and SIIR non-reduction groups (Figures S4E and S4F).

The prognostic value of changes in SII after ICIs or NAC

We documented neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts one week before starting treatment (baseline) and one week before surgery (endpoint) in all patients. The median SII was 829.1 at baseline, which decreased to 460.6 at the endpoint. A joint model was developed to elucidate the correlation between changes in the SII trajectory during neoadjuvant therapy and the OS risk of patients. Figure S6 illustrates the individualized trajectories of the SII over time for patients in different treatment groups, with the average SII in the ICIs treatment cohort showing a significant downward trend over the course of treatment.

Within the ICIs cohort, the median SII was 829.1 (532.0, 1125.5) at baseline, which decreased to 473.4 (297.0, 770.2) at the endpoint. The longitudinal sub-model delineated the temporal evolution of the SII for each patient, incorporating fixed effects. The outcomes revealed a significant decrease in log(SII) over time by −0.20 (95% CI: (−0.24, −0.15), p < 0.001). Pertaining to the survival sub-model, where death served as the endpoint event, the joint model analysis indicated that a one-unit increase in log(SII) was associated with a 0.39-fold increase in the instantaneous risk of death (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.02–2.30, p = 0.031; Table 2). In the NAC cohort, the median SII was 625.1 (393.7, 965.4) at baseline, which decreased to 465.2 (283.2, 869.3) at the endpoint. The longitudinal sub-model demonstrated that log(SII) decreased over time by −0.08 (95% CI: (−0.14, −0.04), p < 0.001). However, the JM analysis of SII and the instantaneous risk of death among patients undergoing the NAC treatment regimen revealed the association between increased log(SII) and the risk of death was not statistically significant (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.61–1.52, p = 0.858; Table 2).

The risk of treatment failure and the recurrence pattern

This investigation further elucidated the prognostic implications of SIIR in treatment failure. In the ICIs cohort, treatment failure occurred in 39 of 161 patients. In the NAC cohort, treatment failure occurred in 35 of 122 patients. Figure 4 delineates the failure rates at specified follow-up intervals of 12, 18, and 24 months, and a comparative histogram of recurrence sites throughout the follow-up period. Within the ICIs cohort, patients with SIIR non-reduction group demonstrated significantly increased treatment failure rates at 12 months (18.7% vs. 8.1%, p = 0.048), 18 months (26.7% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.015), and 24 months (30.7% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.003) (Figure 4A and Table S5). Similar findings were observed in the external validation cohort, in the SIIR non-reduction group, patients manifested a significantly higher treatment failure rate after 18 months (15.6% vs. 35.9%, p = 0.045). Competing risks regression in our recurrence analyses, during the follow-up period, there were 37 cases (cumulative incidence rate of 23.0%) of recurrence in the ICIs cohort and 32 cases (cumulative incidence rate of 26.2%) in the NAC cohort. Specifically, in the ICIs cohort, the SIIR non-reduction group exhibited a significantly higher risk of recurrence (HR 2.82, 95% CI: 1.43–5.53, p = 0.003, Table S3). Further breakdown by recurrence pattern showed a significantly increased risk of local recurrence (HR 5.58, 95% CI: 1.24–25.07, p = 0.025). The corresponding cumulative incidence curves (Figure S10A) demonstrate a clear separation between groups (fine-gray test, p = 0.002). In contrast, no significant difference in recurrence risk was observed between SIIR groups in the NAC cohort (Table S4 and Figure S10B). The distribution histogram of recurrence patterns within the ICIs cohort revealed that among patients experiencing recurrence, those with SIIR non-reduction exhibited significantly higher rates of local than those with SIIR reduction (12.0% vs. 2.3%, p = 0.025, Figure 4B). Conversely, within the NAC cohort, no statistically significant differences were observed in the timing of treatment failure (Figures 4C and 4D) or recurrence patterns between the SIIR reduction and non-reduction groups (p > 0.05).

Association of SIIR with adjuvant chemotherapy benefits

To further exploratory analysis the potential relationship between SIIR and the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy (AC), the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to adjust for the impact of ypTNM staging covariates on survival prognosis in the internal center. Before TPTW, the ICI cohort consisting of 86 SIIR reduction patients receiving AC was not associated with an improvement in OS compared to those who did not receive AC (91.4% vs. 88.9%, HR 0.68 95%CI: 0.08–5.03, p = 0.711), no statistical difference was observed in DFS (88.9% vs. 88.8%, HR 0.98 95%CI: 0.12–7.82, p = 0.986) between the two groups (Figure S11A); after IPTW, no statistical difference was observed between the two groups (OS: 91.4% vs. 87.4%, HR 0.63 95%CI: 0.09–4.39, p = 0.638; DFS: 88.9% vs. 87.4%, HR 0.86 95%CI: 0.12–6.47, p = 0.884) (Figure S11B). Among the 75 SIIR non-reduction patients, those who received AC had a significantly better 3-year OS (80.4% vs. 28.6%, HR 0.23 95%CI: 0.07–0.73, p = 0.007) than those who did not receive AC (Figure S11C). After IPTW, AC treatment was significantly associated with improved OS (80.4% vs. 30.8%, HR 0.25 95%CI: 0.08–0.73, p = 0.011; Figure S11D). In the NAC cohort, neither OS nor DFS showed significant differences, even after applying IPTW (Figure S12).

Exploratory comparison of SIIR with other immune-inflammatory biomarkers

To further evaluate the specificity and clinical utility of SIIR in predicting immunotherapy outcomes, we conducted an exploratory comparative analysis. Nomogram models were constructed by integrating each candidate biomarker (baseline SII, baseline NLR, NLR change rate, and SIIR) with ypTNM staging. The model incorporating SIIR + ypTNM demonstrated the best discriminative ability, with a C-index of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.66–0.80) (SII-baseline: 0.68 (95%CI: 0.60–0.77) vs. NLR-baseline: 0.70 (95%CI: 0.61–0.79) vs. NLR-changes rate: 0.70 (95%CI: 0.62–0.78)) and an AUC of 0.77 (95%CI: 0.67–0.86), outperforming models based on baseline SII (AUC: 0.70, 95%CI: 0.59–0.79), baseline NLR (AUC: 0.71, 95%CI:0.61–0.81), NLR change rate (AUC: 0.70, 95%CI: 0.60–0.80) (Figure S9A). In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) showed that the SIIR + ypTNM model consistently provided the highest net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, further supporting its incremental prognostic value over other models (Figure S9B). These findings suggest that SIIR may offer superior predictive utility compared to other commonly used inflammatory markers in the context of neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

Discussion

This is the first multicenter cohort analysis to evaluate the effect of SIIR on the prognosis of patients with AGC undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy or combination with ICIs. The findings revealed that SII had significantly decreased after ICIs treatment. SIIR reduction group during ICI treatment was associated with improved response rates, improved survival, and reduced recurrence rates, an association that was not observed within the chemotherapy-alone cohort. Joint model indicated that during ICI treatment, an increase in SII was independently correlated with an elevated risk of overall mortality in patients with AGC, suggesting an association between elevated SII and poorer survival prognosis.

Dammeijer et al. confirmed the cascade reaction between inflammatory factors and immunotherapy.41,42,43,44 These papers revealed that changes in the SII (platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count) during treatment reflect shifts in the tumor-host immune-inflammatory state. It may serve as an ideal biomarker to predict the efficacy of ICI therapy. Wang et al.45 demonstrated that a high preoperative SII was associated with poorer long-term survival in patients with AGC. A prospective study46 from the Netherlands showed that an elevated SII increases the risk of lung and colorectal cancers. An Italian SOUL cohort study47 revealed the superiority of the SII in predicting the OS and disease control rate among patients with urinary cancer receiving immunotherapy. However, these studies focused solely on preoperative or pre-neoadjuvant treatment indicators to predict prognosis, overlooking the comprehensive relationship between the extent of tumor response during neoadjuvant treatment and dynamic changes in hematological markers. Our results showed that the SII level significantly decreased in GC patients after treatment with ICIs. A reduction in SIIR of ≥35% during treatment was associated with a higher response rate, survival benefit, and a lower recurrence rate in patients receiving ICIs, whereas this predictive association disappeared in those treated with chemotherapy alone. Consistent with this concept, accumulating evidence from the neoadjuvant chemotherapy setting has demonstrated that dynamic changes in SII provide additional prognostic and predictive value beyond baseline measurements alone. Ni et al. reported that greater reductions in SII following neoadjuvant chemotherapy were associated with improved therapeutic response and survival outcomes in breast cancer patients, highlighting the clinical relevance of SII dynamics in identifying high-risk subgroups.48 Shang et al. reported that elevated post-treatment SII predicted poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer36; and Cao et al. found that decreased SII and increased LMR correlated with longer PFS in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma.37 Similarly, in metastatic RCC, SII elevation after 12 weeks of ICIs was associated with disease progression.38 This is consistent with our findings. ICIs and chemotherapy differ substantially in how they modulate the immune-inflammatory state. ICIs restore antitumor immunity primarily by reactivating cytotoxic T cell responses and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment, whereas chemotherapy exerts non-specific cytotoxic effects that suppress both tumor and immune-related cells. This fundamental mechanistic difference may explain why SIIR shows prognostic relevance in the ICIs group but not in the NAC group.

An expanding body of research posits that cancer is a disease of considerable heterogeneity and complexity, and its onset, progression, and metastasis are influenced not only by the intrinsic traits of the tumor itself, but also by the systemic inflammation status.49,50 Notably, substantial associations have been identified between systemic immune-inflammatory responses and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions.51 Although the exact mechanism by which SII predicts the efficacy of ICIs has not been fully elucidated, SII provides a more comprehensive reflection of the dynamic balance between host immunity and systemic inflammation compared with other inflammatory indices.21,52 Elevated SII levels, often due to increased neutrophil or platelet counts and decreased lymphocyte counts, are indicative of an immunosuppressive environment. Reduced lymphocyte levels may correlate with decreased tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and weakened antitumor T cell responses, leading to impaired adaptive immunity and resistance to ICIs. Qu et al. reported that dynamic changes in NLR and PLR levels were closely related to resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy in GC.53 The decline of neutrophil counts following immunotherapy may signal relief from immunosuppression and restoration of antitumor immunity. Neutrophils secrete immunosuppressive mediators and angiogenic factors such as VEGF, which promote tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune evasion.54 They are also targets and regulators of ICI resistance.55,56,57

Additionally, ICI treatment induces peripheral immune reconstitution within the first few cycles (approximately 3–6 weeks), characterized by rapid proliferation and clonal expansion of activated CD8+ T cells.24,33,35 During effective treatment, neutrophil and platelet levels tend to decrease, which contribute to tumor angiogenesis, EMT, and immune escape.27,29,30,31,58 At the same time, lymphocyte counts usually increase. Increased infiltration of lymphocytes, especially CD8+ T cells, has been associated with improved immunotherapy response.59,60 Moreover, platelets may protect tumor cells from immune-mediated cytotoxicity, potentially affecting the efficacy of immunotherapy.61,62,63 Similarly, Labelle et al.64 suggested that platelets directly activate the NF-kB and TGF-b/Smad signaling pathways, inducing EMT.65,66 A previous study67 from our center investigating tumor microenvironment immune escape phenotypes revealed that patients with immunosuppressive profiles exhibited enriched EMT. This may be one reason an elevated SIIR indicates a poor response to ICI therapy. Therefore, patients with decreased SII after ICI therapy are more likely to experience restored immune activation and better clinical outcomes, reflecting the dynamic interplay between immune activation and systemic inflammation. In contrast, chemotherapy may non-selectively reduce neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, disrupting the link between peripheral inflammatory markers and the underlying immune state. This nonspecific suppression likely accounts for the lack of prognostic value of SIIR in the NAC group. Future large-scale prospective studies are needed to further validate our findings, and elucidating the underlying biological mechanisms would be an interesting and valuable direction for future research.

While traditional tools like ypTNM staging or TRG scoring rely on postoperative pathology, SIIR is a dynamic, non-invasive biomarker derived from peripheral blood that can be assessed repeatedly during NAT. Our findings suggest that patients with a reduction of 35% in SIIR tend to have higher radiologic response rates, lower recurrence risk, and improved survival. Therefore, SIIR could potentially serve as a real-time indicator of biological response to treatment. In clinical practice, persistent elevation or insufficient reduction of SIIR during NAT may warrant closer monitoring and multidisciplinary discussion, more frequent surveillance, or early initiation of adjuvant therapy. Conversely, a marked decrease in SIIR may support continuation of the current regimen. While our data are retrospective, they suggest that SIIR may complement existing staging systems and help refine treatment decisions. Moreover, our study revealed a significant correlation between SIIR and the survival benefit of AC. Patients experiencing a SIIR > −0.35 during treatment demonstrated a significant association in improved OS upon receiving AC, whereas no evident correlation was observed between the reception of AC and survival improvement among patients with a SIIR ≤ −0.35. These outcomes indicate that the SIIR might be a critical factor in selecting AC beneficiaries. Our subgroup analysis revealed that patients with SIIR non-reduction following neoadjuvant ICIs may represent a biologically higher-risk phenotype, characterized by persistent systemic inflammatory activation and potentially incomplete immunologic tumor control. In this context, adjuvant chemotherapy may contribute to the eradication of residual or micrometastatic disease not sufficiently cleared by neoadjuvant therapy. Local tumor shrinkage and eradication of microscopic residual disease are not biologically equivalent endpoints. It should be emphasized that the current findings regarding the predictive value of SIIR for AC benefit are exploratory in nature and require further validation. In future work, the multi-institutional collaborative studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs will assess the reproducibility and clinical utility of SIIR in guiding adjuvant chemotherapy strategies.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that a decrease in the SIIR during immunotherapy correlates with a better ORR and pathological regression in patients with AGC. Transient changes in SII also significantly and independently predicted OS. Meanwhile, during immunotherapy for AGC, a rate of change in SII less than −0.35 (SIIR ≤ −0.35) does not exclude the possibility of deriving benefits from adjuvant chemotherapy. The potential of the SIIR to assist in risk stratification and clinical decision-making for patients with GC undergoing ICI treatment should be further developed, necessitating larger-scale prospective studies for a deeper investigation.

Limitations of the study

This study had several limitations. Being retrospective in nature, it is inherently subject to selection bias and residual confounding, which also prevented the application of advanced causal inference methods such as marginal structural models or formal sensitivity analyses. The observed associations have not been biologically validated, and the limited number of events may reduce the precision of effect estimation. Moreover, inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein, interleukins, IL-6, TNF-α, and peripheral T cell subsets were not assessed, restricting a comprehensive evaluation of the immune landscape. Heterogeneity in ICIs and NAC regimens and the limited external validation sample size may affect the generalizability of the observed association between SIIR and adjuvant chemotherapy benefit. Although three longitudinal measurements were included, the limited sampling frequency may compromise the robustness of longitudinal prediction, and external joint model construction with full performance assessments was not conducted. Incomplete PD-L1 CPS data precluded a head-to-head comparison between SIIR and established immune biomarkers. Additionally, the fixed SIIR threshold may face challenges in broader application due to variations in detection platforms or population characteristics. Incomplete documentation of G-CSF use in some cases could also limit data representativeness, though cross-checking with electronic medical records ensured data were collected before potential G-CSF administration. Finally, inter-laboratory differences in hematology analyzers might introduce systematic discrepancies in SII measurements. These limitations highlight the need for prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, standardized regimens, and more frequent longitudinal data to validate our findings. Despite these limitations, this study represents the first multicenter investigation of SIIR’s prognostic value during neoadjuvant therapy across different regimens, focusing on dynamic immune-inflammatory changes rather than baseline status, and confirming SIIR as a potential marker for identifying patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy and adjuvant chemotherapy.

Experimental model and study participant details

This retrospective multicenter study initially reviewed 453 patients with locally advanced gastric adenocarcinoma (cT2-4aN0/+M0) who received neoadjuvant treatment between December 2018 and December 2021 at two centers: the Affiliated Union Hospital of Fujian Medical University (n=326) and Zhangzhou Hospital (n=127). Patients were excluded if they had concurrent malignancies; prior exposure to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or any other anticancer therapy; intraoperative or postoperative confirmation of distant metastases; a history of gastric surgery; prior use of corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents for comorbid conditions; postoperative death within 3 months; concurrent autoimmune diseases; infectious fever within 2 weeks prior to admission; receipt of immunomodulatory agents; premature discontinuation of treatment after only one cycle of neoadjuvant therapy; incomplete laboratory data before or after neoadjuvant therapy; missing CT imaging records; or loss to follow-up. A total of 410 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis (see Figure S1 for details), comprising an internal cohort of 283 patients and an external cohort of 127 patients. The median age of the included patients was 63 years (IQR, 56-69 years), including 292 male and 118 female patients. Patients were allocated retrospectively according to the treatment they had actually received in routine clinical practice rather than by randomization. In the internal cohort, 161 patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy ICIs and 122 patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone. In the external cohort, 71 patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus ICIs and 56 patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone. Overall, 232 patients were included in the combined ICIs treatment group and 178 patients were included in the NAC group. All patients received at least 2 cycles of neoadjuvant therapy, followed by gastrectomy within 2-3 weeks after completion of treatment.

This study involved the collection and analysis of clinical, pathological, laboratory, and imaging data only and did not affect patient management. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (IRB No. 2024KY247). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized and handled in accordance with institutional and national guidelines to protect patient privacy and confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.This study is not a clinical trial.

Method details

Perioperative chemotherapy

All patients in this study received at least two cycles of fluoropyrimidine-based neoadjuvant therapy. The cohort was divided into those receiving NAC and those receiving chemotherapy combined with ICIs. Patients in the ICIs group received one of the following PD-1 inhibitors in combination with chemotherapy: Camrelizumab (200 mg, IV, Day 1, n = 107); Nivolumab (360 mg every 3 weeks or 240 mg every 2 weeks, IV, Day 1, n = 42); or Sintilimab (200 mg, IV, Day 1, n = 12). Chemotherapy regimens included SOX (S-1 plus oxaliplatin), DOS (docetaxel, oxaliplatin, and S-1), and FLOT (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel). Patients in the ICIs group received NAC combined with PD-1 inhibitors. After every two treatment cycles, contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic CT scans were performed to assess tumor response based on RECIST version 1.1 criteria.68 These evaluations determined whether patients continued NAT or proceeded to surgery. The objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving either complete response (CR) or partial response (PR). In addition, tumor regression was also classified using the Becker tumor regression grading (TRG) system, which includes the following categories: TRG1a (no residual tumor cells), TRG1b (<10% residual tumor cells); TRG2 (10–50% residual tumor cells); and TRG3 (50% or more residual tumor cells).69 TRG 1a/1b was considered a good pathological response (GRNC), whereas TRG 2–3 indicated a poor response (PRNC). According to our institutional perioperative treatment protocol, patients were generally advised to complete a minimum of eight total cycles of perioperative chemotherapy, including postoperative adjuvant treatment. The number of preoperative cycles varied based on treatment tolerance, tumor response, surgical timing, and patient condition. In the absence of radiologically confirmed disease progression or unacceptable toxicity, adjuvant chemotherapy was administered using the same preoperative regimen to complete the planned perioperative treatment course, rather than as an independent salvage strategy. The distribution of preoperative treatment cycles and the proportion of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy were comparable across SIIR subgroups (Table S1), indicating balanced treatment exposure between groups. The criteria for stopping treatment were patient refusal, tumor progression, intolerable toxicity, or investigator’s decision. An Independent Data Monitoring Committee monitored patient safety and study conduct.

The clinical data collection and definitions of end points

Adverse outcomes occurring within 30 days post-surgery was systematically recorded using the hospital’s electronic medical record system, designated as postoperative complications, and subsequently stratified by severity using the Clavien–Dindo classification system.70 Complications classified as Clavien-Dindo grade 3A or higher were deemed severe. OS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the last follow-up or death due to any cause. DFS was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the first detection of disease recurrence, death, or last follow-up.71,72 For all survival analyses, survival time was defined as time from the data of surgery to death or recurrence or to the last follow-up. Recurrence patterns included local recurrence (involving the anastomosis site, remnant stomach, and perigastric lymph nodes), peritoneal metastasis (comprising peritoneal and pelvic implants, and malignant ascites), hepatic metastasis, and other distant metastases (distant lymph node involvement, affecting the brain, lungs, or bones). Early treatment failure is defined as treatment failure (including recurrence or death) occurring within 12 months after surgery. Fine and Gray competing risks regression was used to compare recurrence patterns. Events indicative of treatment failure were recognized as recurrences or death, and the timeframe for treatment failure was defined as the period from surgery to the manifestation of such events. Longitudinal laboratory (SII) and survival data were systematically collected from all the patients. The SII was calculated as the platelet counts multiply the ratio of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte. SIIR is derived by calculating the ratio of the change in SII values before and after treatment to the initial SII value. (SIIR = (SIIpost-treatment − SIIpre-treatment)/SIIpre-treatment). Accordingly, SII was assessed at three standardized timepoints: (i) within one week prior to the initiation of NAT; (ii) after completion of two cycles of NAT (within one week before the third cycle); (iii) within one week prior to surgery. 11 patients discontinued treatment after only two cycles and proceeded directly to surgery. For these patients, only timepoints (i) and (iii) were available, and the mid-treatment (ii) value was missing. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model73 was used to explore the association between SIIR and OS, and the point at which the hazard ratio (HR) crossed 1 was identified as a potential threshold. A notable inflection point was observed at SIIR = –0.35, where the HR crossed 1. A bootstrap procedure with 1,000 resamples was conducted to estimate the distribution of the HR = 1 crossing point. The bootstrap-derived inflection point was –0.35 (95% confidence interval: –0.50 to –0.37), indicating good stability. Based on this analysis, patients were categorized into two groups: the SIIR reduction group (SIIR ≤ –0.35) and the SIIR non-reduction group (SIIR > –0.35).

Joint model of longitudinal and survival data

Traditional static models assume that the hazard ratios (HRs) remain constant over time, thereby overlooking the dynamic nature of individual risk profiles. Data from 283 patients in Union Hospital affiliated to Fujian Medical University were included model and analyzed, and Bayesian shared-parameter model was used to construct a clinically dynamic joint model. Specifically, we delineated a mixed-effects linear relationship sub-model to chart the longitudinal trajectory of the SII across time, along with a COX risk sub-model designed to simulate the timing of mortality events. A joint model (JM)74 was formulated to elucidate the correlation between fluctuations in the SII trajectory and overall mortality risk in patients with GC, through shared parameters across these sub-models.

The SII was log-transformed (natural logarithm, log(SII)) to reduce skewness and improve model fit. The log(SII) variable was used in both the longitudinal sub-model (LME) and the survival sub-model (Cox) within the JM framework. To fit the JM, the association of longitudinal SII was adjusted for age, ECOG, tumor size, thus including age, ECOG, tumor size, time effects and the logarithmic value of the SII (log (SII)) as variables. We collected neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts at three time points for all patients: one week before the initiation of treatment (baseline), one week before the third treatment cycle (middle), and one week prior to surgery (endpoint). In consideration of the effects of changes in SII over time, a linear mixed-effects (LME) model was employed to evaluate the trajectory of SII changes within the JM.75 Furthermore, we identified independent prognostic factors for OS through a Cox proportional hazards sub-model (p < 0.05), including ypTNM stage and log(SII) during the treatment period.

JM integrates the longitudinal sub-model (LME) with a Cox sub-model (survival sub-model) that includes ypTNM, neurovascular infiltration, postoperative comorbidity, sex covariates, ultimately establishing a joint model characterized by longitudinal trajectories. In both the ICIs and NAC cohorts, two patients were randomly selected, and their SII trajectory changes and survival trends were depicted based on the JM.

Details of the construction of the joint model (JM)

We have stated descriptive statistics for baseline and post-treatment SII values, as well as the magnitude of SII changes, stratified by cohort and by SIIR classification. In the ICIs cohort, the median SII decreased from 829.1 (IQR: 532.0-1125.5) at baseline to 473.4 (297.0-770.2) post-treatment. Stratified analyses by SIIR classification, among SIIR reduction group patients, SII declined markedly (median change: −602.3; IQR: −896.3 to −412.9), whereas in the SIIR-non-dec group, SII change was minimal or even elevated in some cases (median change: 31.5; IQR: −81.4 to 249.4). Whereas, in the NAC cohort, the median SII decreased from 625.1 (393.7-965.4) at baseline, to 465.2 (283.2-869.3) post-treatment. among SIIR-dec patients, SII declined (median change: −389.8 (−708.6 to −251.3),in the SIIR-non-dec group, SII change was 59.6 (−90.0 to 332.0) (Table S6). These findings support the potential value of dynamic SII monitoring during treatment and highlight the heterogeneity of systemic inflammatory responses among patients.

A mixed-effects linear relationship sub-model was delineated to chart the longitudinal trajectory of the SII across time, along with a COX risk sub-model designed to simulate the timing of mortality events. A joint model (JM)21 was formulated to elucidate the correlation between fluctuations in the SII trajectory and overall mortality risk in patients with GC, through shared parameters across these sub-models.

Regarding the potential issue of multicollinearity among covariates, we conducted a systematic evaluation of correlations between candidate variables prior to model construction. Specifically, we performed an exploratory analysis using a Spearman correlation heatmap (Figure S5). Based on both clinical relevance and statistical independence, only a subset of variables was included in the final model: ypTNM stage, sex, postoperative comorbidity, and neurovascular infiltration. Furthermore, we assessed multicollinearity among the selected covariates using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). As shown in Table S7, all VIF < 2.0, indicating no significant multicollinearity and supporting the appropriateness of our covariate selection. Taken together, these findings suggest that the covariates retained in the final model were appropriate, clinically relevant, and did not exhibit meaningful multicollinearity.

To evaluate the validity of the proportional hazards (PH) assumption in the survival sub-model of the joint model, Schoenfeld residual tests were performed for each covariate and for the global model. The results indicated that all individual covariates had p-values greater than 0.1, and the global test also yielded a p-value > 0.1 (Table S8), suggesting that the PH assumption held for the model as a whole. These findings support the appropriateness of the Cox model structure and the statistical reliability of the estimated hazard ratios.

Model diagnostics indicated good internal performance: the DIC (1173.74) and WAIC (1143.64) values were within an acceptable range for joint models (∼1000–1500), suggesting a reasonable balance between model fit and complexity (Table S9). MCMC diagnostics (Figure S7) showed satisfactory chain mixing and convergence, and the conditional model demonstrated superior fit compared to the marginal model after accounting for random effects.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using STATA (version 16.0) and R (version 4.2.0; http://www.r-project.org). Within the R environment, the coxph(⋅) function from the survival package was used for Cox regression analyses. JMs were constructed using the JointModel(⋅) function in the JM package.76,77 Monte Carlo simulations were performed using the survfitJM(⋅) inherent function within the JM package. Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests and described as frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, depiction was through medians (interquartile range, IQR) or means (± standard deviation, SD), contingent upon their distribution. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and inter-curve differences were analyzed using the log-rank test. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
