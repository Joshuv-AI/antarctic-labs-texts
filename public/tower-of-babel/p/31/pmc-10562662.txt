Common and distinct patterns of acquired uniparental disomy and homozygous deletions between lung squamous cell carcinomas and lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Musaffe Tuna, Gordon B Mills, Christopher I Amos
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcquired uniparental disomy (aUPD) is a chromosomal alteration that can lead to homozygosity of existing aberrations. We used data from The Cancer Genome Atlas SNP-based arrays to identify distinct and common aUPD profiles in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). Moreover, we tested relevance of aUPD for homozygous deletion (HMD), overall survival (OS), and recurrence-free survival (RFS). Overall, we found significantly higher aUPD (q = 5.34E-09) in LUSC than in LUAD. A significant portion of HMD was associated with aUPD in LUSC (24.9%) and LUAD (19.7%). We identified segmental, whole-chromosome arm and whole-chromosome aUPD, in which whole 7p arm aUPD was restricted to LUSC, while whole-chromosome 3 aUPD was observed only in LUAD, and whole-chromosome 21 aUPD was common to both LUSC and LUAD. The most frequent aUPD and HMD were observed at CDKN2A/B region in both LUAD and LUSC. In LUAD, aUPD and HMD at CDKN2A/B region were associated with shorter OS (q < 0.021 and q < 0.005), and RFS (q < 0.005 and q < 0.005), while heterozygous deletion was not associated with OS and RFS. In contrast, no association was found between aUPD at CDKN2A/B region and survival in LUSC. In LUAD, CTLA expression was significantly lower in samples with aUPD at CDKN2A/B regions than in samples without copy number and allele-based changes. Immune infiltration correlated with aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B, gain at HLA class I region, and aUPD at whole-chromosome q-arm or whole chromosome in LUAD, but not in LUSC. Both LUSC and LUAD have common and distinct patterns of aUPD regions with differing frequencies of occurrence and associations with outcome.Keywords: Lung squamous cell carcinoma, Lung adenocarcinoma, Acquired uniparental disomy, Homozygous deletions, Genomics, Survival, Mutations, Immune checkpoint genes, Whole-genome doubling, Immune infiltration

Introduction

DNA copy number alterations (CNAs) are a hallmark of most cancers, but genomic changes do not always lead to copy number alterations. If one allele is deleted and the remaining allele is duplicated or repaired, these changes are not reflected as CNAs. This type of change, which can be functional if one of the alleles is aberrant, is called acquired uniparental disomy (aUPD) or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity. UPD, first described by Engel in 1980 [1], was named based on both alleles originating from the same parent, resulting in homozygosity for the affected region but no change in copy number. UPD can exist either as a segmental, whole-chromosome, or whole-chromosome arm. The loss of one allele and reduplication of the remaining allele is proposed as the causal event for the generation of whole-chromosome arm aUPD [1], and mitotic recombination for segmental aUPD [[2], [3]]. Error-prone repair of the deleted arm or segment through homologous recombination may be an alternative mechanism for the acquisition of segmental aUPD [4]. Regardless of the mechanism, aUPD is important because it leads to homozygosity for existing alterations that can have functional consequences. When there are mutations or epigenetic changes on a chromosome, aUPD can lead to differential regulation of RNA transcripts which can result in a more aggressive phenotype as well as an association with recurrence and metastasis [[4], [5], [6]].

Lung cancer is the leading cause of death from cancer around the world [7]. In the United States in 2023, new lung cancer cases are projected to be 238,340 with 132,330 deaths [8]. The two major lung cancer subgroups are non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC) (80-85% of lung cancers) and small-cell lung cancers (SCLC) (10-15% of all lung cancers). NSCLC is comprised of two major subtypes; lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD, which accounts for about 40% of all lung cancers) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC, which accounts for about 30% of all lung cancers). LUSC and LUAD are treated differently and exhibit different survival outcomes. LUAD and LUSC have shared mutations in selected genes; TP53, RB1, ARID1A, CDKN2A, PIK3CA, and NF1, although the frequency of TP53, CDKN2A, and PIK3CA mutations is higher in LUSC [[9], [10]]. Similarly, 26% of focal deletions and 21% of focal amplifications are shared for both histological subtypes [11]. However, the majority of deletions and amplifications are distinct between the histological subtypes, and mutations such as STK11and KRAS are much more frequent in LUAD while mutations in KMT2D, HRAS, and CDKN2A are more common in LUSC. Structural alterations including MET, NTRK2, ALK, RET, and ROS1 fusion are more often observed in LUAD [[9], [10], [12], [13]]. Homozygous deletion (HMD) occurs when both copies of allelic region are deleted and heterozygous deletion (HZD) occurs when only one allelic region is deleted. Prior studies of lung cancer have not studied whether acquired uniparental disomy (aUPD) is correlated with the development of HMD or HZD, or the effects of these different kinds of structural rearrangements on survival for these two lung cancer subtypes. The development of HMD is relevant for tumorigenesis because it results in no functional copies of genes in the affected region, and indicates regions where tumor suppressor loci are likely to occur.

In this study, we evaluated whole chromosome, whole chromosome-arm, and segmental region aUPD and found that the CDKN2A/B region was the most frequent region of aUPD and HMD in both LUAD and LUSC. Therefore, we focused on the CDKN2A/B region, and found that even though aUPD and HMD were frequent events in both lung cancer subtypes, but the association with outcome was different between LUAD and LUSC. Our data support the concept that even though these two lung cancer subtypes share some aUPD and HMD regions, they are different diseases with different underlying mechanisms of etiology and progression. Previously, we had shown that aUPD at the CDKN2A/B region was associated with poorer survival in head and neck cancer samples. Our data indicate that aUPD at the CDKN2A/B region is associated with a similar poor survival effect on outcome in LUAD.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

We retrieved SNP-based microarray data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (stored in the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Clinical and expression data were retrieved from the XENA cancer browser website (https://xenabrowser.net/). Mutation data were acquired from cBioPortal (https;//cbioportal.org/study). In this study, we included only LUSC and LUAD samples. We excluded small cell (1), papillary squamous cell carcinoma (4), and basaloid squamous cell carcinoma samples (10 samples) from LUSC cohort. The total number of samples included 1754 with 808 from LUSC (404 tumors and 404 matching normal samples) and 946 from LUAD (473 tumors and 473 matching normal samples). Although LUAD is heterogeneous and has multiple subtypes, including lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary, solid, invasive mucinous, and colloid adenocarcinoma. We treated LUAD as a single subtype to obtain adequate power, although early-stage LUAD can be further subclassified. Sample demographics are summarized in Supplemental Table S1. The median age at the clinical onset of lung cancer was 67 in LUSC and 68 in LUAD. The gender breakdowns were 256 and 101 female and 217 and 303 male for LUSC and LUAD, respectively. No significant demographic differences were noted. Cancers were also located in five different anatomic sides; left lower, left upper, right lower, right middle, and right lower lobes (Supplemental Table S1).

Acquired uniparental disomy and homozygous deletions analyses

QC (Quality Control) and CHP files (containing gene-level information) were generated by using CEL files (containing probe-level intensities) and Genotyping Console Software (http://tools.thermofisher.com/). Samples passing the threshold (contrast QC ≥ 0.4 and QC call rate ≥ 86) were included in this study. aUPD regions in the tumor were identified using matching normal samples via Copy Number Analyzer for GeneChip software (http://www.genome.umin.jp) as previously described [14,15]. The definition of aUPD is distinct from LOH. There is one copy loss in LOH due to heterozygous deletion, which would result with one copy number (n=1). However, there is no copy number loss in loq 2 ratio in aUPD (n=2). In aUPD, the changes are at the allele level. One allele is deleted and the remaining allele is duplicated. The smallest regions of aUPD were identified by comparing aUPD regions at the 5’ and 3’ endpoints. Gene localization was found by using Dec 2013 human genome browser (NCBI Build 38/hg38; http://genome.ucsc.edu). If a breakpoint occurred in the centromere and included the whole arms of the chromosome, it is described as a whole-chromosome arm (from telomere to centromere) aUPD. If a breakpoint occurred in the telomeric region, this was defined as telomeric aUPD. When a minimum of two breakpoints occurs, it was considered as centromeric or intrinsic aUPD. Telomeric and centromeric aUPDs together are considered segmental. If aUPD occurs over the whole-chromosome, it is considered as whole-chromosome aUPD. An HMD was defined if the log2 ratio is ≤-0.3 in both alleles over a region. When the deleted region was smaller than 5Mb, it was considered a focal deletion. aUPD is defined as an allele-based change, and both alleles originate from the same parent. If the existing chromosome carried a deletion, aUPD leads to HMD in these regions. However, not all HMD regions were associated with aUPD. Some HMDs map to common or rare fragile sites, and some are located in large genes or tumor suppressor genes (Supplemental Fig S1).

Statistical analysis

The Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to compare aUPD and HMD among stages, anatomic sides, and age. Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed to compare aUPD and HMD between gender, locations, and genome-doubling status. Pearson's chi-squared test was used to compare HMD between groups. Whole genome-doubling (WGD) status was determined in TCGA tumor types, as reported by Taylor and colleagues [16]. Cox proportional hazard model was used for univariate and multivariate survival analyses. Kaplan-Meier plot was used to estimate overall survival and recurrence-free survival. Student's t-test was used to compare gene expression between groups. Gene enrichment analysis was performed by using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [17,18]. Lymphoid cell fractions were obtained from Aran et al [19]. The total 64 immune and stromal cell types including lymphoid, myeloid, stromal, stem, and other cell populations were determined based on RNA-seq and microarrays data by using xCell tool [19]. Lymphoid cells include CD+ memory T-cells, CD4+ naïve T-cells, CD+ T-cells, Tcm cells, Tem cells, CD+ Tcm, CD+Tem, CD8+ T-cells, CD8+ naïve T-cells, CD8+ Tcm, CD8+ Tem, Tregs, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, Tgd cells, NK cells, NKT, B-cells, naïve B-cells, Memory B-cells, class-switched memory B-cell, pro-B-cells, plasma cells. Correlation analysis was performed by linear regression model. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare lymphoid cell fractions between the following groups: 1) Samples with aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B region and samples with no copy number or allele-based changes at CDKN2A/B region. 2) Samples with gain, deletion or aUPD at HLA class I region and samples with no copy number or allele-based changes in the same region. Samples that did not exhibit the same aberration across the complete HLA region were excluded from this analysis. STATA was used to perform all statistical analyses (College Station, TX). Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted False Discovery Rate (q-value) was used for statistical analyses. Q-values less than 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment were considered significant.

Results

Acquired uniparental disomy

aUPD is important because it leads to homozygosity for existing alterations. When there are mutations or epigenetic changes on a chromosome, aUPD can lead to differential regulation of RNA transcript which can result in a more aggressive phenotype as well as associated with recurrence and metastasis. In order to determine common and distinctive aUPD regions in LUAD and LUSC, first we compared frequency of total, telomeric, centromeric, segmental, whole chromosome arms and whole chromosome aUPD. We found that total frequency of aUPD was significantly higher in LUSC 82.7% (334/404) than LUAD 58.6% (277/473) (q = 5.34E-09, Fig. 1A and Supplemental Figs. S2 and S3A). Of the total 1952 aUPD identified (mean 9.6, median 4) in LUSC, 903 (mean 4.5, median 2) were telomeric and 927 (mean 4.6, median 1) were centromeric (also called intrinsic), which all together were called segmental [1830 (mean 9.1, median 3)]. The remaining LUSC aUPD consisted of 130 (mean 0.6, median 0) whole chromosomes, 104 (mean 0.5, median 0) whole p-arm, and 81 (mean 0.4, median 0) whole q-arm. In LUAD, the total number of aUPD was 1392 (mean 3.0, median 1), of which 559 (mean 1.2, median 0) were telomeric, and 728 (mean 1.5, median 0) were centromeric comprising segmental aUPD [n = 1287 with mean 2.7 and median 1)], and 108 aUPD (mean 0.2, median 0) were whole-chromosome, 57 (mean 0.1, median 0) whole p-arm, and 57 (mean 0.1, median 0) whole q-arm. The telomeric (q = 6.89E-13), centromeric (q = 2.76E-04), segmental (q = 5.34E-09), whole p-arm (q = 8.99E-05), and whole q-arm (q = 7.67E-03) aUPD were significantly higher in LUSC than in LUAD, except for whole-chromosome aUPD, which was not statistically different (q = 0.051) (Supplemental Fig. S3A).

Association of aUPD, WGD, and EGFR and KRAS mutations and clinipathologic features

EGFR and KRAS mutations are significant players in LUAD. In addition, WGD is an important event in cancer. Therefore, we tested whether aUPD associated with WGD, EGFR or KRAS mutations, and found a significant association between WGD and aUPD in LUAD (q = 0.0009) and LUSC (q = 0.0009) (Fig. 1B and C and Supplemental Fig. S3B and C). In contrast, we observed no difference in the frequency of aUPD between samples with and without EGFR (q = 0.23) or KRAS (q = 0.48) mutations (Fig. 1D and E and Supplemental Fig. S3D and E) in LUAD. We also explored the association between aUPD and stage, gender, age, anatomic site, and location in LUAD and LUSC, and histotypes in LUAD and found association only between age and aUPD in LUAD and LUSC, and between aUPD and upper anatomic site in LUAD (Supplemental Fig. S4).

Frequency of aUPD in whole chromosomes and chromosomes arms level

aUPD occurs at either segmental, whole-chromosome or chromosome-arm level. The mechanism of each type may different. Therefore, we tested the frequency, distribution of aUPD at chromosome-arm and whole-chromosome level, and specific aUPD for LUSC and LUAD. In LUSC, the most frequent aUPD in 404 tumors occurred at chromosome 17p (24.5%), 9p (23.8%), and 17q (22.5%) (Fig. 2A). In LUAD, frequently affected chromosomes included 6p (10.8%), 13q (9.9%), and 14q (9.5%) (Fig. 2A). When we evaluated whole-chromosome aUPD in LUSC, aUPD was observed in all chromosomes except for chromosome 3, with the most frequent aUPD affecting chromosomes 17 (5.5%), 21 (3.7%), 22 (3.2%), and 13 (3.0%) (Fig. 2B). In LUAD, whole-chromosome aUPD was found in all chromosomes with the most frequent on chromosome 3 (2.1%), 14 (2.1%), 12 (1.9%), and 21 (1.9%). aUPD in whole-chromosome 3 was exclusively observed in LUAD. aUPDs at chromosomes 13, 14, and 21 were common in both LUSC and LUAD (Fig. 2B). When we compared whole p-arms, we found aUPD of chromosome 17p (6.4%) and 9p (4.2%) were dominant in LUSC, and 17p (2.1%) and 6p (1.9%) were the most frequent in LUAD (Fig. 2C). The most common whole q-arm aUPD involved chromosome 1q (5.7%) and 9q (4.7%) in LUSC, and chromosome 3q (2.1%) and 1q (1.5%) in LUAD (Fig. 2D). The most recurrent regional aUPD in LUSC were at TP53 (17p), 9p (CDKN2A, CDKN2B), KCTD2 (17q), RBX1, TCF20, XRCC6 (21q) in LUSC, and TP53 (17p), HLA, MUC21 (6p), and MUC16, PDE4A, KEAP1, SMARCA4 (19p) in LUAD.

Prevalence of HMD in LUSC and LUAD

We found significantly higher HMD in patients with LUSC (total 402 HMD, 42.8%, 173 of 404 tumors) compared to those with LUAD (total 198 HMD, 20.3%, 96 of 473 tumors) (q = 6.38E-07) (Fig. 3A). Of the 402 HMD in LUSC, 367 were focal, and 34 were larger than 5 Mb. Of the 198 HMD in LUAD, 177 were focal, and 15 were larger than 5 Mb. The length of HMD regions was significantly smaller in LUSC (mean 803,580 and median 366,602) than in LUAD (mean 1,043,222 and median 588,962) (q = 0.02) (Fig. 3B). HMDs can associate with aUPD, and also can occur in fragile sites and other regions.

Frequency of HMDs that associate with aUPD common fragile sites and other regions

aUPD leads to homozygosity for existing alterations such as mutations or deletions and can thus lead to HMD regions. Thus, we tested whether HMD was associated with aUPD. A subset of HMD regions were associated with aUPD in LUSC (24.7%) and LUAD (18.7%) (q = 0.50) (Table 1 and Fig. 3C). Moreover, HMD also occurs in fragile sites. A chromosomal fragile site is a specific heritable point on a chromosome prone to form a gap and has the potential to break when the cell is exposed to replication stress. Fragile sites are further classified as a common or rare based on their occurrence rate. We found that only 13.2% and 6.1% of HMDs were at common fragile sites in LUSC and LUAD (q = 0.20), respectively (Table 1 and Fig. 4C). The length of HMD associated with aUPD was significantly larger than HMD at common fragile sites in LUSC (q = 0.001) (Fig. 3D) and LUAD (q = 0.001) (Fig. 3E). The remaining HMD could be in the regions that contain large genes, or tumor suppressors, essential or other genes, or at rare fragile sites (Fig. 3C).

Association of HMD with clinicopathologic features

In addition to aUPD, we also tested whether HMDs were associated with stage, location, anatomic site, age, gender and WGD. When we compared HMD between stages (I and II versus III and IV), locations (central and peripheral parenchyma), anatomic sites (lower and upper lobes), earlier and older ages, and gender, we found significant differences only in gender with HMD being significantly higher in males compared to females (q = 0.01) (Supplemental Fig. S5A) in LUSC. No differences were observed among histologic subtypes, stage, gender, age, location, and anatomic sites in LUAD (Supplemental Fig. S5B). No association was found between HMD and WGD in LUAD (q = 0.20), while an association was found between HMD and WGD in LUSC (q = 0.02).

ORF genes in the most frequent HMD regions in LUSC and LUAD

Deleted regions contain genes that negatively regulate cell growth. Deletions of tumor suppressor genes or non-coding RNAs could confer a selective growth advantage and more aggressive phenotype to the cancer cells. The shape of a deleted region is determined at least, in part, by the balance of the removal of genes that negatively regulate growth with the deletion of genes that positively regulate growth. We therefore assessed the most frequent HMD regions in LUSC and LUAD. In LUSC, the most prevalent HMD regions were located at chromosome 9p (30.6%, 123 of 402 HMD), at 2q (10.0%), 8p (9.0%), and 3p (8.0%) (Fig. 4A). The most recurrent HMD was in the LRP1B loci (6.47%) at chromosome 2q, and HMD in this gene was associated with fragile sites. HMD in CSMD1 (7.7%) at chromosome 8p, and FHIT at 3p (1.49%) were frequent in LUSC. The number of genes located in HMD regions ranged from 0 to 325 in LUSC and from 0 to 173 in LUAD. In addition to LRP1B, CSMD1, and FHIT, partial deletions of CTNNA3, VTI1A, PDE4D, and PTPRD were observed. The number of deleted exons/introns was between one exon/intron and 28 exons in LUSC and between one exon/intron and 32 exons in LUAD. Some of these partial HMD genes are at fragile sites such as LRP1B, FHIT, and MACROD2, and some were in large genes such as CSMD1.

miRNAs in the most significant HMD regions in LUSC and LUAD

miRNAs play important regulatory roles in cells. Therefore, the loss of miRNAs in HMD deleted regions could affect the expression of genes and consequently their function through the loss of regulatory effects. Therefore, we assessed miRNAs in HMD regions. Interestingly, 15.2% of HMD regions harbored miRNAs, with the most frequent being miR31 (8.0%) in the CDKN2A/B region in LUSC. In LUAD, the most frequent HMD regions were chromosome 9p (39.4%, 78 of 198 total HMD), 21q (5.1%), and 5q (4.6%) (Fig. 4B). 21.2% of HMD regions included miRNAs, with miR31 (9.1%), once again being the most frequent. The most common recurrent HMD region across LUSC (30.6%) and LUAD (39.4%) was at chromosome 9p (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, a significantly higher portion of HMD at chromosome 9p was associated with aUPD (13.7%) in LUSC and only 5.6% in LUAD (q < 0.0001). HMD associated aUPD at the CDKN2A/B region was significantly higher in LUSC (8.71%) compared to LUAD (3.03%) (q < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C).

Association of aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B with overall survival in LUAD

We found that the CDKN2A/B region was the most frequently observed in both LUAD and LUSC. Therefore, next we focused on the CDKN2A/B region and tested the association for 5-year overall survival (OS) and/or recurrence-free survival (RFS). In LUAD, we found that HMD at CDKN2A/B region was associated with worsened OS compared to having neither HMD, HZD nor aUPD at the same region (CDKN2A/B negative group) (q = 0.005) (Fig. 5A) and compared to patients with HZD (q = 0.05) (Fig. 5B). In contrast, OS in cases with HZD of CDKN2A/B were not significantly different from the CDKN2A/B aberration negative group (q = 0.34) (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, patients with aUPD at CDKN2A/B region were also associated with poorer OS (q = 0.02) (Fig. 5D) compared to the CDKN2A/B aberration-negative group. However, no association was found between OS and aUPD when compared to cases with HZD at the same region (q = 0.13) (Fig. 5E). We also assessed two driver genes, EGFR and KRAS mutations for effects on survival and found that EGFR mutations (q = 0.40) or KRAS mutations (q = 0.34) were not associated with OS.

Association of aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B region with recurrence-free survival in LUAD

We also assessed aUPD and HMD at CDKN2A/B region for 5-year recurrence-free survival (RFS), and found that HMD at the CDKN2A/B region was associated with shorter RFS compared to the CDKN2A/B aberration negative group (q = 0.005) (Fig. 6A), and compared to samples with HZD (q = 0.02) (Fig. 6B). We also found that aUPD at the CDKN2A/B region was associated with shorter RFS compared to the CDKN2A/B negative group (q = 0.005) (Fig. 6D) and compared to HZD at the same region (q = 0.02) (Fig. 6E), Similar to OS, no association was found between EGFR mutations (q = 0.74), KRAS mutations (q = 0.87) and RFS in LUAD. In multivariate analysis (including the following variables: stage, age, EGFR, and KRAS mutations), aUPD at CDKN2A/B region was associated with shorter RFS (q = 0.02), but not associated with OS (q = 0.07), while HMD at CDKN2A/B region was associated with worse OS (q = 0.03) and RFS (q = 0.005) (Supplemental Fig. S6A and S6B).

Association of aUPD or HMD with survival in EGFR or KRAS mutated LUAD samples

We divided samples by KRAS and EGFR mutations status in LUAD and found aUPD and HMD at CDKN2A/B region were significantly associated with poor RFS in KRAS mutation-negative (q = 0.02 and q = 0.02) and EGFR mutation-negative (q = 0.01 and q < 0.0001) groups. HMD at CDKN2A/B region was also associated with shorter OS in KRAS (q = 0.01) and EGFR mutation-negative group (q = 0.008) in LUAD. Interestingly, the majority of samples with aUPD (62.5%) and HMD (68.42%) at CDKN2A/B region were in stage I and II LUAD (Supplemental Fig. S6C and S6D).

Association of aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B with survival in LUSC

In contrast, in LUSC, even though most of the samples with aUPD (74.60%) and HMD (84.44%) at the CDKN2A/B region were in stage I and II, no association was found between OS and HMD (q = 0.97) (Fig. 5G) and aUPD (q = 0.97) (Fig. 5H). Moreover, no association was found between RFS and HMD (q = 0.81) (Fig. 6G), and individuals with aUPD at CDKN2A/B (q = 0.71) (Fig. 6H) in LUSC.

Expression of genes and mutations in samples with aUPD and HMD at CDKN2A/B

Then, we tested whether expression level of genes are different between samples with HMD or aUPD at CDKN2A/B and the CDKN2A/B negative group. By gene enrichment analysis, we found a statistically significant enrichment for immune pathways in both groups (Supplemental Table S2A and S2B). CTLA4 was expressed at significantly lower levels in the aUPD or HMD CDKN2A/B positive group compared to the CDKN2A/B negative group (Supplemental Fig. S7A). Expression of PD-L1 was also significantly lower in HMD samples compared to the CDKN2A/B negative group, but no difference was observed between aUPD positive and negative samples (Supplemental Fig. S7B). Other significantly lower expressed genes in the CDKN2A/B positive group included CD27, CD37, CD79A, CD1E, CD4, CD19 (Supplemental Fig. S7C-H), CD88, CD80, CD86, CD8A, and CD84. In contrast, expression of KDM1A, MCM2, MCM4, and MCM6 was significantly higher in the aUPD positive group, while expression of those genes was not different between the HMD positive and negative groups (Supplemental Fig. S7I, J, K, and L). When we compared mutations between samples with aUPD and HMD at CDKN2A/B to the negative group, we found 41 mutated genes were mutually exclusive in the CDKN2A/B negative group. Three of those genes, RB1, MET, and BRCA1 were mutated in 5.6%, 4.4%, and 4.0% of CDKN2A/B negative group, respectively.

Correlation between whole chromosome or chromosome arms and lymphoid cell fraction

Based on our survival data in the CDKN2A/B region, we tested whether lymphoid cell fractions were different between the samples with aUPD or homozygous deletion, and no changes. In LUAD, we found lower lymphoid fraction in samples with aUPD or homozygous deletion at CDKN2A/B compared to the negative group (Supplemental Fig. S8). We also observed inverse correlations with lymphoid cell fractions and aUPD at CDKN2A/B region (r = -0.193, p = 0.033, CI: -0.369 to -0.016), and with CDKN2A/B deleted samples (r = -0.256, p = 0.001, CI: -0.399 to -0.112), with whole-chromosome q arm aUPD (r = -0.162, p = 0.011, CI:-0.287 to -0.037) and with whole-chromosome aUPD (r = - 0.200, p < 0.0001, CI: -0.305 to -0.096) in LUAD (Supplemental Fig. S8). In addition, based on previous data in the literature, we tested whether lymphoid cell fractions were different among the samples with gain, aUPD, deletion, and no changes in the HLA class I region in chromosome 6p. We found lower lymphoid cell fraction in samples with gain the HLA class I region compared to samples without change in the counterpart region, but no difference was observed between the samples with loss or aUPD at HLA class I region and samples without change in the same region. We also observed inverse correlation with gain at the HLA region and lymphoid cell fraction (r = -0.05, p = 0.011, CI: - 0.09 to – 0.01).

When we performed the same analyses in LUSC we found no difference in lymphoid cell fraction between samples with in the aUPD or HMD CDKN2A/B positive group versus samples in the CDKN2A/B aUPD and HMD negative group. Moreover, no difference was observed in lymphoid cell fraction in samples with copy number gain or loss or aUPD in HLA class I region versus samples with no change in the same region.

Discussion

We examined the genome-wide aUPD pattern of 404 LUSC and 473 LUAD to identify shared and distinct regions. As aUPD can lead to homozygosity for existing alterations, we investigated HMD regions to identify the frequency with which aUPD was associated with HMD. Consistent with studies of copy number alterations [20], both aUPD and HMDs have shared and distinct regions between the two NSCLC subtypes. The differences between subtypes may be due to the cell of origin, cellular context, heterogeneity of tumor, and exposure to carcinogenic agents. Exposure to different environmental factors, and the intrinsic or germline genomic architecture of an individual may also influence the genomic changes in the tumor.

In general, the frequency of aUPD and HMD was significantly higher in LUSC than in LUAD. aUPD of whole-chromosome 3 was specific to LUAD, while aUPD in whole p-arm at chromosome 7 was specific to LUSC. The frequency of aUPD in whole-chromosome 17, in whole p-arm at chromosome 5p, 9p, and 17p, and whole q-arm at 1q and 9q were significantly higher in LUSC compared to LUAD. However, not all aUPD regions are enriched in LUSC as aUPD in the whole q-arm at chromosome 3q was significantly higher in LUAD than in LUSC. Similarly, when segmental aUPDs were included, aUPD at chromosome 6p was significantly higher in LUAD than in LUSC. Other regions of aUPD were found in both LUAD and LUSC including TP53 (17p), and CDKN2A/B (9p).

In late-stage tumors, total aUPD was significantly higher in LUSC. Further aUPD and upper lung-located tumors in LUAD, and aUPD in males were associated in LUSC for reasons that remain to be determined but may relate to differences in exposure to tobacco carcinogens.

Moreover, we observed a significant association between genome-doubling and the frequency of aUPD in both LUAD and LUSC. Previously, an association was found between WGD and sub-clonal mutations, survival, copy-number alterations, and TP53 mutations [16,[20], [21], [22]]. TP53 mutations were also associated with aUPD [5]. Taken together, data indicates that TP53 may play a role in the formation of aUPD and genome-doubling.

When we examined HMD regions, we found a significantly higher portion of HMD was due to aUPD than to common fragile sites in both LUSC and LUAD. However, a significant number of HMDs were not in aUPD region or at common fragile sites, consistent with a previous study in cancer cell lines [23]. These HMDs may arise at rare fragile sites, in large genes like CSMD1, or tumor suppressor genes. The shape of a deleted region is determined at least in part by the balance of the removal of genes that negatively regulate growth with the removal of genes that positively regulate growth. The difference in the frequency and shape of HMD regions in LUSC and LUAD may reflect relative roles for tumor suppressors or growth promoting genes in the pathophysiogy of these diseases and potentially reflect differences in co-abberations in each tumor type.

Interestingly, aUPD at the TP53 region was recurrent in LUSC and LUAD but was not associated with HMD. A significant portion of HMD at CDKN2A/B region, which was frequently affected in both LUSC and LUAD was associated with aUPD in LUSC, but not in LUAD. These data suggest that while the same genes have been inactivated in LUSC and LUAD resulting in convergent evolution, the mechanisms underlying inactivation in LUAD and LUSC are distinct. As HMD arising through aUPD could have different functional impacts than HMD arising through other processes, identification of mechanisms for the occurrence of somatic alterations is crucial for a better understanding of the pathophysiology of lung cancers.

HMD regions associated with common fragile sites were significantly higher in LUSC than in LUAD. However, the length of HMD was significantly larger in LUAD than in LUSC. Common fragile sites are prone to breakage upon replication stress [24]. Exposure to environmental factors such as UV, smoking, and Benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide can induce alterations in FHIT and WWOX in lung cancer [25], [26], [27]. Deregulated Cyclin E causes cells to enter mitosis with uncompleted replicated regions, including common fragile sites and late replicating sites contributing to genomic alterations [28]. Taken together, these extrinsic and intrinsic risk factors may lead to the accumulation of HMD providing growth advantages for cancer cells.

Common fragile sites can occur due to replication stress during the early stages of tumorigenesis [24], or due to changes in DNA repair [29], [30], [31]. aUPD may also occur due to replication stress and defective DNA repair and contribute to these processes [4,32]. Thus increased replication stress or defective DNA repair could contribute to the higher frequency of aUPD in LUSC compared to LUAD. aUPD and HMD at fragile sites may thus serve as biomarkers that could identify patients that could benefit from drugs targeting replication stress such as ATR or WEE1 inhibitors or DNA damage repair such as PARP inhibitors.

Interestingly, consistent with previous reports HMD at CDKN2A/B were associated with shorter OS and RFS in LUAD [33,34]. In this study, association between aUPD at CDKN2A/B region worsened survival was also observed. However, when we divided samples by EGFR and KRAS mutation status, aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B region were associated with the EGFR and KRAS mutation-negative group. This data indicates that samples with homozygosity at CDKN2A/B region may need to be considered as a separate subgroup from patients with EGFR and/or KRAS mutations in LUAD.

Interestingly, HZD of CDKN2A/B was not associated with OS and/or RFS in LUAD. Moreover, RB1, BRCA1, and MET mutations were mutually exclusive to samples without CDKN2A/B aberrations in LUAD. The result from our study indicates that samples with homozygous CDKN2A/B regions and samples with RB1, BRCA1, or MET mutations are two different subgroups of LUAD. Although aUPD and HMD were frequent events in both lung cancer subtypes, the association with outcome was different between these lung cancer subtypes. Our data support the concept that even though these two lung cancer subtypes share some aUPD and HMD regions, they are different diseases with different underlying mechanisms of etiology and progression. Previously, we had shown that aUPD at the CDKN2A/B region was associated with worsened survival in head and neck cancer samples [6]. Our data indicate that aUPD at the CDKN2A/B region in LUAD is associated with a similar effect on outcomes as in head and neck cancer.

In contrast, HMD and aUPD were not associated with OS and RFS in LUSC. These data indicate that although the same alterations exist in both subtypes, their impact on outcome is different. Moreover, most aUPD and HMD at CDKN2A/B were identified in stage I and II NSCLC, suggesting that homozygosity of CDKN2A/B may play a role in progression for early-stage lung adenocarcinoma.

We found cell cycle and immune regulatory genes were significantly differentially expressed in samples with aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B compared to samples without copy number and allele-based changes in CDKN2A/B region. Expression of PD-L1 was lower in HMD-positive samples, while expression of CTLA4 was significantly lower in HMD and aUPD-positive samples. CD27, CD37, CD79A, CD1E, CD4, and CD19 were expressed at lower levels in CDKN2A/B positive samples. Recently, it has been reported that deletion at CDKN2A/B region predicted poor response to single-agent immunotherapy [33], which is compatible with the association with the expression of immune markers and checkpoint inhibitors in samples with aberrations at CDKN2A/B. In addition, we observed an inverse correlation between aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B region, gain at HLA class I genes aUPD at whole-chromosome q-arm and whole-chromosome and lymphoid cell fraction in LUAD, but not in LUSC. The association of aUPD or HMD at CDKN2A/B region with changes in immune characteristics could explore the worse OS and RFS in LUAD. In addition, the correlation between the gain in the HLA genes at chromosome 6p and low lymphoid infiltrate is in agreement with a previous report [35]. Together this data supports an association between aUPD and the tumor immune environment. Taken together, data indicate that samples with HMD and aUPD in CDKN2A/B region may not benefit from immune checkpoint inhibition in LUAD. In conclusion, the status of CDKN2A/B and HLA class I region, and whole chromosome or whole q-arm aUPD may contribute to treatment decisions in LUAD. Both LUSC and LUAD have common and distinct patterns of aUPD regions with differing frequencies of occurrence and associations with outcome.
