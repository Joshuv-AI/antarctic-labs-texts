Reimagining Phage Therapy for MDR Pathogens: From Biobanks to Health System Integration—A Review
Authors: Abay A Ayele, Wenfeng Liu, Minfeng Xiao, Alem A Kalayu, Woldaregay E Abegaz, Abel A Negash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, exemplified by Klebsiella pneumoniae, pose a critical and escalating threat in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where limited diagnostic and therapeutic options exacerbate mortality. Bacteriophage (phage) therapy has re-emerged as a promising alternative, offering high specificity, biofilm disruption, and potential for low-cost production. However, a significant translational gap persists; while in vitro studies and results personalized phage therapy demonstrate efficacy, the pathway to clinical integration, especially in LMICs, remains obstructed by systemic barriers including the lack of local phage banks and undefined regulatory pathways. This review moves beyond a summary of biological promise to propose a novel, structured framework for implementation tailored to resource-limited health systems. We synthesize current evidence to introduce the 5-P roadmap, a cohesive strategy encompassing the establishment of integrated phage–host biobanks; development of context-appropriate preparations; rigorous preclinical validation emphasizing phage–antibiotic synergy; the parallel development of policy and regulatory pathways; and pilot clinical trials designed for subsequent health system integration. By addressing these interconnected pillars simultaneously, this framework provides an actionable blueprint to advance phage therapy from a laboratory concept to a scalable, equitable, and sustainable adjunct within national antimicrobial resistance strategies in LMICs.Keywords: Bacteriophage therapy, Multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, Antimicrobial resistance, Low-resource settings

Key Summary Points

Antimicrobial resistance has renewed interest in bacteriophage therapy, but its clinical translation remains limited by regulatory, manufacturing, and implementation challenges.This article reviews the current landscape of phage therapy development, focusing on scientific, regulatory, and logistical barriers to routine clinical use.We synthesize recent advances in phage discovery, characterization, and application, including personalized and compassionate-use approaches.Emerging regulatory frameworks, such as national guidelines for magistral phage preparations, are highlighted as key enablers of hospital-based phage therapy.The work identifies critical gaps and priorities needed to support safe, scalable, and evidence-based integration of phage therapy into modern healthcare.

Introduction

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections have become one of the most pressing global health threats, placing renewed strain on health systems and patient outcomes. The erosion of antibiotic effectiveness is particularly devastating in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where constrained diagnostic capacity, limited therapeutic options, and high infection burdens amplify the clinical and economic toll. Among MDR pathogens, MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae exemplifies the crisis: it is a leading cause of hospital-acquired infections and sepsis, and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) infections have been associated with mortality rates of 30–50% in many settings [1]. Prevalence of CRKP among K. pneumoniae infections globally is about 29%, though in some regions such as in South Asia, Greece, and India prevalence has been reported to exceed 65–70% [2–5]. The stagnation in antibiotic discovery [6, 7] and the rapid evolution of resistance mechanisms demand alternative treatment strategies that are effective, scalable, and adaptable to resource-limited health systems. This context has catalyzed renewed interest in bacteriophage therapy as a promising tool to combat antimicrobial resistance.

Bacteriophages (phages) selectively infect and lyse bacteria, offering distinct advantages over traditional antibiotics, including high specificity, biofilm disruption, and minimal impact to the host microbiota [8]. Given their low-cost scalability and potential for local sourcing, and adaptability to regional pathogen landscapes, phages offer unique advantages in resource-limited settings which bear the greatest burden of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR). where traditional antibiotic pipelines are failing. Recent studies from LMICs, such as phage isolation from wastewater in Tunisia and Bangladesh, and phage–antibiotic synergy experiments in Kenya, illustrate both real-world feasibility and therapeutic potential [9–11]. Notably, phage therapy also demonstrates superior activity against biofilm-associated infections [12, 13], a particularly relevant feature for device-related complications in underresourced healthcare systems [14–18].

Despite the growing global interest in phage therapy, research specific to low-resource health system contexts remains scarce in scientific literature. This limited evidence base not only reflects underrepresentation of LMIC settings but also contributes to broader challenges in translating phage therapy into practice, including regulatory uncertainty, limited infrastructure, and inequitable access between urban referral hospitals and rural clinics. Addressing this gap therefore requires integrated strategies that couple scientific investigation with health system strengthening and policy development tailored to LMIC realities.

This Review examines how phage research and the use of phage therapy can be promoted in LMICs and proposes a strategy for a scalable, context-appropriate phage research and clinical use in LMICs, using K. pneumoniae as a representative MDR pathogen. In this review, we go beyond conventional summaries of phage biology to highlight underexplored dimensions, such as integration with one health, links to existing AMR surveillance networks, and ethical frameworks for vulnerable patient populations. Finally, we propose a structured, phased roadmap, the 5-P framework, that emphasizes phage–host banks, preparations, preclinical validation, policy/Regulation, and pilot trials as interconnected pillars for translation. This Review aims to spark new thinking on how global phage innovation can be responsibly adapted to LMIC realities, offering a transferable model for equitable, sustainable implementation.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Phage Biology and Therapeutic Principles

Phages are viruses that selectively infect and lyse bacterial hosts, offering a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics, particularly in the fight against MDR pathogens such as K. pneumoniae. Their natural biology underpins several properties highly desirable in antimicrobial therapy. The phage life cycle typically involves receptor-mediated attachment, injection of the viral genome, and either lytic replication, resulting in bacterial destruction or, in temperate phages, lysogenic integration into the host genome [19, 20]. For therapeutic applications, strictly lytic phages are preferred owing to their rapid bactericidal activity and minimal risk of horizontal gene transfer.

Beyond their ability to lyse bacteria, phages possess unique therapeutic advantages rooted in their biological specificity. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, which indiscriminately target both pathogens and commensals, phages act with remarkable strain-level precision [21]. This not only minimizes microbiota disruption but also lowers the risk of secondary infections and immunological imbalance, critical considerations in settings with fragile healthcare infrastructure and high patient comorbidity.

Phages can also overcome one of the most intractable problems in infectious disease: bacterial biofilms. These structured communities, shielded by extracellular polymeric substances, are notoriously resistant to antibiotics and host immunity. Many lytic phages encode depolymerases that degrade biofilm matrices, exposing embedded bacteria to phage attack and resensitizing them to co-administered antimicrobials [22–24]. This dual activity, lysis and biofilm degradation, has been demonstrated in diverse MDR pathogens such as Klebsiella strains and forms the mechanistic basis for both monophage and cocktail-based therapeutic strategies [25, 26].

However, deploying a single phage (monophage therapy) can be undermined by narrow host range and rapid resistance emergence. To mitigate this, phage cocktails, blends of multiple complementary phages, have become a preferred formulation. Cocktails offer broader coverage and reduce the likelihood of bacterial escape through mutation [8]. Further refinement has led to the use of phage-derived lytic enzymes, such as endolysins and depolymerases, which can act independently of viable phage particles and be engineered for enhanced potency [27].

Building on these strategies, a growing movement advocates for personalized phage therapy, matching specific phages to patient-isolated strains [28]. This approach is enabled by regional phage banks that catalog locally active isolates for rapid deployment. Since pathogens often develop regionally unique resistance mechanisms and diversity which often differ from global reference strains, localized phage resources can significantly accelerate effective treatment [29–32].

Another frontier in therapeutic application is the synergistic use of phages with antibiotics. This phage–antibiotic synergy can enhance efficacy through mechanisms such as membrane destabilization or improved intracellular access [29, 33, 34]. Conversely, some antibiotics may increase bacterial susceptibility to phages by altering surface receptor expression [35]. This bidirectional interaction has been validated in both in vitro and clinical studies, with early evidence showing improved bacterial clearance and delayed resistance emergence when combinations are used [36, 37].

Delivering phages effectively requires context-sensitive approaches tailored to infection sites and healthcare logistics. Topical formulations are effective for skin or wound infections [37]; oral delivery suits gastrointestinal conditions but often requires protective encapsulation [38]. Intravenous routes offer systemic access but must contend with immune clearance [39]. Inhalation, intravesical, and catheter-based administrations have all been explored with promising results for respiratory and urogenital infections, including K. pneumoniae-induced pneumonia [40, 41].

Compared to antibiotics, phage therapy offers several systemic advantages: specificity, co-evolutionary adaptability, low toxicity, and microbiota preservation. While phage stability remains a concern, especially under temperature stress, the development of thermostable and encapsulated formulations is rapidly progressing. In resource-limited settings, the low-cost sourcing of phages from local environments and their potential for rapid, decentralized propagation further enhance their appeal as a sustainable therapeutic solution (Table 1).

Barriers to Implementation of Phage Therapies in Low-Resource Settings

While phage therapy offers exceptional target specificity and safety, its long-term effectiveness is challenged by several biological and practical barriers. Chief among these are the rapid evolutionary adaptability of bacteria, the emergence of phage-resistant mutants, and the logistical difficulty of continuously updating phage formulations to match evolving pathogens. Similar to antibiotic resistance, bacterial resistance to phages can arise through mutations in surface receptors, modification or masking of outer structures such as lipopolysaccharides, or activation of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas defense systems that degrade invading phage genomes [54, 55]. This ongoing coevolutionary arms race underscores the need for dynamic therapeutic strategies, including robust phage biobanking capacity and rapid reconfiguration of phage cocktails to counter newly emergent resistant strains.

In high-income countries, this cycle is increasingly supported by real-time genomic surveillance [56], high-throughput phage screening [57], and established phage biobanks [58]. However, in LMICs, these capabilities remain limited. Local pathogen diversity, combined with inadequate access to high-throughput sequencing, regional phage biobank, and diagnostic infrastructure, hinders timely customization of therapies. Without genomic profiling of circulating multidrug-resistant strains and efficient platforms for phage matching, even the most promising phage therapies face implementation bottlenecks. Addressing this gap will require international partnerships that support the co-development of sequencing hubs, local phage biobanks, and local cocktail design pipelines aligned with regional resistance patterns.

Implementation Experiences and Evidence from LMICs

Phage therapy research in LMICs is gaining momentum, with growing efforts directed toward phage isolation and characterization, though clinical translation remains limited [59]. Translation of phage therapy into clinical practice has been hindered by multiple interconnected challenges. Chief among these is the absence of regional phage biobanks and high-throughput screening capacity, which limits timely access to well-characterized therapeutic candidates. These technical gaps are compounded by limited genomic sequencing infrastructure for comprehensive safety evaluation, scarcity of animal and early-phase clinical studies, and the lack of standardized methodologies for phage preparation and testing. In addition, low awareness among clinicians, insufficient funding, weak regulatory frameworks, and a shortage of trained personnel further constrain the development of sustainable phage-therapy programs. Addressing these bottlenecks, particularly through investment in regional biobanking systems, will be critical to enable safe, evidence-based clinical translation in LMIC settings. These limitations hinder the identification of broadly effective phage candidates and delay the development of scalable therapeutic formulations that match local resistance profiles [60, 61].

Multiple studies in LMICs have attempted to isolate phages against MDR K. pneumoniae, but host range has consistently emerged as a limiting factor. For instance, Shah et al. [62] reported that phage RAM-1 was effective against only 3 of 16 clinical K. pneumoniae isolates, while Ananna et al. [63] and Soleimani Sasani and Eftekhar [64] found K. pneumoniae susceptibility rates below 25%, severely narrowing clinical applicability without phage cocktail strategies.

Broad-host-range phage cocktails, such as those formulated in studies by Michodigni et al. [65] and Kumari et al. [66], have shown improved efficacy but introduced additional complexity. These include challenges in ensuring batch consistency, tracking resistance dynamics, and validating safety. Notably, genomic safety assessments remain inconsistently applied across LMIC studies. While some reports identified favorable traits, such as depolymerase activity, absence of integrase or lysogeny genes, and lack of known virulence factors, these remain exceptions rather than standard practice. Without consistent whole-genome sequencing and virulence profiling, clinical confidence in phage safety remains limited.

Moreover, most research remains confined to in vitro assays, with limited extension to animal studies, constraining our understanding of immune clearance, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics. For example, Fayez et al. [67] demonstrated topical efficacy of phage ZCKP8 against K. pneumoniae in a rat wound model, yet key aspects such as systemic delivery, immune clearance, and pharmacokinetics were unaddressed. Overall, animal studies remain scarce and human investigations even rarer, leaving critical questions about systemic administration unresolved. Although delivery strategies such as gel-based and oral formulations have been proposed, few have undergone rigorous validation under physiological conditions.

Notably, a handful of promising translational opportunities are emerging. Phage–antibiotic synergy in vitro such as with imipenem was observed in investigation by Michodigni et al. [65], offering a potential path to revive legacy antibiotics through combination therapy. Furthermore, certain phages such as ZCKP8 and KPO1K2 have been noted for harboring putative depolymerase, suggesting potential biofilm-degrading capabilities in animal models, which is a desirable trait for tackling device-associated infections [67, 68]. While these findings are not yet clinically validated, they highlight functional properties worth pursuing in future trials.

Despite these encouraging findings, most phage studies in LMICs continue to operate under experimental frameworks. Without formal regulatory approval or structured clinical trial frameworks, scalable adoption remains aspirational. As the following section outlines, regulatory uncertainty and manufacturing constraints continue to present some of the steepest obstacles to implementation.

Regulatory and Clinical Approval Challenges

The regulatory landscape for phage therapy remains fragmented and underdeveloped, with challenges spanning both high-income countries (HIC) and LMICs. However, LMICs face deeper and more structural hurdles. Unlike antibiotics, which benefit from standardized dosing, broad-spectrum indications, and mass-manufacturing pipelines, phage therapy’s precision and customizability challenge existing drug approval systems [69–71]. Current frameworks often fail to accommodate the biological variability of phages, including their capacity to evolve, necessitating new regulatory standards for host range determination, genetic stability, and quality control.

In high-income settings, regulatory agencies such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have shown cautious flexibility through expanded access programs and compassionate-use cases [72]. Belgium’s magistral preparation model allows pharmacies to produce patient-specific phage preparations under medical supervision. In November 2024, Portugal’s medicines authority INFARMED issued Deliberação nº 112/CD/2024, enabling the use of bacteriophages as magistral (compounded) preparations in hospital pharmacies for individualized patient treatment. The guideline requires phage products to be clinically justified and prepared according to established pharmacy compounding standards under professional oversight. This framework expands treatment options for difficult or antimicrobial-resistant infections while ensuring quality, safety, and monitoring [73]. In contrast, most LMICs lack even provisional guidelines, resulting in legal ambiguity that discourages clinical use, delays trial initiation, and deters manufacturing investment [74]. To transition phage therapy beyond its current experimental status, LMICs must develop adaptive yet rigorous regulatory pathways that accommodate personalized formulations while upholding safety and efficacy standards. This will likely require regional harmonization, guided by models such as World Health Organization (WHO)’s prequalification programs or regional regulatory harmonization initiatives such as African Medicines Agency. Additionally, incorporating adaptive clinical trial designs, centralized phage banks, and well-defined clinical endpoints could help facilitate more pragmatic and scalable evaluations of phage efficacy.

Manufacturing constraints also limit access to phage therapy. Phage production requires host bacterial strains, bioreactors, and biosafety-compliant protocols. Ensuring the consistency, purity, and endotoxin-free status of preparations demands adherence to Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP), a high-cost standard often unattainable in LMIC contexts [69, 75]. Although limited scalability has been demonstrated, such as phage amplification using E. coli hosts, most LMICs still lack the industrial infrastructure, trained personnel, and regulatory oversight required for large-scale clinical manufacturing [76]. Addressing these limitations will require coordinated investments in biotechnology infrastructure, training, and public–private partnerships. Interim strategies, such as mobile production units, thermostable formulations, and modular GMP-compliant facilities, could provide cost-effective stopgaps while longer-term capacity develops. Ultimately, overcoming these manufacturing and distribution challenges is critical for ensuring equitable access to phage therapy, especially in regions where MDR pathogens are endemic and healthcare systems are most strained [77].

Challenges and Future Directions of Phage Therapy in LMICs

Despite growing global interest in phage therapy, particularly in response to the rising global burden of MDR infections, clinical translation remains limited in LMICs. Clinical case reports and early-phase interventions have demonstrated therapeutic potential in treating severe infections, including bloodstream, urinary tract, and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Many of these successes have emerged under compassionate-use frameworks, where personalized phage preparations, administered as single agents or cocktails, led to bacterial clearance and clinical recovery in patients with antibiotic-refractory infections. Typically, these preparations are tailored through in vitro sensitivity testing and delivered via intratracheal, topical, or intravenous routes, depending on the infection site [78]. While anecdotal and limited in scale, these outcomes reinforce the promise of phage therapy as a safe and viable adjunct or alternative, particularly in high-risk or treatment-resistant populations [79].

Beyond these anecdotal successes, pilot trials conducted in more structured environments have reported measurable improvements, including reduced bacterial load, symptom resolution, and lowered inflammatory markers [80]. Such outcomes lay the groundwork for more definitive phase II and III trials. Randomized controlled trial (RCTs) are underway largely in high-income settings across Europe and the USA [81]. Outside these regions, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial in India on chronic wound infections (CTRI/2021/12/038527; registered on December 24, 2021) demonstrated statistically significant wound sterilization and healing in phage-treated patients, without serious adverse events. [82]. Even so, the global evidence base remains disproportionately skewed toward HICs, leaving LMICs largely underrepresented, where MDR burden is highest. Variations in pathogen prevalence, resistance phenotypes, and health system capacity further limit generalizability, underscoring the need for regionally grounded trials.

An emerging concern in phage therapy is its potential impact on the human microbiome. Although phages are typically strain-specific, repeated or systemic administration may disrupt microbial community structure, leading to dysbiosis, altered immune responses, or other downstream effects, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [42, 52, 83–85]. Given the microbiome’s central role in immunity, metabolism, and inflammation, longitudinal studies incorporating microbiome and immune profiling are essential to evaluate the safety and ecological impact of phage therapy across diverse populations, especially in LMICs where host–pathogen dynamics may differ. At the same time, bacterial resistance to phages, through mechanisms such as receptor modification, CRISPR-Cas systems, and biofilm-mediated shielding, poses a significant challenge to therapeutic durability [86, 87]. However, this coevolutionary tension also drives innovation: rotating phage cocktails, phage–antibiotic combinations, and engineered phages with expanded lytic functions offer promising counter strategies. Advancing these approaches will require large-scale, locally anchored phage biobanks as resource and data foundations, integrated with genomics, systems biology, and computational modeling.

The absence of regional phage biobanks further constrains clinical scalability of phage therapy. When linked to genome sequencing, high-throughput phage screening platforms, and standardized data-sharing systems, such repositories could accelerate local matching against MDR strains and reduce reliance on external inputs. Beyond clinical utility, biobanks also serve as engines for local research, workforce training, and early-stage manufacturing capacity. Yet in LMICs, these systems remain scarce and underfunded, delaying translation from laboratory discovery to bedside application.

Phage production, formulation, and administration pose additional, practical hurdles in LMICs. High ambient temperatures and uneven cold-chain capacity jeopardize phage stability during storage and distribution, while advanced stabilization options—lyophilization, encapsulation, and nanostructured formulations—are not yet routine because of cost, scalability, and operational complexity [88]. The absence of treatment standards, evident in heterogeneous dosing (titers, frequency, duration), complicates both clinical implementation and regulatory appraisal. Addressing these gaps will require starter-scale, modular or mobile GMP-compliant units, local quality control (QC) capacity (including endotoxin testing, sterility, potency, and residual-solvent assays), and large, pragmatic trials to define site- and route-specific dosing protocols. Oral, inhaled, intravenous, and catheter-based administration each offers distinct logistical advantages in resource-limited settings; clear indications, dosing bands, and monitoring metrics will be essential for wider adoption.

At the policy level, regulatory and ethical bottlenecks persist. While some HICs have pioneered adaptive approval models, such as Belgium’s magistral preparation framework, most LMICs lack clear guidance for evaluating, licensing, or monitoring phage products. This ambiguity disincentivizes investment, stymies domestic production, and delays clinical uptake. Regulatory agencies often lack the technical capacity to evaluate biologics, complicating approval processes even when candidate phage therapies are available [89–91]. Ethical issues further complicate implementation, particularly informed consent in low-literacy settings, equitable access to customized treatments, and cultural perceptions of virus-based therapies. Embedding phage therapy into national AMR strategies, aligned with WHO recommendations, can help build regulatory trust and global harmonization. International partnerships, among LMIC governments, universities, academic institutions, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and global health donors, will be critical in fostering transparent and context-sensitive oversight models.

Despite the absence of regional phage biobanks, together with infrastructure and regulatory hurdles, phage therapy, nonetheless, is still positioned as a potentially cost-effective option for LMICs. While antibiotic development is often cited as costing in excess of USD 1.5–2.0 billion from discovery to market approval, reported costs for bacteriophage discovery (e.g., isolation and initial characterization) are substantially lower [92, 93]. However, these figures are not directly comparable, as the latter typically reflect only early-stage activities and exclude downstream costs associated with manufacturing, quality control, preclinical testing, and clinical evaluation. In particular, large-scale production of clinical-grade phage preparations presents significant technical and financial challenges, including the removal of bacterial endotoxins and other pro-inflammatory contaminants without compromising phage activity. Consequently, although phage therapy may offer advantages in flexibility and development timelines, its total cost to approval remains uncertain and should not be assumed to be inherently lower than that of conventional antibiotics. Hospital-based antibiotic therapy often incurs daily costs that are unsustainable for patients in low-income contexts [94, 95]. In contrast, phages can be sourced from environmental reservoirs such as wastewater or soil [96], offering an abundant and locally adaptable alternative to synthetic drugs. A recent health economic model from Australia estimated savings of $67 million over 3 years by substituting phage therapy for standard treatment in sepsis cases [97]. Moreover, the ability to formulate phages into shelf-stable powders or lyophilized forms could enhance accessibility in remote regions lacking refrigeration infrastructure [98]. Nevertheless, realizing the promise of phage therapy in LMICs will require targeted investment in regulatory frameworks, GMP-compliant production, and distribution infrastructure [49].

Toward Implementation: A Roadmap for Phage Therapy in LMICs

As outlined in the preceding sections, the successful implementation of phage therapy in LMICs remains constrained by the lack of regional phage biobanks, as well as infrastructural, regulatory, and clinical limitations. Addressing these multidimensional barriers requires a phased, structured roadmap grounded in local context, scientific rigor, and policy feasibility.

Phase 1: Phage–Host Biobanks

The first phase involves establishing an integrated phage and host biobank that combines both physical collections with genomic and phenotypic databases (Fig. 1). This effort should begin with the systematic isolation and characterization of phages from various environmental reservoirs, such as hospital wastewater [99], farm runoff, and river water, with an emphasis on targeting dominant MDR pathogens (such as K. pneumoniae) circulating in the region [100]. Then high-throughput genome sequencing and host-range profiling [101] should follow, enabling the creation of genotype–phenotype association databases to guide rational phage selection. These datasets can also support early-stage safety screening by flagging undesirable features such as lysogeny, antibiotic resistance genes, and known virulence factors, ensuring that only strictly lytic and genomically safe phages advance to therapeutic development. Together, these resources will not only help identify phages with reasonably broad host range yet sufficiently host-specific to avoid targeting microbiome, depolymerase activity, and other therapeutically relevant traits but also enable rational phage cocktail design tailored to local resistance landscapes to maximize coverage and suppress resistance.

Phase 2: Context-Appropriate Preparations

The second phase focuses on developing clinically viable and context-appropriate phage preparations (Fig. 1). In many LMIC settings, the transition from laboratory amplification to therapeutic-grade formulations is hindered by limited purification facilities, high consumable costs, and lack of validated quality-control systems. While phage amplification is a key step, it is insufficient for therapeutic use because of the risk of endotoxin contamination and residual organic solvents that can trigger immune reactions such as pyrexia and systemic inflammation [102]. Therefore, beyond standard purification, efforts should emphasize affordable, scalable, and safe purification workflows that are adaptable to local resource levels. Quality-control measures, including titer verification, endotoxin quantification, sterility testing, and pH and solvent-residue assessment, should be standardized yet flexible enough to fit existing laboratory capacities [103]. Depending on available infrastructure and cold-chain logistics, phage preparations may be formulated as liquids for short-term application or lyophilized powders for improved stability and distribution under variable environmental conditions. In this context, the aim extends beyond purity to practicality, ensuring that phage preparations remain both safe and feasible within LMIC production environments.

Phase 3: Preclinical Validation

The third phase involves rigorous preclinical validation (Fig. 1). A systematic assessment of phage–antibiotic synergy is essential, beginning with in vitro assays and extending to relevant in vivo models, particularly for locally available agents such as colistin or fosfomycin [104]. These efforts help ensure both therapeutic efficacy and affordability. Subsequently, well-designed randomized clinical trials should be conducted in LMIC healthcare settings to evaluate safety and effectiveness across real-world contexts. Where possible, these trials should incorporate longitudinal microbiome monitoring to assess host–microbe–phage interactions, particularly in immunocompromised or high-risk populations.

Phase 4: Policy and Regulatory Pathways

The fourth phase focuses on developing the regulatory and infrastructural scaffolding necessary for sustainable phage therapy deployment (Fig. 1). On the basis of preclinical data and clinical case reports, LMICs should establish context-appropriate regulatory frameworks, including technical guidelines, expert consensus documents, and regionally harmonized standards to facilitate the accelerated approval of clinically validated phage preparations. These efforts will require capacity building among regulatory agencies to evaluate biological products, alongside the development of initial infrastructure units capable of meeting GMP standards—such as modular or mobile manufacturing systems suited to resource-limited settings. Regulatory experience from initiatives such as Belgium’s magistral preparation model or the WHO’s prequalification scheme [105] may help inform the design of locally adaptable approval pathways. Integrating regulatory planning with early-stage production strategies can enable a smoother and more coordinated transition from research success to clinical scalability.

Phase 5: Pilot Implementation and Systems Integration

The fifth and final pillar focuses on translating phage research into clinical practice by establishing the systems required for sustainable, nationwide implementation (Fig. 1). Establishing decentralized phage biobanks equipped with cost-effective production units is essential. Pilot implementation studies within hospital settings must be coupled with comprehensive healthcare worker training programs that cover administration, storage, and monitoring, building the foundational workforce for scale-up. These pilots should be designed as proof-of-concept not just for clinical efficacy but also for the entire supply chain logistics, testing the distribution of thermostable formulations from central hubs to peripheral clinics. Concurrently, public health agencies should develop surveillance systems to track phage resistance. The ultimate goal of this phase is to generate the operational evidence and procedural templates needed to formally embed phage therapy into national health systems, ensuring that successful pilots lead directly to sustainable, equitable access beyond urban referral centers.

This 5-P roadmap offers a technically feasible and politically aware strategy for implementing phage therapy in resource-constrained environments. It moves logically from foundational resources (biobanks) through product development (preparations, preclinical validation) and enabling environments (policy) to the critical stage of real-world testing and systems-building (pilot implementation). The framework’s power lies in its integration; success in one pillar is contingent on progress in the others.

Ultimately, the goal is not merely to demonstrate that phage therapy works in an LMIC context but to build the necessary bridges, workforce capacity, supply chains, financing models, and official treatment guidelines that allow it to transition from a research-intensive intervention to a fully integrated public health tool. By embedding considerations of scale, equity, and sustainability into each pillar of the roadmap, this approach positions phage therapy not as a standalone novelty but as a scalable, context-appropriate, and essential component within national strategies to combat the devastating burden of antimicrobial resistance.

Conclusions

MDR pathogens, particularly K. pneumoniae, represent a growing public health challenge in LMICs, where limitations in diagnostics, therapeutics, and antibiotic development capacity exacerbate their impact. In this context, bacteriophage therapy has attracted renewed interest owing to its specificity, activity against biofilms, and potential adaptability to local health systems.

This review aimed to synthesize the current scientific, regulatory, and health-system landscape for phage therapy in LMICs and to identify key barriers that have limited its clinical translation to date. The evidence reviewed highlights persistent challenges across phage discovery, manufacturing, regulatory oversight, and clinical evaluation, which have largely confined phage therapy to preclinical or compassionate-use settings.

To structure future efforts, we propose the 5-P roadmap, encompassing phage–host biobanks, preparations, preclinical validation, policy development, and pilot implementation, as a conceptual framework to guide coordinated research and capacity-building initiatives. While further empirical validation is required, this phased and multidisciplinary approach may help inform context-appropriate strategies for advancing phage therapy research and evaluation in LMICs, including Ethiopia, and support its cautious integration into broader antimicrobial resistance response efforts.
