Genomic analysis of prophages from Burkholderia gladioli exposes extensive genetic diversity
Authors: Angelle P. Britton, Jonathan J. Dennis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Burkholderia genus of Gram-negative bacteria are known for their extensive metabolic capabilities, their relatively plastic genomes, their high intrinsic resistance to antibiotics, and their ability to cause various diseases in humans, animals, and plants. Burkholderia gladioli is one such species, that is an opportunistic pathogen in people, causing sometimes fatal pneumonia, or in plants, causing onion bulb rot, mushroom soft rot, or rice panicle blight. Therapies that utilize bacterial viruses (bacteriophages, or phages) to counter or control pathogenic bacteria are a promising alternative to antibiotics. Here, we present genomic characterization of four new B. gladioli induced prophages, including their phage particle characteristics, host specificity, genomic content relatedness, and potential therapeutic activity. These findings indicate a widespread lysogenic state within the B. gladioli population. Further investigation of the interactions between bacteriophages / prophages and their targeted B. gladioli bacteria will inform on the genomic diversity, environmental survival, and pathogenic potential of B. gladioli, and importantly, the use of B. gladioli phages in phage therapy applications.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12923-4.

Background

The “Red Queen hypothesis” suggesting that certain organisms must constantly adapt and proliferate in order to survive against ever-evolving opposing species in a co-evolutionary arms-race, has shaped the genetic plasticity and diversity of bacteria and that of their parasites, bacteriophages (or phages) [1, 2]. In addition to rapid adaptation through mutation to avoid immediate phage infection, most bacterial chromosomes contain dormant prophages conferring “superinfection immunity”, whereby the insertion of a phage genome into the bacterial chromosome may provide a benefit to both the bacterial host and the phage / prophage [3]. During phage infection and injection of its DNA into the cell, a phage can follow one of two lifecycles: (1) the phage DNA can be used immediately as a template to propagate new phage progeny resulting in phage lysis (lytic lifecycle), or (2) the phage DNA can be inserted into the bacterial chromosome (lysogenic or temperate lifecycle), replicating along with the bacterial cell until certain environmental conditions induce the phage DNA to excise and enter back into its lytic form [4]. Due to the metabolic burden of replicating prophage DNA, and the risk of future cellular lysis through reactivation of the prophage, one apparent advantage of retaining prophages by the host bacterium is through expression of determinants encoded by the prophage that provide the host cell with increased fitness [5]. In a process typically known as “lysogenic conversion”, prophage genes not required for routine phage function can provide a host bacterium with advantageous functions, including virulence factor expression, antibiotic resistance, anti-phage defenses or exclusion, and improvements to host metabolism [6–12]. In a specific example, DLP4 prophage DNA enhances the antibiotic resistance profile of a Stenotrophomonas maltophilia clinical isolate, thereby confounding therapeutic treatment of the bacterial pathogen [13]. Prophage genes can have a substantial effect on the phenotypes of the host bacteria, can influence the evolution of microbial metabolic pathways, and increase the diversity of the bacterial species. Along with bacterial plasmids, integrons, and transposons, phages and prophages are considered to be mobile genetic elements (MGEs) [14], and the number of MGEs harboured (or in this case, prophages carried) by a particular cell depends in part on the bacterial genus or species. For example, although plasmids are more commonly found in Enterobacteriaceae, two to five prophages are commonly found in the chromosomes of Burkholderia bacteria, although differing in number between species and strains [14, 15].

Burkholderia gladioli is a Gram-negative, aerobic bacterium, which can be found mostly in soil, water, and as a commensal or phytopathogen to various plants, including onions, garlic, gladioli, carnations, and rice [16–21]. Along with its phytopathogenic potential, B. gladioli can also cause serious respiratory infections in individuals living with cystic fibrosis (CF), chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), COVID-19, or generally immunocompromised [22–25]. As most Burkholderia bacteria are naturally resistant to many antibiotics, novel treatment strategies for agricultural and clinical B. gladioli infections are urgently needed [16, 26, 27]. A promising treatment option for antimicrobial resistant bacterial pathogens are phages, due to their strong target specificity, lack of significant side effects, and abundance in the environment [16]. Historically, although lytic phages have been preferred for phage therapy, recent strategies have been tested and utilized that employ lysogenic phages [28]. For example, in vivo treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection in Drosophila melanogaster with temperate phages MP22 and D3112 showed that lysogens (bacteria with integrated prophage) have reduced twitching and swimming motility, resulting in reduced virulence of the strain [29, 30]. Furthermore, phage cocktails utilizing multiple temperate phages infecting through different phage receptors have been shown to be as effective in reducing Burkholderia bacterial growth as cocktails including a lytic phage [31]. Alternatively, temperate phages can be genetically modified to abolish lysogenic activity, as with the deletion of a phage repressor in phage KS9 and subsequent effective treatment of Burkholderia cepacia complex (Bcc) bacteria in a Galleria mellonella infection model [32].

Although numerous phages active against the bacteria of the Bcc have been isolated and characterized, few phages have been isolated that are specific for the closely related B. gladioli. Currently, the only reported B. gladioli phages are BgManors32 (NC_070955.1), BgVeeders33 (OK665841.1), Mana (NC_055863.1), Maja (MT708549.1), and vB_BgIM_WTB (WTB) (OQ735257.1) [33–35]. Additionally, phage WTB is the only B. gladioli phage to be phenotypically characterized, specifically in the context of B. gladioli’s interaction with black fungus [35]. Although the known B. gladioli phages were isolated from soil and wastewater, an untapped source of potential phages active against B. gladioli are the prophages harboured in B. gladioli chromosomes. Due to the scarcity of known B. gladioli phages in general, as well as the possibility that B. gladioli prophages carry potentially deleterious genes that could impact phage therapy treatments though lysogenic conversion, the investigation of B. gladioli prophage genomics is crucial.

Previous studies have isolated and characterized Burkholderia phages and prophages, and have demonstrated that an avenue to understanding the background, characteristics, and therapeutic potential of phages is through an analysis of their genomes [36]. In this study, we describe the isolation, activity, morphology, and genomic content of three novel prophages from B. gladioli strains, vB_BgM-AB1 (AB1), vB_BgM-AB4 (AB4), and vB_BgM-AB6 (AB6), and the genomic identification and morphology of prophage vB_BgS-AB2 (AB2). Herein, we report upon the genomic characterization of these AB phages, alone and in the context of the previously published B. gladioli phages. We present evidence that indicates that B. gladioli phages are highly genetically diverse, and provide classification of four new viral genera Ehbeemonovirus, Ehbeedivirus, Ehbeetetravirus, and Ehbeehexavirus. In addition, we determine the presence of phage gene products that are probable “moron” elements, and discuss the potential outcome of any such lysogenic conversion.

Mitomycin C inductions, phage purification and propagation

B. gladioli strains D1170, D1336, and PK1 were subcultured 1:50 in LB broth at 225 RPM to an optical density (OD600) of 0.1 (Thermo Scientific Evolution 201). To induce prophage excision, 0.5 µg/mL, 1.0 µg/mL, 2.0 µg/mL, or 4.0 µg/mL of Mitomycin C (Sigma, M0440) (MMC) was combined with 1.5 mL of Suspension Media (SM, 50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgSO4 [Fisher, M63]), and 8.5 mL of subculture, and incubated aerobically with shaking (225 RPM) for 20 h at 30 °C. Inductions were centrifuged at 10,000 RCF for 10 min (Eppendorf 5810 R), and filter sterilized with a 0.45 µM MCE (Millipore, SLHAR33SS) filter. The lysates pH was adjusted to a pH of seven with 1 M NaOH if necessary. To determine phage presence and activity, 10 µL of MMC induction lysates and controls were spotted onto bacterial double-agar overlays of B. gladioli strains. Pure phage lysates were prepared following three rounds of plaque purification [65]. Phage lysates were propagated by collecting six double-agar overlay plates with a total of 2 mL of SM, and filtered with a 0.22 µM MCE (Millipore, SLGSR33SS) filter.

Host range and electron microscopy

The infective activities of phages AB1, AB4, and AB6 were assessed against a collection of 126 strains of Burkholderia sp., Acinetobacter sp., Pandorea sp., Achromobacter xylosoxidans, and Ralstonia picketti (Table 1). Phage lysates at a titre of 1 × 108 PFU/mL were serially diluted in SM to 10− 7 and 5 µL of each dilution was spotted onto double-agar overlays in biological and technical triplicate. Phage activity was categorized according to the ability to clear bacterial growth, or form plaques on each strain. To produce a clean lysate for transmission electron microscopy (TEM), AB1 lysate was purified with CsCl density gradient centrifugation [46]. TEM lysates for AB4 and AB6 were obtained by propagation on LB 0.7% top agarose. AB2 TEM lysates were prepared by the induction of PK1 with 2.0 µg/mL MMC as previously stated, centrifuged for 15 min at 10,000 RCF, and filtered twice with a 0.22 µM MCE filter. A carbon-coated copper grid was loaded with 10 µL of phage lysate, stained with 4% uranyl acetate, and imaged using Philips/FEI Morgagni transmission electron microscope with an 80 kV charge-coupled device camera (University of Alberta Department of Biological Sciences Advanced Microscopy Facility).

PEG precipitation, DNA isolation, genome assembly and annotation

To obtain a high titre AB1 lysate for genome isolation, the phage was precipitated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) as follows: One mL of PK1 overnight culture, 1 mL of high titre phage lysate, 10 mL of SM and 88 mL of LB broth were incubated for 18 h at 30 °C with shaking (225 RPM). Phage lysate was collected, 1 M NaCl was added, followed by 10% PEG 8000, and rocked at room temperature for 30–60 min until fully dissolved. To precipitate phage, the phage solution was incubated overnight at 4 °C, centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 RPM (19,319 RCF), and resuspended in 2 mL of SM.

Phage gDNA was isolated using an established phenol-chloroform extraction method [66]. Illumina sequencing and library preparation was completed by SeqCenter (Pittsburgh, PA) using an Illumina DNA Prep Kit and the Illumina NextSeq2000 platform. Phages AB1 and AB2 were assembled by SeqCenter using unicycler (v. 0.5.0). The phage galaxy web platform was used for most bioinformatic programs [67]. For phages AB4 and AB6, quality of paired-end reads was assessed via FastQC (0.74 + galaxy1), trimmed with Trimmomatic (v. 0.39 + galaxy2), and assembled with unicycler (0.5.1 + galaxy0) [68–70]. Genome assembly quality and coverage were confirmed with Quast (5.3.0 + galaxy0), and CheckV (1.0.3 + galaxy0) confirmed high quality, complete genome assemblies [71, 72]. Assembly values can be found in Table S1. Contig coverage and assembly of the prophage was confirmed by comparing the assembled sequence to the prophage sequence in the bacterial chromosome, and PCR with sanger sequencing was completed to address any discrepancies in the sequences with two distinct primer sets (Table S2). Structural annotation was performed using GeneMarkS (v. 4.28), Glimmer (Geneious prime v. 1.5), MetaGeneAnnotator (1.0 + galaxy1), Bakta (v. 1.8.1; AB1 and AB2 by SeqCenter), and Prodigal (2.6.3 + galaxy0, AB4 and AB6) [73–77]. Assignment of putative protein coding sequences (CDS) was processed manually and putative function of CDS were assessed via BLASTp analysis using taxid 10,239 (viruses) and taxid 10,293 (bacteria) with an E-value cutoff of 1.0E− 3 [78, 79]. Annotations were supported by conserved domain searches using the Interproscan plugin on Geneious prime (v 2.1) with CATH, CDD, NCBIFAM, Pfam, PANTHER, PIRSF, PRINTS, PROSITE, SMART, SUPERFAMILY databases, along with HmmerWeb (v.2.41.2) [80–90]. Assembled genomes were screened for tRNAs (ARAGORN, galaxy v. 0.6), and integrons (Integron Finder, 2.0.5 + galaxy0) [91, 92]. Whole genome sequences of D1170, D1336, and PK1 were analyzed with PHASTEST for the presence of prophage regions, and generated regions were identified as matches to the isolated phages [93, 94]. Phage tRNA genes were obtained from the tRNADB-CE database, and aligned with the predicted tRNA regions using MAFFT on Geneious Prime (v. 1.5) [95]. Comparison to known phages using average nucleotide identity (ANI) was computed with FastANI, with a default low cutoff value of 70% (galaxy version 1.3) [96].

Genome comparison & phylogenetics

Gene cluster visualization of all B. gladioli phage genomes was computed by clinker with homologous regions above an identity of 0.3, and generated figures were stylized in Adobe Illustrator (v. 29.6.1) [97, 98]. Common genes were labelled according to shared annotation, or if a non-AB phage gene product was annotated as hypothetical, the homologous gene product was labelled according to the AB phage annotation. Viral proteomic trees were generated by VipTree (v. 4.0), using the B. gladioli phage genomes as query sequences, prodigal for gene prediction, genetic code 11, and with dsDNA sequence and prokaryote host for reference viruses [99]. Rectangular trees were generated in VipTree by selecting the top 50 similar viruses (highest SG score) for each phage. Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of the terminase large subunit was calculated by Clustal Omega on EMBL-EBI, and visualized in Geneious Prime [100–102]. Cluster analysis was employed using vContact2 (Viral CONTigs Automatic Clustering and Taxonomy) on KBase (v. 0.9.19) using the Prokaryotic Viral RefSeq 201 database, the Markov cluster (MCL) protein-cluster algorithm, and the ClusterONE viral-cluster algorithm [103, 104]. Viral clusters (VC) and interactions were visualized with Cytoscape (v. 3.10.2) [105]. For taxonomic assignment, intergenomic sequence similarity was calculated using VIRIDIC web [106]. Further genera demarcation was investigated with phylogenetic analysis of marker proteins using the One Click method on Phylogeny.fr [107, 108] .

Results and discussion

Isolation, host range, and morphology

Mitomycin C was used to induce prophages from B. gladioli strains D1170, D1336, and PK1, and resulting lysates were screened for phage activity (Fig. 1A). Antibacterial activity of the induction lysates was demonstrated by the clearing of bacterial growth or plaquing on B. gladioli strains (Fig. 1B). On the original spot tests, the presence of plaquing indicated phage activity, whereas the presence of clearing can indicate high titre phage, lysis from without (phage adsorption, but not replication), or the presence of bacteriocins produced from the induction strain [109]. Further investigation of activity using double-agar overlays to assess for plaquing identified the presence of active phage particles from the induction of D1170 and D1336 with 0.5 µg/mL MMC (Fig. 1B). Phage AB1 was induced from B. gladioli D1170, and phages AB4 and AB6 were both induced from B. gladioli D1336. To obtain pure lysates of each phage, AB4 was plaque purified on CEP071, AB1 on PK1, and AB6 on PK1. Phage AB2 was discovered via whole genome sequencing of a select AB1 lysate, and identified as a prophage of PK1 by referencing the PK1 genome. Multiple rounds of MMC induction of PK1 were performed, up to a concentration of 4.0 µg/mL MMC, however no plaquing was observed, and a suitable host strain for AB2 has yet to be found. The AB2 genome was not present in any other AB1 lysates sequenced. Propagation of phages AB1, AB4 and AB6 to standard high titre values was difficult, and a maximum titre of 108 PFU/mL was obtained after multiple methods were trialled. All phages were tested against 126 strains of Burkholderia sp., Acinetobacter sp., Pandorea sp., Achromobacter xylosoxidans, and Ralstonia picketti, and found to exclusively plaque or clear on B. gladioli strains (Table 1). Although these phages display a narrow host range, it is possible that these phages are active against other species not tested. To investigate the morphology of AB1, AB2, AB4, and AB6, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that phages AB1, AB4, and AB6 had a myovirus morphology, with contractile tubular tails, and icosahedral heads, and AB2 showed a siphovirus morphology, with a long flexible tail (Fig. 2). These phenotypic characteristics contribute to the limited knowledge of B. gladioli phages in the literature.

Prophages AB1 and AB6 show high sequence identity and may participate in anti-phage defense and host virulence

Prophage AB1 isolated from B. gladioli D1170 has a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) genome of 44,444 bp with a GC content of 62.1%, 53 putative protein coding sequences, and no tRNAs or integrons (Fig. 3A; Table S3). The presence of phage-encoded integrons was examined because of their potential to contribute to lateral transfer gene transfer, previously shown to encode genes involved in AMR [110]. The integration site of the AB1 genome into B. gladioli D1170 was found to be adjacent to a DNA mismatch repair protein MutS. BLASTn analysis of the AB1 genome displayed similarity to Burkholderia prophage vB_HM795, which was induced from Burkholderia pseudomallei hemoculture (OR990505.1) and Human Metagenome phage Caudoviricetes sp. isolate ct9861 (BK033109.1) (Table 2) [111]. The termini of AB1 were chosen by mapping the genome against the most similar phage, vB_HM795. Indicative of a temperate lifestyle, AB1 contained a terminase, an integrase, and an AlpA transcription regulator downstream of the integrase, which has been shown to play a role in prophage excision [112]. Additionally, genes which may contribute to the lytic-lysogenic decision were also present, including a CI repressor protein, which may contribute to the suppression of lytic functions, and a nitroreductase, previously shown in E. coli to trigger phage lambda to enter the lytic cycle during SOS response [113, 114]. However, the AB1 genome also possessed a glycosyl hydrolase, which may contribute to the breakdown of peptidoglycan, demonstrating the potential lytic function of AB1 [115]. Virion structure and assembly of AB1 was encoded in a module of consecutive proteins which includes 10 putative tail proteins, three head-capsid proteins, and four baseplate proteins, among others.

Isolated from B. gladioli D1336, AB6 has a 44,703 bp dsDNA genome, with a GC content of 61%, 51 putative CDS, and no tRNAs or integrons (Fig. 3B; Table S4). Analysis of the B. gladioli D1336 genome showed that the AB6 genome was integrated adjacent to a DusA tRNA-dihydrouridine synthase and a tRNA-His. BLASTn of the AB6 genome showed similarity to phages vB_HM795, and Caudoviricetes sp. isolate ct9861, the same phages as AB1 (Table 2). Further analysis revealed that the modular structure and genetic content of the AB6 genome was similar to AB1, visualized in Fig. 3. This included the terminase gene, the virion structure and morphogenesis module, and putative lytic associated genes. The AB6 termini were chosen by aligning to vB_HM795 and AB1, however one gene upstream was chosen as the start site instead, because the putative coding sequence overlapped with the terminase large subunit.

Gene cluster comparison of AB1 and AB6 demonstrated a similar modular structure, that began with a large virion morphogenesis and structure module and continued with several other homologous genes that may be involved in lytic activity, virulence, and DNA metabolism (Fig. 3C). Notably, AB1 and AB6 both contained a putative virulence-associated protein E (VapE) domain-containing protein, which has previously been identified in a virulence region of genomic and plasmid Dichelobacter nodosus DNA, the causative agent of ovine footrot [116]. In D. nodosus, this region contained several other vap genes, and was thought to have originated by prophage insertion because of the presence of integrase and repressor homologs [117]. However, since it’s discovery in the 1990s, there has not been much investigation into the vap genes or their role in phages. BLASTp analysis of the putative VapE protein from AB1 demonstrated similarity to VapE in the most related phage vB_H795, but other less related phages mostly annotated the similar protein as a DNA primase. Given the history of prophage involvement in the vap genes of D. nodosus, the unique annotation of the VapE gene product may demonstrate a moron gene representing potential lysogenic conversion, although the AB1 and AB6 genomes did not contain the other vap genes previously identified in the region [116, 117]. While the VapE protein may not result in a full virulence factor secreted by these genomes, shown by the absence of other vap genes previously found in the cluster, it could show a partially preserved virulence function. However, through genomic characterization we can only speculate, and future phenotypic studies exploring the association of virulence with the VapE gene product would be necessary to explore answers to this question. As well, the genetic identification and further functional characterization of any potential phage-encoded virulence factor genes would be vital in screening phages for their potential use in phage therapy or biocontrol.

Another facet of the AB1 and AB6 phage genomes that demonstrated the interplay between the phage and host genetic content, was the presence of genes which may contribute to anti-phage defense. A prophage may carry gene products that provide the bacterium protection from superinfection from other phages [118, 119]. Different prophage encoded mechanisms that prevent infection by other phages have been observed in numerous phages, for example, through the gene products gp15 in E. coli phage HK97, lpt in Streptococcus thermophilus phage TP-J34, and Tip in Pseudomonas aeruginosa phage D3112 [120–122]. The presence of anti-phage defense systems in prophages has been used as an example of prophages existing as MGEs, because of the benefits to the host cell [119]. Both AB1 and AB6 contained an OLD (overcoming lysogenization defect) nuclease, which was first recorded in observation of integrated P2's ability to protect the host from phage lambda attack through inactivation of phage replication and abortive infection [123, 124]. The AB6 genome putatively contained a single OLD nuclease gene like P2, which had also been described in Thermus scotoductus [125]. In comparison, the AB1 genome putatively contained the Gabija anti-phage defense system, comprised of an OLD nuclease (GajA) followed by a UrvD helicase (GajB) [126]. Recently described in Bacillus cereus, the Gabija system was activated upon nucleotide depletion after phage infection, wherein GajA acted as a DNA nicking endonuclease to deactivate phage replication and can further result in abortive infection through the destruction of host genomic DNA [126]. However, to date, the function of GajB (UrvD-helicase) is not fully understood [12, 126]. This protein complex has been further identified in Burkholderia pseudomallei and Bacillus cereus, where it showed protection against various siphoviruses and podoviruses [127–129]. Although both phages AB1 and AB6 contain the OLD nuclease, and the genes were present in similar locations in their genomes, their gene products do not share homology with each other. Overall, the AB1 and AB6 genomes share high sequence identity and a similar modular structure with each other, and have certain regions with high sequence homology. These genomes demonstrate characteristics that may explain host cell lysogenic conversion, including the putative VapE virulence gene, and the putative OLD nuclease systems. Given that the OLD nuclease systems between AB1 and AB6 did not show homology with each other, the presence of this element in both genomes may further demonstrate the propensity of acquiring putative moron elements in these genomes. Identifying the role that anti-phage defense genes play in prophage genomes and their expression in the host cells is crucial for the advancement of temperate phage therapy and the potential risk of superinfection immunity. Future investigation of AB1 and AB6 lysogens could help to decipher the role that the potential phage-encoded OLD-nuclease plays in phage defense. Additionally, the potential differences between the two OLD systems present in these genomes could be examined for differences in activity and phage specificity.

The AB2 phage genome shares identity with B. gladioli phage BgVeeders33 and contains a putative partitioning cassette

Phage AB2 from B. gladioli PK1 has a 39,987 bp double-stranded DNA genome, with no tRNAs or integrons, 61.3% GC content, and 58 putative protein coding sequences (CDS) (Fig. 4A; Table S5). Analysis of the B. gladioli PK1 genome found that the AB2 genome integrated into a tRNA-Arg. The AB2 genome shows similarity to Burkholderia phages vB_RY3 (PQ789826.1) and vB_RY5 (PV072764.1) (Table 2). The termini of AB2 were chosen by mapping the genome against the most similar phage, vB_RY3. The AB2 genome encodes many putative proteins containing domains shared with genes from Escherichia coli phage lambda [130]. The putative lambda-like genes in AB2 that may be involved in the phage lifecycle include a lambda-like CI repressor protein, a CII regulatory protein that is thought to be involved in the lysis-lysogeny decision, and a two-domain tyrosine site-specific recombinase [131–133]. As well, several proteins are encoded which may be involved in tail structure and assembly, including T, M, L, K, I, and J [130]. While not all phage lambda essential genes were present, our observations highlight an interesting aspect of the AB2 genome.

The AB2 genome contains a putative ParB/ThiF-partitioning cassette, including a ParB protein, a ThiF-family protein, and PRTRC system proteins A, B, F, C, and E. This partitioning cassette is thought to play a role in segregation during bacterial cell division, and is considered to be an MGE [134, 135]. This cassette has been recorded in Acidithiobacillus caldus, Delfia sp Cs1-4, Elizabethkingia anopheles, and plasmid pPniHB1_1 of Pseudomonas nitroreducens [134–136]. Although the ThiF-cassette has been observed in multiple organisms, the function itself has not been studied, and other than in NCBI accessions, the cassette has not been recorded in phage genomes. BLASTp of the genes in this cluster demonstrated similarity to B. gladioli and B. glumae bacterial strains, Ralstonia phage Dina, and B. gladioli phage BgVeeders33. These CDS shared higher similarity (Query cover, E-value, and % Identity) to bacterial proteins, versus their phage counterparts. Comparing the AB2 genome with characteristics of phage-like MGEs, including temperate phages, phagemids, tailocins, phage-inducible chromosomal islands (PICIs), and gene-transfer agents (GTAs), AB2's putative genomic content overall correspond to a canonical temperate phage [137]. With the potential presence of the partitioning cassette, it was considered that AB2 was retained as a phagemid, however the AB2 genome did not appear to contain a putative toxin/anti-toxin system or an obvious origin of replication [137]. A large-scale midi-prep of MMC treated PK1 culture was attempted to isolate AB2 as a plasmid, but was unsuccessful (data not shown). Thus, it is plausible that the ThiF-partitioning cassette in AB2 may represent a MGE which potentially contributes to host maintenance and segregation [138, 139]. However, more research must be conducted on the function of this cassette in AB2 to determine if it has any role in bacterial partitioning.

When comparing AB2 with the other B. gladioli genomes, the genome of AB2 shared the most genomic similarity with B. gladioli phage BgVeeders33 (Fig S1). Gene cluster comparison of these genomes demonstrated homology in several putative temperate phage genes and genes, which may be involved in DNA metabolism (Fig. 4B). This exists in comparison to AB2’s most similar phage genome vB_RY3, where most of its shared genes were thought to be involved in virion structure (Fig. 4B). The putative ThiF-partitioning cassette present in AB2 shared homology with a BgVeeders33 gene cluster that was annotated as hypothetical, and a di-nucleotide-utilizing enzyme (PRTRC-system ThiF-family protein in AB2). The presence of the cassette in AB2 and potentially BgVeeders33, but not in vB_RY3, further demonstrates the potential of B. gladioli prophages involvement in bacterial host cellular activities.

The AB4 phage genome shares identity with B. gladioli phage Mana and contains a putative bacterial pathogenicity island

Phage AB4 isolated from B. gladioli D1336 has a dsDNA genome of 35,722 bp, 65% GC content, 48 CDS, and no integrons (Fig. 5A; Table S6). The genome contained one tRNA-Val on the complement strand at 3143 bp, upstream of the putative terminase large subunit. It has been hypothesized that tRNAs are acquired into prophage genomes during improper excision. However, phage genomes possess a deletion bias for tRNAs, in that the tRNA must provide an advantage to the phage for it to remain in the genome [140]. One main advantage a tRNA provides to a phage is the ability to translate proteins at a higher rate, which helps the phage replicate more efficiently [140]. Alternatively, tRNA genes in the bacterial chromosome serve as specific phage integration hotspots through homologous recombination, provided the phage is carrying a related tRNA gene sequence [95]. Although a Valine tRNA was predicted for AB4, the region does not share some common characteristics of typical tRNAs. For example, at 120 bp, this region is longer than an average phage tRNA, and also overlaps with another coding sequence. However, when AB4’s predicted tRNA was aligned to all known phage Valine (GAC) tRNAs, it shared 57% sequence identity on average (Fig. S2). Similarly to AB2, the AB4 genome integrated into a tRNA-Arg on the B. gladioli D1336 genome. Compared to known phage genomes, AB4 showed similarity to Burkholderia phages Milagro (OM638609.1), and KL3 (NC_015266.1) of the Kayeltresvirus genus (Table 2) The termini of AB4 were chosen by mapping the genome against the most similar phage, Milagro. The AB4 genome showed a more complex genomic map than the other AB phages previously discussed (Fig. 5A). The virion structure and morphogenesis module was interrupted by a putative DNA methylase, and an operon of genes potentially involved in phage lysis. Although similar temperate phage genes were present as the other discussed phages, including a terminase, integrase, and repressor proteins, the AB4 genome contained several more genes potentially involved in lytic activity. A P2-like lysis cassette contained two putative holins, which may be responsible for permeabilizing the host cell inner membrane, an endolysin, thought to degrade the cell wall, and a P2 Rz-Rz1 pair homolog, LysB-LysC, which has be shown to form a heteromeric complex to fuse the inner and outer membranes and carry out lysis of the host cell [141–143]. In addition to these P2 homologs, the AB4 genome contained a modular structure similar to P2, from AB4 CDS 01 to 27 (Fig. S3). All putative gene products in this region were called in similar order, except the P2 antiholin, LysA, orf30 (in place of AB4 DNA methylase), and fun(Z) [141]. However, AB4 did not contain all the hallmarks of a canonical P2-like phage, as it did not contain a translational frameshift in the tail assembly genes, or a significant overlap region between the LysA and LysB gene. Unlike phage P2, AB4 encoded a putative bacterial pathogenicity island, PAAR2, which will be discussed further.

Prophages encoded on bacterial chromosomes can contain “moron” genes, which are genes not needed for the lifecycle of the phage, but which may provide supplementary functions to the phage or host bacterium [3]. For example, temperate phages can encode polymorphic toxins (PTs), effectors of the Type V and Type VI secretion system (T6SS), that can contribute to the inhibition or killing of bacterial competitors [144]. The main components of the T6SS in bacteria consist of a Hcp composed phage-like tail tube, a VgrG spike, and a PAAR (proline-alanine-alanine-arginine) spike extension [145, 146]. The PAAR2 cluster, putatively annotated in the AB4 genome, is a T6SS effector (TseV1) which has been identified in Pseudomonas sp. and Salmonella sp., and contains a PAAR protein, a VRR-Nuc structurally unique nuclease (TseV), and an immunity protein (TsiV) [145, 147]. The TseV1 effector has not been studied extensively, but the related TseV2 and TseV3 T6SS effectors have previously exhibited toxic activity against E. coli and Acinetobacter baylyi [145]. Effectors of the T6SS can be encoded separately from the T6SS structural gene cluster, thus, it appears that the PAAR2 effector on the AB4 genome may provide the host bacterium with competitive advantage if the effector is not already present on the host chromosome. Phenotypic characterization of this effector encoded on the AB4 genome is needed to elucidate any potential anti-microbial properties it provides to the bacterium via the host’s endogenous T6SS.

Comparison of AB4 with its most similar phages, Bcc phage Milagro and B. gladioli phage Mana, demonstrated that the three phages had a very similar modular structure (Fig. 5B). The genes shared are quite diverse, including several genes potentially involved in virion structure and morphogenesis, as well as putative lytic and lysogenic genes. Furthermore, the putative PAAR2 cluster encoded on the AB4 genome shares homology to genes in the Mana genome, which were annotated as hypothetical [33]. Similar to AB2 and BgVeeders33 with the ThiF-cassette, phage Milagro did not share homology with this cluster found only in phages AB4 and Mana. These results further suggest that B. gladioli phage genomes harbour moron genes which could possibly contribute to lysogenic conversion.

Comparative analysis of B. gladioli phages

Considering that little work has been completed on B. gladioli phage genomes in general, or the relationships between all the B. gladioli phage genomes, we decided to investigate the genomic and proteomic relationships of the B. gladioli phages and the subsequent classification of the AB phages [35]. As previously shown, each of the AB phages share some genomic characteristics with one other B. gladioli phage (Figs. 3C, 4B, 5B). However, comparison of all nine B. gladioli phages to each other, using VIRIDIC and gene cluster comparison, show that overall, the B. gladioli phages are diverse, and the previously observed genomic pairings are relatively distinct from one another (Fig. S1, Fig. S4). This comparison also demonstrates the genomic similarity between phages WTB and Maja. To investigate genome-wide relationships between the B. gladioli phages in a larger context, VContact2 was used. VContact2 investigates gene-sharing networks and calculated viral-clusters (VC) to demonstrate subgroupings based upon the quantity of gene-sharing between phage genomes [103]. All nine B. gladioli genomes were used as input sequences, and clustered with 3449 total viruses, comprising of 551 viral clusters. All clustered viruses were categorized into 12 orders, 25 families, and 738 genera. There were 259 outlier viruses which were not clustered, and 445 viruses which overlapped between more than one cluster. The median number of viruses in each viral cluster was 10. Upon visualization with an edge weighted spring-embedded model, where phage genomes sharing more protein clusters are placed closer together, two individual networks containing the B. gladioli phages were observed. AB2 showed direct connections with five viral protein clusters, containing a total of 39 genomes (Fig. 6). However, AB2 shared a protein cluster with only one other phage, BgVeeders33, and no other B. gladioli phages were present in this network. Additionally, Burkholderia phage KS9, was an overlap genome between the AB2 cluster and one other. This demonstrates that these genomes shared genetic similarity to one another. Phages AB1, AB4, and AB6 were all present in the same network, which totalled 6 viral protein clusters, and shared connections with 63 phage genomes (Fig. 7). Whereas AB1 and AB6 were the only two phages in one viral cluster, AB4 and Mana shared a viral cluster with a total of 46 phage genomes, thus demonstrating that AB1 and AB6, and AB4 and Mana both shared genomic similarity to one another. As well, phages WTB and Mana shared a viral cluster along with 6 other phages (Fig. S5), whereas BgManors32 shared a viral cluster with Salmonella phage SEN34 alone (Fig. S6). Whereas vContact2 demonstrates overall genetic relationship to one another, additional methods were necessary to define genera for these phages.

To further observe the relationships between the B. gladioli phages, VipTree was used to visualize phylogenetic relatedness through BLASTx computed genome-wide similarities [99]. Phage genomes were analyzed against 5632 dsDNA prokaryotic viruses and found to be related to 3928 viruses, comprising of five virus families: Autographiviridae, Straboviridae, Drexlerviridae, Schitoviridae, and Herelleviridae, and 5 host groups: Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, Cyanobacteria, and Bacteriodata (Fig. 8). To establish genomic taxonomy of each of the AB phages, individual rectangular trees were generated by selecting the 50 most related genomes (highest SG score). The AB1 and AB6 rectangular trees (Fig. 9) demonstrated that these phages shared the same node alone, and were separate from other related phages. Further confirmation of genomic relatedness was calculated using VIRIDIC to measure intergenomic sequence similarities [106]. The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) indicates that viruses with average nucleotide identity (ANI) of 95% share species identity, and above 70% share genus identity, usually calculated by VIRIDIC [142]. Investigation of BLASTn similar viruses of AB1 and AB6 showed no similarity scores above 70% to any phage, with AB1 and AB6 sharing 60.9% similarity to each other (Fig. S7, Fig. S8). ICTV also requires that genus-level groupings be monophyletic in signature genes. Phylogenetic analysis of the AB1 and AB6 major capsid protein, and terminase large subunit demonstrated high sequence similarity between these signature genes, however, both AB1 and AB6 showed monophyly. Thus, due to the defined criteria, we proposed the creation of two new viral genera for AB1 and AB6, Ehbeemonovirus and Ehbeehexavirus, respectively. Although these phages show high similarity in the signature genes, when comparing the genomes to each other, we observed a significant section of the genomes which did not show homology (Fig. 3C), which may explain the lower overall similarity score and the subsequent proposed taxonomic assignment.

ViPTree phylogenetic trees of AB2 showed that AB2 shared a node with Burkholderia phage KS9, which AB2 also showed genomic similarity by sharing a vContact2 viral cluster (Fig. 10A). Although AB2 and BgVeeders33 shared some genomic similarity through a viral network in a vContact2 subgroup, they showed relatedness as members of neighbouring nodes when viewing phylogeny through ViPTree (Fig. 10A). This was further investigated through sequence similarity by VIRIDIC, which demonstrated that AB2 shared a 30.9% similarity with KS9 and 18.8% similarity with BgVeeders33 (Fig. S9). These results were further supported by the previously mentioned vContact2 viral cluster. However, since AB2 does not share a sequence similarity over 70% to any published virus, and investigation of signature genes demonstrated monophyly, we proposed the creation of a new genus Ehbeedivirus.

Phages Mana and AB4 shared neighbouring nodes in the ViPTree phylogenetic analysis, wherein their two most closely-related phages, KL3 and AP3, respectively, were also present in the AB4 vContact2 viral network (Fig. 10B). Although AB4 is similar to more phages than the other AB phages, upon analysis of intergenomic relatedness with VIRIDIC, no similarity values were above the 70% threshold for genera, and upon analysis of signature genes, AB4 was in a different clade than all other similar phages (Fig. S10). As well, upon analysis of signature genes, the AB4 genes do not share a node with any other phage, and appear to be highly separate. Due to these results, we proposed the creation of a new genus, Ehbeetetravirus.

In addition to investigating the individual AB phages, we aimed to analyze the phylogenetics between all of the B. gladioli phages. Proteome-based phylogenetic trees affirmed the relationships computed by vContact2 viral cluster networks, showing relatedness between the established B. gladioli gene pairs (Fig. 11A). As well, whereas phages AB1, AB6, AB4, Mana, AB2, BgVeeders33, and BgManors32 were members of a single clade, phages WTB and Maja were members of a second clade. A detailed phylogenetic analysis of phage WTB was completed by Wang et al. [35]. To further establish the relationship between the B. gladioli phages, multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of the terminase large subunit of all the B. gladioli phages was computed with Clustal Omega, which further affirmed the relationships between AB1 and AB6, AB4 and Mana, AB2 and BgVeeders33, WTB and Maja, and BgManors32 alone (Fig. 11B). However, further functional analysis of these phages is required to investigate their physical characteristics, and to deduce whether their genetic similarity results in similar phenotypic behaviours.

Conclusion

Herein, we have described the morphology and genomic relationships of four newly isolated novel prophages from four different B. gladioli strains. These findings suggest that lysogeny is common within the B. gladioli population, and that B. gladioli prophage sequences identified using genomic prediction software can produce functional phage particles. We have characterized these functional phage particles derived from prophage sequences in terms of host range and particle morphology, and have determined their relationships to other associated phage families. As B. gladioli prophages can potentially harbour moron genes that express deleterious genes products causing lysogenic conversion, investigation of these prophage sequences is imperative prior to their use in any phage therapy treatment.
