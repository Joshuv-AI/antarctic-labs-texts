Comparative long-term sedation efficacy of remimazolam vs propofol in malignant patients with mechanical ventilation: an exploratory non-inferiority randomized study
Authors: Zhen-nan Yuan, Fang Cao, Hai-jun Wang, Shi-ning Qu, Chu-lin Huang, Hao Wang, Hao Zhang, Xue-zhong Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhanced comfort and effective sedation are essential for mechanically ventilated patients, particularly in the ICU. This study evaluates the safety and effectiveness of remimazolam, a novel ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, compared to propofol in this patient population. A randomized controlled trial was conducted on patients with malignancy aged 18–80 years requiring mechanical ventilation for over 24 h in the ICU of the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences from June 2022 to May 2025. The trial was registered prior to patient recruitment at chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2200058409). Patients received either remimazolam or propofol with regular analgesia. The primary outcome was the ability to maintain light sedation as measured by the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS: from-2 to + 1). Compared to propofol, if remimazolam achieves a proportion of patients maintaining light sedation that is not less than 10% at 6-, 24-, and 48 h, we will consider this indicative of noninferiority. Secondary outcomes included 7-day extubation rates, ICU length of stay, 28-day survival rates, the proportion of patients requiring adjunctive sedation, incidence of adverse events such as hypotension and delirium, and inflammatory response indicators. A total of 110 patients were enrolled (55 remimazolam, 55 propofol). Baseline characteristics were comparable. Both sedation methods achieved comparable success in reaching target sedation levels at 6-, 24- and 48-hour post-initiation of sedation (difference >-10%, P for noninferiority testing < 0.05). Extubation rates at 7 days were similar (78.3% for remimazolam vs. 76.5% for propofol, p = 0.866) and the median ICU length of stay did not differ significantly (8.2 days for remimazolam vs. 7.3 days for propofol, p = 0.546). Survival rates at 28 days were high and similar for both groups (98.2%, p = 1.000). Both groups required dexmedetomidine for adjunctive sedation (9.1% vs. 10.9%, p = 1.000). Adverse events were also comparable. Notably, the remimazolam group showed a lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio at 24 and 48 h compared to the propofol group (p = 0.048 and p = 0.046, respectively). Introduction

Mechanical ventilation is a critical component of care for patients in intensive care settings, particularly in those with severe respiratory impairments, including cancer patients. Sedation is essential in these scenarios, promoting patient comfort and facilitating ease of care while minimizing agitation and discomfort during mechanical ventilation1,2. Given the evolving understanding of sedation strategies, the philosophy of “everyday comfort sedation” (eCASH) has emerged, advocating for techniques that are patient-centered and emphasize the minimization of sedative drug administration while ensuring adequate sedation and pain relief3.

Traditionally, propofol has been the sedative of choice in intensive care unit (ICU) settings due to its rapid onset, short duration of effects, a certain protective effect on postoperative delirium4 and decreased duration of mechanical ventilation5. However, the drug carries a risk of adverse effects, including hypotension6. Benzodiazepines are traditionally linked to an increased risk of delirium, especially in critically ill patients, more so than propofol7.

Remimazolam, a novel ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, metabolism is rapid, making it theoretically a safer option when prolonged sedation is necessary8–10, particularly in patients with compromised hepatic or renal function11,12. The pharmacodynamic characteristics of remimazolam also demonstrate good adaptability across different populations. Research shows that, in pediatric and adolescent patients, the pharmacokinetic properties of remimazolam are similar to those in adults, but dosage adjustments based on body weight are necessary to ensure appropriate sedation depth13. In elderly patients, due to the decline in physiological function, caution is also required when using remimazolam, and dosage may need to be adjusted to avoid adverse reactions such as delayed awakening14,15. Although remimazolam is metabolized more quickly, hepatic dysfunction may also affect its metabolism and increase the risk. Its clinical use in mechanically ventilated ICU patients with malignancy remains underexplored. This study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of remimazolam as a sedative agent compared to propofol in mechanically ventilated patients with malignancies, specifically assessing the incidence of hypotension and delirium, as well as the rate of achieving target sedation.

Patients and methods

Study design

This study was a randomized controlled trial aimed at evaluating the safety and effectiveness of remimazolam versus propofol for sedation in mechanically ventilated patients with malignancies in the ICU of the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The trial ran from June 2022 to May 2025 and was approved by the ethics committee of the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives before enrollment. All methods in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the CONSORT 2010 guidelines. The study was registered at chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2200058409; 2022-04-01; Principal investigator: Xue-zhong Xing) before patient recruitment.

Participants

Participants with malignancies included adult patients aged 18 to 80 years who required mechanical ventilation for more than 24 h. Exclusion criteria included patients with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (requiring deep sedation), those with significant hemodynamic instability (requiring norepinephrine at a dose of more than 0.1 µg/kg·min to maintain stable blood pressure), known hypersensitivity to the study drugs, and patients with severe comorbid conditions (e.g., severe heart failure). Based on previous literature16,17, the efficacy rate of maintaining light sedation with propofol is approximately 90%. Using a non-inferiority study design with α = 0.025 and 1 - β = 0.90, a difference of 10% in the efficacy rate for maintaining light sedation with remimazolam is considered significant. Therefore, it was determined that 190 participants would be required in each group. Taking into account a potential exclusion rate of 10% of patients, a total of 209 participants were needed for each group. However, since this study is a preliminary exploratory investigation, each group included 55 patients for the study.

Randomization and intervention

Eligible participants were randomly assigned to receive either remimazolam or propofol using a computer-generated randomization sequence, ensuring a blinded allocation process. Patients in the remimazolam group received an initial bolus dose of 5 mg, followed by a continuous infusion starting at 0.15 mg/kg/h, titrated to achieve target sedation levels measured by the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) scores18 between − 2 and 1, with a maximum infusion rate of 0.3 mg/kg/h. Patients in the propofol group received a loading dose of approximately 1-1.5 mg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion starting at 2.0 mg/kg/h, which was also adjusted to maintain target RASS scores, with a maximum infusion rate of 4.0 mg/kg/h. Both groups received standardized analgesia according to hospital protocols. During the initial 48 h, the sedation depth was titrated with assessments performed every 0.5 h, and subsequent routine sedation evaluations were conducted every 4 h by the attending physician for dose adjustment. If patients presented with agitation or excessive sedation between scheduled RASS evaluations, nurses adjusted the infusion dose in conjunction with the attending physicians. If patients did not achieve the target sedation effect upon reaching the maximum dose, they were supplemented with dexmedetomidine.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the proportion of patients achieving targeted sedation levels at predefined intervals (6-, 24- and 48-hour post-initiation of sedation) and the percentage of time within the target sedation range without rescue sedation within 48 h after the initiation of sedation. Secondary outcomes included the 7-day extubation rates, defined as the percentage of patients successfully extubated within 7 days of sedation; ICU length of stay, calculated from admission to ICU until discharge; 28-day survival rates, defined as the percentage of patients who died within 28 days after sedation initiation; the incidence of adverse events, including hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg requiring intervention) and delirium; and inflammatory response indicators — neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) levels, collected at baseline and 6, 24, and 48 h after sedation initiation to evaluate inflammatory responses. For delirium assessment, the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) was adopted, with assessments conducted every 12 h (q12h). The assessments were performed by attending physicians and specialized nurses, all of whom had completed standardized professional training for CAM-ICU and were blinded to the patient grouping during the assessment process.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.0. The study is a prospective randomized single-blinded clinical parallel trial. Randomization was conducted with a computer-generated random number table by an independent statistician; allocation concealment was maintained via sealed opaque envelopes, and patient enrollment was done by a dedicated research nurse. This study used a single-blind design, but nursing staff could not be blinded due to distinct color differences between remimazolam and propofol, a noted study limitation. Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups for continuous variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Compared to propofol, if remimazolam achieves a proportion of patients maintaining light sedation that is not less than 10% at 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h, we will consider this indicative of noninferiority. The analysis of the proportion of patients remaining intubated and the proportion of ICU patients was conducted using Kaplan-Meier analysis, with the log-rank test employed to compare survival curves between groups. A one-sided alpha level of 0.025 was used for non-inferiority tests, and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 was applied for all other comparative analyses; a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. The analysis of inflammatory markers over time was conducted using repeated measures ANOVA to determine differences between groups. This study design, focusing on long-term sedation outcomes and inflammatory responses, aims to provide valuable insights into the use of remimazolam in ICU settings for patients with malignancies.

Results

Demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 140 patients were screened, and 110 patients were included (Fig. 1), with 55 allocated to the remimazolam group and 55 to the propofol group (Table 1). The demographic and baseline characteristics of the participants were similar between both groups, including age, sex and underlying comorbidities. The median age of patients in the remimazolam group was 72 years (IQR, 50.0–76.0), with 33 females (60.0%). In the propofol group, the median age of patients was 70 years (IQR, 58.0–76.0), with 41 females (74.5%). No significant differences were observed in age, gender, the types of malignancies, the severity of illness as assessed by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II scores, SOFA scores and comorbidities, and the reasons for requiring mechanical ventilation at baseline, ensuring comparability between the two groups (Table 1).

Primary outcome -sedation outcomes

The primary outcome of the study, the proportion of patients achieving target sedation levels as measured by the RASS, showed favorable results for both groups (Table 2). At 6 h post-initiation of sedation, 81.8% of patients in the remimazolam group reached the target sedation levels compared to 83.6% in the propofol group (difference − 1.8%, 95% CI: -9.8% to 6.2%, P for noninferiority testing = 0.004). At 24 h, these rates were 80.0% and 81.8%, respectively (difference − 1.8%, 95% CI: -9.6% to 6.0%, P for noninferiority testing = 0.004). By 48 h, 87.2% of patients in the remimazolam group maintained adequate sedation compared to 83.6% in the propofol group (difference 3.6%, 95% CI: -6.2% to 13.4%, P for noninferiority testing = 0.002). The percentage of time within the target sedation range was 90.3% in the remimazolam group and 90.6% in the propofol group (difference − 0.3%, 95% CI: -8.8% to 8.2%, P for noninferiority testing = 0.001). During the first 48 h of sedation, a total of 660 RASS assessments were conducted in both the remimazolam and propofol groups (Fig. 2). The remimazolam group achieved target sedation levels in 616 assessments, resulting in a success rate of 93.3%, while the propofol group reached target sedation in 539 assessments, with a success rate of 81.7%.

Secondary outcomes -extubation rates, length of ICU stay and survival rates

The 7-day extubation rates were comparable between the two groups, with 78.3% of patients in the remimazolam group can extubate by Day 7, versus 76.5% in the propofol group with no statistical significance was determined (p = 0.866, Fig. 3A). The median length of stay in the ICU was similar for both groups, with patients in the remimazolam group staying for a median of 8.2 (2.6–14.8) days and those in the propofol group for a median of 7.3 (2.5–15.6) days. The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.546). In the remimazolam group, 6.3% of patients (3 out of 55) did not transfer out of the ICU at 28 days, while in the propofol group, the proportion was 7.4% (4 out of 55). The proportion of patients in both groups who did not transfer out of the ICU at different time points showed no statistically significant difference (Fig. 3B, p = 0.074). This outcome suggests that neither sedative provided a distinct advantage concerning the duration of ICU admission in this cohort of mechanically ventilated patients. One patient in each group experienced death during their ICU stay, with both cases attributed to respiratory failure (Table 3). The 28-day survival rates were closely aligned in both groups, with 98.2% of patients in the remimazolam group and the propofol group. The difference in mortality was not statistically significant (p = 1.000), indicating that the use of either sedative did not adversely affect survival rates within the specified time frame.

Secondary outcomes-patients requiring adjunctive sedation to achieve target sedation and adverse events

In the remimazolam group, 5 patients (9.1%) required dexmedetomidine as adjunctive sedation, while in the propofol group, 5 patients (10.9%) needed adjunctive sedation. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in the proportion of patients requiring adjunctive sedation to achieve target sedation (p = 1.000, Table 3). In the remimazolam group, a total of 4 patients (7.3%) experienced low blood pressure that required pharmacological intervention, while 2 patients (3.6%) developed delirium. In contrast, the propofol group reported 6 cases (10.9%) of low blood pressure requiring drug intervention, 3 cases (5.5%) of delirium during the same time frame. There were no statistically significant differences in the adverse effects between the two groups (Table 3).

Secondary outcome - inflammatory markers

In this study, we investigated the effects of remimazolam and propofol on inflammatory markers—neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), in patients with sepsis. The baseline characteristics of the two groups, including gender, age, severity scores, and the proportion of patients receiving adjunctive antibiotics and corticosteroid use, showed no significant differences (Table 4). Our initial analysis revealed no significant statistical differences in NLR values between the two groups at initial, 24 h, and 48 h (all p > 0.05). However, upon conducting a subgroup analysis focused on the septic patients, distinct differences emerged (Fig. 4). At baseline, the remimazolam group had an NLR of 25.6 ± 13.1, compared to 25.7 ± 13.3 in the propofol group, with no significant between-group difference (p = 0.324). Notably, in the septic subgroup, the remimazolam group was associated with lower NLR values at 24 h (13.4 ± 7.9) compared to the propofol group (19.1 ± 7.8) (p = 0.048). This trend persisted at 48 h, with the remimazolam group having a lower NLR of 12.8 ± 9.0 versus 18.8 ± 7.6 in the propofol group in this subgroup (p = 0.046).

Discussion

This pioneering study evaluated the safety and efficacy of remimazolam, a novel ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine sedative, in patients with malignancy requiring mechanical ventilation, compared to propofol. All study subjects were patients with malignancy from a specialized cancer hospital, a deliberate design based on the unique clinical features of this population. Our findings indicate that remimazolam exhibits comparable effectiveness in achieving target sedation in critically ill cancer patients. Both groups demonstrated high rates of target sedation, with remimazolam achieving 93.3% and propofol 81.7%. Importantly, we observed no statistically significant differences in sedation requirements over time, suggesting that remimazolam can be utilized as an effective alternative to propofol with a similar safety profile.

A randomized controlled trial revealed that while remimazolam and propofol provided similar sedation durations, patients in the remimazolam group reported higher satisfaction without an increased incidence of adverse events10. Additionally, remimazolam showed less heart rate variability and better acid-base balance during sedation19, as well as reduced liver and renal dysfunction in critically ill patients. In postoperative settings, it successfully achieved appropriate sedation depth without significant hemodynamic impact20, demonstrating good efficacy in managing pain and anxiety. Overall, remimazolam presents promising safety and effectiveness for long-term sedation and lung protection in mechanically ventilated patients, potentially warranting broader clinical applications in critical care as research advances.

In our study, adverse events, including the need for instances of hypotension or delirium, were comparable between the groups. Specifically, remimazolam had a 7.3% hypotension rate and 3.6% delirium incidence, which were not significantly different from the 10.9% hypotension and 5.5% delirium rate in the propofol group. These results are crucial as they demonstrate that remimazolam can be safely administered without increasing the risk of significant side effects compared to propofol, particularly in a population of cancer patients, who are often immunocompromised. A weakened immune system can reduce the body’s ability to resist infections and inflammatory responses, thereby increasing the risk of delirium21. Specifically, patients with compromised immunity are more susceptible to infections, which can trigger a systemic inflammatory response that impacts the central nervous system and leads to delirium22.

The biotransformation of remimazolam primarily relies on esterases in the body, especially carboxylesterase 1 (CES1)11. This characteristic allows remimazolam to be swiftly metabolized into the inactive metabolite CNS7054, resulting in rapid drug effect cessation. The elimination half-life of remimazolam is typically short, approximately 20 to 40 min, and its plasma concentration declines quickly after intravenous infusion, reducing the drug’s retention time in the body23,24. Clinical studies have shown that the clearance rate of remimazolam can reach up to 1.15 L/min, with a relatively small volume of distribution at steady state (about 35.4 L)25. This allows the drug concentration to quickly reach clinically effective levels during sedation, and consciousness can rapidly return after infusion cessation. Due to its unique pharmacokinetic characteristics, remimazolam exhibits good controllability in sedation effects, significantly reducing the risk of cardiovascular and respiratory depression associated with the drug24,25.

Additionally, we performed an exploratory subgroup analysis in septic patients, which found that remimazolam was associated with lower NLR values (a key inflammatory response indicator) at 24 and 48 h in this cohort. This observational trend aligns with preclinical studies suggesting potential anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties of remimazolam26–30; in animal models, the drug has been shown to regulate immune cell function, inhibit proinflammatory cytokine release, and exert protective effects in acute kidney injury and cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury via inflammatory modulation27,29, hinting at biological effects beyond conventional sedation. Notably, this subgroup analysis was not pre-specified as a primary or key secondary endpoint of the study, and was further limited by a small sample size, unadjusted multiple comparisons, and inherent susceptibility to type I error. No definitive conclusions regarding remimazolam’s anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory effects in human septic patients can be drawn therefrom, and these findings are purely hypothesis-generating. Such potential properties of remimazolam require rigorous validation in well-designed, large-sample, dedicated prospective clinical trials, and it is premature to infer the drug’s specific suitability for critically ill cancer patients based solely on this exploratory observation.

However, this study does have limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the lack of blinding for study personnel could introduce bias, especially given the distinct visual differences between remimazolam and propofol. Secondly, despite following randomization protocols, some baseline characteristics between groups were not perfectly balanced, potentially leading to confounding results. Furthermore, the use of adjunct sedatives was determined at the discretion of the attending physician, which may have influenced outcomes. Additionally, due to considerations for patient safety, hemodynamically unstable patients were excluded, and as this is a pilot study, accurate sample size calculations were not performed, resulting in insufficient statistical power to detect differences in key clinical outcomes including mortality, delirium, and hypotension. Moreover, the findings of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in the septic subgroup may be susceptible to type I error and should thus be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Future studies with larger sample sizes are anticipated to validate the preliminary results of this research.

In conclusion, remimazolam is a safe and effective sedative for long-term sedation in mechanically ventilated cancer patients, with sedation efficacy and safety comparable to propofol. A preliminary exploratory finding indicates potential immunomodulatory effects in the sepsis subgroup, which merits further investigation. Given the limitations of this pilot study (inadequate power for subgroup analyses), large, well-powered randomized controlled trials are needed to validate remimazolam’s role in ICU sedation and its potential applications in inflammatory conditions.
