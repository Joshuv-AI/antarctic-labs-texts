HDAC3 Preserves the primordial follicle reserve by epigenetically suppressing ferroptosis in pregranulosa cells
Authors: Ziqi Chen, Jiantao Guo, Meng Gao, Huarong Wang, Han Cai, Qingfeng Yang, Xinyu Yang, Yi Lin, Zijian Zhu, Shaogang Qin, Yibing Bao, Ting Zhao, Longping Liu, Tengteng Wang, Bo Zhou, Hua Zhang, Jianbin Wang, Hua Guo, Guoliang Xia, Chao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The non-renewable primordial follicle (PF) pool underlies female reproductive longevity, yet PF numbers decline sharply before puberty for reasons that remain unclear. Here, we identify histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) in pregranulosa cells (pGCs) as a key suppressor of ferroptosis in PFs. Constitutively low HDAC3 expression in pGCs is essential for PF survival: conditional deletion of Hdac3 caused profound PF depletion and ultimately premature ovarian insufficiency. Loss of HDAC3 preferentially triggered ferroptosis, as supported by ferroptosis-associated transcriptional signatures and dysregulation of genes governing lipoxin biosynthesis and cellular iron homeostasis following Hdac3 depletion. Correspondingly, alterations in proteins central to ferroptotic pathways corroborated this mechanism. Together, these findings demonstrate that constitutive HDAC3 expression in pGCs safeguards the PF reserve by restraining ferroptosis and is therefore indispensable for female fertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06128-x.

Introduction

As the non-renewable reservoir of oocytes, the primordial follicle (PF) pool is fundamental for sustaining the female reproductive lifespan and promoting long-term health in aging women [1]. PFs are maintained in a metabolically and transcriptionally quiescent state and consist of a meiotically arrested oocyte encased by flattened pregranulosa cells (pGCs) [2]. Although a small subset of PFs is periodically activated after birth, the majority undergo extensive degeneration and eventual death with age for largely unknown reasons [3–7]. More than half of all PFs are lost by puberty [6], and the remaining follicles decline gradually until menopause [3, 4]. Clinically, excessive PF depletion is tightly linked to premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), marked by exhaustion of the PF pool and onset of menopause before age 35–40 [8, 9]. Thus, elucidating why PFs can remain dormant for years after birth—and under which conditions they die—is essential for understanding and ultimately treating POI.

Previous studies, including our own, indicate that both autophagy and apoptosis within oocytes are not only crucial for the massive oocyte attrition that occurs before PF formation, but also for determining the initial PF endowment in perinatal ovaries [10–12]. Notably, follicle atresia is developmentally stage-dependent [6, 13–15]. Preantral follicle atresia appears to involve autophagy or ferroptosis [15], whereas antral follicles degenerate primarily through apoptosis [14]. For PFs, existing work shows that prepubertal PF loss is not mediated by classical apoptotic pathways [6], and although autophagy contributes to PF regulation in Atg7-deficient mice [13], the precise mechanisms governing PF death—particularly those involving programmed death in pGCs and POI unrelated to none genetic defects—remain unresolved.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death, is driven by intracellular iron accumulation and uncontrolled phospholipid peroxidation [16, 17], ultimately compromising membrane integrity and triggering cell death [18]. Ferroptosis has been implicated in ovarian aging, POI, and multiple cancers [15, 19]. In primary follicles, mutation of the POI-associated gene Basonuclin 1 induces ferroptosis and POI in mice. Similarly, in high-calorie diet–induced POI models, ferrous ions, hydroxyl radicals, and malondialdehyde increase, while glutathione (GSH) decreases [20]. These findings prompted us to consider whether ferroptosis might influence PF fate under physiological conditions. Given that ferroptosis is activated by dysregulation of environmental factors such as iron, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and amino acid metabolism [18], we examined whether ROS and lipid metabolism determine PF survival via ferroptosis. Indeed, recent work shows that low ROS levels maintain PF dormancy by inhibiting ferroptosis and preserving fertility [21], and that ferroptosis is activated within follicles during obesity and may regulate the primordial-to-primary follicle transition [22]. Thus, ferroptosis—and its ties to ROS, iron, and lipid metabolism—may play a crucial role in determining PF fate.

Epigenetic modifiers, which sense environmental cues, also regulate ferroptosis in the reproductive system, as demonstrated in several studies, including our own [23–30]. Among them, HDAC3, a class I histone deacetylase, is required for primordial germ cell formation, oocyte chromosome migration under physiological conditions, and for preventing implantation failure, decidual defects, and infertility under pathological conditions [23, 24, 27, 28, 31, 32]. Reduced HDAC3 expression in oocytes of aged mice, or siRNA-mediated knockdown in young oocytes, leads to meiotic defects and aneuploidy in offspring [23, 24]. Our work also shows that HDAC3 in granulosa cells of fully grown follicles governs oocyte meiotic resumption prior to the gonadotropin surge in both humans and mice [28, 33]. Supporting the hypothesis that the mechanisms underlying PF death reside within the PF niche itself [6], this study identifies HDAC3 in pGCs as a critical protector of PFs, preventing their ferroptosis in prepubertal mice.

Results

Constitutive HDAC3 expression is required to maintain the PF reserve

To investigate the role of HDAC3 in PFs, we first examined its localization and expression dynamics in embryonic and neonatal mouse ovaries. HDAC3 expression changed dynamically from fetal to newborn stages. In fetal ovaries, HDAC3 was undetectable in pGCs between 13.5 and 15.5 dpc (Fig. S1). However, its expression gradually increased from 17.5 dpc and continued rising throughout perinatal PF formation. In newborn mice, ovarian HDAC3 expression increased in a time-dependent manner at both 5 dpp and 7 dpp—coinciding with the initiation of the first wave of PF activation (Fig. S1). These observations suggest that HDAC3 contributes to fetal ovarian development in a temporally regulated manner.

Postnatally, although HDAC3 levels were lower in pGCs of PFs than in granulosa cells of growing follicles (Fig. 1a, b), HDAC3 expression in PF pGCs remained consistently detectable at puberty, 3 months, and 8 months of age (Fig. 1b). To directly test whether HDAC3 is required to preserve PFs, we established an ovary culture model in which HDAC3 activity was inhibited in vitro. 2 dpp ovaries were cultured with either the HDAC3-specific inhibitor RGFP (3.0 µM) or SC (2.5 µM). Both inhibitors significantly increased global histone acetylation after 1 day of culture (equivalent to 3 dpp in vivo) and after 5 days (equivalent to 7 dpp) (Fig. 1c, e). After 5 days, ovaries treated with either inhibitor were visibly smaller than controls (Fig. 1d). Although 1-day treatment did not alter PF numbers, 5-day exposure to either RGFP or SC markedly reduced both PF and total follicle numbers (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1f, g). These results demonstrate that a low but sustained level of HDAC3 is essential to prevent premature PF depletion.

To further evaluate the in vivo function of HDAC3 in PF maintenance, we generated a pGC-specific Hdac3 knockout by crossing Hdac3flox/flox (Hdac3f/f) mice with Foxl2-CreERT2 mice, yielding Hdac3flox/flox; Foxl2-CreERT2 (Hdac3cKO) females. Tamoxifen (20 µg/g body weight) was administered at 1, 3, and 5 dpp, and knockout efficiency and ovarian morphology were analyzed at defined timepoints (Fig. 2a). At 10 dpp, HDAC3 expression was strongly reduced in ovaries from Hdac3cKO mice (Fig. 2b, c), yet follicle numbers and ovarian morphology remained comparable to Hdac3f/f mice (Fig. 2d-f). By contrast, at 2 and 3 weeks of age, Hdac3 depletion caused a dramatic loss of the ovarian reserve: PF numbers declined to ~ 30% and ~ 20% of control levels, respectively (P = 0.003 and P = 0.018) (Fig. 2g-l). First, we examined phosphorylation and subcellular localization of FOXO3a (p-FOXO3a), which translocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm during PF activation [34]. To validate this conclusion, we systematically evaluated the expression of proteins involved in PF activation pathways, including components of the mTORC1 and PI3K–PTEN–AKT–FOXO3 signaling pathways, both of which are canonical pathways controlling PF activation [34]. Consistent with the FOXO3a data, neither RGFP nor SC altered expression of mTORC1, phosphorylated mTORC1, PTEN, p27, AKT, phosphorylated AKT, RPS6, or phosphorylated RPS6 (Fig. 3e, f). Together, these results indicate that the PF loss observed after HDAC3 inhibition is unlikely to arise from an increased rate of PF activation but instead reflects a failure to maintain PF survival.

The lack of HDAC3 does not induce significant ovarian apoptosis or autophagy

To determine which modality of programmed cell death mediated by HDAC3 deficiency underlying PF loss, we systematically assessed canonical markers of apoptosis and autophagy.

TUNEL assays revealed no significant increase in ovaries following RGFP treatment in vitro (Fig. S2a, b). Consistently, active Caspase-3 protein levels remained unchanged (Fig. S2c), and mRNA expression of apoptosis-related genes—including Bax, Bad, Mcl1, Bcl2, and Casp8—was comparable to controls (Fig. S2d). These results indicate that HDAC3 inhibition does not elicit overt apoptosis. To extend these findings in vivo, we examined ovaries from Hdac3f/f and Hdac3cKO mice at 10 dpp. Immunofluorescence staining showed no significant differences in γH2AX, cleaved Caspase-3, or TUNEL signals (Fig. S2f–i), further demonstrating that apoptosis is unlikely to be the primary mechanism underlying PF depletion in the absence of HDAC3.

We next assessed whether HDAC3 deficiency affects autophagy. Both immunofluorescence staining and Western blotting revealed that LC3-II levels were unchanged following HDAC3 inhibition in vitro (Fig. S3a-c). The mRNA analysis showed stable expression of autophagy related genes under the same conditions (Fig. S3d), and LC3 protein levels were similar between Hdac3f/f and Hdac3cKO ovaries in vivo (Fig. S3e, f). Collectively, these data indicate that HDAC3 loss does not significantly alter ovarian autophagy.

GC-specific Hdac3 knockout disrupts lipid metabolism and ion homeostasis

To explore the molecular causes of PF loss following Hdac3 depletion in GCs, we applied geographical position sequencing (GEO-seq) to systematically profile transcriptomic changes across follicle developmental stages. As shown in Fig. 2, HDAC3 protein levels were markedly reduced in ovaries from Hdac3cKO mice at 10 dpp, while PF numbers remained intact. PF depletion became apparent by 14 dpp (Fig. 2d-f), suggesting that critical molecular events leading to PF loss occur between 10 and 14 dpp. We therefore selected 10 dpp ovaries as the earliest informative timepoint for GEO-seq.

Frozen sections from Hdac3f/f and Hdac3cKO ovaries were prepared, and adjacent sections were used for immunofluorescence validation and GEO-seq. Only Hdac3cKO sections with confirmed HDAC3 deletion were included (Fig. S5a). GCs from primordial, primary, and early secondary follicles were microdissected and collected (Fig. S5b). Transcriptomic analysis identified 162 upregulated and 564 downregulated genes in Hdac3cKO GCs (Fig. S4a). Gene ontology analysis revealed enrichment of dysregulated genes in processes related to amino acid metabolism, organic hydroxy-compound metabolism, metal ion complex binding, vesicle membrane components, and intrinsic organelle membranes (Fig. S4b). Notably, several differentially expressed genes were associated with lipoxin biosynthesis, organophosphate ester transport, and iron homeostasis (Fig. S4c-d). Together with our earlier findings that PF loss was not attributable to aberrant PF activation, apoptosis, or autophagy, these data suggest a metabolic and ionic imbalance as a potential driver of PF depletion.

Given the transcriptomic shifts, we hypothesized that ferroptosis, characterized by intracellular iron accumulation and uncontrolled lipid peroxidation [18, 19, 35], might contribute to PF loss. Examination of ferroptosis-related genes in the GEO-seq dataset revealed strong upregulation of transferrin receptor 2 (Tfr2), which mediates transferrin-dependent iron uptake (Fig. S4e). Likewise, acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (ACSL4) mRNA was elevated. ACSL4 is a key isozyme that activates polyunsaturated fatty acids to produce fatty acyl-CoA esters, thereby promoting susceptibility to ferroptosis and modulating lipid peroxidation [36]. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed increased ACSL4 protein in PFs of Hdac3cKO mice (Fig. S4f). Thus, Hdac3 deletion in ovarian GCs enhances ACSL4 and Tfr2 expression, implicating ferroptosis in PF loss.

Collectively, these findings indicate that GC-specific Hdac3 knockout induces ferroptosis-related molecular changes, which ultimately drive the extensive loss of PFs observed in vivo.

Inhibition of HDAC3 causes ferroptosis in cultured ovaries in vitro

To test our hypothesis that HDAC3 depletion triggers ferroptosis in pGCs of PFs and consequently drives PF loss in mouse ovaries, we conducted a series of in vitro assays to reassess the causality of RGFP-induced follicle depletion. First, lipid peroxidation fluorescence was markedly increased in 2 dpp ovaries cultured with RGFP for 2 days compared with controls (Fig. 4a, b). Second, transmission electron microscopy confirmed a substantial reduction in mitochondrial volume, accompanied by attenuated or absent cristae in pGCs of PFs following RGFP treatment (Fig. 4e), consistent with previously described ultrastructural hallmarks of ferroptosis [18]. Third, ACSL4 protein expression was significantly elevated (Fig. 4c–d, f), and Ferritin levels showed a modest increase after 1 day of RGFP exposure (Fig. 4f). Fourth, the mRNA analysis verified a robust upregulation of Tfr2, consistent with our in vivo observations (Fig. 4g; Fig. S4e). To further determine whether ferroptosis mediates PF loss following HDAC3 inhibition, we performed in vitro rescue assays. Ovaries were assigned to four groups: control (DMSO), RGFP (HDAC3 inhibitor), RGFP + Fer-1 (20 µM Ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor), and Fer-1 alone. After 2 dpp, ovaries were cultured under these conditions for 5 days, PF counts revealed that the RGFP + Fer-1 group retained significantly more PFs than the RGFP group (P = 0.000079) (Fig. 4h–i). Western blotting further showed that ACSL4 protein levels were lower in the RGFP + Fer-1 group than in the RGFP group (Fig. 4j). Thus, HDAC3 inhibition induces ferroptosis in PF GCs, whereas suppressing ferroptosis effectively rescues PF loss.

We next validated this assumption using an independent ferroptosis-induction model. Ovaries cultured with Erastin (5 µM), a known ferroptosis activator, exhibited markedly elevated lipid peroxidation after 2 days (equivalent to 4 dpp in vivo) (Fig. 4k–l). Extending Erastin treatment to 5 days (equivalent to 7 dpp in vivo) significantly reduced total follicle numbers compared with controls. Notably, PF numbers declined to approximately half of control levels, decreasing from 6033.8 ± 418.7 per ovary to 3667.5 ± 540.2 per ovary (Fig. 4m–n). Collectively, these results further demonstrate that ferroptosis is the principal mechanism underlying PF loss induced by HDAC3 deletion.

HDAC3 contributes to ferroptosis by impairing cellular iron homeostasis

To investigate how HDAC3 influences ferroptosis in pGCs, we reanalyzed the GEO-seq data to identify ferroptosis-related transcriptional changes. Acsl4 and the phospholipid hydroperoxidase Gpx4, which protects against lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [37], were both upregulated. Tfr2 and Fundc2 transcript levels also increased, with elevated Tfr2 likely enhancing plasma membrane iron uptake. In contrast, Fdxr and Got1 exhibited the opposite pattern in pGCs of PFs (Fig. 5a). Although total cellular iron content remained unchanged, ferrous iron levels were significantly elevated in GCs of RGFP966-treated ovaries (Fig. 5b–c). Expression of Tf remained stable (Fig. 5l, q), suggesting that steady Tf levels may help maintain total iron content. To examine iron distribution more precisely, we isolated cytoplasmic and mitochondrial fractions from RGFP966-treated pGCs (Fig. 5j) and measured compartment-specific ferrous iron using dedicated markers (Fig. 5d). Cytoplasmic ferrous iron levels remained largely unchanged, but mitochondrial ferrous iron increased nearly fourfold, indicating a striking disruption of mitochondrial iron homeostasis.

To further explore HDAC3-dependent changes in iron-metabolism proteins in vivo, we examined regulators associated with dysfunctional iron handling [38]. Loss of HDAC3 markedly increased p53 and p21 levels (Fig. 6a, i–j), whereas p16 remained unchanged (Fig. 6b, k). Both IRP1 and IRP2—RNA-binding proteins that control iron homeostasis—were reduced (Fig. 6c–d, l–m). Although IRP2 deficiency typically lowers ISC-containing proteins such as NDUFS1, SDHB, and UQCRFS1 [39], all three proteins were elevated in this study, indicating IRP2 activation rather than suppression. FTMT, a mitochondrial ferritin that buffers free ferrous ions, was also elevated (Fig. 6e, n). Moreover, oxidative phosphorylation-associated proteins NDUFS1, SDHB, and UQCRFS1 were upregulated (Fig. 6f–h, o–q). HDAC3 affects iron homeostasis indirectly in pGCs by regulating H3K27ac levels

To determine how HDAC3 regulates transcription of ferroptosis-related genes, we reanalyzed publicly available ChIP-seq data for H3K27ac in pGCs of PFs [40]. H3K27ac levels were markedly elevated in both HDAC3-inhibitor–treated groups compared with other acetylation sites (Fig. 7a–d). H3K27ac peaks were highly enriched within 1 kb of transcription start sites (76.9%), supporting its role as a promoter-proximal transcriptional regulator. Using the cCRE database from ENCODE [41, 42], we identified H3K27ac-associated regulatory elements within promoter regions of several ferroptosis-related genes (Fig. 7e–g). In summary, constitutive HDAC3 expression in pGCs is essential for maintaining the ovarian reserve by preventing ferroptosis. Loss of HDAC3 disrupts iron homeostasis, causes mitochondrial dysfunction, and elevates lipid peroxidation. Mechanistically, HDAC3 controls ferroptosis in pGCs of PFs via epigenetic regulation of H3K27ac. HDAC3 thus acts as a key protective factor against premature reproductive aging.

Discussion

Among mammalian organs, the ovary is exceptional in undergoing extensive cell loss shortly after birth [43–45]. Limited evidence indicates that prepubertal PF loss in mice is not driven by apoptosis [6], whereas autophagy in PF oocytes may contribute to determining PF fate [13]. Alongside our recent findings showing that ferroptosis underlies PF depletion in models of ovarian ROS elevation or overweight [21, 22], the present study identifies HDAC3 in pGCs as a key epigenetic safeguard that prevents ferroptosis and thereby preserves PF survival in prepubertal mice. Consistently, Hdac3 deletion caused mitochondrial structural defects and elevated expression of Tfr2 and Acsl4—two critical mediators of ferroptosis [18, 46–48]. Moreover, we ruled out apoptosis, autophagy, and aberrant PF activation as contributors to PF loss upon HDAC3 impairment. Given that Hdac3 deficiency in pGCs yields a POI-like phenotype, our findings underscore the essential role of HDAC3 in maintaining the ovarian reserve under physiological conditions. As no precise mechanism has previously explained prepubertal PF attrition, this work provides the first evidence linking accelerated PF atresia to disrupted epigenetic regulation in follicular somatic cells, offering new potential targets for POI treatment.

Our results demonstrate that ferroptosis originating from pGCs—not apoptosis, or autophagy–induced cell death—is the principal cause of PF atresia in prepubertal mice following HDAC3 deletion or inhibition (Fig. 6). Overactivation of PFs was excluded as well, as the key dormancy marker phosphorylated FOXO3a remained nuclear [49–52]. Copper-induced cell death was also unlikely because ferroptosis inhibitors cannot rescue this process [53]. Thus, constitutive HDAC3 expression in pGCs likely protects the PF pool before puberty by suppressing premature ferroptosis. Intriguingly, ferroptosis increases with age in rat ovaries [54]. Because HDAC3 is also expressed in pGCs of adult PFs, future studies are needed to examine whether HDAC3 similarly supports PF longevity in adulthood.

HDAC3 in dormant PFs suppresses ferroptosis-related gene expression to prevent premature PF loss. Epigenetic mechanisms modulate cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis through transcription factor activity and post-transcriptional regulation [55–57]. HDAC3 is a major regulator of H3K27 acetylation turnover [58]. Here, reduced transcription of Fdxr and Got1 coincided with marked enrichment of H3K27ac at their promoter regions. Although promoter-proximal H3K27ac typically reflects transcriptional activation, excessive acetylation can impede promoter clearance and disrupt enhancer–promoter resetting, ultimately hindering transcription [59–62]. Our previous work demonstrated that transcription factor SP1 regulates PF formation by controlling pGC development and promotes EGF-like gene expression after LH-induced HDAC3 degradation, which restores H3K27ac in GCs [10, 33]. SP1 also binds the promoters of Tfr2 and Gpx4 [63, 64], and we have shown that SP1 regulates cholesterol metabolism during the primordial-to-primary follicle transition by modulating Fdx1 transcription [22]. And in the ischemia/reperfusion induced ferroptosis in the intestine, SP1 binds to the Acsl4 promoter region to initiate cell death as well [65], which infers that HDAC3 could be pivotal for reducing ferroptosis related gene expression in pGCs as well.

The ability of HDAC3 to suppress ferroptosis is tightly linked to its support of mitochondrial function and lipid metabolism. GOT1, a component of the malate–aspartate shuttle, transfers reducing equivalents from the cytosol to the mitochondria as NADH to sustain oxidative phosphorylation (OxPHOS). Loss of Got1 sensitizes pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells to ferroptosis [66]. Thus, reduced GOT1 likely impairs NADH supply, disrupts OxPHOS, promotes catabolism, and increases free ferrous iron. FDXR, a mitochondrial cytochrome P450 electron transport component, is required for steroidogenesis, hemoglobin synthesis, and ISC biosynthesis [67, 68]. Fdxr down-regulation, therefore, impairs ISC production, leading to mitochondrial iron accumulation rather than cytosolic iron elevation [69]. FDXR mutations cause mitochondrial disorders associated with defective ISC synthesis and dysregulated iron homeostasis via IRP1/2-mediated post-transcriptional mechanisms [70, 71]. To maintain oxidative phosphorylation, ISC-containing proteins such as NDUFS1, SDHB, and UQCRFS1 are upregulated to re-establish ISC sufficiency, consistent with our observations. Altogether, this study reveals that HDAC3 in pGCs is indispensable for sustaining mitochondrial homeostasis and preventing ferroptosis, thereby ensuring the long-term stability of the ovarian reserve.

Ferroptosis is strongly dependent on iron and ROS and is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to ovarian diseases [15, 18, 72]. We recently demonstrated that lipid dysregulation in PFs triggers ferroptosis and that elevated ROS in PF oocytes of aging mice is a critical driver of this process [21]. In the present study, specific knockout of Hdac3 in pGCs led to enrichment of genes involved in lipoxin synthesis, organophosphate ester transport, and cellular iron homeostasis in GCs, correlating with disrupted lipid metabolism, impaired ion homeostasis, and induction of ferroptosis. Functionally, inhibition of ferroptosis with Ferrostatin-1 mitigated PF loss, whereas activation of ferroptosis using Erastin increased lipid peroxidation and PF depletion in vitro. Collectively, these results establish ferroptosis as the primary mechanism underlying the POI-like phenotype induced by HDAC3 deficiency in pGCs, highlighting the essential role of constitutive HDAC3 in protecting dormant PFs from ferroptosis.

Given that key ferroptosis regulators are druggable targets and that HDAC inhibitors can enhance ferroptosis in cancer cells [73, 74], both HDAC3 and ferroptosis-related proteins represent potential therapeutic targets for POI. For example, ferroptosis induction via CoQ10 depletion has been explored as an ovarian cancer therapy [75]. Additionally, a pathogenic ferroptosis mechanism underlying POI was identified in Bnc1-deficient mice, suggesting that ferroptosis inhibitors could be leveraged to treat POI [15]. In agreement, our study implies that strategies aimed at suppressing ferroptosis or preserving HDAC3 activity may offer promising therapeutic avenues for POI patients.

Methods

Mice

C57B6/J mice and CD-1 mice were purchased from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). Female mice mated with male mice overnight and the vaginal plug was checked the following morning, which was considered 0.5 days post coitus (dpc). The day the pups were born was designated 0.5 day postpartum (dpp). Hdac3flox/flox mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (ME, USA), and Foxl2-CreERT2 mice were gifted by Prof. Kui Liu. All animals were maintained under a 12h/12 h light/dark cycle at 24–26 ℃ with free access to chow and water at a SPF grade facility. The experiments were carried out in accordance with the principles and guidelines for the use of laboratory animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Agricultural University (AW81601202-3–11).

Tamoxifen administration toHdac3flox/flox; Foxl2-CreERT2mice

Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was resuspended to 100 µg/µL in 95% (vol/vol) ethanol and further diluted with corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to a final concentration of 20 µg/µL. The mice were injected with tamoxifen (20 µg/g body weight) at 1 dpp, 3 dpp and 5 dpp in Hdac3flox/flox; Foxl2-CreERT2 mice (Hdac3cKO, cKO) and Hdac3flox/flox mice (controls).

Ovary isolation and culture

Ovaries were isolated in 4℃ PBS and cultured on cell culture inserts (Millipore, MA, USA) in six well plates containing 2 mL of DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Life Technologies, CA, USA). After that, they were placed in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37℃ with saturated humidity. The medium was added in streptomycin and penicillin and changed every other day. RGFP (S7229, Selleck, Shanghai, China) and SC (sc207902, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA) are the two specific HDAC3 inhibitors. The working concentrations of the two inhibitors are 3 µM and 2.5 µM, respectively. Ferrostatin-1 (SML0583, Sigma, USA) is the inhibiter of ferroptosis, whose working concentration is 20 µM.

Granulosa cells isolation

Ovaries with disrupted tunica albuginea were digested into cell suspensions by the addition of trypsin-EDTA (0.25%, 37 °C). The enzymatic reaction was terminated by the addition of pre-cooled foetal bovine serum (FBS). The cell suspension was then filtered using a cell sieve and spun at 3000 rcf for 5 min. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in Medium 199 and transferred to a six-well cell culture plate, which was incubated in CO2 incubator (5%, 37 °C) for 4–6 h to remove the oocytes and obtain granulosa cells.

Western blotting analysis

Ovaries were lysed with WIP (CellChip, BJ Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (CellChip, BJ Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Then, each sample’s total protein was separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, MA, USA). Each sample was incubated with respective antibodies as required, and the antibodies involved are listed in Supplemental Table S1. Chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon 5200, Tanon, Shanghai, China) was used to observed the results.

Immunofluorescence

Ovaries were fixed in cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight. Then the samples were dehydrated gradient in alcohol, transparent in xylene, embedded in paraffin. The thickness of sections collected from mice before 2 w was 5 μm, while after 2 w was 8 μm, respectively. In short, the sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was performed by microwaving for 12 min in 0.01% sodium citrate buffer (pH = 6.0). For immunofluorescence analysis, sections were blocked with 10% normal donkey serum and incubated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 555-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at 37℃ and Hoechst 33,342 (B2261, Sigma, USA). The antibodies involved are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Real-time PCR analysis

RNAs from cultured ovaries were extracted by TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and quality of total RNA were detected using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Nano-3000, ALLSHENG, Hangzhou, China). Every 500–1000 ng of total RNAs per sample was applied to perform reverse transcription (RT) using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). qPCR was conducted and analized on an LightCycler® 96 (Roche, UK) using a standard protocol. The primers used for test genes are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

TUNEL

The thickness of ovarian sections was 5 μm. The apoptotic cells on each section were detected using a TUNEL BrightGreen Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China).

Lipid peroxide levels assay

Mice ovaries were digested into single cells using trypsin. After which, the cells were washed with PBS to remove FBS. Cells were incubated for 20 min at 37℃ using 3 µM BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, CA, USA) and Hoechst 33,342 (B2261, Sigma, USA). The cells were washed with PBS, and observed by fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Geographical position sequencing (GEO-seq)

Freshly recovered mice ovaries were isolated quickly without any buffer solution, and immediately froze in liquid nitrogen. Then, ovaries were wrapped in OCT. Before collecting ovarian samples for GEO-seq usage, set the temperature on the cryostat to −20 °C and the cutting thickness of the ovarian samples was set to 7 μm. Then, adjacent sections on each slide were used for GEO-seq after HDAC3 immunostaining. The remaining experimental procedures were carried out according to the literature of Chen et al. [76]. The transcriptome analysis was performed by Novogene accordingly (Beijing, China).

Iron and ferrous iron measurement

The content of iron and ferrous iron in cytoplasm and mitochondria was detected by Iron Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, USA) following the recommended procedure. Fe2+ in the homogenized sample was specifically captured by a chromogen to form a blue colored complex. Crude isolation of cytoplasm and mitochondria from GCs with Cell Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The concentration of ferrous iron in cytoplasm and mitochondria was determined by measuring the intensity of the color at 590 nm with microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, USA). Total iron content can be determined in the same way with the participation of reducing agent.

Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Granulosa cells were treated with formaldehyde (37%) and then lysed. The chromatin was digested to shear the DNA into smaller fragments. The sonicated chromatin was incubated with an anti-H3K27ac antibody. The antibody-protein-DNA complex was precipitated using magnetic beads. The beads were washed to remove non-specifically bound material. The crosslinks were then reversed, and the protein-DNA complexes were eluted from the beads. The eluted DNA was treated to reverse the crosslinks, allowing the DNA to be purified. The purified DNA was analysed by qPCR to determine the regions of DNA bound by H3K27ac.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For each sample, the ovaries were isolated quickly and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Then, the treated ovaries were processed and wrapped in epoxypropane resin following standard TEM.

Statistical analysis

All results were repeated more than three biologically independent experiments. And the values are presented as the means ± S.D. The data were analized by ANOVA or t test and considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
