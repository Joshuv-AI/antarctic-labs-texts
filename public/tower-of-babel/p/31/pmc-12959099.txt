Instagram in the Clinic: A New Challenge for Patient–Physician Communication
Authors: Jayashree Ravikumar
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

“Doctor, I saw it on Instagram”, a phrase that is increasingly heard in outpatient settings. Patients now arrive quoting influencers, trending reels, and viral health tips. From detox teas to supplement stacks, the influence of social media often outweighs that of qualified clinicians.

This shift poses a dual challenge. On one hand, it reflects greater curiosity and patient autonomy; on the other, it increases exposure to misinformation. A systematic review found that nearly a third of health-related content on platforms like Instagram and TikTok is misleading, particularly on topics like cancer, nutrition, and weight loss.1 In South Asian community clinics, where digital access is growing but health literacy is variable, this misinformation can lead to delayed care, poor adherence, and heightened anxiety.

The growing influence of social media on health beliefs has prompted scholarly attention to the broader phenomenon of health misinformation online. Researchers emphasize that these platforms often amplify emotionally appealing but scientifically unsupported content, which can spread rapidly and influence public perception.2 This is especially concerning in clinical practice, where patients may unknowingly trust viral trends over verified medical guidance. As such, understanding the dynamics of misinformation propagation is essential for clinicians who seek to engage more effectively with digitally informed patients.

Physicians have historically served as the primary influencers of public health. Today’s social media personalities may communicate more engagingly, but they lack the personalized understanding and accountability that the physician–patient relationship provides. This is our enduring advantage: we offer tailored advice rooted in both evidence and empathy, something social media cannot replicate.

Rather than dismissing patients’ online discoveries, clinicians must approach them with openness. Acknowledging patients’ curiosity and exploring their sources of information can become powerful tools for education. Shifting our role from correctors to collaborators can help rebuild trust and improve communication.

Social media is not going away. The real question is whether we, as clinicians, can evolve alongside it by guiding patients to think critically, encouraging responsible digital habits, and perhaps even participating in the online conversation ourselves. Partnerships between health professionals and digital creators may serve as a promising bridge across this widening gap.
