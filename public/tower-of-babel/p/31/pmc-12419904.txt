Patient-reported outcomes following patella fractures
Authors: Viktor Schmidt, Emilia Möller Rydberg, Matthias Krause, Olof Wolf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPatella fractures can greatly impact knee function and quality of life. The primary aim of this study was to assess patient-reported outcomes one-year post-patella fracture. A secondary aim was to evaluate outcome differences based on sex, age, fracture classification, treatment modality, and patients’ expectations.MethodsThis observational study included all patients aged ≥ 18 years at injury with a registered patella fracture in the Swedish Fracture Register between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2023. The Short Musculoskeletal Function Assessment (SMFA) questionnaire measured patient-reported outcomes, focusing on the bother and mobility indices. Changes in SMFA and EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D) scores were analyzed from pre-injury (recall) to one year post-injury. Patients’ expectations and recovery status were assessed separately.ResultsAmong the 8,726 study participants, 27% (n = 2,339) completed the one-year follow-up assessment of patient-reported outcome measures. A significant overall decline in function was observed one year post-injury, indicated by a mean increase of 9.1 (95% CI 8.4 to 9.8) in the bother index and 12.2 (95% CI 11.4 to 13.0) in the mobility index. A minor difference was observed, with females reporting somewhat less successful outcomes than males. A more pronounced functional decline was observed in patients aged ≤ 65 years compared to their older counterparts (those aged > 65 years). Patients receiving operative treatment showed a greater decline in functional outcomes, as measured by SMFA scores, than those managed nonoperatively. More complex fractures, as categorized by the AO/Orthopaedic Trauma Association classification, were associated with worse outcomes. Complete recovery was more probable for patients anticipating such an outcome.ConclusionPatella fractures result in a measurable decline in patient-reported functional outcomes one year after injury. Introduction

Patella fractures are relatively uncommon injuries, accounting for approximately 1% of all fractures, with an annual incidence of 13 per 100,000 individuals.1,2 Despite their rarity, these fractures can lead to considerable morbidity, including pain, restrictions in range of motion, muscle weakness, difficulties with weightbearing tasks such as walking, running, and climbing stairs, and a diminished quality of life (QoL).3-5 Treatment, encompassing operative and nonoperative approaches, is determined by fracture classification, displacement, and impairment of the extensor mechanism.6 Fractures with minimal displacement and preserved extensor function may be managed nonoperatively.7 However, displaced fractures or those with disrupted extensor mechanisms typically require operative intervention.8

Historically, the standard surgical procedure for patella fractures involves combining tension band wiring (TBW) and Kirschner wires.9 While TBW may yield acceptable outcomes, it is associated with a relatively high complication rate, including hardware irritation, implant failure, nonunion, infection, and secondary surgery.10-12 These complications have the potential to compromise outcomes.

In recent years, locking plate (LP) fixation has emerged as a viable surgical alternative for treating patella fractures.13,14 Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that LPs offer superior stability compared to conventional TBW,15 and early clinical studies suggest that LP fixation may reduce the risk of postoperative complications.16,17 However, no randomized controlled trials have conclusively compared TBW with LP fixation.18

Despite the recognized complications and functional limitations following patella fractures, there are limited data on patient-reported outcomes, especially from large-scale observational studies. Understanding the impact of patella fractures on patients’ function and QoL is crucial for guiding treatment decisions and improving rehabilitation protocols.

This study sought to assess the functional outcomes at one year post-patella fracture using validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs): the Short Musculoskeletal Function Assessment (SMFA),19 and EuroQoL five-dimension five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L).20,21 Specifically, we aimed to compare outcomes and identify factors associated with impaired functional recovery, such as age, fracture classification, treatment modality, and patients’ expectations.

Methods

This observational study analyzed prospectively collected data from the Swedish Fracture Register (SFR), a national quality register established in 2011.22 By 2020, the SFR had achieved 100% coverage of Swedish orthopaedic trauma departments; national completeness was 62% for tibia and patella fractures in 2023 when compared to the National Patient Register, with one-third of departments demonstrating completeness > 80%;23 and multiple studies have validated its accuracy and reliability.22,24,25 Both inpatient and outpatient fractures are registered by the treating orthopaedic surgeons, regardless of treatment modality.22,26 Fractures are classified according to the AO/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) 2007 classification.27 This study included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with a patella fracture and registered in the SFR between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2023.

Patient characteristics

Of the 8,726 patients who met the inclusion criteria, the mean age was 63 years (SD 19). The cohort comprised 5,559 females (64%). The SMFA questionnaire assesses functional status in patients with musculoskeletal disorders.29 It is validated to measure a broad range of musculoskeletal injuries and includes bother and dysfunction indices, with higher scores indicating worse function (where 100 is the worst possible score and 0 is the best possible score).19,29 In this study, we assessed lower limb function using the bother index and the mobility subcategory of the dysfunction index. The bother index evaluates how much a patient is troubled by their injury across various functional domains. The mobility index focuses on function and includes questions such as, “How difficult is it for you to climb stairs?”, “[…] to bend or kneel down?”, and “[…] to walk?”. Each question in the SMFA has five levels.

The EQ-5D is a standardized, self-reported, non-disease-specific instrument for measuring health-related QoL (HRQoL). It describes health in five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activity, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression). In the EQ-5D index, a score of 0 indicates death, while a score of 1.0 signifies optimal HRQoL. The scoring system is considered less sensitive, but is appropriate when measuring changes in HRQoL over time at the aggregate level.20

In the SFR, baseline scores for SMFA and EQ-5D are obtained via recall within three weeks of injury, and responders are then invited to fill in the same PROMs one year post-injury. We focused the analysis on the change in PROMs, i.e. the difference between baseline and one-year follow-up (δ values). The larger the difference, the greater the impairment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics. Changes in SMFA and EQ-5D scores were calculated by subtracting the baseline scores from the one-year post-injury scores. The SFR used EuroQoL five-dimension three-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L) before 2019; thus, mapping from EQ-5D-3L to 5L was performed for these patients.30 Group differences were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Because of multiple testing, Bonferroni correction was used. Linear regression modelling was used with SMFA as outcome. Sex, age, classification, treatment, open, simultaneous, and contralateral fracture were included in the models. Patients respond to what degree they expect to recover at baseline and report level of recovery at one year. These responses were crosstabulated and compared between fracture types and sex. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 and 95% CIs were used. All analyses were performed with R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing v 4.5.0 (2025-04-11)), using the rms-package (v. 6.7 to 1) for regression modelling and contrasts, knitr (v. 1.45) for reproducible research, ggplot2 (v. 3.5.1) for plots, and Gmisc (v. 3.0.3) with Greg (v. 2.0.2) for table output.

Results

The most common fracture types were AO classification (Figure 2), C1 (29%), and B1 (21%). Most fractures were treated nonoperatively (Table I). PROMs with pre-injury and one-year responses were completed by 2,339 patients (27%) (Figure 3).

Changes in PROMs

There was an increase in bother and mobility indices one-year post-injury, indicating impaired function. Before fracture (baseline), the bother index had a mean of 9.5 (SD 13.9) and a median of 4.2 (IQR 53 to 77) (Supplementary Material). One year after injury, the mean increase in the bother index was 9.1 and the median increase was 6.25.

The baseline mobility index had a mean of 10.9 and a median of 2.8 (Supplementary Material). Patients were worse one year after injury: the mean increase in the mobility index was 12.2 and the median increase was 8.33. The EQ-5D index’s mean decrease one-year post-injury was 0.031 (95% CI 0.023 to 0.039).

Analyzing age, younger to middle-aged people (aged ≤ 65 years) fared significantly worse for mobility than older people. However, the bother index was not significantly different. Regarding sex, there was a similar trend towards worse results for females, significantly impacting mobility but not leading to significantly increased bother (Table II).

Fracture classification

Fracture severity seemed to be partially associated with outcome: complete articular (C) fractures have the poorest prognosis, followed by avulsion fractures (A1) (Table III). C3 fractures are the worst for all categories and significantly worse than all other classes regarding mobility and bother. There was also a trend in treatment, with nonoperative treatment being more common for simpler fractures, A2 (88%, n = 266), B1 (96%, n = 1,742), and B2 fractures (92%, n = 498), compared to A1 (75%, n = 816), C1 (57%, n = 1,446), C2 (49%, n = 428), and C3 fractures (43%, n = 575).

Treatment modality

Operative fracture treatment yielded inferior patient outcomes compared to nonoperative management. This observation held for both the mobility and bother indices as well as EQ-5D. Comparing different surgical methods, patients treated with cerclage fared worse than those treated with plate and screws; this result was significant for both the mobility and bother indices (Table IV).

Association between outcome and age, sex, and fracture class

Analyzing outcome at one year post-injury relative to baseline, regression analysis revealed better outcomes with increasing age and male sex for SMFA mobility and bother (Figure 4 and Figure 5, Supplementary Material). In addition, C3 fractures, open fractures, contralateral patella fractures, or simultaneous other fractures demonstrated worse outcomes for both indices. Moreover, surgically treated fractures generally exhibit poorer outcomes compared to non-surgical treatments; however, internal fixation with plates and screws constitutes a notable exception.

Patient expectations

Patients were asked, “To what degree do you expect to recover?” at baseline and “To what degree did you recover?” at follow-up. Patients who expected to recover well were more likely to do so, and vice versa (Figure 6a). Discussion

This nationwide observational study of a sizable cohort was conducted to examine PROMs after patella fractures using the SFR. Our results indicate a measurable functional decline at one year post-injury, irrespective of fracture classification or treatment modality. While most patients sustain only a modest deterioration, specific subgroups, younger individuals, females, and those with more complex fractures exhibit a more pronounced decline in self-reported functional capacity.

In line with previous studies on lower limb fractures, younger patients in our cohort reported greater functional impairment than older adults.31 This outcome persisted across age dichotomies (≤ 65 and > 65 years) and in the regression analysis stratified by ten-year increments.

Although previous research frequently indicated that advanced age negatively impacts musculoskeletal injury prognosis, our findings suggest younger patients, due to higher functional demands, may experience disproportionate impairment from seemingly minor deficits.3,5 Regarding age as a prognostic factor, it is crucial to address missing baseline values, as most studies do not record pre-injury functional status. The effect of the fracture is often not directly assessed; the data, instead, reflect the patient’s pre-existing functional capacity. This can lead to biased interpretations, misattributing lower baseline function in older patients to fracture-related impairment rather than their overall health condition. Additionally, younger and male patients disproportionately experience high-energy trauma or sports-related injuries, potentially resulting in complicated fracture patterns and poorer outcomes.32 The classification system may not adequately differentiate complex fracture subtypes because it does not consider displacement or the number of fragments.

Logically, avulsion fractures (A1) fare worse than isolated body fractures (A2) due to disruption of the extensor mechanism. It is plausible that lateral (B1) fractures result from direct trauma, while medial (B2) fractures are associated with patellar dislocation and yield poorer outcomes. Patients sustaining higher-grade patella fractures, such as AO/OTA C fractures, especially C3 fractures, demonstrated the most significant decline in patient-reported function. Complex and comminuted fractures likely cause more significant extensor mechanism disruption, complicating surgical and rehabilitation procedures. Moreover, these fractures affect the articular component more significantly than simpler fractures. The inferior outcomes observed in more complex fractures have also been seen in other anatomical regions, underscoring the overall significance of fracture morphology on functional prognosis.33

To elucidate the impact of a patella fracture, we can compare it to ankle fractures using δ SMFA mobility and bother scores. The most severe patella fractures (type C) resulted in functional impairment comparable to the worst ankle fractures (type C). In contrast, the milder patella fractures (types A2 and B) had a comparable effect to the least severe ankle fractures (type A1).33

Operatively treated fractures present a greater decline in SMFA scores than those managed nonoperatively. This result is hardly surprising, given that surgical management is generally reserved for more severe fractures with significant displacement or disruption of the extensor mechanism, factors which may not be captured by classification. These patients presumably suffer a more severe initial trauma, potentially prolonging their functional recovery. Conversely, patients whose fractures are treatable without surgery often present with minimal displacement and an intact extensor function, offering a more favourable recovery course.7 Nonoperative treatment had much more favourable outcomes, even in more severe fracture patterns (e.g. C3). This observation highlights the incompleteness of the AO classification of fracture characteristics, specifically the degree of displacement and disruption of the extensor mechanism.

Operative treatment using plate and screw fixation (n = 43) yielded better outcomes compared with pins/cerclage (n = 582). Although this result might suggest an advantage of modern plating techniques, conclusive assertions necessitate randomized comparisons or larger datasets with more granular data on fracture complexity.18 Additionally, these results pertain exclusively to A1 and C fractures, as A2 and B fractures rarely underwent surgical intervention.

Another important aspect is the influence of psychological factors, such as patient expectations. Our findings suggest that patients who expected a full recovery at baseline showed a higher probability of regaining pre-injury function. The link between psychology and outcomes is well documented in the broader field of orthopaedics, where motivational and psychosocial factors have been shown to play a crucial role in rehabilitation.34-36 A treatment approach using the effect of these factors would be interesting to assess in future fracture treatment algorithms.

Despite the clinical relevance of the results, this study has limitations. Foremost among these is the relatively low response rate for the one-year follow-up. This may introduce a responder bias, as patients with better function or milder symptoms could be less motivated to complete follow-up questionnaires. Moreover, we cannot rule out that comorbidities or other factors not captured in the register influenced the response decision and the reported outcomes. Another limitation is the lack of data on specific rehabilitation protocols, which may have varied across centres and influenced recovery trajectories.

A major strength lies in the large sample size and the national coverage of the SFR, enabling the inclusion of patients treated in hospitals of diverse sizes and clinical practices. This inclusiveness strengthens the generalizability of our results to a broad population of individuals sustaining patella fractures. Additionally, when comparing responders with non-responders (Table I), the differences were small, with mainly a slight tendency for responders to be older and female, which is in line with previous studies.33,37 Moreover, we used validated instruments (SMFA and EQ-5D) to capture patient-reported outcomes, further enhancing the reliability of the data.

In conclusion, patient-reported function significantly declines one year after sustaining a patella fracture. Complex fracture patterns, younger age, female sex, and negative recovery expectations are associated with greater functional impairments.

Take home message

- Patella fractures lead to significant long-term functional impairment, particularly in younger patients, women, and those with complex fractures, aligning with previous studies on lower limb fractures.

- Operative treatment generally results in worse functional outcomes than nonoperative management, likely reflecting greater fracture severity rather than the surgical intervention itself; however, newer fixation methods may offer improved results.

- Patient expectations strongly influence recovery, highlighting the importance of psychological factors in rehabilitation, a concept increasingly recognized in musculoskeletal injury recovery literature.
