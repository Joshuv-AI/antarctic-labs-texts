Medicare Part D Formulary Coverage of Disease-Modifying Therapies for Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Pengxiang Li, Matthew J. Klebanoff, John K. Lin, Keya D. Doshi, Salim Chahin, Jalpa A. Doshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study examines Medicare Part D formulary coverage of disease-modifying therapies for patients with multiple sclerosis, comparing coverage of brand-name and generic drugs.

Introduction

Approximately 25% to 30% of US patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) are insured by Medicare.1 Self-administered oral and injectable disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) covered under Medicare Part D can reduce relapses and slow disease progression but are high-cost specialty drugs. In recent years, several frequently used DMTs began facing generic competition (eAppendix in Supplement 1), which could lower costs for the Part D program and its beneficiaries. However, under the original Part D benefit design, Part D plans may have been incentivized to favor brand-name DMTs over their generic counterparts, given large rebates from brand-name manufacturers and limited financial risk.2,3,4 Starting in 2025, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) altered these dynamics by (1) increasing plans’ financial liability from 15% to 60% of total spending in the Part D catastrophic coverage phase4 and (2) implementing an annual out-of-pocket cap of $2000 in combination with the option to spread out-of-pocket costs through monthly payments (increasing the likelihood of beneficiaries filling DMTs and reaching catastrophic coverage).5 Hence, coverage may have shifted for DMTs with generics available as well as for brand-name–only DMTs.

Methods

We examined DMT formulary coverage (weighted by enrollment), using the 2013 to 2025 Medicare Prescription Drug Plan Formulary files available from the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. First, we studied the 4 DMTs with generics available (pregeneric entry to post-IRA), evaluating the percentage of Part D plans’ brand-only, generic-only, brand and generic, or no coverage. Second, we studied formulary coverage for all self-administered DMT drug classes (2021-2025), including DMTs with generics available and brand-name DMTs without generics available. Statistical analyses were conducted from July to September 2025, using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute), followed STROBE guidelines, and were exempt from institutional review board approval due to the use of publicly available, deidentified data.

Results

A substantial proportion of Medicare Part D plans exclusively covered brand-name DMTs or both the brand-name and generic versions, even after entry of a generic on the market (Figure 1). However, formulary coverage in 2025 for the brand-name versions of the 3 oral DMTs fell to nearly 0 and decreased substantially for the injectable glatiramer acetate (20.8%). In 2025, all 4 DMT agents with generics available enjoyed near-universal coverage (84.0%-100.0%), whereas brand-name–only DMTs were covered less frequently, with 8 of 11 having less than 25% coverage (Figure 2). Coverage dropped substantially from 2024 to 2025 for some brand-name–only agents (eg, diroximel fumarate, 76.0%-46.8%; interferon β-1a, 47.7%-19.9%) and remained low for others (eg, monomethyl fumarate, 13.3%-17.4%; ozanimod, 11.2%-6.5%; cladribine, 6.2%-5.5%).

Discussion

Prior to 2025, many Part D plans covered brand-name DMTs even when generics were available, which likely reflected the influence of large manufacturer rebates.2,3 Encouragingly, by 2025, Part D formulary coverage for brand-name DMTs declined sharply, whereas coverage of their generic counterparts increased. This change could lower spending on the 4 DMTs with generics available. Concerningly, coverage for brand-name DMTs without either a generic or in-class therapeutic substitute was also limited.

These findings have several implications. For MS DMTs, generic entry alone did not guarantee immediate adoption on formulary, given skewed incentives under Part D. Although the IRA likely changed these dynamics, policymakers should be aware that narrowing of formularies to exclude brand-name–only DMTs may lead to negative clinical outcomes for patients with MS.6 Clinicians should be familiar with the changing coverage landscape to avoid access delays for their Medicare patients with MS. Limitations include a descriptive study design precluding causal inference and lack of information on manufacturer rebates that may influence plan coverage decisions. Ongoing monitoring will be needed to understand how formulary coverage of DMTs and other high-cost specialty drugs evolves in future years and its impact on Part D program costs, patient access, and outcomes.
