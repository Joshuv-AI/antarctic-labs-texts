Mesenchymal stem cells reverse ovarian dysfunction by inhibiting autophagy in polycystic ovary syndrome mice
Authors: Qiwei Liu, Lei Zhan, Liang Kong, Yuanyuan Ma, Jinjuan Wang, Junhui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder that affects the reproductive health of 5%-18% of women of reproductive age worldwide. However, PCOS is yet to be completely cured. As a biomaterial that regulates the conditions of the female reproductive system, many studies have demonstrated that mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have significant therapeutic potential for restoring impaired ovarian function. The purpose of the study was to examine the therapeutic benefits of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (Huc-MSCs) in mice with dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)-induced PCOS and to assess their effects on ovulation, sex hormones, and the estrous cycle.MethodsFlow cytometry (FCM) was used to identify Huc-MSCs. The expression of the autophagy-related proteins LC3 II, Beclin1, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) was evaluated by Western blot (WB) and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Changes in the number of autophagosomes were assessed by transmission electron microscope (TEM). DHEA was subcutaneously injected into female C57BL/6J mice to develop a PCOS model. The morphological changes in the ovaries and the number of oocytes at various stages of development in PCOS mice were observed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The levels of sex hormones were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and the estrous cycle of PCOS mice was monitored using vaginal smears.ResultsThe PCOS patient group exhibited significantly higher levels of ATG5 and Parkin than the control group and a significant increase in the number of autophagosomes in the GCs of PCOS patients compared with those of non-PCOS women. Huc-MSCs are capable of repairing the impaired estrous cycle in PCOS mice and decreasing the level of testosterone (T) and the ratio of luteinizing hormone (LH)/ follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (LH/FSH ratio). Huc-MSCs are also able to repair ovulatory functions and facilitate oocytes maturation in PCOS mice. Autophagy suppression can improve oocytes viability in mice. Huc-MSCs also can inhibit granulosa cell autophagy, promote oocytes development, maintain homeostatic hormone levels, and restore the estrous cycle in PCOS mice. Huc-MSCs have the potential to treat ovarian dysfunction in PCOS mice by inhibiting autophagy pathway. In KGN cells and in DHEA-induced PCOS mice, mTOR expression was significantly downregulated. Huc-MSCs treatment markedly upregulated mTOR levels.ConclusionsOur results suggest that Huc-MSCs improve ovarian dysfunction in PCOS by inhibiting autophagy, providing experimental evidence for the clinical application of hUC-MSCs and offering a new therapeutic approach for the treatment of PCOS.Keywords: Polycystic ovarian syndrome, Autophagy, Mesenchymal stem cells, Ovarian function

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) affects approximately 5–18% of women of reproductive age worldwide. It is the most common endocrine disorder with persistent.

effects spanning from adolescence through postmenopause [1]. Characterized by a spectrum of clinical presentations, PCOS is defined by features such as clinical/ biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, polycystic ovarian morphology, and elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels [2]. In addition to reproductive complications, PCOS is associated with significant multisystem risks, including infertility, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disorders, endometrial cancer, and psychological disorders, underscoring the need for comprehensive clinical management. A core pathological feature of PCOS is the arrest of follicular development, which disrupts ovarian homeostasis. Emerging research indicates that impaired follicular maturation plays a central role in ovarian dysfunction, and strategies aimed at preserving or restoring follicular homeostasis may offer therapeutic potential [3]. Despite the substantial health burden imposed by PCOS, its molecular underpinnings remain incompletely understood, highlighting the need for deeper mechanistic insights into the development of targeted treatments.

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved lysosomal degradation pathway that plays a vital role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by recycling damaged organelles and macromolecules, and by clearing dysfunctional cytoplasmic components [4]. While traditionally regarded as a cellular housekeeping mechanism, emerging evidence implicates dysregulated autophagy in the pathogenesis of reproductive disorders [5]. In the context of follicular development, autophagy exerts a dual regulatory function as it governs the differentiation of granulosa cells (GCs) and modulates steroid hormone biosynthesis [6]. During folliculogenesis, autophagy ensures the functional adaptability of GCs by maintaining a balance between cell survival and apoptosis. However, excessive autophagic activity can induce programmed death of GCs, resulting in follicular atresia and developmental arrest [7]. Thus, considering that GCs are critical mediators of oocytes-microenvironment interactions, their autophagy-dependent homeostasis is essential for proper oocytes maturation. Disruption of autophagy in GCs can impair follicular dynamics, thereby contributing to infertility commonly associated with PCOS [8]. Moreover, autophagy plays a key role in ovarian steroidogenesis by regulating cholesterol transport and the enzymatic processes involved in androgen and estrogen synthesis [6]. This dual role positions autophagy as a central regulator of both follicular maturation and endocrine function. Therefore, aberrant autophagy may serve as a fundamental mechanism driving the follicular arrest and hormonal disturbances observed in PCOS.

Our study revealed that GCs from PCOS patients exhibited hyperactivated autophagy compared with those from healthy controls, indicating that disrupted autophagic homeostasis contributed to ovarian dysfunction in PCOS. This pathological imbalance highlights autophagy as a potential therapeutic target for addressing the underlying mechanisms of the disorder. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (Huc-MSCs), known for their regenerative capabilities and immunomodulatory effects, have previously shown therapeutic efficacy in various conditions, including reproductive disorders [9, 10]. Huc-MSCs have several characteristics like low immunogenicity, greater abundance, and paracrine effect, which make Huc-MSCs a potential option to improve ovarian function [11]. It has been suggested that Huc-MSCs as stem cell-based therapy can inhibit oxidative damage in microenvironment of the ovary, promoting hormone secretion, exerting immunomodulation effects and regenerating damaged tissues [12–14]. Besides, Huc-MSCs also have advantages such as easy access, non-invasiveness, and fewer ethical concerns which make them widely used to regenerative medicine research [15]. Now, Huc-MSCs have offered potential cures for treatment of various diseases and have emerged as therapies in clinical medicine. In our investigation, Huc-MSCs administration restored ovarian function in PCOS models through two principal mechanisms: suppression of excessive GC autophagy via activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, and normalization of steroid hormone levels. Moreover, by reducing autophagy overactivation in PCOS mice, Huc-MSCs effectively reversed follicular developmental arrest and reestablished ovarian homeostasis. These findings provide evidence of a causal relationship between aberrant GC autophagy and PCOS pathogenesis, and identified Huc-MSC-based therapy as a promising strategy for targeting the root causes of ovarian dysfunction in PCOS. To the best of our knowledge, our study provides the first evidence that Huc-MSCs can ameliorate PCOS phenotypes through autophagy modulation, offering a novel therapeutic approach for this multifaceted systemic disorder.

Materials and methods

PCOS patient samples

Human GCs were collected from age-matched women (25–30 years old, BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m²) who were undergoing assisted reproductive technology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between September 2021 and September 2022. PCOS was diagnosed according to the Rotterdam criteria, requiring at least two of the following features: oligo/anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology. The control group consisted of normo-ovulatory women with infertility attributed to male-factor or tubal causes. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. 20170046), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Demographic characteristics of participants are summarized in Table 1. The levels of luteinizing hormone (LH), the ratio of LH to FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) (LH/FSH), and testosterone(T) in the PCOS patient group were significantly different from those in the non-PCOS patient group. The LH level in the PCOS patient group (22.77 ± 8.861 uIU/ml) was higher than that in the non-PCOS patient group (5.69 ± 1.361 uIU/ml). The LH/FSH ratio and T levels in the PCOS patient group were significantly higher than those in the non-PCOS patient group: LH/FSH (3.013 ± 0.9433 vs. 0.8605 ± 0.3490, P < 0.001), testosterone (3.336 ± 0.2263 nmol/L vs. 1.348 ± 0.3693 nmol/L, P = 0.003).

Clinical characteristics of study participants

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Human serum samples were taken on the second day of menstruation. ELISA kits from Shanghai Kepei Biotechnology Co., Ltd were used to measure the serum levels of ATG5 and Parkin in both control and PCOS patient groups. The concentrations of ATG5 and Parkin were determined based on standard curves. For mouse serum, the levels of FSH, LH, and T were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions carefully. Hormone concentrations were calculated using the standard curve method.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) detection

Several pieces of 1 mm³ mouse ovary tissue and human granulosa cell masses (obtained from clinically discarded follicular fluid were centrifuged in a 1.5 ml tube at 3000 rpm for 10 min to pellet the suspended cells, which were then embedded in 4% agarose) were processed for transmission electron microscopy. All samples were then fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 4 h, followed by three rinses in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 15 min each. Post-fixation was performed using 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature. After additional three washes in phosphate buffer, the cells were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, infiltrated overnight with a 1:1 mixture of acetone and 812 embedding resin, and subsequently polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were prepared, double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, air-dried, and examined using TEM with a JEM-1400Flash instrument.

Human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells

Huc-MSCs were obtained from the Second Affiliated Hospital of South China University, following ethical approval (No. 202205-01) and informed consent. Umbilical cord segments measuring 10–15 cm were rinsed three times in phosphate-buffered saline containing penicillin and streptomycin, with blood vessels carefully removed. The remaining tissue was minced into 1 mm³ pieces and cultured in 100 mm dishes containing DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% glutamine. After 7 days, the culture medium was replaced, and cells were harvested by centrifugation and subsequently subcultured [16]. Huc-MSCs were passaged upon reaching 80–90% confluence and expanded up to passages 5–6. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed positive expressions of CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105, and negative expression of HLA-DR. The multipotent differentiation capacity of the Huc-MSCs was validated by their ability to differentiate into chondrocytes, adipocytes, and osteocytes [17].

Induction of PCOS mice and study design

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by Anhui Medical University (No. LLSC20170062). Ninety 3-week-old female C57 mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Following a 3-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 15 per group): Control, DHEA (PCOS model induced by dehydroepiandrosterone), DHEA + Huc-MSCs (Dilute the cell suspension to a final concentration of 2 × 10⁷ cells/ml. Administer 100 µl of the cell suspension per mouse via tail vein injection using an insulin syringe), DHEA + Huc-MSCs + RAPA (rapamycin, prepare a 0.3 mg/ml RAPA solution. Administer 100 µl per mouse via intraperitoneal injection using an insulin syringe, once daily for 7 consecutive days), DHEA + RAPA, and DHEA + CQ (chloroquine, prepare a 4 mg/ml CQ solution. Administer 100 µl per mouse via intraperitoneal injection using an insulin syringe, once daily for 7 consecutive days). To establish the PCOS model, 3-week-old C57 mice received daily subcutaneous injections of DHEA (6 mg/100 g body weight in a solution of 90 µl corn oil and 10 µl of 95% ethanol; total volume: 100 µl/mouse) for 20 consecutive days [18, 19]. Control mice were administered subcutaneous injections of pharmaceutical-grade soybean oil at a volume of 100 µl per day. The success of PCOS induction was confirmed by analyzing ovarian morphology and serum sex hormone levels using ELISA. In the DHEA + Huc-MSCs group, mice received a single tail vein injection of Huc-MSCs at a concentration of 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL. Mice in the DHEA + Huc-MSCs + RAPA group received both the Huc-MSCs injection and daily intraperitoneal injections of RAPA (0.3 mg/mL, 100 µl) for 7 days. The DHEA + RAPA group was administered RAPA alone, while the DHEA + CQ group received daily intraperitoneal injections of CQ (4 mg/mL, 100 µl) for 7 days. Mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital and euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Estrous cycle monitoring

Estrous cycles in mice were monitored over a 10-day period using daily vaginal smear tests. A volume of 50 µL of saline was gently instilled into the vaginal cavity using a sampler, and the fluid was smeared onto glass slides for Giemsa staining. The estrous cycle stage was determined microscopically based on the predominant cell types observed in the vaginal smears. In the proestrus stage, smears primarily showed numerous nucleated epithelial cells. During estrus, keratinized epithelial cells were more abundant than nucleated epithelial cells. The metestrus stage was characterized by the presence of both leukocytes and keratinized epithelial cells, whereas in the diestrus (anestrus) stage, only leukocytes were observed.

Ovulation and collecting oocytes

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 10 IU regnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG), followed by 10 IU human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) after 42–48 h. MI and MII oocytes were collected from oviducts 12–15 h post-hCG. Oocytes were isolated from cumulus complexes using hyaluronidase in G-GAMETE medium.

Morphological analysis and follicle statistics

Mouse ovaries were fixed in 10% formaldehyde solution for at least 24 h, followed by dehydration through a graded alcohol series and embedding in paraffin. Tissue sections were cut, dried at 45 °C, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). After staining, slides were dehydrated, mounted, and examined under a light microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE 80i) for histological analysis. Follicles were classified and counted based on developmental stage: primordial, primary, secondary, or early antral. Primary follicles were characterized by a single layer of cuboidal GCs and a visible zona pellucida. Secondary follicles exhibited multiple layers of GCs without an antral cavity. Corpora lutea were identified as post-ovulatory structures composed of residual follicular wall cells, including both granulosa and theca cell populations.

Human granulosa-like tumor cells (KGN) culture

KGN cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin, and streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were treated for 48 h with DHEA (100 µM), CQ (50 µM), RAPA (100 nM), Huc-MSCs (2 × 10⁵ cells), or various combinations of these treatments. Culture supernatants were subsequently collected for further analysis.

Western blot analysis

Proteins from mouse ovarian tissues and KGN cells were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Beijing, China) supplemented with 2% phosphatase inhibitors and 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Beyotime). Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: LC3 II (rabbit monoclonal; Sigma, L7543), Beclin1 (rabbit monoclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, D40C5), mTOR (rabbit monoclonal; Abcam, ab25880), and GAPDH (mouse monoclonal; Immunoway, YM3029). All antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:1000, except GAPDH, which was used at 1:5000. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bioworld, BS12478 and BS13278) at a dilution of 1:10000 for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Biosharp) and quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.46), with GAPDH serving as the internal loading control.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the mean ± S.D. for continuous variables. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Statistical data were determined by t test and one-way ANOVA using SPSS 17.0 software. Comparisons between the two groups were assessed using an unpaired Student’s t-test and between multiple groups using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni correction. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Autophagy was elevated in both the serum and GCs of women with PCOS

In our study, autophagy levels were evaluated by measuring serum ATG5 and Parkin protein concentrations in patients with PCOS and healthy controls. As shown in Fig. 1A, the PCOS group exhibited significantly higher levels of ATG5 (10.421 ± 3.858 vs. 2.944 ± 0.991) and Parkin (1727.83 ± 932.56 vs. 623.69 ± 231.81) than the control group. TEM analysis (Figs. 1B, C) further demonstrated a significant increase in the number of autophagosomes in the GCs of PCOS patients compared with those of non-PCOS women.

Histopathological analysis of ovarian sections revealed distinct patterns of follicular development among the experimental groups (Fig. 2G). Inhibition of autophagy in DHEA-induced PCOS mice produced improvements in ovarian function comparable to those observed after Huc-MSCs treatment. Autophagy activation led to complete arrest of folliculogenesis and the absence of corpora lutea, underscoring its detrimental effects on ovarian physiology. Analysis of follicle numbers at different developmental stages revealed that Huc-MSCs treatment effectively restored ovulation in PCOS mice (Fig. 2H). These findings collectively indicate that the recovery of ovarian function by Huc-MSCs in PCOS mice primarily depends on autophagy suppression, rather than direct stimulation of follicular development.

Huc-MSCs stimulated oocytes maturation by inhibiting autophagy

Modulation of autophagy had a significant impact on oocytes maturation in PCOS mice (Fig. 3A). Activation of autophagy with RAPA significantly impaired meiotic competence, resulting in the lowest proportion of MII oocytes falling below those observed in untreated PCOS mice. In comparison, co-administration of Huc-MSCs effectively restored oocytes maturation to levels comparable with those of healthy controls. Similarly, inhibition of autophagy using CQ produced a rescue effect similar to that of Huc-MSCs treatment. Further analysis of the number of dead cells showed the highest levels of cell death in RAPA-treated PCOS mice, which was significantly reduced following Huc-MSCs administration (Fig. 3B). These findings highlight a functional association between autophagy suppression and improved oocytes viability, highlighting the ability of Huc-MSCs to promote follicular maturation primarily through inhibition of the autophagic pathway.

Huc-MSCs improved the estrous cycle and hormonal profiling in PCOS mice

Monitoring of the estrous cycle revealed complete restoration of normal cyclicity in Huc-MSCs-treated PCOS mice, in contrast to the disrupted and acyclic pattern observed in DHEA-induced PCOS controls (Fig. 4A). These coordinated endocrine and physiological improvements support the conclusion that the therapeutic effects of Huc-MSCs on PCOS-associated hyperandrogenism and reproductive dysfunction are closely linked to suppression of autophagy. Serum hormone analysis demonstrated that Huc-MSCs treatment normalized endocrine parameters in PCOS mice (Fig. 4B). Mice with autophagy activation showed significantly elevated LH/ FSH ratios and increased testosterone levels compared to controls. Inhibition of autophagy using CQ restored these hormone levels to the normal range, mirroring the effects observed in the Huc-MSCs-treated group. RAPA-induced autophagy further aggravated hormonal imbalance in PCOS mice; however, co-treatment with Huc-MSCs effectively attenuated this effect. These findings highlight the importance of autophagy suppression in mediating the endocrine benefits of Huc-MSCs therapy in PCOS.

Serum testosterone levels in PCOS mice showed clear autophagy-dependent variation. Activation of autophagy with RAPA worsened hyperandrogenism, resulting in the highest testosterone levels among all groups (Fig. 4B). Inhibition of autophagy using CQ effectively reduced testosterone concentrations to levels comparable with those of healthy controls. Huc-MSCs treatment similarly normalized androgen levels, producing effects equivalent to those observed with CQ. Consistent trends were also observed in the LH/FSH ratios, further supporting the conclusion that Huc-MSCs-mediated endocrine normalization depends on autophagy suppression. These findings identify autophagy modulation as the primary mechanism by which Huc-MSCs alleviate PCOS-associated hyperandrogenemia.

Huc-MSCs decreased the autophagy in GCs

Quantitative ultrastructural analysis using TEM revealed that Huc-MSCs treatment effectively suppressed autophagy in the GCs of PCOS mice (Figs. 5A, B). In normal control mice, only a few autophagosomes were observed (indicated by red arrows), whereas DHEA-induced PCOS mice showed a significant accumulation of autophagic vacuoles. Administration of Huc-MSCs significantly reduced the number of autophagosomes, restoring them to levels comparable to those in physiological conditions. This reduction was similar to that produced by pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with CQ. ATG5 and Parkin were regulated in an autophagy-dependent manner in KGN cells treated with DHEA (Fig. 5C). Co-treatment with RAPA further enhanced DHEA-induced autophagic activity, resulting in the highest expression levels of LC3 II, and Beclin1. Huc-MSCs treatment effectively counteracted this increase, restoring the expression of these genes to baseline levels (Fig. 5D). Pharmacological inhibition of autophagy using CQ in DHEA-treated cells produced the lowest levels of ATG5, Parkin, LC3 II, and Beclin1, closely resembling the suppressive effects observed with Huc-MSCs therapy (Figs. 5C, D). mTOR is a central negative regulatory kinase of autophagy, and its changes determine autophagic activity. In KGN cells and in DHEA-induced PCOS mice, mTOR expression was significantly downregulated (Figs. 5D, E). Huc-MSCs treatment markedly upregulated mTOR levels (Figs. 5D, E). The autophagy-related markers LC3 II and Beclin1 were highly expressed in RAPA-treated PCOS mice but were significantly reduced following CQ treatment (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, Huc-MSCs therapy suppressed LC3 II and Beclin1 expression in both the DHEA and DHEA+RAPA groups, producing outcomes comparable to direct pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with CQ in DHEA-induced PCOS mice (Fig. 5E). These results provide strong evidence that Huc-MSCs restore ovarian follicular homeostasis in PCOS primarily by inhibiting excessive autophagy in granulosa cells.

Discussion

Emerging evidence highlights autophagy is a complex and context-dependent regulator of folliculogenesis, exerting both beneficial and harmful effects on ovarian physiology [20]. Autophagy plays an important role in follicle formation and maturation, with autophagic activity predominantly observed in medium-sized follicles. Previous studies have shown that defects in oocytes maturation are linked to deficiencies in key autophagy-related proteins, including BECN1 and ATG7 [21]. Increased expression of autophagic markers in rat oocytes suggests that excessive autophagy may contribute to germ cell degradation [22]. Moreover, ATG7 knockout in mice disrupts oocytes development and results in infertility [23]. These findings demonstrate that precisely regulated autophagy is essential for maintaining follicular integrity and normal female reproductive function.

Several studies have demonstrated that excessive autophagy is closely associated with programmed cell death and apoptosis, highlighting its dual role in ovarian physiology [20]. Overactivation of autophagy can trigger the self-destruction of GCs, ultimately impairing ovarian function [24]. In mouse models, elevated autophagic activity has been shown to reduce ovarian reserve [25]. Autophagy is also stimulated by oxidative stress, which may impair oocytes quality and maturation and in turn, promote follicular atresia [26]. The mTOR is a key negative regulator of autophagy and was significantly downregulated. mTOR pathway plays a critical role in controlling GCs autophagy during folliculogenesis and is inversely related to autophagic activity. Inhibition of mTOR increases LC3 II and Beclin1 expression, thereby promoting autophagy [27]. mTOR itself is regulated by the upstream phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT pathway. Suppression of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR axis can lead to extensive GCs apoptosis by reducing oxidative stress [28, 29]. Huc-MSCs treatment significantly upregulated mTOR expression, restoring its inhibitory control over autophagy. In this study, the simultaneous activation of mTOR and reduction of LC3 II and Beclin1 suggest that recovery of ovarian function in PCOS following Huc-MSC treatment may be mechanistically dependent on mTOR-mediated inhibition of autophagy. This finding links metabolic signaling pathways with the restoration of follicular homeostasis. Autophagy appears to influence follicular development primarily by regulating GCs apoptosis through the mTOR signaling pathway.

Follicular arrest at the antral stage (5–10 mm in diameter) in PCOS arises from a pathological interaction between endocrine imbalance and dysregulated autophagy. Elevated LH/FSH ratios and hyperandrogenism synergistically increase oxidative stress, creating a feedforward loop that sustains anovulation [30, 31]. Testosterone has been shown to directly enhance autophagic activity in GCs by upregulating Beclin1 and LC3, contributing to follicular arrest and promoting premature luteinization [32]. Beyond the ovary, excessive autophagic flux also contributes to systemic manifestations of PCOS, including pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance [33–35]. At the same time, paradoxically, it may promote endometrial hyperplasia through complex crosstalk between apoptosis and autophagy. This widespread dysregulation highlights the role of autophagy as both a mediator and an amplifier of PCOS pathophysiology. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting autophagy must be carefully balanced to suppress excessive ovarian autophagy while preserving autophagic homeostasis in metabolic and endometrial tissues.

Our findings identify pathological hyperactivation of autophagy as a central driver of PCOS pathogenesis (Fig. 6). GCs from PCOS mice exhibited significantly elevated expressions of Beclin1 and LC3 II compared to controls, indicating dysregulated autophagy. Experimental induction of autophagy further aggravated follicular degeneration, as evidenced by increased oocytes loss, whereas pharmacological inhibition of autophagy successfully restored oocytes maturation rates to near-physiological levels. Increased autophagic activity was directly associated with endocrine disturbance, as peak serum testosterone levels coincide with maximal autophagic marker expressions. Restoration of normal autophagic activity not only rescued folliculogenesis but also re-established ovulatory function, reflected by normalization of the estrous cycle. These findings position GC autophagy as a key integrator of hyperandrogen and follicular arrest in PCOS. The demonstrated therapeutic value of autophagy modulation highlights its promise for addressing the core reproductive-metabolic dysfunctions of PCOS, by suppressing harmful autophagic overactivation while preserving essential cellular proteostasis.

Huc-MSCs exert a positive regulatory effect on follicular development in PCOS by modulating autophagy

Huc-MSCs represent a promising therapeutic option for PCOS due to their dual capacity for immunomodulation and regulation of cellular proteostasis. Compared with conventional MSCs sources, Huc-MSCs offer improved cost-effectiveness, reduced immunogenicity, and strong paracrine signaling potential [35, 36]. Our study highlights their unique ability to reverse PCOS-associated ovarian dysfunction through activation of the mTOR regulating kinase of autophagy. This mechanism effectively suppresses abnormal autophagy in GCs. By restoring mTOR-mediated regulation of autophagic activity, Huc-MSCs normalize key endocrine parameters, including the LH/FSH ratio and testosterone levels, while promoting oocytes maturation. In doing so, they disrupt the pathological feedback loop between hyperandrogenism and autophagy, providing a targeted and multifaceted strategy for restoring ovarian homeostasis in PCOS.

This autophagy-focused therapeutic mechanism is consistent with the previously established role of Huc-MSCs in preserving ovarian reserve in models of premature ovarian insufficiency. It now extends that benefit to the pathophysiology of PCOS [37, 38]. Activation of the mTOR pathway not only inhibits excessive autophagosome formation driven by Beclin1 and LC3 but also acts synergistically with the anti-inflammatory properties of Huc-MSCs to stabilize the follicular microenvironment [39, 40]. Importantly, the consistent efficacy of Huc-MSCs across various ovarian disorders highlights their function as integrators of metabolic and immune signaling, capable of reprogramming disease-specific molecular pathways.

Conclusion

Our findings identify autophagy modulation as a key underlying mechanism and supports Huc-MSCs transplantation as a targeted therapeutic strategy that simultaneously addressing both reproductive and metabolic abnormalities in PCOS. This shift from symptom-oriented management to pathway-specific intervention represents a promising direction for future PCOS therapy development. Nevertheless, our study has several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, our study did not assess the long-term reproductive capacity of mice after Huc-MSCs treatment, the health status of their offspring, or the persistence of restored ovarian function. Secondly, the specific mechanism by which Huc-MSCs regulate autophagy has not yet been fully elucidated. Lastly, KGN cells are a tumor-derived granulosa-like cell line and may not faithfully model normal granulosa-cell autophagy biology; therefore, our findings should be validated in primary granulosa cells in future studies.
