A Transverse–Sigmoid Sinus Dural Arteriovenous Fistula with Multi-Compartmental Sigmoid Sinus Architecture Involving Both Normal Venous Drainage and Shunt Outflow
Authors: Yusuke Otsu, Shuichi Tanoue, Hideaki Shigematsu, Kohei Miyagi, Sosho Kajiwara, Yukihiko Nakamura, Kimihiko Orito, Kiyohiko Sakata, Takayuki Kawano, Masaru Hirohata, Motohiro Morioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo report a rare transverse–sigmoid sinus (TSS) dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF) with complete internal septation of the sigmoid sinus, enabling anatomical separation of shunt flow, normal cortical venous drainage, and successful transvenous Onyx embolization while preserving normal venous return.Case PresentationA 69-year-old man presented with left-sided pulsatile tinnitus. DSA revealed a TSS dAVF fed by the branches of the ascending pharyngeal, occipital, and middle meningeal arteries. The sigmoid sinus exhibited complete internal compartmentalization; the inner compartment carried a retrograde shunt flow, and the outer compartment maintained antegrade cortical drainage via the lateral tentorial sinus. 3D rotational angiography and fusion imaging clearly delineated the compartments. A 5-Fr SOFIA Select (Terumo Neuro, Tustin, CA, USA) and a GREACH microcatheter (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) were used to access the shunt compartment transvenously. After confirming the absence of internal jugular vein opacification, Onyx 18 (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA) was injected to completely obliterate the shunt without reflux. Postprocedural angiography revealed complete fistula occlusion and preserved cortical venous drainage. The patient’s tinnitus partially resolved, and he was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 1.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of identifying dural sinus compartmentalization in dAVFs. Preoperative imaging enables compartment-specific embolization while preserving physiological venous drainage. Thrombosis-induced septation may underlie this anatomy, emphasizing the need for tailored endovascular strategies.

Introduction

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) at the transverse–sigmoid sinus (TSS) often present with pulsatile tinnitus and exhibit complex venous drainage patterns that influence clinical management and outcomes.1,2) Although the sigmoid sinus may occasionally contain internal septations, these compartments are typically interconnected, allowing shunt flow and physiological drainage to coexist within a single lumen. However, in rare cases such as ours, complete anatomical compartmentalization occurs, with the shunt flow and normal venous drainage fully separated into distinct sinus compartments.3,4) Recognition of this compartmentalization is crucial for preserving normal cortical venous outflow during endovascular treatment.3,5) Preoperative imaging techniques, such as 3D rotational angiography and fusion imaging, are essential for identifying these sinus compartments.4) When the shunt and drainage channels are clearly delineated, selective embolization of the shunt compartment can be achieved while sparing the drainage pathway.1,5)

Here, we report a rare case of a TSS dAVF with complete internal septation of the sigmoid sinus, forming 1 compartment dedicated to pathological shunt flow and the other dedicated to physiological cortical drainage. Transvenous Onyx embolization was successfully performed, obliterating the shunt while preserving normal venous return from the temporal cortex.

Case Presentation

A 69-year-old man with no relevant medical history presented with left-sided pulsatile tinnitus. He was referred to Kurume University School of Medicine after the DSA performed at the previous institution revealed a dAVF at the left TSS. Left external carotid arteriography revealed arteriovenous fistulas fed by branches of the ascending pharyngeal artery (APA), including the hypoglossal and jugular branches, converging into shunted segments within the jugular bulb (Fig. 1A–1C). Shunt drainage did not proceed antegrade into the left internal jugular vein (IJV); instead, it drained into the right TSS and subsequently into the right IJV (Fig. 1A and 1B). These shunted segments coursed through narrow bundled channels within the sigmoid sinus, suggesting the presence of internal compartments rather than merely dilated dural branches. The occipital artery (OA) supplied the shunt via 3 branches: the jugular branch feeding near the jugular bulb (similar to the APA), the mastoid branch entering the transverse–sigmoid (T–S) junction, and the transosseous branch near the torcular. Additional feeders included the middle meningeal artery, which supplied the T–S junction via its petrosquamous branches (Fig. 1A–1C). In the venous phase of the left internal carotid artery angiography (ICAG), the temporobasal and posterior temporal veins drained antegrade into the left TSS via the lateral tentorial sinus (Fig. 1D–1F). Fusion imaging revealed that normal cortical drainage entered the outer compartments of the sigmoid sinus, separate from the shunted compartments (Fig. 2A–2I).

As our treatment strategy, we selected transvenous Onyx embolization because the multi-compartment structure of the sinus could not be fully occluded with coils alone or coil-assisted techniques. A 90-cm 6-Fr FUBUKI Dilator (Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) was introduced via the right femoral artery and positioned in the left common carotid artery. For venous access, an 85-cm 5-Fr Axcelguide (Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced through the right femoral vein into the right IJV. A coaxial system consisting of a 120-cm 3.2-Fr TACTICS Plus (Technocrat Corporation, Aichi, Japan) and a 135-cm 2.6-Fr Carnelian HiF-S (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was used for arterial catheterization to selectively access the left OA and APA. This setup allowed for detailed delineation of the vascular anatomy, aiding accurate mapping and facilitating catheter navigation during transarterial embolization. For the transvenous approach, a 115-cm 5-Fr SOFIA Select (Terumo Neuro, Tustin, CA, USA) was advanced via the Axcelguide into the left sigmoid sinus through the right IJV. A 157-cm 1.7-Fr GREACH microcatheter (Tokai Medical Products) was then navigated into the shunt compartment. Despite the difficulty posed by the internal septations, the microcatheter was successfully positioned in the shunted compartment close to the segment fed by the jugular branch (Fig. 3A and 3B). After confirming the absence of reflux into the hypoglossal and jugular branches of the APA, Onyx 18 (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA) was slowly injected with intermittent pauses. Embolization was performed with care to completely fill the shunted compartment while avoiding migration into the outer compartments that received normal venous drainage from the lateral tentorial sinus. The Onyx cast effectively occluded the bundled shunt compartments (Fig. 3C). Postembolization angiography of the APA confirmed complete obliteration of the shunt (Fig. 3D). Venous-phase ICAG revealed preserved antegrade drainage from the lateral tentorial sinus to the outer compartment of the sigmoid sinus, which was unaffected by the embolic cast (Fig. 3E and 3F). In the final assessment, left external carotid angiography demonstrated a reduction of shunt flow at the TSS compared with the pre-treatment angiograms (Fig. 3G and 3H). Pulsatile tinnitus resolved gradually after treatment, but did not completely resolve. He was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 1 and a plan for staged management, with subsequent embolization of the residual shunts extending from the transverse sinus to the torcular sinus scheduled at a later date.

Discussion

TSS dAVFs often exhibit a multi-compartmental structure within the affected sinus, leading to the formation of shunted venous pouches.4) In this case, cortical venous drainage from the temporal lobe via the lateral tentorial sinus flowed antegrade, whereas the shunt flow within the TSS drained retrograde into the same sinus, demonstrating true bidirectional drainage within separate compartments.3,4) From an embryological perspective, this suggests that developmental septations in the sigmoid sinus can result in complete anatomical partitioning of the shunt and physiological drainage pathways.2) Typically, dural sinuses contain multiple lumens compartmentalized by trabeculae or septa. However, these compartments usually communicate, allowing both shunt flow and normal drainage within a single lumen.6) In our case, complete septation likely occurred following central thrombosis and subsequent fibrosis, resulting in 1 compartment dedicated solely to the shunt and another to cortical drainage. Septations structurally stabilize the dural sinus and prevent its dilation or collapse under hemodynamic stress.7) Conversely, they may alter venous flow patterns, predisposing patients to stenosis, thrombosis, or anomalous hemodynamics that can trigger dAVF formation.8,9) In particular, thrombosis may act as a key initiating factor in the pathogenesis of dAVF by altering the local venous pressure and remodeling the sinus architecture. Several clinical reports have documented de novo dAVF formation following cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, suggesting that thrombus-induced venous hypertension and angiogenic remodeling play crucial roles in fistula development.10,11) In this patient, thrombosis in the central compartment may have caused fibrosis and organization, leading to the formation of a rigid septation that completely divided the sinus.

The arteriovenous shunt likely developed within this isolated central compartment, leading to complete anatomical segregation of the shunt flow and normal cortical venous drainage (Fig. 4A–4C). This unique structure poses challenges in catheter navigation and embolic delivery, because the presence of an internal septum creates physically isolated compartments that resist microcatheter advancement into the shunt pouch. Similar challenges have been documented in cavernous sinus dAVFs with septation, where microcatheter access required careful techniques such as the “turn-back” method or specific trans-septal navigation strategies to reach the shunt compartment.12,13) Advanced imaging techniques such as 3D rotational angiography and fusion imaging are crucial for understanding complex sinus anatomy and precisely identifying the compartments involved in shunt flow and physiological drainage.4,14) These tools facilitate targeted transvenous embolization using Onyx, which allows selective occlusion of the shunt compartment without compromising the normal drainage route.1) Anatomical preservation is particularly important because the loss of cortical venous drainage may lead to venous infarction or hemorrhage. Recent studies have demonstrated that dAVFs with cortical venous reflux are significantly associated with brain parenchymal compromise, including ischemic infarction and hemorrhage, highlighting the critical role of maintaining physiological venous pathways.15) In this case, the preserved outer compartment allowed for safe embolization with favorable clinical outcomes. Although the shunt compartment was completely separated from the normal venous sinus compartment, angiographic findings suggested continuity between the shunt pouch and a surrounding arterial network. Therefore, while the embolized structure itself represented an intrasinus shunt compartment, the possibility of partial retrograde penetration of the embolic material into the adjacent arterial network during transvenous embolization cannot be completely excluded. This anatomical relationship highlights the transitional nature between arterial networks and intrasinus shunt compartments in complex TSS dAVFs.

Conclusion

This unique case of TSS dAVF featured complete internal septation of the sigmoid sinus, creating distinct compartments for shunt flow and normal cortical drainage. Through precise preoperative imaging and compartment-specific transvenous Onyx embolization, shunt obliteration was achieved without compromising the normal venous return. Our findings highlight the clinical importance of recognizing venous sinus compartmentalization, suggesting that thrombosis-driven septation may influence dAVF pathogenesis and support more tailored endovascular strategies based on the individual venous anatomy.
