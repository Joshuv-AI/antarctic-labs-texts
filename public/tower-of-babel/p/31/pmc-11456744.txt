Extra‐Articular Abscess Expansion as a Specific Complication of Septic Arthritis of the Shoulder Joint: Report of Three Cases with Extension of the Infection through the Biceps Groove Producing a Large Abscess
Authors: Jong‐Hun Ji, Darshil Parikh, Byung‐Jun Hwang, Jinyoung Jeong, Seungbae Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeptic arthritis of shoulder is a rare clinical entity as the metaphysis is extracapsular and there is no communication between epiphyseal and metaphyseal vessels. Septic arthritis of the shoulder joint is a diagnostic and surgical emergency because joint destruction develops rapidly and can cause significant morbidity and mortality. Unusual complications of septic arthritis of the shoulder joint may include extra‐articular abscess extension to the upper arm through the biceps groove and osteomyelitis of the greater tuberosity.Case PresentationSeptic arthritis of the shoulder, if left untreated, can lead to complications such as extra‐articular abscess extension and osteomyelitis. Three patients with septic arthritis of the shoulder joint with no clear history of trauma were reported in this study. The initial presentation was pseudoparalysis with upper arm swelling. MRI diagnosed septic arthritis of shoulder joint together with an upper arm abscess. Arthroscopic debridement with through irrigation and open drainage of the extra‐articular abscess extension to the upper arm improved both the shoulder pain and abscess completely. However, if shoulder pain or abnormalities in laboratory findings continue after initial treatment, uncontrolled septic arthritis or secondary osteomyelitis are possibilities that should be concerned. MRI is a useful tool for detecting those atypical complications.ConclusionsRarely, septic arthritis of the shoulder joint can extend to the upper arm through the biceps tendon groove and cause an abscess. Also, acute osteomyelitis of the tuberosity should be considered in patients with long‐standing refractory septic arthritis of the shoulder joint who have continued pain and uncontrolled laboratory findings after initial treatment.Keywords: Abscess; Arm; Arthritis, Infectious; Osteomyelitis; Shoulder Joint

Introduction

Septic arthritis of the shoulder joint is a relatively a rare clinical entity, with an incidence of 2–3/100,000 people. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common causative organism. 1 , 2 Septic arthritis of the shoulder joint is a diagnostic and surgical emergency because joint destruction develops rapidly and can cause significant morbidity and mortality. If septic arthritis of the shoulder joint is not treated adequately, it could lead to secondary osteomyelitis, severe joint destruction, and permanent damage.

Septic arthritis should be considered as a differential diagnosis in all patients with painful swelling and painful limitation of motion. However, septic arthritis of the shoulder joint does not show that typical presentation and instead appears only as abnormal laboratory findings. During treatment, the causative organisms, risk factors, and specific symptoms of the septic shoulder arthritis should be considered. Treatments depends on the patients' symptoms, age, and general health status. Antibiotic treatment or surgical drainage should be considered. 3 , 4 , 5 This can either be achieved surgically by arthroscopy or open arthrotomy.

Septic arthritis of the shoulder joint can have several complications. It can destroy the articular cartilage and cause permanent damage to the joint in that way, or it can cause joint fusion, osteomyelitis, degenerative arthritis, sepsis, pathologic dislocation, and so forth. Regular patient examinations, such as laboratory tests, physical examinations, and radiology, are required.

A long duration of septic arthritis in the shoulder joint can cause extra‐articular abscess extension (upper arm abscess) and very rarely osteomyelitis of the greater tuberosity (GT). MRI is a useful tool for detecting those atypical complications.

The object is an unusual case report of shoulder purulent abscess spreading to the upper arm. We treated three cases of extra‐articular abscess extension (upper arm abscess) caused by septic arthritis of the shoulder joint. Such upper arm abscesses should be considered in cases of uncontrolled septic arthritis in the shoulder joint. Also, acute osteomyelitis could develop in the GT of the humerus. Furthermore, during the management of any shoulder joint infection, refractory shoulder pain and abnormal laboratory findings could indicate uncontrolled septic arthritis. The aim of the study was to report rare cases of shoulder septic arthritis with complications such as extra‐articular abscess expansion or osteomyelitis.

Case Report

This report was approved by an institutional review board (IRB), which waived the requirement for participant informed consent (IRB no. DC24ZASI0050).

Case I

A 49‐year‐old man visited the outpatient department with sudden onset right shoulder pain that developed on his dominant side after excessive hammering but without a specific trauma history. His pain was severely aggravated by shoulder movement. The nature of his pain was dull, and it continued for 1 week and was aggravated 2 days before he came to our hospital. He got two injections in his shoulder every 2 days at a local clinic. However, his pain continued to worsen, the painful limitation of motion increased, and upper arm swelling and local heat and redness developed across his upper extremity. When he was referred to our hospital, the painful swelling in his upper arm had increased. He could not elevate his arm at all. He had no previous medical history of diabetes, hypertension, tuberculosis, or hepatitis. Simple radiologic findings of the shoulder joint showed no specific abnormality.

Contrast‐enhanced MRI showed multiloculated, rim‐enhanced fluid collection in the glenohumeral joint and a large amount of fluid around the bicipital groove. Also, huge cystic lesions (arrow) with surrounding edema were seen in the biceps brachii muscle (Figure 1). The preoperative laboratory findings showed an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)/C‐reactive protein (CRP)of 64 mm/h/18.66 mg/dL. The peripheral white blood cell (WBC) count was 15,100 and segmented neutrophil was 91%. The MRI did not show any high signal intensity of the GT that was compatible with acute osteomyelitis. Informed consent was obtained from the patient before the surgical procedures.

MRI finding of the upper arm abscess extension of the septic arthritis in case I. Huge cystic lesions (arrow) with surrounding edema was seen in the biceps brachii muscle (A). Large fluid collection was found around the bicipital groove (B). After treatment, small‐sized bony edema of the humeral head was found (C). However, his symptoms and upper arm abscess disappeared completely.

Case II

A 41‐year‐old man visited the outpatient department with sudden onset right shoulder pain that developed on his dominant side while doing his job as a farmer without a specific trauma history. Previously, he was able to lift heavy materials on the farm, but his pain was severely aggravated by shoulder movement. The nature of his pain was dull, and it continued for 1 month and was aggravated at night 3 weeks before he came to our hospital. He had not undergone any acupuncture on his shoulder, but three times he had intra‐articular injections on his shoulder at a local clinic. Nonetheless, his shoulder pain and painful limitation of motion increased, and swelling and local heat and redness developed across the upper extremity. He took oral antibiotics at a local clinic for the last 7 days before he was referred to our hospital, and on his presentation to us, his upper arm showed no swelling, no head, and mild tenderness. However, he could not elevate his arm at all. His range of motion was as follows: forward flexion 10°, abduction 10°, external rotation at side 10°, and his internal rotation at the back was at the sacral level. He had no previous medical history of diabetes, hypertension, tuberculosis, or hepatitis. Simple radiologic findings of the shoulder joint showed no specific abnormality. Contrast‐enhanced MRI showed huge cystic lesions with surrounding edema in the biceps brachii muscle, long head, and short head. Also, multiloculated, rim‐enhanced fluid collection was found in the subdeltoid, subcoracoid bursal area, and glenohumeral joint (Figure 2). Subchondral erosion with some bone marrow enhancement in the humeral head tuberosity and a large amount of glenohumeral joint fluid collection were also shown. His preoperative laboratory findings for ESR/CRP were 78 mm/h/5.65 mg/dL. Informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Huge cystic lesions (arrow) with surrounding edema was seen around the bicipital groove and the biceps brachii muscle (B). Multiloculated rim enhanced fluid collection was found in the subdeltoid and subcoracoid bursal area (C).

Case III

A 62 year‐old female patient visited the outpatient department with sudden onset left shoulder pain. She worked as a cleaner in a building, and her shoulder pain developed after a corticosteroid injection in her left shoulder 6 weeks previously. Initially, she refused further investigation for her shoulder pain because of her work. As the pain increased, she went to a local clinic for an ultrasound, and she was diagnosed with inflammation and given antibiotics. However, her pain was aggravated, and painful limitation of motion and swelling increased. Also, a localized increase in temperature and redness were found. She could not elevate her arm at all. This pain was dull and continued for 1 month but was aggravated 2 weeks before she came to the hospital. Her past medical history showed hypertension but not diabetes, tuberculosis, or hepatitis. Plain radiographs showed no specific abnormality. Contrast MRI showed a cystic lesion with surrounding edema in the subscapularis, subdeltoid, subacromial bursitis, and synovitis (Figure 3A). Bicipital tenosynovitis (Figure 3B) and fluid extension into the humeral shaft level (Figure 3C) were also found. The initial ESR/CRP was 68 mm/h /3.55 mg/dL. Also, informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Contrast MRI showed cystic lesion with surrounding edema. Abscess formation in subscapularis, subdeltoid, subacromial bursitis, and synovitis (A). Bicipital tenosynovitis and fluid extension into humeral shaft level were found (B, C).

Surgical Treatment

In all cases, arthroscopic synovectomy and debridement were done in the shoulder joint with copious irrigation and debridement. Severe tenosynovitis change was shown in the anterior, superior, inferior, and posterior shoulder joint capsule (Figure 4). Initially, a conventional posterior viewing portal and anterior working portal were made for a complete synovectomy around the rotator interval and the anterior and inferior capsule. Then, we moved the viewing portal into the anterior portal to perform arthroscopic debridement of the posterior shoulder joint. We found severe infected hyperemic synovitis and bursitis in the subacromial space, so a subacromial bursectomy and debridement were performed. Finally, open drainage and debridement of the abscess extension in the upper arm completely improved the shoulder pain and abscess. All patients received the same surgical procedure.

Arthroscopic findings of the septic shoulder arthritis showed severe inflammation, redness, and thickening of synovial membrane, besides fibrinous deposition in the glenohumeral joint.

Postoperative Management

Postoperatively, clinical symptoms and laboratory findings improved. Two weeks postoperatively, CRP had decreased dramatically. The acute shoulder pain and limited range of motion resolved quickly. Prompt arthroscopic irrigation and synovectomy, combined with open drainage of upper arm abscess improved their symptoms completely. Organisms from the synovial fluid and wound cultures showed methicillin‐sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) in the first and third patients and S. intermedius in the second case. In the first and third cases, cefazolin, flomoxef, and clindamycin were administered. In the second case, bacitracin was administered. Typically, intravenous antibiotics were administered for 2–4 weeks, and oral antibiotics were administered for 2 weeks. These patients were followed up for 2 years after surgery, and fortunately, there was no recurrence.

Follow‐Up

In the first case, 2 weeks postoperatively, CRP had decreased from 18.66 to 4.13. In spite of the arthroscopic irrigation and synovectomy, along with extensive curettage and debridement of the upper arm, the pain was not completely improved. Four weeks postoperatively, a follow‐up MRI showed high signal intensity of the GT, which was suspected to be acute osteomyelitis (Figure 5A). Therefore, a second operation was performed. Curettage and irrigation were done on the GT because of the ongoing osteomyelitis. In the operation field, a proximal humeral bony defect and porotic bony change were found, and extensive granulation tissue and infected bone debris were removed. Cement beads were inserted in the bone defect of the GT after aggressive curettage (Figure 5B). The follow‐up CRP decreased to 0.29 and 0.05 by 2 and 3 weeks, respectively, after the second operation. Seven weeks later, the antibiotic beads were removed, and an allo‐bone chip graft was inserted in the defect of the GT. Clindamycin was added to the cefazolin, and those oral antibiotics were prescribed for another 3 weeks. Three months later, another MRI was taken for follow‐up, and there were no clear findings such as osteomyelitis or GT defect (Figure 6). Two years later, However, a follow‐up X‐ray showed a cortical bone defect of humeral head (Figure 5C), but he had pain‐free shoulder motion. His forward flexion, abduction, external rotation at side, and internal rotation at the back were 165°, 165°, 30°, and T8, respectively (TABLE 1).

In the second case, the same initial surgical procedure was carried out. Two weeks postoperatively, CRP had decreased dramatically from 5.65 to 0.13. Prompt arthroscopic irrigation and synovectomy combined with open drainage of the upper arm abscess improved his symptoms completely. Six months postoperatively, a follow‐up MRI showed complete healing and decreased signal intensity of the GT. The ASES, the University of California, Los Angeles rating scale (UCLA), and simple shoulder test score (SST) scores improved from 36, 7, 1, to 91, 28, and 9, respectively. Two years postoperatively, the patients had no complaint of shoulder pain and had returned to his job.

In case III, arthroscopic synovectomy and open drainage of the upper arm abscess improved her symptoms. The patient was discharged with organism‐specific antibiotics. Her CRP came down from 3.55 to 0.19 in 2 weeks.

Discussion

Septic Arthritis of the Shoulder with Complications

In our series of patients, extra‐articular abscess extension to the upper arm developed after septic arthritis of shoulder joint, along with tuberosity osteomyelitis in one case. Despite 1–2 weeks of intravenous antibiotic treatment, symptoms did not improve, and CRP elevation was found. In these cases of septic arthritis of the shoulder joint, intravenous antibiotics could not improve all the clinical symptoms and laboratory findings. The early detection of secondary complications could prevent poor clinical outcomes after refractory septic arthritis.

Acute septic arthritis of the shoulder joint can extend to the entire upper extremity through the biceps tendon groove, allowing the development of upper arm abscess. Also, secondary osteomyelitis of the GT can occur on rare occasions. In our series, upper arm abscesses and secondary osteomyelitis of the GT (one patient) developed even after the administration of intravenous antibiotics during the early period of acute septic arthritis. Early detection of these complications prevented further damage to the shoulder joint.

Causative Factors of Septic Arthritis of the Shoulder

Septic arthritis of the shoulder joint after an intra‐articular steroid injection is considered a common disease that requires a high index of suspicion. Multiple risk factors are associated with septic arthritis of the shoulder joint. Some patients show an infection after intra‐articular administration of steroids. 6 , 7 Also, patients with septic shoulders are highly associated with medical complications such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic anemia, rheumatoid arthritis, and chronic lung disease. Regardless of treatment, they often have considerable systemic complications such as sepsis, septic shock, or osteomyelitis. Staphylococcus aureus is the most frequently causative organism in such infections. 1 , 2 Osteomyelitis can result from widespread, contaminated open fractures or failed surgical interventions. Early surgical treatment of septic arthritis and the use of appropriate antibiotics provide the best opportunity for joint recovery. 3 , 4 In our series, although the patients used antibiotics at local clinics prior to sampling, we were able to find the causative organism and use antibiotics specific for those organisms.

Managements of Septic Arthritis of the Shoulder

Several treatment options are available for shoulder infections: antibiotic therapy, arthroscopic irrigation and debridement, open synovectomy, and PROSTALAC (prosthesis with antibiotic‐loaded acrylic cement) insertion. Stutz et al. set up a treatment protocol by dividing 78 cases of septic arthritis into four stages and suggested treatment for each stage. 8 Our patients fit into stage III (thickening of the synovial membrane, compartment formation). Even though arthroscopic irrigation and debridement combined with systemic antibiotic therapy were sufficient to cure septic arthritis in 67% of the stage III group, our patients showed the secondary complication of upper arm abscess extension. Stutz et al. reported that the combination of arthroscopic irrigation and systemic antibiotic therapy cured 91% of the affected joints and that the number of arthroscopic procedures and the efficacy of treatment depended on the initial stage of the infection. In our first and third cases, we were able to perform the surgical intervention and intravenous antibiotics earlier than in the second case. Therefore, we could expect better clinical outcomes and no osteomyelitis of the GT bone, which occurred in the case with delayed treatment. Also, in the other two cases, the range of motion of the elbow joint improved dramatically after arthroscopic irrigation of the shoulder joint, open irrigation of the upper arm abscess, and only 1 week of intravenous antibiotics.

In 16 cases of septic arthritis, Parisien et al. obtained excellent outcomes with no recurrence through arthroscopic irrigation and debridement. 5 We also performed through arthroscopic irrigation and debridement, but in one case, acute osteomyelitis on the GT was not cured by a single operation and arthroscopic management alone. Previous studies showed that overall, 70% of patients with shoulder infection were successfully treated with a single operation. 9 , 10 Compared with the open surgical group, the arthroscopic surgery group had higher incidences of perioperative septicemia and urinary tract infection but similar average lengths of stay, hospital charges, and blood transfusion rates and a lower incidence of osteomyelitis. 1

In our three cases, each patient had entire upper arm extension of the abscess that we suspect spread along the biceps tendon sheath and its groove. All three patients had heavy manual labor or active work, and needed to use their arm repeatedly, and we suspect that activity could be one of the aggravating factors in spreading the infection from the septic arthritis of the shoulder joint to the upper arm. The arthroscopic finding for the first patient showed a completely torn biceps tendon. Easy abscess drainage was thus possible through the empty biceps groove. In the second patient, delayed diagnosis and treatment caused osteomyelitis of the GT. It is essential to effectively track the improvementsin laboratory findings (ESR, CRP) and clinical symptoms. Also, MRI scans are the standard for evaluating active and inactive infections and assessing the extent of bone and joint involvement. In our series, neither joint destruction nor infectious changes were observed in the initial plain radiographs. Even in the early phase, septic arthritis could extend to the upper arm through the bicipital groove. Delayed diagnosis and treatment could cause the osteomyelitis of GT or further bony change in the shoulder joint. A follow‐up X‐ray showed an irregular osteolytic lesion of the GT, but that lesion did not progress further in the follow‐up X‐ray in our case.

In managing septic arthritis, an early MRI workup should be done to evaluate subsequent complications, such as upper arm abscess extension or acute osteomyelitis of the GT. Furthermore, those findings should be used in the differential diagnosis of the pseudoparalysis of the shoulder joint.

Conclusion

Rarely, septic arthritis of the shoulder joint can extend to the upper arm through the biceps tendon groove and cause an abscess. Also, acute osteomyelitis of the tuberosity should be considered in patients with long‐standing refractory septic arthritis of the shoulder joint who have continued pain and uncontrolled laboratory findings after initial treatment.
