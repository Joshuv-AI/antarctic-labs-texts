Zinc finger protein 544 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma by generating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment
Authors: Jie Tang, Ming Xu, Xilong Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThis study identified zinc finger protein 544 (ZNF544) as a biomarker in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and aims to delineate its functional role.MethodsThe correlation between ZNF544 expression and clinical parameters of patients with HCC (n = 50) and the malignant biological behavior of HCC cells was analyzed in vitro (n = 5/group) and in vivo (n = 10/group).ResultsZNF544 was elevated in tumors of patients with HCC (p = 0.0006). Patients with HCC exhibiting high ZNF544 expression was associated with a more advanced clinical stage (p = 0.0026) and showed greater infiltration of M2 macrophages (p = 0.0031) and Tregs (p = 0.0094). ZNF544 knockdown inhibited the pro-angiogenic ability of HCC cells (p <0.01). Increased ZNF544 expression accelerated the growth of orthotopic tumors (increased >80%, p <0.0001) and the infiltration of M2 macrophages (increased >60%, p = 0.0008) and Tregs (increased >80%, p = 0.0029) within the orthotopic tumors, and accelerated lung metastasis (number of mice with lung metastasis increased >160%). ZNF544 bound to the spermatogenesis-associated serine-rich protein 2 (SPATS2) promoter and activated its transcription, whereas SPATS2 enhanced the triosephosphate isomerase (TPI1) mRNA stability. ZNF544 promoted glycolysis in HCC cells in a SPATS2-dependent manner. SPATS2 knockdown suppressed glycolysis in HCC cells and inhibited orthotopic tumor growth, which was rescued by TPI1 overexpression. Ivermectin was identified as a pharmacological inhibitor of ZNF544, effectively suppressing the progression of HCC.ConclusionsZNF544 accelerates HCC by activating SPATS2/TPI1 and promoting glycolysis and immune escape. Targeting ZNF544, including compounds such as ivermectin, may offer a preliminary avenue for further investigation in HCC.Impact and implicationsThis research underscores the role of ZNF544-SPATS2 as a pivotal regulator of TPI1 expression in HCC. TPI1 shapes the immunosuppressive TME and accelerates growth and metastasis by promoting glycolysis and facilitating the infiltration of M2 macrophages and Tregs. Our research suggests that ZNF544 could serve as a novel biomarker for HCC, and the pharmacological inhibitor of ZNF544, ivermectin, emerges as a promising therapeutic option for HCC treatment.Keywords: Prognostic markers, RNA-binding protein, Transcription factors, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constitutes ∼90% of liver cancer diagnoses and is predominantly associated with factors such as aflatoxin, HBV, and HCV, as well as metabolic disorders, and patients with HCC typically present with tumors in advanced and incurable stages.1 Therefore, the discovery of biomarkers has become crucial to promising early detection, precise diagnosis, and personalized treatment plans.2 The tumor microenvironment (TME) is essential to the modulation of the response to antitumor therapy in HCC.3 Immunosuppressive cells, including regulatory T cells (Tregs) and tumor-associated macrophages, function as the primary components of the TME and engage in intricate crosstalk with HCC cells, facilitating immune evasion.4 Macrophages are the most abundant innate immune cells in TME.5 Alternatively activated macrophages (M2) secreted insulin-like growth factor-1 and C–C motif ligand 20 to recruit Tregs and impair CD8+ T cell effects in HCC.6 Identifying biomarkers closely related to these immune cells is of paramount importance in guiding immunotherapy and improving prognosis in HCC.

Zinc finger proteins (ZNFs) represent the largest family of transcription factors (TFs) in the human genome and have been implicated in various diseases, particularly several types of cancer, including HCC.7 For one of them, ZNF544 has been correlated with the infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, and macrophages in the HCC microenvironment.8 However, its functional role has not been dissected. Our preliminary bioinformatics prediction showed that ZNF544 was a possible biomarker candidate with correlation with M2 macrophages and Tregs in HCC. The spermatogenesis-associated serine-rich protein 2 (SPATS2, also known as SPATA10) is an RNA-binding protein (RBP) that was upregulated in HCC, and SPATS2 expression demonstrated a positive correlation with immune cell infiltration and the expression of immune-related gene markers in HCC.9 In this study, we identified SPATS2 as a target of ZNF544 and triosephosphate isomerase (TPI1) as a target of SPATS2. As the fifth enzyme in glycolysis, TPI1 is best known for its role in the interconversion of the three-carbon sugars dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and is involved in various metabolic processes, including gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and fatty acid biosynthesis.10,11 Interestingly, a cluster of TPI1+ malignant hepatocytes was related to the suppression of CD8+ T-cell infiltration and HCC metastasis.12 However, its association with M2 macrophages and Treg infiltration in HCC has not been revealed. We thus hypothesized that ZNF544-induced SPATS2 overexpression may contribute to M2 macrophages and Treg infiltration in HCC in a TPI1-dependent manner.

Materials and methods

More details are provided in the Supplementary materials and methods.

Clinical samples

The utilization of patients' tissue specimens was formally endorsed by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pudong New Area People’s Hospital (approval number: 2023-D-15) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all individuals. Fifty clinical samples of patients who underwent radical resection of HCC at Shanghai Pudong New Area People’s Hospital between January 2020 and December 2023 were collected. Tumor tissue specimens that were completely resected during surgery were collected, and paracancerous tissues were excised 2 cm from the border of the cancerous tissue. The excised tissues were confirmed by a pathologist.

Cells and cell culture

HuH-7 (TCHu182), PLC/PRF/5 (TCHu119), and MHCC97-H (SCSP-5092) were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). MHCC97-L (SNL-366) and Mahlavu (SNL-693) were purchased from Sunncell (Wuhan, Hubei, China). All HCC cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. THLE-2 cells (CC–Y1833, EK-Bioscience, Shanghai, China) were cultured in bronchial epithelial growth medium plus 5 ng/ml EGF, 70 ng/ml phosphoethanolamine, and 10% FBS. All cells were free of mycoplasma, bacterial, or fungal infections.

HCC cells were treated with S-adenosylmethionine (SAM, S5109, Selleck, Shanghai, China) at 200 μM,13 ivermectin (S1351, Selleck), or nystatin (S1934, Selleck) at 10 μM14 for 24 h. Cells treated with the solvent DMSO served as controls. Overexpression (OE) lentiviral vectors were purchased from Hanbio (Shanghai, China). ShRNA lentiviral vectors and CRISPR/Cas9 lentiviral vectors were purchased from VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). Specific shRNA sequences and knockout gRNA sequences can be found in Table S1.

Establishment of orthotopic models

All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Pudong New Area People’s Hospital. Male huHSC-C-NKG-ProM mice (6 weeks) were procured from Cyagen (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). MHCC97-H cell suspension (2 × 106) mixed with Matrigel (1:1) was injected into the left lobe of the mouse liver.15

Tregs or macrophages were depleted in mice as previously described.16 After tumor cell inoculation, mice received intraperitoneal injections of CD25-neutralizing antibody (MAB223, Novus Biological) or control IgG (14-4714-82, Thermo Fisher) twice a week (400 μg). Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 μl of clodronate liposomes (40337ES10, Yeasen) or control liposomes (40338ES10, Yeasen) containing only PBS 24 h before cell implantation. The mice were injected with 100 μl of clodronate liposomes or empty liposomes every 5 days. For the ZNF544 inhibitor, mice were gavaged with 10 mg/kg ivermectin daily;17 vehicle-treated mice served as controls. Non-tumor-bearing mice received ivermectin or vehicle via oral gavage for 65 days for toxicity assessment.

For survival analysis (n = 10/group), monitoring ended when mortality reached 90%. Three weeks after tumor cell inoculation, 10 surviving mice per group were euthanized for liver tumor collection. Lung metastases were harvested from randomly selected surviving mice at 3 or 6 weeks.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with p <0.05 considered significant. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Non-parametric comparisons used the Wilcoxon matched-pairs test (paired samples) or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test. Parametric comparisons used unpaired t tests (two groups) or one-/two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post-hoc tests.

Results

ZNF544 is associated with a dismal prognosis in HCC

We used HCC tumor and adjacent normal tissues from the GSE169289 and GSE135631 datasets and analyzed transcriptomic changes in GEO2R. The threshold for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was set as p-adjust <0.01, |Log2FoldChange| >1 (Fig. 1A and B, Tables S2 and S3). DEGs (Table S4) in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-liver HCC (LIHC) project were downloaded from the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) website18 with the same filtering threshold. Prognostic markers (Table S5) originating from the TCGA-LIHC project with p <0.01 were downloaded from UALCAN.19 Lastly, we downloaded the TFs existing in Homo sapiens (Table S6) from AnimalTFDB v4.0.20 Eleven TFs were obtained by cross-screening these five data sets (Fig. 1C). ZNF544 was elevated in HCC samples from the two Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets and in TCGA-LIHC samples from GEPIA (Fig. 1D). As analyzed in UALCAN, high expression of ZNF544 predicted worse overall survival in patients with HCC (Fig. 1E). ZNF544 was positively correlated with the infiltration of M2 macrophages and Tregs in HCC in Sangerbox21 (Fig. 1F).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed increased expression of ZNF544 in collected HCC tumor tissues (Fig. 1G). We performed dual-labeling immunofluorescence of ZNF544 and HepPar1, a marker specific for both hepatocytes and HCC cells, in HCC tumors and adjacent samples. ZNF544 expression was observed in hepatocytes or HCC cells (Fig. 1H). The number of M2 macrophages (CD68+CD206+) and Tregs (CD4+FOXP3+) infiltrated in tumor tissues was significantly increased with high expression of ZNF544 (classified according to the mean of the IHC score of 9.82) (Fig. 1I and J). Moreover, ZNF544 expression was associated with higher AFP-L3, more advanced clinical stages, and poorer tumor differentiation (Table S7).

ZNF544 was also elevated in five HCC cell lines compared with THLE-2 cells (Fig. 1K and L). There was no significant difference in the expression of ZNF544 between HBV-negative HCC cells (HuH-7 and Mahlavu) and HBV-positive HCC cells (PLC/PRF/5 and MHCC97-L). However, the highly metastatic MHCC97-H cells (HBV-positive) showed higher expression of ZNF544 than other HCC cells. The expression of ZNF544 may also be related to the metastasis of HCC cells.

ZNF544 dysregulation is mediated by DNA hypomethylation in HCC cells

Using the Shiny Methylation Analysis Resource Tool,22 we observed reduced methylation levels in the CpG islands within the ZNF544 promoter across HCC tumor samples (Fig. S1A). CpG site methylation and ZNF544 expression showed significant negative correlations (Fig. S1B). The UALCAN database also confirmed a significant reduction in overall promoter methylation of ZNF544 in HCC tumor samples (Fig. S1C). Promoter hypomethylation of ZNF544 was found in HCC cells compared with THLE-2 cells (Fig. S1D). HuH-7 and MHCC97-H were treated with SAM as a methyl donor. SAM increased methylation at the ZNF544 promoter in HCC cells (Fig. S1E) and reduced ZNF544 transcription (Fig. S1F).

ZNF544 knockdown (Fig. 2A) in HCC cells was achieved by shRNA lentiviral infection. CCK8 (Fig. 2B), EdU staining (Fig. 2C), and colony formation assays (Fig. 2D) revealed that ZNF544 silencing was detrimental to HCC cell proliferation. Knockdown of ZNF544 inhibited the motility (Fig. 2E and F) and angiogenesis (Fig. 2G) of HCC cells.

The migration and invasion of HCC cells were examined using wound healing assays (E) and transwell assays (F). (G) The angiogenesis-inducing capacity of HCC cells was examined using tube formation assays. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way (A, C-G) or two-way ANOVA (B) (n = 5). ZNF544 drives the creation of an immunosuppressive TME in HCC

MHCC97-H cells overexpressing ZNF544 were constructed (Fig. 3A) and administered into the livers of huHSC-C-NKG-ProM mice, followed by treatment with the Tregs-neutralizing antibody (anti-human CD25) or the macrophage scavenger (clodronate liposomes) (Fig. 3B). The mice with ZNF544 overexpression had a lower survival rate, reaching a 90% mortality rate by Day 42 post-tumor implantation. Anti-CD25 or clodronate liposomes ameliorated ZNF544-accelerated death in mice (Fig. 3C). Tumor tissues and lung tissues were obtained from mice 3 weeks after tumor cell inoculation. The ZNF544-OE group harvested heavier orthotopic tumors, and anti-CD25 or clodronate liposomes slowed the tumor growth (Fig. 3D). ZNF544-OE enhanced the expression of transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) in orthotopic tumors, whereas anti-CD25 or clodronate liposomes did not affect the expression of ZNF544 but attenuated the TGF-β1 and PCNA expression (Fig. 3E). Infiltration of Tregs and M2 macrophages was increased in orthotopic tumors overexpressing ZNF544, and infiltration of these two types of cells was blocked by anti-CD25 or clodronate liposomes (Fig. 3F and G). Clodronate liposome-mediated macrophage clearance also resulted in a reduction of approximately 25% in Tregs. Reduced infiltration of active CD8+ T cells (CD8a+Granzyme B+) was observed in ZNF544-overexpressing tumors (Fig. 3H). Depletion of Tregs and M2 macrophages increased the number of active CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues (Fig. 3H). Lung metastatic tissue was collected from mice 3 weeks after tumor implantation. ZNF544-OE also promoted lung metastasis in mice, with an augmentation in the number of metastatic nodules in the lungs and the infiltrated area of metastatic tumors. Inhibition of Tregs or M2 macrophages reduced the infiltrated area of tumors in the lungs, whereas the suppressing effects of anti-CD25 or clodronate liposomes on metastatic nodule formation were not significant (Fig. 3I).

(E) ZNF544, TGF-β, and PCNA protein expression in orthotopic tumors (n = 5). Flow cytometry analysis of infiltrating Tregs (F), M2 macrophages (G), and active CD8+ T cells (H) (n = 5). (I) Number of metastatic tumor nodules and infiltration area (n = 10). Statistical significance was assessed using the unpaired t test (A), one-way ANOVA (D-H), and Kruskal–Wallis test (I). SPATS2 is a target of TF ZNF544

Genes that were positively correlated (Pearson’s correlation coefficient >0.6) with ZNF544 expression in HCC tumor tissues were obtained from UALCAN (Table S8). The heatmap in Fig. S2A shows only the top 25 positively correlated genes ranked according to the correlation coefficient. The genes positively associated with ZNF544 expression were cross-screened with DEGs screened in the GEO dataset, DEGs screened in GEPIA, and prognostic markers in UALCAN, yielding two intersecting genes: MSH2 and SPATS2 (Fig. S2B). Knockdown of ZNF544 suppressed the mRNA expression of SPATS2, but not MSH2 (Fig. S2C). Therefore, SPATS2 was selected as a downstream target of ZNF544.

SPATS2 expression was elevated in HCC samples from the GEO datasets (Log2FoldChange = 1.46 in GSE169289, 1.48 in GSE135631), as well as in TCGA-LIHC samples from GEPIA (Fig. S2D). The overexpression of SPATS2 was identified in HCC cells (Fig. S2E and F). As predicted by UALCAN, high expression of SPATS2 predicted worse overall survival in patients with HCC (Fig. S2G). The Sangerbox platform revealed that SPATS2 expression was also positively correlated with the infiltration of M2 macrophages and Tregs in HCC tumor tissues (Fig. S2H). The ZNF544 ChIP-seq sample in the Cistrome Data Browser23 showed that there is a binding possibility between ZNF544 and the upstream promoter of SPATS2 (Fig. S2I). ChIP-qPCR assays confirmed that ZNF544 specifically bound to the SPATS2 promoter fragment (chr12: 49,367,142-49,367,489) and that this binding effect was enhanced in HCC cell lines (Fig. S2J). A luciferase reporter vector driven by the SPATS2 promoter was constructed, and ZNF544 silencing in HCC cells reduced the transcriptional activity of the SPATS2 promoter (Fig. S2K).

ZNF544-accelerated HCC progression is dependent on SPATS2 expression

MHCC97-H cells overexpressing ZNF544 were further infected with shRNAs for SPATS2, and shRNAs for SPATS2 blocked ZNF544-induced SPATS2 transcription (Fig. 4A). These MHCC97-H cells were used to develop an orthotopic tumor model. Mice in the ZNF544-OE + Scramble-shRNA group exhibited a 90% mortality rate by Day 42 post-tumor implantation, whereas mice from SPATS2-knockdown cells demonstrated improved survival outcomes (Fig. 4B). SPATS2 knockdown reduced tumor weight (Fig. 4C) and lung metastasis after 3 weeks of tumor implantation (Fig. 4D). Fig. 4E shows the reduced SPATS2 expression in transplanted tumors. SPATS2 knockdown also suppressed the ZNF544-driven immunosuppressive TME, with a reduction in tumor-infiltrating Tregs and M2 macrophages (Fig. 4F and G).

SPATS2 stabilizes TPI1 mRNA

We predicted mRNAs that have a binding relationship with SPATS2 protein from RNAInter24 and observed an outstanding correlation between TPI1 mRNA and SPATS2 protein (Fig. S3A). TPI1 was also overexpressed in HCC in GEPIA (Fig. S3B), and TPI1 expression was positively correlated with the ZNF544 and SPATS2 expression in HCC (Fig. S3C). Moreover, TPI1 expression was associated with poor prognosis in patients with HCC (Fig. S3D) and was positively correlated with infiltration of M2 macrophages and Tregs in HCC tumor tissues (Fig. S3E).

The upregulation of TPI1 was verified in HCC cells (Fig. S4A and B). SPATS2 protein bound to TPI1 mRNA, and this binding was enhanced in HCC cells (Fig. S4C). HCC cells with ZNF544 knockdown were further infected with SPATS2-OE. Knockdown of ZNF544 attenuated SPATS2 and TPI1 expression, whereas SPATS2-OE enhanced TPI1 expression in the cells (Fig. S4D and E). Knockdown of ZNF544 decreased SPATS2-bound TPI1 mRNA, which was reversed by SPATS2-OE (Fig. S4F). In addition, the knockdown of ZNF544 diminished TPI1 mRNA stability and shortened half-life (t1/2), whereas restoration of SPATS2 enhanced TPI1 mRNA stability (Fig. S4G and H).

ZNF544 mediates HCC glycolysis in a SPATS2/TPI1-dependent manner

As detected by extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Fig. 5A), the knockdown of ZNF544 attenuated glycolysis and glycolytic reserve in HCC cells (Fig. 5B), whereas overexpression of SPATS2 enhanced glycolysis in the HCC cells (Fig. 5A and B).

We constructed cells with SPATS2 knockout (SPATS2-KO) and infected them with ZNF544-OE or TPI1-OE. Successful knockdown of SPATS2 downregulated TPI1 (Fig. 5C). ZNF544-OE failed to enhance TPI1 expression, but TPI1-OE succeeded in rescuing TPI1 expression. ECAR assessment was performed (Fig. 5D). SPATS2 knockdown resulted in the inhibition of cellular glycolysis. Combined TPI1-OE rescued glycolytic activity, whereas ZNF544-OE did not have this effect (Fig. 5E).

ZNF544 knockdown inhibited glucose uptake, which was reversed by SPATS2-OE (Fig. 5F). SPATS2-KO also inhibited cellular glucose uptake, and TPI1-OE but not ZNF544-OE restored cellular glucose uptake (Fig. 5G). ZNF544 knockdown decreased the production of lactic acid, and SPATS2-OE increased the content of lactic acid (Fig. 5H). Similarly, TPI1-OE rescued the reduction in lactic acid production in the presence of SPATS2-KO, whereas ZNF544 failed to function in the HCC cells with SPATS2-KO (Fig. 5I).

ZNF544/SPATS2/TPI1 signaling affects the malignant behavior of HCC cells

SPATS2-KO reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 6A) and mobility (Fig. 6B and C) of HCC cells, which were restored by combined TPI1-OE, and ZNF544-OE failed to enhance cell proliferation and mobility. SPATS2-KO decreased the expression of PCNA, vimentin, and N-cadherin, and TPI1-OE reversed the changes in protein expression induced by SPATS2-KO (Fig. 6D). SPATS2-KO attenuated the vasculogenic capacity of HCC cells, which was also rescued by TPI1-OE but not ZNF544-OE (Fig. 6E). SPATS2-KO hampered the release of the pro-angiogenic factor vascular endothelial cell growth factor A (VEGFA) from HCC cells, and TPI1-activated glycolysis also enhanced VEGFA secretion (Fig. 6F).

ZNF544/SPATS2/TPI1 signaling affects HCC progression

MHCC97-H cells with Scramble-KO, SPATS2-KO, SPATS2-KO + NC-OE, SPATS2-KO + ZNF544-OE, or SPATS2-KO + TPI1-OE were constructed. Survival monitoring of mice demonstrated that mice in the SPATS2-KO group had the best survival rate, whereas mice in the SPATS2-KO + TPI1-OE group had the worst survival rate, reaching a 90% mortality rate by Day 58 of tumor implantation (Fig. 7A). Tumors were collected from mice 3 weeks after tumor implantation. The orthotopic tumors formed by cells with SPATS2-KO were reduced in weight, while the in vivo growth of cells overexpressing TPI1 was accelerated (Fig. 7B). Examination of protein expression in orthotopic tumor tissues confirmed the knockout effect of SPATS2 in tumor tissues, which resulted in attenuated expression of TPI1, PCNA, vimentin, and N-cadherin. However, overexpression of TPI1, but not ZNF544, rescued PCNA, vimentin, and N-cadherin expression within tumor tissues (Fig. 7C). Knockout of SPATS2 decreased lactic acid and VEGFA contents in tumor tissues, which were reversed by overexpression of TPI1 (Fig. 7D). In addition, knockout of SPATS2 inhibited the infiltration of Tregs and M2 macrophages in tumors, whereas TPI1 overexpression reactivated the immunosuppressive TME of HCC (Fig. 7E and F). Six weeks after tumor implantation, lung tissues were collected from mice. SPATS2-KO inhibited lung metastasis of tumor cells, while forced expression of TPI1, but not ZNF544, restored the lung metastasis of tumor cells (Fig. 7G). SPATS2-KO also suppressed the expression of PCNA, vimentin, and N-cadherin in lung metastatic tumor tissues. Forced expression of TPI1, but not ZNF544, restored the expression levels of these proteins in lung metastatic tumor tissues (Fig. 7H).

Virtual screening reveals ivermectin as a potential inhibitor of ZNF544

To explore pharmacologic targeting strategies for ZNF544, we used the molecular structure of the ZNF544 protein (AlphaFold ID: AF-Q6NX49-F1) as the drug receptor. We performed molecular docking with 2,545 FDA-approved drugs from PubChem25 using AutoDock Vina. The top five drugs ranked by Vina score were identified as potential modulators of ZNF544, and the binding mode of the drug ligands to the ZNF544 protein receptor was visualized (Fig. 8A). A search of the PubChem database for drugs corresponding to PubChem CIDs revealed four CIDs for the same drug, ivermectin (CID: 71308642, 3085416, 4330618, 139031854), in isomeric forms, while another CID corresponded to nystatin (CID: 60196358). We treated HCC cells with the highest-scoring ivermectin isomer available (SMILES structure with CID: 4330618) and nystatin, respectively. Ivermectin significantly suppressed ZNF544 protein expression, whereas nystatin had no significant effect on its protein expression (Fig. 8B). Therefore, ivermectin was selected as an inhibitor of ZNF544 for subsequent analysis. Ivermectin treatment effectively suppressed SPATS2/TPI1 mRNA expression in HCC cells (Fig. 8C).

Mice inoculated with MHCC97-H cells without any intervention exhibited a 90% mortality rate by Day 65 post-tumor inoculation, whereas the survival of mice treated with ivermectin was prolonged (Fig. 8D). The weight of tumors harvested from mice in the ivermectin group was reduced 3 weeks after tumor implantation (Fig. 8E), and reduced expression of ZNF544/SPATS2/TPI1 was observed in these tumors (Fig. 8F). Ivermectin reduced the levels of lactic acid and VEGFA in tumor tissues from mice (Fig. 8G). Additionally, ivermectin also reduced the infiltration of M2 macrophages (Fig. 8H) and Tregs (Fig. 8I) in tumor tissues while increasing the infiltration of active CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8J). Moreover, ivermectin reduced both the number and area of metastatic tumors harvested from the lungs of mice 6 weeks after tumor implantation (Fig. 8K). Concurrently, expression of PCNA, N-cadherin, and vimentin was diminished in the metastatic lung tumor tissues (Fig. 8L). Simultaneously, oral administration of ivermectin at the experimental dose for 65 days to tumor-free mice did not cause significant damage to major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys), confirming that this dose of ivermectin exhibited no apparent toxic effects in mice (Fig. S5).

Discussion

Because of its advantages of being non-invasive, low-cost, and widely validated, AFP-L3 is the most commonly utilized biomarker for HCC detection worldwide.26 Here, the correlation between ZNF544 expression and higher AFP-L3 contents, more advanced clinical stages, and poorer tumor differentiation suggests its potential as a biomarker in HCC. Moreover, we have substantiated the role of ZNF544 in four out of the 14 hallmarks of cancers, including avoiding immune destruction, activating invasion and metastasis, inducing or accessing vasculature, and sustaining proliferative signaling.27 The M2 polarized macrophages, characterized by the expression of several receptors including CD206, can induce Tregs, and a high density of CD68+ tumor-associated macrophages in the intratumor region and peritumor region was associated with poor overall survival, presence of vascular invasion, and a higher TNM stage.28 CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ Tregs constitute a subset of the immunomodulatory cell population that can induce immunosuppression by inhibiting the differentiation and cytokine or antibody production of their target cells.29 Pro-tumoral M2-like macrophages and Tregs secrete pro-angiogenic factors, thereby accelerating uncontrolled angiogenesis and promoting vascular immaturity.30 Our findings that the expression of ZNF544 was related to the enrichment of Tregs and M2 macrophages in patients with HCC and angiogenesis in HCC cells constitute the first novelty of this study. Furthermore, the accelerated tumor growth and metastasis induced by ZNF544 overexpression in vivo were slowed by anti-CD25 or clodronate liposomes.

SPATS2 was revealed to be one of the 11 RBPs that were associated with the overall survival of patients with HCC.31 Additionally, SPATS2 silencing hampered the proliferation, invasion, and migration of HCC cells and impeded HCC tumor development in nude mice.32,33 However, the function of SPATS2 in TME remains unclear, particularly in HCC. Here, we first identified ZNF544 as a TF regulating its transcription and revealed its involvement in immunosuppressive TME in HCC. RBPs play an indispensable role in cellular physiology by regulating the posttranscriptional expression of target RNA transcripts.34 We then found TPI1 as a target of SPATS2 and an immunomodulator in HCC. As summarized by Enríquez-Flores et al.,35 TPI1 is a potential target in cancers because of its common post-translational modifications. In human lung adenocarcinoma, TPI1 activity is regulated through a process of phosphorylation at Ser21, catalyzed by a class of enzymes known as salt-inducible kinases, which serves to modulate the flux between the completion of glycolysis and the subsequent production of glycerol lipids.36 Our identification of SPATS2 as a regulator for its mRNA stability will no doubt offer new insights into the modification of this glycolytic enzyme. TPI1 has also been reported to be targeted by natural compounds atractylenolide-1 and daidzin in cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer and HCC.10,37 As for its sole role, TPI1 was highly expressed in breast cancer tissues and cells, and TPI1 promoted breast cancer cell glycolysis, proliferation, and metastasis.38 Lactic acid, the principal metabolic byproduct of glycolysis, is not merely a waste product; rather, it functions as a nutrient.39 Tumor glycolysis also plays a key role in instigating immunosuppressive networks that are critical for cancer cells to escape immune surveillance, which is immune evasion.40 For instance, the production of lactate and the hydrogen ions that are produced during these processes are responsible for the acidification of the extracellular environment, which inhibits the function of immunosuppressive cells, including M2-type macrophages and lymphocytes, to facilitate the survival of cancer cells.41 Therefore, TPI1 dysregulation-mediated glycolysis might be responsible for the compromised immunosuppression by Tregs and M2 macrophages.

Although the findings of this study indicate that ivermectin may be capable of modulating ZNF544 expression and exerting antitumoral effects in the HCC model examined, it is important to note that these results remain preliminary. The evidence supporting the use of ivermectin specifically in HCC is still limited, underscoring the need for caution in interpreting its potential relevance. Consequently, further studies will be essential to clarify whether ivermectin or other ZNF544 targeting strategies hold genuine translational promise for HCC.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the ZNF544-SPATS2 axis induced TPI1 overexpression in HCC. TPI1 shaped the immunosuppressive TME and accelerated growth and metastasis by promoting glycolysis and facilitating the infiltration of M2 macrophages and Tregs. These results suggest that targeting the ZNF544 pathway, for instance, with ivermectin, could provide a valuable direction for future studies investigating HCC progression and metastasis.
