Estimation of Residual Gastric Content After 3 or 4 h Fasting for Breast Milk in Infants: A Pilot Study With Randomization
Authors: Sára Morell, Ahmed Uslu, Ali‐Reza Modiri, Hanna Andersson, Peter Frykholm
Published: 2025

European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (ESAIC) guidelines [1] recommend 3 h fasting for breast milk. These recommendations were mainly based on gastric emptying studies in neonates, suggesting that gastric emptying is likely to be complete by 3 h after ingestion of breast milk [2, 3, 4, 5]. However, there are to date no randomized controlled trials (RCT) comparing the new 3 h to the old 4 h fasting recommendation for breast milk.

The primary aim of this study was to provide data for sample size calculation and evaluate the feasibility of a multicenter RCT comparing 3 and 4 h fasting time for breast milk in infants, using gastric ultrasonography to measure gastric antrum CSA.

This was a randomized controlled, non‐blinded pilot study. Ethical approval was granted by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (number 2022‐01965‐01, date 4 May 2022). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05573165).

Breast‐feeding infants and toddlers, 0 to 24 months old, scheduled for elective surgery or procedures requiring anesthesia or sedation were eligible for inclusion.

The participants were randomized to either 3 or 4 h of preoperative fasting for breast milk (fasted from midnight for all other nutrients). The parents were asked to evaluate the degree of hunger of their child using a numeric rating scale (NRS) scale from 1 (not hungry) to 5 (very hungry).

The child was placed in the right lateral decubitus (RLD) position, either on the OR table or in the parent's lap. A linear probe L25 13–6 MHz was used to visualize the gastric antrum in the sagittal plane, in which the liver, the aorta, and/or the superior mesenteric artery were seen. From the three scans, an average gastric antrum CSA was calculated (CSA = π × D 1 × D 2/4) and from this, the GCV was estimated using two different algorithms: Spencer et al.: (0.035 × CSA (mm2) + 1.52 × age (years)) − 7.8/weight (kg) [6] and Schmitz et al.: GCV (mL kg−1) = (0.0093 × CSA (mm2)−0.96) [7, 8].

An oro‐gastric tube was inserted after induction and any residual gastric content was suctioned while the infant was rotated from the supine to semi‐prone right and left positions.

Statistical analyses were performed using R Studio 2023.12.01. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean (standard deviation), median [interquartile range] or number (percentage) as appropriate. Wilcoxon rank sum exact test was used to compare CSA between groups. p‐values < 0.05 were considered significant. To determine the sample size required for a larger randomized controlled trial, we used CSA divided by randomization group to predict the spread of CSA among infants and used a loop function in R to generate 1000 studies with generated CSA measurements based on our CSA data.

Twenty‐two infants were randomized, but three were excluded in the 3 h group (one due to the administration of an unusually large premedication volume before induction, two due to technical reasons). The median actual fasting time was 180 [45] min in the 3 h group and 240 [30] min in the 4 h group. The participants' degree of hunger was classified as 3.5 [1.0] in the 3 h group and 3.0 [1.9] in the 4 h group.

Gastric antrum CSA was 1.06 cm2 [0.59] in the 3 h group and 1.17 cm2 [0.68] in the 4 h group (Table 1). The difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.9). None of the participants had a CSA > 3.07 cm2 (Figure 1). When gastric antrum CSA was converted into GCV, both Schmitz's and Spencer's algorithms resulted in several negative values for GCV. Using either algorithm, none of the participants had a GCV > 0.5 mL kg−1 (Figure 1). Gastric suctioned volume was recorded in nine infants; the maximum volume was 1.3 mL.

The sample size for an RCT based on GUS antral CSA with a design to show non‐inferiority of three compared to 4 h fasting with a desired power of 80% and an alpha of 0.05 was calculated to be over 1000 participants per group.

In this pilot study we thus found no statistically significant difference in CSA between infants randomized to 3 or 4 h of fasting for breast milk. On average, the cross‐sectional areas were very small and in line with previous studies on breast milk in preterm infants [2, 3]. Among limitations, the study was obviously underpowered to detect a small statistically significant difference, and it was not blinded. Gastric suctioning was only done in 50% of the subjects.

We conclude that an age‐stratified observational study of gastric emptying may be more relevant and feasible than a large multicenter RCT to investigate the impact of breast milk fasting time for the risk of aspiration. Further research is needed to identify an evidence‐based CSA cutoff value for an empty stomach as well as an improved algorithm for estimating gastric content volume in infants.
