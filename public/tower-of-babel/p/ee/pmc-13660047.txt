Community supporters’ experiences of Team Orange, a community-based dementia support program in Japan: a qualitative content analysis
Authors: Kazuki Yokoyama, Suguru Shimokihara, Kiyotaka Shimada, Kosuke Yama, Masayuki Kobayashi, Ryo Miyajima, Machiko Itagaki, Yoshiko Sakamoto, Rie Nagata, Nozomu Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTeam Orange is a Japanese national dementia initiative, established under the government’s National Framework for Promotion of Dementia Policies. It connects people living with dementia (PLWD) and their families with trained community volunteers (“Orange Supporters”) who provide practical daily assistance and foster local support networks. By 2023, the initiative had expanded to nearly 1,900 teams and 33,000 supporters nationwide. Despite this growth, the perceived benefits of these roles among Orange Supporters remain largely unexplored. This study examined the benefits of Team Orange through supporters’ lived experiences in Sapporo, Japan.MethodsA qualitative content analysis was conducted with 16 Orange Supporters recruited via purposive sampling from eight community hubs in Sapporo, Japan. Semi-structured interviews were conducted between December 2024 and March 2025. Data collection and analysis continued until data saturation was reached.ResultsTwo main themes were identified. The first, “Lessons learned in Team Orange by Orange Supporters,” comprised four categories: (1) Respecting the personhood of PLWD and their families, (2) Understanding and supporting diverse needs, (3) Discovering the strengths of PLWD and their families, and (4) Promoting community inclusion through collaboration. The second theme, “Comprehensive benefits of Team Orange identified by Orange Supporters,” included three categories: (1) Increased dementia awareness in daily life, (2) Empowerment through interdependent contribution, and (3) Enhanced emotional well-being from the activities.ConclusionsTeam Orange appears to function as a platform through which supporters develop positive perceptions and practical skills for interacting with PLWD, while experiencing personal empowerment and enhanced emotional well-being. As a culturally embedded, policy-driven model unique to Japan, Team Orange offers transferable insights for the development of dementia-friendly community initiatives internationally, with implications for improving care, promoting social engagement, and reducing social isolation-related health risks among older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07996-5.Keywords: Team Orange, Dementia, Community support, Volunteer, Qualitative research, Japan, Dementia-friendly community

Background

The number of people living with dementia (PLWD) worldwide is projected to increase from an estimated 57 million in 2021 to 153 million by 2050 [1, 2]. In response, the World Health Organization has called for integrated person-centered care (PCC) that respects the rights and dignity of PLWD [3]. Aligning with this global movement, the Japanese government established the “National Framework for Promotion of Dementia Policies” in 2019, which builds upon the earlier “New Orange Plan” [4, 5]. A key initiative of this framework is “Team Orange,” a nationwide program that utilizes trained community volunteers, known as Orange Supporters, to provide practical daily life assistance, monitor well-being, and foster local support networks, ensuring that PLWD can continue living with dignity in their communities [5–8]. By 2023, this initiative had grown to include nearly 1,900 teams and 33,000 Orange Supporters across Japan [8].

Team Orange is organized at the level of community comprehensive support centers (CCSCs) and is coordinated by professional staff based at those centers, known as Orange Coordinators, who recruit and prepare supporters, plan activities, and match supporters to the needs of PLWD and their families [9]. Support for PLWD is provided mainly through two formats. The first is a regular community hub, typically held about once a week at an accessible local venue, where PLWD, families, and supporters gather and where activities are planned around the wishes of PLWD to promote their social participation. The second is individual matching, in which a coordinator pairs supporters with a particular person or family; supporters then provide tailored assistance such as accompanying PLWD on outings (for example, shopping, walks, or visits to cafés and hair salons), home visits for conversation and hobby support, and regular check-ins. Throughout, the Orange Coordinators provide ongoing backing to supporters—offering advice, accompanying them during individual support when needed, and linking the team with related community resources such as dementia cafés and other local services.

While existing literature indicates that dementia training can improve supporters’ knowledge and attitudes [10], and that volunteer-led programs enhance well-being for PLWD and their caregivers [11, 12], research focusing specifically on the Team Orange model remains limited. This model is unique because of its national scale and structured supporter training system [5, 9]. Despite its rapid expansion, the subjective experiences of its core members, particularly regarding the perceived benefits of their roles, remain largely unexplored. Team Orange aligns with the global focus on creating “dementia-friendly communities (DFC),” which Alzheimer’s Disease International [13] defines as a place or culture in which PLWD and their caregivers are empowered, supported, and included in society, understand their rights, and recognize their full potential. Furthermore, recent reviews emphasize that the success of these communities often depends on cultural inclusivity, achieved by leveraging existing social structures to develop tailored initiatives [14]. By highlighting the key benefits of Team Orange based on supporter experiences, program managers can scale the initiative successfully and adapt it for diverse communities.

Therefore, this study aims to qualitatively clarify the benefits of Team Orange based on the lived experiences of Orange Supporters. The findings are expected to inform the management and improvement of Team Orange, contribute to the development of supporter education, and ultimately advance the creation of sustainable DFC.

Methods

Participants

Participants were Orange Supporters actively involved in Team Orange initiatives implemented by CCSCs in Sapporo City, Japan. The inclusion criterion was being a registered Orange Supporter, a status that required the completion of two official training programs. Active involvement was operationally defined as participating in Team Orange activities at least twice a month for three months or more. The initial “Dementia Supporter Training Course” equipped individuals with basic knowledge of dementia and the skills required to offer support. The subsequent “Dementia Supporter Step-Up Course” allowed them to deepen their understanding of specific support methods through activities such as peer discussions. Collectively, this two-tiered training ensured that all participants possessed both foundational knowledge and practical experience in dementia support. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age under 18 years, (2) difficulty understanding the purpose and methods of the study, or (3) inability to communicate in Japanese.

To recruit participants, we collaborated with Orange Coordinators who managed Team Orange at eight CCSCs in Sapporo. At the time of the study (January 2025), Team Orange in Sapporo City comprised 15 community hubs and 228 registered Orange Supporters, eight of these hubs took part in this study. These hubs were comparable in scale, each involving, on average, 3.5 Orange Supporters, 1.4 PLWD, 0.7 family members, and 4.9 other local participants per activity, with no substantial differences in size between sites. Approximately two participants were recruited from each hub to ensure representation across multiple hubs rather than concentrating participants within a limited number of sites. Prior to data collection, a briefing meeting was held with the coordinators and participants as part of a collaborative research project with Sapporo City and the CCSCs, during which the purpose and procedures of the study were explained. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling based on ongoing data analysis to ensure diversity in participant characteristics. Although Team Orange involves PLWD and their families as well, this study focused specifically on Orange Supporters to clarify their experiences. No eligible Orange Supporters refused to participate or withdrew from the study.

Data collection

We conducted semi-structured interviews to explore participants’ experiences with Team Orange. The study was explained to potential participants in advance, and those who agreed to participate were enrolled. The interviewers were four male occupational therapists with clinical experience in psychiatric, geriatric, and community care settings; two held a PhD, one held a master’s degree, and one was a graduate student. They conducted interviews individually in a private space at the Team Orange activity site. To minimize potential bias, interview questions were designed to explore participants’ experiences across multiple levels, from individual support to group and community engagement within Team Orange. We asked the following questions: “What roles have you experienced in your activities as an Orange Supporter?”; “How do you feel about your activities as an Orange Supporter?”; “Through your activities with Team Orange, what changes have occurred within yourself or your team?”; and “What have you been conscious of, or what approaches have you developed to understand the hopes and wishes of PLWD and their families?” At the end of each interview, the interviewer reviewed the content with the participant to ensure that nothing had been omitted. Each interview lasted approximately 20–40 min. Each participant was interviewed once; no repeat interviews were conducted. With the participants’ consent, all interviews were audio recorded for analysis, with the interviewers taking field notes during and after each interview. The data were collected from December 2024 to March 2025.

Data analysis

The researchers transcribed the audio interviews verbatim and analyzed the data using content analysis [15], guided by Berelson’s method [16]. The analytical procedure involved several steps. First, the researchers read the transcripts to identify passages related to the roles, lessons, and benefits experienced by Orange Supporters; although some difficulties were also mentioned, the analysis here centered on the perceived benefits. These passages were then segmented into “meaning units.” Meaning units with similar contents were grouped and assigned descriptive codes. Next, these codes were compared, contrasted, and organized into broader, more abstract categories. These were reported qualitatively rather than as counts or percentages, to preserve the individuality of each account and to avoid equating frequency with importance. To enhance trustworthiness, the second and third authors repeatedly reviewed the entire process, and any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through discussion. Member checking was also conducted by sharing the results with selected participants and incorporating their feedback into the final analysis, ensuring the accuracy of the findings. MAXQDA 24 software (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany) was used to manage and code the data. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines [17], and the completed 32-item checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Category 1. Respecting the personhood of PLWD and their families

This category was generated from three codes of ethical attitude that form the basis of care in this context: accepting the condition of dementia and its associated changes, and respecting the will and pace of each individual as an autonomous person.

Respecting the wishes and position of PLWD and their families

Participants learned that it was necessary in an inclusive society to understand the needs and position of PLWD and their families and to address these needs within the context of Team Orange, as described in the following quotes:

“It is important that participants talk about themselves and respect the individual.” (ID.5).

“I think that there are some sufferings that only PLWD and their family can understand, so I try to understand them and stay by their side as long as I can.” (ID.13).

Normalizing the experience of dementia

Participants learned from their involvement with Team Orange that dementia did not need to be viewed as a special condition, as described in the following quotes:

“We just do what everyone else is doing so that we can live as normal a life as possible, even if we have dementia.” (ID.7).

“A person with dementia can live in a safe and secure environment, which is not a special thing for people from all different types of people.” (ID.12).

Respecting the pace of the person without forcing activities

Participants learned that it was important to respect PLWD and their own pace of activity, as described in the following quotes:

“It is important not to force participation, but to stop or take a break if you get tired of the activity. We also tell them that they can do it later and get it done in time.” (ID.9).

“The first step is to ask the person to spend some time in this area from a short distance away. PLWD will approach you on their own if they are interested.” (ID.10).

These three codes indicated that supporters expressed respect for personhood in concrete, practical ways—honoring the wishes and circumstances of PLWD and their families, allowing people to engage at their own pace, and treating dementia as part of ordinary life rather than as something special.

Category 2. Understanding and supporting diverse needs

This category was generated from two codes related to specialist knowledge and skills to understand the reasons behind PLWD and their families’ words and actions and to ensure smooth communication based on knowledge of the characteristics of dementia.

Clarifying how to better communicate with PLWD and their families

Participants learned that it was important to communicate naturally and respectfully with PLWD as well as with other people in their family, as described in the following quotes:

“It’s okay to talk normally with PLWD. Listening intently and interpreting what PLWD are trying to say will make the person with dementia feel more comfortable.” (ID.6).

“It is important for PLWD and their families to communicate just as much as staff and other supporters do. Avoiding special treatment will help make the environment more comfortable for everyone.” (ID.8).

Understanding the reasons for the difficulties and behaviors of PLWD

Participants learned that when encountering difficulties or challenging behaviors of PLWD, it is important to first seek to understand the reasons from various perspectives, as described in the following quotes:

“When PLWD don’t know what to do, I try to think about why he or she is confused and try to help.” (ID.2).

“I try to understand why PLWD ask for help from their perspective.” (ID.16).

These two codes indicated that supporters sought to understand the reasons behind the words and behaviors of PLWD and to adjust their communication to each individual accordingly.

Category 3. Discovering the strengths of PLWD and their families

This category was generated from two codes related to active and positive involvement that foster motivation and enjoyment in life by discovering the capabilities of individuals rather than focusing on what PLWD and their families cannot do, and by utilizing these abilities and strengths.

Focusing on abilities despite dementia

Participants learned that there were things they could do even if they had dementia by collaborating in activities with PLWD on Team Orange, as described in the following quotes:

“It is important to find things that PLWD can do, and do them together.” (ID.6).

“There are many PLWD who are physically very healthy. Even if there is overlap in what they say, there are many things they can do.” (ID.14).

Motivating people to participate through pleasant experiences

Participants learned that making Team Orange an enjoyable experience increased motivation to participate, both for themselves and for PLWD, as described in the following quotes:

“We both value praise. When you get praised, well, you’re still motivated, right?” (ID.3).

“Participants of Team Orange say they enjoy the activity and are willing to try the next one with us.” (ID.9).

These two codes indicated a focus on strengths and enjoyment, in which supporters recognized the retained abilities of PLWD and used enjoyable experiences to encourage participation.

Category 4. Promoting community inclusion through collaboration

This category was generated from three codes on building an environment and system where not only individuals, families, and professionals, but also local residents and people from various walks of life work together to ensure that PLWD can continue to participate as members of society.

Participating in activities with different people and with common goals

Participants learned the importance of working together with people from different backgrounds and experiences, with a common purpose in the activities of Team Orange, as described in the following quotes:

“People with various circumstances do not have many opportunities to meet other people. That is why I thought it would be wonderful if we could work toward a common goal through exchanges and activities together.” (ID.11).

“Team Orange is a group of people who are all working together. In that respect, the current meeting is very important for the participants.” (ID.15).

Sharing experiences with people in various positions and seeking solutions

Participants learned the importance of consulting and exchanging experiences with others in various positions within the team, including PLWD, their families, fellow supporters, and comprehensive support staff, and of drawing on these exchanges to identify multiple possible solutions, as described in the following quotes:

“I don’t know on my own how men care for the opposite sex, such as how to choose clothes, so I ask others in Team Orange about their previous experiences and learn from them.” (ID.3).

“Each person’s living situation is different, so when we share our opinions—including with PLWD and their family—we can find better ways to provide support.” (ID.10).

Fostering residents’ understanding of PLWD to enable coexistence

The participants learned that it is possible to live together if community members can support each other and gain basic knowledge to understand community PLWD, not only through the activities of Team Orange but also through other activities, as described in the following quotes:

“If people know how to treat PLWD naturally, such as not suddenly calling out to them from behind or pointing out that they say the same thing over and over again, they, their families, and residents can live together.” (ID.1).

“I believe that if someone leads the way in understanding dementia and collaborates in activities with PLWD, we can blend in with everyone.” (ID.4).

These three codes indicated that supporters fostered inclusion at the community level, bringing together people from different backgrounds through joint activity, mutual consultation, and a growing shared understanding.

Category 1. Increased dementia awareness in daily life

This category was generated from two codes pertaining to personal preparation and specific knowledge of dementia.

Fostering personal preparedness for dementia

Through Team Orange activities, participants were able to build knowledge about dementia and social connections that would be useful if they, their family members, or other individuals around them had dementia, as described in the following quotes:

“Think about what happens when I have dementia. I want to be able to do as much of myself as possible and feel comfortable asking for help with things I cannot do myself.” (ID.2).

“We discuss in meetings what we will do when I have dementia. I want to do as much normal life as possible.” (ID.7).

Recognizing the early signs of dementia

Participants were able to suspect and recognize early signs of dementia based on the words and actions of others, as described in the following quotes:

“If someone keeps pressing the wrong house chime or something by mistake, I try to worry about whether this is an early symptom of dementia.” (ID.12).

“The neighbor’s wife told me that someone had stolen her property. We were good friends, and I told her to let me know if there was anything happening.” (ID.16).

These two codes indicated that participants applied their awareness of dementia both to their own future circumstances and to their everyday interactions with others. This included noticing possible early signs of dementia in the community.

Category 2. Empowerment through interdependent contribution

This category was generated from three codes related to individuals supporting and caring for each other through their activities and the sense of achievement and pleasure of feeling useful to oneself that comes from one’s actions having an impact on others or producing concrete results.

Facilitating spontaneous mutual support

Participants developed a natural tendency to support each other, fostering a sense of mutual concern among everyone involved, including PLWD, their family members, and supporters, through Team Orange, as described in the following quotes:

“It was difficult to listen to people at the cafe or volunteer, but now we are able to naturally support each other.” (ID.1).

“It is effective to have friends who can honestly accept and care for each other’s experiences and personalities.” (ID.9).

Applying lived experience to help others

Participants found meaning in utilizing their experiences to assist others, identifying this practice as a core role in Team Orange, as described in the following quotes:

“We listen to each other’s stories and share stories from each side, ‘You are all doing this, I am doing that’.” (ID.8).

“What I do for someone else becomes my knowledge and experience and gives me something to live for. I want to continue this activity.” (ID.15).

Feeling rewarded by building the team

Participants recognized the value of Team Orange in enabling PLWD, their family members, supporters, and staff of CCSCs to work together as a team, as described in the following quotes:

“We have been building up Team Orange in a state of exploration, and although it is not clear what we can do for PLWD, we are all able to work together.” (ID.13).

“We feel like we are proactively working together, so we are not the giver and the receiver, but we are equal friends.” (ID.14).

These three codes indicated that the benefit lay in reciprocity: supporters both gave and received support, drew worth from putting their own experience to use, and felt rewarded by building the team together. Participants described these relationships as collaborative rather than based on fixed roles of helping and being helped.

Category 3. Enhanced emotional well-being from the activities

This category was generated from three codes related to positive emotions and upliftment felt directly from the activity and interactions with others.

Enabling participants to continue activities without psychological strain

Participants were able to participate in Team Orange activities without any psychological burden, which they considered to be the key to their sustained participation, as described in the following quotes:

“There is nothing in particular that I find difficult in my activities as a supporter. The sense of freedom makes it easy to participate.” (ID.12).

“If I start to feel it’s hard, I won’t be able to continue. Now I am able to help without any burden.” (ID.15).

Energizing participants through activities

Participants witnessed themselves becoming more energized and empowered through Team Orange activities, as described in the following quotes:

“Participants enjoyed learning about dementia and playing games, which helped them do it in a positive way, without any bad feelings at all.” (ID.3).

“As the participants become more comfortable, they feel safe and can joke around and work energetically as a team.” (ID.5).

Finding happiness in mutual good health

The participants found that they were in good spirits with each other through Team Orange, as described in the following quotes:

“I’m delighted to talk to you about how well I’ve been this week and that I’ve seen you.” (ID.4).

“When we do activities together in Team Orange, we can see their lively faces. We think it’s great that we can see that.” (ID.6).

These three codes indicated that participants experienced Team Orange as a low-pressure and enjoyable environment. The absence of burden, opportunities for shared enjoyment, and the satisfaction of seeing others doing well were described as sources of positive emotional experiences.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the benefits of Team Orange, a new Japanese initiative aimed at creating a dementia-friendly society, from the perspective of Orange Supporters. The participants were drawn from eight community hubs and varied in their motivations and roles; data were collected and analyzed until saturation was reached, supporting the trustworthiness of the findings [18]. The findings were organized into two themes—the lessons supporters learned in Team Orange and the comprehensive benefits they identified—which we discuss in turn below.

Lessons learned in Team Orange by Orange Supporters

The learning acquired by Orange Supporters through Team Orange activities suggests an enhancement in their awareness and practical skills. Their primary learning was about respecting the personhood of PLWD and their families, which embodies the principles of person-centered care advocated by Kitwood [19]. When practiced by trained professionals, PCC has been shown to reduce the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia and to improve quality of life [20]. In respecting the wishes and position of PLWD and their families, normalizing the experience of dementia, and respecting the pace of the person without forcing activities, supporters prioritized the will and ability of the individual, fostering genuine support for the personhood of PLWD.

Building on this foundation of PCC, supporters worked to understand and support diverse needs. They did so through communication—clarifying how to better communicate with PLWD and their families and understanding the reasons for the difficulties and behaviors of PLWD. This learning extended beyond addressing difficulties to actively discovering the strengths of PLWD and their families, which aligns with the strengths-based approach [21]. By focusing on abilities despite dementia and motivating people to participate through pleasant experiences, supporters applied this approach directly. Strengths-based approaches emphasize the autonomy, assets, and goals of the individual client and can generate self-esteem, self-efficacy, and a sense of hope [22]. As supporters came to understand individual characteristics, their awareness informed more tailored support, in turn deepening both person-centered and strengths-based practice.

These practices extended beyond individual interactions into promoting community inclusion through collaboration. Supporters realized PCC principles across the community through concrete actions, participating in activities with different people and with common goals and sharing experiences with people in various positions and seeking solutions. Through these collaborative processes, participants reached the crucial realization that fostering residents’ understanding of PLWD enables coexistence. Such community-focused efforts can be seen as grassroots practices for building DFC [9], a global initiative to ensure that PLWD live with dignity as integrated members of society.

Notably, while PCC is also a key concept in the training of Orange Supporters, completing basic dementia care programs alone may not be sufficient to generalize it into the comprehensive skillset required for practical dementia care [23]. However, the learning of Orange Supporters within Team Orange exemplifies a process in which the core elements of PCC are organically linked and progressively deepened, suggesting that Team Orange’s continuous, practice-based learning environment may address the challenges of practical application. This learning environment facilitates a continuous cycle of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation, as described in Kolb’s experiential learning theory [24], deepening supporters’ practical understanding of care for PLWD. Through this interrelated learning, Orange Supporters may develop the practical skills needed to support PLWD in living autonomously as respected individuals in the wider community.

Comprehensive benefits of Team Orange identified by Orange Supporters

The comprehensive benefits identified by Orange Supporters comprised three categories: increased dementia awareness in daily life, empowerment through interdependent contribution, and enhanced emotional well-being from the activities.

First, in the area of increased dementia awareness in daily life, a key aspect participants described was fostering personal preparedness for dementia. This suggests that Team Orange functioned not only as a place to acquire dementia literacy, but also as a place where everyone involved in the team regarded dementia as a personal matter and made concrete preparations for the future. The Japanese government is proposing a “new perspective on dementia” aimed at social inclusion, promoting dementia preparedness as a personal matter for each citizen, because dementia can affect any individual within the community [7–9]. Those with experiences of talking with or engaging in activities with PLWD had lower public stigma toward dementia than those without such interaction experiences [25], suggesting that Orange Supporters’ ongoing contact with PLWD may similarly foster familiarity and preparedness. A second benefit was recognizing the early signs of dementia. In addition to studying dementia through the training program, Orange Supporters were able to recognize early signs of the behaviors and difficulties of PLWD through contact with them. Practical and repeated learning enhances the competence and engagement of dementia supporters, increasing their knowledge and self-efficacy [26] as well as their motivation and participation in activities [27]. The fact that regular contact with neighbors has been shown to be associated with milder cognitive decline [28] suggests that Orange Supporters may help notice early changes associated with dementia. This function is particularly crucial, as negative attitudes toward dementia in society or fear of a dementia diagnosis prevents individuals from disclosing their concerns regarding alterations in behavior [29], which often delays the help-seeking behaviors of both PLWD and their family members [30]. By contributing to this “new perspective on dementia” [7, 8], Team Orange appears to play an important role in raising dementia awareness among those involved.

Empowerment through interdependent contribution highlights the powerful roles of reciprocity and peer support. Supporters’ facilitation of spontaneous mutual support and the value they derived from applying lived experience to help others point to a dynamic of mutual empowerment. Shifting from the traditional unidirectional model of volunteering, a reciprocal model of giving and receiving roles among all stakeholders builds a foundation for equity and solidarity [31]. The development of successful befriending and peer support relationships was often facilitated by observations of similarities in characteristics and the shared experience of caring [32]. Given that Team Orange is specifically described as a bridge that connects the needs and support of PLWD and the volunteers themselves—with the aim of community participation and coexistence in society [7, 8]—it can be concluded that the goals of the participants as well as PLWD are mutual. Peer support often enables social interaction in the face of loneliness and social isolation, leading to increased confidence and empowerment among PLWD [33]. Supporters’ sense of feeling rewarded by building the team, together with a developing sense of group belonging, suggests that Team Orange has become a space wherein peer support continues to mature and people collaborate around the shared experience of dementia.

Enhanced emotional well-being from the activities is crucial for sustainability. The social connections reflected in participants finding happiness in mutual good health likely contribute to these positive emotional experiences, fostering a resilient and supportive community. This is intrinsically linked to enabling participants to continue activities without psychological strain. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s stance on maintaining the “voluntary nature” of Team Orange’s activities [7, 8] ensures participants’ autonomy and freedom to choose the type and extent of participation. This sense of self-determination, by eliminating any feeling of obligation, likely secures the psychological safety that allows the activities to genuinely energize participants. In this way, Team Orange functions as a resource for ongoing social engagement. The significance of providing such a resource becomes clearer when the risks associated with a lack of social activity are considered. Recent studies have noted that social frailty, which includes concepts such as the lack of social activity and regular contact with neighbors, increases the risk of disability and mortality [34]. Furthermore, social inactivity and loneliness have been identified as major domains of social frailty associated with cognitive impairment [35]. From a psychological perspective, the perceived level of social activity has also been reported to be most strongly related to major depressive disorder in older adults [36]. Therefore, it is suggested that Team Orange activities, by offering opportunities for social engagement with low psychological strain, may function as a crucial preventive measure against physical, cognitive, and mental health risks. This can also be interpreted as a practical example of “social prescribing” [37], where community-based social connections are utilized to combat isolation and improve well-being. Thus, Team Orange appears to function not merely as a venue for volunteer activities but as a community-based component within integrated care systems for older adults, helping individuals maintain their social and mental health.

Although Team Orange is shaped by Japan’s specific cultural and policy context, several of these findings may inform dementia care in other countries. The reciprocal, peer-based model—in which trained community volunteers both provide and derive benefit from support—resonates with international efforts to build DFC and to mobilize community assets through approaches such as social prescribing [13, 14, 37]. The emphasis on respecting personhood, normalizing dementia, and fostering a sense of belonging reflects person-centered principles that are widely shared across health and social care systems. For settings developing volunteer-based or community-embedded dementia support, the experiences reported here suggest that embedding such programs within existing local structures, and framing volunteering as mutual contribution rather than one-directional service, may help sustain engagement and strengthen community capacity. At the same time, the culturally specific nature of Team Orange means that careful adaptation to local norms, resources, and policy contexts would be essential.

Limitations and future research directions

This study had a few limitations. First, the participants were recruited based on purposive sampling but were limited to Orange Supporters participating in Team Orange in Sapporo, Hokkaido, Japan. The generalizability of the study to Team Orange in other regions must be carefully considered. Given the small sample and single-region setting, the findings are best understood as in-depth, context-specific insights rather than generalizable conclusions, and some interpretations in the Discussion remain tentative. In addition, the present study deliberately focused on the perceived benefits to clarify the value of this new initiative; although participants also spoke about difficulties during the interviews, these challenge-related data were not analyzed here. Because this was an in-depth study of supporters who remain actively involved, the predominantly positive accounts may also reflect a degree of social desirability. A dedicated analysis of the challenges experienced by supporters, PLWD, and families—together with their suggestions for improvement and the perspectives of supporters more directly engaged in assisting family caregivers and PLWD—is an important next step that could inform the management and development of Team Orange. Furthermore, this study aimed to qualitatively analyze the audio data collected in the interviews to generate insights into the experiences of Orange Supporters. Future research should use these insights to develop quantitative indicators to examine the effectiveness of Team Orange.

Conclusions

This qualitative content analysis explored how Orange Supporters experienced Team Orange, a community-based dementia support initiative in Sapporo, Japan, identifying the lessons they learned and the benefits they perceived. The three core areas of benefit identified—increased dementia awareness, empowerment through contribution, and enhanced emotional well-being—are not isolated outcomes but are deeply interconnected: increased awareness may reduce anxiety and foster psychological well-being, which in turn creates a foundation for reciprocal peer support. By consciously fostering this virtuous cycle, Team Orange can function as a platform for experiential learning, a hub for peer support, and a community-based resource for social connection, thereby contributing to the development of a resilient and DFC.
