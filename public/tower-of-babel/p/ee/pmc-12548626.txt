Characterising ocular injuries in competitive combat sports in Texas: a retrospective case–control study
Authors: Uchenna E Akanno, Mishaal Malik, Maya Alik, Reza Ashrafi, Anne Xuan-Lan Nguyen, Albert Y Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to determine the incidence and impact of ocular injuries among the different combat sports disciplines of boxing, mixed martial arts (MMA), kickboxing and Muay Thai in Texas, USA.DesignA case–control study was conducted to analyse retrospective postmatch physical reports from combat sports matches that took place in the state of Texas from January 2019 to January 2022. Ocular injuries and other match characteristics such as sport type and match outcome were identified by postmatch physical reports. Postmatch physical reports were collected from the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation database. Statistical analysis was used to stratify injuries and compare the impact of injuries on match outcome.SettingCombat sports fighters in Texas, USA.Participants3070 participants were included in the study. Participants were fighters who participated in combat sports matches in Texas, USA, between January 2019 and January 2022.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe original plan was to measure the incidence of ocular injuries across different combat sports including boxing, MMA, kickboxing and Muay Thai. However, due to a limited sample size of kickboxing and Muay Thai matches, the ocular injury incidence was only measured for boxing and MMA. The association between ocular injury and match outcome was assessed using χ2 statistical analysis.ResultsThe respective incidence rates of ocular injuries in boxing and MMA were 9.7 and 12.2 injuries per 100 matches. The association between ocular injury and match outcome (win, lose or draw) was statistically significant in boxing but not statistically significant in MMA matches.ConclusionsOur findings revealed that ocular injuries are significantly associated to losing a boxing match (p=0.011), but not associated to match outcome in MMA (p=0.232). Additionally, MMA matches report a larger variety of ocular injuries compared with boxing matches.

Introduction

Combat sports comprise several contact sports where two fighters engage in full one-on-one combat.1 Boxing is one of the oldest sporting traditions as it was invented around 1500 B.C. in present-day Ethiopia.2 Due to its extreme violence, boxing became obsolete around the world for many centuries before experiencing a revival in England in the late 17th century.2 Today, boxing has evolved into a global powerhouse through its immense popularity and establishment of transnational and international governing bodies such as the International Boxing Federation, International Boxing Organization and World Boxing Association to name a few.3 Muay Thai and kickboxing, on the other hand, both have origins in Asia, Thailand and Japan, respectively, but have recently gained traction in the USA after the establishment of US-based organisation International Kickboxing Federation in 1992, which sanctions both combat sports.4 Mixed martial arts (MMA) has become one of the fastest growing sports in the world after gaining legitimacy and increased regulation in North America.5 With MMA becoming fully sanctioned across the USA in 2016 and the formal recognition of MMA in the UK under the Licensing Act 2003 (Amendment Order 2013), which classifies it as regulated entertainment, the sport has gained greater global acceptance.6 7

The rise in approval and popularity of combat sports has translated into increased research into these sports.811 A systematic review of injuries sustained by MMA fighters found facial lacerations and abrasions to be most common.12 A 15-year retrospective cohort study looking at common injuries sustained by amateur and professional kickboxers listed the majority of injuries were to the head (57.8%) with lacerations (70.6%) and fractures (20.6%) as the most common injury types.13 Studies looking at competitive boxers found that 44% of overall boxing injuries pertain to the upper extremities, with 90% of those affecting the hands and fingers.14 Specifically, ‘Boxer’s knuckle’ is the most common injury, characterised by sagittal band metacarpophalangeal joint injuries.15 16

With the mass participation in the different combat sports of boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai and MMA, it is crucial for the world of medicine to understand the physical implications of each of these sports as it relates to injury prevalence and stratification. Knowledge of common injuries in each combat sport is essential for the ringside physician to be prepared to quickly protect and care for each fighter. This knowledge can be used during prefight examination, ringside coverage of the match, and postmatch evaluation and treatment of injuries.2

The mechanism of offensive damage infliction among the combat sports of boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai and MMA is uniquely different across each sport. In boxing, the objective is to land punches on the opponent while avoiding incoming strikes.17 Kickboxing is a martial art in which the use of punching and kicking is permitted.17 Muay Thai is another martial art that is characterised by its use of punches, kicks, elbows and knee strikes.18 Lastly, MMA is a full-contact combat sport that incorporates the use of striking and grappling from multiple disciplines such as boxing, kickboxing, wrestling, Muay Thai, karate, judo and more.19 With the stark difference in offensive modalities between the combat sports disciplines, we expect to see differences in the prevalence of sustained injuries across the different combat sports. MMA injury rate studies estimate that injury incidence ranges from 22.9 to 28.6 per 100 match participations with 66.8%–78% of injuries involving the head or face.20 Similarly, data from the Nevada State Athletic Commission showed that the injury rate for professional boxing was found to be 17.1 per 100 boxer-matches with 14% of injuries involving the eye.21 These past studies, along with others, have focused on reporting injury prevalence in large body regions rather than specific organ systems.13 20 21 Among these injuries, facial and ocular trauma has vaguely been reported.

Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between ocular injury and match outcome in MMA and boxing matches in Nevada, USA.22 23 However, there is no published research examining this relationship in the state of Texas, nor has prior work incorporated kickboxing and Muay Thai into the analysis. Studying the differences between the two states is important because of the variations in athletic commission regulations, demographics and the popularity of combat sports, which may influence injury patterns and outcomes. Combat sports in Texas are regulated by the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR), whereas regulation in Nevada is governed by the Nevada State Athletic Commission. These states have their own guidelines for glove specifications, medical oversight and reporting standards, which affect overall safety, competition and equipment.2325 This study aims to confirm previous findings in the understudied region of Texas, USA, while expanding the scope to include multiple combat sports. Additionally, this study will stratify ocular injuries by type, providing a more detailed understanding of their impact on match outcomes, suspension time and the round in which matches end.

Methods

Postmatch physical reports from all professional and amateur boxing, kickboxing, MMA and Muay Thai matches in the state of Texas from January 2019 to January 2022 were obtained from the TDLR—Combative Sports Division and included in this study. To retrieve the necessary data, a Public Records Request form was submitted to the Public Records office at the TDLR. Data obtained from all four combat sports included: date of the event, type of sport, fight level, sustained injuries, fight outcome (knockout, technical knockout, submission, decision by the judges, draw, disqualification, no decision), ending fight round and suspension time. Knockouts are defined as when the fighter loses consciousness due to a strike. Technical knockouts occur when the fighter is still conscious but unable to defend themselves from strikes. Submission is when a fighter decides to quit fighting mid-match through a hand or verbal signal.24 25

All injuries discussed in this study were diagnosed and managed by attending ringside physicians. Under TDLR guidelines, the assignment of ringside physicians is contingent on holding a full, unrestricted licence to practise medicine within the state of Texas.25 Their responsibilities include performing medical examinations on contestants before the match, remaining ringside during matches, immediately examining contestants who suffer a knockout, concussion, or other head injury, and conducting a postmatch examination. These postmatch examinations produce the physical reports that were analysed in this study. Physicians must report them to TDLR within 1 hour after the sporting event.25 In this study, ocular injuries were classified as any injury to the eye. Since the documentation of injuries was at the discretion of attending ringside physicians rather than official diagnostic protocol, no strict operational injury definition was imposed on this study. Documented ocular injuries included eye laceration, eyelid and eyebrow lacerations, pupil injuries, contusion, haematoma, corneal abrasions and orbital fractures. These injuries were not confirmed through radiographic or supplementary diagnostic testing.

The mean, median, SD and IQR were calculated for all continuous variables. Proportion estimates are reported with 95% CIs calculated using the Wilson score method, due to small sample sizes for kickboxing and Muay Thai sports. To investigate the association between eye injuries and match outcomes (win, lose or draw), a χ2 test of independence was performed, with a null hypothesis stating that there is no association between eye injury presence and game outcome. We would reject our null hypothesis if our calculated χ2 value were greater than the χ2 critical value. To further evaluate the impact of eye injuries on athletic performance for non-normally distributed data, we employed a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The null hypothesis for this test states that the median for the population of differences between the paired data is zero, and the paired data are the presence of an eye injury and ending round for matches. Statistical significance was defined as a p value below 0.05, and p values were not adjusted for multiple analyses. All statistical computations were executed using the R Foundation for Statistical Computing in Vienna, Austria (R Core Team 2022). Graphs and tables were constructed using Microsoft Excel and Word. No other exclusion criteria were included in the study. Data from January 2019 to January 2022 were analysed.

Results

1535 competitive combat sport matches that took place in Texas from January 2019 to January 2022 were evaluated in our study. Of these, 801 matches were boxing, 678 were MMA, 39 were Muay Thai and 17 were kickboxing. Ringside physicians reported 511 injuries, representing 16.6% of all fighters (95% CI 15.4% to 18.0%). The overall incidence rate of injuries in combat sports was 33.3 injuries per 100 matches (95% CI 31.0 to 35.7), with sport-specific rates of 25.7 for boxing (95% CI 22.8 to 28.9) and 43.8 for MMA (95% CI 40.1 to 47.6). Of the 511 injuries, 297 were from MMA (58.1%), 206 from boxing (40.3%), 4 from kickboxing (0.8%) and 4 from Muay Thai (0.8%). 31.7% of the 511 reported injuries were ocular injuries (n=162) compared with 68.3% being non-ocular (n=349). Since our sample sizes for kickboxing and Muay Thai were small, further study conclusions have been limited to boxing and MMA only.

Nearly all the boxing injuries recorded were at the professional level (n=205, 99.5%) compared with the amateur level (n=1, 0.5%). Of the 206 injuries sustained in boxing matches, 37.9% were reported as ocular injury (n=78) compared with 62.1% that were non-ocular injuries (n=128) (table 1). The ocular injury incidence rate in boxing was 9.7 ocular injuries per 100 matches. 13 discrete ocular injury types were recorded in boxing. The most common ocular injury sustained in boxing was eyebrow laceration (n=36, 46.2%), followed by eyelid laceration (n=18, 23.1%), and globe laceration (n=8, 10.4%) (table 2). Orbital fractures accounted for 6.41% (n=5) of the ocular injuries and ocular haematoma accounted for 3.9% (n=3) of the ocular injuries (table 2). Additionally, the majority of ocular injuries sustained impacted fighters’ left eye (n=41, 52.6%).

In the recorded MMA matches, 228 (76.8%) injuries were sustained at the professional level compared with 69 (23.2%) injuries sustained by amateur fighters. Data revealed that 83 of the 297 injuries sustained in MMA matches were ocular injuries (28%), whereas 214 injuries were non-ocular (72.1%). The ocular injury incidence rate in MMA was 12.2 ocular injuries per 100 matches. Three of the most common ocular injuries sustained in MMA matches were eyebrow laceration (n=31, 37.4%), eyelid laceration (n=11, 13.3%) and upper eyelid laceration (n=6, 7.2%) with a total of 25 discrete ocular injury types recorded (table 2). Eyelid laceration likely includes either upper or lower eyelid laceration, and upper eyelid laceration refers to injuries localised to the upper eyelid only. We reached out to the TDLR for clarification but received no response. An orbital fracture was recorded once in MMA (n=1, 1.2%) and eyelid haematoma was recorded 3 times (3.6%) (table 2). The majority of MMA ocular injuries affected fighters’ right eye (n=47, 60.3%).

There were a total of 475 fighters who sustained injuries in boxing and MMA. Of these injured fighters, 151 sustained an ocular injury, 98 (64.9%) lost their match in comparison to 53 (35.1%) who won their match (figure 1). Of the 297 injured MMA fighters, 214 (72.1%) did not suffer an ocular injury and had a mean end-round of 2.98 for their match. The 83 (27.9%) who did suffer an ocular injury had a mean end-round of 3.96. Of the 122 injured boxing fighters surveyed, the 34 (28%) who suffered an ocular injury had an average end round of 5.97 while the 88 (72%) who did not have an average end round of 4.05. For MMA fighters specifically, of the 176 surveyed, 125 (71%) did not suffer an ocular injury and had a mean end round of 2.22, while the 51 (29%) that did sustain an ocular injury had an average end round of 2.63. To assess the impact of ocular injuries on fighter performance, a Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was conducted on the data that did not conform to a normal distribution. We observed a disparity in the ending rounds among fighters who sustained an ocular injury during the match. The results of our analysis revealed that when fighters experienced an ocular injury during both MMA and boxing matches, it resulted in a later ending round.

The 325 (68%) fighters in MMA and boxing who did not suffer an ocular injury had an average suspension time of 58.6 days while the 155 (32%) who suffered an ocular injury had a mean suspension time of 43 days. Of the 193 boxers, 118 (61%) fighters who did not sustain an ocular injury had a mean suspension time of 73 days. The 75 (39%) who did suffer an ocular injury had an average suspension time of 50.8 days. For MMA fighters, 80 (28%) suffered an ocular injury with a mean suspension time of 35.7 days, and the 207 (72%) who did not suffer an ocular injury had an average suspension time of 50.3 days. The results of our analysis revealed that when fighters experienced an ocular injury during boxing and MMA matches, it did not result in a longer suspension time period.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Discussion

While previous studies have identified the prevalence of injuries in competitive combat sports, very few characterise the ocular injuries sustained.11 13 22 23 Through our statistical analysis, we identified key injury patterns in boxing and MMA matches. This investigation revealed novel information about injuries sustained, which allows physicians and sports regulation committees to recognise injuries more efficiently and care for fighters during and after matches.

Our study found that MMA had a higher incidence of overall injuries compared with boxing (43.8 vs 25.7). This confirmed previous research that general injury prevalence is higher in MMA compared with boxing, while expanding the scope to include kickboxing and Muay Thai matches. A consecutive controlled case study from 2000 to 2013 found that MMA overall injury incidence is slightly higher as compared with boxing in Edmonton, Canada.26 Another study confirmed these findings in Nevada, USA.22 Our study further supports this association, demonstrating consistent results in a new geographic setting—Texas, USA—thereby contributing to the growing body of evidence on injury incidence across different regions. This increased incidence of injury in MMA could be attributed to sport playing style and rules. While both are classified as striking combat sports, MMA and boxing differ heavily due to regulations.17 25 27 Whereas boxing is limited to upper extremity and torso strikes only by the opposing fighter’s hands, MMA allows fighters to inflict full-body damage using all four extremities.19 Ultimately, we believe the higher injury prevalence in MMA compared with other combat sports is due to the greater variety of striking mechanisms allowed.

Another notable finding from our analysis is that MMA matches present a greater variety of ocular injuries compared with boxing matches. For boxing, the most common ocular injuries consisted of eyebrow, eyelid and globe lacerations, respectively, with 13 discrete injury types. In comparison, the most common injuries sustained in MMA matches included eyebrow, eyelid and upper eyelid laceration, with a total of 25 discrete injuries recorded. This larger variety of injuries sustained in MMA matches can be partially explained by the different styles of striking offence between both combat sports. MMA rules allow strikes to the ocular region with hands, knees and feet, whereas boxing rules only allow striking to the ocular region with hands.17 25 27 This greater set of striking tools gives MMA fighters more ways to inflict damage on their opponents, which ultimately results in more types of ocular injuries.20 27 28 Furthermore, each sport’s equipment can have a significant impact on the ocular injury type. According to current rules, boxers are required to wear 8–20 ounce enclosed gloves, which can vary in padding thickness, thumb size and materials.25 29 30 This requirement was added to worldwide boxing rules and regulations to mitigate concerns regarding impact forces delivered to a fighter’s opponent.29 In contrast, MMA fighters wear 4–6 ounce fingerless gloves with individual spaces for each digit.31 A 2014 comparative study looking at kinetic data for boxing and MMA gloves found that a traditional MMA glove produced 4–5 times greater peak force compared with a traditional boxing glove.32 Not only did MMA gloves exhibit a load rate five times faster, but they also enabled a more rapid development of mechanical force.32 Ultimately, this study found that material, thickness, stiffness and various other kinetic differences alter injury mechanisms in both sports.32 These factors may contribute to the greater variety of ocular injuries in MMA compared with boxing.

Our findings also support current, limited literature discussing types of facial and ocular injuries in combat sports. A systematic review of epidemiological data on combat sport injuries found that the most common MMA and boxing injuries were facial lacerations.33 An analysis of MMA match data from the Nevada State Athletic Commission from 2001 to 2020 found that eyebrow and eyelid lacerations were the most common orbital injury.23 A prospective, non-comparative consecutive study in Cameroon found that ocular surface lesions, including subconjunctival haemorrhage and lid laceration, account for the most common injury sustained in boxing.34 Ultimately, our study confirms current findings that eyebrow lacerations are the most common ocular injury in both MMA and boxing matches. By demonstrating similar results in the state of Texas, this study reinforces the generalisability of these findings and highlights the need for targeted and consistent medical oversight to address this prevalent injury.

The association between ocular injury and match outcome was statistically significant in boxing but not MMA. This relationship is in accordance with previous research in Nevada, USA for the sport of boxing but not MMA.23 We postulate that our sample size may have contributed to these results. Further research must be done to examine this relationship in MMA, to determine whether the lack of significance is due to insufficient statistical power or underlying differences in injury patterns between the sports or regional commissions. However, boxing’s statistically significant findings seen for boxing could be explained by its striking patterns and regulations.28 30 In boxing, fighters usually only sustain repeated blows to their upper extremities and face, increasing ocular injury likelihood.21 We infer that if a fighter is consistently receiving blows to their face and body, they are more likely to sustain an ocular injury, become weaker through the course of the match and ultimately lose. Additionally, we postulate that if a fighter sustains an ocular injury, their ability to defend or attack may be compromised due to vision impairment.

Lastly, our analysis revealed that fighters experiencing an ocular injury during boxing and MMA matches did not result in a longer suspension time period. These results are consistent with injury patterns sustained in combative sports. In boxing, the most common injuries include hand and wrist fractures and dislocations.14 For MMA, after facial lacerations, the most common injuries include hand and nose injuries.12 Fractures and dislocations, on average, require more healing time when compared with facial and orbital lacerations alone. Additionally, suspension times in combat sports are dictated by match injury severity.25 Considering these factors, the additive effect of ocular injuries in addition to other common injuries could be minimal, as suspension time is dictated by the most traumatic injury sustained.

Limitations

While evaluating our results, there are important limitations to consider. Data from the TDLR database had to be manually extracted from 400 pages of handwritten notes rather than a structured dataset, thus introducing potential inconsistencies. We occasionally encountered illegible handwriting and unclear comments, requiring us to rely on personal interpretation. Additionally, since each match had its own unique ringside physician, different abbreviations and phrases were used for the same injury. This created challenges in recording data, which could have been avoided by using ICD-10 codes instead. It is important to note that not all ocular injuries are immediately apparent postmatch. Because ringside physicians document injuries within only 1 hour of the event’s conclusion, some injuries may go unreported. This study did not adopt a strict operational injury definition due to the lack of diagnostic criteria used by the ringside physicians. Another important limitation is sample size. Our data only include matches from 2019 to 2022, which includes the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, a limited number of matches took place, thus reducing the power of our study. Due to these limitations, we were unable to include kickboxing and Muay Thai in our final analysis.

The absence of regulatory guidance on personal protective equipment may influence the occurrence of ocular injuries in combat sports.35 Typically, protective headgear is worn at the amateur level of combat sports and not at the professional level; however, this varies by jurisdiction. TDLR does not address the use of personal protective equipment such as headgear or eyewear in its combative sports rules and laws, suggesting that headgear decisions are left to individual promoters. This lack of standardisation may have introduced variability in injury incidence and influenced the outcomes observed in our dataset.

Another limitation of the study is the absence of detailed demographic information for the fighters, such as age, sex, weight class or years of experience. These variables could act as confounding factors, potentially influencing both injury risk and reporting patterns. Additionally, the study lacks follow-up data on fighters after the event, which limits the ability to capture delayed-onset injuries or assess the progression and outcomes of reported injuries.

Conclusions

Our study characterised ocular injury prevalence in competitive boxing and MMA matches and reported the most common ocular injuries sustained in each sport. 31.7% (n=162) of all observed injuries sustained in combat sport matches were ocular injuries. 37.9% of boxing match injuries (n=78) and 27.95% of MMA match injuries (n=83) were ocular injuries. The most common ocular injury in both sports was eyebrow laceration with a prevalence of 46.3% in boxing and 37.4% in MMA. We also found that ocular injuries are significantly correlated to losing a boxing match, but not correlated to match outcome in MMA. Additionally, MMA matches report a larger variety of ocular injuries compared with boxing matches. Our study highlights significant trends within these sports and urges combat sports regulation committees to consider these findings when reviewing regulations. This study also confirms prior findings regarding ocular injury prevalence in combat sports and the association between ocular injury and boxing match outcomes. It extends current knowledge in the field of combat sports by demonstrating that these relationships hold true in the state of Texas, under the TDLR regulating body. This further emphasises the broader applicability and robustness of these findings and associations.

To promote athlete safety, we recommend that regulatory bodies adopt the use of ICD-10 codes for more precise and standardised documentation of injuries. This system would improve consistency in injury tracking and follow-up care by allowing for uniform diagnosis and treatment protocols across different matches and events. Furthermore, we advocate for greater consideration of ocular injuries when making decisions to halt a match. For instance, while minor injuries like eyelid lacerations may not require immediate stoppage, more severe injuries such as globe lacerations should prompt an immediate end to the fight. Using our findings, ringside physicians and match referees should have a greater awareness of which types of ocular injuries would occur in the respective combat sport. This understanding will allow them to recognise and address these injuries during and after matches more efficiently.

Given the potential career-altering effects of ocular injuries, it is imperative that combat sports governing bodies reevaluate current regulations, including ringside protocols, protective gear standards and match termination criteria. By addressing these issues, the safety and longevity of athletes can be significantly improved.26 36
