A Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine-based Collision Prediction System with an Angle Optimization Algorithm for Electron Beam Therapy
Authors: Hideharu Miura, Toshiya Okazue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of this study was to develop and validate a Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM)-based collision prediction system for electron beam therapy. This system enables a quantitative pretreatment assessment of mechanical interference between the electron applicator and the patient’s body surface. It also automatically proposes safe alternative beam angle combinations.Materials and Methods:We geometrically approximated a 20 cm × 20 cm electron applicator mounted on a TrueBeam STx linear accelerator as a 190-mm cube. We extracted body and table contours, as well as isocenter coordinates, from the RT Structure Set. For each combination of gantry, table, and collimator angles, the minimum clearance distance between the applicator model and the body contour was calculated. The discretized angle space was visualized using heatmaps of the minimum clearance distance. An optimization algorithm was implemented to calculate a score for each candidate angle set using a weighted sum of angular deviations from the original plan and to identify the best alternative angle configurations.Results:The system was evaluated using an adult female anthropomorphic phantom and four intentionally selected electron regions (superior, inferior, medial, and lateral around the left nipple). For the superior, inferior, and medial regions, the initial planned angles produced geometric intersections. However, the system-suggested alternative angle sets eliminated these intersections in three-dimensional visualization and were confirmed to be collision-free on the linear accelerator.Conclusions:The proposed system requires no additional hardware, integrates with current workflows, and provides a practical tool for collision risk assessment and angle optimization in nearsurface electron therapy.

INTRODUCTION

To ensure patient safety, prevent equipment damage, and maintain geometric accuracy, preventing mechanical collisions between the patient and the treatment delivery system is essential. Collisions can lead to treatment interruptions, hardware failure, and serious adverse events. While safety verification typically involves physical confirmation, pretreatment prediction is highly desirable to streamline clinical workflows. Complex geometries, such as oblique gantry angles or irregular patient positioning, significantly reduce clearance. Furthermore, daily physiological variations and patient motion increase the risk of collision, necessitating systematic and reproducible collision avoidance strategies.

Numerous studies have addressed collision prediction for photon therapy techniques such as stereotactic body radiation therapy and volumetricmodulated arc therapy.[12345678910111213] However, electron beam therapy presents a unique challenge: the electron applicator must be positioned extremely close to the patient surface to minimize air scatter and maintain penumbra quality.[14] This proximity leaves a very narrow safety margin, particularly in regions with curved or tilted anatomy. Despite this high risk, dedicated planning support systems for electron therapy are limited, and existing solutions rarely account for the specific geometric constraints of physical applicators.

In this study, we developed a collision prediction system based on Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine radiotherapy (DICOM-RT) structure sets, specifically for electron beam therapy. This system utilizes patient contours from treatment plans and a geometric model of the applicator. The primary objective is to support safety assessment during the planning stage by identifying potential collisions and automatically proposing safe alternative combinations of angles, table, and collimator angles, thereby enhancing both safety and efficiency in electron treatments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data acquisition and geometric modeling

We developed a collision prediction system for electron beam therapy using Python (version 3.11.11; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and the following libraries: Numpy (version 1.24), Matplotlib (version 3.10.0), and pydicom (version 3.0.1). The overall workflow of the proposed DICOM-based collision prediction and angle optimization system is summarized in Figure 1. The system extracts body contours, table structures, and isocenter coordinates from DICOM-RT Structure Sets and RT plan files. All coordinate transformations and rotations for the gantry, table, and collimator were implemented in accordance with the International Electrotechnical Commission 61217 clinical coordinate system and angle conventions. In this study, the patient body contour was defined using the external “body/external” structure in the RT Structure Set, and the treatment table was represented as a collision object using surface and internal volume structures. These patient-specific contours were treated as rigid surfaces for the purpose of geometric collision assessment.

Workflow of the proposed Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) -based collision prediction system, illustrating DICOMRT import, geometric modeling, collision detection, heatmap generation, angle optimization, and output of recommended angles. DICOM: Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, BEV: Beam’s eye view, 3D: Three dimensional, SSD: Source–surface distance, RT: Radiotherapy

Modeling then focused on a 20 cm × 20 cm electron applicator mounted on a TrueBeam STx linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Physical measurements indicated external dimensions of approximately 190 mm × 190 mm in the horizontal plane. Although the applicator’s actual height is approximately 400 mm, its upper portion remains inside the treatment head assembly and does not directly interfere with the patient. Therefore, to simplify the collision risk assessment, the applicator was geometrically approximated as a rigid 190 mm × 190 mm × 190 mm cube positioned at the nominal source–surface distance (SSD) of 1000 mm. The cube was placed such that its distal face was located 35 mm above the planned skin surface at the beam center, referencing the nominal clinical gap of 30 mm. The applicator dimensions, SSD, and air gap were implemented as adjustable parameters to accommodate different machine specifications.

Collision detection and visualization

Collision detection was performed in two stages. First, the system reconstructed the treatment geometry at the planned angles and calculated the minimum clearance by evaluating the signed shortest distance between the cubic applicator model and the body contour point cloud (RT Structure). These results were visualized via a graphical interface, with potential interference zones highlighted in a beam’s eye view (BEV) to facilitate intuitive clinical assessment.

In the second stage, the system performed a comprehensive clearance analysis across a discretized angle space. Gantry angles were sampled in 5° increments (0°–350°), while table and collimator angles were sampled at clinically relevant intervals (table: 0°, ±30°, ±60°, and ± 90°; collimator: 0–75° in 15° steps). The calculated clearances were visualized as a two-dimensional heatmap. To support clinical decision-making, clearance values were categorized into four risk levels: collision (d ≤ 0 mm), warning (0 < d ≤ 10 mm), caution (10 < d ≤ 20 mm), and safe (d > 20 mm).

Search algorithm for alternative angle combinations

When the initially planned beam configuration resulted in geometric intersection between the applicator model and the patient or table contours, an automated search was performed to identify collision-free alternative angle combinations. For a given reference configuration (G0, T0, and C0) of gantry, table, and collimator angles, the system first constructed a discretized angle space defined as follows: gantry angles G were sampled every 5° from 0° to 355°, table angles T were sampled at clinically used positions (−90°, −60°, −30°, 0°, 30°, 60°, and 90°), and collimator angles C were sampled at 0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 75°. For each triplet (G, T, and C) in this grid, the minimum signed distance d (G, T, C) between the cubic applicator model and the union of the body and table contours was computed, where negative values indicate geometric overlap and positive values indicate clearance.

To ensure a basic safety margin, only candidates satisfying a minimum clearance threshold of d (G, T, C) ≥5 mm were retained for optimization because this threshold was chosen to cover small setup uncertainties and intrafractional motion such as respiration in near-surface electron treatments. For each remaining candidate, the angular deviation from the original plan was evaluated separately for gantry, table, and collimator using a circular distance function.

Δ(a, b) = min(|a−b|, 360°−|a−b|),

where a and b denote two angles expressed in degrees. The deviations for gantry, table, and collimator were denoted as ΔG = Δ(G, G0), ΔT = Δ(T, T0), and ΔC = Δ(C, C0), respectively.

To minimize geometric deviation from the original beam orientation while maintaining sufficient clearance, a scalar cost score S (G, T, C) was defined as a weighted sum of these angular differences:

S (G, T, C) = ωGΔG + ωTΔT + ωCΔC,

with weights ωG = 1.0, ωT = 1.0, and ωC = 0.1. The lower weight assigned to the collimator angle reflects that, compared with changes in gantry or table orientation, modest collimator rotations tend to produce relatively minor changes in electron dose distributions and can often be compensated by adjusting the internal cutout. This weighting therefore allows collimator rotation to be used preferentially to recover clearance. All eligible angle sets were ranked in ascending order of S (G, T, C), and the five lowest-score combinations were reported as recommended alternatives together with their corresponding minimum clearance values.

System validation

The system was validated using an adult female anthropomorphic phantom (ATOM Phantom, Sun Nuclear). Computed tomography (CT) images were acquired (Optima CT580 RT, GE Healthcare) with a slice thickness of 2.5 mm and imported into the Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS) (ver. 16.1.2, Varian Medical Systems). Four treatment sites (superior, inferior, medial, and lateral aspects of the left breast) were selected to represent clinically relevant variations in breast shape and beam incidence.

Initial “collision-risk” plans were deliberately created with the beam axis perpendicular to the surface at an SSD of 1000 mm. For validation, radiation technologists physically reproduced the planned angles and the suggested alternative angles on a TrueBeam STx linear accelerator. Mechanical clearance for the four original configurations was visually assessed to confirm the presence or absence of collision. For the system-suggested alternative angle combinations, additional quantitative verification was performed on the linear accelerator. For each of the recommended noncollision configurations, the minimum clearance between the applicator and the phantom surface was measured at the visually closest point using a ruler (1 mm resolution) and compared with the corresponding predicted minimum distance.

RESULTS

Figure 2 presents the three-dimensional configurations of the electron applicator, phantom surface, and treatment table at the initial planned angles for the four regions evaluated (superior, inferior, medial, and lateral). Geometric intersections were observed between the applicator model and the body contour in the superior, inferior, and medial regions. However, no intersection was found in the lateral region. Physical verification on the linear accelerator confirmed these findings: collision occurred in the superior, inferior, and medial regions, whereas the lateral region remained collision-free. Thus, for the four initially planned regions, the qualitative collision outcome predicted by the system (collision vs. no collision) was in complete agreement with the physical verification on the linear accelerator.

For the superior region, proximity zones emerged due to the clavicle and mandible, whereas gantry angles on the opposite side yielded predominantly large safety margins. In the inferior region, the abdomen contributed to the formation of low-clearance regions, while small table rotations around the neutral position maintained sufficient clearance. In the medial region, proximity risks were primarily caused by the contralateral breast and extended across a wide range of gantry–table combinations, indicating that simple angular adjustments provide limited benefit. In the lateral region, collision risk increased only under conditions of large table rotation, and most sampled angles remained in the safe or caution range. Because local collimator rotation alone provided limited clearance improvement, individual collimator-dependent maps are not shown. An automated search for alternative angles was subsequently performed for the three regions where collisions were predicted: the superior, inferior, and medial regions.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed a collision prediction and visualization system utilizing DICOM-RT structure data and a simplified geometric model, specifically addressing the high risk of collisions in electron beam therapy. Our findings demonstrated that in anatomically challenging regions, including the clavicle, abdomen, and contralateral breast, initial plans often result in mechanical interference. The system effectively visualized these risks through BEV highlighting and clearance heatmaps, providing a robust decision-support tool for treatment planners.

The optimization algorithm successfully balanced the trade-off between collision avoidance and deviation from the original plan. When only small angular adjustments were required, the system identified safe configurations with minimal changes. Conversely, configurations that required large angular deviations, particularly in the medial breast region, served as a critical clinical indicator. Such cases suggest that simple angular adjustments may be insufficient and may necessitate a fundamental reassessment of patient positioning, immobilization, or even consideration of alternative treatment modalities. This ability to quantitatively identify geometrically infeasible or clinically impractical plans at an early stage of the workflow represents a significant advantage of the system. In the phantom experiment, the measured minimum clearances for the system-suggested noncollision configurations exceeded the modeled values by approximately 4–8 mm (mean ≈ 5 mm), indicating that the model behaves conservatively in terms of clearance estimation, largely due to the use of a slightly larger clinical air gap (35 mm) than the nominal 30 mm assumed in the geometric model.

Compared to previous methods, such as sensor-based real-time monitoring or computer-aided design (CAD)-based virtual environments,[151617] our DICOM-based approach offers superior integration into existing workflows. It requires no additional hardware and leverages the RT Structure Set, which is already a standard output of the treatment planning process. This makes the system highly adaptable to patient-specific conditions, including the presence of boluses or specialized immobilization devices. While commercial CAD systems are powerful, they often require manual configuration for new accessories; our system allows for rapid adjustment of parameters such as applicator dimensions and SSD, ensuring high extensibility across various clinical environments.

Despite its benefits, there are still several limitations. First, the cubic approximation of the electron applicator is inherently conservative. The real applicator has rounded edges and a tapered housing, whereas the simplified 190 mm × 190 mm × 190 mm cube assumes sharp rectangular edges and flat faces. Consequently, the model may classify some configurations as collisions even when the actual applicator would retain a small clearance, thus biasing the prediction deliberately toward the safe side. Second, the current model incorporates only limited assumptions regarding setup and mechanical uncertainties and relies on static, CT-derived contours. In practice, residual table or gantry misalignment, daily setup variation, and patient motion may reduce the true clearance compared with the modeled geometry. We addressed these factors only partially by introducing a fixed clearance threshold (e.g., a 5-mm margin), which provides a simple buffer but cannot fully account for all sources of uncertainty, particularly for thoracic and abdominal sites where respiratory motion may influence collision risk.[18] Another limitation is that validation was limited to a single anthropomorphic phantom and four breast regions, which restricts generalizability. Although the system correctly identified the presence or absence of collision for all tested regions and the predicted clearances agreed with measured values within approximately 5 mm on average, the current dataset remains too small to derive robust accuracy, sensitivity, or specificity metrics across diverse clinical scenarios. Future validation should include multiple patients and diverse anatomical sites. Finally, this study focused solely on geometric safety and did not evaluate the dosimetric impact of the suggested angle changes. Because altering beam angles in electron therapy can significantly affect surface dose and penumbra, collision-free configurations identified by the system should be verified dosimetrically in TPS before clinical implementation. In future work, we plan to integrate TPS-based dose evaluation into the optimization.

CONCLUSIONS

The proposed DICOM-based collision prediction system enables reliable pretreatment assessment of mechanical interference in electron beam therapy and provides automated angle optimization. Its seamless integration into existing workflows and validated geometric accuracy make it a valuable decision-support tool for improving patient safety and treatment efficiency. However, further validation with larger datasets, incorporation of motion uncertainties, and dosimetric evaluation are essential to fully establish its clinical applicability.
