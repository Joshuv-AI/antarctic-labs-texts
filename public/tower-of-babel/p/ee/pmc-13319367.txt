Natural wealth or growth trap? The economic effects of resource rents, financial inclusion, and green innovation
Authors: Gamze Destek, Mehmet Akif Destek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNatural resources (NRs) and their role in influencing economic growth have received a lot of concern in recent years. The poor performance by many nations endowed with NRs has spurred discussion on this topic. The present study seeks to investigate the effects of mineral resource rents, financial inclusion, environment-related technological innovations, globalization, and renewable electricity generation on the domestic income in nine OECD (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development) countries between 1990 and 2020. The study enables the coefficients in the equation to differ according to country and time. We apply fixed-effect and random-effect equation models in the study to test the equation. The study finds that mineral resource rents and renewable electricity generation have negative effects on economic growth in the nine countries, while financial inclusion, environment-related technological innovations, and globalization have positive connections with the economic growth of the nine countries.

Introduction

Natural resources (NRs) serve as an underlying stimulator for economic development as they provide the basic ingredients required for industrial production. Most citizens on earth depend on NRs for their livelihood. Resource-rich nations easily collect massive funds from the trade and extraction of their resources. With appropriate management, these resources could be leveraged for better benefits for the worst-off in society. However, in some situations, NRs serve as factors underlying poor economic outcomes—that is, “the resource curse.” The notion is that nations with many NRs grow slower than nations with scarce NRs. However, the endless harvesting of NRs has posed significant economic and ecological hardships for humans on earth. Resource-rich nations give more importance to rent-seeking activities rather than focusing on either high-quality or appropriate use of NRs. Well-designed institutions could show the “curse could be a blessing in disguise.” Increased demand, particularly in developed nations, accentuates the merits as well as demerits associated with using NRs for meeting development objectives.

In recent years, the issue has been focused on the basic problem of resource curses versus resource blessing in terms of abundance. The core problem with resource curses is associated with the problem of understanding why resource abundance generates poor outcomes rather than better outcomes, contrary to the prediction provided by mainstream economic theories. According to Van der Ploeg,1 an increase in resource rents is associated with an appreciation in the value of the currency, reduced production, and gloomy scenarios for economic growth. These negative effects are more pronounced in countries with high volatility and weak governance. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that improvements in living standards and shifts in energy consumption patterns reduce reliance on natural resource income, particularly in economies moving away from resource-based growth models, by promoting economic diversification and resilience.2 Multiple studies have investigated and demonstrated the significance of abundant NRs in promoting economic progress. The question of whether NRs are crucial for economic advancement was the focus of those studies. According to Auty,3 the sluggish economic growth observed in several Latin countries during the 1970s can be attributed primarily to the governments’ focus on gaining people’s support by allocating revenues from natural resource exports, rather than focusing on human quality. Sachs and Warner, in their study,4 showed that NRs are associated with a detrimental effect on economic advancement. It is not quite obvious whether the availability of NRs has an impact solely on economic factors or if it also affects environmental factors. In addition, recent studies have highlighted that resource transitions and climate-related policy changes impact economic outcomes not only through production channels but also by increasing transition-related risks and uncertainties. Climate risk raises the operational and adaptation costs, delays investment decisions, and leads to short-term productivity losses during structural transitions.5 In resource-rich OECD (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development) economies, mineral revenues and green energy transitions can, therefore, impact growth through a risk channel where short-term adaptation frictions coexist with long-term sustainability gains. Namely, climate-related transition risks raise operational uncertainty and adjustment costs, and in resource rent-dependent economies, these risks can amplify the growth-dampening effects of resource rents by delaying structural transformation and reallocating resources inefficiently in the short run.6 Furthermore, the intensity of interest in NRs interacts with pressure and policy responses to shape economic performance, demonstrating how transition constraints and institutional capacity support resource-rich economies in a harmonious way during flexible transformations.7 There are also concerns that these potential risks will be further exacerbated by government intervention. Recent research suggests that government intervention associated with growth-oriented policies shapes institutional responses by encouraging short-term investments and organizational adjustments, which can lead to transition inefficiencies and environmental externalities during periods of structural transformation.8 However, there are also views that large-scale policy implementations are necessary to address these risks. Imran et al.2 argue that natural resource revenues, living standards, and energy consumption shape the environmental and economic consequences together and that resource dependence often reinforces short-term inefficiencies in energy transition processes unless supported by effective governance and diversification policies.

Despite abundance of NRs, the OECD countries face vulnerability challenges, which makes them suitable for studying the “resource curse” phenomena. However, the results of this study can be generalizable to other countries facing comparable vulnerability challenges. Figure 1 presents the trend in mineral rents over the years in selected OECD countries. There is fluctuation in resource rents over the years. Compared with other countries, Sweden and Turkey have shown a fluctuating trend in mineral rents over the years, reflecting the differences in resource endowments and domestic policies toward extraction and trade. These differences are important for studying the relationship between resource rents and economic growth, as rapid increases in resource rents often give rise to economic vulnerabilities, including adverse exchange rate appreciation and a decline in the proportion of other sectors that contribute to national income, commonly referred to as the Dutch disease effect. On the contrary, stable resource rents in Australia, Finland, Greece, and Japan may signal either diversification of the economy or declining reliance on extractive industries. Based on these considerations, the empirical analysis conducted below will concentrate on the following nine OECD countries: Austria, Finland, Greece, Hungary, Japan, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and Turkey. These nations share considerable diversity in the level and change of natural resource rents based on relatively similar institutional settings. As shown in Figure 1, these countries demonstrate considerable diversity in terms of the level and dynamic pattern of mineral resource rents (MNRTs). Financial inclusion (FINC) is the equitable provision of financial services at a reasonable cost and at the appropriate time.9 FINC is an integral part of the financial system and is crucial for attaining economic progress. Countries use FINC as a tool to fight poverty and income inequality. FINC pertains to the capacity of a household to access a variety of financial services.10 FINC strongly shapes individuals’ economic prosperity because it provides access to credit, insurance, and payment services. Strong financial system ensures access of marginalized people to financial services. Ensuring access to financial services for marginalized groups is vital for sustainable development. Therefore, FINC is broadly associated with inclusive economic growth and is viewed as an integral driver for achievement of the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).11 Increased financial access correlates with technological innovations, which is fundamental for economic development and betterment.12 FINC enables citizens to engage in economic activities. It enables the empowerment of marginalized citizens as they would no longer depend on their savings for spending on their education and starting a business to pursue opportunities for improvement. Increased FINC translates to high savings and investments, leading to economic development. This is generally associated with bettered macroeconomic performance. Therefore, a positive relation between FINC and economic prosperity is generally foreseen. Some developed countries continue to evaluate their financial resources dedicated to economic and societal progress every year. There has been an increasing use of information and communication technologies (ICT) instruments in financial services, leading to the prominence of technology in the financial industry. Deposit accounts, loans, insurance, and automatic electronic transfers are only some funds dedicated to FINC. Improved financial systems enable governments to raise funds, providing creative sources for funds.13 Ahamed and Mallick14 state that financial systems play a significant role in nurturing development, improving efficiencies, and providing opportunities for improvement in enhanced financial systems.

This endeavor investigates the role of MNRTs, FINC, the development of environment-related technologies, globalization, and renewable electricity output (REO) in shaping economic performance in 9 OECD countries over the period 1990–2020. These OECD countries are selected due to their significant contributions to global wealth, coupled with their large natural resource reserves. Moreover, these countries have diverse socioeconomic landscapes and varying degrees of reliance on mineral resources. These OECD countries have attracted substantial consideration due to their rapid economic growth. This study adds to the resource-growth debate in several ways. While earlier research has focused largely on developing or resource-dependent countries, we turn to OECD economies, where strong institutions, advanced technologies, and diversified production structures may alter the way resource rents influence growth. We also broaden the scope of analysis by examining resource rents, alongside FINC, globalization, renewable electricity, and environment-related technology factors that have rarely been studied together in this context. By doing so, we highlight how modern drivers of growth interact with resource dependence in advanced economies. In addition, the study addresses cross-country differences within the OECD by allowing for heterogeneity in effects, showing that the growth impact of resource rents and related factors is not uniform across nations. These contributions provide a clearer picture of how resources and complementary forces shape economic outcomes in developed settings and offer policy insights that go beyond the traditional resource curse framework. Moreover, it distinguishes clearly between short-term adjustment frictions and long-term structural dynamics, thus giving a more nuanced analysis on the effects of resource dependence and transition on growth. Finally, because it focuses on a heterogeneous set of OECD members at differing stages of resource income and transition, it identifies effects on growth that can emerge from a given institutional setup. Based on above explanations, the main research question of this study is as follows: how do natural resource rents and the energy transition jointly affect economic growth in OECD countries, particularly in terms of short-run adjustment frictions and long-run structural dynamics?

Research gap

Extensive studies exist on the determinants of economic growth. Studies by Romer (1990),15 Hall and Jones,16 and Romer (1986)17 are among the numerous studies performed to gather knowledge on the determinants of economic growth. Furthermore, some studies have examined and pondered the importance of offering a plentiful supply of NRs to foster economic expansion. These investigations were carried out to ascertain the necessity of NRs for economic progress. The body of studies on growth fueled by NRs can be divided into two distinct areas. Several studies18,19,20,21,22,23 have consistently found that NRs are detrimental. Using data of eleven African states, Nhabinde and Heshmati20 found evidence of the resource curse phenomenon. Using the data of fourteen oil-abundant nations, Saeed22 found evidence of resource curse phenomena. Pérez and Claveria23 explained the negative linkage between natural resource rents and development in Africa. They assumed that there could possibly be a high level of corruption, which might hinder economic development progress. The resource curse literature seeks to explain reasons for the weakness in the ability of resource-rich nations to adequately exploit their resource advantage for economic development compared with resource-lean nations. Pérez et al. argue that resources tend to hinder economic growth, rather than conferring the ability to enhance its progress. The problem that hinders progress may be attributed to unpredictable rent, civil conflict, and Dutch illness.

The second group of research, conducted by24,25,26,27,28,29, has shown that NRs are valuable assets. Kwakwa et al.25 found that the rents generated from NRs affect GDP growth. Using the same model, Altaee et al.28 found evidence that supports the resource curse theory. The presence of an excessive number of NRs negatively affects economic development. Nasir and Redmond27 reported that the presence of NRs improved growth in thirty countries. Aljarallah and Angus29 found that resource rents had a limited impact on development in Gulf countries.

However, Lederman and Maloney30 were doubtful about how NRs impact the process of development. They claimed that several of the indicators used in the previous analysis may not be related to the specific NRs available. Upon considering the fixed variables in their research, the detrimental impact of resources vanished, indicating that it is not the specific proxy they used that influences the conclusion. Instead, it is the connection between the proxy for NRs and certain unobserved national characteristics that affect the outcome. The present study surpasses previous studies by allowing the coefficients of the model to vary across different nations and time periods. The present study is more thorough than earlier studies because the sample countries possess varied traits. Our methodology allows us to consider distinct characteristics and diverse elements of the countries that remain constant over time. This is achievable due to our utilization of a systematic approach.

The potential role of FINC in shaping economic growth has been the subject of great intertest for researchers in the last few decades.31,32,33,34,35,36,37 There is ample evidence supporting the positive contribution of FINC to the economic growth. The central idea is that FINC increases consumers’ ability to access a wide range of financial services, including credits and insurance, thus influencing an increase in household income. According to the evidence provided by Levine,38 it is apparent that even if other sources influencing economic growth are controlled for, economies with more banks and stock markets have the potential for faster growth in the next decades. The paramount aspect is that the universal availability of financial sources in society’s different fields is indispensable and cannot be overemphasized.38 Another aspect where FINC is pertinent is in relation to the concept of poverty alleviation. Developed financial systems, which remain freely accessible to the entire populace with no costs, can potentially reduce the costs linked to acquiring information and carrying out operations.9 Furthermore, such systems also have the latent potential to influence the savings rates, investment decisions, use of technical innovation, and contribution to economic growth.9

The link between NRs and economic growth has attracted much attention since the seminal research by Sachs and Warner.39 There is divergent thought on the link between NRs and economic output. There is evidence in the literature for the resource curse phenomenon, which asserts an inverse link between resource rents and economic growth. Some studies have supported the phenomenon of resource blessing. Our research tests the link between MNRTs and economic growth for specific member countries in the OECD coalition. Additionally, it tests the role of FINC, environmental technology, renewable electricity generation, and globalization on economic growth. This research contributes to the existing body of knowledge on the link between mineral resources and output in the following ways: first, it is different from existing research in that it explores three key areas. At the outset, it should be noted that our research would concentrate on nine member countries within the OECD with high levels of prosperity. As far as we have discerned, no existing research has investigated the subject of resource rents and economic growth in these nine member countries, in particular within the OECD. There have been investigations on the subject for specific nations, however. Specifically, these countries are major exporters of NRs themselves. Additionally, the problem of heterogeneity could potentially emerge because the research sample could be defined by an assortment of different attributes or qualities. There is a diversified set of unique factors in the group of countries. We propose an extension to existing research in that we allow coefficients in the equation to change over time for different countries. Thus, in this case, the use of conventional models in econometrics would result in incorrect inferences. By following a research method, it becomes possible to recognize specific factors in different countries that remain unchanged over time. The research goal is to identify the equation between the expansion of resources and economic development with respect to differences among countries. The research explores the relation between mineral resources and economic development in the context of globalization, technology innovation in relation to the environment, FINC, and technical innovation in relation to the environment.

Theoretical background

This section provides an intuitive theoretical framework that channelizes the effects of mineral resources and FINC, along with other important economic variables. MNRT is the core variable of this study; hence, this study utilizes the resource curse theory as theoretical background. This theory argues that most countries with abundant resources underperform other countries because the rapid inflow of rents from NRs leads to the devaluation of currency and makes other sectors less competitive in the global market, which, in turn, crowds out other sectors such as manufacturing and agriculture. According to Auty,40 resource-dependent countries often face economic vulnerabilities because the processes of extraction and use of NRs cause adverse trade shocks, including adverse exchange rate appreciation and a decline in the proportion of other sectors that contribute to national income. Moreover, resource-abundant countries may face political instability, income inequality, and rent seeking. All these activities have unfavorable economic and social consequences. Moreover, the excessive utilization of NRs often gives rise to adverse environmental externalities, which are negatively associated with economic wellbeing. Hence, the extraction of NRs impedes the attainment of growth targets in the long run. However, the efficient management and utilization and resource rents can reduce poverty and promote long term development. Hence, we expect MNRTs to have a negative impact on economic growth.

This study also uses FINC in the growth model. As a theoretical framework, we use access to finance theory, which states that an increase in access to financial products and services enables marginalized segment of the society to participate in economic activities and, hence, is important for national income. Moreover, an increase in FINC enhances the accessibility of various financial products, such as credit and insurance, to consumers, hence leading to an increase in household income, which improves output.41 FINC, an integral part of the financial system, is generally linked to high inclusive economic growth. Consequently, increased FINC has the capacity to foster more comprehensive economic growth. Hence, we expect that FINC will have a positive impact on economic growth.

Following the study by Romer,42 this endeavor also uses globalization in the growth model. Globalization is vital for increasing the production of goods and services, which can promote economic growth. An increased degree of globalization enables countries to produce and export more goods and services, which leads to more economic growth. Hence, we expect a positive impact of globalization on economic growth. We further include environmentally related technologies in the growth model. Environmentally related technologies are considered crucial for both economic growth and environmental quality. Hence, environmental-related technological innovation (ERT) is linked to sustainable development. Countries with high levels of ERT are expected to grow faster than other countries. An increase in REO implies that more renewable sources are used for electricity output.43 Renewable energy sources are vital for addressing environmental concerns at the cost of low economic growth.44 Moreover, an increase in REO and ERTs cause development with fewer carbon emissions. Although there may be some adjustment costs associated with environment-related technologies at first, an increasingly large list of literature stresses the role of these technologies as drivers of economic growth based on innovation. Based on the Porter Hypothesis, properly formulated environmental regulation policies have the chance to accelerate technological innovation and improve productivity. Based on endogenous growth theory, expenditures on environment-related technologies can raise knowledge accumulation and improve market growth, especially within technologically advanced OECD countries. As a consequence, ERTs should be counted on to drive economic growth with higher resource efficiency and innovation, based on the concept of “green growth.”

Baseline results

The findings of both the FE and RE models show that the MNRTs and REO have negative impacts on economic growth. Nevertheless, the results of both the FE and RE models show that FINC, ERT, and globalization (KOFG) are positively related to the economic growth of OECD countries.

The reason underlying the negative impact of MNRTs on economic growth is the rapid flow of rents derived from NRs, which results in currency devaluation and reduces the competitiveness of other sectors in the global market. Consequently, this displacement effect hinders the growth of sectors such as manufacturing and agriculture. In addition, the results show that FINC is positively related to the economic growth. More access of people to various financial products increases the household income, which increases economic growth. Consequently, increased FINC has the capacity to foster economic growth.

Furthermore, the results show that globalization (KOFG) is positively related to economic growth, which implies that an increased degree of globalization allows nations to produce and export more goods and services, which leads to more economic growth. Moreover, we find that the REO is negatively related to economic growth, which suggests that the REO is vital for addressing environmental concerns at the cost of low economic growth. The results show that the ERT is positively related to economic growth, which implies that the development of ERT is crucial for both economic growth and environmental quality. The implementation of technological improvements not only enhances the quality of goods and services but also promotes the growth of new industries and the emergence of new employment opportunities. The development and advancement of new technologies are crucial for driving economic growth, especially in OECD countries that are actively working to enhance their economic expansion.

The results of four different econometric techniques are summarized and presented in Table 8. As can be seen, unlike all other variables, different techniques yield different results in REO coefficients. This is particularly relevant to the distinction between short-term and long-term equilibrium. Discussions

In recent decades, there has been substantial research on the impact of abundant NRs on economic growth. Nevertheless, the ongoing debate revolves around the fundamental question of whether an abundance of NRs is a curse or a blessing. The nexus between economic growth and NRs has been widely discussed. Researchers have varying opinions on the nexus between resource rents and economic growth. This study examines the influence of MNRTs, FINC, the development of ERTs, globalization, and REO on national income in 9 OECD countries. This study utilizes FE and RE methods to estimate the model. For a robustness check, this study uses FMOLS, DOLS, and quantile regression approaches. The findings are as follows: (1) MRNTs negatively affect economic growth in sample countries; (2) REO negatively affects economic growth in sample countries; (3) FINC is positively related to the economic growth of OECD countries; and (4) ERT and globalization are positively related to the economic growth of OECD countries.

This study has great policy implications, especially for OECD countries. The findings of the study underscore the adverse consequences of resource rents and hence suggest the need for stronger resource governance and diversification strategies that reduce overreliance on extractive industries. Moreover, the rents received from resources should be used for long-term viable projects, which promote long-term growth and development. Furthermore, the paper emphasizes the importance of deepening financial system to achieve long-term growth prospects. More access to financial products and services will enable these countries to achieve inclusive growth. The long-run positive impact of REO and development of ERTs highlights the substantial impact of governance reform in facilitating the adoption of energy-efficient policies and technology. The governments must prioritize the development and implementation of legislation that encourages energy efficiency in all sectors. Energy transition will enable these countries to achieve development with fewer carbon emissions.

Out with the OECD framework, it should be noted that insights garnered within the scope of this research can be applicably useful for developing nations within Asia and South America, which have been driven by a high degree of dependence on NRs and are presently within an energy transition phase. Within these developing economies, resource-driven rents can potentially pose a challenge toward short-term growth due to an overall delay in structural transformation, and expenditure on renewable energy and environmental technologies can be supportive for long-term growth, depending on the efficiency of the institutional setup. Growth within developing nations should ideally focus on transition cost measures associated with fiscal policies and not be dependent on natural resource extraction.

Limitations of the study

Despite several important contributions to literature, this study has some limitations, which need to be addressed in future studies. First, the study used MNRTs as a proxy to represent natural resource rent. Future studies may include oil and gas rents in the model to examine differences in the impacts of these rents on economic performance. Second, the study is limited to nine OECD countries. Future studies may be conducted by considering other OECD countries to check the generalization and validity of these findings. Third, there is evidence that more effective use of technology, particularly artificial intelligence tools, yields positive results in achieving sustainable development goals.45,46,47,48 In this context, future studies could explore the effective use of artificial intelligence technologies in preventing the natural resource curse. Fourth, we use the panel data econometric techniques to test the hypotheses. Future studies may investigate country-specific differences in the relationship between variables.

In addition, Financial inclusion (FINC) is created as a compound index via principal component analysis (PCA). In particular, after making these variables scale-free via standardization, with a focus on comparing them, PCA is performed on these variables to identify commonality among them and then uses the first principal component, which captures the greatest amount of total variability, as the FINC index. CSD tests

Initially, we examined the presence of cross-sectional dependence (CSD) by conducting three different residual-based CSD tests. Diagnosing cross sectional dependence in important because it results in misleading skewed estimates. We use the Breusch & Pagan LM test, Pesaran-scale LM test, and Pesaran-scale CD test to assess whether the residuals exhibit spatial autocorrelation. The Breusch & Pagan LM test is particularly appropriate for testing CSD in panel data models with large time periods (T) and small number of cross-sectional units (N). The Pesaran-scale LM test is an improved version of the Breusch‒Pagan LM test that takes into account heteroscedasticity. The Pesaran CD test is a scaled version of commonly employed test Breusch & Pagan LM test.

Fixed and random effects

To assess the impact of mineral rents and financial inclusion along with other control variable on national income, we use random effects and fixed effect methods. The main reason for using these models is that there are several attributes of nations that do not vary over time and may influence the nexus among variables. Therefore, fixed effects and random-effects models consider the impact of these attributes. In fixed-effects model, we assume different intercept for each group or time in the regression equation. One of the advantages of the fixed-effects models is that it captures unobserved fixed characteristics over time, or across space. Moreover, the fixed-effects models take into account the individual heterogeneity, which permits each entity to have its own intercept. Random effects, on the other hand, allows for the estimation of both time-invariant and time-varying effects across individual entities. The random effects models are more reliable when dealing with large datasets. The random effects model rests on the assumption that individual-specific effects are not correlated with the regressors, although they may be correlated across individuals.51

MMQR technique

For a robustness check, this study uses Fully Modified ordinary Least Square (FMOLS) and Method of Moments Quantile Regression (MMQR) approaches. The FMOLS method offers more robust estimates because it deals with the problem of endogeneity by including the lagged dependent variable and instrumental variables in the model.52 Moreover, the FMOLS method offer robust estimates in case of non-stationary panel data, and small-sample biases. The MMQR method captures heterogeneity of the distribution by providing more robust estimates. The method provides coefficients across different points of the distribution rather than only at the mean. The MMQR approach accounts for outliers, heteroscedasticity, and non-normality.
