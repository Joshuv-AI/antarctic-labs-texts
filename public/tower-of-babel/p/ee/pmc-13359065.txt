Ten-year spontaneous intracranial hypotension with acquired tonsillar descent and syringomyelia from a T3–4 ventral dural defect: importance of digital subtraction myelography localization and role of direct repair. Illustrative case
Authors: Anna Chan, Azam Ali Baig, Marian Byrne, Waleed Butt, Hari Krovvidi, Georgios Tsermoulas, Athanasios Zisakis, Alexandru Budu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSpontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is an underrecognized yet increasingly diagnosed cause of orthostatic headaches. Reduction of intracranial CSF volume and pressure produces characteristic imaging findings such as diffuse pachymeningeal enhancement and cerebellar tonsillar descent, mimicking a Chiari malformation type I. Distinguishing SIH from other causes of hindbrain herniation is critical for prompt recognition and targeted treatment to resolve symptoms and radiological abnormalities.OBSERVATIONSA 55-year-old male patient presented with a history of persisting postural headaches, back pain, and sensory loss. MRI of the spine demonstrated a subtle ventral epidural fluid collection extending along the cervical and thoracic spine. Intraoperative digital subtraction myelography confirmed a CSF leak site at the T3–4 level due to a dural defect. Under the same anesthetic, a posterior T3–4 laminectomy and intradural repair of the ventral dural defect were performed. The patient recovered with complete resolution of symptoms and no neurological deficits or weakness. Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is a condition characterized by orthostatic headaches classically due to loss of CSF from the spinal canal. CSF leaks may result from dural defects, meningeal diverticula, or CSF-venous fistulae, which are categorized according to etiology. Trauma, connective tissue diseases, and medical interventions are known to be some causative agents, although many are idiopathic.1,2

In conjunction with low-pressure headaches, patients may experience neck pain, visual and vestibulocochlear disturbances, and nonspecific psychological changes. Although rarely considered a surgical emergency, the symptoms of SIH can significantly disrupt the quality of life in individuals. Characteristic imaging such as MRI reflects the reduced intracranial CSF volume, diffuse pachymeningeal enhancement, venous engorgement, and acquired cerebellar tonsillar descent, mimicking Chiari malformation type I.1,3 The anatomical location of the CSF leak point is often difficult to pinpoint. Dedicated imaging such as CT myelography, and more recently digital subtraction myelography (DSM), is required to help locate the defect.2,4 Conservative treatment options include bed rest, oral hydration, and caffeine intake as initial options.5 Epidural blood patches (EBPs) can also be utilized as an effective treatment for SIH.6–8 Surgical intervention should be considered for patients who do not experience relief from the EBP.3 Here, we report the case of a patient with persistent symptomatic SIH who achieved significant resolution of symptoms and improved radiological findings after surgical intervention.

Illustrative Case

A 55-year-old man presented with orthostatic headaches, back pain, gait disturbance, and sensory loss in the C2–6 dermatomes. Over time, behavioral changes were noted by his family members, such as pursuing uncharacteristic hobbies and making inappropriate comments, which were suggestive of a frontal syndrome. Because of these symptoms spanning 10 years, he had previous neurological reviews and underwent two nontargeted EBP procedures, which were ineffective. On examination in the neurosurgery clinic, there was C2–6 dermatomal loss of vibration and pinprick sensation, a positive Romberg’s test, and diffuse brisk reflexes.

Initial neuroimaging revealed 10 mm of tonsillar decent mimicking a Chiari malformation type I with high cervical cord edema and signs of early syrinx formation. MRI of the head and spine with contrast was performed, revealing a ventral spinal longitudinal extradural CSF collection (SLEC) from C6 to T6, a reduced mamillopontine distance of 4 mm, lumbar venous plexus engorgement, associated syrinx formation around C2, cord edema spanning C1–6, and expansion suggestive of intracranial hypotension, as shown in Figs. 1 and 2, which were obtained 6 months apart. A CT myelography study was arranged, which failed to identify the CSF leak point. DSM was then performed, which highlighted a probable CSF leak at T3–4; however, movement artifact made it challenging to interpret (Fig. 3A). Procedure

Under general aesthetic, the patient was transferred to the catheterization laboratory. Prone DSM was performed with 6 ml of contrast material injected intradurally, confirming a CSF leak ventrally at T3–4 (Fig. 3B). The patient was transferred to the operating room after placement of a T3 pedicle marker. He was the positioned prone on a Montreal mattress, with his head secured in a Mayfield clamp. Fluoroscopy was used to confirm the T3–4 levels. A standard midline posterior approach was performed, targeting the T3–4 region with extended right lateral exposure to accommodate for pedicle drilling. A T3–4 laminectomy was then performed after a second intraoperative level check. On the right side, a facetectomy involving the superior and inferior articular processes of T4 was undertaken, followed by pedicle drilling to facilitate adequate lateral and ventrolateral exposure of the spinal cord. Attention was directed to the expected site of CSF leakage at the T3–4 disc level. The dura was opened sharply in the midline, and hitch stitches were applied to maintain exposure. The arachnoid membrane was carefully dissected, and the dentate ligament was sectioned to permit gentle mobilization of the spinal cord. With intermittent and minimal retraction, the ventrolateral aspect of the cord was explored and motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were repeated at the end of each retraction episode. A ventral dural defect was identified, through which a portion of the arachnoid appeared to have herniated. The margins of the defect were clearly delineated (Fig. 4A). A dural substitute (DuraGen) was passed through the defect to buttress the ventral dura and secured with Prolene sutures (Fig. 4B). A second patch was then placed ventral to the cord but not sutured, effectively wrapping and reinforcing the repair. After placement of the patch, the last two MEP runs demonstrated a significant decrease in MEP activity on the right lower limb; however, activity in the right abductor hallucis muscle was present. The mean arterial pressure (MAP) was kept above 80, and no obvious contusional injury was noticed; therefore, the decrease in MEP activity was associated with cord retraction. The dura was closed primarily using 5-0 PDS sutures, and a Valsalva maneuver confirmed a watertight seal without evidence of residual CSF leakage. Hemostatic adjuncts, including Hemopatch and Tisseel, were applied over the repair. Laminoplasty was not performed due to insufficient bony purchase on the right side. Hemostasis was achieved, a surgical drain was placed, and the wound was closed in anatomical layers. Final monitoring was performed at the end: somatosensory evoked potentials were present, with MEPs present and normal on the left but reduced on the right. Small signs of contraction were observed in the gastrocnemius muscle, but they were severely reduced; contraction remained present in the abductor hallucis muscle. Persistence of MEP on the left ruled out an anterior spinal cord infarct, and normal MEP on the right abductor hallucis muscle was reassuring. Postoperatively, low-pressure headache improvement was experienced immediately and no weakness found on neurological examination. The patient was transferred to the ICU to have his MAP augmented to above 85. On recovery, only mild weakness was noted in the right lower limb. Repeat neurological observations were performed that suggested recovery of the mild deficit. Based on the intraoperative findings, MEP results, and reassuring postoperative recovery, we did not believe an immediate postoperative MRI study was required. From our experience, gentle retraction and manipulation of the spinal cord tends to manifest as a mild to moderate postoperative deficit, which recovers within the first 48 hours.

Follow-up imaging (Fig. 5) demonstrated full resolution of the syrinx and edema as well as the intracranial hypotension features seen on preoperative MRI of the spine. Visible resolution of the Chiari malformation to 0.8 mm was observed. On early and late clinical follow-ups (3 and 8 months postprocedure, respectively), there was resolution of orthostatic headaches, no sensory loss, improved gait and behavioral symptoms, and no rebound intracranial hypertension. Discussion

According to the multidisciplinary consensus guideline for the diagnosis and management of SIH, T2-weighted contrast MRI of the brain and spine should be performed as the first-line imaging modality if suspected.9 Patients should be referred to a specialist center with relevant facilities, including CT myelography and/or DSM facilities. Myelography aims to accurately pinpoint the origin of the defect and classify the type of spinal CSF leak to plan treatment, as MRI alone fails to achieve this.2,4,9 Prone DSM should be considered if standard DSM is nondiagnostic but clinical suspicion remains high. Standard DSM (nongeneral anesthetic) investigation can be limited due to movement artifact. In cases with high clinical suspicion, a prone general anesthetic DSM study has two benefits: better resolution for diagnostic yield and facilitation of surgical treatment under the same general anesthetic.

Nontargeted EBP should be offered as the first-line treatment in all patients with a clinical SIH diagnosis, with or without imaging evidence within 2 weeks.9 Conservative methods should be advised concurrently. Given the limited evidence of symptomatic relief from conservative management, EBP deliverance should not be delayed. EBPs have shown to be effective in both targeted and blind administration, providing long-term symptom improvement in some severe or complicated cases according to previous literature.7,8

Other interventions such as fibrin glue injection can be offered. Epidural fibrin glue injection is a documented option for a persistent CSF leak or when blood patch placement or suturing is not suitable.10

The management of ventral dural tears in SIH has evolved significantly, with a shift toward less invasive posterior approaches. While anterior approaches remain important in selected cases, posterior transdural and indirect techniques offer a balance between access and safety.11

The debate between patching and suturing reflects a broader surgical principle: the trade-off between definitive repair and procedural risk. Current trends favor patch-based techniques, particularly when direct suturing would require excessive spinal cord manipulation.12

Furthermore, the recognition of SIH as a cause of acquired Chiari-like changes and syringomyelia underscores the importance of understanding CSF dynamics.

Observations

In patients with persisting symptoms or only temporary relief from EBP, surgical management should be considered if medically appropriate. This should be done in a shared, multidisciplinary approach, involving the patient, neurosurgeon, neurologist, and neuroradiologist, as done in our case. Patients who undergo neurosurgical treatment for SIH have a high rate of symptomatic recovery, and many remain asymptomatic after several months.3

Identifying the CSF leak point proved a challenge in this case, as with the majority of SIH cases. As advised in the guidelines, our patient underwent a DSM study to determine the location of the CSF leak, including a second DSM study performed prone, where the leak point was confirmed, followed by surgery. The patient only underwent DSM when placed under our care, despite several years having elapsed since the unsuccessful treatment with two EBPs. This imaging modality was key to the surgery, proving its use in effectively locating the defect. We advocate its use in patients with SIH.

A noteworthy aspect of this case is our patient’s particularly long journey to obtain a diagnosis and appropriate treatment, illustrated by the prior multiple referrals over years before coming under our care. At presentation, he displayed features suggestive of mild cognitive impairment, likely due to SIH-related brain sagging and frontotemporal disturbance. Another cause could be glymphatic system dysfunction. The glymphatic system is a perivascular network that clears harmful metabolites in the brain and allows CSF and interstitial fluid exchange. Studies have shown correlations between glymphatic system impairment and neurodegenerative conditions and mild cognitive impairment.13,14 Unfortunately, formal neuropsychology assessments did not form part of our diagnostic workup, and this was recognized as a limitation. Our case presents the importance of being aware of varied patient presentation, where symptoms may be influenced by comorbidities that are not necessarily visible or physical, which will aid the delivery of appropriate specialist care and good outcomes.

In our patient, the aims of the surgery were to prevent further progression of the syrinx and to relieve the low-pressure headaches. Postoperative follow-up imaging illustrated complete resolution of the CSF epidural collection and cord edema, complete resolution of the syrinx, and significantly improved brainstem and cerebellar tonsillar placement. This mirrored the patient’s asymptomatic clinical condition.

Our patient represents a case in which surgical intervention fully resolved symptoms of SIH and resolution of pathological changes, including syringomyelia and tonsillar descent, was noted on imaging.

Lessons

This report addresses a case of full recovery in a patient presenting with long-standing symptomatic SIH achieved by surgical intervention. The diagnostic and clinicoradiological trajectory in this case was a combination of a 10-year diagnostic delay in chronic SIH with Chiari-like descent and syrinx. Repeat dynamic myelography was required after nondiagnostic imaging, and direct repair reversed both intracranial hypotension features and spinal cord changes when a focal ventral leak was identified. The successful results highlighted by both patient outcome and postoperative imaging emphasize the need for prompt diagnosis and the efficacy of surgery in cases of SIH where appropriate.
