No survival benefit is associated with pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy in lymph node–negative early-stage endometrioid endometrial cancer
Authors: Yakup Yalcin, Serenat Eris Yalcin, Bilge Cetinkaya Demir, Bahadir Kosan, Kemal Ozerkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe therapeutic role of lymphadenectomy in endometrial cancer remains controversial. Although systematic pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy (PPLND) has been suggested to improve survival in certain patients, randomized trials have failed to demonstrate a definitive benefit. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic value of lymphadenectomy according to risk groups in patients with early-stage, node-negative endometrioid endometrial cancer.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of 654 lymph node–negative patients with FIGO 2009 stage I–II endometrioid endometrial carcinoma who underwent primary surgical treatment at Bursa Uludağ University between 2000 and 2025. Patients were categorized into three surgical groups: no lymphadenectomy (n = 139), pelvic lymphadenectomy (PLND, n = 119), and combined pelvic plus para-aortic lymphadenectomy (PPLND, n = 396). Clinicopathological features, recurrence patterns, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS) were compared across groups. Subgroup analyses were performed for low-, intermediate-, and high–intermediate-risk categories.ResultsAdverse pathological features including stage IB–II disease, higher grade, deep myometrial invasion, and LVSI were more frequent in the PPLND group (all p < 0.001). Recurrence rates were 9.4% (13/139) in the no-lymphadenectomy group, 5.9% (7/119) after PLND, and 8.3% (33/396) after PPLND, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.575). However, local recurrences predominated in the no-lymphadenectomy group, whereas distant relapses were more common after PPLND (p = 0.016). In univariate analysis, FIGO stage, LVSI, deep myometrial invasion, and adjuvant chemoradiotherapy were associated with poorer DFS; in multivariate analysis, only chemoradiotherapy remained an independent adverse factor (HR 2.30, 95% CI 1.04–5.10, p = 0.041). For OS, age was the sole independent prognostic factor (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.07–1.11, p < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier analysis by risk group showed no significant survival differences: in the low-risk group, PPLND patients displayed more favorable DFS/OS curves without statistical significance (DFS p = 0.340; OS p = 0.278). No survival benefit was observed in intermediate (DFS p = 0.725; OS p = 0.935) or high–intermediate-risk groups (DFS p = 0.449; OS p = 0.543).ConclusionsSystematic lymphadenectomy does not provide a therapeutic survival benefit in early-stage, node-negative endometrioid endometrial cancer, irrespective of risk stratification. The principal value of nodal assessment lies in accurate staging and guiding adjuvant therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03640-6.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common malignancy of the female genital tract in developed countries, and its incidence is steadily increasing worldwide, with approximately 80% of cases diagnosed at an early stage [1]. In uterus-confined early-stage EC, primary treatment consists of surgical staging, including total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and lymph node assessment through pelvic and/or para-aortic lymphadenectomy or sentinel lymph node mapping [2, 3]. Although the prognostic significance of systematic lymphadenectomy in EC has been well established, its therapeutic value remains a matter of considerable debate. Some investigators advocate routine systematic lymphadenectomy due to its role in refining disease staging and guiding postoperative treatment planning, whereas others discourage its universal application given the potential risks, such as prolonged operative time, increased intraoperative blood loss, major injuries including vascular or ureteral damage, and postoperative complications such as lymphedema and thromboembolism [4]. In parallel with this ongoing controversy, sentinel lymph node mapping has emerged as an alternative nodal assessment strategy aimed at preserving staging accuracy while reducing surgical morbidity, particularly in patients with apparent early-stage disease [5].

Retrospective studies such as the SEPAL trial have suggested that performing systematic pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy (PPLND) may improve survival outcomes, particularly in patients with intermediate- and high-intermediate-risk EC, possibly due to the oncological benefit of achieving complete cytoreduction with no residual disease [6–8]. However, two large prospective randomized controlled trials—the ASTEC trial and the Italian study conducted by Benedetti Panici—both of which predominantly included low-risk patients, failed to demonstrate any survival advantage from lymphadenectomy. It should be noted that in both studies, only pelvic lymphadenectomy (PLND) was performed, while para-aortic lymphadenectomy was omitted. Furthermore, approximately 30% of patients assigned to the no-lymphadenectomy arms subsequently received adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy, which has been criticized for confounding the interpretation of outcomes. This treatment crossover may have obscured a potential survival benefit attributable to lymphadenectomy, thereby limiting the ability of these trials to definitively establish its therapeutic role [9, 10]. Similarly, the PORTEC-1 trial demonstrated no survival advantage of adjuvant radiotherapy in low- and intermediate-risk EC irrespective of lymph node status, further raising doubts regarding the therapeutic contribution of systematic lymphadenectomy [11].

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the therapeutic value of lymphadenectomy by comparing survival and recurrence outcomes among patients with endometrioid-type EC who did not undergo lymphadenectomy, those who underwent pelvic lymphadenectomy, and those who underwent combined pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy but were node-negative.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study included a total of 654 patients who underwent primary surgical treatment for endometrioid-type endometrial carcinoma at the Division of Gynecologic Oncology, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Bursa Uludağ University Faculty of Medicine, between January 2000 and May 2025. The study received ethical approval from the Bursa Uludag University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Protocol No. 2025/803/14–22) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the ethics committee exempted the requirement for informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients with a histopathological diagnosis of endometrioid-type endometrial carcinoma, limited to stage I–II disease according to the 2009 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification, and who had undergone at least total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy as part of primary surgical treatment were included in the study.

Patients with non-endometrioid histology (serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma, mixed histology, or undifferentiated carcinoma), FIGO stage III–IV disease, uterine sarcoma, or synchronous/metachronous malignancies were excluded. Those who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or hormonal therapy prior to surgery, as well as patients who underwent fertility-sparing treatment (such as progestin therapy or ovarian preservation in young women), were also excluded. In addition, cases with incomplete data on surgical procedure, pathological findings, adjuvant treatment, or follow-up outcomes, patients who underwent only palliative surgery or biopsy, and those who could not undergo standard surgical treatment due to severe comorbidities were not included in the analysis.

Clinical and pathological assessment

Demographic and clinicopathological variables including age, body mass index (BMI), tumor size, histological grade, depth of myometrial invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), number of harvested lymph nodes, surgical approach (laparotomy or laparoscopy), recurrence and recurrence site were retrieved from electronic medical records. Adjuvant therapy modalities (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or chemoradiotherapy) were also recorded. Patients were divided into three risk groups based on stage, histological grade, and LVSI status.

Based on the extent of lymphadenectomy, patients were categorized into three groups: those who did not undergo lymphadenectomy, those who underwent pelvic lymphadenectomy, and those who received combined pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Given the retrospective design and long study period, no uniform institutional algorithm was applied to determine the extent of lymphadenectomy. Surgical decision-making at our center was guided by accumulated clinical experience and established risk stratification principles. In earlier periods, lymphadenectomy was primarily based on the Mayo Clinic criteria, with at least pelvic lymphadenectomy performed in patients not meeting low-risk features. Para-aortic lymphadenectomy was applied selectively according to the patient’s overall risk profile (e.g., obesity, significant cardiovascular comorbidities) and the surgeon’s intraoperative clinical judgment. According to the Mayo Clinic criteria, patients with endometrioid histology, grade 1–2 tumors, < 50% myometrial invasion, and a primary tumor diameter ≤ 2 cm are considered at low risk for nodal metastasis, in whom systematic lymphadenectomy may be safely omitted. Pelvic lymphadenectomy included removal of lymph nodes from the external, internal, and common iliac chains, as well as the obturator region. Para-aortic dissection encompassed the pericaval, aortocaval, and left para-aortic regions, extending cranially up to the level of the renal vein.

Risk group classification

Patients were further stratified into three prognostic risk groups—low-, intermediate-, and high–intermediate-risk—according to stage, histological grade, and LVSI status, as defined below.

The low-risk group consisted of patients with stage IA, grade 1–2 tumors and negative LVSI. The intermediate-risk group included patients with stage IB, grade 1–2 and negative LVSI, as well as those with stage IA, grade 3 tumors without LVSI. The high–intermediate-risk group comprised patients with stage IA–IB tumors and positive LVSI, stage IB grade 3 tumors irrespective of LVSI status, and all stage II tumors regardless of grade.

Follow-up

Postoperative follow-up was conducted every 3–6 months during the first 2 years and every 6 months thereafter up to 5 years. Follow-up of patients who completed their 5th year continued annually. Further imaging with CT (computed tomography) or PET/CT (positron emission tomography/computed tomography) was performed in the presence of clinical findings suggesting recurrence or metastasis. Findings confirmed by imaging methods according to RECIST 1.1 (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors) criteria or by biopsy were considered disease recurrence. Recurrences were classified as local (vaginal, pelvic lymph nodes, bladder, rectum), distant (mid or upper abdominal cavity, para-aortic lymph nodes, lung, liver, bone, brain), or both if present simultaneously. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from surgery to disease recurrence or last follow-up date; overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to cancer-related death or last follow-up date.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as median (range), while categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Prognostic factors for DFS and OS were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and log–log survival plots and was not violated. To evaluate the potential confounding effect of the long study period, a period-based sensitivity analysis was performed. Patients were stratified into two treatment eras (≤ 2012 and ≥ 2013), and 5-year OS and DFS were compared using Kaplan–Meier analysis with follow-up censored at 60 months. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 867 patients with endometrial cancer were initially identified. Of these, 151 patients were excluded due to non-endometrioid histology (serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma, mixed histology, or undifferentiated carcinoma). An additional 62 patients were excluded because of FIGO stage III–IV disease, of whom 49 were upstaged due to the presence of lymph node metastases. After application of the exclusion criteria, the final study cohort consisted of 654 patients with lymph node–negative, FIGO stage I–II endometrioid endometrial cancer. Of these 654 patients, 139 (21.2%) underwent no lymphadenectomy, 119 (18.2%) had pelvic lymphadenectomy, and 396 (60.6%) received combined pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy (Table 1). Patients without lymphadenectomy were younger (median age 60 years) and more frequently presented with low-risk features, with stage IA (86.3%) and grade 1 tumors (62.6%) being predominant. In contrast, patients in the pelvic plus para-aortic group had larger tumors (median 3.5 cm), more frequent stage IB–II disease (38.4%), grade 3 histology (17.7%), as well as higher rates of deep myometrial invasion (35.6%) and LVSI (21.8%) (all p < 0.001). The number of harvested lymph nodes increased with surgical extent, with a median of 19.5 in the pelvic group and 31 in the pelvic plus para-aortic group (p < 0.001).

In the low-risk group, 28.9% of patients had no lymphadenectomy, 22.0% underwent pelvic dissection, and 49.1% received combined pelvic plus para-aortic dissection. The majority of intermediate- (73.8%) and high–intermediate-risk patients (77.3%) were treated with combined lymphadenectomy. Overall, the distribution of risk groups significantly varied according to the extent of lymphadenectomy (p < 0.001). Surgical approach and adjuvant therapy patterns also differed significantly. Laparoscopy was most frequent in the pelvic group (55.5%), whereas laparotomy predominated in the pelvic plus para-aortic group (91.2%; p < 0.001). The majority of patients without lymphadenectomy received no adjuvant therapy (66.9%), while radiotherapy and chemoradiotherapy were more common after extended dissection (p < 0.001). Recurrence occurred in 13 of 139 patients (9.4%) in the no-lymphadenectomy group, 7 of 119 patients (5.9%) in the pelvic lymphadenectomy group, and 33 of 396 patients (8.3%) in the pelvic plus para-aortic lymphadenectomy group, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.575). Regarding recurrence patterns, local recurrences predominated in the no-lymphadenectomy group, whereas distant recurrences were more frequent in the pelvic plus para-aortic lymphadenectomy group (p = 0.016).

In univariate Cox regression analysis, FIGO stage, LVSI, deep myometrial invasion, and adjuvant treatment were significantly associated with DFS (Table 2). Compared with stage IA, patients with stage IB disease had a more than twofold increased risk of recurrence (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.16–3.56, p = 0.014), while stage II disease was not significantly different (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.56–1.85, p = 0.953). The presence of LVSI was associated with worse DFS (HR 2.02, 95% CI 1.11–3.67, p = 0.021), as was deep myometrial invasion > 50% (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.10–3.31, p = 0.022). Regarding adjuvant therapy, chemoradiotherapy significantly increased the risk of recurrence compared with no adjuvant treatment (HR 2.83, 95% CI 1.33–6.00, p = 0.007). Other variables, including age, BMI, tumor size, grade, surgical method, and type of lymphadenectomy, were not associated with DFS in univariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, only adjuvant chemoradiotherapy remained independently associated with poorer DFS (HR 2.30, 95% CI 1.04–5.10, p = 0.041). FIGO stage, LVSI, and depth of myometrial invasion lost their significance after adjustment.

In univariate Cox regression analysis, age and deep myometrial invasion were significantly associated with OS (Table 3). Each one-year increase in age was associated with a 10% higher risk of mortality (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.08–1.12, p < 0.001). Patients with > 50% myometrial invasion also had significantly poorer OS (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.15–2.19, p = 0.005). Regarding FIGO stage, stage IB disease was linked to worse OS compared with stage IA (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.10–2.14, p = 0.012), whereas stage II did not show a significant difference (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.42–1.51, p = 0.488). Other variables, including BMI, tumor size, tumor grade, LVSI, surgical method, type of lymphadenectomy, and adjuvant treatment, were not significantly associated with OS. In the multivariate model, only age remained as an independent prognostic factor for OS (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.07–1.11, p < 0.001). FIGO stage and deep myometrial invasion lost their statistical significance after adjustment.

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses demonstrated that in the low-risk group, patients who underwent pelvic plus para-aortic lymphadenectomy had more favorable DFS and OS curves; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (DFS p = 0.340, Fig. 1A; OS p = 0.278, Fig. 1B). In the intermediate-risk group, no survival benefit was observed according to the extent of lymphadenectomy (DFS p = 0.725, Fig. 2A; OS p = 0.935, Fig. 2B). Similarly, in the high–intermediate-risk group, DFS and OS did not significantly differ between the groups (DFS p = 0.449, Fig. 3A; OS p = 0.543, Fig. 3B). When the entire cohort was analyzed, survival curves for the pelvic plus para-aortic group appeared more favorable, but the differences were not statistically significant (DFS p = 0.477, Fig. 4A; OS p = 0.376, Fig. 4B). In the subgroup of patients without adjuvant treatment, both DFS and OS outcomes were comparable across the groups (DFS p = 0.527, Fig. 5A; OS p = 0.419, Fig. 5B). Regarding event numbers, 22 recurrences and 79 deaths were observed among 373 patients in the low-risk group, 11 recurrences and 35 deaths among 127 patients in the intermediate-risk group, and 20 recurrences and 45 deaths among 154 patients in the high–intermediate-risk group. The relatively low number of events across subgroups should be considered when interpreting the absence of statistically significant survival differences.

Discussion

In our study, significant differences in clinicopathological characteristics and treatment patterns were observed among patients who underwent no lymphadenectomy, pelvic lymphadenectomy alone, or combined pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Patients in the PPLND group presented with larger tumors, more advanced stage disease, higher tumor grade, deeper myometrial invasion, and higher rates of lymphovascular space invasion. Nevertheless, Kaplan–Meier analyses and multivariable models showed that the extent of lymphadenectomy had no independent effect on either disease-free survival or overall survival. Adjuvant chemoradiotherapy emerged as the only independent adverse factor for disease-free survival in multivariate analysis; however, this finding most likely reflects confounding by indication. Patients receiving combined adjuvant therapy generally had more aggressive pathological features and unfavorable tumor biology, which inherently increased their risk of recurrence, rather than an adverse effect attributable to the treatment itself. For OS, age emerged as the sole independent prognostic factor, with no significant associations observed for other variables. Collectively, these findings indicate that increasing the extent of lymphadenectomy does not confer a direct therapeutic benefit on survival outcomes. These imbalances likely reflect real-world surgical decision-making, whereby surgeons tend to perform more extensive lymphadenectomy in patients with adverse intraoperative or pathological features, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

The therapeutic value and optimal extent of lymphadenectomy in endometrial cancer remain subjects of ongoing debate. In a study conducted by Battista et al., it was reported that 98.3% of surgeons performed pelvic lymphadenectomy, while 90.2% performed both pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy in patients with endometrial cancer [12]. Conversely, another investigation demonstrated that para-aortic lymphadenectomy is generally reserved for selected cases and is performed routinely in fewer than 10% of patients [13]. A survey of members of the Society of Gynecologic Oncologists (SGO) further revealed considerable variation in practice: 50% of respondents reported performing para-aortic lymphadenectomy up to the level of the inferior mesenteric artery, whereas 11% extended the dissection to the level of the renal vein [14]. Interestingly, one study suggested that para-aortic lymph node metastases are more frequently located in the supramesenteric region [15]. Given such heterogeneous and often conflicting evidence, the present study aimed to further clarify both the extent and the therapeutic impact of lymphadenectomy in the management of endometrial cancer.

To date, only two prospective randomized trials have evaluated the therapeutic value of lymphadenectomy in endometrial cancer [9, 10]. Both studies, which included large patient cohorts, compared pelvic lymphadenectomy with no lymphadenectomy and demonstrated no improvement in either disease-free survival or overall survival. The absence of therapeutic benefit has been attributed to the exclusive assessment of pelvic lymphadenectomy, the predominance of low-risk patients, and the limited number of cases with advanced-stage disease or non-endometrioid histology. Para-aortic lymphadenectomy was performed in up to 26% of patients in the Italian trial, whereas it was conducted in only a minority of patients in the ASTEC trial, further complicating comparisons between study arms. In a large SEER database analysis including 11,603 women with stage IA grade 3 endometrial cancer, lymphadenectomy appeared to improve patient outcomes; however, this effect disappeared after adjustment for confounding factors such as age and comorbidities [16]. Another study demonstrated no survival benefit of lymphadenectomy in intermediate-risk endometrial cancer [17]. Similarly, the absence of survival advantage for lymphadenectomy in stage IA G3, IB G1–3, and II G1–3 endometrial cancer was confirmed in the Australian national endometrial cancer study [18]. In line with these findings, Ignatov et al. also reported that systematic pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy did not provide a survival benefit for patients with stage I–II endometrioid endometrial cancer at intermediate- or high-intermediate-risk [19]. In our study, to ensure a homogeneous cohort, patients with non-endometrioid histology and advanced-stage disease were excluded. Only node-negative, stage I–II endometrioid cases were included, allowing a more accurate assessment of the prognostic impact of lymphadenectomy. Adequate numbers of patients were represented in both the pelvic (18.2%) and pelvic plus para-aortic (60.6%) groups. Consistent with the aforementioned studies, we found no beneficial effect of lymphadenectomy on either OS or DFS. Even after stratification into low-, intermediate-, and high-intermediate-risk subgroups, lymphadenectomy did not demonstrate a survival advantage.

In a large cohort of 5,446 patients with endometrial cancer, univariable analysis suggested that lymphadenectomy improved overall survival in patients at intermediate and high risk; however, this favorable effect disappeared after adjustment for age and comorbidities [20]. Similarly, in our study, multivariable analysis did not demonstrate an association between lymphadenectomy and survival outcomes. For disease-free survival, only adjuvant chemoradiotherapy emerged as an independent factor, most likely reflecting confounding by indication. For OS, age was identified as the sole independent prognostic factor, confirming the adverse impact of advanced age on survival.

It has been difficult to distinguish the therapeutic effect of lymphadenectomy itself from the additional impact of adjuvant therapy on microscopic metastases. To clarify the survival effect of lymphadenectomy and to minimize any potential influence of adjuvant treatment, we evaluated 265 patients who received no adjuvant therapy and were managed with surgery alone. In this subgroup analysis, neither PLND nor combined PPLND improved disease-free survival or overall survival, suggesting that the extent of dissection does not provide a direct therapeutic benefit. By contrast, Eggemann et al. reported a survival advantage of combined pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy in intermediate- and high-intermediate-risk patients who did not receive adjuvant therapy [21]. Similarly, Todo et al. described a survival benefit of PPLND in intermediate- and high-intermediate-risk cohorts [6]. However, these findings are confounded by heterogeneity in adjuvant treatment, particularly higher rates of chemotherapy in the extended dissection groups, making it difficult to determine whether the observed survival benefit was attributable to the surgical procedure itself or to systemic therapy.

In a large retrospective analysis (the SEPAL study), combined pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy was compared with pelvic lymphadenectomy alone in patients with endometrial cancer at intermediate or high-intermediate-risk of recurrence, and a survival advantage was reported for the combined dissection [6]. However, as this study did not include a control group without lymphadenectomy, its findings should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, an association between the number of lymph nodes removed and patient outcomes was reported, with the removal of more than 11 nodes being linked to a significant improvement OS. In our study, however, despite the median number of lymph nodes removed being substantially higher than in previous reports (19.5 for PLND and 31 for PPLND), we were unable to demonstrate a positive impact on survival.

Although the therapeutic role of systematic lymph node dissection (LND) remains unconfirmed, assessment of nodal status continues to hold substantial clinical and prognostic value. According to the literature, the rate of lymph node positivity has been reported to be 1–3% in low-risk EC, 10–16% in intermediate-risk EC, and 15–35% in high-intermediate-risk EC [21–26]. Determining nodal involvement therefore provides critical information for risk stratification, guiding adjuvant treatment decisions, and improving prognostic accuracy. To reduce the morbidity associated with systematic lymph node dissection while maintaining—and even improving—diagnostic accuracy, sentinel lymph node (SLN) mapping has been proposed as an alternative staging strategy. Accumulating evidence indicates that SLN mapping, particularly when combined with ultrastaging, provides a higher detection rate of nodal metastases compared with conventional systematic lymphadenectomy [27]. This advantage is largely attributable to enhanced identification of low-volume disease, including micrometastases and isolated tumor cells, which may be overlooked by routine histopathological assessment of systematically removed lymph nodes. Several studies have reported that SLN mapping increases the detection rate of nodal metastases by approximately 30–50% while being associated with significantly lower perioperative morbidity, including reduced rates of lymphedema, lymphocyst formation, and vascular or neural injury [5, 28]. Importantly, recent multicenter data have demonstrated that SLN mapping improves the detection of nodal involvement even in low-risk, low-grade endometrial cancer, a patient population in which systematic lymphadenectomy is frequently omitted [29]. Taken together, these findings underscore that the principal value of SLN mapping lies not only in reduced surgical morbidity but also in superior staging accuracy, supporting SLN mapping as a rational, evidence-based, and modern approach for nodal assessment in early-stage endometrial cancer.

An additional consideration in the interpretation of node-negative disease in the era of SLN mapping and ultrastaging is the presence of isolated tumor cells (ITCs). Although ITCs do not currently result in upstaging according to FIGO classification, accumulating evidence suggests that they may reflect biologically aggressive tumor behavior rather than a purely pathological finding. Recent multicenter data indicate that ITCs are associated with an increased risk of metastasis and recurrence, supporting their potential clinical relevance [30]. Accordingly, patients classified as node-negative by conventional histopathological criteria may still harbor occult nodal disease detectable only through ultrastaging. This observation underscores the need for cautious interpretation of node-negative status and suggests that the identification of ITCs may justify closer surveillance and, in selected cases, consideration of individualized adjuvant treatment strategies.

In addition to traditional clinicopathological factors (FIGO stage, grade, myometrial invasion, and LVSI), modern risk stratification increasingly relies on molecular classification (POLE-mutated, MMRd/MSI, p53-abnormal, and NSMP), which refines both recurrence risk estimation and treatment tailoring. Emerging evidence suggests that MSI/MMRd tumors may carry a higher risk of occult lymph node metastasis in apparent early-stage endometrial cancer, potentially influencing the extent and rationale of nodal assessment even when conventional uterine factors appear favorable [31]. Furthermore, molecular subtypes have been shown to stratify prognosis and recurrence risk even among patients with nodal disease, supporting the concept that integrating molecular markers with conventional pathology is essential for accurate risk assessment and individualized adjuvant strategies. Therefore, the interpretation of “node-negative” early-stage disease and decisions regarding nodal assessment and adjuvant therapy are increasingly optimized when molecular classification is considered alongside standard histopathological parameters.

The strengths of our study include the direct comparison of three groups (no lymphadenectomy, pelvic lymphadenectomy, and combined pelvic/para-aortic lymphadenectomy), the exclusive inclusion of node-negative patients, stratification and comparison of patients according to risk groups, the large cohort size, long duration of follow-up, performance of surgery by gynecologic oncologists, and the standardized nature of lymphadenectomy with an adequate number of nodes removed. In addition, a subgroup analysis of patients who did not receive adjuvant therapy allowed us to exclude the potential confounding effect of adjuvant treatment.

The limitations of the study are mainly related to its retrospective design. In particular, selection bias cannot be fully excluded, as the decision to perform pelvic plus para-aortic lymphadenectomy was influenced by intraoperative findings and the surgeon’s clinical judgment. Consequently, patients in the PPLND group had more adverse clinicopathological features, and although multivariable analyses were performed, residual confounding related to “surgical intuition” may persist. Propensity score–based methods were not applied given the clinical heterogeneity between groups and concerns regarding generalizability. The association between adjuvant chemoradiotherapy and worse disease-free survival reflects confounding by indication, as patients selected for intensified adjuvant treatment had a higher baseline risk of recurrence due to adverse clinicopathological characteristics. This inherent treatment-selection bias cannot be fully eliminated in retrospective analyses. Finally, molecular classification data (e.g., POLE, MMR/MSI, p53 status) were not routinely available across the entire study period; therefore, we could not incorporate molecular subgroups into the primary analyses, which may further refine both occult nodal risk and recurrence prediction in contemporary practice.

The most appropriate study design to clarify the effect of lymphadenectomy on survival in endometrial cancer would be a multicenter, randomized controlled trial including patients from all risk groups, with random assignment to either no lymphadenectomy or pelvic and/or para-aortic lymphadenectomy, and without additional adjuvant therapy. However, such a study is unlikely to be feasible due to ethical constraints. Therefore, evidence for any potential therapeutic contribution of lymphadenectomy must be supported by large-scale retrospective analyses with high external validity.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that lymphadenectomy does not provide a therapeutic survival benefit in node-negative, early-stage endometrioid endometrial cancer, regardless of whether patients are classified as low-, intermediate-, or high-intermediate risk. Given that the principal value of lymph node assessment lies in accurate staging and in guiding adjuvant treatment decisions, consideration may be given to approaches that offer high diagnostic accuracy with lower morbidity, such as sentinel lymph node mapping.
