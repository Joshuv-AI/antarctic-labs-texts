Pre-treatment MRI-derived fractal dimension as a prognostic marker in cervical cancer treated with definitive radiotherapy with or without concurrent chemotherapy
Authors: Sedef Gökhan Açıkgöz, Ayfer Bakır, Büşra Demir Çendek, Havva Beyaz, Süheyla Aytaç Arslan, Fevziye İlknur Kayalı, Rahşan Habiboğlu, Yılmaz Tezcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-treatment magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a central role in local staging and treatment planning for cervical cancer. Fractal dimension (FD) analysis quantifies tumor structural complexity on MRI and has emerged as a non-invasive imaging biomarker. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of MRI-derived FD in patients with cervical cancer treated with definitive radiotherapy (RT) with or without concurrent chemotherapy (CCRT).MethodsThis retrospective study included 84 patients with histologically confirmed cervical cancer treated with definitive RT, most of whom received concurrent chemotherapy (CCRT), between 2019 and 2025. FD was calculated from pre-treatment T2-weighted MRI using the box-counting method. Fractal analysis was performed on axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, and the mean FD was obtained by averaging values across all planes. Associations between FD and HPV/p16 status, baseline hemoglobin levels, FIGO stage, and tumor volume were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression models.ResultsThe median FD was 1.44 (range: 1.20–1.61). FD showed a weak but statistically significant positive correlation with FIGO stage (p = 0.024) and a moderate positive correlation with tumor volume (p < 0.001). No significant associations were observed between FD and HPV/p16 status or baseline hemoglobin levels (p > 0.05). Each 0.1-unit increase in FD was significantly associated with worse OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 4.668, p < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 4.769, p < 0.001). FD was not significantly associated with local recurrence or distant metastasis.ConclusionsMRI-based fractal analysis may provide a quantitative measure of tumor morphological complexity and intratumoral heterogeneity and demonstrates a significant association with survival outcomes in cervical cancer patients treated with definitive RT or CCRT. FD analysis, derived from routine pre-treatment MRI, may complement conventional clinical parameters for risk stratification; however, further validation in larger and less selected patient cohorts is required.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Fractal dimension, MRI, Survival analysis, Chemoradiotherapy

Background

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignancy among women worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality [1]. Each year, approximately 570,000 new cases and 266,000 deaths are reported globally [2], with the vast majority of cases attributable to persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types [3]. Despite substantial advances in screening strategies and therapeutic approaches, cervical cancer continues to pose a significant clinical challenge, particularly in patients diagnosed with locally advanced disease [4].

Definitive chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) remains the standard treatment for locally advanced cervical cancer [5]. Despite this, approximately one-third of patients develop local recurrence (LR) or distant metastasis (DM), leading to considerable heterogeneity in treatment response and survival outcomes [6]. Prognosis is traditionally assessed using clinical parameters such as FIGO stage, tumor volume, HPV status, and hemoglobin (Hb) levels [7]. However, these markers primarily reflect tumor burden and patient-related factors, providing limited insight into the intrinsic structural and molecular heterogeneity of the tumor [4]. Consequently, treatment responses and clinical outcomes can differ substantially even among patients with comparable conventional clinical profiles [4].

These limitations underscore the need for reliable, non-invasive biomarkers that can provide prognostic information beyond conventional clinical parameters [8]. Increasing evidence suggests that traditional prognostic markers alone are insufficient to capture the complex biological behavior of cervical cancer, emphasizing the importance of advanced predictive approaches that account for tumor heterogeneity [8, 9].

In this context, quantitative imaging has emerged as a promising approach in oncology. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived radiomic biomarkers enable the extraction of high-dimensional quantitative features that reflect tumor intensity, shape, and texture, and have demonstrated prognostic value across various malignancies [10]. Importantly, recent studies have emphasized that imaging-derived biomarkers must be robust and reproducible, and that methodological standardization is essential to ensure their clinical applicability and generalizability [11, 12].

In cervical cancer, MRI plays a central role not only in staging but also in providing prognostic information. Radiomics-based approaches using MRI have shown potential in predicting survival outcomes, treatment response, and recurrence risk [13]. However, these approaches often rely on complex feature extraction pipelines and high-dimensional datasets, which may limit their clinical translation [14].

Fractal dimension (FD), derived from fractal geometry, offers an alternative quantitative approach by providing a single metric that reflects the structural complexity and spatial heterogeneity of tumors. Higher FD values indicate increased structural irregularity, which has been associated with more aggressive tumor behavior and adverse pathological features in oncologic imaging studies [14, 15]. Despite these promising attributes, the application of FD remains limited, and existing evidence is scarce. Moreover, available studies are methodologically heterogeneous, with variations in two-dimensional (2D) versus three-dimensional (3D) approaches, boundary-based versus texture-based FD calculations, and differences in image preprocessing pipelines. These inconsistencies may affect reproducibility and limit comparability across studies [12].

To date, there is a paucity of studies specifically evaluating the prognostic role of FD in cervical cancer MRI, and most existing research has focused either on conventional radiomic features or on FD in other tumor types. Therefore, the specific contribution of FD within the broader radiomics framework remains to be clearly defined [10, 12].

In this context, the present study aims to evaluate the prognostic significance of MRI-derived FD in patients with cervical cancer treated with definitive radiotherapy (RT) with or without concurrent chemotherapy. FD was also analyzed in relation to established clinical and pathological parameters, including hemoglobin level, HPV status, tumor volume, and FIGO stage, as well as survival outcomes, including local control, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). This study suggests that FD may serve as a simple, non-invasive, and reproducible imaging biomarker with potential utility in risk stratification and personalized treatment planning. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the early investigations evaluating the prognostic value of FD in cervical cancer MRI and contributes to the limited existing evidence in this field.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study included patients with biopsy-confirmed cervical carcinoma who underwent definitive RT or CCRT, between December 2019 and March 2025. Patients were excluded if they lacked evaluable pre-treatment pelvic MRI, had FIGO stage IVB disease, had received prior pelvic RT or any previous oncologic treatment. Four patients were excluded due to failure to complete definitive CCRT, including two due to chemotherapy-related bone marrow toxicity, one due to patient refusal, and one due to severe RT-related toxicity (diarrhea). Patients who did not complete definitive CCRT were excluded to reduce treatment heterogeneity and ensure a more homogeneous study population; however, this may introduce selection bias, as treatment completion status could be associated with clinical outcomes. Patients with a follow-up duration of less than 6 months were excluded to ensure standardized response assessment, as treatment response in cervical cancer is routinely evaluated at 6 months. Accordingly, all survival analyses were conducted within this selected cohort of patients with ≥ 6 months follow-up. This approach may introduce selection bias by excluding patients with early progression or mortality and may lead to overestimation of survival outcomes.

All patients were staged according to the 2018 FIGO classification. Pelvic MRI was performed after histopathological confirmation and before treatment initiation. Cases with poor image quality, motion artifacts, or inadequate tumor delineation were excluded from the final analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the 1st Medical Research Scientific and Ethics Evaluation Committee of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital (Decision No: TABED 1-1880-2025; Date: 19 November 2025). Ethical approval was obtained prior to data access and analysis, and all patient data were accessed only after approval had been granted.

A total of 84 patients who met the inclusion criteria and underwent pre-treatment pelvic MRI at our institution were included in the study. All MRI examinations were performed using standardized cervical imaging protocols on 3.0-T MRI scanners.

Image selection and ROI extraction

Tumor segmentation was not performed volumetrically. Instead, a 2D ROI-based approach was adopted using T2-weighted fast spin echo (FSE) images in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes. For each plane, the slice demonstrating the largest tumor cross-sectional area was selected.

A square ROI was placed within the central tumor region on each selected slice. The ROI encompassed approximately 40–60% of the tumor area to balance representativeness and methodological consistency while minimizing boundary effects and partial volume artifacts. Necrotic or cystic components were not explicitly excluded if present within the selected ROI. This approach was intentionally adopted to preserve intratumoral structural heterogeneity, as FD analysis aims to capture complexity arising from both viable and non-viable tumor components.

To account for variations in tumor size while ensuring methodological consistency, fixed ROI dimensions of 24 × 24, 32 × 32, 48 × 48, or 64 × 64 mm were selected according to tumor size and adjusted to the in-plane spatial resolution. This tumor-adapted ROI approach is consistent with radiomics principles emphasizing feature stability and reproducibility [16].

ROI placement was performed in consensus by two experienced radiation oncologists following a predefined protocol to enhance reproducibility. However, formal quantitative assessment of interobserver variability was not performed and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Although this 2D representative slice-based approach improves practicality and reproducibility, it may not fully capture the spatial heterogeneity of the entire tumor. Future studies incorporating 3D-dimensional volumetric segmentation may provide a more comprehensive assessment.

Image preprocessing and fractal analysis

MRI examinations were performed using the same 3.0-T system with a standardized T2-weighted protocol, and spatial resolution was consistent across patients, minimizing variability in pixel size. Images were analyzed in their native resolution without resampling, and ROI-based analysis was used to further reduce the impact of motion artifacts and partial volume effects.

All images were initially acquired in 16-bit DICOM format. DICOM files were subsequently converted to 8-bit TIFF format and normalized to grayscale prior to fractal analysis. This conversion was performed to ensure compatibility with the ImageJ-based fractal analysis workflow and to standardize grayscale intensity across all images. The transformation to an 8-bit scale reduces variability in intensity distribution, facilitates reproducibility, and aligns with previously published radiomics and fractal analysis methodologies. Although this process may lead to partial loss of gray-level information, normalization was applied to preserve relative intensity differences and maintain the structural characteristics relevant for fractal dimension analysis.

Gaussian filtering (σ = 35 pixels) was applied to reduce high-frequency noise and intensity inhomogeneity while preserving structural characteristics relevant for fractal analysis. The selection of this parameter was based on empirical optimization in line with prior fractal imaging studies.

Fractal analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.48; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to ensure standardized and reproducible image analysis, following a modified protocol based on previously published methodologies.

Fractal dimension measurement

FD was calculated using the box-counting algorithm implemented in ImageJ software. The analysis was performed using box sizes of 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 32, and 64 pixels. For each patient, FD values were independently derived from axial, coronal, and sagittal ROIs. Plane-specific FD values were calculated for each imaging orientation, and the mean FD across the three planes was used for all correlation and survival analyses. The use of mean FD across three orthogonal planes was preferred to reduce the influence of plane-dependent variability and to provide a more stable and representative estimate of tumor structural complexity, rather than relying on a single-plane measurement. FD values obtained from pre-treatment MRI were analyzed in relation to clinical outcomes, including local control, PFS, and OS. Associations between FD and HPV status as well as baseline hemoglobin levels were also evaluated.

Treatment and follow-up

All patients received definitive RT or CCRT in accordance with institutional treatment protocols. RT consisted of external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) delivering 45–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions, followed by high-dose-rate (HDR) intracavitary brachytherapy with a total dose of 24–30 Gy administered in 3–5 fractions. Concurrent chemotherapy with cisplatin- or carboplatin-based regimens was administered to eligible patients. Of the 84 patients, 81 (96.4%) received concurrent chemotherapy, while 3 patients (3.6%) were treated with RT alone due to advanced age, comorbidities, or poor performance status.

Tumor response of the primary lesion was assessed 6 months after completion of treatment using pelvic MRI and clinical examination and was classified as complete response or partial/no response according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. OS and PFS were calculated from the initiation of RT to death, disease progression, or last follow-up, as appropriate. Local control was defined as the absence of radiological progression at the primary tumor site. LR and DM were defined as radiological progression detected on MRI or PET-CT during routine follow-up, with histopathological confirmation obtained when available.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0). The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. FD values were summarized using median values for descriptive purposes, whereas mean FD values were used in correlation, regression, and survival analyses.

Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data distribution; for comparisons among three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Associations between continuous variables and ordinal or categorical parameters were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

To evaluate the independent prognostic value of FD, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed. Tumor volume was included as a surrogate for tumor burden. Given the limited sample size and number of events, the number of variables included in the model was restricted in accordance with recommended events-per-variable considerations to minimize overfitting.

FIGO stage was not included in the multivariable model due to the very small number of early-stage cases (n = 3), which could lead to unstable estimates. Other clinically relevant variables such as nodal status, performance status, and histological subtype were also excluded for the same reason.

Two multivariable Cox regression models were constructed for PFS. Model 1 included FD and tumor volume, whereas Model 2 additionally included treatment-related variables (number of chemotherapy cycles and overall treatment time).

Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All patients in the final study cohort, defined after application of the predefined exclusion criteria, contributed risk time and were appropriately censored in accordance with standard survival analysis methodology. FD and tumor volume were dichotomized using median values to reduce the risk of data-driven overfitting. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

A total of 84 patients with cervical cancer who received definitive RT or CCRT were included in the study. The median follow-up duration was 23 months (range, 6–70 months). The mean age was 53.3 ± 13.8 years. Most patients had locally advanced disease (FIGO stages IIB–IVA, 96.4%). The median tumor volume was 78 cm³ (interquartile range [IQR], 40–142). Distribution of fractal dimension

Descriptive statistics are presented using median values. The pretreatment FD derived from MRI ranged from 1.20 to 1.61, with a median value of 1.44 (interquartile range [IQR], 1.38–1.47). To facilitate clinical interpretation, FD values were scaled to 0.1-unit increments (FD_0.1) and used in subsequent analyses.

To evaluate the reproducibility and inter-plane consistency of FD measurements, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was performed across axial, sagittal, and coronal planes. The results demonstrated excellent agreement, with an ICC of 0.944 (95% CI: 0.919–0.962, p < 0.001), supporting the robustness and reliability of the FD measurements used in subsequent analyses.

Effect of fractal dimension on local recurrence and distant metastasis

The impact of FD on LR and DM was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. No statistically significant association was observed between a 0.1-unit increase in FD and the risk of LR (hazard ratio [HR], 1.094; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.426–2.812; p = 0.852) or DM (HR, 2.023; 95% CI, 0.461–8.870; p = 0.350).

Survival analyses

The effect of FD on OS was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. During follow-up, 18 patients (21.4%) died, while 66 patients (78.6%) were censored at the last follow-up. A 0.1-unit increase in FD was significantly associated with an increased risk of death (HR, 4.668; 95% CI, 2.035–10.708; p < 0.001). Overall model fit was confirmed by the omnibus test (χ² = 15.529; p < 0.001).

PFS analysis included all 84 patients. During follow-up, 34 patients (40.5%) experienced disease progression defined as LR, DM, or disease-related death while 50 patients (59.5%) were censored without progression. A 0.1-unit increase in FD was significantly associated with shorter PFS (HR, 4.769; 95% CI, 2.749–8.273; p < 0.001). The model was statistically significant based on the omnibus test (χ² = 34.409; p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences in OS or PFS were observed according to RT duration or the use of concurrent chemotherapy (p > 0.05).

In Model 1, which included FD and tumor volume, FD remained significantly associated with PFS after adjustment for tumor volume (HR = 4.84; 95% CI: 2.83–8.28; p < 0.001), whereas tumor volume was not significantly associated with PFS (p = 0.308).

In Model 2, which additionally included treatment-related variables (tumor volume, number of chemotherapy cycles, and overall treatment time), FD remained significantly associated with PFS (HR = 5.297; 95% CI: 2.138–13.123; p < 0.001), while tumor volume (p = 0.392), number of chemotherapy cycles (p = 0.768), and overall treatment time (p = 0.344) were not statistically significant (Table 4).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between FD, derived from pretreatment MRI, and clinical outcomes in patients with cervical cancer treated with definitive RT or CCRT. FD was evaluated as a quantitative imaging parameter reflecting tumor morphological complexity and heterogeneity. Currently, established clinical factors such as FIGO stage, tumor volume, lymph node involvement, and Hb level are widely used for prognostic assessment in cervical cancer [17]. However, these conventional parameters provide limited insight into the structural heterogeneity and underlying biological complexity of the tumor [18, 19].

In recent years, increasing interest has focused on imaging-based biomarkers aimed at addressing these limitations. Radiomic approaches, in particular, have demonstrated substantial potential in predicting PFS and OS by offering complementary prognostic information beyond traditional clinical variables [8, 20]. Within this context, fractal analysis represents a relatively simple, interpretable, and low-dimensional alternative to conventional radiomic techniques [14]. Higher FD values reflect increased architectural complexity, vascular irregularity, and scale-independent heterogeneity, features that are commonly associated with aggressive tumor biology and treatment resistance [14, 21].

In the present study, FD demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with tumor volume, suggesting that increasing tumor size is accompanied by greater morphological complexity. This finding is consistent with those of a large multicenter retrospective study in breast cancer (n = 1109), in which higher FD values were associated with increased tumor size, enhanced morphological complexity, and independent prognostic significance for survival [21]. These results support the concept that FD captures not only tumor burden but also geometric irregularity and intratumoral heterogeneity [22].

From a clinical perspective, FD represents a feasible and interpretable imaging biomarker. Unlike conventional size-based metrics, FD captures spatial complexity that is closely linked to tumor biology. Compared with high-dimensional radiomic models, FD is computationally simple, reproducible, and less dependent on complex feature extraction pipelines, although it should be considered complementary rather than a standalone biomarker [12].

HPV status is a well-established prognostic and predictive factor in cervical cancer, with HPV-positive tumors demonstrating improved response to CCRT and superior survival outcomes [23]. These differences are thought to arise from distinct molecular mechanisms, including altered cell-cycle regulation mediated by E6 and E7 oncoproteins, impaired DNA damage repair, local immunosuppression in the immune microenvironment, and metabolic remodeling [6]. Such molecular alterations may not be directly reflected in macroscopic tumor morphology or imaging-based measures of heterogeneity. This is because fractal analysis primarily captures features related to tissue architecture, vascular complexity, and scale-independent heterogeneity [14]. Therefore, HPV-related biological variations may not be directly reflected in macroscopic tumor structure, and the lack of a statistically significant relationship between fractal dimension and HPV/p16 status in our study is consistent with these findings.

Tumor hypoxia is a well-established negative prognostic factor in patients with cervical cancer treated with RT or CCRT [6]. Low Hb levels impair oxygen delivery to tumor tissue, thereby reducing radiosensitivity and contributing to treatment resistance [24]. Although Hb thresholds ranging from 10 to 12 g/dL are commonly used in clinical trials to evaluate anemia-related hypoxia and treatment response [24, 25]. However, Hb level is a systemic and time-dependent parameter and does not directly reflect the intrinsic structural characteristics of the tumor. Although low Hb levels have been reported to be associated with OS and PFS in several studies [7, 26], other investigations have suggested that Hb is not an independent prognostic factor and does not significantly differ between patients with and without recurrence [27, 28]. These conflicting findings may explain the lack of a statistically significant association between FD, which reflects tumor structural characteristics, and Hb level in our study.

Although this study did not demonstrate a direct effect of FD on the development of LR or DM, FD demonstrated statistically significant associations with OS and PFS in this cohort, suggesting its potential prognostic value. Notably, each 0.1-unit increase in FD was associated with a significant increase in the risks of death and disease progression, suggesting that FD may reflect imaging features associated with aggressive tumor biology. The lack of a significant association between FD and the risks of LR or DM may indicate that effective local and systemic treatments can achieve disease control [6, 29], whereas underlying tumor heterogeneity contributes primarily to systemic progression and adverse survival outcomes [21].

The lack of correlation between FD and HPV/p16 status or Hb level further supports its potential role as a complementary imaging biomarker reflecting tumor heterogeneity [6]. Consistent with previous reports, imaging-derived features such as FD appear to be more strongly associated with global clinical outcomes, including survival, than with isolated recurrence patterns [8, 18]. MRI-derived FD has been linked to tumor heterogeneity, invasion potential, and microscopic spread, all of which may contribute to disease progression and mortality [21]. Collectively, these findings suggest that FD may provide additional prognostic information alongside conventional clinical and pathological parameters [8, 30]. From a translational perspective, one of the main advantages of FD is its feasibility for integration into routine clinical workflows. FD can be derived from standard pretreatment MRI without additional imaging acquisition, increased cost, or substantial computational burden. This practical applicability enhances its potential clinical utility as a noninvasive imaging biomarker.

In this context, FD may support more refined risk stratification by identifying patients with aggressive tumor characteristics who may benefit from treatment intensification or closer follow-up. However, prospective validation and methodological standardization are required before FD can be routinely implemented in clinical decision-making.

These findings should be interpreted as a promising preliminary signal rather than definitive evidence, given the retrospective design and absence of both internal and external validation.

In addition, RT duration and the use of concurrent chemotherapy were not significantly associated with survival outcomes in the present cohort, suggesting that the observed association between FD and survival outcomes was consistent across models adjusted for the evaluated treatment-related variables, within the constraints of the available dataset. However, subgroup analyses were limited by the small number of patients treated with RT alone, which restricts the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding treatment subgroup effects.

However, this study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and relatively modest sample size may limit generalizability. The limited number of events also constrained the inclusion of multiple covariates in the multivariable model, which may introduce residual confounding. In addition, important prognostic variables such as nodal status, performance status, and histological subtype were not included due to sample size constraints. The single-center setting and absence of an external validation cohort further restrict broader applicability. Although MRI examinations were performed using a standardized 3.0-T protocol, the influence of imaging parameters on texture-based features cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, treatment-related details, including RT technique and brachytherapy fractionation, were not consistently available.

Patients with follow-up shorter than 6 months and those who did not complete definitive treatment were excluded as part of the predefined study design. While this approach improved cohort homogeneity and ensured standardized response assessment, it may introduce selection bias by excluding patients with early progression or poor outcomes. As a result, survival outcomes may be overestimated, and the observed associations between FD and survival endpoints should be interpreted with caution. This limitation reflects the retrospective design and constraints of the available dataset, which did not allow re-inclusion of these patients or additional sensitivity analyses.

Another limitation is the absence of a formal assessment of interobserver and intraobserver variability. In this study, ROI delineation was performed by consensus between two experienced radiation oncologists to ensure methodological consistency and reduce variability in contour placement. In addition, FD reflects global structural characteristics of tumor architecture, which may reduce sensitivity to minor local variations in ROI boundaries. The high average-measures ICC observed across axial, sagittal, and coronal planes (0.944; 95% CI, 0.919–0.962; p < 0.001) further supports the consistency of FD measurements. However, formal reproducibility analyses were not performed. Moreover, the use of a 2D ROI-based approach may not fully capture 3D tumor heterogeneity, and formal test–retest reproducibility was not assessed. Although tumor dimensions were available across imaging planes, their specific relationship with FD was not further explored and warrants further investigation, particularly in relation to brachytherapy planning. Finally, histological grading data were incomplete, precluding further subgroup analyses.

Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating volumetric 3D analyses and standardized imaging protocols are warranted. Integration of FD with multiparametric MRI, radiomic features, and molecular biomarkers may further improve prognostic modeling and support the development of robust imaging-based predictive frameworks.

Conclusions

Pretreatment MRI-based fractal analysis demonstrates prognostic relevance in cervical cancer by providing a quantitative assessment of tumor morphological complexity and heterogeneity. In the present study, FD was associated with both PFS and OS in patients treated with definitive RT or CCRT, and this association remained significant after adjustment for tumor volume and selected treatment-related variables. These findings suggest that FD may provide additional prognostic information beyond conventional tumor volume assessment. Accordingly, FD may represent a potentially useful noninvasive imaging biomarker for improved risk stratification and more individualized treatment planning. Given its computational simplicity and feasibility using routine MRI, FD may serve as a practical adjunct to current imaging-based assessment strategies. However, given the retrospective design, selected study population, and potential for selection bias, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating volumetric 3D analyses, along with integration of multiparametric MRI, radiomic features, and molecular biomarkers, are warranted to further clarify the clinical utility and generalizability of FD.
