Unconventional magnon-mediated spin torque enabled by ferroelectric domain engineering in multiferroic BiFeO3
Authors: Yuhan Liang, Xingyu Yan, Xiaoyu Jiang, Dingsong Jiang, Hao Bai, Zi-An Wang, Hetian Chen, Xiaofu Qiu, Daniel Pharis, Xiaoxi Huang, Rakshit Jain, Dingfu Shao, Wanjun Jiang, Di Yi, Daniel C. Ralph, Tianxiang Nan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spin current provides an energy-efficient approach for manipulating magnetization, when its spin polarization aligns with the magnetization direction. However, conventional spin-source materials possess high crystalline symmetry, restricting spin polarization to be orthogonal to both spin and charge current directions. Here, we overcome this limitation by utilizing the concept of magnon-mediated spin-orbit torque through integration of the insulating multiferroic BiFeO3 with a conventional spin-source material. We observe that spin polarization generated by conventional spin-source material can excite unconventional magnon polarization due to the interplay between cycloidal antiferromagnetic order and the ferroelectric domain structure in BiFeO3. This produces an unconventional magnon torque that allows deterministic, field‑free switching of in‑plane magnetization collinear with the current direction, unattainable with conventional spin-source materials. Our results establish multiferroic-based heterostructure as a symmetry‑engineered magnon spin source, paving the way for low-power spintronic devices.

Introduction

Spin currents, flows of spin angular momentum characterized by their spin polarization (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{\sigma }}}$$\end{document}σ) enable efficient manipulation of magnetization via spin-orbit torques1, crucial for developing emerging magnetic logic and memory devices2–6. Electron-mediated spin currents can be induced by the spin Hall effect7,8 (SHE) or Rashba-Edelstein effect9 (REE) in spin-source materials. Nevertheless, the most commonly used spin-source materials, such as polycrystalline heavy metals10, exhibit high crystalline symmetry, constraining spin polarization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{\sigma }}}$$\end{document}σ to be orthogonal to both the charge and spin current directions. Specifically, when a charge current (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${J}_{c}$$\end{document}Jc) flowing along the x-axis generates a spin current (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${J}_{m}$$\end{document}Jm) propagating along the z-axis, the spin polarization is confined strictly to the y-axis, producing only a conventional damping-like spin torque \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{\tau }}}}_{{DL}}^{y}\propto {{\bf{M}}}\times ({{\bf{y}}}\times {{\bf{M}}})$$\end{document}τDLy∝M×(y×M) (Fig. 1a). Alternatively, spin angular momentum can be carried by magnons, the collective excitations of magnetic orders, which efficiently propagate magnon currents (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${J}_{m}$$\end{document}Jm) through magnetically-ordered insulators over long distances without involving charge transport25–32. In heterostructures of spin-source/antiferromagnetic-insulator/ferromagnet, spin currents from a spin-source layer excite magnons in the antiferromagnetic insulator, generating a magnon current that subsequently exerts magnon torque on an adjacent ferromagnetic layer33–35. This approach presents an attractive pathway toward more energy-efficient spintronic devices36. More importantly, this magnon-mediated spin torque can leverage the symmetry of the antiferromagnetic layer, offering an additional degree of freedom for spin torque engineering. Recent studies have shown that the amplitude of magnon torque can be tuned by controlling the ferroelectric order in a heterostructure incorporating multiferroic BiFeO337–39. However, controlling the direction of magnon spin polarization to generate unconventional magnon torques remains unexplored.

In this work, we experimentally demonstrate that two-variant ferroelectric domains with distinct spin cycloid structure in BiFeO3 enable control over magnon spin polarization (Fig. 1c). In epitaxial SrRuO3/BiFeO3/Ni80Fe20 heterostructures, we observe efficient generation of unconventional magnon torque \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{\tau }}}}_{{DL}}^{x}\propto {{\bf{M}}}\times ({{\bf{x}}}\times {{\bf{M}}})$$\end{document}τDLx∝M×(x×M), with spin polarization rotated by up to 11° from the conventional y-axis direction dictated by symmetry. This unconventional magnon torque strongly depends on the orientation of the ferroelectric domains and is absent in samples with disordered domains, consistent with the symmetry analysis of BiFeO3, which possesses ferroelectrically coupled spin cycloid structures. We further demonstrate deterministic, field-free magnetization switching along the x-axis which is prohibited by symmetry with conventional spin torque. These results reveal that control of the ferroelectric domain structures in multiferroic materials provides a route to leverage hidden symmetries within the antiferromagnetic order, opening pathways for advanced functionalities in future magnon-based spintronic devices.

Measurement of unconventional magnon torque

To experimentally confirm the presence of unconventional magnon torque, we fabricated epitaxial heterostructures consisting of 11 nm SrRuO3/40 nm BiFeO3/5 nm Ni80Fe20 on orthorhombic (o) DyScO3 substrates with (110)o orientation (see Methods). The slight in-plane compressive strain introduced by the (110)o-oriented DyScO3 substrate leads to a well-defined, two-variant ferroelectric domain structure in BiFeO3, separated by 71° domain walls50. A bulk-like spin cycloid structure in the BiFeO3 thin film is stabilized, with propagation vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{k}}}}_{1}$$\end{document}k1 ([\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{1}\bar{1}0$$\end{document}1¯1¯0]) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{k}}}}_{2}$$\end{document}k2 ([\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\bar{1}0$$\end{document}11¯0]) corresponding to the two domain variants53. SrRuO3, exhibiting significant spin Hall conductivity, serves as the spin-source layer that excites magnons in the BiFeO354–57. The crystalline quality of the SrRuO3 and BiFeO3 layers was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the two-variant domain structure was characterized using reciprocal space mapping (RSM) and piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) (see Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with previous studies50. All spin torque measurements were performed at room temperature, well above the Curie temperature of SrRuO3 (~160 K).

Discussion

We have demonstrated an unconventional magnon torque in heterostructures based on multiferroic BiFeO3. By engineering the ferroelectric domain structure and leveraging the spin-cycloid ordering of BiFeO3, we reduced the system's symmetry and overcame the geometric constraints typically imposed on spin-torque polarization. Through systematic ST-FMR and second harmonic Hall measurements performed on SrRuO3/BiFeO3/Ni80Fe20 devices with a well-defined two-variant ferroelectric domain structure, we observed unconventional magnon torque with spin polarization rotated up to 11° away from the conventional symmetry-constrained y-axis. This symmetry-engineered unconventional magnon torque enables deterministic, field-free type-x magnetization switching.

Symmetry analysis indicates that controlling the crystallinity of BiFeO3 offers a route to flexibly modulate magnon spin polarization. In addition, the intrinsic switchability of ferroelectric polarization in BiFeO3 presents a pathway for non-volatile, electric-field control of these unconventional magnon torques. These findings establish a new platform for exploring advanced magnonic device functionalities enabled by multiferroic materials.

Methods

Sample growth

Epitaxial SrRuO3/BiFeO3 heterostructures were deposited on (110)o-oriented DyScO3 substrates using pulsed laser deposition (PLD) with a 248 nm KrF excimer laser. SrRuO3 and BiFeO3 were deposited at substrate temperatures of 670 °C and 700°C, under an oxygen partial pressure of 110 mTorr and 150mTorr, respectively. The laser fluence at the target surfaces was approximately 1.5 J/cm2 with a pulse repetition rate of 5-7 Hz. Subsequently, the samples were cooled to room temperature in an oxygen-rich atmosphere and transferred through air to a magnetron sputtering chamber with a base pressure of 1×10-8 Torr for the deposition of ferromagnetic metals. The ferromagnetic Ni80Fe20 layer was deposited at an argon pressure of 3 mTorr, followed by a 3 nm Ti capping layer to prevent oxidation. Film thicknesses were measured using X-ray reflectivity.

Device fabrication

Devices for ST-FMR and second-harmonic Hall measurements were fabricated on the same sample chip using standard photolithography, followed by argon-ion milling. Subsequently, 10 nm Ti / 150 nm Cu bilayers were deposited for electrode contacts using a standard lift-off process. ST-FMR devices were patterned into 16μm-wide and 80μm-long microstrips with ground-signal-ground (GSG) electrodes, while devices for second harmonic Hall measurements were patterned into 16μm-wide and 80μm-long Hall bars with 4μm-wide Hall contacts.

Magnon torque measurements

For ST-FMR measurements, a Keysight E8257D microwave generator was used to provide a 15 dBm microwave excitation, modulated by an internal oscillator at 1713 Hz. The mixing voltage signal was detected using a DSP 7265 lock-in amplifier. For second harmonic Hall measurements, an AC current at 2333 Hz was supplied by the internal voltage source of the DSP 7265 lock-in amplifier. Both the first- and second-harmonic Hall voltage signals were simultaneously recorded using a single DSP 7265. Magnetic fields for both ST-FMR and second harmonic Hall measurements were applied using a GMW projected-field magnet mounted on a Newport motion-controlled stage.

Magnon-torque-induced magnetization switching measurements

Field-free magnetization switching induced by magnon torque was measured using a probe station under zero applied magnetic field. Magnetization switching assisted by an out-of-plane magnetic field, as described in Supplementary Note 11, was performed in a Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS). All measurements were conducted at room temperature. A Keithley 6221 source meter was used to generate a single square wave with a 1 ms period and a 50% duty cycle, corresponding to a pulse duration of 500μs. Following the application of the current pulse, the system waited for 8 s before measuring the second-harmonic Hall voltage using a DSP 7265 lock-in amplifier at 2333 Hz and a current amplitude of 2 mA.
