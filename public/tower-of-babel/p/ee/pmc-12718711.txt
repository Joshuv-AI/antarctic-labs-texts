PM2.5 exposure induces transcriptomic changes in ARPE-19 cells with activation of TGFβ-mediated signaling pathways: A next-generation sequencing approach
Authors: Chueh-Tan Chen, Zhi-Hu Lin, Tung-Yi Lin, Yi-An Lin, Hsin Yeh, Wei-Jyun Hua, Ching-Yao Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has emerged as a critical environmental threat to ocular health; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms affecting the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) remain largely uncharacterized. This study aimed to investigate transcriptomic alterations in RPE cells following PM2.5 exposure and to identify key regulatory pathways involved.Methods:Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was used to investigate differential gene expression in ARPE-19 cells upon PM2.5 exposure. Bioinformatic analyses, including pathway enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), were performed to identify affected signaling cascades. Functional assays—including cell viability, wound healing, and Transwell migration—were conducted to evaluate phenotypic changes. Quantitative RT-PCR (Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction) and ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) validated gene expression and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) secretion. TGF-β stimulation and receptor inhibition were applied to dissect pathway involvement.Results:Comprehensive analysis revealed substantial changes in gene expression profiles, with pathway enrichment highlighting the activation of cell migration-related pathways such as focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, extracellular matrix (ECM)–receptor interaction, tight junction, and adherens junction. Notably, the TGF-β, MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase), and PI3K/AKT (Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase / Protein Kinase B) pathways were significantly modulated. Functional assays showed that PM2.5 exposure enhanced ARPE-19 cell viability and migratory capacity. Among the differentially expressed genes, angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4) was markedly upregulated following PM2.5 stimulation. Pharmacological inhibition of TGF-β signaling abrogated PM2.5-induced ANGPTL4 expression, suggesting a pivotal role of the TGF-β pathway in mediating these effects.Conclusion:These findings demonstrate that PM2.5 induces transcriptomic reprogramming and activates the TGF-β signaling cascade in RPE cells, thereby enhancing cellular migration. Specifically, ANGPTL4 was identified as a key downstream effector of this pathway. This study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms by which air pollution contributes to retinal disease pathogenesis and suggests potential therapeutic targets for preventing PM2.5-induced retinal injury.Keywords: ANGPTL4, PM2.5, Retinal pigment epithelium cell, TGF-β

1. INTRODUCTION

Air pollution is a major global environmental concern with profound impacts on human health.1 Among various pollutants, particulate matter (PM) constitutes a complex mixture of airborne solid and liquid particles originating from industrial emissions, vehicular exhaust, and natural sources.2 Particle size is a key determinant of toxicity; fine particulate matter (PM2.5), defined as particles with diameters less than 2.5 μm, can penetrate deep into the alveoli and enter systemic circulation, eliciting diverse biological effects. Epidemiological evidence increasingly demonstrates that chronic PM2.5 exposure elevates the risk of ocular, cardiovascular, respiratory, and neurodegenerative diseases.2,3

The detrimental effects of long-term PM2.5 exposure on the ocular system have gained growing attention.4,5 Although most studies have focused on anterior segment disorders such as keratitis, conjunctivitis, and dry eye disease, the retina—particularly the macular region—is also highly susceptible to environmental insults. Recent studies indicates that PM2.5 contributes to macular degeneration through mechanisms including oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and genetic or epigenetic alterations.6–8 The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) is crucial for maintaining retinal integrity and visual function, and emerging evidence identifies PM2.5 as a risk factor for retinal disease. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying PM2.5-induced RPE dysfunction remain incompletely understood. Previous studies suggest that PM2.5 can trigger oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation in RPE cells, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, thereby disrupting cellular homeostasis.9–11 These pathological changes are thought to contribute to the early stages of macular diseases, including age-related macular degeneration (AMD).

Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) is a key mediator in the pathogenesis of AMD.12 During disease progression, TGF-β signaling promotes choroidal neovascularization (CNV), fibrosis, and EMT in RPE cells, ultimately leading to irreversible vision loss.13 TGF-β operates through both canonical (Smad-dependent) and non-canonical (Smad-independent) pathways.14,15 In the canonical pathway, TGF-β binds to its receptors (TGF-βR), activating Smad transcription factors that regulate genes associated with fibrosis, ECM production, and EMT-related transcriptional programs.16,17 This sequence of events drives RPE cells to lose epithelial characteristics, acquire mesenchymal traits, and gain enhanced migratory capability, thereby accelerating macular structural damage.18 In addition, TGF-β can activate noncanonical pathways, including MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase), and PI3K/AKT (Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase / Protein Kinase B) signaling cascades, which regulate cell survival, cytoskeletal remodeling, and inflammatory responses.19 These pathways are closely linked to endothelial cell migration, CNV formation, and the fibrotic complications observed in late-stage AMD.12,20 Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting TGF-β signaling may offer promising avenues for AMD intervention.

Given the critical role of transcriptomic regulation in cellular responses, we hypothesized that PM2.5 exposure could induce significant transcriptional alterations in RPE cells. In this study, next-generation sequencing (NGS) was used to systematically investigate the transcriptomic changes induced by PM2.5 exposure in ARPE-19 cells, with a focus on key signaling pathways such as TGF-β and PI3K/AKT that may mediate PM2.5-related retinal injury.

2. METHODS

2.1. Materials

PM2.5 (NIST® SRM® 1650b; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was generated under tightly controlled laboratory conditions from a heavy-duty diesel engine, with particles collected over 200 hours using a heat exchanger. Its chemical complexity—comprising well-quantified polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), nitro-PAHs, elemental and organic carbon fractions, and trace metals—makes it a representative surrogate for ambient diesel-derived PM2.5.21,22 PM2.5 was suspended in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Invitrogen-Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) at 25 mg/mL and freshly diluted to target concentrations in culture medium immediately before use. The Alamar Blue solution was purchased from Bio-Rad (Berkeley, CA, USA). Transwell® filters (Millicell®; MCEP24H48; Lot# M151278) were obtained from EMD Millipore Corporation. Human transforming growth factor-β1 (TGFβ; H8541; Lot# MKBT8035) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (RR037A; Lot#AO52250A) was purchased from Takara Bio Inc. (Japan). TOOLS 2xSYBR qPCR Mix (FPT-BB05; Lot#TA0826A01) was purchased from (BIOTOOLS Co., New Taipei city, Taiwan). All primers were synthesized by (PURIGO Biotechnology Co., Ltd., New Taipei city, Taiwan).

2.2. Cell lines and cell culture

The human retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19 (ATCC, Manassas, VA) was cultured in DMEM/F12 (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid [HEPES]: 15 mM, sodium pyruvate: 0.055 g/L, glucose: 3.15 g/L; Invitrogen-Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, MA), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Biological Industries, Cromwell, CT), 2 mM L-glutamine (Biological Industries), 1% nonessential amino acids (NEAA; Biological Industries), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (all from Biological Industries) were purchased via Bertec Enterprise Co., Ltd., New Taipei City, Taiwan. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

2.3. RNA isolation and NGS analysis

Total RNA was extracted from ARPE-19 cells using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) reagent and purified according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer, and integrity was assessed via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, with only samples having RIN >7.0 were selected. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) quantified with Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay, and checked for size distribution using the Bioanalyzer. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, yielding ~40 million reads per sample. Raw reads were quality-checked and trimmed using Trimmomatic, aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2, and read counts were quantified using feature counts. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2, applying thresholds of |log₂FC| >1 and false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05.

2.4. Pathway enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis were identified in R (v4.1.0) using the DESeq2 package (v1.30.1). Genes with an absolute log₂ fold change greater than 1 and a Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted p value below 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Functional annotation and enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were carried out using the iDEP platform (ver. 0.91),23 applying an adjusted p value cutoff of 0.05 for significance. To complement this analysis, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, v4.1.0) was applied to a preranked gene list—ordered by log₂ fold change—to uncover broader pathway-level perturbations, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05.

2.5. Cell viability

Cell viability was measured using the Alamar Blue assay (Bio-Rad, Berkeley, CA). ARPE-19 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 5 × 10⁴ cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 hours. Cells were then treated with PM2.5 at concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 200 µg/mL for 24 or 48 hours. Following treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing Alamar Blue reagent diluted 1:10, and cells were incubated for 4 hours at 37°C. Subsequently, 100 µL of the reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate and incubated for an additional 2 hours to allow full dye reduction. Fluorescence was measured at 450/570 nm using a TECAN Sunrise™ ELISA Reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated controls.

2.6. Wound healing assay

ARPE-19 cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁴ cells/well in 12-well plates and grown to confluence over 24 hours. Two parallel scratches were created in the monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip guided by a plastic ruler. Cells were immediately treated with PM2.5 (100 µg/mL). Wound margins were imaged at 0 and 24 hours using a Carl Zeiss light microscope (Carl Zeiss Canada, North York, ON, Canada) equipped with Zen software. Three random fields per well were analyzed, and wound closure was quantified as the percentage reduction in wound area using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD). All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

2.7. Migratory assay

Cell motility was assessed using a modified Boyden-chamber (Transwell®) assay. ARPE-19 cells were pretreated with 100 or 200 µg/mL PM2.5 for 24 hours, harvested, and resuspended at 5 × 10⁴ cells per 200 µL in serum-free medium. The cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 300 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. Cells were allowed to migrate for 24 hours at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed using a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed in 70% methanol for 8 min, air-dried, and stained with a 1:20 dilution of crystal violet solution. Quantification was performed by counting stained cells in five randomly selected microscopic fields per insert.

2.8. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for TGF-β

Macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 hours. Cells were then treated with PM2.5 (12.5-200 μg/mL) for an additional 24 hours. Culture supernatants were collected for cytokine analysis. Secreted TGF-β levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Cat. 88-8350-88; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm with a 570 nm reference filter using a TECAN Sunrise™ ELISA Reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). Cytokine levels in treated samples were normalized to the control group (vehicle only), which was defined as 100%.

2.9. RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

ARPE-19 cells were treated with PM2.5 (100 or 200 µg/mL), TGF-β (10 ng/mL) and/or TGF-β inhibitor (10 μM) for 24 hours. Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) or a Quick-RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research). cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). The qPCR of the obtained cDNA was carried out with a Thermo QuantStudio3 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using TOOLS 2xSYBR qPCR Mix Kit (TOOLS, Taiwan). GAPDH expression served as an internal control. Relative expression of the target gene vs that of GAPDH was determined using 2−ΔΔCT method (ΔΔCT = (CtTarget gene − CtGAPDH) − (CtTarget gene−CtGAPDH)); Ct: cycle number when the fluorescence value of the sample is equal to the threshold value). All primers are listed in Table 1.

2.10. Statistics

Statistical analyses were carried out in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Differences between two groups were evaluated by unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. A *p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

3.1. PM2.5 induces transcriptomic profile changes in ARPE-19 cells

Next-generation sequencing was performed to assess transcriptomic alterations in ARPE-19 cells following PM2.5 exposure. As shown in Fig. 1A, heatmap analysis revealed a clear segregation of gene expression profiles between the control and PM2.5-treated groups. The volcano plot in Fig. 1B illustrated the extent of differential expression, highlighting numerous genes with both statistically significant p values and substantial fold changes. Red dots indicate genes meeting both criteria, emphasizing the broad transcriptomic response induced by PM2.5. These results suggest that PM2.5 triggers substantial alterations in gene expression patterns.

To further explore the potential biological relevance of these changes, bioinformatic analyses were conducted to evaluate disease associations (Fig. 1C). This analysis revealed correlations between DEGs and multiple pathological conditions, particularly various types of cancer. Notably, ocular diseases, including open-angle glaucoma and primary open-angle glaucoma, emerged as significantly enriched categories, suggesting that PM2.5 exposure may impact not only cancer-related pathways but also ocular degenerative disorders.

Subsequently, network analysis of significantly enriched pathways was performed. DEGs mapped to canonical pathways, summarized in Table 2, with Fig. 1D highlighting major affected pathways such as TGF-β signaling, MAPK signaling, PI3K-AKT, and Hippo pathways. Additionally, pathways involved in cytoskeletal regulation, ECM–receptor interaction, cell adhesion, and tight junction integrity were prominently represented, suggesting that PM2.5 may disrupt cellular structure and migratory function, consistent with previous findings.9,24

3.2. PM2.5 increases migration of ARPE-19 cells

To evaluate the effects of PM2.5 on RPE cells, cell viability, morphology, and migration capacity were assessed in ARPE-19 cells treated with varying concentrations of PM2.5. As shown in Fig. 2A, cell viability increased following 24- and 48-hour exposure. However, cell counting revealed that the total cell number was only slightly elevated after 48 hours of PM2.5 treatment (Fig. 2B). Phase-contrast microscopy further revealed morphological alterations in ARPE-19 cells exposed to increasing PM2.5 doses (Fig. 2C). Specifically, treated cells displayed a more elongated and irregular morphology compared to controls, suggesting cytoskeletal remodeling and stress responses induced by PM2.5. Given the potential effects on cytoskeletal dynamics and cell motility (Fig. 1D), a wound healing assay was performed. As shown in Fig. 2D, PM2.5-treated cells exhibited accelerated wound closure over 24 hours relative to controls. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in wound closure in the PM2.5 group. Complementing this, a Transwell migration assay demonstrated that PM2.5 treatment significantly increased the number of migrating cells (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these findings indicate that PM2.5 exposure not only maintains cell viability but also enhances RPE cell motility.

3.3. PM2.5 induces genetic changes correlated with enrichment of the TGF-β Signaling Pathway

Comprehensive bioinformatic analysis of PM2.5-mediated DEGs (Table 2) pinpointed the TGF-β signaling cascade—long implicated in cellular migration—as the foremost pathway perturbed by PM2.5 exposure (Fig. 3A). Enrichment analyses revealed significant upregulation of TGF-β ligands and receptors, including TGF-β1 and TGF-βRI/II, alongside elevated expression of downstream effectors such as p107, E2F4/5, DP1, and the proto-oncogene c-Myc (Fig. 3B). Concurrently, negative-feedback modulators including Smurf1/2, Clu1, and Skp1 were also induced, indicating activation of intrinsic inhibitory circuits. In contrast, transcripts encoding extracellular TGF-β antagonists—namely latent TGF-β binding protein 1 (LTBP1) and activin—were markedly downregulated (Fig. 3C), further biasing signaling toward pathway activation. To validate these transcriptomic findings at the protein level, ELISA was performed on conditioned medium from ARPE-19 cells, revealing a dose-dependent increase in secreted TGF-β following PM2.5 treatment (Fig. 3D). Together, these results demonstrate that PM2.5 exposure amplifies TGF-β signaling and implicates this pathway in the enhanced migratory phenotype observed in ARPE-19 cells.

3.4. PM2.5-induced TGF-β signaling-mediated multiple molecules

In addition to TGF-β pathway molecules, the 10 most strongly PM2.5-induced transcripts were identified, led by ANGPTL4, MMP1, CRYBG1, CHAC1, CSF2, ESM1, WNK4, IL6R, HSPA6, and MMP3 (Fig. 4A). Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed a concentration-dependent increase in each transcript relative to untreated controls (p < 0.001 for all genes at 200 µg/mL; Fig. 4B). Given that PM2.5 also increased TGF-β levels,10 we next investigated whether TGF-β signaling mediated this transcriptional effect. ARPE-19 cells were treated with recombinant TGFβ1 (10 ng/mL). As shown in Fig. 4C, several genes were induced by TGF-β (p < 0.05). Co-treatment with the selective TGF-β receptor I inhibitor SB431542 (10 µM) significantly attenuated the expression of ANGPTL4, CRYBG1, CSF2, ESM1, HSPA6, and MMP3 to levels below basal controls (Fig. 4C). Among these, ANGPTL4 expression decreased by up to 84%, suggesting that it is a key downstream target of TGF-β signaling in the context of PM2.5 exposure. Interestingly, WNK4 behaved differently: TGF-β1 slightly increased WNK4 mRNA, whereas SB431542 paradoxically caused a further elevation of WNK4 transcripts (Fig. 4D). Moreover, SB431542 significantly abolished the migration caused by PM2.5 (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PM2.5 regulates multiple pathways in ARPE-19 cells, with a significant subset of transcriptional responses mediated through TGFβ1-dependent signaling.

4. DISCUSSION

In the present study, we employed NGS to comprehensively profile transcriptomic alterations in ARPE-19 cells following exposure to PM2.5. We identified a subset of ten DEGs most robustly induced by PM2.5, several of which are directly implicated in the TGF-β signaling cascade. Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis revealed that PM2.5 exposure upregulated key components of the TGF-β axis, including TGF-β, TGF-βRI, and TGF-βRII, as well as non-canonical mediators such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathway members. Functionally, we confirmed that PM2.5 enhanced ARPE-19 cell migration in vitro, and that this effect could be attenuated by pharmacological inhibition of TGF-β receptor activity. In addition to promoting ARPE-19 cell migration, PM2.5 exposure enhanced cell viability as measured by the Alamar Blue assay but showed minimal effect in the CCK-8 assay, as reported previously.9,10,25 This discrepancy may be due to differences in culture medium composition or the detection principles underlying Alamar Blue26 and CCK-827 assays. Whether PM2.5 enhances mitochondrial function remains to be determined. Collectively, our data demonstrate that PM2.5 promotes aberrant retinal pigment epithelial cell migration through activation of the TGFβ-mediated network, thereby providing mechanistic insight beyond previously reported observations of EMT induction and elevated TGF-β expression in ARPE-19 cells.9,10

RNA-Seq has emerged as a powerful tool for unbiased characterization of transcriptomic dynamics, offering higher sensitivity and a wider dynamic range compared to microarrays. However, no prior studies have conducted transcriptomic analyses on the effects of PM2.5, nor dissected the specific downstream gene networks involved. This study addresses these gaps by (i) identifying a comprehensive set of PM2.5-responsive genes using NGS, (ii) elucidating novel targets such as ANGPTL4 within the TGF-β axis, and (iii) integrating transcriptomic data with functional analyses to detail the mechanisms of PM2.5 exposure and TGFβ-driven migration. Specifically, our data suggest that PM2.5 upregulates positive regulators of TGF-β signaling, such as TGF-β1 and TGF-βRs, while downregulating negative-feedback inhibitors including Smurf1/2, LTBP1, and activin. The coordinated increase in transcription factors p107, E2F4/5, and DP1 suggests crosstalk between TGF-β signaling and cell cycle control,28,29 potentially promoting migration by regulating cytoskeletal dynamics and matrix remodeling.30 These findings are consistent with previous reports that PM2.5 induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and mitochondrial damage, which in turn activate TGFβ/Smad/ERK/p38 signaling, ultimately leading to EMT in ARPE-19 cells.10,25 Furthermore, PM2.5 upregulates MMP1 and MMP3 transcripts, thereby potentially enhancing EMT and promoting cell migration.9,31 These multilevel changes highlight a feed-forward loop in which PM2.5-triggered TGFβ activation promotes downstream effectors, thereby amplifying migratory behavior. Overall, this transcriptome-wide approach revealed a broader PM2.5-responsive gene network in retinal pigment epithelial cells. PM2.5 exposure significantly altered gene expression in ARPE-19 cells, with enrichment primarily in cancer-related diseases and retinal diseases. Importantly, bioinformatic analysis highlighted pathways associated with ECM organization, focal adhesion, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and the prominent TGF-β pathway (Fig. 1D). Retinal diseases such as AMD and glaucoma share key pathways with cancer, including inflammation, EMT, angiogenesis, and oxidative stress. In particular, the TGF-β signaling plays a pivotal role in these important mechanisms.32–35 This mechanistic overlap suggests that PM2.5 may promote retinal degeneration through molecular cascades similar to those observed in oncogenesis.

AMD is characterized by aberrant RPE cell dysfunction, subretinal fibrosis, and chronic inflammation. Environmental exposure to PM2.5 has been shown to enhance RPE cell migration and induce EMT, a key event in pathological retinal remodeling. These changes may contribute to AMD progression by promoting subretinal fibrosis and CNV,36,37 yet the mechanistic understanding remains limited. The present findings, showing upregulation of 10 genes (ANGPTL4, MMP1, CRYBG1, CHAC1, CSF2, ESM1, WNK4, IL6R, HSPA6, and MMP3) in ARPE-19 cells exposed to PM2.5, shed light on potential molecular intermediaries through which airborne particulates compromise RPE homeostasis. In particular, we found that TGF-β regulates ANGPTL4, CSF2, ESM1, MMP3, and CRYBG1, which may be relevant to macular degeneration.38,39 Moreover, WNK4 was expressed in high abundance when TGF-β signaling was inhibited (Fig. 4C), implying a pivotal role for WNK4 in the regulatory pathway governing PM2.5-induced TGF-β signaling. Nonetheless, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this interaction remain to be clarified. A common denominator among these genes is their regulation by TGF-β, a cytokine that governs cell survival, ECM remodeling, and immune modulation in ocular tissues. Our findings may provide a plausible molecular framework: PM2.5-induced TGFβ activation in RPE cells may promote cell migration and contribute to AMD pathogenesis. Notably, this study provides the first evidence that PM2.5 exposure markedly upregulates ANGPTL4 expression in RPE cells, with a positive correlation to TGFβ expression. Unlike prior reports in which hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) was shown to transcriptionally enhanced both vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and ANGPTL4 in neovascular lesions,40,41 our findings suggest that PM2.5 directly amplifies ANGPTL4 levels. ANGPTL4 may act synergistically with VEGF to promote CNV and vascular hyperpermeability, and further exacerbate subretinal fibrosis through endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT).42 Elevated aqueous ANGPTL4 concentrations have been associated with larger choroidal neovascular (CNV) lesion areas and more frequent intravitreal anti-VEGF injections.38 Patients requiring monthly dosing exhibit significantly higher ANGPTL4 levels compared with those on extended dosing intervals, underscoring its potential as a biomarker for personalized treatment stratification. Future studies are needed to further dissect the role of ANGPTL4 in ARPE-19 cells.

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. First, it remains unclear whether all forms of PM2.5 elicit the same response in ARPE-19 cells. Given the compositional complexity of PM2.5—including organic compounds, elemental carbon, PAHs, and trace metals—it is challenging to identify an appropriate inert particle as a control. Future study will explore whether compositionally inert particles can be identified or formulated to test whether they induce comparable biological responses and thereby help delineate the specific mechanisms mediated by the diesel-derived PM2.5 used in this study. Second, whether the PM2.5 concentrations used in vitro accurately reflect those encountered in vivo requires further clarification. Although the exact concentration of PM2.5 reaching the retina in vivo remains uncertain, previous studies have applied comparable or even higher doses in retinal and pulmonary models to simulate chronic exposure scenarios. For instance, a study in Atlanta, Georgia, showed that the 24-hour personal PM2.5 exposure among COPD patients in Atlanta, Georgia, ranged from 1.81 to 154.58 μg/m³, with a geometric mean of 14.12 μg/m³. Assuming an average daily inhalation volume of 20 m³/d, this equates to approximately 282.4 μg/d of PM2.5 exposure.43 Moreover, recent studies have employed similar concentrations to examine PM2.5-induced effects on ocular cells, including human corneal epithelial and ARPE-19 retinal pigment epithelial cells.8–10,44 Thus, the concentration range used in our study aligns with established models and serves to approximate relevant exposure conditions.

Third, although ARPE-19 cells are a convenient model of the RPE, they may not fully recapitulate the functional complexity of primary retinal pigment epithelial cells. Fourth, by focusing primarily on transcriptional changes associated with cell motility, we did not investigate how PM2.5 may influence immune responses within the retinal microenvironment. Finally, possible crosstalk between PM2.5 and other retinal cell types—such as choroidal endothelial cells and microglia—remains unexplored and warrants further study.

Overall, these findings suggest that PM2.5 exposure triggers broad transcriptomic reprogramming in retinal pigment epithelial cells, activating signaling pathways commonly associated with TGF-β signaling. Collectively, the data provide critical molecular insights into how environmental pollutants such as PM2.5 may contribute to ocular and systemic disease by modulating of key signaling networks. This study underscores the vulnerability of RPE cells to environmental insults and identifies the TGFβ/ANGPTL4 axis as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating PM2.5-induced retinal injury.
