Enhancing organ and vascular contrast in preclinical ultra-low field MRI using superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles
Authors: Sheng Shen, Neha Koonjoo, Thomas Boele, Jiaqi Lu, David E J Waddington, Marie Zhang, Matthew S Rosen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuperparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) are characterized by their exceptional susceptibility and relaxivity at ultra-low field (ULF) regimes, make them a promising contrast agent (CA) for ULF MRI. Despite their distinct advantages, the translation of these properties into clinically valuable image contrast in ULF MRI remains underexplored. In this study, we investigate the use of SPIONs to generate in vivo MRI contrast at 6.5 mT within the organs and vascular system of rodents. This investigation includes comprehensive SPION characterization and phantom imaging experiments to validate the utility of SPIONs to produce positive image contrast and to facilitate phase-sensitive imaging at ULF. Optimized balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) and spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) MRI sequences are used to generate in vivo contrast by leveraging the distinctive properties of SPIONs at ULF. Imaging studies in rodents reveal positive organ contrast attainable in magnitude images, and MRI phase maps can be used to visualize the vascular system. This work demonstrates the effectiveness of SPIONs in enhancing preclinical organ and vascular imaging at ULF; it bridges the gap between the study of the distinctive physical properties of SPIONs and the demonstration of in vivo image contrast.Subject terms: Translational research, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Although most MRI examinations are conducted at high field strengths (above 1.5 T), the last five years have brought recognition of the need for more accessible and affordable MR scanners operating at lower field strengths1. Typically, these scanners operate at a main magnetic field strength below 100 mT and are referred to as low-field (LF, <100 mT) or ultra-low field (ULF, <10 mT) systems2. Low-field scanners offer advantages in terms of affordability, portability, and ease of deployment1–5. In 2020, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved a commercial 64 mT system from Hyperfine, Inc. which has been used for bedside neuroimaging and more recently it was employed for stroke diagnosis in neonates in an intensive care unit5–8. Current research in the realm of low-field MRI is exploring performance advances and expanding application horizons in novel clinical contexts9,10.

Contrast agents (CAs) are widely recognized for their outstanding capability to enhance MRI thus supporting the development of new MRI-based diagnostic techniques. Roughly 25% of all clinical MRI examinations use injected CAs as a diagnostic imaging tool11. These contrast agents modulate the proton relaxation times of nearby water molecules, which in turn contribute to improved image contrast. Based on their physicochemical characteristics in vivo, these CAs distribute in the blood and extravascular-extracellular space. For vascular-related pathological conditions such as inflammatory/infectious disorders, tumors or stroke, CAs can provide information on location, the disease progression, treatment planning and response to therapy12–15. Contrast-enhanced MRI is an universally applied technique which plays a crucial role in augmenting MRI contrast, and provides novel biomarkers12, particularly in breast13 and early-stage tumor imaging scenarios14,15. Given the wide utility of CAs in conventional high-field MRI, there is substantial interest surrounding their use in LF and ULF MRI. The behavior of CA-modulated contrast enhancement at LF and ULF is complicated, given that both the in situ relaxivities of CAs, as well as the T1 relaxation rate of a given tissue, have a very strong dependence on magnetic field strength16,17.

CAs can either enhance MR signals (positive CAs) or reduce the MR signal (negative CAs)18,19. Clinical professionals generally prefer positive CAs because they do not compromise the underlying tissue contrast, and the explicit image contrast enhancement is attributed directly to the contrast agent itself, whereas the cause of negative contrast can be ambiguous and could be loss of signal linked to artifacts or void20,21. Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) are the most widely applied positive CAs in clinical practice11,22. However, with the widespread use of GBCAs, concerns have arisen about the accumulation of GBCAs in organs, especially the brain, and some severe side effects such as fatal nephrogenic systemic fibrosis have been identified23–26. Alternatively, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) approved by the FDA as an iron replacement therapy for patients with anemia are also being used as a CAs27,28. SPIONs have generated significant interest among clinicians and researchers from a more bio-compatible alternative to GBCAs. SPIONs, however, are typically utilized as negative CAs in conventional MRI29,30 with some use as dual CAs investigated31,32.

Ultra-low field MRI presents new opportunities for the application of SPIONs. The relaxivity of SPIONs exhibits a magnetic field dependency33 that becomes particularly advantageous in ULF settings. At ultra-low magnetic fields, the relaxivity of SPIONs is significantly greater compared to higher magnetic fields (>1.0 T), and the ratio of the transverse-to-longitudinal SPION relaxivity approaches unity34–37. These two properties make SPIONs a promising candidate for the generation of positive contrast at ULF18. Additionally, SPIONs maintain their high magnetic susceptibility at ULF while the magnetic susceptibility of the human body becomes negligible12,37; this results in essentially background-free susceptibility contrast, a valuable property for susceptibility-related MRI. The enhanced relaxivity of SPIONs at ULF has been demonstrated through sample characterization and in phantom imaging35–37. Our previous work37 has also explored the impact of SPIONs on the MRI signal at ULF and developed a high-sensitivity approach using pulse sequences with high sensitivity to frequency-shifted off-resonance signals. These findings provide substantial evidence for the potential of SPIONs in ULF MRI. However, further investigation is necessary for translating the exceptional properties of SPIONs at ULF to clinically accepted in vivo contrast, and to establish the diagnostic potential of SPIONs in ULF MRI.

Here, we characterize and compare five different classes of SPIONs and explore their efficacy in rodents in vivo to enhance contrast in ULF MRI. Building upon our previous work37, we conducted phantom imaging experiments on the SPIONs using both balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) and spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) pulse sequences, which produce T2/T1-weighted and T1-weighted image contrast, respectively. The effectiveness of the different SPIONs in producing positive contrast is evaluated in T2/T1-weighted MR images and in T1-weighted MR images. Furthermore, from the reconstructed SPGR phase maps, we demonstrated the substantial phase contrast attributable to the high susceptibility of SPIONs. Finally, we carried out in vivo SPION-enhanced imaging showing positive contrast in the spleen and pancreas, negative contrast in the liver of rats in both T2/T1-weighted and T1-weighted MRI images, and visualizing the circulatory systems of rats at ultra-low field with SPION-enhanced MRI phase maps.

Results

Properties of SPIONs at ultra-low magnetic field

Four SPION samples with different particle size distributions and/or different surface coatings were obtained from Imagion Biosystems (San Diego, CA, USA). We conducted nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxivity and magnetic susceptibility measurements to characterize their properties at 6.5 mT (see Table 1). The commercially available SPION-based drug ferumoxytol (Feraheme) was used for comparison. Each SPION was diluted in either deionized (DI) water or in a 1% agarose-DI mixture. Susceptibility measurements were conducted on SPION samples at a concentration of 100 μg (Fe3O4)/ml. The relaxivity and susceptibility values of SPIONs in different aqueous environments are shown in Table 1. The results indicate that the relaxivity and susceptibility are primarily influenced by the core size of the SPIONs, with both parameters increasing with core size. At the same time, they are also affected by the SPION coatings and solvents. Notably, the transverse-to-longitudinal relaxivity ratios of all SPIONs closely approach unity, indicating the suitability of SPIONs as positive contrast agents18,38,39.

SPION-enhanced positive-contrast imaging and phase-sensitive imaging in phantoms

To demonstrate the advantages of using SPIONs as a CA in ULF MRI, we conducted a series of phantom imaging experiments with the objective of generating diverse contrast by taking advantage of the unique relaxivity and susceptibility properties of SPIONs. Expanding on our previous work37, we employed a 3D-bSSFP sequence to produce T2/T1-weighted images and utilized a 3D-SPGR sequence to establish T1-weighted contrast, which is widely accepted as positive contrast in clinical settings; furthermore, we reconstructed phase maps from the SPGR scans and used them to assess the susceptibility effect of SPION on MRI signals.

SPION-enhanced positive contrast imaging was performed using a bSSFP sequence. The MRI signal acquired using bSSFP sequences is determined by various factors, including the local homogeneity of the main magnetic field, the magnetic resonance relaxation properties of the imaged objects (i.e., T1 and T2), and the acquisition parameters (such as flip angle and repetition time)39. The simulated transverse magnetization profiles of our bSSFP sequence obtained using Eq. (2) (see Materials and methods) are shown in Fig. 1. For three selected excitation flip angles at 30°, 60°, and 90°, the magnitude profile was plotted against Larmor frequency offset37,40. Each of the three flip angles produces distinct MR signal patterns as a function of off-resonance frequency, indicating that bSSFP-based image contrast can be modulated by utilizing CAs that are able to change the local magnetic field distribution and by varying the acquisition parameters (i.e., flip angle). The steady-state MRI signal obtained from the bSSFP sequence is highly sensitive to magnetic field inhomogeneity, which typically precludes its use over wide fields of view in high-field MRI41. However, at ULF, we leverage this feature to create contrast intentionally in SPION-enhanced MRI as the local accumulation of SPIONs will alter the local magnetic field due to their superparamagnetism, resulting in MRI signal changes.

To demonstrate control of contrast resulting from local magnetic field shifts and choice of flip angles, we scanned a phantom (Fig. 2a) comprised of a cylindrical container filled with DI water surrounding a sample tube containing an agarose-SPION mixture (25 nm, PEG20K) at a SPION concentration of 100 μg (Fe3O4)/ml. The main magnetic field B0 of the scanner is perpendicular to the axis of the cylindrer phantom. Figure 2b–d shows bSSFP scans acquired at 30°, 60°, or 90°, alongside corresponding simulated MRI images (Fig. 2e–g). We plot the pixel values in two regions labeled P1 and P2 for each of the simulated and acquired data (Fig. 2h, i). The region P1 is close to the SPION sample and the MRI signal in this region is strongly influenced by the high SPION susceptibility. The region P2 is situated at the periphery of the field of view and is presumed to be unaffected by local SPION susceptibility.

In all images, the central regions are markedly impacted by the high susceptibility of the SPIONs, and the distorted magnetic field from the high magnetic susceptibility of the sample creates a pattern of bright and dark stripes in the images. For bSSFP with a flip angle of 30°, the overall MRI signal intensity is reduced but local magnetic field shifts near SPIONs generate regions of hyperintense signal. We could quantify this effect by comparing the higher pixel values in region P1 to those in region P2 (Fig. 2h, i). Conversely, in the image acquired at 90°, the overall MRI signal intensity remains high but pixel values in region P1 are lower than those in region P2. These results demonstrate that the shifts in local magnetic field by the SPIONs combined with the choice of the flip angle can be leveraged to modify image contrast, which is consistent with our previous work37. The MRI images obtained at 60° exhibited a compromise between the overall MRI signal intensity and the generation of positive MRI contrast. Notably, in the SPIONs sample tube, T1 and T2 are significantly reduced due to the high concentration and relaxivity of SPIONs. Additionally, magnetic field variations in the sample further shortened T2*. With a long echo time of 19.5 ms, magnetization decreased considerably before acquisition, leading to a significantly reduced signal intensity.

SPION-enhanced positive-contrast imaging was performed using a SPGR sequence. We show here the utility of the SPGR sequence to create SPION-enhanced T1-weighted contrast in the ULF regime. We tested and optimized our SPGR sequence using a phantom consisting of DI water and five different concentrations of the 14 nm SPIONs coated with COO- prepared in 1% agarose, as illustrated in Fig. 3a, b. The concentrations ranged from 0.1 to 100 μg (Fe3O4)/ml. Spectroscopic measurements of the T1 and T2 values of these samples were also carried out as shown in Fig. 3g.

The T1-weighted SPGR images of the SPION phantom are shown in Fig. 3c, d, acquired with 3 different flip angles, 120°, 60°, and 30°. As the T1 weighting in the SPGR scan is positively related to the flip angle42,43, the image obtained with a 120°-flip angle exhibits the strongest T1-weighted contrast. Among these samples, DI water (labeled as sample 0) has the longest T1, making its signal nearly invisible in Fig. 3c. Conversely, Sample 5, the SPIONs sample with a concentration of 100 μg (Fe3O4)/ml, has the shortest T1, resulting in the highest signal intensity in Fig. 3c. Adjusting the flip angle of the SPGR scan allows for the modulation of T1 contrast. Notably, in the images acquired with a 30°-flip angle, DI water becomes visible (see Fig. 3e). The pixel value distribution of each sample in all scans are presented in Fig. 3f; with the decrease in flip angle, the influence of T1 on image contrast diminishes; samples with long T1 values exhibit increased intensity in the images. Meanwhile, we observed that larger flip angle enhances T1 contrast, but causes overall decrease in signal intensity, leading to a lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

SPION-enhanced phase-sensitive imaging was performed. The phase of MRI signals is highly sensitive to variations in the local magnetic field. Given that SPIONs can efficiently modify the local magnetic field even at ULF strengths, we conducted phantom imaging experiments to explore the potential of SPIONs in enhancing phase-sensitive imaging. There are two phantoms: the first is a 3 mg/ml CuSO4 solution cylindrical phantom (as seen in Fig. 4a); the second includes a sample tube containing 100 μg (Fe3O4)/ml SPION (25 nm, PEG 20 K) prepared with 1% agarose and inserted into the center of the cylindrical phantom (as seen in Fig. 4e). Both phantoms were scanned using the SPGR sequence with a flip angle of 30°. Compared to the image acquired with phantom without the SPION sample (Fig. 4b), the MRI signal from the highly concentrated SPION sample in Fig. 4f does not appear in the acquired image due to its extremely short T2 and the high distortion of the local magnetic field. In this magnitude image, the influence of SPIONs on the MRI signal is highly localized, primarily within the SPIONs sample itself but also in the adjacent regions.

In addition to traditional magnitude image contrast, SPIONs can also modulate the phase of MRI image. A phase map reconstructed from the same scan of Fig. 4b is depicted in Fig. 4c which exhibits good signal uniformity, indicating the high homogeneity of the magnetic field in the imaging region. In contrast, in the presence of the SPION sample, the phase map reconstructed from Fig. 4f is shown in Fig. 4g which shows a symmetric pattern, indicating that the magnetic field was distorted by SPIONs. Moreover, numerical simulations were carried out to validate the local magnetic field distortion caused by the presence of SPIONs. The magnetic field in the region of interest (ROI) of the MRI scanner is highly homogeneous, and the impact of the CuSO4 phantom on the magnetic field distribution is negligible, so the simulated field distortion in this region is set to 0 in the absence of SPIONs (see Fig. 4d). Figure 4h shows the simulated magnetic field distortion caused by the presence of SPIONs, quantified by the shift in the proton Larmor frequency. The accumulated phase shift is directly proportional to the magnetic field distortion. However, the simulated magnetic field map is not linearly related to the phase map due to phase wrapping44. Based on the results obtained, the acquired phase maps of both phantoms are closely correlated to the magnetic field distribution in the presence or not of the SPION sample. These results represent a significant step beyond our previous work37 as it demonstrates a new approach for visualizing the presence of SPIONs at ULF.

SPION-enhanced positive-contrast for in vivo rodent imaging

Based on the phantom imaging results, our optimized acquisition schemes including T2/T1-weighted imaging, T1-weighted imaging, and phase-sensitive imaging were translated to in vivo rat imaging for further assessing the potential of the SPIONs as CA at ULF. In these experiments, we utilized two distinct types of SPIONs produced by Imagion Biosystems. These SPIONs shared a common core size of 25 nm but with PEG coatings of different lengths: PEG2K and PEG20K referring to either a 2 K gmol−1 or a 20 K gmol−1 molecular weight. These two types of SPIONs were selected based on their blood half-life; particularly PEG2K SPIONs exhibited a blood half-life of 3.6 h, while PEG20K SPIONs had a prolonged blood half-life of 13.7 h in mice. The blood half-life was determined through the magnetic relaxometry method (as described in detail in Appendix 3, Supplementary Materials).

Rat imaging with a bSSFP sequence was performed following PEG2K SPION administration. To observe contrast variations across various organs and tissues, a precontrast scan was performed, followed by four postcontrast scans with evenly spaced time intervals. A single 4 mg (Fe)/kg dose of PEG2K-coated SPIONs was administered and then imaged over a 105-min time course as demonstrated in Fig. 5a. The organs identifiable in Fig. 5a are delineated in Fig. 5b, including the spleen, kidneys, heart, pancreas, and liver. After SPION administration, positive contrast was observed in both the spleen and pancreas, and negative contrast was observed in the liver. To quantify the MRI signal intensity in the regions of the spleen, pancreas, and liver, we quantified the pixel value distribution within the outlined regions in Fig. 5b. As plotted in Fig. 5c, the MRI signal intensity of the spleen and pancreas increased over time, while that of the liver decreased markedly as from the initial postcontrast scan and remained low throughout the experiment. The accumulation of SPIONs in different organs over time led to continuous changes in the local magnetic field within these organs, which in turn strengthened or weakened the MRI signal intensity of various tissues and organs at different time points. For instance, the spleen (slice 2) was nearly imperceptible at 35 min but became more prominent at 105 min in the postcontrast imaging results.

Rat imaging with a SPGR sequence was performed following PEG2K SPION administration. A similar experimental protocol was implemented to validate the use of SPGR to produce SPION-enhanced positive contrast in vivo at ULF. The same 4 mg (Fe)/kg SPION dose was administered to a rat for postcontrast scans. Both the precontrast and postcontrast imaging results are shown in Fig. 6a. The liver was clearly distinguishable in the precontrast scan, and the spleen, kidneys, pancreas were noticeable after SPION administration. In the precontrast scan, the spleen and the pancreas were indistinguishable from the surrounding tissues however, in the postcontrast scans, due to increased MRI intensity in both these organs and decreased MRI signal intensity in surrounding tissues and organs, notably the liver, the spleen and the pancreas became clearly visible. Like the bSSFP scans, the spleen and the pancreas exhibited positive contrast, and the liver displayed negative contrast. The organs identified in Fig. 6a are presented in Fig. 6b. We quantified the MRI signal intensity of the spleen, pancreas, and liver, and presented the results in Fig. 6c. As observed in the box plot, the MRI signal of the spleen in the SPGR scans increased gradually, which corroborated with the hypersignal visible in the outlined spleen region in all four postcontrast scans. In comparison, in the bSSFP SPION-enhanced experiment, the outline of the spleen disappeared at certain time points.

SPION-enhanced phase-sensitive in vivo imaging in rats

Phase-sensitive imaging in the phantom experiments showed promising results. To assess the value of SPIONs in performing phase-sensitive rat imaging in vivo at ULF, we reconstructed phase maps acquired from the SPGR scans after the administration of either PEG2K SPIONs or PEG20K SPIONs.

Phase-sensitive imaging results following PEG2K SPIONs administration were obtained. Precontrast and postcontrast phase maps were generated from the corresponding magnitude images in Fig. 6a. The evolution of the phase maps over the 105-minutes period is shown in Fig. 8a. As seen, once the PEG2K-coated SPIONs enter the bloodstream, the homogeneous phase map over the entire rat body gradually shifts into more heterogeneous phase maps that delineate the entire rat circulatory system. Phase shifts in the lung and abdominal blood vessels caused by the PEG2K-coated SPIONs were observed, outlining the shape of the lung and the path of abdominal blood vessels as highlighted in Fig. 8b. Additionally, phase shifts in the heart and cervical blood vessels outline the shape of the heart and the path of cervical blood vessels (Fig. 8c). Lastly, the phase changes in the spleen and liver were also observed as shown in Fig. 8d, e, respectively. The phase of the spleen remained relatively stable over time, indicating low PEG2K-coated SPIONs uptake. Conversely, the phase of the liver underwent significant changes over time, signifying a high PEG2K-coated SPIONs uptake. PEG2K-coated SPIONs-enhanced phase imaging not only displayed the vascular system of rats but also provided insight into its uptake in different organs.

Phase-sensitive imaging results following PEG20K SPION administration were obtained. Precontrast and postcontrast phase maps were generated from the corresponding SPGR magnitude images in Fig. 7a following the administration of PEG20K-coated SPIONs (see Fig. 9a). PEG20K-coated SPIONs caused phase shifts in the lung, spleen, and abdominal blood vessels, outlining the shape of the lungs, spleen, and the path of abdominal blood vessels, as observed in Fig. 9b. Similarly, Fig. 9c highlights phase shifts in the heart, liver, and cervical blood vessels, outlining the shape of the liver, heart, and the path of cervical blood vessels. The phase changes in the postcontrast phase maps presented in Fig. 9b, c remained relatively stable. This stability indicates that most SPIONs remain in the bloodstream, corroborating the prolonged blood half-life of PEG20K-coated SPIONs. Figure 9d displays the phase distribution in the heart and liver at 105 minutes after the administration of either PEG2K-coated or PEG20K-coated SPIONs. The phase map enhanced by SPIONs with a PEG2K coating shows notable phase shifts in the liver, while the phase map enhanced by SPIONs with a PEG20K coating displays continuously high phase shifts in the heart. This demonstrates the utility of PEG2K-coated SPIONs for modulating organ and tissue contrast and PEG20K-coated SPIONs as blood-pool contrast agents.

Discussion

The distinctive physical properties of SPIONs are particularly pronounced in ULF settings. This work demonstrates the benefits of these properties for MRI contrast agents and uses them to achieve valuable image contrast in preclinical ULF MRI, highlighting the potential of SPIONs for both organ and vascular imaging. In general, we reveal the versatility of SPIONs as contrast agents for MRI in the millitesla regime and outlines potential pathways for their clinical application. We strongly believe that our work bridges the gap between studies of the distinctive physical properties of SPIONs and demonstration of in vivo image contrast.

Previous research on SPIONs for ULF MRI primarily focused on their physical attributes35,36. Our previous in vivo studies37 revealed a strong but complex influence of SPIONs on MRI signal. Our present work comprehensively characterizes the properties of multiple types of SPIONs at ULF, elucidates the mechanisms of applying different physical properties to enhance MRI contrast through numerical simulations and phantom imaging, and describes approaches for adjusting and optimizing contrast. Furthermore, by focusing on use case-specific imaging concerns, we demonstrate specialized techniques to enhance in vivo contrast in rodents. Notably, positive contrast in organs is achieved by leveraging exceptional relaxivity features, while strategies to visualize the vascular system take advantage of the large magnetic susceptibility of these nanoparticles.

This preliminary results also represents the first visualization of the vascular system of rodents with ULF MRI, validating the feasibility of ULF MRI for vascular imaging. Additionally, quantification of MRI signal intensity and qualitative analysis of phase maps such as shown in this work can be used to distinguish SPIONs uptake, providing insights into the biodistribution of SPIONs with different coatings. Furthermore, the preclinical MRI experiments described in our work was performed at 6.5 mT and is generally applicable to both low field (LF) and ULF MRI due to the magnetic field dependency of SPIONs. Compared to clinical field strengths, at LF and ULF conditions, the high magnetic susceptibility of SPIONs, and the time scale of molecular motion and field fluctuations compared to the reciprocal of the Larmor frequency, significantly enhances longitudinal relaxivity (r1) and reduces the r2/r1 ratio, leading to an effective T1 contrast agent45. The imaging results from this study demonstrate their potential to assess pathology in the spleen and blood vessel diseases, such as stroke and angioma. Additionally, SPIONs with specialized coatings can be developed to achieve optimal absorption in target lesions while maintaining their distinct low-field properties. Therefore, we anticipate that by optimizing pharmacokinetic properties and further improving protocols, SPIONs will have broad application prospects in various cancer diagnosis.

An optimized protocol for SPION delivery was implemented showing significant improvement in image contrast. As seen in Fig. S1 (in Supplementary Materials), the administration of a lower concentration of PEG20-coated SPIONs (2 mg/kg) resulted on lower phase variations in the in vivo scan. Additionally, the utilization of Ferumoxytol at a concentration of 4 mg/kg, for comparison was also conducted in vivo (see Fig. S2 in supplementary materials), where minimal phase enhancement was observed. The selected PEG-coated SPIONs at 4 mg/kg dosage, also the safety dosage limit in this study, resulted in the optimum protocol. A dosage comparison of different contrast agents and SPIONs used in iron deficiency treatment is also included in the supplementary materials (see Appendix 4).

To summarize SPION-enhanced positive contrast imaging as well as phase sensitive imaging in rodents, in all in vivo scans, the final image contrast is determined by multiple factors, including scan parameters, the intrinsic properties of human tissues and SPIONs, as well as their uptake and distribution. The uptake and distribution of SPIONs in vivo are predominantly influenced by the interaction between SPIONs and the reticuloendothelial system (RES), which comprises various cell-type. The RES cells that take up SPIONs are primarily located in the liver and spleen46. In this study, the rat liver absorbed a large amount of SPIONs, resulting in high magnetic field variation and significantly short T1, T2, and T2*, which reduced signal intensity in both bSSFP and SPGR scans. The spleen also absorbed SPIONs, but in smaller amounts, leading to a relatively small reduction in T1 and an increase in signal intensity in SPGR scans (i.e., T1-weighted imaging). The bSSFP scan highlighted the spleen in magnitude images as well, leveraging the signal magnitude modulation of the Larmor frequency offset. Moreover, the SPIONs used in this study were coated with PEG, which prolongs their circulation time in the blood and thereby increases their uptake by other tissues and organs. Theoretically, SPIONs can also be significantly taken up by bone marrow and lymph nodes46, but due to limitations in image resolution and SNR, these distinctions are not observed effectively in this study. SPION uptake by pancreases have been reported in previous work47, which is validated here with an increase in signal intensity in the region of the pancreas in SPGR scans. Knowing that the blood half-life of SPIONs is primarily determined by their surface properties, including material composition, structure, size, and charge34, PEG-coated SPIONs are designed to remain in the blood longer due to their surface structure and readlack of surface charge. The vascular system of rats is observed in phase maps enhanced by PEG-coated SPIONs of two different sizes, taking advantage of the distinctive susceptibility of SPIONs in enhancing phase contrast in SPGR scans. Notably, phase maps demonstrated SPION uptake in the lungs in this study, which is rarely reported in high-field MRI due to significant magnetic field variation that hampers lung imaging48. Low-field MRI has been reported to be effective for lung imaging due to the reduced field variation caused by tissue interfaces48. The enhanced phase in the lungs observed in this study demonstrated SPION absorption and indicated potential for lung evaluation.

The main limitation of this study is that our research exclusively focused on healthy rats. The clinical utility of SPIONs in ULF MRI will hinge on their ability to enhance specific pathology and functional changes within MRI images. As such, further investigation in disease models before clinical translation is essential. In the future, our efforts will be directed towards tailoring SPIONs CAs for specific diseases and developing dedicated MRI sequences. In particular, PARTS49 (positive contrast with alternating repetition time SSFP) and GRASP50 (gradient echo acquisition for superparamagnetic nanoparticles) have shown utility in high-field MRI and variants of these sequences may have utility at ULF. Advanced numerical simulation is another effective way to understand SPIONs-enhanced MRI, and to further optimize scan parameters. While simple phantom models were included in this study for simulation, future work which would include the complex anatomical structures, SPION dosage and magnetic field direction will be investigated. In addition, low SNR is a common challenge for ULF MRI, which results in long acquisition times. Promising avenues include the development of advanced high-efficiency pulse sequences and the implementation of state-of-the-art image post-processing techniques51,52 tailored to ULF MRI.

As an affordable and easily transported technology, ULF MRI holds the promise to make MRI more accessible to a broader population. The incorporation of appropriate CAs in this regime has the potential to enhance its diagnostic capabilities. The research presented here underscores the efficacy of SPIONs in improving the imaging of organs and vascular system of rodents, suggesting the potential that the application of SPIONs in ULF MRI will expand the range of diagnostic options for a larger population. We anticipate the clinical adoption of tailored SPIONs as CAs in the context of ULF MRI.

Materials and methods

ULF MRI system

All the NMR measurements and imaging experiments were conducted on our ULF MRI scanner previously described in37,53. The 6.5;mT magnetic field is generated using a biplanar electromagnet powered by a direct current (DC) source (854 T, DANFYSIK, Denmark). Over a spherical 20 cm diameter ROI, the magnetic field inhomogeneity within this ROI is maintained below 10 Hz. The RF coil employed for phantom imaging was a transmit-receive coil with a modified solenoid structure, and the RF coil for rat imaging was a transmit-receive coil with a solenoid structure as described previously37. A custom-made active diplexer was used, with low power loss and low phase distortion, essential for phase cycling and RF spoiling during our scans. Gradient control, RF pulse generation, and data acquisition were performed with a Redstone NMR Spectrometer (Tecmag, Houston, TX, USA). A 500 W pulsed power amplifier (BT00500-AlphaS, TOMCO Technologies, Stepney, SA, Australia) was used for 1H pulses at 276 kHz. The custom-built gradient set and Techron gradient amplifiers (Elkhart, IN, USA) were used to generate gradient fields as high as 1 mT m−1.

In vitro MR relaxivity and susceptibility measurement at 6.5 mT

We calculated the transverse and longitudinal relaxivity of SPIONs using Eq. (1) (see Materials and methods section), where T1, 2 denotes the transverse and longitudinal relaxation time of SPIONs samples, T10, 20 denote the transverse and longitudinal relaxation time of solvent, c denotes the concentration of SPIONs sample, and r1,2 denotes the transverse and longitudinal relaxivity. The T1 and T2 values of SPIONs sample with 5 different concentrations (0.1, 1, 10, 50, and 100 μg (Fe3O4)/ml) were measured to fit Eq. (1) for relaxivity calculation. The T1 of samples was measured using a spectroscopic inverse recovery sequence, while the T2 was measured using a spectroscopic CPMG sequence. During the T1 measurement, the recovery time after each RF excitation was set to be longer than 5 times the T1 of the sample.

The susceptibility of SPION samples was evaluated indirectly, as previously introduced40. We acquired the susceptibility of SPIONs sample by fitting the MRI images to simulated images. We applied a bSSFP sequence to scan the phantom illustrated in Fig. 2a. We calculated the magnetic field distortion in the phantom due to the susceptibilities of deionized water, test tubes, and SPIONs samples. Based on the relationship between magnetic field shift and MRI signal in bSSFP scan54,55, we simulated MRI images of the phantom. The MRI signal in bSSFP scans can be described by Eq. (2), where Δf denotes frequency shifting, M0 denotes equivalent magnetization, and TR represents repetition time. The susceptibility of test tubes and DI water was set to 0 and −9.77 × 10−6, respectively in the simulation. Finally, the susceptibility of SPION samples can be acquired by fitting the MRI images to simulated images. All magnetic resonance measurements and phantom imaging are performed at a temperature of 26.7 ± 0.4 °C in the ROI.

The saturated magnetic moment for SPIONs used in in vivo imaging is 73.6 Am2/kg (Fe). We also performed numerical simulations to calculate the magnetic moment and susceptibility of SPIONs with varying background magnetic field strengths (See Appendix 2—Fig. S3 in Supplementary materials).

MRI scans

We employed two pulse sequences to perform imaging experiments in this study: 3D-bSSFP54,55 and 3D-SPGR42,43.The 3D-SPGR sequence utilized both RF spoiling and variable gradient field spoiling; a standard RF spoiling scheme with a 117° phase increment was applied consecutively to each RF pulse42 and a random-pattern gradient field was employed at the end of each cycle. In phantom imaging, all scans featured an echo time of 19.5 ms, a repetition time of 39 ms, and all data were pseudorandomly undersampled by 30% to accelerate acquisition. The matrix size for the scans was 54 × 64 × 5, and the voxel size was 2 mm × 2 mm × 20 mm, while bSSFP scan applies an average time of 60, resulting in a total scan time of 10 min and 28 s, and SPGR scan applies an average time of 80, resulting in a scan time of 13 min and 54 s. In rat imaging experiments, the scans used a flip angle of 70°, an echo time of 17 ms, a repetition time of 34 ms, 140 averages, and all data were pseudorandomly undersampled by 30%. The matrix size for these scans was 45 × 80 × 11, with a voxel size of 1.4 mm × 1.7 mm × 5.5 mm, resulting in a total scan time of 27 min 43 s. MRI images were reconstructed using inverse fast Fourier transform with zero-filled under-sampled k-space. Phase maps were reconstructed from the SPGR scans, with a high-pass filter applied to eliminate banding artifacts caused by main magnetic field inhomogeneity.

Phantom preparation

The cylindrical container (shown in Fig. 2a) and test tube supporter (shown in Fig. 4a) used in phantom imaging were 3D printed from ultraviolet-activated resin, with an internal diameter of 90 mm, and an internal height of 110 mm. The test tubes were conical centrifuge tubes with a volume of 15 ml, 14.4 mm internal diameter, and 16.4 mm external diameter.

Animal preparation

Adult male Wistar rats (n = 3), weighing between 325 and 453 g, were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington MA USA). All the animal experiments followed a protocol (2018N000126) approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Massachusetts General Research Institute and adhered to ARRIVE guidelines. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. An integrated sliding bite bar was used to hold the rat head in position and aligned with the isocenter of the scanner. For the entire in vivo experiment, anesthesia was maintained by 1–1.5% isoflurane through a nose cone. For each in vivo experiment, the rat was dosed at 4 mg (Fe)/kg of body weight SPION, which was first diluted into 1 ml heparin saline and administered intravenously via a catheter inserted in the tail vein. A precontrast scan was conducted by injecting only 1 ml of heparin saline. Following SPION administration, four consecutive scans were acquired at 35-min intervals. A total of five scans were acquired. Oxygen saturation as well as cardiac and respiratory rates were continuously monitored during experiments with a Model 1025 T Monitoring and Gating System (Small Animal Instruments, Stony Brook, NY, USA). At the end of each in vivo experiment, the rats were euthanized under deep anesthesia by isoflurane overdose. Death was confirmed by cervical dislocation or thoracotomy.

Statistics and reproducibility

Boxplots were used to display the pixel values in the rat organs. The average pixel values were applied to compare MRI signal intensity changes in rat spleen and liver. Two categories of experiments were performed: spectroscopic magnetic resonance measurements and MRI. The reproducibility of the MR measurements was confirmed by repeating the experiments three times. Similarly, the reproducibility of the phantom imaging was validated by performing three scans. With three rats included in this study, each scanned multiple times per experiment, the reproducibility of the imaging results is substantiated by the experiments presented.
