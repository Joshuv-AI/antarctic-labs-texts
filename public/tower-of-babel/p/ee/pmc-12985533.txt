Viscosity-Controlled Magnetic Field Effects in Homogeneous Photoredox Catalysis Enabled by Ionic Liquids
Authors: Mingli Sun, Jie Cheng, Chenli Chen, Jialong Jie, Hongmei Su, Song Gao, Linan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In conventional low-viscosity solvents, magnetic field effects (MFEs) in photoredox catalysis are often negligible because photogenerated radical ion pairs (RIPs) diffuse apart before significant spin evolution occurs. This study reports using ionic liquids (ILs) as a tunable homogeneous “solvent cage” to observe distinct low-field MFEs in the phenothiazine-mediated photoinduced reductive dechlorination of aryl chlorides. Experimental results demonstrate that MFEs increase significantly with bulk viscosity, reaching saturation at approximately 1000 Gs with a maximum enhancement of about 15%, consistent with the hyperfine coupling mechanism (HFCM). Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TA) reveals that the ionic liquid environment effectively reduces the radical cage escape rate, matching it with the spin evolution rate. This allows the external magnetic field to intervene in the back electron transfer (BET) process. However, unlike strongly confined micellar systems, the contribution of the triplet charge recombination (TCR) pathway here is moderate, intrinsically limiting the magnetic enhancement amplitude. These findings establish that MFE magnitude is determined by both viscosity-controlled cage dynamics and the efficiency of the TCR channel, providing a mechanistic basis for designing spin-modulated homogeneous photoredox systems.

1. Introduction

Photoredox catalysis has fundamentally transformed modern chemical synthesis by unlocking reaction pathways that were previously inaccessible through thermal chemistry [1,2,3,4]. By accessing open-shell radical intermediates via single electron transfer (SET), this paradigm enables the construction of complex molecular architectures with remarkable atom economy under mild conditions [5,6]. In contrast to conventional strategies that rely on frontier orbital engineering and Marcus theory to drive forward electron transfer [7,8,9], regulating reaction outcomes through the electron spin state of radical intermediates emerges as a powerful, alternative paradigm [10]. The spin dynamics of radical ion pairs (RIPs), which constitute the essential intermediates in photoredox cycles, dictate the selection of reaction pathways subject to spin conservation constraints [11]. Consequently, the ability to manipulate spin dynamics offers a unique and non-invasive handle to modulate catalytic outcomes that is distinct from traditional variations in temperature or catalyst structure [12,13,14].

External magnetic fields provide the most direct means to access this level of control [15]. The theoretical foundation of this regulation lies in the distinct reactivity of spin isomers. Specifically, back electron transfer (BET) to the ground state is spin-allowed and rapid for the singlet radical ion pair, whereas the process is spin-forbidden and consequently much slower for the triplet state [10,11]. Therefore, by modulating the intersystem crossing (ISC) between singlet and triplet manifolds, an external field can dictate the partition between unproductive BET and productive cage escape [11] (Figure 1). Two primary mechanisms govern this spin evolution. The Δg mechanism is driven by the difference in Larmor precession frequencies of the constituent radicals and typically requires high magnetic fields to become significant in organic systems due to the minimal variation in g-factors [16,17]. In contrast, the hyperfine coupling mechanism (HFCM) is particularly attractive for practical catalysis as it functions effectively under weak magnetic fields [17,18]. In this low-field regime, the applied field lifts the degeneracy of triplet sublevels, which effectively retains the radical pair in a specific spin state and modulates the reaction efficiency [17]. This magnetic regulation holds the promise of switchable catalysis, in which reactivity can be tuned remotely without altering the chemical composition of the system.

However, realizing significant magnetic field effects (MFEs) in solution-phase photoredox catalysis has proven to be an elusive goal due to the timescale mismatch in conventional reaction media [19,20]. In standard low-viscosity organic solvents such as acetonitrile, the diffusive separation of radical pairs occurs on a timescale that is significantly faster than the nanosecond-scale hyperfine-driven spin evolution [16,21,22]. Although recent strategies utilizing confined media such as micelles have successfully amplified MFEs via forced proximity [23,24,25,26], developing a homogeneous platform with simple composition and tunable properties to precisely modulate microscopic cage dynamics remains critical for elucidating spin control mechanisms and process engineering. The central challenge lies in engineering a reaction environment that can synchronize the kinetic timescales of diffusion and spin evolution to amplify the magnetic sensitivity of the system.

To address these challenges, we propose the use of ionic liquids (ILs) to construct a homogeneous reaction platform with tunable viscosity. While the correlation between solvent viscosity and the magnitude of MFEs is well-documented in fundamental photochemical studies of direct substrate excitation [27,28,29,30,31], the strategic utilization of viscosity to control spin dynamics in multicomponent photoredox catalytic cycles remains underexplored. Ionic liquids possess a wide viscosity adjustment window, and their unique internal Coulombic force fields may provide an additional confined environment for stabilizing RIPs [27,29,32]. In this study, we systematically altered the bulk viscosity of the system by finely adjusting the ratio of an acetonitrile and IL binary mixture. Using the photoreductive dechlorination of aryl chlorides mediated by phenothiazine derivatives (PC) as a model, we successfully observed distinct low-field MFEs. By combining these MFE experiments with time-resolved spectroscopy, we aim to elucidate the microscopic kinetic mechanism governing the spin dynamics in this system. Specifically, we investigate how the high-viscosity environment modulates the competition between the cage escape and spin evolution. Furthermore, this study seeks to clarify the role of the triplet charge recombination (TCR) pathway in determining the magnitude of magnetic sensitivity in homogeneous media compared to confined micellar systems [33], providing a mechanistic basis for designing spin-modulated photoredox cataysis.

2. Results and Discussion

Initially, we selected 4-chlorobenzonitrile as the model substrate, 10-methyl-10H-phenothiazine as the photosensitizer, and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as the sacrificial electron donor to investigate the reaction characteristics under 375 nm laser excitation. To quantify the magnetic sensitivity, the Magnetic Field Effect (MFE) was defined as follows:(1)MFE=Yield(B)−Yield(0)Yield(0)×100% where Yield(B) and Yield(0) represent the product yield in the presence and absence of an external magnetic field, respectively. It should be noted that these yields were measured at a fixed reaction time (typically low conversion) to reflect the initial reaction rate. In pure acetonitrile, despite the high reaction efficiency, the system exhibited negligible magnetic sensitivity, with a slightly negative MFE of approximately −1.8% at 300 Gs. However, the incorporation of ILs (1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate) induced a dramatic reversal in magnetic sensitivity. As the volumetric ratio of acetonitrile to ILs was adjusted from 1:3 to 1:9, the MFE at 300 Gs progressively increased from ~2.1% to ~9.1% (Figure 2a). Although extrapolating this trend suggests a further enhancement in pure ILs, experiments in the neat ILs were impeded by the poor solubility of the substrates. Consequently, the 1:9 ratio was identified as the practical limit, balancing the viscosity-induced MFE enhancement with sufficient solubility to maintain reaction homogeneity. The field-dependence profiles reveal a consistent saturation behavior across different viscosity regimes. Taking the 1:9 ratio as an example, the MFE rose sharply in the low-field region (0–1000 Gs) and reached a saturation plateau of approximately 12% beyond 3000 Gs (Figure 2b). This characteristic saturation profile aligns perfectly with the HFCM [16,18]. In this regime, the external magnetic field lifts the degeneracy of the triplet sublevels; once the Zeeman splitting exceeds the hyperfine coupling strength, the S ↔ T± intersystem crossing channels are energetically inhibited. Given that the MFE remained largely stable in the range of 1000 to 5000 Gs, the Δg mechanism is unlikely to be the dominant driver of the observed magnetic sensitivity. Instead, it likely plays a minor counteracting role at high fields, which is masked by the dominant HFCM in this system [21]. To further understand the nature of this enhancement, we compared the HPLC profiles of the reaction mixtures with and without the magnetic field (Figure S1). Consistent with the high specificity previously reported for this transformation [34], the chromatograms displayed identical peak profiles, with no new species detected under the magnetic field. This confirms that the magnetic field improves the reaction efficiency exclusively by modulating the spin dynamics of the intermediate radical pairs, enhancing the yield of the main product without altering the reaction pathway or chemoselectivity.

To ensure that the observed solvent effects were not derived from variations in the photosensitization efficiency, we examined the UV-Vis absorption spectra of 10-methyl-10H-phenothiazine in the mixed solvents (Figure S2). As the ionic liquid fraction increased, a slight bathochromic shift was observed. This phenomenon is attributed to the differential effect of medium polarity on the energetics of ground and excited states, where the highly polar ionic liquid environment interacts strongly with the excited state which possesses a larger dipole moment than the ground state. This interaction stabilizes the excited state more effectively than the ground state, thereby narrowing the HOMO-LUMO energy gap. Crucially, despite this spectral shift, the absorbance at the excitation wavelength (375 nm) exhibited only negligible variations without any systematic trend. This confirms that the light-harvesting capability is effectively constant across all samples, allowing us to attribute the significant variations in reaction outcomes confidently to the modulation of cage dynamics rather than differences in the initial concentration of excited states. It is crucial to note that the baseline yield at zero field exhibited a slight decline from 7.3% for the acetonitrile/ILs (1:3) ratio to 6.6% for the (1:9) ratio as viscosity increased (Figure S3). With the interference of light absorption ruled out, this trend is consistent with the suppression of cage escape kinetics and enhanced geminate recombination. However, the magnitude of the saturated magnetic field effect was progressively amplified. This observation underscores a fundamental trade-off: the viscous environment limits the diffusion-controlled background reaction but significantly enhances the magnetic sensitivity by extending the lifetime of the radical ion pairs. We also conducted a full time-course study for the PC1/Sub1 system (Figure S4). While the reaction yields under both 0 Gs and 1000 Gs eventually converged to a high conversion of ~85% after prolonged irradiation (>300 min), the reaction trajectory under the magnetic field rose significantly faster during the initial phase (0–120 min). This confirms that the magnetic field functions as a kinetic promoter by suppressing the unproductive BET step and increasing the steady-state concentration of active radicals, thereby accelerating the global reaction rate without altering the final achievable yield.

Subsequently, we compared six phenothiazine-based photosensitizers with distinct N-substitution patterns to assess the universality of this magnetic sensitivity (Figure 3). As shown in the field-dependence profiles, all photocatalysts adhered to the characteristic saturation trend of the hyperfine coupling mechanism, although their saturation amplitudes varied. PC2 (unsubstituted phenothiazine) exhibited the weakest response, reaching a saturation plateau of only ~4.3% at 1000 Gs. Notably, a slight decline in MFE was observed for PC2 at fields exceeding 1000 Gs. From a structural perspective, PC2 possesses a relatively smaller effective hyperfine coupling (Aeff) due to the lack of complex N-substituents, leading to early HFCM saturation [35]. Consequently, the counteracting effect of the Δg mechanism, which induces T0 ↔ S conversion at higher fields, likely becomes discernible once the weak HFCM effect saturates [21]. PC3, PC4, and PC5 displayed intermediate performance, with MFE values converging to approximately 8% at 3000 Gs. In contrast, PC1 (N-methyl) and PC6 (N-4-trifluoromethylphenyl) emerged as the most superior catalysts, achieving MFE values as high as ~13–14% at 3000 Gs. In these systems, the stronger effective hyperfine coupling (particularly enhanced by 19F nuclei in PC6) likely maintains a dominant HFCM effect that masks any minor negative contributions from the Δg mechanism [33]. The consistency of the HFCM profile across this diverse set of structures confirms that the viscosity-controlled spin regulation strategy is generalizable within this class of photocatalysts.

We utilized femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy to deeply investigate the photoinduced electron transfer and subsequent kinetic processes between the photosensitizer PC and substrate Sub-1 in the acetonitrile/ionic liquid mixed system (Figure 4). This investigation aimed to elucidate the reaction mechanism and the microscopic origin of the magnetic field effects. The fs-TA spectral evolution of PC following excitation by a 355 nm laser pulse revealed two broad excited state absorption bands at approximately 390 nm and 650 nm immediately after the pump pulse. These bands are attributed to the singlet excited state of PC (1PC*) (Figure 4a,b). Similarly to the situation in pure acetonitrile solvent (Figures S5 and S6 and Table 1, Entries 1–2), the interaction with Sub1 significantly accelerated the decay process of 1PC*, and new spectral features appeared within 24 ps. These features consisted of clearly visible absorption bands at 390, 510, and 650 nm and are attributed to the formation of an exciplex between 1PC* and Sub-1. Subsequently, the spectral features of the exciplex gradually transformed into a new transient species within 650 ps. This species is characterized by an absorption band at 510 nm, a shoulder peak at 460 nm, and a flat featureless absorption region in the 600–750 nm range. This species is identified as the singlet charge transfer complex (1CTCs). The 1CTCs decayed within 5 ns and led to the formation of final long-lived species characterized by two clearly visible bands corresponding to 3PC* and PC•+ [36,37,38,39]. The coexistence of 3PC* and PC•+ indicates that two additional decay pathways exist besides the back electron transfer pathway that returns the system to the ground state. These pathways are triplet charge recombination to 3PC* [40] and escape from the solvent cage to produce free PC•+ and free Sub1•− (Figure 4c,d). To visually summarize these assignments and kinetic timescales, a schematic diagram illustrating the kinetic mechanism is presented in Figure 4e. This diagram explicitly links the spectral components identified in the EADS (Figure 4c) to their corresponding electronic states (1PC*, Exciplex, CTC, and RIPs), with the specific lifetimes derived from global analysis (10 ps, 170 ps, and 1370 ps) annotated for each step.

Furthermore, using the extinction coefficients of PC•+, reported by the the literature [41] and the extinction coefficient of 1PC* and 3PC* estimated from our transient data (Figure S7), we further quantified the relative contributions and rates for the three decay pathways of CTCs, the cage escape (kCE = 3.1 × 108 s−1) and back electron transfer (kBET 3.7 × 108 s−1), together with a moderate rate of TCR process (kTCR = 5.8 × 107 s−1) (Table 1, Entry 3). A comparative analysis with the kinetics in pure acetonitrile elucidates the critical role of the ionic liquid. In pure acetonitrile, the low viscosity results in extremely rapid diffusive separation, which limits effective spin evolution. However, the introduction of ionic liquids significantly increases the bulk viscosity, leading to a prolonged lifetime of the CTC intermediate. Consequently, the kCE, kBET and kTCR are all significantly reduced compared to the pure solvent (Table 1, Entries 2–3). Crucially, this viscosity-induced deceleration suppresses the cage escape rate (kCE) to a magnitude that is kinetically competitive with the rate of hyperfine-driven spin evolution. This kinetic matching ensures that the radical ion pairs remain within the “solvent cage” for a sufficient duration, allowing the external magnetic field to effectively intervene in the singlet–triplet interconversion and modulate the reaction outcome. It is worth noting that similar spectral evolution trends were observed for PC4 and PC6 systems (Figures S8 and S9 and Table 1, Entries 4–5), confirming the generality of this pathway.

We propose a possible reaction mechanism based on time-resolved spectroscopy (Figure 5). It is evident from the reaction mechanism and previously reported kinetic models that the magnitude of the magnetic field effect is the result of the combined determination of multi-step kinetic competition [33]. We can simplify the relationship between the MFE and the key kinetic steps as follows: First, the MFE is positively correlated with the contribution of the TCR pathway (fTCR), a higher fraction of recombination into the triplet channel amplifies the magnetic sensitivity. Second, regarding the cage escape rate (kCE), effective magnetic regulation requires a kinetic match with the spin evolution rate (kRIP-ISC). If kCE is extremely large relative to kRIP-ISC, the radicals separate before significant spin evolution can occur. Conversely, if kCE is extremely small, the magnetic field loses its ability to effectively regulate the reaction outcome.

These kinetic parameters reveal a critical mechanistic distinction when compared to heterogeneous confined systems. As reported in our recent work, the TCR process is highly sensitive to the polarity of the microenvironment [33]. Nonpolar environments, such as the hydrophobic core of micelles, significantly stabilize the charge-transfer state relative to the locally excited triplet state, thereby reducing the energy barrier for TCR and enhancing its proportion (fTCR > 40%). In contrast, the ionic liquid system used here, while viscous, maintains a relatively high polarity. Consequently, the TCR process accounts for only ~8–9% of the decay paths (Table 1, Entries 3–5). Since the magnitude of the MFE is positively correlated with the proportion of the RIPs that can access the triplet channel (TCR), this intrinsic “polarity bottleneck” limits the maximum achievable MFE in the ILs system (~15%) compared to the larger effects (20–50%) observed in micellar systems.

Building on the optimized catalyst screening, we expanded the substrate scope to six chloroarenes (Sub1–Sub6) to elucidate how nuclear spin distribution regulates MFE (Figure 6). Despite differences in absolute MFE intensities among the three representative photocatalysts (PC1, PC4 and PC6), all systems exhibited a highly consistent dependence on substrate structure. Specifically, substrates dominated by hydrogen nuclei (Sub1, Sub2 and Sub6) exhibited the strongest positive MFE (reaching ~13–15% with PC6). However, a distinct downward trend was observed as the number of fluorine atoms increased, culminating in an anomalous inversion for the perfluorinated substrate. The most striking phenomenon was observed with pentafluorochlorobenzene (Sub5). Despite possessing the strongest theoretical hyperfine coupling (HFC) due to its five fluorine nuclei (I = 1/2), Sub5 exhibited the lowest magnetic responsiveness. In the PC1 system, Sub5 displayed a negative MFE of approximately −5% across the 0–5000 Gs range, and even with the highly active PC6, the effect was suppressed to near-zero (~2%). This counterintuitive phenomenon can be rationalized by two synergistic mechanisms: electronic effects on recombination thermodynamics and kinetic mismatch. First, from a thermodynamic perspective, the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the fluorine substitution significantly lowers the LUMO of substrate, leading to a more positive reduction potential (Esub/sub −) Consequently, the Gibbs free energy for back electron transfer (ΔGBET∘) becomes less exergonic. However, because charge recombination processes are frequently located in the Marcus-inverted region, this reduction in driving force paradoxically accelerates the rate of back electron transfer (kBET). A significant kBET rapidly depletes the singlet radical pair population, thereby severely suppressing the TCR pathway. Since the magnitude of the MFE relies heavily on the efficiency of the TCR channel, this kinetic competition leads to a significantly diminished magnetic response. Second, from a kinetic perspective, the extremely strong hyperfine coupling of the fluorine atoms introduces a mismatch between spin evolution and diffusion kinetics. The strong HFC induces an intrinsically rapid intersystem crossing rate for RIPs (kRIP-ISC). Consequently, even when an external magnetic field blocks the S → T+/T- pathways, the remaining S ↔ T0 mixing remains so fast that it far exceeds the cage escape rate in the viscous ionic liquid environment (kS−T0≫kCE). When spin equilibration occurs much faster than radical pair separation, the magnetic field’s capacity to alter the product distribution becomes negligible, leading to a minimal or saturated magnetic field effect. Therefore, the diminished MFE in Sub5 is proposed to be the result of a double penalty: electronic effects accelerate charge recombination, limiting the TCR path proportion, while the kinetic mismatch in spin dynamics renders magnetic regulation ineffective. This result highlights that simply increasing HFC strength is insufficient; efficient magnetic regulation via HFCM requires a precise kinetic match between HFC-driven ISC and cage escape, while securing an effective triplet recombination pathway.

3. Materials and Methods

Materials:

Unless otherwise stated, all reagents and materials were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification. Ultrapure water (resistivity > 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C) was generated by a Clever-S Pure Water System (Zhiang, Shanghai, China).

Synthesis of PC4-PC6:

N-phenylpheno thiazines were synthesized similarily to the reported procedure [42]. Phenothiazine (10 mmol, 1.00 equiv) was dissolved in anhydrous 1,4-dioxane (30 mL). Aryl bromides (1.2 equiv), tBuOK (1.2 equiv) and [tBu3PH]BF4 (6 mol %) were added, followed by Pd(dba)2 (6 mol %). The reaction mixture was degassed using three freeze-pump–thaw cycles and finally stirred under reflux for 15 h. After reaching room temperature, 100 mL EtOAc and 50 mL water were added to the reaction mixture. After phase separation, the aqueous phase was extracted additionally with 3 × 100 mL EtOAc. The combined organic phases were dried over Na2SO4, the solvent evaporated and the crude product was purified by column chromatography.

Photocatalytic measurements with external magnetic field:

For a typical photocatalytic reaction, a 600 µL aliquot of a degassed mixture of acetonitrile and ionic liquids (1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate), containing the substrate (50 mM), the photocatalyst (PC, 5 mol%), and DIPEA (2.0 equiv.), was placed in a reaction vessel. The mixture was irradiated with a 375 nm CW laser (145 mW; Model: MXL-III-375, New Industries, Changchun, China) under an external magnetic field generated by an electromagnet (Model: WDS-50W, Tindun, Shanghai, China). The zero-field condition (<1.0 Gs) was established by nullifying the residual field with a countercurrent. Detailed experimental parameters are provided in Table S1. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Shimadzu LC-20 system equipped with a ShimNex HE C18-Ph column (150 × 4.6 mm, 3 µm particle size). Analytes were separated using a mobile phase of acetonitrile and water, and their concentrations were quantified against calibration curves prepared from authentic standards.

Nanosecond transient absorption:

The nanosecond transient absorption (ns-TA) spectroscopy was performed on an Edinburgh LP980 spectrometer. The pump pulses were generated by the third harmonic (355 nm, fwhm ≈ 7 ns, 1 Hz) of a Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (Spectral Physics, Lab 170). A 150 W pulsed xenon lamp provided a broadband probe beam. For all experiments, samples were contained in a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette and maintained at ambient temperature (ca. 298 K). The probe light transmitted through the sample was directed into a monochromator and detected by a photomultiplier tube (PMT). Kinetic traces were recorded from 300 to 700 nm using a digital oscilloscope (Tektronix, Beaverton, OR, USA, 1.25 Gs/s) and processed with the manufacturer’s LP900 V8.3.4 software. Kinetic parameters were extracted from the data by fitting to appropriate models, with the goodness-of-fit judged by the weighted residuals and the reduced χ2 value.

Femtosecond transient absorption:

Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy was performed in a pump–probe configuration using a Harpia-TA spectrometer (Light Conversion, Vilnius, Lithuania) coupled with a Ti:Sapphire amplifier (Coherent, Saxonburg, PA, USA; 40 fs, 800 nm, 1 kHz). The 355 nm pump pulses were generated by an optical parametric amplifier (OPA; TOPAS-C, Coherent) seeded by the 800 nm fundamental. A broadband white-light continuum (WLC) probe was produced by focusing a small fraction of the fundamental beam into a CaF2 crystal. The temporal delay between the pump and probe pulses was controlled by a motorized delay stage. The pump beam was modulated at 500 Hz, enabling shot-to-shot referencing. To suppress rotational depolarization effects, the polarization of the pump was set at the magic angle (54.7°) relative to the probe. The two beams were made collinear and spatially overlapped within a 1 mm path length fused silica cuvette. The resulting two-dimensional data maps of differential absorbance (ΔA) as a function of wavelength and time delay were subjected to global analysis using the Glotaran-1.5.1 software. All experiments were conducted at ambient temperature (ca. 298 K).

(1)Determination of the extinction coefficient of 3PC*

Determination of the extinction coefficient of 3PC*

The extinction coefficient of the triplet excited state of the photocatalyst (3PC*) was determined using nanosecond transient absorption (ns-TA) spectroscopy via the triplet–triplet energy transfer (TTET) method [43]. 1,1-Binaphthyl (BN) was employed as the triplet energy acceptor, for which the extinction coefficient at 615 nm is known (ε615nm = 11,300 dm3·mol−1·cm−1).

The intrinsic decay of 3PC* was monitored at its characteristic absorption maximum at 460 nm, yielding a lifetime (τ0) of 20 μs and a first-order rate constant (k0) of 5.0 × 104 s−1. The TTET process was subsequently investigated by observing the quenching of the 3PC* signal at 460 nm and the concomitant formation of the 3BN* signal at 615 nm upon addition of BN. The observed decay rate constant of 3PC* (kt) was determined at various concentrations of BN. A linear fit of the plot of kt versus [BN] afforded the second-order rate constant for energy transfer (kq) between 3PC* and BN, which was found to be 3.7 × 109 M−1 s−1.(2)PC →hν,ISC PC*3 kt=k0+kq[BN]PC*3 → k0PC ratio=kq[BN]ktPC*3+BN →kq[BN] BN*3+PC BN*3=ratio × PC*3ΔOD615nmε615nm=ratio × ΔOD460nmε460nm

The maximum concentration of 3BN* generated upon complete energy transfer was calculated from the maximum absorbance change at 615 nm using its known extinction coefficient. Based on the stoichiometry of the energy transfer process, this concentration corresponds to the initial concentration of 3PC* that was quenched. By correlating this concentration with the initial absorbance change of 3PC* at 460 nm (optical path length = 1 cm), the molar extinction coefficient of 3PC* at 460 nm was calculated to be 17,500 dm3·mol−1·cm−1.

(2)Determination of the quantum yield of 3PC*

Determination of the quantum yield of 3PC*

The triplet quantum yield of PC (ΦT) was determined using a relative method with benzophenone (BP) as the actinometer, which has a known triplet quantum yield of unity (ΦT = 1) and a triplet extinction coefficient of 7100 dm3·mol−1·cm−1 at 520 nm [44]. Solutions of PC and BP were prepared to have identical absorbance at the laser excitation wavelength of 355 nm.

Under identical excitation conditions (355 nm laser pulse), the transient absorption spectra of both samples were recorded. The maximum concentration of the generated triplet state for each species ([3PC*]max and [3BP*]max) was determined from the initial maximum absorbance change (ΔODmax) in their respective kinetic traces and their known extinction coefficients, according to the Beer–Lambert law. Specifically, [3PC*]max was calculated from the ΔODmax at 460 nm using the extinction coefficient determined in the previous step (ε460nm = 17,500 dm3·mol−1·cm−1), while [3BP*]max was calculated from the ΔODmax at 520 nm using the value taken from the literature (ε520nm = 7100 dm3·mol−1·cm−1).

The quantum yield of 3PC* was then calculated from the ratio of the maximum triplet concentrations, as described by the following equation:ΦT(PC)/ΦT(BP) = [3PC*]max/[3BP*]max

This analysis yielded a triplet quantum yield (ΦT) for PC of 0.87, a value consistent with those reported in the literature for similar photocatalysts [45,46,47].

Determination of the extinction coefficient and quantum yield of 1PC*

With the extinction coefficient and quantum yield of 3PC* established, the extinction coefficient of the singlet excited state (1PC*) at 650 nm was derived from the femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) data of PC. The calculation utilized the following equation, which relates the initial singlet absorption to the subsequent triplet absorption signal:ε650nm(1PC*) = [ΦT(3PC*)*△ODmax(650 nm)* ε460nm(3PC*)]/△ODmax(460 nm)(3)

Using this method, the extinction coefficient for 1PC* at 650 nm was determined to be 20,700 dm3·mol−1·cm−1.

(4)Determining the branching ratios and rate constants of the three decay pathway for CTC

Determining the branching ratios and rate constants of the three decay pathway for CTC

The photophysically determined extinction coefficients were subsequently used to quantify the species involved in the decay of the CTC. The maximum concentration of the initially formed singlet excited state, [1PC*]max, was determined from the initial absorbance at 650 nm in the fs-TA experiment and its newly calculated extinction coefficient. The concentration of 3PC* generated from CTC decay, [3PC*]CTC, was determined from the amplitude of the 3PC* signal in the transient spectrum at a long delay time (t = 7 ns) and its extinction coefficient. Similarly, the concentration of the free photocatalyst radical cation (PC•+) formed from CTC decay, [PC•+]CTC, was determined from its spectral contribution at t = 7 ns and its respective extinction coefficient.

The reference spectrum and extinction coefficient for PC•+ were characterized independently. Nanosecond transient absorption spectra of PC under aerobic conditions were recorded following 355 nm laser excitation. Although 3PC* exhibits a long lifetime under anaerobic conditions (~tens of microseconds), it is rapidly quenched by dissolved O2 in aerobic solution (~tens of nanoseconds). This quenching can proceed via an electron transfer pathway (3PC* + O2 → PC•+ + O2•−), leading to the formation of PC•+. Consequently, the ns-TA spectra recorded under these conditions are dominated by the characteristic absorption of PC•+, consistent with previously reported data [39,41]. This allowed for the reliable quantification of [PC•+]CTC.

Assuming that the interaction of 1PC* with a large excess of substrate (1 M) leads to the exciplex, which then converts to the CTC with 100% efficiency, the branching ratios for the formation of 3PC* and free PC•+ from the CTC can be calculated as [3PC*]CTC/[1PC*]max and [PC•+]CTC/[1PC*]max, respectively. The branching ratio of the non-productive back electron transfer (BET) pathway, which returns the radical ion pair (RIP) to the ground state, is then calculated by subtracting the other two ratios from unity:(4)1 − [PC*3]CTC[PC*1]max − [PC+3]CTC[PC*1]max

Once the branching ratios for the three decay pathways were established, their individual rate constants were calculated by partitioning the overall decay rate constant of the CTC (obtained from its lifetime) according to these ratios.

4. Conclusions

In summary, this study successfully constructed a viscosity-tunable homogeneous photoredox catalytic platform based on ionic liquids. We achieved the effective capture and regulation of magnetic field effects in a non-confined homogeneous system. The results establish that ionic liquids act as a controllable “dynamic solvent cage”, effectively delaying the diffusion escape of radical ion pairs to bridge the temporal mismatch with spin evolution. The system exhibited a low-field magnetic field effect of up to 15% on the reaction rate under optimized kinetic matching conditions. Quantitative analysis of femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy further revealed that the magnitude of the MFE is constrained by the delicate competition among back electron transfer, triplet charge recombination, and cage escape. Specifically, we identified that the relatively low efficiency of the TCR pathway in homogeneous media, compared to confined systems, is the primary factor limiting the absolute intensity of the magnetic response. This work provides a quantitative physical model for understanding spin dynamics in complex photochemical reactions and offers important theoretical guidance: future designs of magnetically sensitive photocatalytic systems must simultaneously optimize “viscosity-spin” kinetic matching and the generation efficiency of the triplet reaction pathway.
