Brazilein Impedes IKK Activation by Disrupting RIPK1 Polyubiquitination, Increasing Apoptotic Susceptibility in Cells with Constitutively Active NF-κB
Authors: Kyeong Ah Park, Hee Sun Byun, Jaehee Ha, Chan Sol Kim, Kyung-Cheol Sohn, Sanghee Shin, Chan-Yong Park, Yeon-Jae Cho, Ill Young Lee, Gang Min Hur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ubiquitination of RIPK1 serves as a critical regulatory switch in determining the outcome of prosurvival NF-κB signaling by linking the TNFR1 signaling complex to upstream IKK activation. Therefore, identifying bioactive compounds that modulate RIPK1 ubiquitination has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of TNF, particularly in cancers with constitutively active NF-κB signaling. In our previous in vitro phytochemical study, we demonstrated that brazilin, isolated from Caesalpinia sappan L., inhibits the catalytic activity of the IKK complex during TNF-mediated NF-κB activation without affecting RIPK1 ubiquitination at high concentrations (~50 µM), raising concerns about off-target effects. In this study, we now report that brazilein, an oxidized derivative of brazilin, acts as a potent inhibitor of RIPK1-dependent NF-κB activation upon TNFR1 engagement. Our findings reveal that brazilein markedly suppresses upstream IKK signaling events, including TNFR1-associated RIPK1 polyubiquitination and its interaction with IKKβ. In contrast, brazilein does not affect NIK/IKKα-mediated non-canonical NF-κB activation induced by LIGHT, indicating its specificity for the canonical NF-κB pathway. Moreover, brazilein not only sensitizes cells to TNF-induced apoptosis but also induces apoptosis in A20-deficient and oncogenically transformed cells with constitutive NF-κB activity. Taken together, these results suggest a novel mechanism by which brazilein exerts anti-IKK activity through inhibition of RIPK1 ubiquitination, highlighting its potential as a candidate for NF-κB-targeted cancer therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) is a transcription factor that plays a pivotal role in cancer development by regulating the expression of genes involved in cell survival, proliferation, inflammation, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis (Gilmore, 2021; Mirzaei et al., 2022; Rinkenbaugh and Baldwin, 2016). Accumulating evidence indicates that aberrant or constitutive activation of NF-κB is commonly observed across various malignancies, contributing to tumor progression and resistance to conventional therapies (Ang et al., 2024; Nadiminty et al., 2010; Pasqualucci and Klein, 2022; Zhang et al., 2019a). Transcriptional activity of NF-κB is primarily regulated by the inhibitory kappa B (IκB) family proteins, which sequester inactive NF-κB complexes in the cytoplasm. Upon stimulation by various ligands including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and DNA damaging agents, the canonical NF-κB pathway becomes activated, leading to the degradation of IκB and subsequent nuclear translocation of NF-κB. This results in the transcriptional induction of numerous anti-apoptotic genes, including Bcl-2 family, FLICE-like inhibitory protein (FLIP) and inhibitors of apoptosis proteins (IAPs) (Häcker and Karin, 2006; Pahl, 1999; Rothwarf and Karin, 1999; Safa et al., 2008; Silke and Vucic, 2014).

Although the pro-survival role NF-κB has long attributed to its ability to drive the expression of anti-apoptotic genes, recent studies have uncovered the importance of RIPK1 ubiquitination within TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) complex-I (a membrane-bound signaling platform) as an early and critical determinant of NF-κB activation or cell death (Huyghe et al., 2023; Ju et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2019; Varfolomeev and Vucic, 2022). Upon TNFR1 activation, RIPK1 undergoes extensive ubiquitination involving multiple linkage, including K63-, K11- and linear ubiquitin chains, which serve as scaffolds for the recruitment of signaling intermediates. This ubiquitin-mediated assembly facilitates the activation of transforming growth factor β activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and IκB kinase (IKK) complex, both of which are essential for NF-κB signaling (Chen, 2005; Fan et al., 2010). Conversely, de-ubiquitination of RIPK1 attenuates NF-κB signaling and promotes its translocation into the cytoplasmic death-inducing signaling complex (DISC, also known as complex-II). This transition facilitates TNF-induced apoptosis by enabling RIPK1 to associate with Fas-associated death domain protein (FADD) and caspase-8 (Liu et al., 2016; Moquin et al., 2013; Witt and Vucic, 2017). Currently, it is widely believed that the ubiquitination status of RIPK1 in complex-1 functions as a molecular switch, determining whether the cells adopts a survival state via NF-κB activation or transition toward apoptosis via complex-II formation. Therefore, pharmacological modulation of RIPK1 ubiquitination has emerged as a promising strategy for the development of TNF-based cancer therapeutics.

In a previous phytochemical study, we reported that brazilin, isolated from Caesalpinia sappan L. (Leguminosae), inhibits TNFR1-triggered NF-κB activation in transformed cells, although only at high concentration (≥50 μM), and without affecting upstream IKK components (Jeon et al., 2014). As part of our ongoing efforts to identify more potent NF-κB inhibitors targeting regulatory nodes, we synthesized brazilein, an oxidized derivative of brazilin. Brazilein potently inhibits IKK activation by disrupting membrane-associated RIPK1 polyubiquitination, thereby selectively suppressing canonical NF-κB signaling. This novel mechanism of action not only underscores brazilein’s anti-IKK potential but also highlights its therapeutic relevance in inducing apoptosis in cancer cells characterized by constitutive NF-κB activation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and reagents

All commercial chemicals and antibodies were obtained from the following sources: Anti-phospho-p65 Ser (#3033), anti-phospho-IKKα/β (#2697), anti-NIK (#4994), anti-caspase-3 (#9662) and anti-IκBα (#9242) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA); Anti-NF-κB p52/p100 (sc-7386), anti-TRAF2 (sc-7346), anti-TRAF3 (#6933), anti-IKKγ (sc-8330) and anti-p65 (sc-8008) antibodies were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA); Antiactin (A2228) antibody, brazilin (SML2132), BAY-11 (B5556), Protein A-Sepharose (GE17-0780-01) and Protein G-Sepharose (GE17-0618-01) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St, Louis, MO, USA); Anti-RIPK1 (610459) and anti-PARP (556362) antibodies were from BD Biosciences Pharmingen (San Diego, CA, USA), and anti-IKKβ (05-535) antibodies were from EMD Millipore (Burlington, MA, USA); Anti-TNFR1 (ab19139) antibody was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA); Recombinant TNF-α (410-MT) and LIGHT (664-LI) were from R&D system (Minneapolis, MN, USA); The pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-fmk (218759) and the NF-κB inhibitor TPCA-1 (401481) were obtained from Calbiochem (San Diego, CA, USA).

Synthesis and structural determination of brazilein

Brazilein was synthesized by oxidation of commercially available brazilin. High purity of brazilin was isolated by column chromatography using an ethyl acetate: dichloromethane: hexane (1:1:1) solvent system. The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of the purified brazilin were confirmed by comparison with literature values (Wang et al., 2013). 1H NMR (300 MHz, methanol-d4) δ 7.18 (d, J=8.3 Hz, 1H), 6.70 (s, 1H), 6.60 (s, 1H), 6.46 (dd, J=8.3, 2.5 Hz, 1H), 6.29 (d, J=2.5 Hz, 1H), 3.96 (s, 1H), 3.93 (d, J=11.3 Hz, 1H), 3.69 (d, J=11.3 Hz, 1H), 3.02 (d, J=15.6 Hz, 1H), 2.77 (d, J=15.6 Hz, 1H).

For oxidation, brazilin (10 mg, 0.035 mmol) was dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (1 mL) under 0°C, followed by the addition of iodobenzene diacetate (12 mg, 0.038 mmol). After 10 min, the reaction mixture was filtered, and the filtrate concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified using column chromatography (chloroform: methanol, 5:1), yielding brazilein (6 mg, 60% yield). The NMR analysis of brazilein revealed the following spectral characteristics: 1H NMR (300 MHz, d6-DMSO) δ 9.24 (s, 1H), 7.81 (d, J=8.8 Hz, 1H), 7.11 (s, 1H), 6.57 (dd, J=8.8, 2.4 Hz, 1H), 6.36 (d, J=2.4 Hz, 1H), 6.32 (s, 1H), 5.66 (s, 1H), 4.46 (d, J=11.7 Hz, 1H), 4.01 (d, J=11.7 Hz, 1H), 2.85 (s, 1H).

Cell culture

Human cancer cell lines (HeLa and U87) and NIH/3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblasts were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Bethesda, MD, USA). H-Ras/G12V-transformed NIH/3T3 cells were kindly provided by Prof. Ho-Jin You (College of Medicine, Chosun University, Gwangju, Korea). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated bovine serum, 2 mmol/L glutamine and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin in 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C.

Transient transfection and NF-κB luciferase reporter assay

HeLa cells were transiently transfected with p2xNF-κB-Luc and pRSV-β-galactosidase plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After 24 h, cells were pretreated with varying concentrations of brazilin or brazilein for 30 min, followed by stimulation with TNF (15 ng/mL) for 10h. Luciferase activities were measured using the Luciferase Assay kit (Promega Co., Madison, CA, USA), and NF-κB activity was normalized to β-galactosidase activity. The percent inhibition of NF-κB was calculated relative to TNF-only treated controls, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined using GraphPad Prism ver. 5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

CRISPR/Cas-9-mediated A20 KO U87 cell generation

For A20 gene knockout, gRNA targeting A20 (sequence: TGAAGTCCACTTCGGGCCAT) was cloned into px330-puro vector using the standard protocol with hCas9. gRNA design was performed using the open access software provided at http://crispr.mit.edu/. The targeting plasmid was transfected into U87 cells with Lipofectamine 2000, and after 24 h, cells were selected with 3 μg/mL puromycin for 48 h. Single-cell clones were expanded, and the depletion of A20 gene was confirmed by genomic DNA sequencing.

Cell death assessment

Cell viability was evaluated using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega Co.) which detects intracellular ATP levels. Luminescence was measured using Tecan Infinite 200 Promicroplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Each experiment included triplicate wells and was repeated independently at least three times. Crystal violet staining (0.1% crystal violet in 30% ethanol and 3% formaldehyde) was also performed, and representative images were acquired with an inverted microscope Nikon TE200 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were harvested and examined for the mode of early apoptotic or necrotic/late apoptotic cell death by double staining with fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) in in a Ca2+-enriched binding buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 140 mM NaCl, and 2.5 mM CaCl2), and analyzed using a NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (ACEA Bio sciences, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Emissions from Annexin V and PI were detected at 488 and 532 nm, respectively.

RT-PCR analysis

Transcriptional expression levels of NF-κB downstream genes were analyzed by semi-quantitative RT-PCR analysis. Total RNA was isolated from treated HeLa cells using the easy-BLUE RNA extraction kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Inc., Seongnam, Korea). cDNA synthesis was performed using a RT-PCR kit (Promega Co.) with ologo(dT)-primers. PCR amplication was carried out using specific primer the following human genes (IL1-β, 5′-GTGCTGCCTAATGTCCCCTTGAATC-3′ and 5′-TGCAGAGTTCCCCAACTGGTACATC-3′; IL-6, 5′-TTCGGTCCAGTTGCCTTCTC-3′ and 5′-CAGCTCTGGCTTGTTCCTCA-3′; IL-8, 5′-TCTGCAGCTCTGTGTGAAGG-3′ and 5′-CACCCAGTTTTCCTTGGGGT-3′; cIAP-1, 5′-CTGTGGTGGGAAGCTCAGTAACT-3′ and 5′-TGTGTCTGCATGCTCAGATTTG-3′; cIAP-2, 5′-GGTTCAACATGCCAAGTGGTT-3′ and 5′-CTGATGTGGATAGCAGCTGTTCA-3′; GAPDH, 5′-GACCCCTTCTTGACCTC-3′ and 5′-GCCATCCACAGTCTTCTG-3′).

Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in M2 buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.6, 0.5% NP-40, 250 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 3 mM EGTA, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 0.5 mM PMSF, 20 mM β-glycerol phosphate, 1 mM sodium vanadate, and 1 µg/mL leupeptin). For immunoblots, 50 μg of protein was resolved by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Biomax, Seoul, Korea). For immunoprecipitation assays, lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-IKK-γ or anti-TNFR1 antibody and Protein A or G-sepharose beads. Bound proteins were washed, eluted, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± standard error (s.e) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance between groups was assessed using the Student’s t-test, with p<0.05 considered significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 13.0 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

Brazilein selectively inhibits the canonical, but not the noncanonical, NF-κB pathway

In our previous study, we demonstrated that high concentrations (≥50 μM) of brazilin inhibit NF-κB transcriptional activity without affecting upstream IKK activation (Jeon et al., 2014). To identify a more potent and selective NF-κB inhibitor, brazilein was synthesized via oxidation of brazilin (Fig. 1). This compound retains the tetracyclic isoindoloisoquinoline skeleton, commonly found in various bioactive molecules (Bentley, 2006; Nirmal and Panichayupakaranant, 2014). To evaluate their efficacy, we first compared the ability of brazilin and brazilein to inhibit TNF-induced NF-κB activation using a luciferase assay. Consistent with a previous report, brazilin significantly inhibited NF-κB activation only at 50 μM (Fig. 2A), with an IC50 of 49.30 μM (Fig. 2B). In contrast, brazilein inhibited NF-κB activation in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 7.66 μM (Fig. 2A, 2B), indicating markedly higher potency.

To investigate the underlying mechanism, we analyzed the impact of each compound on the IKKα/β-mediated canonical NF-κB signaling events. Consistently, brazilein effectively inhibited the phosphorylation of IKKα/β, as well as downstream events of the IKK signaling cascade such as phosphorylation of IκBα and p65, with complete suppression at 10 μM (Fig. 2C, right panels). Brazilin, however, required 50 μM to elicit a similar inhibitory effect (Fig. 2C, left panels). Notably, brazilein did not affect the NIK/IKKα-mediated noncanonical NF-κB pathway triggered by LIGHT, a member of the TNF superfamily, as shown by unaltered p100 processing and TRAF2/TRAF3 degradation (Fig. 2D). These results indicate the selective inhibition of the canonical NF-κB pathway by brazilein. We further assessed the transcriptional impact of brazilein by measuring mRNA expression levels of NF-κB target genes. Compared to brazilin, brazilein pretreatment dramatically reduced TNF-induced expression of as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, c-IAP1 and c-IAP2 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E), confirming at least a fivefold greater inhibitory potency.

Brazilein disrupts upstream IKK activation by inhibiting RIPK1 polyubiquitination in the TNFR1 signaling complex

RIPK1 polyubiquitination and its interaction with IKKγ in the TNFR1 complex I plays an essential for canonical NF-κB activation (Huyghe et al., 2023; Ju et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2019; Varfolomeev and Vucic, 2022). To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying anti-NF-κB activity of brazilein, we examined the ubiquitination status of RIPK1 and its association with IKKγ following TNF stimulation. As expected, brazilin did not affect TNF-induced RIPK1 polyubiquitination or its interaction with IKKγ (Fig. 3A, 3B, left), even though it modestly inhibited TNF-induced IKKα/β phosphorylation at 50 μM (Fig. 3A, third panel). These findings are consistent with our previous observations, suggesting that brazilin’s anti-NF-κB activity is unlikely to be achieved through the regulation of upstream events of IKK such as RIPK1 polyubiquitination. In contrast, brazilein completely suppressed TNF-induced RIPK1 polyubiquitination and disrupted its interaction with IKKγ at 10 μM, without altering the level of unmodified RIPK1 (Fig. 3A, 3B, right). These effects correspond with its inhibitory potency of NF-κB, suggesting that, unlike brazilin, brazilein disrupts the interaction between RIPK1 and IKKγ by inhibiting RIPK1 polyubiquitination. To ensure that equal amounts IKK complex were precipitated in each sample, the level of IKKβ in the immunoprecipitates was assessed (Fig. 3B, second panel).

We next investigated the possibility that brazilein might affect the assembly of TNFR1 complex I. As expected, immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that treatment of HeLa cells with TNF led to the immediate recruitment of polyubiquitiated RIPK1 into TNFR1, and this recruitment was significantly impaired by brazilein, but not by brazilin (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, TNF-induced TRAF2 recruitment to TNFR1 complex I remained unaffected by brazilein (Fig. 3C, second panel), indicating that its specific action at the level of RIPK1 engagement. These findings suggest that brazilein antagonizes IKK activation by disrupting RIPK1 recruitment to the membrane-bound TNFR1 complex I.

Brazilein sensitizes cancer cells to TNF-mediated apoptotic cell death

Activation of NF-κB signaling following TNFR1 ligation plays a critical role in promoting cell survival. Consequently, inhibition of this pathway has been shown to sensitize TNF-mediated cell death across a broad range cancer types (Kang et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2004). Given the potent NF-κB inhibitory activity of brazilein, we investigated whether it could enhance the cytotoxic effects of TNF. As shown in Fig. 4A, HeLa cells were largely resistant to treatment with TNF or brazilein alone. However, pretreatment with brazilein markedly sensitized the cells to TNF-mediated cytotoxicity, as evidenced by the cleavage of caspase-8, caspase-3 and PARP-hallmarks of apoptotic cell death (Fig. 4B). This effect was completely abolished by the pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-fmk, confirming that the observed cell death was caspase-dependent apoptosis.

To further validate this finding, flow cytometry analysis was conducted using Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) staining. While treatment with TNF or brazilein alone resulted in minimal apoptotic cell death, pretreatment with brazilein significantly increase the proportion of annexin V+ and annexin V+/PI+ cell (7.45% and 60.75%, respectively) in response to TNF, consistent with early and late stage of apoptosis. In contrast, only a small fraction of cells were stained exclusively with PI, a marker of necrosis, comparable to levels observed in cells treated with known NF-κB inhibitor such as Bay-11 and TPCA-1, which serves as a positive controls for TNF-mediated apoptosis (Fig. 4C).

Brazilein preferentially induces apoptosis in a broad spectrum of cell types with constitutive NF-κB activation

Given its pivotal role in promoting the transcription of anti-apoptotic and oncogenic genes, NF-κB is frequently constitutively activated in a wide variety of tumors (Ang et al., 2024; Nadiminty et al., 2010; Pasqualucci and Klein, 2022; Zhang et al., 2019a). This aberrant activation of NF-κB is believed to play a critical role in determining cell fate between survival and apoptosis. To investigate whether NF-κB activation status influences the cytotoxic potential of brazilein, we utilized paired cell types with genetically altered NF-κB activity. A20 (also known as TNFAIP3, TNFα-induced protein 3) is well-characterized negative regulator of NF-κB signaling that functions by editing polyubiquitin chains on TNFR1-associated signaling components, including RIPK1 (Pujari et al., 2013; Werner et al., 2008). Additionally, the oncogenic H-Ras (G12V) mutant, a constitutively active form of H-Ras, has been shown to promote persistent NF-κB activation even in the absence of external stimuli (Mitin et al., 2001). In agreement with these reports, basal NF-κB transcriptional activity was markedly elevated in both A20-knockout (KO) U87 glioblastoma cells and NIH3T3 cells stably expressing oncogenic H-Ras (G12V), compared to their respective wild-type controls (Fig. 5A). In line with this, TNF-induced RIPK1 ubiquitination of RIPK1 and p65 phosphorylation were markedly prolonged in A20-KO U87 cells, whereas only transient RIPK1 ubiquitination was observed in wild-type U87 cells (Fig. 5B). Similarly, persistent IKK phosphorylation was evident in H-Ras (G12V)-transformed NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 5C), confirming the constitutive activation of upstream NF-κB signaling in these models. Notably, treatment with brazilein alone resulted in significant cytotoxicity in both A20-KO U87 and H-Ras (G12V)-transformed NIH3T3 cells, whereas their wild-type counterparts showed only minimal sensitivity (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that the cellular context of NF-κB activation strongly influences the cytotoxic efficacy of brazilein.

To determine whether the ability of brazilein to inhibit NF-κB contributes directly to its cytotoxic effects, we further examined its impact on RIPK1-mediated NF-κB activation in A20-deficient cells. Consistent with findings in HeLa cells, treatment of A20-KO U87 cells with brazilein completely disrupted the TNF-induced interaction between ubiquitinated RIPK1 and IKKγ and suppressed p65 phosphorylation (Fig. 5E). Importantly, brazilein alone at 10 μM—a concentration sufficient to inhibit p65 phosphorylation—induced significant apoptosis in A20-KO U87 cells, as demonstrated by reduced cell viability (Fig. 5F, bottom panel) and the cleavage of caspase-8, caspase-3, and PARP (Fig. 5G, right panel). In contrast, wild-type U87 cells treated with brazilein alone showed no significant signs of apoptosis (Fig. 5F, upper panel; Fig. 5G, left panel). These findings indicate that anti-NF-κB activity of brazilein can trigger apoptotic cell death in models with constitutive NF-κB activation, such as A20-deficient U87 cells, even in the absence of TNF. Supporting this conclusion, flow cytometry analysis revealed a marked increase in both early and late apoptotic populations in A20-KO U87 cells following brazilein treatment, compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 5H), further demonstrating that brazilein preferentially induces apoptosis in cells harboring constitutively active NF-κB.

DISCUSSION

RIPK1 plays a pivotal role in maintaining the balance between cell survival and cell death following TNFR1 activation. Upon TNF binding, TNFR1 undergoes a conformational change that initiates the assembly of membrane-bound signaling complex known as complex-I (Micheau and Tschopp, 2003). Within this complex, RIPK1 becomes extensively polyubiquitinated with multiple linkage types, including K63-, K48-, K11-, and M1-linked chains. This modification is essential for promoting pro-survival signaling through the recruitment and activation of IKKs (IKKα/β/γ) (Bertrand et al., 2008; Chen, 2005; Ea et al., 2006; Fan et al., 2010; Won et al., 2016). Conversely, insufficient RIPK1 ubiquitination allows its release from complex-I, facilitating the formation of a cytosolic complex-II, which can trigger programmed cell death (PCD) via apoptosis or necroptosis depending on the cellular context (Brenner et al., 2015; Micheau and Tschopp, 2003). Thus, identifying bioactive compounds that modulate RIPK1 ubiquitination at the level upstream of IKK not only highlights the importance of canonical NF-κB signaling in PCD regulation but also provides an attractive therapeutic approach to sensitizes apoptosis-resistant cancer cells to TNF-induced cytotoxicity. The present study demonstrates that brazilein, an oxidized derivative of brazilin, exhibits significantly greater anti-NF-κB activity than its parent compound. More importantly, brazilein selectively inhibits the canonical NF-κB pathway by interfering with RIPK1 polyubiquitination at the TNFR1 complex-I (Fig. 5I). These findings suggest that brazilein may serve as a potential small-molecule inhibitor targeting the upstream component of IKK signaling for cancer therapy.

IKK is a central regulatory node for NF-κB activation downstream of various stimuli, including TNFR1, toll like receptor 4 (TLR4), interleukin-1β receptor (IL-1R), DNA damage and reactive oxygen species (Häcker and Karin 2006; Hur et al., 2003; Karin and Ben-Neriah, 2000). However, the broad role of IKK in physiological signaling raises concern regarding the specificity and potential off-target effects of IKK-targeted NF-kB inhibitors. In a previous phytochemical study, we identified brazilin, a major component of Caesalpinia sappan L., as a broad-spectrum IKK inhibitor that blocks canonical NF-κB activation across diverse receptor systems (Jeon et al., 2014). Furthermore, despite its activity, high concentrations (~50 μM) of brazilin are required to suppress IKK catalytic activity without disrupting upstream TNFR1 complex formation. Thus, identifying more potent analogs of brazilin that act on upstream signaling events of IKK is significant interest. A key finding of this study is that brazilein, unlike brazilin, inhibits IKK phosphorylation of IKK at low concentration by disrupting the interaction between RIPK1 and IKK. These observations support the notion that brazilein acts upstream of the IKK complex, potentially without directly targeting IKKβ’s catalytic activity or downstream NF-κB events such as IκB degradation and p65 nuclear translocation. Furthermore, consistent with prior findings (Jeon et al., 2014), we observed that brazilein selectively blocks IKKβ-mediated canonical NF-κB activation, without affecting IKKα-dependent non-canonical NF-κB signaling. These results suggest that oxidation of brazilin to brazilein significantly enhances its potency by modulating upstream events while preserving its mode of action.

A critical mechanistic insight from this study is the suppression of TNF-induced RIPK1 polyubiquitination by brazilein. Corroborating this, our TNFR1 complex analysis revealed that brazilein prevents the recruitment of polyubiquitinated RIPK1 into Complex I, suggesting that its anti-IKK activity arises from impaired RIPK1 ubiquitination at the membrane-associated TNFR1 complex, rather than from cytosolic targeting. Importantly, brazilein did not affect TRAF2 recruitment to Complex I, indicating that the inhibition of RIPK1 polyubiquitination occurs independently of initial TNFR1 complex formation. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanism through which brazilein modulates the ubiquitination landscape of RIPK1 remains to be elucidated. Given that RIPK1 polyubiquitination is mediated by E3 ligases such as cellular inhibitor of apoptosis proteins 1/2 (cIAP1/2) and the linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC), future studies are needed to define how brazilein impacts this regulatory network (Bertrand et al., 2008; Emmerich et al., 2011; Haas et al., 2009; Ju et al., 2022).

Constitutive NF-κB activation and sustained TNF expression form a well-characterized positive feedback loop in various cancers (Dong et al., 1999; Kagoya et al., 2014; Smirnov et al., 2001; Wang and Lin, 2008). This persistent signaling axis promotes tumor progression by enhancing cell survival, immune evasion, and metastatic potential. Recent studies show that deubiquitination of RIPK1 sensitizes cancer cells to TNF-induced cytotoxicity by promoting the assembly of pro-apoptotic complex-II, composed of RIPK1, FADD, and caspase-8 (Kist et al., 2021; Park et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2019b). Consistent with this, our data demonstrate that brazilein enhances TNF-mediated apoptosis in a caspase-dependent manner, suggesting a model in which interference with RIPK1 ubiquitination augments TNF’s cytotoxic efficacy. Moreover, using genetically engineered cancer cell models with constitutive NF-κB activation, we provide compelling evidence that brazilein preferentially induces apoptosis in this context. These findings support a novel mechanism by which brazilein sensitizes cancer cells to TNF cytotoxicity via modulation of RIPK1 ubiquitination. While further preclinical evaluation is needed to establish the therapeutic efficacy and safety of brazilein, our study highlights its potential as a targeted anti-cancer agent, particularly for malignances characterized by persistent NF-κB activation.
