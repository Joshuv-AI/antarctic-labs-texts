Tick–small mammal relationships in urban and forest environments in Yamagata Prefecture, Japan: First confirmation of the establishment of southern tick species in northern Japan
Authors: Hirotaka Komine, Shinya Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall mammals are prominent hosts for ticks that transmit various infectious diseases to humans and other animals. Despite the widespread presence of ticks and small mammals worldwide and the fact that climate change is a global phenomenon, tick–small mammal relationships and the potential expansion of southern tick species have been investigated only in limited regions. To understand the global risk of tick-borne diseases, examining tick–small mammal relationships in northern regions is essential. In Japan, several tick-borne diseases have emerged, and multiple studies have reported adult and nymphal stages of southern tick species in northern areas. However, whether these species have successfully established in the north can be determined only through the detection of larval stages. Therefore, we examined tick infestation in small mammals captured from forest and urban environments in Yamagata Prefecture, northern Japan. A total of 55 individuals representing seven species were captured across the forest and urban environments. The predominant small mammal in both environments was the large Japanese wood mouse (Apodemus speciosus). Ticks were recovered only from A. speciosus captured in the forest environment. Among the recovered ticks, 29 were Dermacentor bellulus larvae, 13 were D. bellulus nymphs, and 1 was an Ixodes ovatus nymph. These results provide the first verification of the establishment of the southern tick species D. bellulus in northern Japan, and indicate that A. speciosus serves as an immature host for D. bellulus in the forest environment. Future studies are required to clarify tick burden across seasons and years, as well as the potential effects of host traits.Keywords: Apodemus speciosus, Climate change, Dermacentor bellulus, Large Japanese wood mouse, Public health, Tick-borne disease

Introduction

In recent years, the world has seen a global expansion in the incidence of infectious diseases, most of which are zoonoses (Jones et al., 2008), with various mammals and birds serving as the sources of these zoonotic pathogens. Small mammals such as rodents are the primary reservoirs of numerous pathogens, including the etiological agents of Lassa fever, plague, leptospirosis, and hantavirus diseases (Han et al., 2015; Johnson et al., 2020; Keesing and Ostfeld, 2024). Small mammals are also prominent hosts for ticks that transmit various infectious diseases to humans and other animals. At immature stages, several tick species infest small mammals. For example, Ixodes scapularis and I. ricinus, which are key vectors of Borrelia burgdorferi, the causative agent of Lyme disease, infest rodents in North America (Ostfeld et al., 2006) and Europe (Perez et al., 2016), respectively.

At the population level, tick–small mammal relationships are influenced by various biotic and abiotic factors. Biotic factors include host species and interactions between hosts and food resources. For example, the white-footed mouse (Peromyscus leucopus) is relatively more permissive to tick infestation compared with other hosts, including squirrels (Sciurus carolinensis) and opossums (Didelphis virginiana) (Keesing et al., 2009). I. scapularis nymphs infest P. leucopus and chipmunks (Tamias striatus), and the population dynamics of these ticks are significantly affected by the abundance of these hosts in the previous year and the abundance of acorns two years before (Ostfeld et al., 2006). The abundance of infected nymphs is influenced by reservoir host abundance. The abundance of nymphal ticks and rodents is also influenced by the top-down effects of predators, including bobcats and coyotes (Ostfeld et al., 2018).

The abiotic factors affecting tick–small mammal relationships include landscape features and climate factors. Previous studies have demonstrated that landscape features influence the abundance of ticks (Piedmonte et al., 2018) and small mammal hosts (Perez et al., 2020; Mason et al., 2022), as well as the relationship between the two entities (Perez et al., 2016). Moreover, recent global warming may influence the distribution of both ticks and host animals, potentially driving expansion toward the poles and high-altitude regions. In the Northern Hemisphere, southern tick species have shown a tendency to expand northward. For example, Hyalomma spp., the vectors of Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever, which is endemic to North Africa and the Middle East, have been found in Germany (Chitimia-Dobler et al., 2019), Finland (Sormunen et al., 2022), and the UK (McGinley et al., 2021), and began to establish populations in southern Europe (Celina and Černý, 2025). These findings raise concerns regarding the introduction of Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever in Europe. Ixodes ricinus has also expanded to northern areas in Europe over the past several decades (Porretta et al., 2013; Cunze et al., 2022; Da Re et al., 2025). Therefore, understanding tick–small mammal relationships at the population level requires consideration of landscape features and the potential climate-driven expansion of southern tick species.

Despite the worldwide presence of ticks and small mammals and the fact that climate change is a global phenomenon, tick–small mammal relationships and the potential expansion of southern tick species have been well-studied only in North America and Europe. To understand global trends in the incidence of tick-borne diseases, elucidating tick–small mammal relationships in Japan will help fill this gap and provide significant insight into the risk of tick-borne diseases in a changing environment.

Several tick-borne diseases have emerged in Japan over the recent decades such as Japanese spotted fever (JSF) and severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS). Previously, the incidence of these tick-borne diseases rose in southern and western Japan, whereas in recent years they have been increasingly observed in more northern areas of Japan (Japan Institute for Health Security, 2020; 2025). The first case of JSF was reported in 1984 in Tokushima Prefecture, western Japan (Mahara, 1987; Yamaji et al., 2018), and JSF cases were reported in southern and western Japan between 1984 and 1999, whereas cases were reported in central Japan and northern Kanto between 2007 and 2019 (Japan Institute for Health Security, 2020). The first case of SFTS was recorded in 2012 in Yamaguchi Prefecture, western Japan (Takahashi et al., 2014; Yamaji et al., 2018), and SFTS cases were reported only in southern and western Japan in 2013, whereas cases were reported in the central and eastern regions of Japan in 2023 (Kobayashi et al., 2020; Ohno et al., 2025). In 2025, an SFTS case was first reported in Hokkaido, northern Japan (Japan Institute for Health Security, 2025). These reports indicate a geographical expansion of both JSF and SFTS infections toward northern Japan. Although several factors may contribute to this expansion, one plausible explanation is the northward expansion of southern tick species.

In recent years, several southern tick species have been found in northern Japan. For example, Haemaphysalis hystricis adults, H. formosensis adults and nymphs, H. cornigera nymphs, Amblyomma testudinarium adults, and Dermacentor bellulus adult have been found on a remote island belonging to Yamagata Prefecture in northern Japan (Komine and Okabe, 2023). On this island, a survey of ticks infesting birds was conducted approximately 30 years earlier, but no southern tick species were detected (Miyamoto et al., 1993). Adult A. testudinarium have also been recovered from cattle in Aomori Prefecture (Terada et al., 2019) and from brown bears in Hokkaido Prefecture (Nakao et al., 2021). Adult Dermacentor bellulus has also been identified on a brown bear in Hokkaido Prefecture (Nishiumi, 2025). Although previous studies reported tick infestation on brown bears in Hokkaido, no southern tick species were detected (Yamaguti et al., 1971; Kadosaki et al., 1990, 1993). These findings indicate that detection of southern tick species in northern Japan has occurred in recent years, and suggest that these ticks may be beginning to spread toward northern areas.

However, all the aforementioned studies have reported only the presence of adult and nymphal stages of southern tick species in northern Japan. To confirm the successful settlement of southern tick species in northern Japan, the larval stage must be found in the natural environment. Immature stages of D. bellulus are known to infest small mammals in southern and western Japan, including Nagasaki (Kitaoka and Suzuki, 1981) and Kyoto Prefecture (Apanaskevich and Apanaskevich, 2015). Given that small mammals are prominent hosts for the immature stages of ticks, examining tick infestation on small mammals in northern Japan can provide important insights into the settlement of southern tick species in this region. The adult stage of this species has been relatively frequently reported in recent years in northern Japan, including Yamagata (Komine, 2024), Akita (Sato et al., 2017), and Hokkaido Prefecture (Nishiumi, 2025). Therefore, we expected that southern tick species, particularly D. bellulus, may have successfully established in northern Japan, and hypothesized that this species could be detected from small mammals in this region.

Here, we examined tick infestation on small mammals captured from forests and urban environments in Yamagata Prefecture, northern Japan, to elucidate tick–small mammal relationships and verify the settlement of southern tick species in northern Japan.

Material and methods

Study sites

Field surveys were conducted in Tsuruoka City in Yamagata Prefecture, northern Japan. Five study sites were set inside and around the center of Tsuruoka City, representing an urban environment, and five study sites were set inside the forest of the field science center of Yamagata University, representing a forest environment (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 1). The forest was mainly populated by Cryptomeria japonica trees.

Sampling hosts and ticks

Small mammals were captured on the following dates: in 2023, July 30 and 31 in forest, and August 10 in urban; in 2024, July 3 in forest and 30 in urban, August 2 in forest and 22 in urban, and November 1 in forest and 13 in urban (Supplemental Table 2). Sherman-type live traps with baits including whole oats and sunflower seeds were used to capture the small mammals. Ten traps spaced 10 m apart were placed overnight at each study site. The traps were collected the next morning, and the captured animals were euthanized on-site using carbon dioxide gas and transported to our laboratory. Small mammals harbor several ectoparasites, including ticks, mites, lice, and fleas that can potentially bite humans; therefore, to ensure safety before tick recovery in the current study, samples were frozen at −20 °C for more than one week. The small mammals were identified for species and sex morphologically. This study protocol was approved by a Yamagata University animal experiment committee (Permission number: R5-143, R6-130) and Yamagata Prefecture (Permission number: Shireisyousoukandai44gou, Shireisyousoukandai2gou).

All the infesting ticks were carefully collected from the surface of the small mammals using tweezers and a comb. The species and stage of the ticks were morphologically determined under a stereomicroscope, referring to published literature (Yamaguti et al., 1971; Fujita and Takada, 1979; Apanaskevich and Apanaskevich, 2015).

The capture efficiency of small mammals —used as a proxy for the relative abundance of small mammals—was compared between forest and urban environments by applying a generalized linear model. However, this metric represents only a proxy for the relative abundance of wildlife and is therefore not suitable for estimating true abundance. In the current study, it was used to assess whether the relative abundance of small mammals did not differ substantially between the two environments during the study period. A Poisson distribution was fitted as the error distribution, and trapping effort was used as an offset variable. Statistical analysis was performed using R software, version 4.5.2 (R Development Core Team, 2025).

Results

We caught 55 small mammals representing seven species (Table 1, Supplemental Table 3). The small mammals captured from the forest environment were as follows: large Japanese wood mouse (Apodemus speciosus, n = 30), small Japanese wood mouse (Apodemus argenteus, n = 2), Japanese shrew-mole (Urotrichus talpoides, n = 1), and Anderson's red-backed vole (Eothenomys andersoni, n = 1) (Table 1). The small mammals captured from the urban environment were as follows: A. speciosus (n = 18), Japanese grass vole (Microtus montebelli, n = 1), Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus, n = 1), and Dsinezumi shrew (Crocidura dsinezumi, n = 1) (Table 1). Apodemus speciosus was the only species common to both the environments and the dominant species in both environments. No significant difference was observed in capture efficiency of small mammals between the forest and urban environments (estimate = 0.26, standard error = 0.28, z-value = 0.93, p-value = 0.35), indicating that the relative abundance of these animals did not largely differ between the two environments during the study period.

Ticks were only recovered from A. speciosus mice captured in the forest. Ticks were found on 46.7% of the A. speciosus mice captured in the forest, including male (n = 13), female (n = 15), and juvenile (n = 2) animals (Table 2). Most infested mice harbored 1–3 ticks, but the maximum number of ticks recovered from a single mouse was 13 (Fig. 2, Table 2). Of the recovered ticks, 29 were D. bellulus larvae, 13 were D. bellulus nymphs, and 1 was an I. ovatus nymph (Fig. 3, Table 2).

Discussion

In the current study, we captured 55 small mammals representing seven species (A. speciosus, A. argenteus, U. talpoides, E. andersoni, M. montebelli, R. norvegicus, C. dsinezumi), and recovered two tick species (D. bellulus, I. ovatus) from A. speciosus mice captured in the forest environment in Yamagata Prefecture, Tohoku, northern Japan. We found D. bellulus larvae and nymphs from A. speciosus mice, and this is the first report of D. bellulus larvae and nymphs in northern Japan. Dermacentor bellulus is known to be a southern tick species, mainly inhabiting regions of South Asia, Southeast Asia, and southern and western Japan (Apanaskevich and Apanaskevich, 2015; Takada et al. 2019, 2022). No infestation of ticks including D. bellulus was discovered in A. speciosus mice captured in the urban environment, despite the fact that A. speciosus mice inhabit both forest and urban environments with similar relative abundances. These results demonstrate the southern tick species D. bellulus has established at least in the forest environment in northern Japan.

In recent years, this tick species has been found in northern areas such as Kanto, Tohoku, and Hokkaido instead of its original distribution areas. However, only the adult stage of this tick was previously reported, so whether the species had successfully settled in northern Japan or only occasionally introduced was unclear. This study confirms for the first time the presence of the larval and nymphal stages of D. bellulus in northern Japan, indicating that this tick species has completed its life cycle by infesting A. speciosus and has successfully settled in the area.

Previous studies have reported the recovery of adult D. bellulus ticks from wild boars (Sus scrofa) and Japanese black bears (Ursus thibetanus japonicus) in the study area (Komine, 2024), and an adult D. bellulus female recovered from a wild boar oviposited egg, which hatched into larvae (Komine, 2025). Therefore, we speculate that D. bellulus ticks use A. speciosus as a host for molting and wild boars for breeding to complete their life cycle in northern Japan. The range of wild boar has recently expanded into northern Japan, which may positively influence the settlement of D. bellulus in this area by providing reproductive hosts and leading the infestation of A. speciosus by larvae and nymphs. Future studies could test this hypothesis by comparing the presence or absence and relative abundance of D. bellulus between areas with differing wild boar distributions.

Establishment of southern tick species represents an early phase in the emergence of tick-borne diseases in the regions where these southern tick species can transmit pathogens. Therefore, the level of caution among residents and medical workers should increase to prevent potential future outbreaks, particularly when the tick species is of high medical importance. Although D. bellulus infrequently bites humans directly (Yamauchi and Nakatani, 2024), the adult stage of this species is often recovered from large mammals such as wild boar and Japanese black bear. Several pathogenic organisms, including SFTS virus and Rickettsia japonica, have been detected from D. bellulus (Takada et al., 1994, 2019; Yano et al., 1993). Therefore, although cases of direct bites on humans and infections transmitted by this species may not rapidly increase, this species may contribute to enhancing pathogen transmission cycles among wildlife and function as a sentinel for tick-borne diseases. Accordingly, the establishment of D. bellulus may lead to future emergence of tick-borne diseases in the established areas, and surveillance of this species may be useful for assessing risk. This changing situation in northern Japan should be recognized by residents and medical workers to improve awareness of potential future risks.

Apodemus speciosus is a rodent species endemic to Japan, and it is widely distributed throughout the Japanese Archipelago (Nakata et al., 2015). This species plays a crucial role in the forest ecosystem as a consumer, disperser, and food resource for predators (Fujii et al., 2025; Nakata et al., 2015; Sato et al., 2018; Shimada, 2001; Kondo and Shiraki, 2012). In this study, A. speciosus inhabited both environments, and the relative abundance of small mammals did not differ substantially between the two environments during the study period. However, tick infestation was confirmed only in the forest environment and not in the urban environment. In contrast, previous studies examining tick infestation on rodents in urban and forest environments have reported I. ricinus infestation of rodents in both environments in Romania (Borşan et al., 2020) and Germany (Maaz et al., 2018). Other studies have found that the tick burden of small mammals was affected by factors related to habitat structure, such as presence of leaf litter (Andrade-Ponce et al., 2025), and that the presence of reproductive host species was vital to the establishment of tick populations (Tsao et al., 2021). Although the reasons for the absence of ticks in the urban environment in the current study remain unclear, environmental characteristics and wildlife composition in urban area, and habitat preference of D.bellulus may influence the result.

The urban environment in which we conducted the field survey consisted of fragmented patches with limited vegetation cover and low tree density relative to the forest environment. This environment is characterized by human use and specific microclimatic conditions. Large mammals, including deer, Japanese black bear, and wild boar, are absent from the urban environment. The absence of tick infestation on A. speciosus observed in our results may be attributable to local habitat structure, microclimatic conditions, and the scarcity of reproductive host species in the urban environment. Alternatively, this tick species may not currently have expanded into urban environments. We present these data descriptively without statistical analysis because no ticks were detected on hosts in the urban environment and the total sample size was insufficient for valid statistical testing. The habitat preference of D. bellulus also remains unclear, and further studies are required to elucidate the ecological factors underlying these findings.

Previous studies have reported infestation of immature stages of several tick species on A. speciosus (Morishima et al., 2025), and reports of immature stages of D. bellulus infestation have been limited to southern and western Japan. In western Japan, D. bellulus nymphal infestation in A. speciosus was reported in Kyoto Prefecture in 1952 (Apanaskevich and Apanaskevich, 2015) and Tokushima Prefecture in 1995 (Ishiguro et al., 1995). Dermacentor bellulus larval infestation in A. speciosus was reported in Nagasaki Prefecture in southern Japan in 1976 (Kitaoka and Suzuki, 1981) and Hyogo Prefecture in western Japan in 2007 (Takano et al., 2014). These reports clearly indicate that D. bellulus has established its life cycle in southern and western Japan at least several decades ago. However, no study till date has reported D. bellulus infestation in A. speciosus in northern Japan. Infestation of some other tick species has been reported previously in northern Japan, such as I. monospinosus in Aomori Prefecture in 1974 (Fujita and Takada, 1979) and I. persulcatus and I. ovatus in Hokkaido during 1996–2000 (Ito and Takahashi, 2006). This is the first study to confirm the infestation of D. bellulus larvae and nymphs in host animals in northern Japan, verifying the successful establishment of the D. bellulus tick in this region.

Furthermore, surveys of infestation by immature stages of D. bellulus on small mammals across diverse environments in broad areas will help identify geographical gaps between sporadically introduction area and establishment area. Identifying such gaps will clarify the actual frontline of distribution expansion of the species and provide fundamental information for public health. Other southern tick species whose establishment in northern Japan remains unclear, such as A. testudinarium, as well as broadly distributed species present at low density in northern Japan, such as Haemaphysalis longicornis, should also be considered. Surveys of tick infestation on small mammals may provide further insight into the establishment of these southern tick species and the relative density of broadly distributed species. Understanding tick communities on small mammals and their pathogen prevalence will be essential for evaluating the changing risk of tick-borne diseases.

In the current study, we conducted field surveys primarily during summer over two years and detected infestation of D. bellulus larvae and nymphs on A. speciosus in both years, supporting the validity of our results. These surveys allowed us to clearly determine the presence or absence of larval and nymphal stages of this southern tick species on A. speciosus; however, due to the limited sample size, we were unable to examine seasonal variation in tick infestation on the host. Previous studies have demonstrated that tick burden in rodents varies temporally (Perez et al., 2016) and is affected by host traits (Zduniak et al., 2023); therefore, future studies must examine the tick burden in other seasons and years, in addition to investigating the potential effects of host traits. Moreover, we did not investigate the pathogens or antibodies carried by A. speciosus. According to previous studies, A. speciosus can carry pathogens such as Borrelia afzelii (causative agent of Lyme disease; Nakao et al., 1994), Anaplasma sp., Ehrlichia muris, Neoehrlichia mikurensis, Babesia microti (Moustafa et al., 2016), and tick-borne encephalitis virus (Yoshii et al., 2013). Antibodies against B. afzelii and B. garinii were also detected in A. speciosus mice from western Japan (Ishiguro et al., 1995).

Larvae and nymphs recovered from A. speciosus in summer may have emerged from females and larvae, respectively, introduced during spring or early summer. However, if this speculation were correct, all individuals of D. bellulus would be completely extirpated from the area during winter, and engorged females would need to be introduced in spring or early summer and oviposited. Alternatively, both males and females may have been introduced during spring or early summer, successfully infested reproductive hosts, and oviposited. Such events are unlikely to occur repeatedly in the same location each year. As noted above, we observed infestation of D. bellulus larvae on A. speciosus over two consecutive years, and this tick species predominated on the host during the study period. Furthermore, we collected a D. bellulus larva on vegetation by flagging in April 2024, immediately after snowmelt (Supplemental Table 4), at Mt. Kinbou (38.681116879249, 139.8081449022993), near the area where small mammals were captured. Even under warm conditions, egg hatching of this species has been reported to require approximately two months after oviposition (Komine, 2025); therefore, this D. bellulus larva likely hatched from eggs oviposited before winter. These findings indicate that D. bellulus can survive winter in this area. Accordingly, our results do not support the above speculation and instead demonstrate that D. bellulus has established in the area. Further studies are required to elucidate the overwintering strategy of D. bellulus.

The current study demonstrates that larvae and nymphs of the southern tick species D. bellulus were identified on A. speciosus in northern Japan for the first time, confirming establishment of this species in forest environments in northern Japan. Further studies are needed to clarify seasonal variation, the frontline of establishment, pathogen prevalence, and overwintering strategies of this southern tick species. Moreover, elucidating the ecological and epidemiological roles of A. speciosus in forest ecosystems will be necessary to provide insights for the conservation and management of this endemic species.
