Early non-intravenous analgesia in prehospital trauma care: time to act
Authors: Julian Klug, Benedick Satari, Roland Albrecht, Alexander Fuchs, Urs Pietsch
Published: 2026

Background

Despite longstanding recognition of oligoanalgesia, effective pain management in prehospital trauma care remains a persistent challenge. Recent studies have emphasised the importance of initiating analgesia immediately at the scene, highlighting that non-intravenous strategies can facilitate timely treatment without delaying transport [1]. However, real-world data suggest that substantial gaps persist, even within systems staffed by physicians. In particular, barriers related to operational constraints and procedural factors, such as the difficulty of establishing intravenous access, may significantly contribute to undertreatment.

Main text

We read with great interest the article Pain first: rethinking early analgesia in emergency trauma care [1]. The authors compellingly argue that effective pain management should begin immediately at the scene and non-intravenous approaches can facilitate early relief without delaying transport. In our recent retrospective analysis of factors associated with prehospital pain management, we investigated determinants of analgesic underuse in helicopter emergency medical service (HEMS) care [2]. Here, we contribute present additional data that strongly supports these conclusions, while also identifying a critical barrier: the absence of intravenous access.

We analysed 6,815 HEMS missions involving trauma patients who had experienced on-scene pain of at least 3 on the numeric rating scale (NRS), had a Glasgow Coma Scale of at least 13, and had documented pain upon arrival. 5755 (84.4%) missions involved adult patients. The median on-scene NRS was 7.0 [IQR 5.0–8.0]. Despite receiving care from a physician, 26.8% of patients experienced inadequate pain management, arriving at the hospital with an NRS score greater than 3. Most concerning of all, 10.7% received no analgesia despite having documented pain.

Our univariable logistic regression analysis revealed that the omission of analgesia was associated with inadequate pain management (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.92–2.63, p < 0.001). Notably, the absence of intravenous (IV) access was strongly associated with the withholding of analgesia (OR 11.45, 95% CI 9.60-13.66). Although retrospective data cannot prove causality, this robust association suggests that the absence of IV access is a significant barrier to analgesia (Fig. 1).

Deprioritisation occurred more frequently in challenging operational rescue conditions, such as in winter (5.2% vs. 3.6%; OR 1.47, 95%CI 1.15–1.87, p = 0.002) and at night (5.7% vs. 4.5%; OR 1.28, 95% CI 0.75–2.18, p = 0.366). This aligns with the editorial’s context favouring non-IV analgesia, in remote areas, in adverse weather conditions and when there is difficult vascular access.

We urge the adoption of non-intravenous analgesia protocols as standard practice. Pain management should never be dependent on a successful IV cannulation. Oral transmucosal fentanyl citrate (OTFC), intranasal fentanyl or inhalational methoxyflurane offer practical solutions [3, 4]. Given methoxyflurane’s volatile anaesthetic properties, use in confined environments such as ambulances and helicopters without effective air extraction requires strict adherence to device instructions and consideration of occupational exposure limits, to prevent the unintended sedation of medical crews and pilots.

Pain management should be addressed immediately at the scene, alongside other life-saving interventions. Our findings from over 6,000 HEMS missions provide robust empirical support for this patient-centred approach, highlighting that failure to establish IV access should never be an excuse for withholding analgesia. Non-intravenous analgesic strategies are not merely alternatives but essential tools that ensure equitable, early, and effective pain management in all prehospital contexts.

Conclusion

We strongly support the call to recast pain relief as a patient-centred outcome and to enable its initiation from the outset of care. The evidence is clear: oligoanalgesia persists not due to a lack of options, but rather to systemic barriers and the outdated belief that pain management can be delayed. Additional non-intravenous analgesia represents a critical step toward more patient-centered prehospital care.
