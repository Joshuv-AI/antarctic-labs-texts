When social inequities dictate outcomes: the multidisciplinary call of cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Raffaele Brustia, Vincent Leroy, Alain Luciani, Julien Calderaro, Christophe Tournigand
Published: 2025

Liver-related mortality strikes hardest where deprivation is greatest. This sobering truth is powerfully illustrated by Vaz and colleagues,1 who show that even in Sweden—a country celebrated for its universal healthcare—patients with low income bear a disproportionate burden of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA). Linking Swedish registry data on household income and comorbidities in 1827 adults diagnosed with iCCA between 2011 and 2021 revealed that patients with low income had higher incidence rates, lower access to active treatment, and shorter survival.1 The findings dismantle the assumption that equity is guaranteed once care is free at the point of delivery.

The strength of the study lies in its capacity to link several nationwide, high-quality registers through Sweden's personal identification numbers, ensuring near-complete case capture and robust sociodemographic and comorbidity data. The inclusion of waiting times—from first suspicion in primary care to referral, MDT discussion, and initiation of treatment—provides rare insights into system-level delays that may influence outcomes. The authors also addressed the lack of registry data on systemic therapy prior to 2020 by developing an indirect algorithm based on oncology visits and cisplatin–gemcitabine treatment intervals. This pragmatic solution, showing 95% concordance with registry-recorded data after 2020, supports the robustness of their findings. Nevertheless, residual misclassification remains possible, especially for non-standard regimens or patients managed outside oncology departments. Furthermore, the absence of data on body mass index and tobacco exposure—key risk factors not captured—limits the ability to disentangle the interplay between socioeconomic status, biological risk, and outcomes. Finally, while disparities in incidence, treatment, and survival are convincingly reported, the study cannot fully capture the underlying mechanisms, such as diagnostic delays, referral practices, or patient-level barriers.

Sweden is not an outlier. Across Europe, iCCA remains a silent disease, often asymptomatic until advanced stages and frequently diagnosed in emergency settings, where outcomes are dismal.2,3 Notably, even in this study,1 nearly three-quarters of patients were diagnosed only after the onset of clinical symptoms, despite median waiting times surprisingly short: even when the system responds rapidly, iCCA is often detected too late. Silent risk factors such as alcohol consumption, obesity, diabetes, and viral hepatitis are common in deprived populations,2,4,5 and their coexistence can synergistically amplify the risk of advanced liver disease.5 When compounded by distance to hospitals, limited social support,6 low health literacy, and stigma,5 the gap to timely referral widens: one-quarter of patients still die during the index admission.3 Reducing stigma and raising awareness facilitate engagement across the care cascade,5 while addressing modifiable risks can ultimately improve survival.7

These patients form a silent population that does not lobby, rarely features in advocacy campaigns, and whose stories are seldom told. Their excessive and largely invisible mortality is one of the starkest expressions of health inequity.

This paradox is unsettling in its timing. While hepatology and oncology have advanced—imaging, molecular profiling,8 minimally invasive surgery, transplantation and immunotherapy2—disparities in who is diagnosed, treated, and survives have deepened. Cholangiocarcinoma exemplifies this need: regional disparities in Europe1,2,6 and socioeconomic barriers in the United States9 both translate into worse outcomes. The EASL–Lancet Liver Commission5,10 echoes this concern, calling for dismantling silos between primary and hospital-based care, strengthening cross-disciplinary collaboration, and extending services to underserved populations. Breaking silos also means looking beyond hepatology. Oncologists, radiologists, pathologists, surgeons, and primary care physicians all play a critical role in raising awareness and ensuring timely diagnosis of iCCA: stronger educational efforts are essential to improve referral pathways and to embed equity across Europe.

The Swedish data1 should therefore be read not as a national anomaly but as a mirror for Europe, with a clear lesson. Universal coverage removes financial barriers but not structural ones: diagnostic delay, remoteness, health literacy, and comorbidity. In some European countries, a shortage of healthcare professionals and limited access to general practitioners further compounds these inequities. As precision oncology expands—through next-generation sequencing and targeted therapies—the stakes grow higher. If access to innovation follows the same patterns as access to palliation, deprivation will continue to dictate outcomes. Cancer control strategies must embed equity at every stage: early detection, timely referral, clinical decision-making, and allocation of novel therapies. Yet stigma can be reduced—through education, peer support, and initiatives fostering empathy—helping patients engage in care and narrowing the survival gap.5

Vaz and colleagues1 remind us that iCCA is not only a disease of cells, but also of systems and societies. It is time to ensure that silent patients are heard, stigma is challenged, services are extended to the most deprived, and referral to specialist centers is no longer delayed—by breaking down silos in care.
