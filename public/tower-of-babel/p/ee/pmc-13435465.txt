The transmembrane segments of SARS-CoV-2 nsp3 govern viral intracellular trafficking
Authors: Manqi Cao, Lang Qin, Huaan Zhang, Wenhao Wang, Qiao Ling, Jiaxin Zhuang, Fushi Zhang, Zhihui Zhang, Weiyi Huang, Huijie Li, Yushan Xue, Jiayi Yang, Jiayue Liu, Liubing Du, Deyin Guo, Ji-An Pan, Xiaoxue Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfter entry, coronaviruses deliver their genomic RNA to the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment to build replication organelles, yet how the RNA first contacts membranes is unclear. Here, we demonstrate that the first transmembrane helix of nsp3 serves as an address code, inserting into the ER via SEC61A1 during translation and is then escorted to the ERGIC by the COPII factors SAR1A and LMAN1. Deletion or proline substitution of key residues in this helix trapped the RNA in the cytosol and crippled viral replication. A 24-residue peptide (Y24) modeled on the helix inhibited viral replication, and AAV9-delivered A20-2 peptide reduced lung viral RNA and pathology in mice. This host-targeted approach offers a potential broad-spectrum strategy against emerging coronaviruses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01587-y.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, nsp3, Transmembrane segment, Intracellular trafficking, SEC61, ERGIC

Introduction

After entry, positive-strand RNA viruses such as flaviviruses, noroviruses, picornaviruses and coronaviruses rearrange host intracellular membranes to build specialized replication compartments (den [1–3]). These membrane structures are essential for viral RNA synthesis (den [4]). Most of the required membranes are recruited from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus [5]. An early and still open question is how the viral components released during entry reach these membranes, a step that precedes and enables the subsequent formation of virus-induced replication organelles.

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the causative agent of COVID-19, uses its 30-kilobase positive-sense genomic RNA to translate two large polyproteins that are cleaved into 16 non-structural proteins (nsps) by the papain-like protease in nsp3 and the 3C-like protease in nsp5 [6–8]. Among these, nsp3, nsp4 and nsp6 all contain multiple transmembrane segments and cooperate to assemble double-membrane vesicles (DMVs), the sites of viral RNA replication [9–12]. When nsp3 and nsp4 are expressed alone, they are already sufficient to generate DMV-like structures, indicating that nsp6 is not strictly required for initiation [9, 13]. Instead, nsp6 accelerates the process by stimulating lipolysis of lipid droplets, thereby mobilizing additional fatty acids and phospholipids that enlarge and stabilize the DMV network [14]. Recent work has further shown that the prevalent nsp4 T492I mutation, found in all Delta and Omicron sub-lineages, enhances DMV formation by promoting nsp4 homodimerization and increasing ER membrane curvature, thereby facilitating viral transmission [15].

The hijacking of host membrane trafficking machinery is a conserved strategy across diverse positive-strand RNA viruses. Flaviviruses (Dengue virus and West Nile virus) remodel ER to generate vesicular packets that serve as replication compartments (den [2, 4]). Picornaviruses (poliovirus and coxsackievirus) replicate on Golgi-derived membranes, a process hijacking GBF1, Arf1, and PI4KIIIβ via viral protein 3A [16]. Foot-and-mouth disease virus requires Sar1, a COPII GTPase at ER exit sites, indicating pre-Golgi membranes support picornavirus replication [17].

Plant viruses similarly exploit endomembrane trafficking. Potyvirus 6K2 induces replication vesicles at ER exit sites that traffic along microfilaments to plasmodesmata [18]. Tomato bushy stunt virus co-opts ATG2 to deliver phospholipids to replication organelles [19]. Norwalk virus p22 mimics an ER export signal to hijack COPII vesicles, disrupting ER-Golgi transport [20].

Despite this diversity, a common theme emerges: viral proteins engage specific host trafficking factors to deliver their genomes to appropriate membranes [21]. For coronaviruses, while DMVs and their pore complexes have clarified how these chambers support RNA synthesis and how newly made RNAs are exported to the cytosol [9]. In contrast, the earliest step, how the material released from the incoming virion first contacts the membrane, remains poorly defined. The discharged cargo contains genomic RNA and nucleocapsid (N) protein. Studies in SARS-CoV have shown that replicons lacking the N gene replicate efficiently, suggesting that N is dispensable for membrane targeting in that context [22]. For SARS-CoV-2, N protein has been reported to possess a nuclear localization signal and interact with nucleolar proteins [23], but direct evidence for its role in directing viral RNA to intracellular membranes remains lacking. This absence of defined membrane localization data for N may indirectly support a scenario similar to SARS-CoV, wherein the primary membrane-targeting information resides elsewhere-most likely in the nascent viral polypeptides.

Once the viral genomic RNA is released from the particle it behaves like host mRNA, and the first open reading frame encodes a polyprotein that contains multiple transmembrane segments located in nsp3, nsp4, and nsp6 [9, 24]. These hydrophobic segments are recognized while translation is still in progress [25]. The signal recognition particle (SRP) binds to the emerging chain, arrests translation and docks the ribosome-mRNA complex to the SRP receptor, composed of SRPRA and SRPRB, on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [26, 27].

In addition to the SRP-dependent co-translational route, the GET and EMC tail-anchor (TA) pathways deliver multipass proteins to the ER after translation is complete, a process known as post-translational targeting [28, 29]. The choice among these routes reflects the position of the hydrophobic segment within the chain and its overall hydrophobicity [29]. A set of proteins localized on the ER membrane also plays critical roles in the targeting process, such as recruiting and translocating the nascent peptide into the ER lumen. These proteins include the SRP receptor complex, Sec61 complex, and Sec62-Sec63 complex, which mediate co-translational or post-translational translocation [30].

DMVs are found at the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), the membrane network that lies between the ER and the Golgi [31, 32]. This location implies that viral RNA and its products reach a membrane domain distinct from the bulk ER. Given that both SRP-dependent co-translational translocation and GET/EMC-mediated post-translational pathways can deliver proteins to the ER [29], it remains to be determined which route coronaviruses exploit for membrane targeting of their replication complexes. The presence of multiple transmembrane segments in the early viral polyprotein suggests that co-translational, post-translational mechanisms, or a combination thereof, could contribute to delivering the viral RNA to the ERGIC, where DMVs subsequently form.

Current antiviral development against coronaviruses has primarily targeted viral enzymes such as the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and main protease (Mpro), leading to successful drugs like remdesivir and nirmatrelvir [33, 34]. However, the continuous emergence of variants highlights the need for strategies with higher barriers to resistance. Targeting the early step of viral RNA delivery to intracellular membranes offers an attractive alternative: disrupting this conserved host-dependent process would prevent replication organelle formation entirely and is expected to confer broad-spectrum activity across coronaviruses [35].

Peptide-based inhibitors are particularly well-suited for interfering with such membrane-associated protein–protein interactions. Recent advances in peptide engineering, including hydrocarbon stapling, incorporation of non-natural amino acids, and fusion with cell-penetrating peptides, have largely overcome traditional limitations of poor stability and low membrane permeability, positioning peptides as a promising class of antivirals capable of targeting previously undruggable intracellular sites [36–38].

In this study, we delineate the molecular pathway by which SARS-CoV-2 commandeers early host translation machinery for the critical first step of its life cycle: the cotranslational targeting of its non-structural protein to intracellular membranes. We identify nsp3, specifically its first transmembrane segment (TM1), as the principal viral determinant of this process. Crucially, we leverage this mechanistic insight to develop A20-2, a host-derived peptide that disrupts the nsp3-SEC61A1 interaction, establishing a potent host-directed antiviral strategy with broad-spectrum activity against coronaviruses.

Results

Nsp3 can mediate co-translational membrane targeting

Signal peptides and transmembrane segments are recognized by complexes such as the SRP complex as they emerge from the ribosome [39]. This recognition directs the translation machinery to specific membrane sites, including the ER, where translation resumes [40]. Consequently, a portion of the RNA becomes membrane-associated.

To identify which nsps best direct co-translational membrane targeting, we expressed the first four nsps, nsp1, nsp2, nsp3 and nsp4, in HEK293T cells. These proteins are translated earliest from the incoming genomic RNA. Cells were swollen in hypotonic buffer and lysed by ten passages through a 20-gauge needle. After centrifugation at 1000 g, the cytosol was recovered in the supernatant and the membrane pellet was retained (Fig. 1A). RT-qPCR results showed that nsp3 RNA was enriched in the membrane fraction more than any other nsp tested; nsp4 RNA, which also encodes transmembrane segments, was membrane-associated to a lesser but still significant extent, whereas nsp1 and nsp2 RNAs remained largely cytosolic (Fig. 1B). These results indicate that nsp3 is the principal mediator of co-translational recruitment of viral RNA to intracellular membranes.

We next mapped the region within nsp3 that drives its co-translational membrane targeting. Full-length nsp3 comprises 1945 amino acids and contains four transmembrane segments distributed among several functional domains (Fig. 1C). We divided the protein into three fragments, nsp3.1, nsp3.2 and nsp3.3, each retaining intact domains and the predicted membrane spans. Immunofluorescence showed that nsp3.2 and nsp3.3 co-localized with ER and Golgi markers, whereas nsp3.1 remained cytosolic (Fig. 1D–E), confirming that transmembrane segments are required for membrane association. Line-scan analysis revealed stronger overlap of nsp3.2 and nsp3.3 with the ER marker than with the Golgi marker (Fig. 1F–K), which was also confirmed by quantitative analysis of colocalization using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Figure S1A). These data indicate that nsp3-mediated co-translational membrane targeting occurs primarily on ER-derived membranes.

To verify the membrane association of nsp3.2 and nsp3.3, we repeated the fractionation assay. After plasma-membrane disruption, lysates were centrifuged at 1000g to pellet heavy membranes (plasma membrane, nuclear envelope, nucleus-attached ER) and then at 12,000g to collect light membranes (Golgi, mitochondria, vacuoles) [41, 42]. The final supernatant served as the cytosol [41, 42]. Nsp3.2 was markedly enriched in the 1000 g pellet, whereas nsp3.1 and nsp3.3 were less abundant in this fraction (Figure S1B–E), indicating that nsp3.2 is the principal driver of membrane recruitment.

To streamline later experiments we combined the two spins into a single 15,000g centrifugation that cleanly separated cytosol (supernatant) from total membranes (pellet). Under this simplified scheme nsp3.2 again showed the least cytosolic contamination (Fig. 1L–N), confirming its strong membrane association.Together, the above data showed that nsp3 efficiently mediated a co-translational membrane targeting. Nsp3.2 and nsp3.3, which contain transmembrane segments account for this nsp3 activity in mediating the co-translational membrane targeting.

Nsp3 transmembrane segments are essential for viral co-translational membrane targeting

Transmembrane segments act as signals that host proteins recognize during co-translational membrane targeting. To learn which segment is critical, we expressed nsp3S, the C-terminal fragment that carries all four transmembrane spans (Fig. 2A). Earlier work showed that nsp3S plus nsp4 is sufficient to build DMV-like structures, so this fragment retains the full membrane-targeting capacity of nsp3 [9].

To define the contribution of each transmembrane segment, we generated nsp3S mutants lacking individual spans or all four (ΔTM1, ΔTM2, ΔTM3, ΔTM4, ΔTM1-4) (Fig. 2A). Cell fractionation showed that removal of the first span (ΔTM1) or the entire set (ΔTM1-4) shifted most of the protein to the cytosol, whereas loss of TM2–TM4 had only minor effects (Fig. 2B–D). Immunofluorescence confirmed these findings: ΔTM1 reduced ER/Golgi staining, and ΔTM1-4 produced diffuse cytoplasmic signal, indicating that TM1 is the critical element for co-translational membrane insertion (Fig. 2E and F). Quantification of the Pearson's correlation coefficient further demonstrated that deletion of TM1 or all four TMs significantly reduced colocalization with ER/Golgi markers compared to wild-type nsp3S (Figure S2A).

To test the role of the transmembrane segments in viral replication, we used our laboratory reverse-genetics systems for SARS-CoV-2 and MHV. For lab practice convenience, we generated a single-cycle SARS-CoV-2 replicon particle (VRP) (Figure S2B) similar to those described by Luis Enjuanes and Balaji Manicassamy [43]. Huh7.5 cells were co-transfected with an S-deleted SARS-CoV-2 replicon and a VSV-G expression plasmid. After 24 h, VRPs harvested carry VSV-G in their envelope. VSV-G permits entry into many cell lines, while the absence of the S gene prevents spread, so the particles replicate and transcribe the genome once but produce no progeny virus. This system reproduces the intracellular replication of authentic SARS-CoV-2 and can be handled under standard biosafety level 2 conditions.

We scanned the four transmembrane segments for residues that maintain the α-helix, the scaffold required for membrane insertion (Fig. 2G). In each span we replaced one core leucine or valine with proline: L4 in TM1, L6 in TM2, L4 in TM3 and V5 in TM4. The corresponding GFP-TM-mCherry reporters lost their sharp membrane ring and became cytosolic (Figure S3A–I), confirming helix disruption. When the same substitutions were engineered into the SARS-CoV-2 replicon, VRP yield dropped markedly (Fig. 2H) and less viral subgenomic RNAs were detected after infection (Fig. 2I). Parallel mutations introduced at V5 of TM1 and L3 of TM2 in the MHV genome (Figure S3J) gave identical results: infectious particle production and subsequent replication were almost abolished (Fig. 2J–N). The differential effects of TM1 and TM2 mutations on MHV replication between BHK and L2 cells may be attributed to the distinct modes of viral RNA delivery, transfected replicon versus viral infection, which create two different contexts for viral replication and transcription. Notably, TM1 mutations consistently impaired replication in both systems, underscoring its critical role. Thus, an intact α-helix in the nsp3 transmembrane segments is essential for coronavirus RNA synthesis.

Peptides derived from nsp3 transmembrane segments can inhibit viral replication

Because the nsp3 transmembrane segments are essential for co-translational membrane targeting and mutations in these segments severely impair viral replication (Fig. 2H–N), we asked whether peptides that mimic these segments can block the process. We replaced the linker of a GFP-linker-mCherry reporter with the sequences of TM1, TM2, TM3 or TM4, expressed each fusion protein in VRP-infected cells, and measured viral subgenomic RNA as a read-out of replication. All four TM peptides significantly reduced viral RNA levels (Fig. 3A), indicating that they can act as dominant-negative inhibitors of replication.

Because the full-length TM segments are too long for efficient cell delivery, we used the Chou-Fasman algorithm to scan each nsp3 TM for shorter, stably helical fragments. The N-terminal half of every TM was predicted to form a more conserved α-helix than the C-terminal half, with TM1 showing the highest helical propensity (Fig. 3B and C). We therefore split TM1 into TM1.1 and TM1.2 and tested the ability of each peptide to inhibit viral replication.

We inserted TM1.1 or TM1.2 between GFP and mCherry. Immunofluorescence showed that GFP-TM1.1-mCherry ringed the ER, whereas GFP-TM1.2-mCherry was largely cytosolic, confirming that TM1.1 forms the more stable helix (Figure S4A). When the same constructs were expressed in VRP-infected cells, TM1.1 reduced both genomic and subgenomic RNA compared with control levels (Fig. 3D–F).

To explore therapeutic potential of the TM1.1, we synthesized TM1.1 preceded by the cell-penetrating sequence TAT47-57 (YGRKKRRQRRR). The 24-aa chimera, YGRKKRRQRRRGSGIWFLLLSVCL (named Y24), was non-toxic at 100 µM (Fig. 3G). When added to VRP-infected cultures, Y24 significantly reduced genomic RNA levels at concentrations as low as 0.1 µM, although its effect on subgenomic RNA was less pronounced and did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3H and I).

Nsp3 directs viral RNA to intracellular membranes through several pathways

To determine how nsp3 reaches intracellular membranes, we performed immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) on nsp3S and identified potential proteins that escort translating complexes to the ER. These included SRPRB of the SRP pathway, SEC61/62/63 components, and COPII factors SAR1A and LMAN1 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Individual co-IP assays confirmed that nsp3S binds SEC61A1, SEC62, SEC63, ERGIC1, ERLEC1, SRPRB, LMAN1 and SAR1A (Fig. 4B–F and S5A–F), indicating that the SRP pathway, SEC61 complex, SEC62-SEC63 complex and COPII pathway all contribute to nsp3-mediated membrane targeting.

To test whether the co-IP-validated proteins promote nsp3 delivery to the ER or Golgi, we built a cleavage-reporting assay. A plasmid encodes GFP-CS-nsp3S, in which CS is the 3CLpro cleavage site, and separate plasmids encode 3CLpro-HA targeted to the ER (ER-3CL-HA) or Golgi (Golgi-3CL-HA) (Fig. 4G). When GFP-CS-nsp3S reaches the same compartment as 3CLpro, the linker is cut, GFP is released, and the full-length fusion protein level decreases. Thus, any factor that enhances nsp3 arrival at the ER or Golgi will increase the extent of cleavage and provide a quantitative read-out of translocation efficiency.

Using this assay we measured the ability of each candidate to promote nsp3 arrival at the ER or Golgi. SEC61A1 selectively increased ER cleavage (Fig. 4H–K and S6A–D), whereas SAR1A and LMAN1 selectively increased Golgi cleavage (Fig. 4L–O and S6E–L). In contrast, SRPRB, SEC62, SEC63, ERGIC1 and ERLEC1 gave no significant change at either membrane (Figure S5G–N and S6M–AB), indicating that SEC61A1 and the COPII components SAR1A/LMAN1 are the limiting factors that drive nsp3 to viral replication sites.

To confirm the importance of SEC61A1 and LMAN1 for viral replication, we depleted each protein with specific shRNAs and then infected cells with VRP. Quantification of viral subgenomic RNAs showed that knockdown of SEC61A1 or LMAN1 markedly reduced RNA levels, whereas knockdown of SEC62 had no effect, establishing that SEC61A1 and LMAN1 are essential for efficient coronavirus replication (Fig. 4P–S and S5O–V).

In summary, these data outline a two-step route: the Sec61 complex initiates nsp3 insertion at the ER membrane, after which SAR1A and LMAN1 escort the protein to the ERGIC.

SEC61A1-derived peptides can inhibit viral replication

Because the SEC61 complex is required for the first step of nsp3 recruitment to the ER, we asked whether blocking the SEC61A1-nsp3 interaction would stop replication.To map the binding site we first generated truncated fragments of SEC61A1, each approximately 120 amino acids in length and tested each for association with nsp3S. Subsequent co-IP assays revealed that all these fragments could interact with nsp3S (Figs. 5A and S7A). This interaction might be attributed to the presence of transmembrane domains in these fragments, leading to their spatial colocalization with nsp3S on membranes. To further investigate their functional roles, we overexpressed these four truncated constructs in cells and subsequently infected them with VRP. Notably, the SEC61A1 fragment spanning residues 121–240 exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect on viral subgenomic RNA but not genomic RNA compared to the other fragments (Figure S7B and C).

To obtain a short peptide with therapeutic potential, we divided residues 121–240 of SEC61A1 into six 20-aa peptides (A20-1 to A20-6) and expressed each as a GFP fusion. Co-IP screening revealed that only A20-2 binds nsp3S robustly, defining a minimal SEC61A1-derived nsp3-interacting motif (Fig. 5B and C).

Next, we asked whether A20-2 binding to nsp3S can be translated into an inhibitory effect on viral replication. After confirming that the six peptides did not affect cell proliferation robustly (Figure S7D and E), we expressed them in VRP-infected HEK293T cells (Fig. 5D and E) and MHV infected AML12 cells (Fig. 5F and G), and observed that A20-2 reduced genomic and subgenomic RNA levels the most (Fig. 5D–G), confirming antiviral activity. To further investigate the mechanism underlying this inhibitory effect, we co-expressed nsp3S and A20-2 in HEK293T cells. Fractionation assays revealed that overexpression of A20-2 altered the subcellular distribution of nsp3S, increasing its presence in the cytosolic fraction while reducing its association with the membrane fraction (Figure S7F–H). In parallel, we infected both wild-type and SEC61A1-knockdown cells with VRP and overexpressed either A20-2 or a control. The results showed that the antiviral effect of A20-2 was dependent on SEC61A1 (Figure S7I and J). Together, these findings demonstrate that A20-2 inhibits viral replication by affecting SEC61A1 function. To test A20-2 in vivo, we packaged the peptide into adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9), which preferentially transduces lung tissue. Eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice received AAV9-GFP (control) or AAV9-A20-2-GFP by tail-vein injection; after 14 days of transgene expression, animals were challenged intranasally with VRP. Histology revealed marked lung damage in control mice, whereas A20-2-expressing lungs were largely spared (Fig. 5H and I). qRT-PCR of lung homogenates showed a significant drop in viral RNA in the A20-2 group (Fig. 5J). Thus, a host-derived peptide that disrupts nsp3-SEC61A1 interaction acts as a potent host-directed antiviral in vivo.

Discussion

The initial contact between the released viral genome and the host membrane is a decisive yet poorly understood event. Our data provide direct experimental evidence that among the earliest translated viral proteins, nsp3, specifically its first transmembrane segment (TM1), is the principal viral determinant for this process. This finding aligns with and significantly extends prior structural work showing that nsp3 and nsp4 form the core architectural unit of DMVs [9], reveals TM1 of nsp3 as the critical signal that bridges viral translation with host membrane biogenesis. The essential role of an intact α-helix in TM1, and to a lesser extent in other TMs, for viral replication underscores that this is not merely a hydrophobic anchor but a structurally precise signal recognized by the host protein translocation apparatus.

Our interactome and functional dissection reveal that nsp3 does not rely on a single host pathway but rather integrates signals from multiple canonical ER-targeting routes. The simultaneous engagement of SRP components, the Sec61 translocon, and the post-translational Sec62/63 complex suggests a robust, fail-safe mechanism evolved by the virus to ensure efficient membrane insertion. More importantly, our 3CLpro cleavage reporter assay allowed us to functionally segregate these interactions into a coherent two-step pathway: (1) SEC61A1-dependent insertion at the ER, and (2) COPII-dependent anterograde trafficking to the ERGIC via SAR1A and LMAN1. This pathway elegantly explains the longstanding observation that coronavirus replication occurs at the ERGIC: the virus does not target the ERGIC directly but co-opts the natural flow of transport. The essential roles of SEC61A1 and LMAN1, but not SEC62, in viral replication further pinpoint the host machinery’s vulnerable nodes within this hijacked pathway. Although SRPRB, a core subunit of the SRP receptor, co-immunoprecipitated with nsp3S, functional assays using the 3CLpro cleavage reporter and knockdown experiments revealed no requirement for SRPRB in nsp3 membrane targeting. This apparent paradox may be explained by functional redundancy among SRP pathway components or by the possibility that SRPRB interacts transiently but is not rate-limiting under our overexpression conditions. Further studies are needed to dissect the precise role of SRP in coronavirus replication.

The demonstration that peptides mimicking nsp3's TM domains act as dominant-negative inhibitors validated the functional importance of this interface. In turn we shift the strategic from targeting the viral protein to targeting the more conserved host interaction partner, SEC61A1, a logic aimed at overcoming viral mutation and achieving broad-spectrum activity. The identification of A20-2 as a minimal, potent inhibitor of the nsp3-SEC61A1 interaction is a key finding. Its efficacy against both SARS-CoV-2 and MHV in vitro substantiates the broad-spectrum potential of targeting this host factor. The absence of severe cytotoxicity, particularly in relevant cell lines, underscores its therapeutic promise. Most significantly, the in vivo protection conferred by AAV9-delivered A20-2 in a mouse model establishes proof-of-concept for this host-directed approach. The differential sensitivity of genomic and subgenomic RNA to Y24 or A20-2 treatment may reflect the distinct requirements for DMV integrity in their synthesis. Subgenomic RNA production is thought to be more dependent on the specialized microenvironment of DMVs [3, 44], which could explain why perturbations in nsp3-mediated membrane targeting sometimes affect sgRNA more strongly than gRNA. Further investigation into the spatial organization of RNA synthesis within DMVs will help clarify this observation.

This work shifts the paradigm for understanding early coronavirus replication from a focus on static organelle structures to a dynamic understanding of a hijacked protein trafficking pathway. It positions the nsp3-SEC61A1 interface as a novel, vulnerable target for antiviral intervention. The A20-2 peptide, as a first-in-class inhibitor of this interaction, provides a valuable tool compound and a promising therapeutic lead.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our mechanistic dissection of nsp3 membrane insertion and trafficking largely relied on overexpression systems and engineered reporter constructs. While these approaches enabled detailed functional mapping, they may not fully recapitulate the stoichiometry and spatiotemporal dynamics of authentic viral infection. Second, although we identified SEC61A1 and LMAN1 as essential host factors, the interaction between nsp3 TM1 and the Sec61 channel was characterized primarily through co-immunoprecipitation and functional complementation assays; direct biochemical evidence, such as reconstitution of this interaction in purified systems, remains lacking. Third, the 3CLpro cleavage reporter assay, while informative, provides a surrogate readout for nsp3 membrane topology and trafficking; direct visualization of endogenous nsp3 localization and dynamics during infection would further substantiate our conclusions. Fourth, the A20-2 peptide was delivered using AAV9 in a prophylactic regimen in our in vivo model, which does not address therapeutic efficacy once infection is established. Additionally, the pharmacokinetic properties and bioavailability of the peptide itself, independent of viral delivery, warrant further optimization and characterization. Fifth, while we observed no overt cytotoxicity in cultured cells, the long-term consequences of modulating SEC61A1,an essential gatekeeper of cellular proteostasis, remain unexplored, and the therapeutic window of targeting this essential host factor requires careful evaluation in vivo. Finally, our studies were primarily conducted in immortalized cell lines; validation in primary cells and additional animal models, including models of pre-existing disease, will be important for assessing translational relevance. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be critical for advancing our mechanistic understanding and therapeutic development.

In conclusion, by decrypting the cotranslational targeting code of SARS-CoV-2, we have uncovered a critical vulnerability at the virus-host interface. The development and validation of A20-2 demonstrate that strategic disruption of this hijacked pathway is a viable and promising avenue for developing broad-spectrum antivirals against current and future coronavirus threats.

Methods

Cell culture and transfection

Human embryonic kidney epithelial cells (HEK293T), human cervical carcinoma cells (HeLa), human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Huh7.5), baby hamster kidney cells (BHK-21), rat lung epithelial cells (L2), and mouse hepatocytes (AML12) were cultured in DMEM (Cat# C11995500BT-1, ThermoFisher, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat# MK1124500, Mikx, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cat# 15140122, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Plasmid transfection for all cell lines was performed using Liposomal Transfection Reagent (Cat# 40802ES03, Yeasen, China) and Polyethyleneimine (PEI) (Cat# 40816ES03, Yeasen, China), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s recommended ratios.

Plasmid construction

Various SARS-CoV-2 nsp genes were amplified by Phanta® Max Master Mix (Cat# P515-02, Vazyme, China) from plasmids constructed previously (jin ref) and then subsequently cloned into LPC (ref pan), pcDNA3.1 (Cat# V79020, Thermo Fisher, USA), and pLVX(Cat# 632164, Clontech, USA) vectors. Truncated nsp3- and SEC61A1-expressing plasmids, GFP-TM-mCherry fusion protein-expressing plasmids, and GFP-CS-nsp3S-expressing plasmids were constructed using MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix (Cat# RM20523, ABclonal, China) kit according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

All the primers used for cloning were listed in Supplementary Table 3. Plasmids were isolated with EndoFree Mini Plasmid Kit II (Cat# DP118-02, TIANGEN BIOTECH, China) and validated by Sanger sequencing (Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. Guangzhou Branch, China).

Reagents and antibodies

All reagents and antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Viral systems

Virus replicon particle (VRP) production. The replicon plasmid was co-transfected with a vesicular stomatitis virus G protein (VSV-G) expression plasmid into Huh7.5 cells. Cell supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection, centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min to remove cell debris to obtain VRPs, and stored at −80 °C.

Mutant murine hepatitis viruses (MHVs) carrying the disruptive substitutions TM1-V5P and TM2-L3P were generated with the MHV reverse-genetics system established in our laboratory. Virions were produced and expanded in BHK-21 cells, titrated by the TCID50 assay, and subsequently used to infect L2 cells.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)

Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection with indicated plasmids, and total protein was extracted using Lysis buffer (Cat# KTSM1401, Alpalifebio, China) supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), 1 mM PMSF, and 1 mM Na3VO4. A portion of the lysate was reserved as the input control, while the remainder was incubated overnight at 4℃ with the pre-equilibrated magnetic tag antibody-conjugated bead (Cat# KTSM1301, Alpalifebio, China). The next day the beads were washed extensively, and bound proteins were eluted in 1× SDS loading buffer for Western blot analysis.

For mass spectrometry analysis, the nsp3S-HA fusion protein was overexpressed in HEK293T cells. Protein complexes were enriched using the Co-IP method described above. After elution, samples were digested with trypsin and subjected to Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. The resulting data were searched against relevant databases for retrieval and bioinformatics functional enrichment analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Coverslips were placed in 24-well plates, and 1 × 105 HeLa cells were seeded, cultured, and transfected. After 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% goat serum (Cat# 16210064, ThermoFisher, USA). Cells were then sequentially incubated with primary and fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a confocal microscope. Analysis of fluorescence intensity line profiles was performed using ImageJ software (2.16). For each region of interest, a straight line was drawn across the cellular structures (e.g. the nucleus, organelle boundaries) using the “Straight Line” tool. The “Plot Profile” function was then executed to generate a graphical output of fluorescence intensity (in arbitrary units, A.U.) as a function of distance (in pixels or micrometers, after spatial calibration) along the drawn line. The resulting data points were exported for further quantitative comparison, statistical analysis, and graphical presentation.

Cellular fractionation

Harvested cells were resuspended in Hypotonic Lysis Buffer (10 mM HEPES–KOH pH 7.5, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM DTT) and mechanically sheared by passing 10 times through a 26-gauge needle. The lysate was centrifuged at 1000 g, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant, representing the cytosolic fraction, was collected. The pellet was subsequently lysed with RIPA Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.3% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100) to obtain the membrane/organelle and nuclear fractions. Protein and RNA samples from different fractions were used for subsequent Western Blot or qRT-PCR analysis.

3CLpro-cleavage reporter assay

HEK293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding GFP-CS-nsp3S, the indicated host factor (or empty vector control), and either ER-3CL-HA or Golgi-3CL-HA. 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. To preserve nsp3S integrity and prevent aggregation, one aliquot of each lysate was incubated in SDS loading buffer at room temperature for 10 min without boiling prior to SDS-PAGE, as boiling was found to cause loss of nsp3S signal. Additionally, a second aliquot of the same lysate were treated at 100 °C for 10 min to reveal the cleaved GFP fragment. Western blot was performed using anti-GFP antibody to detect both the full-length GFP-CS-nsp3S fusion protein (~100 kDa) and cleaved GFP (~27 kDa). Signal intensities were quantified using Bio-Rad's Image Lab software. In single-protein expression experiments where only one host factor was tested per transfection, the signal from co-transfected mCherry was used as a loading control. The relative intensity was calculated as follows: Relative Intensity = GFP (or GFP-CS-nsp3S) Intensity / mCherry Intensity. In protein concentration gradient expression experiments where the amount of host factor plasmid was titrated, the signal intensity of the β-Actin protein band was used as the internal control, with the calculation formula as follows: Relative Intensity = GFP (or GFP-CS-nsp3S) Intensity/β-Actin Intensity. Increased cleavage efficiency was demonstrated by a decrease in the full-length GFP-CS-nsp3S signal.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells or tissues with TRIzol reagent (Cat# R411-02-AA, Vazyme, China) and reverse-transcribed with the RT kit R412-00 (Vazyme, China). SARS-CoV-2 genomic and subgenomic RNAs were quantified with gene-specific primers on an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system. GAPDH or β-actin served as the endogenous control, and viral RNA levels were calculated by the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method. In selected experiments, aliquots of the same PCR products were resolved on 1% agarose gels; band intensities were used for semi-quantitative estimation.

Knockdown

The pLKO.1-puro vector (Cat# 10878, Addgene, USA) was used to silence genes in cells. Gene-specific shRNA oligonucleotides were synthesized, phosphorylated, annealed, and cloned into pLKO.1-puro. The resulting plasmid was co-transfected with psPAX2 (Cat# 12260, Addgene, USA) and pMD2.G (Cat# 12259, Addgene, USA) into HEK293T cells to generate lentivirus. Virus-containing supernatant was harvested 48 h later and used to infect fresh target cells in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Cat# H9268, Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

Peptide design and activity validation

Short peptides spanning the nsp3-TM1 segment and the SEC61A-binding region of nsp3S were designed by sequence alignment. A cell-penetrating sequence (YGRKKRRQRRR) was appended to the N-terminus of Y24 to facilitate uptake. All peptides were prepared by standard solid-phase synthesis. Cytotoxicity was quantified with the CCK-8 assay. For antiviral testing, peptides were added to the medium at defined intervals before or after virus challenge; inhibition was measured by qRT-PCR and TCID50 assay.

Animal experiments

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University. Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice received AAV9 particles (Cat# 224687, Addgene, USA) encoding GFP or A20-2-GFP by tail-vein injection. After 14 days, the mice were challenged intranasally with 20 µL SARS-CoV-2 VRP (approximately 1 × 107 vg/mL, vg stands for vector genome). At 48 h post-challenge, the animals were euthanized. Lungs were excised: one lobe was fixed for histopathology (H&E), and the remainder was processed for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR quantification of viral RNA.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.0). Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post-hoc tests. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
