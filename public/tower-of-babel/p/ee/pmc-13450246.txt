Impact of RUNX1 mutations at distinct sites on the clinical characteristics and prognosis of patients with acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Yun Huang, Zhiquan Long, Yujiao Zhang, Jiaying Cheng, Junya Sun, Ling Jiang, Haipeng Fu, Xuejie Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients with Runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) mutations are categorized into the adverse-risk group with poor prognosis. However, the impact of RUNX1 mutations at distinct sites on clinical characteristics, chemosensitivity, and prognosis in AML remains incompletely defined. This study aims to elucidate this impact thereby furnishing robust evidence to refine precision therapeutic strategies for individuals harboring RUNX1-mutant AML. MethodsIn this study, 92 AML patients harboring RUNX1 mutation, were enrolled and stratified into five groups based on mutation location: RHD domain, TAD domain, ID domain, outside domain, and biallelic mutations. Baseline demographics, complete-remission (CR) rate and minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity rates after chemotherapy, as well as 2-year overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) were systematically compared.ResultsBiallelic-mutant patients exhibited the lowest CR rate, the highest 2-year relapse rate, and the poorest 2-year OS and EFS. Cox regression analysis identified single-site mutation as an independent favorable prognostic factor. Among patients harboring single-site RUNX1 mutation, ID-domain mutations group achieved the highest two-cycle CR and MRD negativity rates, whereas TAD-domain mutants displayed the lowest MRD-negativity rate.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings indicate that the specific locus of RUNX1 mutation modulates chemotherapy efficacy and outcome, providing critical insights for precision therapy in RUNX1mutant AML.Trial registrationThis study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Approval Number: NFEC-2025-505), and it has been reviewed and approved by the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR2500111148) and registered in the National Medical Research Information Filing Information System of China.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16238-1.

Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common type of acute leukemia in adults. Acquired genetic mutations and chromosomal alterations jointly contribute to the pathogenesis of AML. Mutations in the Runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) gene lead to abnormal hematopoiesis in the bone marrow [1]. RUNX1 mutations are detected in approximately 10% of patients with acute myeloid leukemia [2]. AML patients with RUNX1 mutations are categorized into the adverse-risk group with poor prognosis, and their survival rates after intensive chemotherapy are inferior to those of patients without such mutations [3].

The RUNX1 gene is a critical regulator of hematopoiesis; consequently, RUNX1 mutations are intimately associated with leukemogenesis and disease progression. RUNX1 deficiency reduces CEBPA transcription through conserved promoter and distal enhancer sites, impairing stem cell differentiation, increasing bone marrow monocytes, and decreasing granulocyte progenitors [4]. Introduction of the RUNX1-S291fs300X mutation into human CD34⁺ hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells and human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) enhanced their self-renewal capacity while attenuating granulocytic differentiation. This implies that RUNX1 mutations induce a specific transcriptional program that promotes the transformation of stem/progenitor cells into leukemic cells [5]. However, RUNX1 mutations alone are insufficient to drive leukemogenesis. Mutations in RUNX1 (as a second hit), CDC25C, epigenetic modifiers, splicing factors, and tumor suppressors have been reported to cooperatively induce myeloid malignancies [6].

RUNX1 is an essential transcription factor in hematopoiesis that forms a heterodimer with CBFβ. This complex drives target gene transcription following DNA binding via the Runt homology domain (RHD). RUNX1 contains three functional regions: the N-terminal RHD mediates DNA binding and CBFβ interaction; the C-terminal transactivation domain (TAD) recruits coactivators to enhance transcription; and the inhibitory domain (ID) confers transcriptional repression. Mutations in the RHD reduce RUNX1 DNA-binding affinity, whereas nonsense or frameshift mutations downstream of the RHD produce truncated proteins that act in a dominant-negative manner by sequestering CBFβ and occupying RUNX1-responsive cis elements [7, 8]. Notably, distinct RUNX1 domains differentially regulate its transcriptional output. C-terminally truncated RUNX1 mutants impair the DNA-damage response in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells [9], while the RHD mutant RUNX1-R174Q downregulates p53-dependent transcription and increases genomic instability in the granulomonocytic compartment [10].

Mutations that occur within the gene but at distinct positions can exert divergent biological effects and confer different prognostic implications in patients with AML. A paradigmatic example is CEBPA: lesions located within the bZIP region are classified as favorable alterations, whereas mutations at other sites do not modify the established risk stratification of these individuals [11]. Given that mutations in distinct RUNX1 domains differentially affect DNA binding, transcriptional regulation, and cellular functions including apoptosis and drug response, we stratified RUNX1 mutations according to the affected functional domain to investigate whether mutations in the coding regions of different RUNX1 domains affect the clinical characteristics, chemotherapy efficacy, and prognosis of patients with AML.

Patients and methods

Patients

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Approval Number: NFEC-2025-505), and it has been reviewed and approved by the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR2500111148) and registered in the National Medical Research Information Filing Information System of China. As an investigator-initiated, observational, retrospective clinical study, we collected information of patients with newly diagnosed AML who attended the Department of Hematology, Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University between 2018 and 2022. RUNX1 mutations were identified by next-generation sequencing, and the mutation type and position were annotated. Genomic DNA was extracted from bone marrow mononuclear cells of AML patients using a standard commercial DNA extraction kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Targeted NGS was performed to identify gene mutations using the Ion Torrent™ PGM sequencing platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sequencing library was constructed according to the standard protocol for the targeted gene panel, and raw sequencing data were processed with the corresponding Ion Reporter software for quality control and variant analysis. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of mutation detection, strict quality control criteria were applied: only sequencing data with a sequencing coverage of ≥ 500× were considered credible and included for variant identification. The threshold for defining a positive somatic mutation was set at avariant allele frequency (VAF) of 5%.

Mutations mapping to codons 231–612 were classified as RHD domain mutations; codons 807–1194, TAD domain mutations; and codons 1194–1314, ID domain mutations [12]. Mutations outside these regions were defined as outside-domain mutations, whereas cases harboring two distinct RUNX1 mutations were grouped as biallelic (Fig. 1). The domain boundaries follow the NM_001754.5 transcript and differ from classical exon numbering due to the non coding exon 1. The exclusion criteria were as following: (1) preceding hematological disorders, (2) therapy-related AML or (3) other carcinomas. Patients were diagnosed according to the French-American-British (FAB) and 2016 revision of the World Health.

Treatments

Patients eligible for standard induction therapy commonly received the ‘3 + 7’ regimen comprising daunorubicin (DNR, 60 mg/m2 on days 1–3) or idarubicin (IDA, 12 mg/m2 on days 1–3) with cytarabine (Ara-C, 100 mg/m2 on days 1–7). If CR was not achieved after the first induction, a second induction with the same regimen or regimen consisting of cytarabine (2 g/m2 twice on days 1–3) was administered. Patients ineligible for intensive chemotherapy are typically managed with alternative regimens, such as the VA regimen (Venetoclax 100 mg orally on day 1, 200 mg on day 2, then 400 mg daily on days 3–21 (or 3–28) of each 28-day cycle; Azacitidine: 75 mg/m²/day subcutaneously or intravenously on days 1–7 of each 28-day cycle), venetoclax plus decitabine regimen(Venetoclax: Day 1 100 mg orally once, Day 2 200 mg orally once, Day 3 300 mg orally once, Day 4 through Day 28 400 mg orally once daily; Decitabine: 20 mg/m² intravenously once daily on Days 1–5 of each cycle), CAG regimen(Cytarabine 10 mg/m² subcutaneously or intravenously every 12 h on days 1–14, Aclarubicin 14 mg/m² intravenously once daily on days 1–4, G-CSF 200 µg/m² subcutaneously once daily on days 1–14; withhold if WBC ≥ 20 × 10⁹/L).

If CR was not achieved after the first induction, a second induction with the same regimen was administered. After CR, all patients received two to four courses of consolidation chemotherapy (CMT) based on cytarabine (2.0–3.0 g/m2 twice on days 1–3).

Two standard conditioning regimens were used for patients undergoing transplantation in CR: (1) busulfan (Bu, 3.2 mg/kg/day on days − 7 to − 4) + cyclophosphamide (CY, 60 mg/kg/day on days − 3 and − 2); (2) Bu (3.2 mg/kg/day on days − 6 to − 3) + fludarabine (Flu, 30 mg/m2 on days − 6 to − 2) [13]. Prophylaxis for graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) at our centre included cyclosporine A (CsA) + methotrexate (MTX) + mycophenolate (MMF) for HLA-matched sibling donor (MSD) transplants. For HLA-haploidentical related donors (HID) and matched unrelated donor (MUD) transplants, CsA + MTX + antithymocyte globulin (ATG) and/or MMF was used [14].

Definition of clinical end points

Complete remission: Bone marrow blasts < 5%; absence of circulating blasts or blasts with Auer rods; absence of extramedullary disease; absolute neutrophil count ≥ 1.0 × 109/L; platelet count ≥ 100 × 109/L.

Partial remission: All haematologic criteria of complete remission; decrease of bone marrow blast percentage to 5–25%; decrease of pre-treatment bone marrow blast percentage by at least 50%.

No response: Patients evaluable for response but not meeting the criteria for complete remission, CRh, CRi, MLFS, or partial remission are categorised as having no response before the response landmark.

Relapse was defined as the re-occurrence of 5% leukemic blasts in BM, re-appearance of circulating blasts or development of extramedullary leukemia.

Minimal residual disease (MRD) was performed by multiparametric flow cytometry to detect abnormal leukemia populations with leukemia-associated immunophenotypes in total CD45+cells in patients with CR before each cycle of consolidation therapy. MRD negativity was defined as the detection of < 0.1% abnormal cells, and MRD positive was defined as the detection of ≥ 0.1% abnormal cells. Patients achieved CR were followed up for 2 years to calculate their relapse-free survival (RFS). RFS was measured from the date of first CR (CR1) until death, the first relapse, or the last follow-up in continuous CR [3].

Follow-up methods and procedures

Following informed consent, this study collected patients’ routine clinical information through three phases: baseline (at initial diagnosis), routine treatment period (during inpatient therapy and outpatient follow-up visits), and follow-up period (3 years after completion of standard treatment, with telephone follow-up every 6 months).

For patients who met the inclusion criteria but had died prior to study initiation, a waiver of informed consent was requested. Data for these patients were collected retrospectively through a one-time review of their previous outpatient and inpatient electronic medical records.

Statistical analysis

All clinical data were analyzed using SPSS (SPSS, Chicago, IL), Prism9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Median values and ranges were used for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Groups were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni and Holm methodsto control for familywise error rate. A corrected P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A corrected P-value between 0.05 and 0.1 was considered a trend toward significance. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to determine the influence of factors count on EFS and OS in AML patients after chemotherapy or allo-HSCT, and the results are expressed as hazard ratio (HR) with 95% CI. All statistical tests were 2-sided, a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, A corrected P-value between 0.05 and 0.1 was considered a trend toward significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics of RUNX1-mutant AML patients

This study enrolled 92 de novo AML patients with RUNX1 mutations, all of whom had their RUNX1 mutation sites determined by next-generation sequencing. Based on the mutation sites, the patients were divided into five groups: RHD domain mutations, TAD domain mutations, ID domain mutations, outside-domain mutations, and biallelic mutations, with 25, 16, 20, 24, and 7 patients in each group, accounting for 27.17%, 17.39%, 21.74%, 26.09%, and 7.61% of the total cohort, respectively. As shown in Table 1, baseline characteristics including age, sex, peripheral blood hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, and bone marrow blast percentage were comparable among the five subgroups. Induction chemotherapy regimens and rates of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation were also similar across groups. In this cohort, single-site RUNX1 mutations were more prevalent than multiple-site mutations. The distribution of mutation sites across different RUNX1 domains was relatively balanced, and mutational location was not associated with age, sex, initial clinical features (including peripheral blood counts and bone marrow blast percentage), or karyotype abnormalities.

Impact of CR rate

Among all RUNX1mutant AML in this cohort, 57.3% achieved complete remission (CR) after the first course of induction chemotherapy. The CR rates were 28.57% for the biallelic mutation group, 52% for the outside-domain mutation group, 64.71% for the ID domain mutation group, 46.67% for the TAD domain mutation group, and 72% for the RHD domain mutation group. However, there was no statistically significant difference among these groups (p = 0.2044) (Fig. 2A, B). After two courses of induction chemotherapy, the overall CR rate among these patients was 88.31%, with respective CR rates of 50%, 95.24%, 100%, 92.31%, and 81.82% for the five groups, which were statistically significant (p = 0.0134). Pairwise comparisons between each group using Fisher’s exact test and Bonferroni correction revealed that the CR rate after two courses of induction chemotherapy in the biallelic mutation group was significantly lower than that in the ID domain mutation group (p_adj = 0.043) (Fig. 2C, D).

Impact of MRD

We further analyzed the MRD status of patients who achieved CR after the first and second courses of chemotherapy. Due to the limited number of patients with CR in the biallelic mutation group, this group was not included in the comparison of MRD. Among the patients who achieved CR after the first course of chemotherapy in this cohort, only 41.86% were MRD-negative. There were borderline statistically significant differences in MRD negativity rates among the 4 single-site mutation groups(p = 0.049), with respective rates of 33.33% (Outside domain), 80% (ID domain), 28.57% (TAD domain), and 28.57% (RHD domain). The ID group showed a trend toward a higher MRD-negative rate compared to the RHD group. Although this difference did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction ( p_adj = 0.072 < 0.1), the large effect size (OR = 10) suggests a potentially clinically meaningful difference that warrants further investigation with larger sample sizes. After the second course of therapy, the overall MRD negativity rate increased to 67.35%, with respective rates of 73.33% (Outside domain), 81.82% (ID domain), 30% (TAD domain), and 76.92% (RHD domain), exhibiting a statistically significant difference(p = 0.0425). Although preliminary pairwise comparisons revealed differences between the TAD and ID groups, the TAD and RHD groups, and the TAD and Outside groups, none of the pairwise comparisons remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction.

We further compared the MRD-negative rates between the first and second cycles across the four groups. In the Outside group, the MRD-negative rate increased from 33.33% to 73.33%, with a marginally significant difference (p = 0.0574), suggesting a trend toward deepening response. The ID group has the highest MRD-negative rate of 80.00% at the first induction therapy and 81.82% at the second cycle. In the TAD group, the rate increased marginally from 28.57% to 30.00%, which has no statistical significance (P > 0.9999), indicating a persistently suboptimal depth of response. In contrast, the RHD group exhibited a marked increase in MRD-negative rate from 28.57% to 76.92%, which was statistically significant (P = 0.0213), demonstrating a notable delayed response (Fig. 3).

Impact of EFS, OS in 2 years

There were statistically significant differences in the relapse rates within 2 years among the five groups of patients (p = 0.0302). The 2-year relapse rates for the biallelic mutation group, the outside-domain mutation group, the ID domain mutation group, the TAD domain mutation group, and the RHD domain mutation group were 80%, 35.29%, 54.55%, 27.27%, and 11.76%, respectively. The RHD domain mutation group had the lowest relapse rate, while the biallelic mutation group had the highest. In pairwise comparisons between the groups, raw p-values indicated differences between the RHD and biallelic groups (p = 0.0093) and between the RHD and ID groups (p = 0.030); however, after correction for multiple comparisons, the adjusted p-values were 0.093 and 0.27, respectively, both exceeding 0.05 and thus not statistically significant (Fig. 4A).

There were statistically significant differences in the event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) curves among the five groups (p = 0.0245 for EFS, p = 0.0247 for OS). The biallelic mutation group had the lowest 2-year EFS and OS rates. No statistically significant differences in event-free survival or overall survival were observed among the remaining four monoallelic mutation subgroups. (Fig. 4B, C). As shown in Table 1, the majority of patients underwent Allo-HSCT. Therefore, we further stratified patients in each group according to whether they received Allo-HSCT and examined their EFS and OS. As shown in Fig. 4D, E, for the entire cohort, patients who received Allo-HSCT demonstrated superior EFS and OS compared with those who did not. As for the 5 subgroups, no statistically significant differences were observed except for 2-year OS in the RHD and ID domain mutation groups. These data may suggest that allo-HSCT could improve 2-year OS in patients with RHD and ID domain mutations.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses for EFS and OS are summarized in Fig. 5A and B. As the co-mutation analysis presented earlier demonstrated that the frequencies of other mutations did not differ among these patients, with the exception of IDH1 and IDH2, we did not incorporate the presence of concomitant other mutations into the Cox regression analysis. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the presence of concomitant IDH1 or IDH2 mutations did not demonstrate significant differences in univariate Cox regression analyses for OS or EFS, indicating that these mutations do not influence survival outcomes in the patients of this cohort. However, Univariate Cox regression identified platelet count > 49 × 10⁹/L as a significant predictor of EFS (p < 0.05), whereas age > 60 years and single-site mutations in RUNX1 gene approached significance (p ≈ 0.05); consequently, these three variables were incorporated into the multivariate Cox model. In multivariate analysis, platelet count > 49 × 10⁹/L remained an independent adverse predictor of EFS (p < 0.05), whereas single-site mutations conferred a favorable prognostic influence (p < 0.05). However, None of the listed variables demonstrated a statistically significant association with OS.

Discussion

Although RUNX1 mutation is widely recognized as an adverse prognostic factor in AML [15], the precise and in-depth mechanisms by which it influences AML remain incompletely understood. Studies have shown that certain co-mutations modulate clinical features and complete-remission rates in the context of RUNX1-mutant AML. RUNX1-mutant AML patients with 3, or ≥ 4 co-mutations showed much lower CR rate than that with 2 additional mutations [16]. We observed that only the frequencies of IDH1 and IDH2 mutations differed among patients with RUNX1 mutations at distinct sites; however, the presence of concomitant IDH1 or IDH2 mutations did not alter patient survival. We speculate that the lack of influence of IDH1/2 mutations on treatment outcomes in our study may be attributable to the adverse prognostic impact of RUNX1 mutations masking any effect of IDH1/2 mutations. Indeed, according to the ELN guidelines, patients harboring both IDH1/2 and RUNX1 mutations are classified into the same high-risk adverse prognostic group as those with RUNX1 mutations alone.

Others have reported elevated PEAR1 expression in these patients, with modelling identifying PEAR1 as one of the strongest single-gene predictors of survival, particularly in younger individuals [17].Previous studies have indicated that cytarabine upregulate RUNX1 expression in AML cells, which has been associated with the induction of apoptosis and favorable therapeutic responses. Notably, loss-of-function mutations within the RUNX1 RHD have been linked to impaired caspase-3 cleavage and diminished pro-apoptotic activity, suggesting a potential mechanism by which such alterations could contribute to cytarabine resistance in AML [18]. However, investigations focusing on the impact of the precise mutation site remain scarce. This study demonstrates that the anatomical location of the RUNX1 mutation is associated with therapeutic response and prognosis.

The CR rate post-treatment reflects overall efficacy, while the MRD negativity rate indicates the depth of remission and correlates with relapse and overall survival [19]. Based on the results of complete remission rate and MRD negativity rate, patients with ID domain mutations exhibited more favorable early treatment responses. However, the ID domain mutation group had the highest relapse rate and the shortest EFS among all single-site RUNX1-mutant cases. Intriguingly, ID domain mutation group attained the highest OS within the single-site cohort. We postulate that the seemingly favorable initial chemotherapy efficacy in the ID-domain mutation group could underlie this paradox, enabling effective salvage re-induction therapy after relapse and thereby prolonging overall survival.

Although pairwise comparisons did not remain significant after multiple comparison correction, the TAD group exhibited a markedly lower MRD-negative rate (30%) compared to the RHD (77%), ID (82%), and Outside domain (73%) groups, with corresponding odds ratios of 7.8, 13.5, and 6.9, respectively. These effect sizes suggest a clinically meaningful trend, indicating that the TAD group may harbor distinct characteristics. Further investigation with larger sample sizes is warranted to confirm these findings. The MRD-negative rates between the first and second cycles suggest that chemotherapy may be less effective in clearing MRD in the TAD group. Patients in the ID and TAD groups did not achieve further MRD negativity after re-induction with the same regimen, whereas those in the Outside and RHD groups showed an increasing trend in MRD-negative rates during the second cycle. This may indicate that for patients with ID or TAD domain mutations, if MRD remain positive after the first cycle of chemotherapy, alternative non-chemotherapy strategies may be considered in the second cycle, as re-induction with the same regimen probably not lead to MRD clearance. In contrast, for patients with Outside or RHD domain mutations, even if MRD is not cleared after the first cycle, re-induction with the same regimen may remain a viable option, as MRD negativity may still be achievable during the second cycle.

Patients with RUNX1 mutations are classified as adverse-risk according to the ELN classification, and hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) is the preferred strategy for the adverse risk group [20]. RUNX1mutant AML patients who undergo allo-HSCT exhibit significantly superior survival compared with those who do not receive allo-HSCT [21]. Analyses of the EBMT transplant registry and a Chinese retrospective study indicate that, among patients receiving allo-HSCT, the presence of RUNX1 mutations no longer influences clinical outcomes. These data suggest that the adverse impact of RUNX1 mutations in AML can be overcome when transplantation is performed during first complete remission [22, 23]. We infer that this may explain why the single-site mutation groups, despite differing in the depth of chemotherapy remission, showed no significant differences in 2-year relapse rate, EFS, or OS. Even more, neither the CR rate after induction chemotherapy nor the MRD-negativity rate demonstrated a statistically significant impact on EFS or OS in the Cox regression analysis. In other words, Allo-HSCT improved the prognosis of patients with poor chemotherapy response, consequently, the influence of mutation-site-associated differences in remission depth on patient outcomes may be attenuated. The discrepancy between the overall cohort and subgroup analyses is likely due to diminished statistical power in smaller subgroups and nonrandom allocation of transplantation in this retrospective study. The absence of differences in EFS across all subgroups aligns with the finding that inter-group differences in relapse rate did not reach statistical significance after correction.

Although RUNX1mutant AML patients can derive benefit from allo-HSCT, the RUNX1 mutation is frequently associated with advanced age [2], which often precludes transplant eligibility. Moreover, our data reveal that, allo-HSCT appeared unable to improve EFS and OS in certain subgroups of patients. Patients with biallelic mutations in RUNX1 continue to exhibit inferior 2-year relapse, EFS, and OS rates, reflecting the disadvantage already evident after two induction cycles. This suggests that the adverse impact observed during chemotherapy is not reversed by transplantation. Consequently, RUNX1-mutant patients may require therapeutic strategies beyond standard front-line chemotherapy and allo-HSCT. Prior reports indicate that RUNX1 mutations confer poor outcomes in patients receiving conventional IA3 + 7 induction, yet do not adversely affect CR or EFS in those treated with venetoclax plus hypomethylating agents [24]. An independent cohort likewise demonstrated similar CR rates between RUNX1mutant and wild-type AML under venetoclax-based regimens, implying heightened sensitivity to VEN-containing therapy [25]. Additionally, RUNX1-mutant blasts exhibit impaired ribosomal biogenesis and differentiation, rendering them selectively vulnerable to the protein-translation inhibitor homoharringtonine (omacetaxine) [26]. Regrettably, as enumerated in Table 1, the number of patients in our cohort who received therapeutic regimens other than IA3 + 7 was too small to enable a meaningful comparison of efficacy between alternative therapies and standard chemotherapy.

Finally, we must acknowledge that our study, as a retrospective, single-center clinical investigation, has certain limitations. Due to the size of our cohort, particularly the limited number of patients in the biallelic mutation group, we were unable to further compare and evaluate the impact of allo-HSCT or therapeutic regimens other than IA3 + 7 on outcomes in patients with RUNX1 mutations. Perhaps with the expansion of the cohort, especially with the inclusion of more patients receiving diverse salvage therapy, more robust conclusions may be drawn, and differences in prognosis among distinct single-site mutation subgroups may be further elucidated. Additionally, no in vitro or in vivo functional experiments were performed to validate the mechanistic role of RUNX1 mutation domains in modulating chemotherapy sensitivity. Therefore, the observed associations between mutation site and therapeutic response should be interpreted as correlative rather than causative, and further functional studies are warranted to clarify the underlying biological mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, biallelic RUNX1 mutations constitute the most unfavorable subset among RUNX1mutant AML patients, characterized by markedly inferior chemotherapeutic responses and dismal prognosis. Patients harboring single-site mutations in RUNX1 gene appear to derive greater benefit from induction therapy than their biallelic counterparts. ID-domain mutations appear to be associated with improved initial chemotherapy efficacy, yet relapse remains frequent, whereas TAD-domain mutations yield the shallowest depth of remission. These findings provide a rational framework for tailoring therapeutic strategies in AML with RUNX1 mutation.
