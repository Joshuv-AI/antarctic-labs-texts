Prediction of preeclampsia at 11–14 weeks of pregnancy by using FMF score with a history and uterine artery doppler: A prospective study
Authors: Dipali Prasad, Ruchi Gupta, Ayesha Parveen, Sadia Parween, Poonam Poonam, Manoj Kumar Choudhary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Preeclampsia is a pregnancy-specific hypertensive disorder with serious complications contributing to high maternal and fetal morbidity.Aim of the Study:It is to predict preeclampsia at 11–14 weeks of pregnancy with the objective of comparing sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV of FMF Score with a history and on adding color doppler.Materials and Methods:This was a prospective cohort study of 1 year duration in antenatal women between 11 and 14 weeks of gestation visiting IGIMS antenatal OPD for screening of preeclampsia after taking the consent. The research comprised individuals who were prepared to be followed up after a single-ton pregnancy.Results:There was a significant difference (W = 1876.500, P = 0.006), with the median MAP (mmHg) being highest in the preeclampsia group. There was a moderate negative correlation between Mean PI and Final FMF Score with History + Doppler, and this correlation was statistically significant (rho = –0.35, P = 0.002). In the present study, the final FMF score using history alone (cutoff: <150) demonstrated a sensitivity of 82.4%, specificity of 53.4%, PPV of 40.6%, NPV of 88.7%, and diagnostic accuracy of 61.5%. When Doppler findings were included (history + Doppler), the final FMF score (cutoff: <150) showed a sensitivity of 67.6%, specificity of 90.9%, PPV of 74.2%, NPV of 87.9%, and diagnostic accuracy of 84.4%.Conclusion:This screening test enables us to predict preeclampsia before its clinical presentation. It can be used as a reliable and a cost-effective screening test.

Introduction

Typical symptoms of preeclampsia (PE) after 20 weeks of pregnancy include high blood pressure and significant protein in the urine. In India, 29.54% of maternal fatalities occur as a result of severe preeclampsia and associated complications, whereas 14% of maternal deaths globally occur as a result of this condition.[12] Interim data from the National Eclampsia Registry (NER) by FOGSI-ICOG indicate a high prevalence of hypertensive diseases during pregnancy, with a notable incidence of preeclampsia cases. Nonetheless, the true frequency may be substantially greater, as numerous preeclampsia cases handled by peripheral health workers remain unreported. The prevalence of preeclampsia was documented as 10.3% in the NER 2013 data. Preeclampsia is responsible for the deaths of 76,000 mothers and 500,000 infants each year. Two forms of preeclampsia (PE) exist: preterm, which occurs before 37 weeks of gestation, and term, which occurs at or after 37 weeks of gestation. Preterm PE is more likely to cause worse maternal and perinatal outcomes than late-onset or term PE. The incidence of preeclampsia is higher in low-resource nations than in high-resource ones. Predicting PE in the first trimester is essential for identifying women at elevated risk of developing the illness, facilitating early intervention to enhance placentation and mitigate both the incidence and severity of the problem.[34] Given the intricate nature of preeclampsia (PE), it is essential to establish a prediction procedure utilizing maternal features, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and uterine artery Doppler assessments during the 11-14 week gestational period, especially in nation such as India. The ASPRE trial indicated that in high-risk pregnancies, the administration of aspirin can decrease the incidence of early preeclampsia (before 32 weeks) by around 90% and preterm preeclampsia by 60%.[5] If a pregnant woman is at high risk of preterm preeclampsia (before 37 weeks), she can be given aspirin (150 mg daily from 11–14 weeks to 36 weeks) at this screening period.

Most studies rely on multiple biomarkers for predicting preeclampsia. Expensive biomarkers are excluded in this study, as no reliable single biomarker has been identified yet for this purpose, particularly in resource-limited settings like India. The primary physician can predict preeclampsia by using FMF Score even without using costly biomarker.

This study examines the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the FMF Score compared with history alone and colour Doppler for the prediction of preeclampsia between 11 and 14 weeks of gestation.

Materials and Methods

Antenatal women between 11 and 14 weeks of gestation visiting IGIMS antenatal OPD were recruited in the study after taking the consent. This was a prospective cohort study of 1 year duration. The research comprised individuals who were prepared to be followed up after a single-ton pregnancy. The research did not include patients who were treated for hypertension, had a history of multiple pregnancies, or had a congenital defect. The research had a sample size of 150 patients. Only 122 patients were followed up with, while 28 were lost to follow up. History was taken, which included the patient’s particular age, para, past history (systemic lupus erythematosus, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of renal disorders, history of artificial reproductive techniques, method of conception), and personal history, including smoking. A general physical examination was conducted. Height and weight were calculated. BMI was calculated. Obstetric examination was conducted. BP measurement: the patient was asked to sit comfortably with their arms rested well. A sphygmomanometer was used. A correct-size adult BP cuff was used. It was positioned at the level of the heart. After 5 minutes of rest, BP was calculated in both arms at a 1-minute interval each. The mean arterial pressure was calculated. The patient was advised to get a transabdominal ultrasound at the Radiology Department of IGIMS. Dates were confirmed, fetal cardiac activity seen, and an NT (nasal translucency) NB (nasal bone) scan was done, followed by a Doppler study. A Pulsatility Index (UtA-PI) of the uterine arteries was recorded. These parameters were entered in the FMF (Fetal Medicine Foundation) calculator available on the FMF website. The variables are entered, and at the end of it, 2 scores are obtained: one score gives the risk based on maternal history and the other score is based on maternal characteristics plus Doppler parameters. Patients who had scores more than 1:150 were considered low-risk. Patients with scores less than 1:150 were considered high-risk and started on with tablet aspirin 150 mg once a day. Proteinuria and other severe PE symptoms, as well as high blood pressure beyond 20 weeks of pregnancy, were used to measure the outcomes. We monitored our patients right up until their pregnancies ended. Low birth weight, oligohydramnios, and intrauterine growth restriction were other outcomes.

Discussion

The study’s findings indicate that women who experienced preeclampsia and associated complications exhibited demographic similarities to those who did not. Preeclampsia risk factors have been identified in multiple studies. These include having antiphospholipid antibodies, a history of preeclampsia, diabetes, multiple pregnancies, a family history of the condition, nulliparity, a high body mass index (BMI) before or during pregnancy, a mother’s age being 40 or older, renal disease, hypertension, and an elevated BMI.[6] Our study was unable to reproduce the results due to its small sample size and the fact that participants were drawn from the general community, with a majority of participants being between the ages of 20 and 40.

In the group that did not experience preeclampsia, the mean (SD) of MAP (mmHg) was 77.49 (7.56), but in the group that did, it was 82.74 (9.49). The two groups differed significantly in terms of MAP (W = 1876.500, P = 0.006), with the preeclampsia group having the highest median MAP. Gasse C indicates that mean arterial pressure (MAP) serves as a significant predictor of prenatal hypertension and preeclampsia in nulliparous women, exhibiting a strong correlation with term preeclampsia (0.73; 95% CI: 0.70-0.76).[7] Zhu et al.[8] found that MAP demonstrated superior performance compared to other methods. Indicators at 11-14 weeks of gestation demonstrate efficacy in forecasting preeclampsia, achieving an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.75-0.99). A moderate negative connection (rho = –0.35, P = 0.002) was discovered between the Mean Pulsatility Index (PI) and the Final FMF Score with History + Doppler, according to the research. When using History + Doppler, the Final FMF Score decreased by 645.96 units for every unit increase in Mean PI.

The uterine artery pulsatility index (PI) is a useful tool for screening pregnant women between 11 and 14 weeks who may be at risk of preeclampsia and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). Based on their research, Narang et al.[9] found that at a threshold of 1.70, this measure has a sensitivity of 75.9% and a specificity of 79.2%. Rashid et al.[10] assessed the specificity and sensitivity of PI at 95% and 66%, respectively, finding that uterine artery Doppler is beneficial for identifying preeclampsia and IUGR in early gestation. In the study, 34% of patients displayed a diastolic notch, with almost all subsequently diagnosed with preeclampsia, and 19.8% delivered infants with intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). In this investigation, the Final FMF Score with history (cutoff: <150) had a sensitivity of 82.4%, indicating it accurately detected 82.4% of true positives. The specificity was 53.4%, signifying that 53.4% of genuine negatives were accurately recognized. The NPV was 88.7% and the PPV was 40.6%, for a total of 61.5% accuracy in the diagnosis. With the addition of Doppler (cutoff: <150), the specificity reached 90.9% and the sensitivity reached 67.6%, suggesting a strong ability to identify people who do not have the disorder. There was an 84.4% diagnostic accuracy rate, an 87.9% negative predictive value, and a 74.2% positive predictive value.

The AUROC curve in this study indicated that the FMF score with history had an area of 0.731, which climbed to 0.861 with the inclusion of Doppler. The diagnostic odds ratio for PE was 5.86 (95% CI: 2.21-15.57) based solely on history, and 20.58 (95% CI: 7.58-55.91) when Doppler was included. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for preterm PE was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.74-0.82), and the odds ratio was 16.7 (95% CI: 12.3-22.6) and positive likelihood ratio was 6.64 (95% CI: 5.77-7.63), as demonstrated by Rijo Mathew Choorakuttil et al.[11]

Conclusion

FMF Score with history has higher sensitivity but lower specificity and overall accuracy. It may be better for ruling out a PE but has a higher chance of false positives. FMF Score with History + Doppler offers improved specificity, PPV, and overall diagnostic accuracy. The combination of history and Doppler seems to be a more reliable diagnostic tool overall, with better accuracy and fewer false positives. When it comes to screening for preeclampsia in pregnant women, however, identifying those at high risk requires minimizing the amount of false-negative results. It is important to use the FMF score in combination with history and adding Doppler to stratify the risk for preterm PE in pregnant women. This will allow for the initiation of prophylactic low-dose aspirin in this group of Asian Indian women.
