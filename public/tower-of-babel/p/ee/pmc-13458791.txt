Age predicts long-term functional outcomes following vertebroplasty for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jeremy Tze En Lim, Yong Ng, Yee Gen Lim, Zhixing Marcus Ling, Reuben Chee Cheong Soh, Lei Jiang, Christian Hwee Yee Heng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVertebroplasty provides effective pain relief for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures; however, determinants of long-term functional recovery remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to evaluate baseline predictors of long-term patient-reported outcomes following vertebroplasty.MethodsNinety-two patients who underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty between 2010 and 2022 with complete patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) at baseline, 6 months, and 2 years were included. The primary outcome was Short Form-36 (SF-36) Physical Function at 2 years. Multivariable linear regression models adjusted for baseline PROMs were used to evaluate associations with age, sex, vertebral region, and number of treated levels.ResultsMean age was 76.5±8.4 years, and 82.6% were female. Significant improvements were observed in Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores (77.3 to 25.3), SF-36 Physical Function (9.2 to 41.8), and visual analogue scale (VAS) back pain scores (8.0 to 1.7) at 2 years (all P<0.001). Increasing age was independently associated with poorer 2-year SF-36 Physical Function [β=−17.45 per 10-year increase; 95% confidence interval (CI): −24.43 to −10.47; P<0.001]. Procedural variables were not associated with postoperative outcomes. Baseline physical function demonstrated moderate correlation with postoperative outcomes (r=0.40–0.44) but was not independently predictive after adjustment.ConclusionsVertebroplasty was associated with sustained improvement in pain and function. Increasing age was the strongest observed independent predictor of poorer long-term physical function within this cohort, whereas procedural variables were not significantly associated with postoperative outcomes. These findings suggest that long-term functional recovery following vertebroplasty may be associated more strongly with patient-related than procedural factors within this cohort.Keywords: Vertebroplasty, osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture, patient-reported outcomes, frailty, ageing

Highlight box

Key findings

• Vertebroplasty was associated with sustained improvements in pain and function at 2 years.

• Increasing age was associated with poorer long-term physical function.

• Procedural variables were not significantly associated with postoperative outcomes in this cohort.

What is known and what is new?

• Vertebroplasty improves pain and disability in osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. However, predictors of long-term recovery remain incompletely defined.

• This study suggests that patient-related factors may demonstrate stronger associations with long-term functional recovery than procedural characteristics within this cohort.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Older patients may require more comprehensive perioperative optimisation and rehabilitation strategies following vertebroplasty.

• Future prospective studies incorporating frailty assessment, osteoporosis-management variables, and radiographic parameters are warranted.

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures are a common cause of pain, disability, and healthcare utilisation in ageing populations worldwide (1,2). Percutaneous vertebroplasty has been widely adopted as a minimally invasive treatment for symptomatic fractures, providing vertebral stabilisation and rapid pain relief in appropriately selected patients.

Several clinical studies have reported improvements in pain and functional outcomes following vertebroplasty (3,4). However, the magnitude and durability of treatment benefit remain heterogeneous across patient populations. While sham-controlled trials have questioned the magnitude of treatment effects in certain settings (5,6), observational studies consistently demonstrate clinically meaningful improvements in patient-reported outcomes following vertebral augmentation.

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and the Short Form-36 (SF-36), are widely used to assess recovery following vertebroplasty (7,8). However, most studies focus primarily on average treatment effects, with comparatively limited attention directed towards identifying baseline predictors of postoperative recovery.

Baseline functional status, demographic characteristics, psychological health, and procedural variables such as the number or location of treated vertebral levels may all influence postoperative outcomes. However, the relative contribution of these factors to longitudinal PROM trajectories following vertebroplasty remains incompletely defined.

A clearer understanding of predictors of postoperative recovery may improve patient counselling, expectation management, and interpretation of PROM trajectories following vertebral augmentation. Given the interplay between osteoporosis, frailty, and sarcopenia in ageing populations, understanding predictors of functional recovery is increasingly important in osteoporotic spine care. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate demographic and procedural predictors of patient-reported outcomes following vertebroplasty at 6 months and 2 years. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0131/rc).

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The protocol was approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB No. 2023/2214), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty at a single tertiary referral centre between January 2010 and August 2022. The aim was to identify baseline predictors of patient-reported outcomes at 6 months and 2 years following vertebroplasty for symptomatic vertebral compression fractures.

Inclusion criteria and outcomes

Patients with symptomatic vertebral compression fractures treated with vertebroplasty and complete PROM data at baseline, 6 months, and 2 years were included. PROMs were collected through a prospectively maintained institutional registry at baseline, six months, and two years. Follow-up assessments were obtained during routine clinical review, with telephone or electronic follow-up performed when required according to registry protocols.

Patients undergoing kyphoplasty, revision vertebral augmentation, concomitant spinal fusion, malignancy-related fractures, or with incomplete data were excluded. A total of 92 patients with complete PROM data met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Baseline demographic variables included age, sex, race, and body mass index (BMI). Procedural variables included number of treated levels (single versus multi-level) and vertebral region (thoracic, thoracolumbar, or lumbar). PROMs included the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), neurogenic symptom severity score, visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for back and lower limb pain, and Short Form-36 (SF-36), recorded preoperatively and at 6 months and 2 years. Due to a zero-inflated baseline distribution, the neurogenic symptom severity score was not included in multivariable analyses.

The primary outcome was SF-36 Physical Function at 2 years. Secondary outcomes included SF-36 Physical Function at 6 months, ODI, VAS back pain, and other SF-36 domains.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess associations between baseline variables and postoperative PROMs. Multivariable linear regression models were used to identify independent predictors, adjusting for baseline PROM scores.

Procedural variables were selected a priori based on their potential clinical relevance and prior literature suggesting possible associations between fracture location, treatment extent, and postoperative outcomes following vertebral augmentation. Prespecified predictors included age (per 10-year increase), sex, number of treated levels, and vertebral region. Robust standard errors were applied, and multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Confidence intervals (CIs) are reported from the original regression models and should be interpreted alongside adjusted P values. Analyses were performed using R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical significance was defined using Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P values (q<0.05). Secondary outcomes were considered exploratory.

Correlation analysis

Correlation results are presented in Table 2. Baseline SF-36 Physical Function demonstrated moderate positive correlations with postoperative physical function at 6 months (r=0.44) and 2 years (r=0.40), indicating longitudinal tracking of functional status. Age showed a moderate-to-strong negative correlation with 2-year physical function (r=−0.54), suggesting poorer long-term functional outcomes in older patients. Preoperative mental health was moderately correlated with 2-year vitality (r=0.44). Procedural variables, including number of treated levels and vertebral region, demonstrated weak or inconsistent correlations with postoperative outcomes.

Postoperative outcomes

Significant improvements were observed across most PROM domains (Table 3, Figure 2). ODI improved from 77.3±16.9 preoperatively to 30.5±20.6 at 6 months and 25.3±19.9 at 2 years (both P<0.001). SF-36 Physical Function improved from 9.2±17.2 to 39.2±31.7 at 6 months and 41.8±31.7 at 2 years (P<0.001). VAS back pain decreased from 8.0±2.3 to 1.9±2.7 at 6 months and 1.7±2.7 at 2 years (P<0.001). Improvements were observed across most SF-36 domains, except for general health, which remained unchanged.

Predictors of outcomes

Multivariable regression results are shown in Tables 4,5. At 6 months, none of the prespecified predictors, including age, sex, number of treated levels, and vertebral region, were significantly associated with postoperative outcomes after adjustment (Table 4). At 2 years, increasing age was independently associated with poorer SF-36 Physical Function (β=−17.45 per 10-year increase, 95% CI: −24.43 to −10.47; P<0.001) (Table 5). No other demographic or procedural variables were significantly associated with long-term outcomes. Differences between CIs and adjusted P values reflect the application of false discovery rate correction. Exploratory analysis demonstrated a positive association between preoperative SF-36 Mental Health and 2-year vitality (β=+0.43, 95% CI: 0.18 to 0.67).

Discussion

Main findings

In this cohort, vertebroplasty was associated with substantial and sustained improvements in pain and function, with ODI improving from 77.3 to 25.3 and SF-36 Physical Function from 9.2 to 41.8 at 2 years (both P<0.001). These findings are consistent with randomised trials demonstrating meaningful analgesic benefit following vertebral augmentation (3,4), while also aligning with observational studies demonstrating functional improvement and identifying clinical factors associated with treatment success (9,10). In contrast to prior work, which has largely focused on treatment efficacy, the present study specifically evaluates predictors of functional recovery.

Increasing age was the strongest observed independent predictor of poorer long-term physical function in this cohort (β=−17.45 per 10-year increase; P<0.001), while procedural variables—including treated level and vertebral region—were not associated with outcomes after adjustment. This is consistent with previous vertebroplasty cohorts (9,10) and broader spine literature, where baseline patient factors often outweigh procedural variables in determining postoperative PROMs (11).

Baseline SF-36 Physical Function demonstrated moderate correlations with postoperative outcomes (r≈0.40–0.44), reflecting longitudinal tracking of functional status. However, this did not translate into independent predictive value after adjustment, suggesting that baseline function tracked longitudinally with postoperative outcomes but did not remain independently associated after adjustment. Clinically, this suggests that baseline function may inform expected postoperative levels but should not be used in isolation for patient selection.

A notable finding was the difference between pain improvement and long-term functional recovery. While VAS back pain improved markedly from 8.0 to 1.7, age was not associated with pain outcomes, suggesting that substantial pain improvement was observed across the cohort regardless of age. In contrast, increasing age was associated with poorer long-term physical function. This association may reflect broader factors not captured in the present dataset, including frailty, sarcopenia, comorbidity burden, osteoporosis severity, rehabilitation capacity and sagittal alignment (12). Patient-related factors, including symptom duration and preoperative functional status, have similarly been associated with postoperative outcomes following lumbar spinal stenosis surgery (13). These findings suggest that pain relief and functional recovery may represent distinct postoperative domains and should be considered separately during patient counselling and postoperative optimisation.

The observed gap between analgesic benefit and functional recovery also highlights a potential role for perioperative optimisation. Although evidence for structured rehabilitation following vertebroplasty is limited, persistent functional deficits in older patients suggest that analgesia alone may be insufficient. Incorporating frailty assessment (e.g., Clinical Frailty Scale or FRAIL questionnaire) into preoperative evaluation may help identify patients who could benefit from targeted rehabilitation strategies (14). Potential approaches include formal frailty screening, optimisation of osteoporosis treatment, nutritional support, resistance-based rehabilitation programmes, and early mobilisation pathways.

Exploratory analysis demonstrated an association between preoperative mental health and 2-year vitality, consistent with evidence suggesting that baseline depressive symptoms may adversely influence subsequent low-back-pain outcomes (15). As this association was not prespecified and was not observed across other domains, it should be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, vertebroplasty appears to provide reliable pain relief across age groups, while long-term functional recovery may be influenced more strongly by patient-related factors within this cohort. These findings highlight the need for targeted perioperative optimisation strategies to improve functional outcomes in older patients.

From a clinical perspective, these findings may assist patient counselling and expectation management. While substantial pain relief appears achievable across age groups, long-term functional recovery may be more variable among older patients. Incorporating age-related factors, frailty assessment, and rehabilitation planning into perioperative discussions may therefore improve postoperative expectation setting and resource allocation.

Recent literature continues to highlight the evolving and contested role of vertebral augmentation in osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. A 2024 three-year comparative cohort study reported superior early pain, disability, and satisfaction outcomes after vertebroplasty compared with conservative treatment, although between-group differences were attenuated by 24 to 36 months (16). Recent guideline and evidence-synthesis literature similarly emphasises that treatment decisions should integrate symptom severity, fracture characteristics, patient factors, non-operative options, and the relative risks and benefits of vertebroplasty, kyphoplasty, and newer augmentation techniques (17,18). In this context, the present study adds long-term PROM data suggesting that age-related patient factors may meaningfully influence functional recovery after vertebroplasty.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the absence of a non-operative comparator group limits causal inference regarding the observed improvements following vertebroplasty. Secondly, important radiographic variables including fracture morphology, vertebral height loss, local kyphosis, sagittal alignment, and cement distribution were not analysed and may represent important confounders influencing postoperative recovery (19). In addition, osteoporosis-related variables including bone mineral density, antiresorptive or anabolic therapy, treatment adherence, and interval fracture occurrence were not consistently available and may also have influenced long-term outcomes. Thirdly, frailty, sarcopenia, comorbidity burden, and rehabilitation intensity were not directly measured, and age may therefore represent a surrogate marker for these unmeasured factors. Approximately one-third of eligible patients were excluded because of incomplete PROM follow-up, which may introduce attrition bias. Nevertheless, PROM collection was performed through a prospectively maintained registry using standardised follow-up protocols.

Fourthly, the sample size was modest for multivariable modelling, particularly for smaller subgroups, and smaller associations may therefore have been missed. The thoracolumbar subgroup was especially limited in size and should be interpreted cautiously. Fifthly, the neurogenic symptom score demonstrated a zero-inflated baseline distribution and was therefore excluded from multivariable regression analyses. Finally, psychosocial factors beyond SF-36 Mental Health, such as catastrophising and pain self-efficacy, were not assessed despite known associations with postoperative outcomes (15).

Future prospective studies incorporating frailty assessment, osteoporosis-management variables, radiographic parameters, and structured rehabilitation protocols are warranted to better define modifiable factors influencing functional recovery after vertebroplasty.

Conclusions

Vertebroplasty was associated with substantial and sustained improvements in pain and function. Increasing age was the strongest observed independent predictor of poorer long-term physical function within this dataset, whereas procedural factors showed no significant association with postoperative PROMs. Baseline physical function demonstrated longitudinal tracking with postoperative outcomes but was not independently predictive after adjustment. These findings suggest that long-term functional recovery following vertebroplasty may be associated more strongly with patient-related than procedural factors within this cohort. However, further prospective studies incorporating radiographic, frailty, and osteoporosis-management variables are warranted.
