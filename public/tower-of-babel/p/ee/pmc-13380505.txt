Seizure freedom after vagus nerve stimulation in a patient with SCN3A-related network epilepsy and incomplete penetrance: A case report
Authors: Victoria Esmeralda Juárez-Rivera, Juan Carlos Vera-López, Diana Trinidad-Hernández, Stefan Narváez-Labuhn, David José Dávila-Ortiz de Montellano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetic-based epilepsies often present as distributed neural network disorders, challenging traditional focal versus generalized classifications. We report a 20-year-old male with drug-resistant epilepsy associated with a previously unreported SCN3A missense variant (c.5970C > G; p.Ser1990Arg). Despite failure of multiple anti-seizure medications and a non-localizing electroclinical profile, the patient achieved complete seizure freedom following Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS) therapy. Segregation analysis revealed the variant in asymptomatic relatives, including a parent with subclinical interictal discharges, confirming incomplete penetrance. This case underscores the role of VNS as a transformative therapeutic strategy for genetic network epilepsies where resective surgery is not feasible.

Introduction

Genetic-based epilepsies encompass a broad and heterogeneous spectrum of electroclinical phenotypes that challenge the traditional dichotomous classification into focal and generalized epilepsies. There is increasing evidence supporting the concept of epilepsy as a disorder of distributed neural networks, where genetic factors may predispose to generalized cortical hyperexcitability rather than a single well-defined epileptogenic focus [1], [2], [3].

According to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), drug-resistant epilepsy is defined as the failure of adequate trials of two appropriately chosen, tolerated, and properly used antiseizure medication regimens, whether as monotherapies or in combination, to achieve sustained seizure freedom. Drug-resistant epilepsy is further categorized according to the outcomes of therapeutic intervention, incorporating two dimensions of clinical outcomes and up to three outcome categories [4].

Category 1 evaluates the patient's clinical course and whether seizure freedom has been achieved. Category 2 assesses whether the patient remains seizure-free or continues to experience seizures. Category 3 is considered an indeterminate outcome. Within these three categories, subdivisions A, B, and C are assigned according to treatment-related adverse effects and their impact on the overall therapeutic outcome [4].

The utility of these categories and their subdivisions lies in their ability to distinguish between patients who achieve seizure freedom without adverse effects and those who achieve seizure freedom while experiencing treatment-related adverse effects, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of treatment effectiveness and tolerability [4].

According to the ILAE consensus Scheme for Categorizing Outcome of an Intervention for Epilepsy, the patient was classified as Category 1 (seizure-free) [4].

Case presentation

A 20-year-old right-handed male was referred to a tertiary care center for evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy. He had no relevant perinatal history and achieved appropriate neurodevelopmental milestones.

According to the current International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classifications of epileptic seizures (2017 and 2025), seizure types were categorized based on their onset and classified into 6 different seizure types [2], [5].

Semiology 1 (Age of onset: 14 years): Focal impaired awareness seizure, characterized by sudden behavioral arrest and abrupt loss of awareness. The episodes were brief and occurred without clearly identified motor manifestations. No preceding aura or focal subjective symptoms were reported.

Semiology 2 (Age of onset: 15 years): Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure, characterized by ictal vocalization at onset, followed by a bilateral tonic phase with generalized stiffening and subsequent bilateral clonic movements lasting approximately 30 s. The seizure was associated with impaired awareness throughout the event and was followed by a postictal confusional state.

Semiology 3 (Age of onset: 16 years): Focal aware seizure with cognitive manifestations, consisting of inappropriate vocalizations and transient language impairment preceding larger seizures. These episodes were suggestive of focal onset involving language networks, with preserved awareness during the initial phase.

Semiology 4 (Age of onset: 16–18 years): Focal impaired awareness seizure with cognitive and behavioral manifestations, characterized by aphasia, perseverative word repetition, behavioral arrest, and impaired awareness. Seizures frequently included right cephalic version (versive seizure) and often progressed to focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures.

Semiology 5 (Age of onset: 16–18 years onward): Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure, evolving from a focal seizure with language dysfunction, behavioral arrest, and right head version, followed by bilateral tonic and clonic phases. Postictal manifestations included prolonged confusion, emotional flattening, and recurrent vomiting, with recovery periods lasting from several hours to several days.

Semiology 6 (By 2024, age 20 years): Recurrent focal impaired awareness seizures with cognitive manifestations and versive features, frequently evolving to focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, occurring at a frequency of one to two episodes per month despite treatment with levetiracetam and lacosamide. Psychological stress was identified as a common precipitating factor.

Diagnostic evaluation

Repeated scalp EEG recordings demonstrated abnormal findings, including high-voltage sharp waves and frequent spike-wave complexes, predominantly in bilateral frontal regions. These discharges were often bisynchronous and sleep-dependent, exhibiting secondary bilateral synchrony. A brain MRI (HARNESS protocol) was unremarkable.

FDG-PET revealed a diffuse hypometabolic pattern affecting bilateral striata, orbitofrontal cortices, and the right motor/premotor cortices. A subsequent detailed review identified relative hypometabolism in the left temporal pole and hippocampus, with preservation of the amygdala. Due to the lack of a clearly localized, resectable epileptogenic zone, the patient was not considered a candidate for traditional epilepsy surgery.

Before vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) implantation, the patient was receiving antiseizure therapy with levetiracetam, valproate (500 mg twice daily), and lacosamide (100 mg twice daily). Levetiracetam and lacosamide were added at the age of 19 years. The patient was also receiving folic acid supplementation, but continued to experience epileptic seizures.

The SCN3A c.5970C > G variant met the PM2 (Pathogenic Moderate) criterion according to ACMG/AMP recommendations due to its extremely low frequency in population databases. The recommended frequency threshold for this gene is 0.05%. The variant was not reported in any of the databases consulted, including gnomAD and population-specific databases from Mexico and India. The highest observed frequency in non-founder subpopulations of gnomAD was 0.0%, while the frequency in founder subpopulations was also 0.0%, supporting its rarity in the general population.

In addition, the variant met the PP2 (Pathogenic Supporting) criterion, as it is a missense variant in SCN3A, a gene characterized by a low rate of benign missense variation and in which missense variants represent a common mechanism of disease. Approximately three times more pathogenic than benign missense variants have been reported among the curated variants of this gene, along with a substantial number of missense variants associated with pathogenicity [6].

Regarding population frequency, the variant was not reported in any of the databases consulted, including gnomAD (Aggregated), gnomAD (Max), gnomAD Exome, gnomAD Genome, gnomAD Exome non-UKB, Mexican DB, and India DB, where it was consistently recorded as N/A.

We also recognize that functional studies would provide important evidence regarding the biological relevance of this variant. Unfortunately, the functional characterization of SCN3A variants requires specialized electrophysiological assays that are not currently available at our institution. Therefore, we acknowledge the lack of functional validation as a limitation of our study and have emphasized the need for future investigations to clarify the role of this variant [6].

Treatment and outcome

Following a multidisciplinary evaluation, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) was recommended. After device implantation and activation, the patient achieved complete seizure remission. He has remained seizure-free for over one year of follow-up with no reported adverse effects, representing a significant shift in his disease trajectory.

Given the persistence of seizures despite multiple antiseizure medications, the case was reviewed by a multidisciplinary epilepsy team, which concluded that vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) represented an appropriate therapeutic option. The VNS device was implanted on November 7, 2024, when the patient was 20 years and 10 months old. The reported stimulation parameters were as follows:•Normal Mode: Output current 1.75 mA; frequency 30 Hz; pulse width 500 μs; ON time 30 s; OFF time 5 min; duty cycle 10%.•AutoStim Mode: Output current 1.875 mA; pulse width 500 μs; ON time 60 s.•Magnet Mode: Output current 2.0 mA; pulse width 500 μs; ON time 60 s.

Following implantation and activation of the device, the patient achieved complete seizure remission beginning in 2024. The post-implantation pharmacological regimen consisted of levetiracetam 1.5 g/day, magnesium valproate 500 mg twice daily, lacosamide 100 mg twice daily, and folic acid supplementation.

The patient has remained seizure-free for more than one year of follow-up, with no reported adverse effects, representing a significant improvement in the course of his disease.

Post-implantation EEG demonstrated a reduction in bilateral frontocentral epileptiform activity, as well as intermittent stimulation artifacts corresponding to the monomorphic ON cycles of the VNS device.

Discussion

The SCN3A gene encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.3, which is highly expressed during early cortical development and plays a vital role in neuronal excitability and network formation [7], [8]. While approximately 38 cases have been reported in the literature, the full prevalence and phenotypic spectrum of SCN3A-related disorders remain poorly defined.

This case highlights three clinically relevant aspects:1.Network-Based Phenotype: SCN3A-related epilepsies may present as network disorders with bilateral or synchronous epileptiform activity. Unlike classic juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, these cases show heterogeneous electroclinical features that challenge the focal/generalized dichotomy [3].2.Genetic Interpretation: The variant c.5970C > G has not been previously described in the literature, particularly in the Mexican population. The presence of interictal discharges in an asymptomatic carrier (Fig. 1A) underscores the importance of integrating family EEG data to interpret VUS with incomplete penetrance.3.VNS as a Therapeutic Strategy: While VNS is well-established for syndromes such as Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut, its specific efficacy in SCN3A channelopathies is less well documented [9]. In our patient, VNS provided complete seizure freedom where multiple anti-seizure medications failed. This suggests that neuromodulation should be considered earlier in the treatment algorithm for genetic network epilepsies without a resectable focus.

Neurophysiological and metabolic studies indicate that the epileptogenic network is distributed in nature, which may explain the clinical response observed in our patient following VNS implantation. FDG-PET revealed multifocal metabolic abnormalities characterized by bilateral striatal hypometabolism, bilateral orbitofrontal hypometabolism, right motor-premotor hypometabolism, and left mesial temporal hypometabolism. These findings suggest dysfunction of frontostriatal, limbic, and thalamocortical networks rather than the presence of a single epileptogenic focus, in contrast to patients with a unique and potentially resectable epileptogenic zone.

It is currently recognized that the antiepileptic effects of VNS are mediated through modulation of the so-called vagal afferent network. Following stimulation of the afferent fibers of the vagus nerve, signals are transmitted to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), which projects to brainstem structures including the locus coeruleus, raphe nuclei, and parabrachial nucleus. These structures have extensive connections with the thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, and multiple cortical regions, thereby exerting widespread influence on neuronal excitability and the synchronization of brain networks [10], [11].

Modern neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that VNS induces functional changes in thalamic, limbic, insular, and basal ganglia circuits. In particular, PET-based metabolic connectivity studies have identified alterations in the connections among the brainstem, cingulate cortex, insula, cerebellum, and putamen in patients who respond to treatment. These findings are especially relevant in our case, as the bilateral striatal hypometabolism observed on PET involves structures that are part of the functional networks associated with a favorable response to VNS [10], [11].

Beyond the activation of specific anatomical regions, recent evidence indicates that the primary antiepileptic mechanism of VNS is based on the reduction of pathological functional connectivity and the desynchronization of distributed epileptogenic networks. Several electrophysiological studies have demonstrated that treatment responders exhibit decreased cortical synchronization and reduced functional connectivity during stimulation, a phenomenon that correlates with a significant reduction in seizure frequency [10], [11].

Although this patient does not present the severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy phenotype, most frequently associated with SCN3A pathogenic variants, increasing evidence indicates that SCN3A-related disorders comprise a wide clinical spectrum. Reported phenotypes range from early-onset encephalopathies with significant neurodevelopmental impairment to milder forms characterized by focal epilepsy and preserved cognitive function.

In this context, our patient's presentation of drug-resistant focal epilepsy without major developmental abnormalities is consistent with the less severe end of the SCN3A-associated phenotypic continuum, further supporting the expanding clinical heterogeneity of this genetic disorder.

Therapeutic outcomes in the present case were interpreted according to the framework proposed by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), which classifies the response to each therapeutic intervention into three main categories: seizure-free (Category 1), not seizure-free (Category 2), and indeterminate outcome (Category 3), with additional subdivisions that take into account the presence and impact of treatment-related adverse effects. This approach recognizes that treatment success should not be defined solely by seizure control, but also by treatment tolerability and the overall clinical benefit achieved.

The incorporation of adverse effects into outcome assessment provides a more comprehensive evaluation of therapeutic efficacy. In particular, distinguishing patients who achieve seizure freedom without treatment-related complications from those who attain seizure control at the expense of significant adverse effects is clinically relevant, as these scenarios may have markedly different implications for quality of life and long-term management. Consequently, this classification facilitates individualized therapeutic decision-making and enables a more balanced assessment of the risk-benefit profile of a given intervention.

Furthermore, the ILAE consensus emphasizes that drug responsiveness is a dynamic process rather than a fixed condition and that treatment response should be interpreted within the context of each patient's clinical course. Therefore, outcome assessment should extend beyond seizure frequency alone and incorporate treatment tolerability and patient-centered measures, thereby providing a more accurate estimation of the overall success of therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This case highlights the potential value of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy characterized by a distributed epileptogenic network and the absence of a clearly resectable focus. Despite persistent seizures under multiple appropriately selected antiseizure medications, the patient achieved sustained seizure freedom following VNS implantation, with more than one year of follow-up.

Although the contribution of the identified SCN3A variant remains uncertain, the clinical outcome supports the consideration of neuromodulation as an effective therapeutic strategy in selected patients with drug-resistant network epilepsies. This case further underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach integrating clinical, neurophysiological, neuroimaging, and genetic data when evaluating treatment options for complex epilepsy syndromes.
