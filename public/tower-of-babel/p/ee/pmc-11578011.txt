Clozapine for the treatment of aggressiveness and agitation in advanced dementia
Authors: Arwed E Michael, Nikolaus Michael, Andreas Erfurth, Milenko Kujovic
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistressing behavioural symptoms, particularly agitation and aggressiveness, remain a difficult problem in everyday clinical practice in the treatment of multimorbid patients with dementia. Clozapine may be an effective therapeutic alternative in this context.MethodsIn a retrospective study, patients who had a diagnosis of dementia and had been treated in a specialized geriatric psychiatry unit with clozapine between August 2018 and February 2022 were included, and medical records were systematically reviewed. The Clinical Global Impressions Scale was used to assess improvement, and the Pittsburgh Agitation Scale for symptom reduction. In addition, side effects and clinical features were documented in detail.ResultsA total of 31 patients (median age 82 years) were identified with a mean clozapine dose of 47.2 (SD 35.6) mg. A total of 13 patients tolerated clozapine very well, 10 showed tolerable side effects, and in 10 patients side effects were the reason for stopping clozapine. Behavioural symptoms improved significantly, as indicated by the assessment scores.ConclusionsIn summary, clozapine was effective and well tolerated in 23 patients, suggesting that low‐dose clozapine may help to alleviate the suffering of difficult‐to‐treat multimorbid patients with advanced dementia and their caregivers. However, particular attention should be paid to adverse drug reactions, especially in patients with cardiovascular and pulmonary impairment.Keywords: Alzheimer dementia, antipsychotic drugs, disruptive behaviour, vascular dementia

INTRODUCTION

The development of treatment options for dementia is one of today's most urgent tasks in clinical psychiatry, as the number of people diagnosed with dementia will increase steadily in the coming decades. 1 Since a causal therapy will not be available in the foreseeable future, symptomatic therapies remain of enormous importance. 2 Among the most serious consequences in advanced stages of dementia 3 are the behavioural and psychological symptoms (BPSD), particularly aggressiveness and agitation, including nocturnal restlessness. They contribute significantly to the burden on family or institutional caregivers. 4 , 5 Efficient treatments, on the other hand, could alleviate suffering and the burden for patients and caregivers. 6 , 7 When nonpharmacologic or other drug treatment attempts have failed, atypical antipsychotics continue to be used in such situations, although they have been associated with an increased risk of morbidity and mortality. 8 , 9 Atypical antipsychotics have been indicated as best suited for these purposes. 10

Among these, clozapine is of particular significance. It has unique pharmacological properties and is still considered the gold standard in the treatment of therapy‐resistant schizophrenia. 11 , 12 Among the potential benefits of clozapine is the low incidence of neurological side effects, even with long‐term use; potential disadvantages include important side effects such as sedation and agranulocytosis. 13 In the treatment of dementia, its anticholinergic effects are feared with the risk of inducing delirium, 14 although a potential inhibition of acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase may counteract this. 15 Due to its specific pharmacological properties, clozapine could also play a special role in the treatment of old adults with dementia, as it may be effective in controlling severe behavioural symptoms. Notwithstanding the above concerns, there is evidence in the literature of successful use for this indication, 16 , 17 both in single case reports 18 , 19 and in case series. 20 , 21 , 22 It should be noted here that in contrast to many other countries such as Japan or the USA, where the use of clozapine is limited to the treatment of treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, in Germany, as well as in the UK, the use of clozapine is also possible in Parkinson's disease, a neurodegenerative disease with the same underlying pathophysiology as Lewy body dementia. In Germany, clozapine can also be prescribed in exceptional cases as part of a so‐called ‘individual therapy trial’ for dementia.

The patients in this study well represent, in our opinion, the clinical routine in specialized treatment units dealing with the treatment of advanced dementia. Against this background, we tested the hypothesis of whether clozapine shows good efficacy in the treatment of severe behavioural symptoms in dementia, especially agitation and aggressiveness, and is sufficiently well tolerated, especially not causing delirium.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective, systematic analysis of medical records. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of the World Medical Association of Helsinki; as it was a retrospective study, it was exempt from assessment by the institutional review board/health care ethics committee. Initially, all patients treated from August 2018 to February 2022 in a special geriatric unit with a diagnosis of dementia (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10): F 0X) were recorded (N = 874). Subsequently, these patient data were compared with the information in the digitized hospital pharmacy database to select those patients (N = 31) who were started on clozapine during this period. Depending on the clinical situation, various drugs are used for the therapy of severe behavioural symptoms in our specialized geriatric department, among them frequently (in descending order of priority) antipsychotics such as risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, flupentixol, melperone, and rarely also haloperidol. If at least two antipsychotic therapy attempts have failed and the severity of the symptoms requires it, clozapine is used for symptomatic therapy as one of the rare alternatives in particularly severe cases. This was the case in less than 5% of the patients with dementia treated during the observation period. A specially developed data entry form was used to record information on age, sex, marital status, psychiatric and somatic diagnoses, duration of inpatient treatment, electroencephalogram findings, previous therapy attempts with other antipsychotics, treatment indication, concomitant medication, the exact dosage of clozapine, adverse events and side effects. Dementia diagnosis had previously been made according to ICD‐10 research criteria with the use of a corresponding checklist. 23

The anonymized medical and nursing documentation was evaluated in detail with regard to symptoms and the severity was retrospectively assessed by means of the Clinical Global Impressions Scale (CGI), 24 and the Pittsburgh Agitation Scale (PAS). 25 The CGI‐S measures the severity of the disease and the CGI‐I the improvement. The PAS designates four behaviour groups (Aberrant Vocalization, Motor Agitation, Aggressiveness, and Resisting Care) that are rated according to intensity (0–4). The assessments were made at first by one investigator (AEM) and then reviewed by an independent examiner (MK); in case of discrepancies, the documentation was reviewed again, and the more experienced specialist (MK) determined the assignment.

The data obtained were then processed by means of descriptive and inferential statistics using the statistic software R, 26 the results of the ratings before and after therapy with clozapine were tested for normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test for interval scaled data), and then compared using the appropriate tests (for comparisons the paired t‐test and nonparametric Wilcoxon test; for dependent samples the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, for independent samples the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test), setting the significance level with α = 0.5. Diagrams were created using the R package ‘ggplot2’. 27

RESULTS

Across the 43 months of the selected study interval, 31 patients in all were identified for assessment. The age ranged from 57 to 97, with a median of 82 (interquartile range (IQR) 79–85) years. A total of 13 patients were male, with 14 married, 14 widowed, 1 divorced, and 2 unmarried. The total duration of treatment was between 15 and 231 (median 60, IQR 45.5–79.5) days. All patients suffered from advanced dementia according to the ICD‐10 criteria 23 : 11 patients met the criteria for Alzheimer's disease (AD), six for vascular dementia (VaD), 11 for mixed (MxD; vascular and Alzheimer), one for Parkinson dementia (PD), and two for Lewy body dementia (DLB). All patients were no longer able to undergo dementia testing. A total of 18 suffered from comorbid organic affective disorder (anxious‐depressive), three had been previously diagnosed with major depressive disorder, and three suffered from symptomatic epilepsy. Except for one patient, all were affected by concomitant somatic diseases, most had manifest cardiovascular disease (N = 12), significant vascular risk factors such as arterial hypertension, or diabetes mellitus (N = 14). At the end of therapy with clozapine, the multimorbid patients received a total of additionally five different medications on average. All patients had been insufficiently pre‐treated with on average two different antipsychotics. Clozapine was started at 12.5 mg, except for two cases (with 25 mg) and slowly increased in a first step of further 12.5 mg, then in steps of 25 mg every 1 to 5 days as clinically required. Most of the patients (N = 14) were sufficiently treated with 25 mg administered in the evening, in two patients the dose of 12.5 mg proved to be sufficient, and the medium dose was 47.2 (SD 35.6; range 12.5–175) mg. In 28 patients, the therapy was considered effective, only in three cases it was shown to be ineffective. A total of 13 patients tolerated clozapine very well, 10 patients showed tolerable side effects, 18 patients were discharged with clozapine continued. In 10 patients—eight of them successfully treated—severe side effects, mostly sedation associated with risk of falling, were the reason for stopping clozapine, two patients refused to continue taking clozapine, and in one patient, clozapine was discontinued after she suffered a stroke (most likely due to atrial fibrillation, the treatment of which was started shortly before). In no case did we observe delirium or neutropenia. Inflammatory complications (febrile episodes, pneumonia, urinary tract infections) occurred in nine (29%) cases (for more clinical details see Table 1).

The indication for treatment with clozapine was aggressive behaviour (N = 3), nocturnal agitation (N = 16), or a combination of both (N = 12). With respect to CGI‐S (Table 1), four patients were ‘markedly ill’ (CGI‐S = 5), 17 patients were considered ‘severely ill’ (CGI‐S = 6), and 10 patients even ‘most extremely ill’ (CGI‐S = 7). After treatment (Table 1), all except three patients were classified as improved (CGI‐I = 1–3). Thus, the treatment could be evaluated as successful and well tolerated in a total of 23 patients (74%). Furthermore, behavioural symptoms showed improvement as indicated by the PAS (Fig. 1), with an initial score on average of 12.1 (mean, SD ± 2.9, 95% confidence interval (CI) 11.0–13.1), and 5.1 (mean, SD ± 3.7, 95% CI 3.8–6.5) after therapy, which is a significant difference (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test: P < 0.01).

DISCUSSION

Our data show that low‐dose clozapine can be an effective strategy for treating dementia patients with aggression and agitation.

In the past three decades, three authors have reported substantial case series of successful clozapine treatment of demented patients (Table 2); moreover three studies of single cases of treatment with clozapine in this indication were published. Additionally, Srinivasan and colleagues 28 report on four patients, three older with AD and one younger with alcohol dementia, who were successfully treated over a period of 2–6 months at doses ranging from 12.5 to 50 mg/day, although in one patient the drug was discontinued after 2 months because of sedation. BPSD regressed completely in all, and no severe side effects occurred. As for reports on the use of clozapine in single cases of dementia, one 19 reports a schizophrenic patient with Korsakoff syndrome who could be discharged from a closed ward 4 weeks after starting clozapine at a higher dosage (450 mg/day), although this case should not be considered a typical dementia patient given the principal diagnosis of schizophrenia. Another report exists of a patient with DLB who was successfully treated with clozapine for hallucinations and agitation. 29 A more typical case is found of a 75‐year‐old demented man who was successfully treated for behavioural problems with clozapine at low doses, 18 twice failing to reduce the dose.

Three substantial retrospective case series 20 , 21 , 22 are briefly summarized in Table 2. The retrospective survey by Oberholzer and colleagues concerns treatments more than three decades ago in Switzerland, where 13 of the reported 18 patients suffered from severe dementia. Patients were treated symptomatically for months and observed as inpatients in the hospital showing good tolerability and efficacy. The peculiarity of this study consists in a second part, in which in seven of these patients (all diagnosed with AD, seven female male) a discontinuation of clozapine was attempted, which was abandoned again because of a worsening of the symptoms. The systematic observation of treatments by Lee et al. 20 is comparable to our data in terms of length of therapy, effectiveness, and also the proportion of patients discharged on clozapine. However, the age of the patients was significantly lower, and three instances of deliria occurred under clozapine, which led to discontinuation of the drug in two cases. In addition, half (eight of 16 patients) of the sample had PD. For this group, clozapine continues to be recommended for the treatment of mainly psychotic symptoms. 30 The retrospective evaluation by Teodorescu et al., 22 however, differs in the important detail of treatment duration. A total of 27 moderately to severely ill patients, thus comparable to the studies described so far, were treated and were obviously in an emergency ward. This explains the very short treatment duration of on average less than 5 days (2 to 8 days), with 11 patients treated for only 2 to 4 days. If the dosages here are taken into account (note: in the authors’ table, the last patient is given a dose of ‘10 mg’, which is hardly administrable in this way; for our calculation of the mean dose, 12.5 mg was assumed although 100 mg is more likely correct), higher doses of clozapine would have been reached in a very short time, which in our experience runs counter to the principle of ‘start low, go slow’ and reduces tolerability. Nevertheless, the authors describe treatment with clozapine as relatively well tolerated and effective, and 11 patients were discharged with clozapine.

All of the above authors found that clozapine can be used as an effective and tolerable treatment for demented patients, while emphasizing that severe side effects were mostly absent and, if they occurred, such as delirium or sedation for example, they were well manageable under hospital conditions. This also goes well with our observations. As in the other case series, the very distressing symptoms of aggressiveness and restlessness, especially when they occurred at night as part of a day‐night reversal, 5 were the reason for symptomatic therapy with clozapine. Here it proved to be very effective, indicating a specific, anti‐aggressive efficacy, which has been repeatedly proven and may not be explained by sedation alone. 4 , 31 , 32 Overall, and also in our study, evidence is accumulating that clozapine is effective in treating aggression, nocturnal restlessness, and agitation, which would make it an important drug for the treatment of BPSD.

A serious argument against the use of clozapine is severe side effects such as agranulocytosis, myocarditis, constipation, or delirium. 33 Thus, in a retrospective evaluation of treatments of elderly people with psychotic symptoms, albeit at higher doses (the average daily dose of clozapine was 208 mg for an average of 22 hospital days), Salzman and colleagues 16 describe the occurrence of leukopenia (within 10 weeks in 3 cases), which led to discontinuation of clozapine. Recently, however, this danger has been assumed to be lower. 34 Nevertheless, respiratory symptoms, marked sedation with a tendency to fall, and epileptic seizures, as observed in our series, must also be carefully considered. 35 We have observed further serious side effects, especially severe sedation with motor unsteadiness and associated tendency to fall, intermittent pulmonary infections, febrile episodes that may be associated with hypersalivation and possible aspiration, and in one patient a seizure; however, neither agranulocytosis nor delirium were noted. The latter is often related to the anticholinergic efficacy of clozapine, 36 although it is unclear to what extent the level of the dose and the speed of the dose increase are decisive in this context. 33 It should be noted again that it is important to start very low with clozapine and to increase the dose by slow degrees in order to improve tolerability and to determine the lowest effective dose. With regard to possible haematological side effects or delirium, the question remains open whether these could occur less frequently at very low doses. With regard to febrile episodes or pulmonary infections, it should be mentioned that these are among the frequent complications in the course of advanced dementia and that mortality is already very high in comparable patients. 37 Pneumonia and upper respiratory tract infections are mentioned in the product information of clozapine (and risperidone) as adverse drug reactions, with pneumonia in particular, having a fourfold higher risk of lethality than agranulocytosis, being a common cause of death under clozapine therapy. 38 In fact, even mild neutropenia is associated with increased susceptibility to infections, 39 which must be taken into account when using clozapine in particular and antipsychotics in general in vulnerable individuals. Apart from the immediate objections to clozapine because of the side effects mentioned above, fundamental concerns are raised about the use of antipsychotics in the treatment of dementia patients because of the increased mortality rate 40 and severe cardiovascular events. 9 , 41 We did not observe a cardiovascular complication in this small case series, but one patient suffered a cerebral infarction with aphasia. This patient had recently started anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, and therefore, a causal connection with clozapine cannot be conclusively derived. However, severe side effects and potentially increased mortality pose an ethical dilemma. The suffering of the seriously ill as well as their caregivers 42 must be weighed against the risks described, which in our opinion must be judged against the background of palliative care. 10 , 43 , 44 In our opinion, the administration of clozapine may well be justified after thorough consideration.

Retrospective case series show obvious limitations. 45 A control group is missing, and in addition, despite the very detailed medical and nursing documentation in our department, important information may have been lost or may be missing altogether. Furthermore, there is a limitation that can be summarized under the term ‘recall bias’. 46 Finally, the number of evaluable patients is still small, although to our knowledge this is the largest retrospective case series on this topic to date. Nevertheless, retrospective studies can be useful to raise research questions or to encourage further investigations. They are also a means to critically evaluate rare therapeutic interventions in retrospect and perhaps to use them in a modified, and better‐defined form in the future.

In the case series presented here, only patients with advanced dementia were included. None of the patients was able to consent to the therapy; consent was exclusively given by legal guardians or authorized relatives. This poses another ethical dilemma and should be a reason to clarify such questions preferably at the beginning of a dementia disease. It is possible that many of those affected might view symptomatic therapy, especially of aggression, positively and could give their consent in the context of advance care planning in view of the risks involved. 47
