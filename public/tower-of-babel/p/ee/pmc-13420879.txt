A phase 1 evaluation of inhaled oxytocin: Physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic model informed dosing of a novel heat‐stable oxytocin delivery system
Authors: Pete Lambert, Carl M J Kirkpatrick, Rory Marriott, Siladitya Ray Chaudhuri, David Polidori, Bart Remmerie, Michelle P McIntosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo develop and validate a physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model enabling inhaled oxytocin dose selection for clinical evaluation. Subsequently, to conduct a phase 1 study investigating the pharmacokinetics and safety of selected doses of an optimized inhaled oxytocin product in healthy, non‐pregnant female participants.MethodA general PBPK model for inhaled oxytocin in non‐pregnant female participants was developed with the ability to estimate clinical exposure for new devices and deposition characteristics. This was used to select the oxytocin dose range for a randomized, single‐centre, partially single‐blind, placebo‐controlled, fixed‐sequence phase 1 study in comparison to standard of care injectable doses in a part cross‐over design. Participants received increasing inhaled oxytocin doses (150 μg, 300 μg, 600 μg and 900 μg) in single and/or repeat dose regimens. The primary endpoints were pharmacokinetic characteristics and local tolerability following inhaled dosing.ResultsFifteen of 20 randomized participants completed the study. The pharmacokinetics following inhaled oxytocin administration were consistent with intramuscular dosing with the 150 μg (Cmax = 77 pg/mL, AUC0‐inf = 2488 min.pg/mL) and 600 μg (Cmax = 398 pg/mL, AUC0‐inf = 14 792 min.pg/mL) inhaled oxytocin doses bracketing the 10 IU intramuscular doses (Cmax = 197 pg/mL, AUC0‐inf = 8560 min.pg/mL). Repeat dosing of inhaled oxytocin was well‐tolerated, and no significant treatment‐related adverse events or clinical findings were reported. The PBPK model accurately predicted key PK parameters within two‐fold of the observed values.ConclusionFurther development of inhaled oxytocin as an innovative product to address preventable postpartum haemorrhage in low‐resource settings is warranted.Trial registration: Australia New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN12622001077752p) registered 04/08/2022.Keywords: inhaled delivery, maternal health, maternal mortality, model‐informed development, oxytocin, physiologically‐based pharmacokinetics, postpartum haemorrhage

INTRODUCTION

Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) is the leading direct cause of global maternal mortality. 1 Estimates indicate 15–20 million women per year experience a PPH resulting in an estimated 70 000 deaths, overwhelmingly in resource‐constrained birth settings in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). 1 , 2 Oxytocin injection (10 IU IV/IM) has been shown to be effective in preventing PPH. 3 , 4 Consequently, the WHO recommends that every woman receive a prophylactic dose of oxytocin within 1–2 min of delivery at every birth. 3 However, the requirement for 1) oxytocin injection products to be stored in refrigerated conditions to maintain quality and 2) skilled birth attendants in combination with sterile needles and syringes for safe administration can limit access to good quality oxytocin in low‐resource environments. Approximately 40% of oxytocin injection products sampled in LMICs do not meet internationally recognized quality standards, with many products deteriorating in supply chains due to poor or unreliable cold‐chain infrastructure. 5 In addition, the presence of skilled birth attendants to administer injections safely at deliveries cannot be guaranteed. For example, the skilled birth attendance rate in sub‐Saharan Africa, while improving, is approximately 70%. 6

A single‐use oxytocin dry powder inhaler (inhaled oxytocin – IHO) for the prevention of PPH that aims to simplify use, remove the need for additional consumables and withstand storage conditions outside of the cold chain (including in hot and humid climates) has the potential to overcome the limitations of oxytocin injection products in LMICs. While the inhaled route of administration is frequently chosen to target the lung for respiratory diseases, it can also be used as a reliable portal for systemic delivery, which serves as an alternative to injectable and oral delivery when the latter have their own therapeutic challenges. 7 The IHO programme, led by Monash University, has achieved phase 2 development readiness, having demonstrated the pharmacokinetic (PK) similarity of a prototype IHO product (based on the GSK Rotahaler® inhalation device) to a 10 IU IM injection in healthy non‐pregnant female participants and in the target population, women in the third stage of labour. 8 , 9 In addition, a 3‐year shelf life outside of the refrigerator has been demonstrated for the same product. An independent assessment undertaken in 2022 of pharmaceutical innovations in development for the management of PPH identified IHO as the only innovation with the potential for high impact. 10

Further market readiness and development of the product requires optimization of the device: formulation combination to simplify administration, reduce costs and ensure affordability within LMICs. This has been achieved through simplification of the formulation composition, specifically the removal of two excipients and associated manufacturing steps, and adoption of an easy‐to‐use, low‐cost dry powder inhaler (DPI), the Iconovo ICOone® device. This report describes a phase 1 clinical study to assess the safety and compare the PK characteristics of this optimized IHO device: formulation combination to standard of care oxytocin injection products.

The design and dose selection for this study were informed by the development of a physiologically based PK (PBPK) model. PBPK models for oral delivery are increasingly well established and have regulatory acceptance, 11 and their use has been explored to characterize inhaled therapies targeting local delivery in the pulmonary region, 12 , 13 , 14 However, few examples exist to inform the development of inhaled products for systemic delivery. 15 The current inhaled PBPK model seeks to correlate in‐vitro product performance using standard aerosolization performance assessments (as a surrogate for regional lung distribution) with systemic exposure, based on historical data obtained with a prototype product. This model is then used to predict systemic exposure for an optimized IHO product comprising a new proprietary (ICOone®) device and the simplified formulation described. The new device geometry and altered formulation characteristics of this product will likely lead to different regional lung distribution compared to the prototype. Therefore, there is great utility, in terms of effective clinical trial design, in a model that can translate the measured in vitro aerosolization performance of this new product into prospective predictions of systemic exposure against a range of doses.

While demonstration of bioequivalence is not being targeted, the aim of this development programme is broadly two‐fold. Firstly, to demonstrate similar PK exposure characteristics following IHO dosing to that observed following a 10 IU IM dose, and secondly, to identify an IHO dose that provides systemic exposure (AUC & Cmax) at least as high as 10 IU IM dosing (efficacy target) with a Cmax not more than that observed following 5 IU IV dosing over five minutes (systemic safety margin).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Inhaled dose posology

Inhaled dose posology was informed by a validated PBPK model using multiple input types, as described in Figure 1: physiological and morphometric (default values for a non‐pregnant female), dosing (regional lung distribution of the suspended particulate dose that was measured in vitro), physicochemical (compound‐specific properties like lipophilicity and pKa, and properties like plasma protein binding that depend both on compound and species), and systemic PK parameters. The model was validated by fitting select compound‐specific parameters against observed clinical data in non‐pregnant females from independent clinical studies using a different prototype device, as detailed below. Development and validation of a physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model

GastroPlus® software, version 9.7 (Simulations Plus Inc., Lancaster, CA, USA) was used to construct the combined inhaled PBPK‐Systemic model that consists of distinct components, notably the systemic disposition and the inhaled Pulmonary Compartmental Absorption and Transit (PCAT®) model as per the schematic diagram (Figure 3). Systemic disposition

An initial systemic disposition of oxytocin was described by a three‐compartment PK model fit to data from multiple healthy, non‐pregnant cohorts administered IV oxytocin (Supplementary Information S1) and validated by comparing predictions from this model to observed results (Supplementary Information S2) from an independent published study. 9 The model parameters are described in Supplementary Information S3.

Pulmonary and Oral absorption

Pulmonary absorption and bioavailability were handled by a physiologically based mechanistic multi‐compartment PCAT® model within GastroPlus®. 11 To reflect the rapid peptidase‐mediated degradation in the intestinal tract, the model assumes no oral bioavailability for the swallowed fraction of oxytocin.

Model‐generated default values of lung anatomical and physiological parameters for a standard 28‐year female lung were used, which corresponds to the mean maternal age in low‐ and middle‐income countries. 16 Deposition fractions for mouth/throat and each lung (PCAT) compartment were obtained by translating in vitro deposition patterns measured using the Next Generation Impactor (NGI) (Copley Instruments, UK) operated at a flow rate of 60 L/min flow rate (Supplementary Information S4) of four oxytocin doses (50 μg, 200 μg, 400 μg and 600 μg) previously assessed in an independent phase 1 clinical study 8 when delivered from a different (Rotahaler® dry powder inhalation) commercially‐available device. NGI mimics the inhalation process of sequentially filtering airborne particles of different sizes as they pass through different regions of the lung by collecting particles on a series of stages, each with progressively smaller nozzles. Individual or a collection of stages represent different lung (PCAT) compartments, which enables translating the in vitro data to represent in vivo conditions (as described in Supplementary Information S4). The observed data from the previous Rotahaler study 8 was used to develop the current PBPK model by fitting select physicochemical properties, notably, epithelial permeability in each lung compartment and deep lung degradation (metabolism in the AI compartment). This parameter optimization was done within GastroPlus® using Weighted Sum of Squared Errors, which resulted in the final PBPK model parameters as described in Supplementary Information S3. All other PCAT physicochemical properties were model‐generated within GastroPlus® from the model structure.

The performance of the validated PBPK model was assessed by comparing simulated plasma concentration‐time profiles with the observed data from IHO (50–600 μg) from the Rotahaler study in healthy non‐pregnant females 8 and is shown in Figure 4. Within these simulations, across each dose, the only parameters varied were the administered dose and the measured regional lung deposition.

Dose selection for phase 1 study

Towards the stated objectives of targeting clinically relevant systemic exposures relative to the injection products and based on simulations using the validated mechanistic disposition (PBPK) model described above, this study sought to identify and then investigate three IHO doses delivered using the optimized ICOone® product. Firstly, a low and high IHO dose predicted to deliver systemic exposure that brackets the PK profile observed following a 10 IU IM oxytocin injection. Secondly, the predicted Cmax of the highest IHO dose should be less than the observed Cmax following a 5 IU IV oxytocin injection administered over five minutes. Avoiding high peak concentrations avoids the risk of systemic side effects sometimes associated with IV dosing. The third mid‐range dose should be predicted to deliver comparable exposure in terms of Cmax and AUC to a 10 IU IM injection. This dose will then be assessed in a multiple dosing regimen (based on three consecutive doses with and without an interval between doses) to 1) establish an upper safety threshold dose for IHO (i.e. a dose of 3 x the mid‐range dose without an interval between doses) that is well‐tolerated and does not exceed the maximum Cmax observed following IV dosing, and 2) for the first time, assess the safety and characterize the PK following repeat dosing of IHO at 10 min intervals, simulating a potential PPH treatment scenario.

Predictive PBPK modelling based on the in vitro aerosolization performance for the ICOone product and simulation of three clinical trials, each of 100 subjects, predicted that an IHO 300 μg dose would be comparable to the observed 10 IU IM data in terms of Cmax, AUC0‐3h and plasma oxytocin concentrations at 10 min (Cp10) and 30 min (Cp30) post‐dosing. Consequently, IHO 300 μg was selected as the mid‐range dose, while IHO 150 μg and 600 μg were selected as the lower and upper bracketing doses, respectively (Supplementary Information S5).

Clinical study design

The selected doses from the PBPK modelling above were then evaluated in a two‐part phase 1, randomized, single‐centre, partially single‐blind, placebo‐controlled, single (Part 1) and repeat dosing (Part 2), fixed‐sequence study with IHO (ACTRN12622001077752p) conducted at Nucleus Network (Melbourne, Australia).

Participants

Participants eligible for inclusion in the study were healthy, premenopausal, non‐pregnant, non‐lactating women aged 18–45 years inclusive, with a body mass index of 18–30 kg/m2, who were taking a combined oestrogen‐containing oral contraceptive pill (as one of two contraceptive methods) both prior to and for the duration of the study. Key exclusion criteria included a history of chronic lung conditions, respiratory tract infection within four weeks of screening, and a history of smoking within one month of screening. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is detailed in the Australia New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (www.anzctr.com.au, study number ACTRN12622001077752p). Written informed consent was obtained prior to the performance of any study‐specific procedures.

Investigational products

The inhaled oxytocin formulations comprised a spray‐dried formulation of oxytocin (1.875%‐7.5% w/w) with trehalose, leucine and calcium chloride as excipients, contained within a single‐dose Iconovo ICOone® inhaler device. The placebo inhaled product contained an identical formulation and powder mass to the active product, with the exception that the active substance was replaced by a commensurate amount of the bulk excipient, trehalose. The oxytocin injection products were generic products approved by the Therapeutic Goods Administration, the national medicines regulatory authority of Australia.

Study design

Participants were enrolled on the study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Replacement of participants was permitted in Dosing Sessions 1 and 2. Participants withdrawing after Dosing Session 2 were permitted to be replaced for Dosing Session 3 only.

In Part 1, Dosing Session 1 of the study, 16 participants were randomized equally into four treatment groups to receive 10 IU (17 μg) oxytocin IM, 5 IU (8.5 μg) oxytocin IV over 5 min, IHO 150 μg and placebo to match (PTM) IHO in a random order on separate days with a minimum 24‐hour washout period. The IM injection was administered into the thigh, while the IV dose was administered as a slow push over 5 min to avoid known cardiovascular adverse events associated with bolus administration. For all inhaled doses, study investigators were trained to use the inhaler and trained participants prior to use. In Part 1, Dosing Session 2, those same participants were randomized to receive IHO 600 μg or PTM IHO in a ratio of 14:2. In Part 2 of the study (Dosing Session 3), participants were randomized to receive IHO 900 μg or PTM (as 3 x IHO 300 μg or 3 x PTM IHO 300 μg administered without an interval between administrations) or 3 x IHO 300 μg (or 3 x PTM IHO 300 μg) at 10‐minute intervals (repeat dosing schedule). Due to the increased dose level, a precautionary sentinel dosing approach was used in Part 2, whereby two participants (one placebo, one active) from each cohort were dosed at least 24 h ahead of the remaining members of each cohort. The timings of all procedures after PTM IHO and IHO were identical. An overview of the study design is shown in Supplementary Information S6.

Dosing with PTM IH and IHO was single blind (blinded to participants only) for all participants throughout the study. Although the respiratory risk was deemed low, the investigator remained unblinded to allow differentiation of adverse events related to the PTM IHO from IHO and to monitor the tolerability of the study medication and the safety of the participants.

Assessments and analyses

Safety assessments

Adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs) were monitored throughout the study. Spirometry, clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, electrocardiograms (ECGs) (PR, QRS, QT, corrected QT [QTc] intervals), physical examinations and cardiac telemetry were assessed at specified times after dosing.

A 12‐week interval was scheduled between Parts 1 and 2 of the study to accommodate safe blood volume collection. During this period, a Safety Monitoring Committee conducted a scheduled interim analysis of PK and safety data prior to commencing Part 2 of the study involving higher dosing regimens.

PK assays and non‐compartmental analysis

Venous blood samples were taken, collected into tri‐potassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes and placed immediately on ice prior to centrifugation. Supernatant plasma was then prepared and analysed for oxytocin by Syneos Health (Québec, Canada) using a validated analytical method previously reported. 8

PK samples were collected immediately pre‐dose and at 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 240 and 360 min intervals post‐dose to characterize fully the PK profiles based on this reported period of effect and previous studies. 8 , 9 This sampling strategy is consistent with IM oxytocin dosing, which is reported to have a rapid onset (3–5 min) and limited duration (2 − 3 h) of uterine activity (Pitocin prescribing information). Key PK parameters for comparison between the PBPK model predictions and the clinical study were determined through non‐compartmental analysis and included the maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax), time to Cmax (Tmax), observed plasma concentration at 10 and 30 min post‐dose (Cp10 and Cp30), elimination rate (kel) and area under the concentration‐time curve from time zero (pre‐dose) to infinity (AUC0–inf). A full list of standard NCA summary PK parameters is included in Supplementary Information S7.

Outcomes

The objectives and endpoints of the study were to evaluate the safety and tolerability of IHO administered in single and repeat dosing schedules by monitoring AEs and changes over time in haematology, clinical chemistry, urinalysis, vital signs, spirometry, cardiac telemetry and 12‐lead ECG parameters from pre‐dose values. In addition, to 1) compare the PK characteristics of specified single IHO doses, selected based on the PBPK modelling described, with those of 10 IU IM and 5 IU IV doses, and 2) characterize the PK of repeat doses of IHO, by assessing plasma concentration profiles and specified PK parameters.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was based on feasibility. The study populations assessed included all participants/safety population, which included all participants who received at least one dose of study medication; and the PK population, which included all participants who received at least one dose of study medication and sufficient PK samples to enable non‐compartmental analyses (NCA).

To evaluate the ability of the PBPK model to predict the dose and impact of different oxytocin administration routes on the PK of oxytocin, an NCA was performed, followed by descriptive statistical analysis for oxytocin comparing IHO 150 μg and 600 μg treatments (test) to IM treatment (reference 1) and comparing IHO treatments (test) to IV treatment. PK parameters of interest were Cmax, AUCinf (for IHO 150 μg and 600 μg) and Cp10, and Cp30 (all IHO treatments).

Plasma concentrations were detectable in all participants through to 2 h following IV dosing, 3 h following IM dosing, and 1 h, 2.5 h and 2.5 h following IHO 150 μg, IHO 600 μg and IHO 900 μg dosing, respectively. Rapid absorption was observed for both IM and IH routes of administration, with mean Tmax ~ 12.0 min (IM), ~12.5 min (IHO 150 μg) and ~8.5 min (IHO 600 μg). The observed PK concentration‐time profile following IHO administration was consistent with the IM PK profile (Figure 5A). As expected, the Tmax for the IHO 900 μg dose (22.7 min) was slower than other presentations, reflecting that administration required three separate 300 μg doses in succession without an interval. Thereafter, plasma oxytocin concentrations declined rapidly and similarly, irrespective of administration route or dose. Proportionately, mean Cmax and AUC0‐inf was highest following the IHO 600 μg dose (398 pg/mL, 14 800 min.pg/mL) compared to the IHO 150 μg dose (77 pg/mL, 2488 min.pg/mL) and IHO 900 μg doses (426 pg/mL, 18 439 min.pg/mL) (Table 3). These trends were similarly reflected for Cp10 and Cp30 values. As predicted in the PBPK modelling in the study design, the IHO 150 μg and IHO 600 μg PK profiles bracketed the IM profile through to the 2‐hour sampling point (Figure 5A). In Part 2 of the study (Figure 5B), the PK profiles following administration of 3 x IHO 300 μg doses at 10‐minute intervals showed a lower AUC0‐inf compared to the IHO 900 μg (3 x 300 μg doses as a single bolus) (AUC0‐inf 14 508 min·pg/mL vs 18 439 min·pg/mL, respectively) while elimination rate constants were similar, kel 0.02 min‐1 vs 0.03 min‐1, respectively). Greater inter‐participant variability was observed with the IHO 3 x 900 μg dosing. The Cmax for the IHO 900 μg dose was higher, reflecting administration as a single dose rather than at 10‐minute intervals (IHO 900 μg = 426 pg/mL, IHO 3 x IHO 300 μg doses at 10‐minute intervals = 327 pg/mL).

Relative bioavailability of the inhaled doses, calculated based on the participants who received all doses in Part 1 of the study, compared to the 10 IU IM injection, is 3.1% (IHO 150 μg) and 4.6% (IHO 600 μg). Absolute bioavailability of the IHO 150 μg and 600 μg doses (using AUC0‐inf of 5 IU IV administered over 5 min as the reference) is calculated as 1.7% and 2.6%, respectively (Supplementary Information S11). The reduced bioavailability of the inhaled formulations is driven by pre‐systemic losses, including deposition in the mouth and throat with subsequent swallowing and local degradation within the alveolar interstitial region prior to systemic absorption, whereas post‐absorption systemic clearance is governed by parameters derived from intravenous data.

Finally, this study provides data to assess further the accuracy of the PBPK model developed for IHO dose selection. DISCUSSION

This study provides further evidence that the pulmonary administration of a heat‐stable powder formulation of oxytocin using an optimized product design can deliver the drug effectively and safely in a manner comparable to an IM injection, the current standard of care based on WHO guidelines. Moreover, the optimized product based on a simplified formulation in combination with the ICOone® inhalation device performs more efficiently in terms of systemic absorption than the prototype product previously reported. The systemic exposure following IHO 150 μg and IHO 600 μg administrations bracket the efficacy reference provided by 10 IU IM and provide a dose range for further evaluation. The higher bioavailability of the IHO 600 μg product compared to the IHO 150 μg product likely reflects that the dose strength of the IHO products used in the study is achieved through variation of formulation concentration, with powder mass remaining constant across products. A strength of the study is that for the first time, we have established an upper dose threshold for the IHO product (900 μg) and characterized the pharmacokinetics and safety of repeat IH dosing of oxytocin, simulating potential usage in a PPH treatment scenario. Both of these potential treatment dosage regimens (IHO 900 μg bolus & 3 x IHO 300 μg at 10‐minute intervals) were well‐tolerated locally with no systemic adverse events of significance.

Resource limitations prevented assessment of a mid‐range dose likely comparable to the 10 IU IM dose; these data will allow further refinement of the PBPK model and final definition of the target dose in future trials. A single set of model parameters was estimated to fit the Rotahaler data across all four doses. The resulting model modestly underpredicted the observed data with a 200 μg dose and modestly overpredicted the observed data with 400 μg and 600 μg doses, suggesting some minor nonlinearity associated with absorption that was not included in the model. While the preliminary utility of the PBPK model for dose selection based on in‐vitro performance parameters has been demonstrated for non‐pregnant participants, future work will seek to improve the predictive capability of the model and further apply this approach to the target population for the product, that is, women in the third stage of labour. Studies conducted by our group indicate lung function is largely unchanged postpartum (data on file). The physiological changes of pregnancy, leading inter alia to increased volume of distribution and substantially increased systemic clearance of oxytocin, will need to be accommodated in future models. 9 For the purposes of this study, lung anatomy and physiology parameters for a standard 28‐year‐old female lung were used. Future studies will assess whether any meaningful PK differences are observed in women from lower‐income regions.

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1 targets a reduction in the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR), defined as number of deaths per 100 000 live births, to 70 by 2030. 17 While the international community has made substantial improvements in maternal health since the start of the century, current estimates indicate the global MMR to be 197, with progress stalled over recent years. These global figures obscure the scale of the challenge in LMICs, where the overwhelming burden of mortality exists. In sub‐Saharan Africa, the MMR is estimated at 454, meaning that a 15‐year old girl in this region has an approximately 386 times higher lifetime risk of dying during pregnancy or childbirth (1 in 55) than a similar girl in high‐income nations with accessible healthcare. 18 Addressing the leading cause of preventable maternal mortality, postpartum haemorrhage, will be critical to achieving these SDG ambitions.

Currently, the effective use of oxytocin injections, the first‐line therapy for PPH, in resource‐constrained settings is limited due to a lack of reliable cold‐chain infrastructure to maintain quality and the requirement for sterile needles/syringes and skilled birth attendants. A new heat‐stable injectable formulation of the oxytocin synthetic analogue, carbetocin, has been developed and is being registered and implemented globally. 19 However, this product is currently approved only for use in PPH prevention and not PPH treatment, and the requirement for sterile needles/syringes and appropriately trained and authorized healthcare workers for safe administration remains. Consequently, to serve mothers in the least resourced facilities or out‐of‐facility deliveries, there continues to be a need for a product that can be stored under ambient conditions and can be safely administered with only limited training and supplies. Inhaled oxytocin is designed specifically to meet this need, and this study demonstrates that delivery of oxytocin as a simple, heat‐stable powder formulation via the ICOone® inhalation device in the dose range assessed is quantitatively and qualitatively similar in terms of key PK characteristics to a 10 IU administration by IM injection. The further development of this low‐cost, simple‐to‐administer oxytocin product for the management of PPH in resource‐poor settings is warranted.
