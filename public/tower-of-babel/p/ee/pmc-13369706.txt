1H, 15N, 13C backbone resonance assignment of Escherichia coli AlkB in complex with Zn(II) and α-ketoglutarate
Authors: Sayan Das, Jeffrey A Purslow, Kyle Malcom, Joshua Knott, Vincenzo Venditti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein and nucleic acid alkylation are important genetic and epigenetic modifications. The dynamic balance between alkylation and dealkylation is regulated by distinct sets of enzymes and is essential for maintaining genomic stability. Escherichia coli AlkB is a member of the Alkylation B (AlkB) family of dioxygenases that dealkylates a wide range of nucleic acid substrates in E. coli, thereby playing a crucial role in cellular repair processes and epigenetic regulation. Here, we report the backbone 1H, 15N, 13C chemical shift assignment of E. coli AlkB in complex with Zn2+ and α-ketoglutarate. Experiments were acquired at 20 °C by heteronuclear multidimensional NMR spectroscopy. Collectively, 91% of all 13C, 15N and 1H backbone resonances of Alkb were assigned, with 182 out of a possible 200 residues assigned in the 1H–15N TROSY spectrum. Using the program TALOS + , a secondary structure prediction was generated from the assigned backbone resonances that is consistent with the previously reported X-ray structure of the enzyme. The reported assignment will permit investigations of the protein structural dynamics anticipated to provide crucial insight regarding fundamental aspects in the recognition and enzyme regulation processes.Keywords: Alkylation B, Backbone resonance assignment, AlkB dioxygenases, AlkB demethylases, Transverse relaxation optimized spectroscopy

Biological context

The non-heme Fe2+ and α-ketoglutarate (αKG) dependent dioxygenase family (AlkB) of dealkylases comprises of the prototypical E. coli AlkB and the nine human homologs Alkbh1-8 and FTO. The primary biological function of these enzymes is to hydroxylate substrates such as proteins or nucleic acids (Aik et al. 2014). AlkB dioxygenases are of significant chemical, biological, and medical interest due to their involvement in several critical cellular processes, including epigenetic gene regulation, RNA metabolism, and DNA repair (Fedeles et al. 2015; Zheng et al. 2014). The mechanism of action of all the AlkB enzymes has been well established to proceed via two reaction cycles: (Lu et al. 2014; Martinez and Hausinger 2015) a catalytic oxidative dealkylation of alkylated protein or nucleic acids residues coupled with oxidative decarboxylation of αKG to succinate and CO2 (Falnes et al. 2002; Trewick et al. 2002). The hydroxylation reaction is performed by a highly reactive oxoferryl intermediate, which forms after sequential binding of the secondary and primary substrates to the enzymes.4, (Burns et al. 2024).

Despite the low overall sequence identity, members of this family are predicted to share similar secondary structure elements that fold into a double-stranded β-helix (DSBH) domain, in which Fe2+ is coordinated to a His-Asp-X-His motif (Sundheim et al. 2008). Modulation of substrate selectivity and catalytic turnover of AlkB enzymes is governed by conformational dynamics, as demonstrated by recent NMR studies on E. coli AlkB, (Bleijlevens et al. 2012; Ergel et al. 2014) Alkbh5, (Purslow et al. 2018, 2026) and FTO (Burns et al. 2024; Khatiwada et al. 2022). Quenching of dynamics within the αKG binding site establishes a platform for methylated nucleic acid binding, (Purslow et al. 2018, 2026) while subsequent substrate binding induces αKG dynamics; 13 notably, bulky methylated substrates promote an in-line αKG conformation associated with enhanced catalytic activity.8 Accordingly, the conformational disorder of AlkB enzymes has emerged as a key determinant of their functional regulation and has motivated the development of small-molecule inhibitors targeting this dynamic landscape (Singh et al. 2026). The E. coli AlkB is well known for its substrate promiscuity (Peng et al. 2023) and can dealkylate a wide range of substrates. Elucidating how conformational dynamics govern catalytic turnover and substrate selectivity in AlkB across diverse substrates will provide a structural basis for its substrate promiscuity and inform substrate selectivity across the AlkB enzyme family.

The resonance assignment of E. coli AlkB in complex with Fe2+ and αKG has been previously reported (Shivarattan et al. 2005). However, to study the enzyme in complex with various nucleic acid substrates, we need AlkB to be bound to a diamagnetic and non-reactive metal center along with αKG. Herein, we report the 1H, 15N, 13C backbone resonance assignment of AlkB in complex with Zn2+ and αKG. Zn2+ has been shown to be a good diamagnetic analogue of Fe2+ for structural investigations of AlkB homologue enzymes.8,11,13 This backbone assignment enables future NMR studies on the conformational dynamics of AlkB in the presence of its wide range of alkylated primary substrates.

Materials and experiments

Protein expression and purification

A construct incorporating the E. coli AlkB, the N-terminal domain of E. coli Enzyme I, a N-terminal His-tag, and a Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease cutting site (His6-MBP-TEV-AlkB) (Khatiwada et al. 2020) was cloned into a pET21a vector (Novagen). The vector was then transformed into E. coli strain BL21star(DE3) (Invitrogen) and subsequently plated onto an LB-agar plate containing ampicillin (100 μg/ml) for selection. Cells were grown in D2O-based minimal media at 37 °C (200 rpm). Uniformly deuterated 13C6-glucose (2 g/L) and 15NH4Cl (1 g/L) were used as the sole carbon and nitrogen sources, respectively. Recombinant protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM isopropyl-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at an A600 ~ 0.6, and the temperature was reduced to 16 °C. After 20 h of induction at 16 °C (200 rpm), cells were harvested by centrifugation (45 min at 4,500 g), the supernatant discarded, and the cell pellet resuspended in buffer A (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 300 mM NaCl). The cell suspension was lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer (Avestin Emulsiflex C3) and centrifuged at 45,000 g for 45 min. The supernatant was then passed through a 0.45-μM filter membrane to remove any residual cell debris prior to separation/purification using two successive His-Trap columns (5 mL each; Bio-Rad™). The target protein was eluted using a 100 mL gradient of buffer B (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.375 M imidazole), and the fractions containing the target protein were confirmed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The fractions were then coalesced and incubated with 1 mg of TEV protease at 20 °C overnight. The solution was passed through two consecutive His-Trap columns to remove any uncleaved protein and TEV-protease (expressed with an N-terminal His-tag). The collected AlkB was further purified by ion-exchange chromatography (8 ml Enrich™ Q 10 × 100) with buffer A containing 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, and buffer B containing 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl. Finally, AlkB was concentrated to a total volume of ~ 3 mL using a 10 K MWCO Amicon membrane filter and further purified by gel filtration on a Superdex-75 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 20 mM MES buffer (pH 5.5), 300 mM NaCl.

NMR spectroscopy

To prepare the Zn2+ and αKG-bound form of AlkB, the apo protein was initially buffer-exchanged into 20 mM MES (pH 5.5), 100 mM NaCl, 90% H2O/10% D2O (v/v), and 0.02% NaN3. 100 µM ZnSO4 and 1 mM αKG were added to 15 mL of the resulting solution, and the complex was brought to its final concentration (~ 200 µM of AlkB). NMR spectra were acquired at 20 °C on a Bruker 700 MHz spectrometer equipped with Z-shielded gradient triple resonance cryoprobes. The applications NMRPipe (Delaglio et al. 1995) and Poky (Lee et al. 2021) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8479676/) were used to process and analyze the spectra, respectively. Sequential 1H/15N/13C backbone assignment of AlkB was performed using transverse relaxation optimized (TROSY) versions (Pervushin et al. 1997) of traditional 3D triple resonance correlation studies (HNCO, HNCA, HNCACB, HN(CO)CA, and HN(CO)CACB) (Clore and Gronenborn 1998).

Extent of assignments and data deposition

Backbone 1HN, 15N, 13Cα, 13Cβ and 13C’ chemical shifts of AlkB were assigned using triple resonance methodology (Clore and Gronenborn 1998) and deposited in the BioMagResBank (http://www.bmrb.wisc.edu/) (Ulrich et al. 2008) under the BMRB accession code 53,724. Excluding the 18 proline residues, 187 out of a possible 200 residues were visible in the 1H–15N TROSY spectrum of AlkB. 182 HN correlations were successfully assigned (Fig. 1), accounting for 91% of AlkB backbone amide groups. 5 out of the 187 1H–15N TROSY visible resonances could not be assigned due to lack of 13Cα and 13Cβ resonances. The 18 unassigned cross-peaks are located predominantly within solvent exposed regions of the AlkB active site (Fig. 2), and no additional cross-peaks appear in a 1H–15N TROSY spectrum acquired for a uniformly labeled 15N protein. These observations suggest that the invisible N–H correlations are not the result of incomplete back-exchange, but the result of line broadening due to conformational exchange on the intermediate timescale. The program TALOS + (Shen et al. 2009) was used to predict the Secondary Structure Propensity (SSP) from the assigned 1HN, 15N, 13Cα, 13Cβ, and 13C’ chemical shifts. Comparison of the SSP to the AlkB crystal structure (Yu and Hunt 2009) shows a strong correlation (Fig. 2B), suggesting the secondary structure in solution is comparable to that in the crystal form. The resonance assignment of AlkB in complex with Zn2+ and αKG establishes a foundation for NMR studies aimed at elucidating how conformational dynamics modulate AlkB catalytic activity across different alkylated DNA substrates.
