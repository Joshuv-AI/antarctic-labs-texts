Driving safety evaluation of vehicles on curved bridges under frequent seismic excitations
Authors: Tianyu Wu, Zhijun He, ZeZheng Yin, Zengwei Guo, Guowen Yao, Bo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A seismic analysis framework for the vehicle-bridge interaction (VBI) system, based on ABAQUS software, is proposed in this study. The primary focus is on the driving safety of curved bridges subjected to frequent seismic excitation. Three-dimensional dynamic models are developed for both the vehicle and the bridge. A penalty factor is introduced to control the relative displacement between the contact surfaces, simulating the interaction between the bridge deck and the vehicle wheels. Using the Dateng Gorge curved bridge as a case study, a direct solution method is employed to analyze the safety of driving on the bridge under earthquake excitation, using 10 measured ground motions. The results reveal that vehicles traveling at low speeds on the bridge during an earthquake are more likely to experience seismic effects, increasing the side-slip safety factor (SSF) and making vehicles more susceptible to side-slip. Seismic load characteristics, such as epicentral distance, significantly influence the SSF. Heavier vehicles tend to exhibit a lower SSF, indicating better stability under seismic forces. Pavement conditions also play a crucial role; snowy and wet pavements lead to higher SSF values and an increased risk of side-slip, while dry pavements provide optimal stability. This study highlights the importance of considering these factors when assessing vehicle safety and mitigating side-slip risks on seismic-prone bridges.

Introduction

Earthquake impacts on bridges manifest primarily through their vibration response. In particular, seismic loads of different frequencies in the frequency domain may excite different vibration modes of the bridge, causing abnormal vibrations or displacements when vehicles are driving on the bridge. This issue is more pronounced on curved bridges, where the uneven distribution of lateral forces caused by the curvature of the bridge, combined with the vehicle dynamics, can lead to severe accidents such as vehicle loss of control, skidding, or overturning, thereby threatening driving safety. Therefore, studying the impact of frequency-domain seismic effects on driving safety on curved bridges is crucial for ensuring bridge design, optimizing bridge seismic resistance, and safeguarding driving safety.

With the continuous development of finite element method (FEM), vehicle-bridge coupling vibration models have become more complex and refined. Finite element software enables accurate modeling and analysis of the dynamic characteristics of both vehicles and bridges. This capability facilitates the prediction of vehicle-bridge coupling vibration characteristics and responses, providing vital theoretical guidance and technical support for bridge engineering design and construction1–3. Yang and Yau4 developed a novel vehicle-bridge interaction element and analyzed vehicle dynamics using a simplified single-degree-of-freedom vehicle model on a simply supported beam bridge, demonstrating the practicality and accuracy of the method. Jonsson et al.5 constructed a vehicle-bridge coupling vibration model considering pavement roughness using numerical methods and discussed various factors affecting the vehicle-bridge coupling vibration system. Camara et al.6 examined the vibration characteristics of cable-stayed bridges in ABAQUS, defining vehicle-bridge interaction through node-surface contact and achieving convergence through an iterative process. Lu et al.7 proposed a dynamic analysis framework for vehicle-bridge interaction systems based on the finite element software ABAQUS and used ABAQUS to simulate vehicle-bridge interaction. Wu et al.8–10 developed a general vehicle-bridge coupling system modeling method on the ABAQUS platform, which was applied to the safety verification of the vehicle-bridge-foundation complex coupling system of a sea-crossing bridge in the Bohai Sea under ice loads. Homaei et al.11 proposed the Extended Modified Bridge System method to decouple seismic vehicle-bridge interactions, enabling accurate response prediction without full-system coupling. Liu et al.12 developed a stability prediction method for time-varying vehicle-bridge RTHT systems, showing that higher vehicle speeds and reduced actuator delays improve testing reliability. Aloisio and Alaggio13 compared moving load, 1-DOF, and 2-DOF vehicle models, revealing that road roughness significantly affects the necessity of full VBI modeling. Yu et al.14 demonstrated that excessive speed and poor road conditions amplify dynamic responses in long-span CFST arch bridges, with nonlinear speed effects beyond legal limits. Song et al.15 derived critical vehicle speeds for wind-induced accidents, highlighting reduced safety margins on bridges due to altered aerodynamics and low tire-road friction.

The spatial mechanical characteristics of curved beam bridges are complex, with significant bending-torsion coupling effects. The vehicle’s impact on the upper structure of the bridge, such as overturning, twisting, and creeping effects, plays an important role. These effects can easily form coupled interactions with the bridge’s seismic response, amplifying seismic responses and even leading to asymmetric seismic damage. Currently, research mainly focuses on conventional straight bridges, while little attention has been given to the more complex curved beam bridges, where the vehicle’s dynamic performance and seismic response characteristics are more intricate. As a complex multi-body dynamic system, the interaction between vehicles and bridges leads to significant differences in the seismic response of vehicle-bridge coupling systems compared to individual bridge systems. Kameda et al.16 conducted numerical simulations and found that when the vibration frequencies of the bridge and vehicle are close or identical, the vehicle’s impact can negatively affect the bridge’s seismic response. Ghosh et al.17 proposed a vulnerability assessment method for bridges considering combined vehicle loads and seismic effects, revealing that the vehicle’s impact plays a crucial role in post-seismic vulnerability. Yang and Wu18 studied the stability of a three-dimensional train vehicle model on a seismically shaken bridge, analyzing four sets of historical earthquake records, with peak ground accelerations reaching 0.08 g. The study showed that the bridge is more stable when the vehicle is stationary, compared to when it is in motion. Moreover, the study emphasized the importance of vertical ground motion components in vehicle safety analysis. Kim et al.19 focused on the seismic response of vehicle-bridge interaction systems under moderate seismic effects, including steel monorail and highway elevated bridges. Their results indicated that considering trains as merely additional mass is unrealistic and does not align with the majority of seismic design standards globally.

Currently, research on earthquake-vehicle-bridge interaction primarily focuses on railway bridges, especially addressing the transverse response of railway bridges under seismic excitation and the comfort and safety of operating trains. In contrast, research on the impact of vehicle effects on highway bridge seismic responses is relatively limited. Matsumoto et al.20 used simulation software to analyze train operation safety on bridges under seismic effects, considering the nonlinear behavior of train primary and secondary suspensions as well as the hysteretic characteristics of bridges. Xia et al.21 pointed out that neglecting seismic wave propagation effects in spatially non-uniform earthquake ground motion can lead to misjudgments in vehicle safety during earthquakes. Zeng et al.22 developed a vehicle-bridge coupling program suitable for curved railway bridges. Xie et al.23,24 established a calculation model for the spatial coupling effect between curved bridges and seamless tracks, and analyzed the influence of various factors on track irregularities. Their research found that even when bridge structures can withstand moderate seismic effects and remain elastic, trains may still exceed the derailment factor and wheel load reduction limits. This suggests that the safety of vehicles during seismic events is influenced not only by the bridge’s structural integrity but also by the interaction between the vehicle and the bridge. Recent advancements in VBI analysis leverage computational intelligence and probabilistic methods. Machine learning techniques, particularly deep learning models like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks25 show promise for rapid prediction of complex VBI responses under seismic loading, offering potential efficiency gains over traditional simulation. Concurrently, probabilistic frameworks are being developed to rigorously quantify uncertainties inherent in seismic ground motions, vehicle parameters, bridge properties, and road roughness within the VBI system26,27. These frameworks, often employing Bayesian networks28 or stochastic simulation, enable robust risk and fragility assessment of bridges subjected to combined seismic and vehicular loads. Furthermore, research continues into refining the modeling of the critical non-smooth contact interface between tires and the deck under dynamic and seismic conditions29.

In conclusion, most research on vehicle-bridge interaction has involved the development of custom programs to calculate and solve vehicle-bridge coupling systems, a process that is both complex and time-consuming. This approach is suitable for general dynamic problems, but for complex models with numerous degrees of freedom in both the bridge and vehicle, modeling accuracy and computational efficiency become critical. With the development of commercial software, many scholars have started to explore using commercial software to analyze vehicle-bridge coupling problems. A more effective approach to solving vehicle-bridge coupling issues is to use finite element software to model the entire dynamic system, including both the bridge and vehicle subsystems, as well as the vehicle-bridge coupling system, and to employ established solvers for the analysis.

This study proposes a seismic analysis framework for vehicle-bridge interaction systems based on ABAQUS software. A three-dimensional dynamic model for both the vehicle and bridge was developed, with penalty factors introduced to control the relative displacement at the contact surface, simulating the interaction between the bridge deck and the wheels. While finite element modeling (FEM) and penalty methods are established, our work integrates these techniques to address curved bridge-vehicle interaction under seismic loads, a scenario less explored compared to straight bridges. Prior studies (e.g., 6–8,22) primarily focus on straight bridges or railway systems, whereas our framework evaluates three-dimensional dynamic coupling effects specific to curved geometries, which introduce bending-torsion coupling and asymmetric seismic responses. For this study, there are some scientific hypotheses included: lower vehicle speeds exacerbate seismic-induced side-slip risks due to prolonged exposure to peak ground motions; heavier vehicles exhibit greater stability under seismic loads owing to increased wheel-road adhesion; pavement friction coefficients significantly alter driving safety, with snowy conditions posing the highest risk.

A framework for seismic analysis of vehicle-bridge interaction systems

Vehicle dynamics model

Due to the diversity of vehicle types, including passenger cars, trucks, and buses, each vehicle’s structure and dynamic characteristics differ, requiring the use of different vehicle models for computations and simulations. Commonly used vehicle models include 1/4 vehicle models, 1/2 vehicle models, and full vehicle models in three dimensions. These models simplify certain details while maintaining the basic structure of the vehicle to ensure computational efficiency while reflecting the vehicle’s dynamic characteristics as accurately as possible. In research, to analyze and evaluate the dynamic effects of vehicles on structures such as bridges during road travel, a typical six-wheel heavy truck is often used as the subject of study19 as shown in Fig. 1. This truck model is treated as a dynamic system with 12 degrees of freedom. In this model, the vehicle is simplified into a mass-spring-damping system. The vehicle’s suspension system and wheels are modeled using springs, while the energy dissipation in the suspension and wheels is simulated through viscous damping.

To conduct this analysis, it is first necessary to establish the governing dynamic equations for the vehicle-bridge interaction system under seismic loading, which can account for the combined effects of the vehicle, bridge, and seismic waves, thereby describing the dynamic response of the entire system. The penalty function method has demonstrated strong applicability and effectiveness in handling contact problems7. In static structural analysis, this method can accurately simulate the separation phenomena between contact surfaces, ensuring that non-contact states, which may occur under actual working conditions, are considered during the design and analysis process. In dynamic analysis, the penalty function can also capture changes in the contact state caused by load variations, such as sliding and friction effects-factors that are crucial for ensuring the safety and functionality of the structure. In this study, the penalty function method was used to define the complex contact relationship between the wheel and the bridge deck. By setting the interaction between the wheel and the bridge as a hard contact, allowing separation under certain conditions, it provides a more realistic simulation of various situations the wheel may encounter during operation. These situations include, but are not limited to, different road surface conditions, changes in speed, and vehicle dynamic responses. This approach not only improves the accuracy of numerical simulations but also provides a powerful tool for a deeper understanding of the wheel-bridge contact mechanics.

Seismic force boundary conditions

Based on the mechanism of seismic load action and the force transfer path, it is known that the seismic load directly acts on the parts in contact with the ground. Therefore, in the vehicle-bridge coupling system, only the parts in direct contact with the ground will directly bear the seismic load. In bridge structures, these parts in contact with the ground primarily include the bridge piers, bearings, and foundations. As a result, the pier support points and the bridge bearings become the key locations for seismic force boundaries. To accurately simulate the effect of seismic loads on the bridge, it is essential to appropriately set the seismic force boundary conditions. Seismic loads are typically applied through acceleration time histories (seismic records). Specifically, seismic acceleration is applied at the support points of the bridge piers or the foundation nodes connected to the ground, simulating the dynamic influence of seismic waves transmitted through the foundation to the bridge structure. These boundary conditions reflect the physical characteristics of seismic wave propagation and ensure that the dynamic response of the structure under seismic loading can be accurately calculated.

For the purpose of this study, the Direct Integration Method is used for seismic response analysis, and uniform excitation is applied to input seismic motion. Under uniform excitation, all structural nodes experience the same acceleration time history. This method assumes uniform seismic wave propagation speed and is suitable for preliminary analysis of most bridge structures, particularly when the seismic wave propagation speed is relatively uniform. After determining the interaction forces between the vehicle and bridge subsystems, the dynamic responses of each subsystem under seismic load can be obtained through numerical integration. Numerical integration allows the calculation of various responses of the bridge and vehicle system, such as displacement, acceleration, and stress, providing reliable data for subsequent structural design and evaluation.

FEM of VBI based on ABAQUS software

In this research, both vehicle and bridge models were incorporated into a unified finite element analysis framework using ABAQUS software. The vehicle-bridge interaction was simulated through the penalty method, which defines contact relations while permitting relative separation and sliding between the wheel and bridge deck. A “hard” contact model was employed to characterize the tire-deck interaction. The implicit solver in ABAQUS was utilized to solve the governing equations of motion, with the Hilber-Hughes-Taylor (HHT) algorithm-an enhanced variant of the Newmark-beta method-serving as the time-integration scheme for computing system displacements, velocities, and accelerations at each time step.

Among the bridge components, the curved segment is a continuous girder bridge composed of three 40-meter spans with a curvature radius of 60 m. The unconstrained degrees of freedom of the bearings in the horizontal direction are depicted in Fig. 5. In this study, the bridge structure is modeled using C3D8R solid elements within the ABAQUS software suite. Elastic connections between the superstructure and substructure are established by means of spring elements. A mesh size of 0.25 m is implemented for the main beam, which strikes a favorable balance between computational efficiency and the accuracy of results. In line with the geological conditions at the site, fixed boundary conditions are specified at the foundation base to simulate its supportive action on the superstructure. To accommodate the coupling effect between adjacent bridge spans, the simply supported spans in the vicinity are also included in the model. The overall model of the bridge and its boundary conditions are detailed in Fig. 6(a). Rayleigh damping is used in this study to model structural damping, with damping coefficients set to achieve a 1% damping ratio for the first two vibration modes. This research examines tangential interactions at the interface, where frictional sliding and tangential characteristics are modeled in ABAQUS using a coefficient of friction. To evaluate the impact on vehicle safety when driving across bridges, three distinct friction coefficients were selected: µd = 0.6 (dry road conditions), 0.4 (wet pavement), and 0.28 (snow-covered surfaces)32. The ABAQUS time integration flowchart of VBI system under earthquake load is shown in Fig. 7.

Previous validation studies have confirmed the reliability of this ABAQUS-based approach by comparing numerical results with analytical solutions for idealized vehicle-bridge contact problems, demonstrating excellent agreement4,7.

The three-dimensional vehicle model developed in ABAQUS accurately simulates the structural and dynamic behavior of a real vehicle. It incorporates the vehicle body, bogie, and wheels, with each component connected via rigid body constraints to ensure proper transmission of forces and motion during the simulation, as shown in Fig. 6(b). To control the vehicle’s motion, the model uses the center of gravity as a reference point and applies a curved displacement to simulate the vehicle’s trajectory on a real road. Additionally, to enhance the model’s accuracy, the vehicle’s mass distribution and inertial properties are taken into account. Each component is assigned its respective mass and moment of inertia, ensuring that the simulation results reflect the vehicle’s response to dynamic loading conditions.

Result analysis

Side-slip of a vehicle occurs when it loses lateral stability during motion, causing the body to deviate from its normal trajectory and resulting in lateral displacement. The occurrence of vehicle side slip is closely related to the friction between the wheels and the road surface. When the vehicle is subjected to lateral forces while in motion, friction is generated between the wheels and the road. If the lateral force exceeds the maximum adhesion between the wheels and the road surface, the vehicle will experience side slip. This study uses the side-slip safety factor (SSF) to define the occurrence of side slip accidents. When the SSF approaches 1, it indicates that the vehicle is close to a side slip condition. The influence of vehicle speed

To study the driving safety of vehicles crossing a curved bridge under earthquakes, an analysis of the impact of vehicle speed on driving safety was first conducted. In the simulation, the vehicle speeds were set at 30 km/h, 50 km/h, and 70 km/h, respectively. The vehicle loading is chosen as 5 tons, and the friction coefficient between the wheels and the road surface is selected as 0.6 (specifically for dry pavement).

Figure 8 shows the vertical and lateral contact forces of a vehicle crossing the bridge at different speeds under the P1 seismic load. It can be observed from the Fig. 8 that the vertical contact force on the front axle is smaller than that on the rear axle, which is due to the rearward center of gravity of the light truck. In addition, when the vehicle crosses the bridge at a slower speed, the vertical contact forces on both the front and rear axles are significantly higher compared to those at higher speeds. This is due to the characteristics of the seismic load time-history, where vehicles at slower speeds spend more time on the bridge and experience the peak values of the seismic load. It can also be observed that the lateral contact force on the front axle is smaller than that on the rear axle, which is because the rear axle, with a larger mass, experiences greater centrifugal force when navigating a curve. When the vehicle crosses the bridge at a slower speed, the lateral contact forces on both the front and rear axles are higher compared to those at faster speeds.

Figure 9 shows that the SSF values are influenced by both vehicle speed and seismic action duration. Specifically, when the vehicle speed is lower, the vehicle experiences a longer duration of seismic loading while crossing the bridge, allowing it to be more significantly affected by the seismic load history. Consequently, the SSF value of the vehicle may be larger. Similarly, when the vehicle speed is higher, the vehicle may not experience the extreme values of the seismic load history during its passage, resulting in a potentially smaller SSF value. The computational results presented in this paper more accurately reflect the variation trends and patterns of vehicle SSF values when crossing the bridge at different speeds during an earthquake. It should be noted that due to the inherent randomness of seismic load time histories, the SSF of vehicles at different speeds also shows some randomness under different seismic loads, reflected in the varying differences in SSF for each seismic load condition at different speeds.

The influence of epicentral distances

Figure 9 also shows the variation trend of SSF of vehicles under different earthquake loads (with different epicenter distances). It can be observed from the Fig. 9 that the SSF of the vehicle varies significantly under different seismic loads. Under the P2 and P3 seismic loads, there is a large difference in the vehicle’s SSF on the bridge. For example, when the speed is 30 km/h, the SSF under the P3 seismic load is 0.752, which is 27.03% higher than the 0.592 under the P2 seismic load. Similarly, when the speed is 70 km/h, the SSF under the P5 seismic load is 0.631, which is 88.92% higher than the 0.334 under the P4 seismic load. This indicates that although the P2 and P3 earthquakes, as well as the P4 and P5 earthquakes, are of the same magnitude, their epicentral distances are different, which greatly affects the vehicle’s SSF.

The influence of pavement friction coefficient

In reality, changes in weather conditions can alter the condition of the bridge deck, which in turn affects the friction between the vehicle tire and the bridge deck, influencing the safety of the vehicle’s movement on the bridge. An analysis is conducted on the impact of different bridge deck conditions on vehicle driving safety. To study the driving safety of vehicles crossing a curved bridge under earthquakes, an analysis of the impact of bridge deck friction coefficient on driving safety was conducted. The friction coefficients of the bridge deck are chosen as 0.6, 0.4, and 0.28, representing the bridge deck conditions under dry, wet, and snowy weather, respectively. The vehicle loading is chosen as 5 tons, and the vehicle speed is 50 km/h.

Table 4 presents the SSF for vehicles at different pavement friction coefficients under 10 earthquakes. As can be seen from Table 4, the SSF values on snowy pavement are generally the highest among the three conditions, ranging from 0.463 to 0.817. This suggests that under snowy conditions, vehicles are more prone to sliding, resulting in a higher SSF. However, there is some variation across different earthquakes, with the lowest SSF occurring in P4 (0.463) and the highest in P10 (0.817). The SSF values on wet pavement are slightly lower than those on snowy pavement, ranging from 0.443 to 0.773. This indicates that wet conditions still pose a risk of side slipping, but generally less than snowy conditions. The SSF values on dry pavement are the lowest, ranging from 0.395 to 0.715, which indicates that vehicles experience the least side slip on dry surfaces. Again, there is variability, with the lowest value in P4 (0.395) and the highest in P9 (0.715). The overall trend across the data seems to show that, under higher-magnitude earthquake conditions (e.g., P9, P10), the SSF tends to be higher, especially in the snowy condition. This could be because of the additional impact of the seismic forces contributing to vehicle instability, especially in more extreme weather conditions.

In conclusion, the data shows that pavement conditions have a significant impact on vehicle side slip. Vehicles on snowy and wet pavements are more likely to experience higher side slip, particularly under more intense earthquakes. The dry pavement condition provides the most stability and safety for vehicles. This information highlights the importance of considering both weather conditions and seismic activity when evaluating vehicle safety on bridges or roads.

Conclusions

This study investigates the driving safety of vehicles crossing a curved bridge during frequent seismic events, with a focus on the influence of vehicle speed, loading, and pavement friction coefficient on the risk of side-slip, quantified by the SSF. The key findings are as follows:

(1) A lower vehicle speed increases the SSF, making the vehicle more susceptible to side-slip during an earthquake. Vehicles traveling at higher speeds experience a lower SSF, indicating a reduced risk of skidding. It indicate the SSF values are influenced by both vehicle speed and seismic action duration.

(2) The impact of seismic load characteristics, such as epicentral distance, also significantly influences the SSF, with variations observed under different earthquake scenarios.

(3) Increased vehicle loading results in a lower SSF, suggesting that heavier vehicles are less prone to side-slip. This relationship emphasizes that vehicle weight can play a crucial role in enhancing driving stability, especially under earthquake-induced lateral forces.

(4) Pavement friction has a considerable effect on vehicle stability. Snowy and wet pavements lead to higher SSF values, indicating an increased risk of side-slip, particularly under intense seismic events. Conversely, dry pavements provide the highest stability, with the lowest SSF values observed, underscoring their importance in minimizing the risk of skidding.

Overall, this study highlights the combined effects of vehicle speed, loading, and pavement conditions on vehicle safety during earthquakes. The findings suggest that vehicle operators and infrastructure planners should consider these factors when assessing the risk of side-slip and implementing safety measures on seismic-prone bridges. While this study provides valuable insights into the driving safety of vehicles on curved bridges under frequent seismic excitations, it has several limitations. The research primarily focuses on a specific type of curved bridge and a 12-degree-of-freedom truck model, which may not fully represent the diverse range of bridge geometries and vehicle types encountered in real-world scenarios. Additionally, the framework relies on deterministic analyses, which do not fully address the inherent uncertainties in seismic events, vehicle dynamics, and bridge-vehicle interactions. These limitations suggest the need for further research to enhance the model’s applicability to a broader range of practical conditions.
