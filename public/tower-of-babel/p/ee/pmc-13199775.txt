Protocol for assessing anti-Siglec-9 antibody-mediated tumor phagocytosis by macrophages using pH-sensitive dyes and flow cytometry
Authors: Saitong Muneekaew, Thunchanok Nuchphongsai, Pasut Sasithong, Kritayaporn Saiprayong, Kamonphan Rungrueang, Methichit Wattanapanitch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol to assess and quantify macrophage-mediated cancer cell phagocytosis using pH-sensitive dyes and flow cytometry. We describe steps for isolating and sorting CD14+ monocytes from leukocyte reduction system (LRS) cones, differentiating monocyte-derived macrophages, and preparing K562 leukemic cells for co-culture. We then detail procedures for quantifying phagocytic activity using flow cytometry and pH-sensitive dyes and visualizing internalized K562 cells using a fluorescence microscope. This technique enables the study of macrophage phagocytic function and supports application in cancer immunotherapy research.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Muneekaew et al.1

Before you begin

The following section outlines a step-by-step protocol for evaluating the phagocytic activity of monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) against K562 leukemic cells. This protocol takes approximately 10 days to complete phagocytic assay. The workflow comprises four steps: (1) Isolation of CD14+ monocytes from a leukocyte reduction system (LRS) cone, (2) Differentiation of monocytes into macrophages, (3) Co-culture of macrophages with K562 leukemic cells and (4) Quantification of phagocytosis by flow cytometry and qualitative analysis by fluorescence microscope.

Innovation

We present a detailed protocol for evaluating tumor phagocytosis by MDMs using pH-sensitive dyes and flow cytometry in 96-well plates. This protocol enables specific detection of phagocytic activity, as pH-sensitive dyes only fluoresce under acidic conditions, indicating successful engulfment and phagolysosomal fusion. Flow cytometry provides efficient and quantitative analysis, even with limited numbers of macrophages and tumor cells. Overall, this protocol offers a straightforward and adaptable approach for investigating phagocytic functions of macrophages, supporting application in cancer immunotherapy research, including the evaluation of macrophage phagocytosis in combination with therapeutic antibodies to enhance antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), as well as the assessment of bispecific and trispecific killer cell engagers.

We isolate human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from leftover whole blood specimens, collected from leukocyte reduction system (LRS) cones of anonymous healthy donors. For CD14+ cell isolation, we obtain approximately 3 mL of blood from each LRS cone. Expected outcomes

Figure 2 illustrates the experimental workflow for assessing the phagocytic activity of monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) against K562 leukemic cells. We first isolate PBMCs from LRS cones using density gradient centrifugation, enrich CD14+ monocytes by cell sorting, and subsequently differentiate them into macrophages in the presence of 80 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). Upon sorting, CD14+ monocytes appear small and round (Figure 3A). After 7 days of differentiation, the cells adhere to the plate and exhibit a larger, more elongated morphology (Figure 3A). Flow cytometric analysis confirms the successful isolation of CD14+ monocytes through the expression of CD14 and CD45 (Figure 3B). Following differentiation, macrophages express CD14, CD45 and CD11b, CD80, HLA-DR, CD163 and CD206 indicating successful maturation and polarization (Figure 3C).Figure 2Schematic workflow of this studyFigure 3Characterization of CD14+ monocytes and macrophages(A) Representative morphology of CD14+ monocytes and differentiated macrophages.(B) Flow cytometric histograms showing marker expression in sorted monocytes, C. Following the 7-days differentiation period, we co-culture macrophages with pHrodo Red-labeled K562 cells at varying effect-to-target (E: T) ratios. After 1 h of incubation, we capture representative images and dissociate the co-cultures for staining with macrophage-specific antibodies and subsequent flow cytometric analysis. To distinguish between macrophages from phagocytosed tumor cells, we employ a gating strategy based on side scatter area (SSC-A) versus forward scatter area (FSC-A) to identify and exclude doublets. While a protein-containing buffer is used, the staining intensity was sufficient to reliably distinguish and exclude non-viable cells in Figure 4A. Subsequently, we gate on CD11b (APC/Cyanine 7) to identify macrophage population and pHrodo Red (PE) to detect internalized tumor cells (Figure 4A).1 We include macrophage-only and tumors-only controls to ensure accurate gating. After optimizing the gating strategy, we apply it to the co-cultured samples to quantify the frequency of phagocytosis within the total macrophage population. We calculate the percentage of phagocytosis using the equation provided in Figure 4B. Our results demonstrate that MDMs phagocytose K562 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Specifically, E: T ratios of 1: 4 and 1: 6 display significant increases in phagocytosis compared to E:T ratio of 1:1 (Figure 4B). Siglec-9 (Sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin-9) functions as an inhibitory immune checkpoint that suppresses myeloid cell phagocytosis upon binding to sialoglycan ligands on tumor cells.2,3,4 Disruption of the siglec-9/ligand interaction effectively removes this “don’t eat me: signal” thereby enhancing the phagocytic clearance of leukemic cells.5,6,7 To identify the primary target population for this immune checkpoint, we screen Siglec-9 expression across major PBMC subsets. Our screening results reveal that CD14+ monocytes exhibit the highest percentage and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Siglec-9 compared with CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and CD56+ NK cells (Figure 5A). Overall, this protocol provides a robust platform for evaluating tumor phagocytosis by MDMs using pH-sensitive dyes in a 96-well format. Flow cytometry enables quantitative analysis, allowing objective measurement of phagocytic frequency across thousands of individual cells. This result is complemented by qualitative analysis via fluorescence microscopy, which provides essential visual confirmation that the pH-sensitive signals from within the acidic environment of the phagolysosomes, rather than from extracellular adherent targets. As a result, this protocol provides a straightforward and adaptable approach for investigating phagocytic functions of macrophages, supporting applications in cancer immunotherapy research.

Quantification and statistical analysis

We calculate the percentage of phagocytosis using the Equation (CD11b+ pHrodo+/CD11b+) × 100. We perform statistical comparisons using One-Way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism.

Limitations

Macrophages may engulf apoptotic bodies, membrane fragments, or cellular debris instead of intact leukemic cells. This phenomenon can overestimate the “phagocytic index” as macrophages that internalize only small fragments may still appear positive for the target fluorophore. We utilize Siglec-9 blockade as a model to enhance phagocytosis. While a 1-hour pre-incubation with anti-Siglec-9 antibody is sufficient to observe a significant functional effect in this study, the kinetics of receptor saturation may vary depending on antibody clones and primary donor cells. Furthermore, although our untreated control group displays a clear baseline for phagocytic activity, including isotype controls in future experiments will further help to exclude potential non-specific Fc-receptor engagement.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The traditional “flicking” method to remove supernatants often causes the accidental detachment and loss of both macrophages and non-internalized target cells. This loss distorts the final cell counts and produces inaccurate data.

Potential solution

Use a vacuum aspiration to replace the flicking steps.

Problem 2

Phagocytic activity and Siglec-9 expression levels vary among healthy donors due to age, genetics and previous immune exposure. This variability affects the therapeutic effects of Siglec-9 blockade.

Potential solution

Instead of reporting raw percentages, we can calculate the “Phagocytic Fold Change” or “Relative Phagocytosis Index” by comparing the treated group directly with the control from the same donor.

Problem 3

The efficacy of the anti-Siglec-9 treatment depends on the binding affinity of the antibody and its saturation of the surface receptors. If the concentration is too low, the don’t eat me signal remains active. If it is too high, it may cause non-specific binding or steric hindrance.

Potential solution

Perform an antibody titration assay (dose-response curve) to determine the saturating concentration required for effective blockade before initiating co-culture with K562 cells.

Problem 4

APC/Cy7 for CD11b (macrophage marker) can exhibit signal spread into adjacent channels, including the PE channel used for pHrodo Red if compensation is not correctly configured. This spectral spillover may lead to false-positive results.

Potential solution

Always include single-stain controls (e.g., macrophage stained with anti-CD11b-APC/Cy7 only and K562 stained with pHrodo Red only) to generate an accurate compensation matrix before sample acquisition.

Problem 5

Mechanical scraping of adherent macrophages can cause cell damage and increase autofluorescence, resulting in high background signals.

Potential solution

Use a gentle dissociation reagent, such as TrypLE™ Select Enzyme, facilitating the release of adherent cells while maintaining viability and minimizing background noise.
