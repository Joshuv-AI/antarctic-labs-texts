Sodium Channel mRNA Binding Proteins
Authors: Barry London
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: post-transcriptional regulation, sodium channel, sudden cardiac death

The cardiac sodium channel NaV1.5, encoded by SCN5A, is responsible for the majority of the rapid inward sodium current (INa) that initiates each cardiac action potential.1 Loss-of-function mutations that decrease INa cause ∼20% of cases of Brugada syndrome, inherited conduction system disease, atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death.2 In addition, down-regulation of NaV1.5 expression and function in patients with cardiac ischemia, cardiomyopathies, and heart failure has been implicated in the more common scenarios associated with acquired arrhythmias and sudden death.3

Regulation of NaV1.5 and INa can occur at transcription, post-transcriptionally through interactions of NaV1.5 messenger RNA (mRNA) with microRNAs and RNA binding proteins, during protein translation, post-translationally by interactions of the NaV1.5 protein with beta subunits (NaVβ) or other binding proteins, and by post-translational modifications of the channel or its related proteins.4, 5, 6 These interactions and post-translational modifications can modify the number of NaV1.5 channels translated, the kinetic properties of the channel such as activation and inactivation, and/or trafficking of functional channels to the surface membrane. As one example of the role of RNA binding proteins in NaV1.5 regulation, the expression of the mRNA stabilizing protein HuR increased NaV1.5 mRNA and INa at baseline and following myocardial infarction (MI) in mice.7

In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Kang et al8 show that: 1) CPEB-4 expression at the RNA and protein levels is decreased in human heart failure, in mouse MI models, and in a hypoxic cell line model; 2) knockdown of CPEB-4 in mice and/or human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiac myocytes (iPSC-CMs) leads to decreased NaV1.5 expression, decreased action potential upstroke velocity, decreased INa without a change in channel steady-state activation or inactivation, electrocardiographic changes consistent with the decrease in INa, and a proarrhythmic phenotype; 3) CPEB-4 binds to the cytoplasmic polyadenylation elements in the NaV1.5 3′-UTR, these elements regulate protein expression of luciferase constructs in a CPEB-4 responsive manner, and iPSC-CMs with a mutant cytoplasmic polyadenylation element have less NaV1.5 expression and a smaller action potential upstroke velocity that is no longer responsive to CPEB-4 knockdown; and 4) CPEB-4 overexpression in a mouse MI model increases NaV1.5 expression and INa.

The findings by Kang et al expand our understanding of the role of CPEB-4 in heart failure. Prior studies had shown differential binding of Cpeb4 to the transcription factor Zeb1 mRNA under pathological growth conditions in cardiac myocytes and that a marked decrease in CPEB-4 expression led to increased Zeb1 expression, ventricular hypertrophy, and ventricular dysfunction.9 Here, direct effects of CPEB4 are shown on ion channel expression in mice and iPSC-CMs in the absence of significant ventricular hypertrophy or dysfunction. However, some indirect effects from changes in CPEB-4 through other binding proteins in the intact human heart cannot be excluded.

The authors clearly point out and discuss the apparent effects of CPEB-4 on other cardiac ion channels, including both calcium and potassium channels, that could explain the changes in the action potentials and QTc interval on electrocardiography that they describe. It would be interesting to know whether these changes are present in large animal models and humans. The pleiotropic effects of CREB-4 on ion channel targets do raise questions regarding its potential as a therapeutic target.

The study leaves open some questions. Brugada syndrome, a sodium channel deficiency condition, affects males ∼4 times more than females and specifically affects the right ventricle and septum.10 Here, the effects of sex and channel variation in different regions of the heart are not addressed. The precise mechanisms by which CPEB-4 binding increases NaV1.5 mRNA are also not explored. Understanding these details could ultimately shed light on potential therapeutics.

NaV1.5 regulation is complex at baseline and even more so in disease states. The findings of Kang et al add further to that complexity. We understand little about how the various regulatory mechanisms that control NaV1.5 expression and function interact. For example, does CPEB-4 binding interact with HuR binding or with microRNA-based regulation? It would seem that rules and pathways must exist to coordinate the different levels of regulation that ultimately contribute to the magnitude of INa.

In summary, Kang et al should be congratulated for having defined another potentially important regulatory mechanism that can influence sodium channel expression and arrhythmias in the heart. The overall importance of CPEB-4 as a cause of arrhythmias and its potential to be targeted for therapeutics remains to be seen.
