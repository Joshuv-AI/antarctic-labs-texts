Endocrine Disruptive Effects of the Herbicides Linuron, Chloridazon and Metribuzin on Bovine Granulosa Cells
Authors: Metehan Eser Kahveci, Ayse Arzu Yigit, Ruhi Kabakci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLinuron, chloridazon and metribuzin are herbicides thought to have potential endocrine disruption (ED) effects. This study investigated the effects of linuron, chloridazon and metribuzin on bovine granulosa cell viability and oestrogen (E2) and progesterone (P4) secretion in vitro. Cells were exposed to 10, 50 and 250 μM linuron; 10, 100 and 1000 μM chloridazon, and 25, 250 and 2500 μM metribuzin for 48 and 96 h. At both time points, linuron had no effect on cell viability, whereas chloridazon and metribuzin decreased viability at the highest doses. E2 and P4 release decreased at 96 h in all groups, with significant differences observed at the highest doses (250 μM linuron, 1000 μM chloridazon and 2500 μM metribuzin). The study demonstrated that chloridazon and metribuzin exerted cytotoxic effects at the highest concentrations and that all three herbicides disrupted E2 and P4 secretion in a time‐by‐dose dependent manner. The results of this study may serve to raise awareness of the endocrine‐disrupting effects of herbicides and to promote the consideration of alternatives for use that do not harm animal and human health.Keywords: bovine granulosa cell, chloridazon, endocrine disruptor, herbicide, linuron, metribuzin, steroidogenesis, viability

Introduction

Herbicides, representing 42% of global pesticide use, are extensively employed to control weeds, thereby enhancing agricultural yields to meet the nutritional demands of a growing population. While their widespread use can lower production costs and improve product quality, there are significant concerns regarding their impact on biodiversity, human health, aquatic life through water pollution, and bioaccumulation due to their prolonged half‐life (Sharma et al. 2022).

Linuron is widely used in maize and soybean fields (Pubchem 2025b), chloridazon is widely used in sugar beet fields (Pubchem 2025a), metribuzin is used primarily in potato fields, as well as in soybean and tomato fields, (Pubchem 2025c) for the pre‐emergence and post‐emergence control of broadleaf weeds. It is known that the herbicides used in this study inhibit photosynthesis in weeds that hinder the growth of these commonly cultivated plants (Pubchem 2025a, 2025b, 2025c). There are also reports that linuron (Swarcewicz et al. 2013), chloridazon (Fernández‐Pérez et al. 2010; Kucharski et al. 2012) and metribuzin residues are found in soil (Fernández‐Pérez et al. 2010) and identified as groundwater pollutants (EFSA 2016; Fernández‐Pérez et al. 2010).

It has been demonstrated that linuron is moderately persistent, with a half‐life ranging from 30 to 150 days. Additionally, its effectiveness increases in soils with low organic content, and its migration to groundwater is restricted by absorption and microbial degradation processes; therefore, it is more likely to contaminate surface waters (Chen 2014). It has also been reported that cattle, sheep, cats, and dogs are generally more sensitive to polyurea herbicides, including linuron, than other animals (Gupta 2012). Chloridazon, which belongs to the pyridazinone herbicide group, can move through various soil types or exhibit persistence in soil and water. Chloridazon demonstrates a high degree of persistence in soil under dark and aerobic conditions, while its environmental degradation in water and soil is primarily facilitated by photolysis. (Tomlin 2006). The photolysis half‐life in soil is 69 days, while in aerobic soil, the half‐life ranges from 90 to 152 days. The photolysis degradation time of chloridazon in water has been determined to be 12.5 days. It can migrate from soil to both surface or groundwater and is therefore classified as a potential contaminant capable of polluting water sources (USEPA 2005). Metribuzin, which is a herbicide belonging to the triazinone group, has been identified as one of the herbicides with the highest potential for leaching into groundwater, due to its notably high water solubility (USEPA 2003). In a study conducted in Milan (Loffredo et al. 2021), a metribuzin concentration of 5.84 μg/g in the top 0–5 cm layer of soil, while trace amounts were also detected in the roots and shoots of plants in the same area. Despite being designated as Restricted Use Pesticides by the EPA (Armendáriz et al. 2014; Chen 2014), linuron and chloridazon continue to be utilized in agricultural products within the United States (FR 2024) and Canada (PMRA 2020). No sources have been found that relate to the restricted use of metribuzin.

Excessive exposure to such chemicals if used recklessly and irresponsibly, leads to many adverse effects, including endocrine disruption and reproductive problems. These reproductive problems have a detrimental effect on fertility of animals, resulting in significant economic losses. Therefore, the maintanence of optimal ovarian functionality constitutes a pivotal element in ensuring reproductive health of the animal. Endocrine disruptors interfere with the homeostatic balance by altering the regulation and release of hormones in the body. Numerous herbicides including inorganic and organic arsenicals, phenoxy acid derivatives, bipyridyl derivatives, ureas and thioureas, protoporphyrinogen oxidase inhibitors, triazines and triazoles, amides and acetamides, dinitrophenol compounds, triazolopyrimidine herbicides, imidazolinones, and benzoic acids have been reported to exert endocrine disrupting effects on the reproductive system (Gupta and Gupta 2025).

The ovary contains several differentiated cell types that regulate reproductive functions. The follicular wall consists of an outer layer of theca cells and an inner layer of granulosa cells. Granulosa cells (GC) synthesize oestrogen (E2), which plays an important role in follicular development, while both granulosa and theca cells synthesize progesterone (P4). Oestrogen further enhances the effect of FSH and LH by promoting the proliferation of GC within the follicle (Hsueh et al. 1984).

A study investigating the effects of linuron on bovine luteal cells revealed a marked inhibition of P4 release (Bilmen et al. 2016). Although there is an in vitro study reporting that linuron affects PGE2, testosterone, and oxytocin synthesis in cows (Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017) and an in vivo study reporting that chloridazon shows estrogenic effects in rats (Vargová et al. 1995), no studies showing the effects of both herbicides on oestrogen and progesterone secretion of the GC have been found. Furthermore, no research has been identified that investigates the effects of metribuzin on either the male or female reproductive system. Triazins are herbicides that are used for weed growth, and metribuzin, atrazine, and simazine are known as symmetrical triazines according to the placement of the nitrogen atom in their structure (Velasco et al. 2021). However, it was reported that atrazine, a triazine derivative like metribuzin, had no effect on the viability of myometrial, endometrial, ovarian granulosa, and luteal cells in cows (Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017), and that simazine, which is also another derivative, increased the proliferation, viability, and progesterone release in swine GC (Grasselli et al. 2018).

The herbicides examined in this study are known to exert endocrine‐disrupting effects in different physiological systems. However, evidence regarding their potential endocrine disrupting effects on the female reproductive system is quite limited. The increasing use of herbicides and consequent exposure to these chemicals contribute to their accumulation in the body. Studies have reported that atrazine, a herbicide, is even present in human milk, cervical mucus, follicular and sperm fluid (Wagner et al. 1990). Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of linuron, chloridazon, and metribuzin herbicides, which are widely used in agricultural spraying today, on the viability of bovine GC and the in vitro secretion of E2 and P4 hormones by these cells. The data obtained from this study may identify the effects of these herbicides on the female reproductive system, the assessment of their impacts on both human and animal health, and the contribution to increasing public awareness about herbicide.

Materials and Methods

This study was supported by The Scientific and Technological Research Council of Turkey (Grant Number: 119O020).

Isolation and Incubation of the Granulosa Cells

Ovaries from cows slaughtered at local abattoirs were immediately placed in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Sigma‐Aldrich, P4417) containing 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution (Sigma‐Aldrich, A5955) at 37°C and transported to the laboratory within 1 h. Ten ovaries containing antral follicles were used for the isolation. Some of the isolated cells were utilized for this study, while the remaining were cryopreserved in nitrogen at −186°C to be used in subsequent experiments. All experiments were conducted using a single pooled sample of bovine GC obtained from the 10 ovaries. Granulosa cells were isolated using a modified version of the protocol described by Glister et al. (2014). In the laboratory, ovaries were washed with 70% ethanol and PBS respectively, and the cells were collected from > 5 mm follicles by aspiration with an 18‐gauge needle. To eliminate oocytes, the cell suspension was filtered with a sterile 100 μm cell strainer (Corning, 431,752) and the cells were washed twice with PBS by centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min at 4°C. Then, 2 mL of sterile distilled water was added to the cells for 10 s to induce erythrocyte lysis and the volume immediately adjusted to 15 mL with PBS. The suspension was centrifuged again at 1000 g for 5 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of DMEM F12‐HAM medium with L‐glutamine and 15 mM HEPES (Sigma‐Aldrich, D8900). For cell counting and viability determination, 20 μL of cell suspension and 20 μL of trypan blue solution (0.4%) (Sigma‐Aldrich, T8154) were combined and the mixture was analysed using an automatic cell counter (Countess II, Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA).

In order to prepare the experimental setup, 2 different 96‐well cell culture plates for 48‐ and 96‐h incubation periods were labelled with three wells each for the control group, linuron, chloridazon, and metribuzin for low, medium, and high doses. Then, DMEM F12‐HAM medium with L‐glutamine and 15 mM HEPES containing (Sigma‐Aldrich, D8900) 10% Fetal Calf Serum (FCS) and 1% antibiotic‐antimycotic was prepared for the first 24 h incubation of the cells. This medium was aliquoted with a volume of 100 uL for each labelled wells of two 96‐well plates (Corning Life Sciences, Cat no: 3595) 2 × 104 cells were added to each well. The cells were incubated in an incubator (Binder, CB 150, GmbH) adjusted at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow the cells to attach to the plates. After 24 h, cells were exposed to different concentrations of herbicides.

Before chemical treatment, stock solutions of linuron (Supelco, 36,141) in 0.1% pure ethyl alcohol, and chloridazon (Supelco, CRM06796) and metribuzin (Supelco, 36,165) in 0.1% sterile dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma‐Aldrich, D2650) were prepared. Ethanol and DMSO were used as a vehicle control. Then, they were diluted in incubation medium containing 1% antibiotic‐antimycotic and 1% ITS premix (10 μg/mL insulin, 5.5 μg/mL transferrin, 5 ng/mL sodium selenite) (Sigma‐Aldrich, I3146). This medium was used in the control group and in the preparation of the application doses of endocrine disrupting herbicides. Final in vitro treatment concentrations of linuron were 10, 50, 250 μM (L10, L50, L250), and of chloridazon were 10, 100, 1000 μM (CL10, CL100, CL1000), and of metribuzin were 25, 250, 2500 μM (M25, M250, M2500). For the evaluation of steroid synthesis, GC were also supplied with 10– 7 M androstenedione (Sigma‐Aldrich, PHL89562) as a precursor of E2. Doses were determined by multiple preliminary studies based on a literature review of in vivo and in vitro studies, which used low, medium and high doses to determine the range of effects.

At the end of the first 24 h of incubation, the old medium was discarded and 100 μL of fresh medium containing endocrine disruptor at the doses given above for each well was added to the adherent cells. The plates were then incubated for 48 h.

Viability Tests and Hormone Analysis

After 48 h, the medium samples were collected from the wells and stored at −20°C for hormone analysis. The cells remaining on the plate were used for cytotoxicity analysis by WST test (CELLPRO‐RO 11644807001). After that, the plate marked for 96 h was placed back in the incubator for another 48 h. According to the WST assay procedure, WST is a tetrazolium compound, and added to the cell medium and incubated; the tetrazolium salts in WST are reduced by taking electrons only from living cells and form a colour change by transforming into a structure called formazan, and this change is measured colorimetrically to determine the viability level in the cells. For this purpose, 100 μL each of phenol red‐free DMEM F12‐HAM (Sigma‐Aldrich, D6434, USA) containing 10% antibiotic‐antimycotic solution, 1% ITS premix and 1% L‐glutamine was added to the 48‐h plate wells in which the medium was collected for hormone analysis. In addition, cell‐free medium as blank and medium containing 0.1% ethyl alcohol/DMSO were added to 9 new wells for 3 replicates each. Then, 10 μL of WST‐1 kit was added to all wells, wrapped with foil to prevent light and left in the incubator for 4 h. At the end of incubation, the plate was placed in a microplate reader in the dark and absorbance values were measured against 450 nm and 650 nm. Net absorbances were calculated according to the formula (abs450‐abs650)‐blank. These values were used to determine the effect of these compounds on viability by comparing with the control groups, which were assumed to have 100% viability. The viability ratio was calculated according to the formula = absorbance of treatment group/absorbance of control group x100. For the plate incubated for 96 h, medium sampling and WST test were repeated in the same way.

The secreted E2 and P4 levels were measured via an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay using the Cobas e601 automated immunoanalyzer for in vitro diagnosis test (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) by using Elecsys assay reagents (06656021119 and 07092539190 respectively). In this method, the sample and biotinylated hormone‐specific antibody are incubated to form an immunocomplex and the immunocomplex is formed depending on the amount of hormone. Streptovidin‐coated microparticles and a hormone derivative labelled with a ruthenium complex are added to this mixture and an antibody‐hapten complex is formed. The whole complex starts to adhere to the solid phase with the help of streptovidin‐biotin interaction. The reaction mixture is aspirated into the measuring cells and voltage is applied to the electrodes causing chemiluminescence. The intensity of the glow is recorded, which allows the quantification of the targeted analyte of the sample. The detection limits of E2 and P4 was < 5 pg/mL and < 0.05 ng/mL and inter‐ and intra‐assay CVs were 3.7% and 5.7% for oestrogen and 2.9% and 3.6% for progesterone assay respectively.

Statistical Analysis

The data obtained at the end of the study were assessed using GraphPad 6.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). After the homogeneity of variance test was carried out by Levene's test, for the comparison of the data according to time (48‐96 h), a dependent t‐test, to compare dose‐dependent changes, one‐way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparison test were used. The p < 0.05 level was considered significant.

Discussion

Since herbicides leave residues on agricultural products through spraying and contaminate water sources by leaching into the environment, they may also constitute a potential source of risk for the health of living organisms in nature and humans consuming these products. Our study is the first to investigate the effects of chloridazon and metribuzin herbicides on cell viability and E2 and P4 synthesis in bovine granulosa cell culture. Although Wrobel and Mlynarczuk (2017) investigated the effects of linuron on E2 secretion in GC and E2 and P4 secretion in luteal cells, the present study is significant in that it examined the effects of linuron on both E2 and P4 secretion. The cells utilized in the study were obtained from a total of 10 ovaries and subsequently pooled, which can be regarded as a potential limitation of the study.

Due to their low biodegradation rates, linuron, chloridazon, and metribuzin are known to be present in soil, surface water, or groundwater (Loffredo et al. 2021; Swarcewicz et al. 2013; Vidal et al. 2022) and can easily spread out and bioaccumulate in humans and animals consuming plants or water contaminated with these compounds due to their lipophilic properties (McKinlay et al. 2008). Considering the potential for bioaccumulation, the doses applied in this study were intentionally set higher than those expected from natural environmental exposure. Therefore, the doses used in the study are higher than the daily doses that people can be naturally exposed to through drinking water or surface water.

Effects of Linuron on Cell Viability

The present study demonstrated that GC exposed to 10, 50, and 250 μM linuron up to 96 h exhibited no toxic effects or alterations in viability even at relatively high doses and prolonged exposure periods. These findings are consistent with those of Wrobel and Mlynarczuk (2017), who observed that lower linuron doses (0.1, 1, and 10 ng/mL) did not affect bovine myometrium and endometrium cells and ovarian granulosa and luteal cells after 72 h. Although the exact mechanism responsible for the pesticide toxicity is yet not fully understood, it has been reported that increased reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), which induce systemic oxidative stress, play a key role. Mitochondria and NADPH oxidase enzymes are the two main sources of ROS associated with the observed toxicity (Kalyanaraman et al. 2016). Studies conducted with diuron, a herbicide belonging to the thiourea group like linuron, have indicated that it can inhibit oxidative phosphorylation and gluconeogenesis and decrease the mitochondrial ATP/ADP ratio (Owen and Halestrap 1993). In another in vitro study, it was reported that diuron‐induced toxicity may be caused by generation of ROS (Kao et al. 2019). Based on the results obtained with diuron, the lack of effect of the doses of linuron used in our study on the viability of GC may be attributed to the assumption that these doses do not alter ROS production in GC.

Effects of Chloridazon on Cell Viability

No in vivo or in vitro studies examining the impact of chloridazon on the viability of reproductive cells in any species were identified in the literature. However, it has been reported that LCS‐1, a similar derivative, at a dose of 250 μM, inhibits superoxide dismutase, an enzyme that plays a very important role in defending against free radicals formed during cell energy production in the bloodstream forms of the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma brucei, thereby reducing cell viability. It has been noted that a decrease in the activity of this enzyme may lead to the accumulation of free radicals, causing oxidative stress and subsequent cellular damage (Steverding and Barcelos 2020). In our study, the cytotoxic effect of chloridazon observed only at the highest dose after 48 h and at 100 μM after 96 h compared to the control group is thought to be associated with this mechanism. Furthermore, despite the difference in cell type used, the study mentioned above is similar to our study in terms of the doses used.

Effects of Metribuzin on Cell Viability

The results that the lowest dose of metribuzin in our study did not affect the cell viability compared to the control group is consistent with the findings of previous studies conducted with lower doses of atrazine in bovine (Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017) and swine (Basini et al. 2012) GC with shorter incubation periods than our study. This outcome suggests that metribuzin may not affect granulosa cell viability at concentrations lower than 25 μM. The decrease in cell viability at higher than 25 μM in our study is also consistent with other previous studies in human neuroblastoma cells (Abarikwu and Farombi 2015) and human lymphocytes (Flores‐Maya et al. 2005). In addition, when cell viability was compared according to incubation periods, it was found that metribuzin reduced cell viability similarly to chloridazon from the middle dose. However, this impact was more prominent in metribuzin, especially at higher doses.

The WST assay, utilized in this study, measures cell viability by evaluating the activity of mitochondrial dehydrogenase. Our study indicates that the viability did not change with the linuron treatment. However, chloridazon and metribuzin significantly decreased cell viability at different doses and incubation times, indicating that chloridazon and metribuzin can impair mitochondrial function of the cells in these groups. Mitochondrial ROS are likely the primary mechanism for the cytotoxic effects of herbicides. ROS production is a crucial factor influencing cell viability, and there may be variances in ROS production among cell types and between herbicides and their metabolites (Mohammed et al. 2020). Thus the varying impacts of the chemicals used in our study on the viability of GC may be attributed to this reason.

Effects of Linuron on Oestrogen and Progesterone Concentration

Previous studies conducted with cultured bovine GC without an added oestrogen precursor (i.e., androgen) show that 72 h treatment with 10 ng/mL atrazine increased E2 secretion without affecting cell viability. The same study also reported that doses of linuron ranging from 0.1 to 10 ng/mL did not affect E2 secretion, whereas a dose of 1 ng/mL reduced P4 secretion (Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017). Studies with porcine GC supplemented with an androgen showed that 48 h treatment with 10 μM atrazine (Basini et al. 2012) or simazine (Grasselli et al. 2018) increased P4 secretion while decreased E2 secretion. In cultured human GC, 10–20 μM atrazine either increased basal (unstimulated) aromatase activity (androgen added; Holloway et al. (2008)) or decreased FSH‐induced E2 and P4 secretion without affecting cell viability (no androgen precursor added; Pogrmic‐Majkic et al. (2018)). In addition, 20 μM of this pestiside decreased FSH‐induced E2 secretion and inhibited the expression of mRNA CYP19A1 and LH‐receptor (LHR) in cultured rat GC with an androgen (Fa et al. 2013). In our study with the androgen precursor added, linuron did not affect E2 release at any dose after 48 h. The same result was obtained at 96 h at the doses of 10 and 50 μM. These results are consistent with the findings of Wrobel and Mlynarczuk (2017). As noted in that study, this outcome may be related to linuron's impairement to the conversion of androgens to oestrogens in bovine GC. Atrazine was reported to increase E2 release at a dose of 10 ng/mL (Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017), in contrast to other studies indicated that it inhibits E2 release at 10 μM (Basini et al. 2012; Grasselli et al. 2018) and at 10–20 μM doses. Latter results are consistent with the inhibitory effect of metribuzin on E2 secretion observed only at the highest dose in our study, suggesting that the endocrine disruptive dose of metribuzin may be much higher. It should be noted that some studies did not add an androgen precursor which is needed for E2 secretion and/or did not add a stimulator of aromatase (i.e., FSH) to the culture medium. Thus, it is suggested that definitive conclusions on the effect of linuron and atrazine on GC function cannot be drawn without further investigations.

No studies investigating the impact of linuron on the P4 levels released from GC were found in the literature. However, a study conducted on bovine luteal cells reported that exposure to linuron for 72 h did not affect the P4 level at 0.1 ng/mL, but caused suppression at 1 ng/mL (Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017). Although this finding aligns with our study in terms of dose‐dependent suppression of the hormone level, it differs in that it concludes that the highest dose of 10 ng/mL does cause a statistically significant effect on P4 when the cell types used are ignored. In a previous study on bovine luteal cells (Bilmen et al. 2016), it was emphasized that 50 μM linuron did not affect P4 secretion at the same incubation times, consistent with our results, whereas higher doses of linuron (100 and 200 μM) suppressed P4 secretion. These findings collectively suggest that linuron may have a more pronounced P4‐reducing effect in luteal cells compared to GC.

In a study in pregnant rats administered linuron, the expression of enzymes and proteins involved in the conversion of cholesterol to progesterone and progesterone to testosterone was not affected in male offspring (Wilson et al. 2009). These findings align with our findings in which low and medium doses of linuron did not affect E2 and P4 levels. Although it occurs in different cells (gonads, adrenal cortex, placenta), steroid hormone synthesis follows similar mechanisms in female animals. The decrease in E2 and P4 levels observed at high doses of linuron in our study is consistent with and supports the findings that exposure to high doses of linuron reduced the expression of P450scc and 3β‐HSD enzymes, which are crucial for the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone and pregnenolone to P4, respectively, in male offspring (Ding et al. 2017).

Effects of Chloridazon on Oestrogen and Progesterone Concentration

In the present study, only at the highest dose of chloridazon also suppressed E2 synthesis at 96 h. P4 synthesis was similarly suppressed at the highest chloridazon concentration at 48 h and at all doses at 96 h. Although linuron and metribuzin did not significantly affect P4 synthesis, the marked suppression of this hormone by high‐dose chloridazon highlights its detrimental effect on P4 production following short‐term exposure. No studies have been found in the literature regarding the effects of chloridazon on reproductive hormones. An observation in porcine GC has revealed that DDE, an organochlorine pesticide related to chloridazon, at doses ranging from 0.3 to 320 μg/mL inhibited P4 synthesis, which is associated with suppression of cAMP synthesis and CYP11A1 expression, thereby inhibiting steroidogenesis (Crellin et al. 2001) also supports our results.

Although some of the studies conducted with chloridazon and related chemicals in the group of organochlorine pesticides supported our findings, it is important to note that different findings were obtained even with the metabolites of the chemicals used in these studies. This suggests that herbicides with similar chemical composition may not have the same effects. Though the enzyme involved in E2 and P4 production steps was not examined in our study, the mechanism underlying the suppression of E2 synthesis and P4 synthesis and the inhibition of all doses at 96 h by chloridazon may be related to the inhibition of the activities of some enzymes that have a critical role in E2 and P4 production by acting as androgen receptor antagonists at these doses. Considering that the herbicides used in this study reduced cell viability, particularly at the highest doses, it is possible that the observed decrease in steroid secretion may be attributed to the decline in cell viability. Nevertheless, our findings warrant further detailed investigations to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Effects of Metribuzin on Oestrogen and Progesterone Concentration

Since no previous studies regarding with the endocrine effects on GC of metribuzin, we compared our results with other studies conducted on atrazine, which is structurally similar to metribuzin but does not belong to the triazinone group, and which reported different effects on the synthesis of E2 and P4 in different species (Grasselli et al. 2018; Holloway et al. 2008; Wrobel and Mlynarczuk 2017). Fa et al. (2013) used rat GC in their study and employed testosterone as an oestrogen precursor and FSH to stimulate aromatase. They have reported that atrazine reduced FSH‐induced LHR and CYP19A1 mRNA expression and E2 production in rat GC. The inhibitory effect on E2 secretion of the highest dose of metribuzin (2500 μM) is consistent with these reports that atrazine may function as an androgen receptor antagonist by reducing LHR expression. In the aforementioned study, it was reported that atrazine reduces E2 secretion by decreasing LHR expression in GC. This reduction is significant because LHR activates intracellular second messengers and affects steroidogenesis (Chin and Abayasekara 2004). These findings may be considered for the inhibitory effect of metribuzin on E2 secretion. In addition, Wrobel and Mlynarczuk (2017) reported that 1 ng/mL atrazine exposed bovine luteal cells without the use of androgen precursors and Holloway et al. (2008) reported that 10–20 μM of this herbicide reduced P4 synthesis in human GC using androgen precursors. These studies emphasize the need for further investigation of the effects of metribuzin on progesterone synthesis in GC, considering the use or non‐use of androgen precursors, cellular differences, species differences (bovine, rat, and human GC), different receptor and enzyme expression profiles, and the observation of these effects at much lower doses. Additionally, to better understand the effects of the herbicides examined in this study on the female reproductive system and to determine whether the observed decrease in E2 and P4 secretion is a result of direct interference with steroidogenic pathways or indirectly due to reduced cell viability, future studies should determine the expression levels of proteins (e.g., CYP19A1 and 3β‐HSD) involved in this mechanism.

Conclusion

This is the first study to investigate the effects of linuron, chloridazon and metribuzin on granulosa cell viability, oestrogen and progesterone secretion over such a wide dose range. The results suggest that prolonged exposure to high doses of these herbicides may adversely affect ovarian cells, which are critical for reproductive physiology, potentially disrupting hormone secretion. These findings underline the need for careful environmental monitoring and risk assessment of these herbicides in agricultural ecosystems. Further studies should evaluate oxidative stress parameters and the expression of key steroidogenic enzymes (CYP19A1, 3β‐HSD, StAR) to clarify the underlying mechanisms. However, future studies should be conducted with bovine GC at bovine body temperature (i.e., 38.5°C) to verify results of the present study. Furthermore, similar studies should be repeated in vivo to prove whether the doses found in living organisms will have a similar effect.

Ethics Statement

Since slaughterhouse waste tissues were used in the study, an approval was obtained that ethical approval was not required. The term “procedures performed with dead animals or tissues, slaughterhouse materials, waste foetuses” is employed in the “2. section of the ‘k paragraph’ of Article 8 of the ‘Regulation on the Working Procedures and Principles of Local Ethics Committees for Animal Experiments’”, as published in the Official Gazette dated 5 February 2014 and numbered 28,914. In your project, titled ‘In vitro Effects of Linuron, Chloridazon and Metribuzin on Bovine Granulosa Cell Steroidogenesis’, the use of cow ovarian tissue obtained from slaughterhouse waste products as experimental material meant that it was evaluated within the scope of the relevant article of the regulation, and ethics committee approval was not required.
