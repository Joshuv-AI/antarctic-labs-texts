Multiple hybrid zones involving four Cardamine species and their triploid progeny: watching allopolyploid speciation in action?
Authors: Judita Zozomová‐Lihová, Marek Šlenker, Barbora Šingliarová, Kristína Pulišová, Terezie Mandáková, Karol Marhold
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary Hybrid zones provide excellent opportunities to study evolutionary processes linked to interspecific gene flow, including introgression, genetic erosion, polyploid establishment, and speciation. The genus Cardamine (Brassicaceae) serves as an excellent model for polyploid evolution, including one of the few well‐documented neo‐allopolyploid species that have evolved in the last 300 yr.Using a combination of flow cytometric screening of nuclear DNA content, next‐generation restriction site‐associated DNA sequencing, and genomic in situ hybridization, we uncovered an unprecedented case of extensive interspecific hybridization in Cardamine, involving four parental species and their predominantly triploid offspring.We demonstrate the recurrent and polytopic origins of both autotriploids and allotriploids, the latter integrating different parental genomes. Our findings highlight Cardamine rivularis as a central player in this system, likely producing unreduced female gametes at a high rate, which drives the formation of diverse triploids. However, this species may also face the risk of genetic swamping and ecological displacement. The substantial genetic variation of the hybrids, their high frequency, partial fertility, and efficient clonal spread suggest significant evolutionary potential.Overall, we propose that these hybrid zones provide a rare and valuable natural laboratory for studying the emergence of neo‐allopolyploids and the mechanisms shaping polyploid evolution.

Introduction

Hybridization and polyploid formation are widely recognized as important evolutionary drivers and mechanisms of adaptation and speciation in vascular plants (Mallet, 2005; Soltis & Soltis, 2009; Abbott et al., 2013; Van de Peer et al., 2017; Heslop‐Harrison et al., 2023; Edger et al., 2025). Although prezygotic and postzygotic reproductive barriers limit interspecific mating, frequent reports of hybrids and the high frequency of allopolyploids indicate that these barriers regularly fail (Soltis & Soltis, 2009; Vallejo‐Marín & Hiscock, 2016; Abbott, 2017). Hybridization is often associated with natural or human‐induced environmental disturbance, as it promotes secondary contact between previously isolated species (Anderson, 1948; Gómez et al., 2015; Todesco et al., 2016). Such disturbances also create ecologically heterogeneous and unstable microhabitats with reduced competition that can be exploited by a variety of hybrid genotypes (Wolf et al., 2001; Arnold et al., 2012; Abbott & Rieseberg, 2021).

The formation of hybrid zones, that is, regions where genetically distinct populations meet and mate, producing offspring of mixed ancestry (Harrison, 1993), can have diverse evolutionary consequences. They can be a source of new genetic variation by promoting adaptive introgression between lineages or the formation of a new homoploid or allopolyploid species (Stebbins, 1959; Rieseberg et al., 2007; Soltis & Soltis, 2009; Arnold et al., 2012; Abbott et al., 2013; Abbott & Brennan, 2014; Goulet et al., 2017). Interspecific hybridization can also strengthen premating reproductive isolation due to selection against maladapted hybrids or costly interbreeding (a process known as reinforcement; Jiggins & Mallet, 2000; Hopkins, 2013; Goulet et al., 2017). Conversely, hybridization can also be destructive when it leads to the collapse of formerly separate lineages or species through lineage fusion or speciation reversal (Seehausen et al., 2008; Kearns et al., 2018) or when it causes the decline and even extinction of parental species via demographic or genetic swamping (Wolf et al., 2001; Gómez et al., 2015; Todesco et al., 2016). Despite intensive research, it remains unclear which ecological or genetic parameters favor different hybridization outcomes and to what extent they are predictable and repeatable.

While homoploid hybrid speciation in plants is considered rare and is also more difficult to prove (Arnold, 1992; Rieseberg, 1997; Abbott et al., 2010; Nieto Feliner et al., 2017; Nevado et al., 2020), allopolyploid speciation appears to be much more common (Soltis et al., 2004b, 2009; Soltis & Soltis, 2009; Van de Peer et al., 2017). Fertilization involving unreduced gametes is believed to be a predominant mechanism of polyploid formation within populations, usually as a two‐step process via triploids (triploid bridge) or possibly even in one step after the union of two unreduced gametes (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998; Husband & Sabara, 2004; Soltis et al., 2004b; Kreiner et al., 2017a). Although the rate of unreduced gamete production in natural populations is generally low, it can vary between and within species and in response to environmental factors (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998; Kreiner et al., 2017a, 2017b; Brown et al., 2024). Increased rates of unreduced gamete production could explain the frequent recurrence of polyploidy in certain plant lineages, but studies comparing unreduced gamete production with rates of neopolyploid formation in populations are largely lacking (Kreiner et al., 2017b).

Triploids in natural and sexually reproducing populations are traditionally considered rare, maladaptive, and only temporarily persisting because of reduced fertility caused by irregular meiosis and a high proportion of aneuploid gametes (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998). It is also expected that newly formed triploids will be selectively eliminated due to frequency‐dependent mating (principle of minority cytotype exclusion; Levin, 1975), unless there are counteracting forces, such as recurrent origins, alterations in reproduction mode (switch to autogamy, asexual seed formation, and/or increased vegetative growth), or assortative mating in response to spatial or ecological separation (Husband & Sabara, 2004; Robertson et al., 2011; Hojsgaard et al., 2014; Chrtek et al., 2017). However, a growing number of studies suggest that the frequency and evolutionary role of triploids in natural populations have been underestimated (Herben et al., 2016; Šingliarová et al., 2019, 2023; Šmíd et al., 2020; Morgan et al., 2021; Bartolić et al., 2024, 2025), which merits reassessment and further research.

Recently formed natural allopolyploids represent unique evolutionary models to investigate processes of polyploid formation, the immediate impacts of genome doubling, and evolutionary changes in the earliest stages of putative allopolyploid speciation (Edger et al., 2025). Nevertheless, only a few such neopolyploid systems that have evolved in the last 300 yr have been detected and studied in more detail, including Spartina (Ainouche et al., 2003, 2004), Senecio (Abbott & Lowe, 2004), Tragopogon (Soltis et al., 2004a), Mimulus (Vallejo‐Marín & Lye, 2013; Vallejo‐Marín et al., 2017), and Cardamine (Urbanska et al., 1997; Mandáková et al., 2013). Edger et al. (2025) not only showed some commonalities in their evolutionary trajectories but also emphasized that each polyploid represents a unique story. Despite thorough studies, significant knowledge gaps remain, highlighting the value of discovering and characterizing additional systems (see e.g. Welles & Ellstrand, 2016, 2020) that could provide further insights and reveal other facets of polyploid evolution.

Cardamine L. (Brassicaceae) comprises nearly 300 species world‐wide (Marhold et al., 2021), with allopolyploidy playing a fundamental evolutionary role (Lihová & Marhold, 2006; Carlsen et al., 2009; Marhold et al., 2018), making this genus an attractive model for studying mechanisms of polyploid evolution. The outstanding case of neopolyploid speciation documented from the Western Alps – C. schulzii Urbanska – was initially considered an autohexaploid derived from a triploid hybrid C. insueta Urbanska (C. rivularis auct. non Schur × C. amara L. subsp. amara) and a textbook example of allopolyploid speciation via a triploid bridge (Urbanska et al., 1997; Urbanska & Landolt, 1999). Nevertheless, recent evidence has shown that it is a trigenomic hybrid that persists locally through perennial growth and clonal reproduction (Mandáková et al., 2013; Zozomová‐Lihová et al., 2014). By contrast, triploid C. insueta is more vigorous, variable, and abundant, probably due to the combined effects of multiple origins, clonal growth, and partial fertility (Urbanska‐Worytkiewicz, 1977; Mandáková et al., 2013; Zozomová‐Lihová et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2020). In addition to this well‐studied example from the Alps, several older allopolyploids have also been identified and studied in the genus (Lihová et al., 2006; Mandáková et al., 2014, 2019; Šlenker et al., 2021; Kantor et al., 2023). However, cases of spontaneous interspecific hybridization are relatively rare and geographically restricted (Marhold et al., 2002; Lihová et al., 2006, 2007), suggesting that the reproductive barriers are strong (also experimentally proven by Urbanska‐Worytkiewicz, 1977) and are broken only exceptionally.

One of the recently reported cases of interspecific hybridization concerns a triploid Cardamine × rhodopea Ančev, found in the West Rhodope Mts. in Bulgaria, putatively arising from hybridization between two diploids, C. matthioli Moretti and C. rivularis Schur (Ančev et al., 2013). Our pilot studies showed a large variation in genome size, which not only supported the presence of a diploid–triploid hybrid zone (Melichárková et al., 2020) but also indicated more complex patterns of interspecific gene flow than hypothesized by Ančev et al. (2013), for the following reasons. Cardamine rivularis commonly forms autotriploids in otherwise diploid populations (Marhold, 1994; Melichárková et al., 2020), suggesting that the triploids detected in the contact zones (Ančev et al., 2013) could be of both autopolyploid and hybrid ancestry. Furthermore, traces of introgression between C. rivularis and a related Balkan species, C. acris Griseb., were recently detected (Šlenker et al., 2024), and a sympatric occurrence of another taxon, C. amara subsp. balcanica Ančev, Marhold et Kit Tan (Marhold et al., 1996), has also been discovered in the West Rhodope Mts. (unpublished data). Thus, up to four taxa occur in sympatry or in spatial proximity to each other in the Bulgarian mountains, increasing the potential for interspecific gene flow.

Here, we conducted a population genetic analysis in the mountains of western Bulgaria to address some of the knowledge gaps outlined above, particularly in relation to the identification of key traits and factors leading to the successful formation and survival of hybrids and polyploids, as well as their predictability and repeatability. Using nuclear DNA content data, genome‐wide single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from restriction site‐associated DNA sequencing (RADseq), genomic in situ hybridization (GISH), and pollen quality estimates, we aimed to investigate the spatial extent, frequency, direction, and outcome of interspecific gene flow and infer its evolutionary potential. We hypothesize that hybrid formation and introgression have occurred repeatedly here and involved multiple species, resulting in variable offspring on which natural selection can act. We also investigate autotriploid formation in C. rivularis within and outside the hybrid zones. By analyzing spatial cytotype and genetic structure, we test whether autotriploids reflect rare events of unreduced gamete formation that spread and persist through clonal growth, or frequent recurrent origins that maintain ploidy mixtures within populations. Here, we have identified the dynamic interplay of environmental and intrinsic factors that promote repeated hybridization and parallel neopolyploid formation. This study adds to the growing support of interspecific gene flow as a creative force that contributes significantly to biological diversity and opens up a new natural model system for future research on polyploid evolution.

Materials and Methods

Study species

Cardamine matthioli Moretti and C. rivularis Schur are closely related diploid species belonging to the same species complex of C. pratensis (Marhold et al., 2018; Melichárková et al., 2020), which diversified around the onset of the Pleistocene (Kantor et al., 2023). They have a parapatric distribution and grow in similar damp habitats, but in different bioclimatic niches with little overlap. While C. matthioli is widespread in central and southeastern Europe and occurs mainly in the lowland to montane zone, C. rivularis grows in the subalpine to alpine zone of the Southern Carpathians and the Bulgarian mountains (Melichárková et al., 2020). Despite the bioclimatic and altitudinal divergence of these two species, they currently co‐occur in the West Rhodope Mts. in Bulgaria at altitudes of 1200–1800 m, supposedly due to human interference (Ančev et al., 2013). Cardamine acris Griseb. is a diploid–polyploid polymorphic species distributed in the montane to subalpine belt in several mountains of the Balkan Peninsula and consists of several allopatric phylogeographic lineages, of which the Eastern Balkan lineage (comprising only diploids) extends to Bulgaria (see Šlenker et al., 2024). Despite its range overlap with C. rivularis and traces of introgression (Šlenker et al., 2024), C. acris has not yet been recorded from the same sites as C. rivularis. Finally, C. amara L. is a widespread and phylogenetically more distant diploid–tetraploid species, represented in the Balkan Peninsula by the diploid C. amara subsp. balcanica Ančev, Marhold et Kit Tan, which grows in the upper montane to subalpine zone of western Bulgaria and northeastern Greece (Marhold et al., 1996) and probably extends into southern Serbia and North Macedonia (Šlenker et al., 2021). Its occurrence at the sites in the West Rhodope Mts., from where C. × rhodopea Ančev was reported (Ančev et al., 2013), was discovered during the present study. Importantly for this study, all of these taxa are rhizomatous and outcrossing perennials (mixed mating has only been reported for C. amara) that can reproduce vegetatively via stolons, rhizomes, or leaf vivipary, and are confined to similar types of wetland habitats such as wet meadows, pastures, stream banks, springs, and bogs (Lövkvist, 1956; Salisbury, 1965; Tedder et al., 2015; Marhold et al., 2018). Despite the same diploid chromosome number (2n = 16), they differ in genome size. Cardamine amara has the smallest genome (2C = 0.45–0.52 pg), while C. rivularis and C. acris both have a genome about twice as large (2C = 1.13–1.20 pg), and C. matthioli shows intermediate values (2C = 0.69–0.97 pg; Melichárková et al., 2020; Šlenker et al., 2021, 2024).

Plant sampling

Here, we sampled and analyzed four Cardamine species (C. matthioli, C. rivularis, C. acris, and C. amara, represented by both subsp. amara and subsp. balcanica) from multiple localities within and outside their contact zones, as described in more detail below and listed in Supporting Information Table S1. We discovered and sampled seven localities in western Bulgaria with the co‐occurrence of two to three taxa, C. matthioli, C. rivularis, and C. amara subsp. balcanica, hereafter referred to as hybrid zones (Fig. 1; Table 1). One hybrid zone (ZEL) was found in the Vitosha Mts. along the Selska reka stream, another hybrid zone was located in the pass between the Rila Mts. and Rhodope Mts. (YUN), and five zones were in the West Rhodope Mts., found along three different rivers, the Dospat river (BIT, POB, 3 km apart, separated by the settlement of Pobit Kamat), the Ribna river (KAT), and the Semiza river (JEB, BEG, 4.3 km apart). Sampling was conducted along the streams, in the adjacent wet meadows, bogs, and springs, resulting in 22–88 individuals per hybrid zone (Fig. 1b), with the exact spatial position determined using the Emlid Reach RS2 GNSS receiver and the Emlid Flow application (Emlid Tech Ltd, Budapest, Hungary). Horizontal accuracy of positioning was up to 5 cm in localities with RTK (Real‐Time Kinematic) correction (provided by ROMPOS® and SOLITECH AD, Sofia) and up to 50 cm in localities without internet connection, where only a satellite‐based positioning system was available. The exact positions of the sampled individuals were recorded to assess the frequency of hybrid and parental individuals and their spatial patterns. Phenotypically, only C. amara subsp. balcanica could be identified with certainty in the field, while the distinction between C. matthioli, C. rivularis, and hybrids appeared problematic. In similar spatial detail, we also sampled eight populations of C. rivularis outside the contact zones (Figs 1a, S1; Table S1): at five localities in the Southern Carpathians, Romania (Făgăraş, Lotru, Cindrel Mts.) and at three localities in the Rila and Vitosha Mts. (Bulgaria), sampling 21–41 individuals per population. In addition, we sampled 14 populations of pure C. matthioli throughout its range (from Switzerland to Greece; see Melichárková et al., 2020), six populations of C. amara subsp. balcanica in Bulgaria (Marhold et al., 1996) outside the contact zones, and two populations of C. amara subsp. amara in Romania (Table S1).

Silica gel‐dried leaf samples were collected and processed for flow cytometric measurements of DNA content and isolation of genomic DNA for RADseq. They represented a total of 359 individuals from the hybrid zones and 224 individuals of C. rivularis outside the contact zones (Table S1). Flower buds of selected individuals (3–25 per hybrid zone, 85 in total, representing different phenotypes) were fixed in an ethanol : acetic acid (3 : 1) mixture and stored in 70% ethanol at −20°C for further chromosome counting and GISH analyses. Herbarium specimens of selected flowering individuals (6–24 per hybrid zone, 97 in total, representing different phenotypes; supplemented by 35 individuals from pure populations outside the contact zones, Table S1) were made and preserved for pollen quality assessments.

In addition, RADseq data from five Bulgarian populations of C. acris (the Eastern Balkan intraspecific lineage) were obtained from Šlenker et al. (2024). Finally, the dataset was supplemented by five samples of C. serbica Pančić (Lazarević et al., unpublished), which were used as an outgroup in phylogenetic tree reconstructions. Voucher specimens of each population analyzed in the present study are deposited in the herbarium SAV.

Nuclear DNA content measurements and chromosome counting

Relative nuclear DNA content was estimated by flow cytometry (Doležel et al., 2007) using the CyFlow Space instrument (Partec, Münster, Germany) with a UV‐LED excitation source. Silica gel‐dried leaf samples were co‐processed with leaf tissue of Solanum lycopersicum ‘Stupické polní rané’ (2C = 1.96 pg; Doležel et al., 1992) as an internal standard using Otto buffers and DAPI fluorochrome (Doležel et al., 2007) following a protocol described in detail in Methods S1. Relative nuclear DNA content (2C value, expressed in arbitrary units; a.u.) was determined based on the ratio of G0/G1 peak positions between the sample and the internal standard.

To determine the relationship between relative nuclear DNA content and ploidy levels, chromosome counting was conducted in selected cytometrically analyzed individuals originating from contact zones (see Fig. S2). Mitotic chromosome counts were performed in 85 individuals using young anthers of flower buds collected and fixed in the field. Chromosome spreads were prepared following Mandáková & Lysak (2016), stained with DAPI (2 mg ml–1) in Vectashield antifade, and analyzed under a Zeiss Axioimager Z2 epifluorescence microscope equipped with a CoolCube camera (MetaSystems). Fluorescence signals were captured for further evaluation.

RADseq data generation and processing

Total genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from silica gel‐dried leaves using the Exgene Plant SV Kit (GeneAll, Seoul, Korea) and purified with 0.4× AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The double‐digest RADseq libraries were prepared according to the protocol of Peterson et al. (2012) with some modifications. Digestion of gDNA was performed with HpyCH4V (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), followed by A‐tailing and adaptor ligation with a 5‐bp barcode. The pooled ligation products were size‐selected and PCR‐amplified using the universal P5 primer and the indexed P7 primer with 6‐bp indices. The detailed protocol is described in Methods S2. Libraries were sequenced with 150‐bp paired‐end reads on Illumina systems at Novogene, Cambridge, UK.

Demultiplexed, quality‐filtered, and deduplicated Illumina sequencing reads were mapped to the genome and plastid of Cardamine amara (NCBI accession nos. GCA_040955855.1, KY562580.1) using BWA 0.7.5a (Li, 2013), and the resulting BAM files were processed with Picard Toolkit 2.22.1 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Variant calling was performed in GATK 4.4.0.0 (McKenna et al., 2010), capturing biallelic SNPs with no > 20% missing genotypes. Samples with missing data above 50% were discarded. Unlinked RADseq loci were then detected using the identifiRadLoci.workflow (Šlenker, 2024) to allow selection of a single random unlinked SNP required for certain analyses (see below). Further details on data processing can be found in Methods S3.

Evidence of interspecific gene flow, identification of hybrids, and their genomic composition

To test for the presence of interspecific gene flow, we employed several approaches that assess the overall genetic structure within the data and can distinguish between the pure parental individuals and the genetically admixed individuals. These included a Bayesian clustering approach implemented in STRUCTURE v.2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000), a NeighborNet constructed in SplitsTree4 (Huson & Bryant, 2006) based on Nei's genetic distances (Nei, 1972) calculated in the R package StAMPP (Pembleton et al., 2013) using R 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2024), and principal component analysis (PCA) based on the covariance matrix, implemented in the R package adegenet (Jombart, 2008; Jombart & Ahmed, 2011). The STRUCTURE analyzed 100 datasets, each containing a single randomly selected SNP from RADseq loci with at least six SNPs. Details of the STRUCTURE analysis can be found in Methods S3. Next, to verify and visualize the different genomic compositions of the detected hybrids, we calculated the pairwise relatedness coefficient between the four parental species from pure populations and the identified hybrid individuals from each hybrid zone, using the method‐of‐moments approach with Polyrelatedness (Huang et al., 2014). The distribution of pairwise relatedness values was depicted as violin plots using the R package vioplot (Adler et al., 2022).

Introgression tests

As a measure of genome‐wide admixture, we calculated a hybrid index (Buerkle, 2005) and interspecific heterozygosity for each individual in hybrid zones based on parental taxa from genetically pure populations. Hybrids with different parental genome combinations were analyzed separately. Hybrid indices ranged from zero to one, representing pure individuals of each parental lineage, with C. rivularis set as parent 0 and the other parental taxa set as 1. genodive v.3.06 (Meirmans, 2020), which accepts polyploid genotypes, was used to calculate the hybrid index using all SNPs as input. The R package Introgress (Gompert & Buerkle, 2010) estimated the maximum likelihood (ML) of the hybrid index and interspecific heterozygosity, defined as the proportion of an individual's genome containing alleles inherited from both parental populations. Unlike GenoDive, Introgress was run on a subset of SNPs that differed between the parental populations, and the triploid genotypes were converted into the diploid ones (preserving homozygote and heterozygote genotypes).

Furthermore, we examined patterns of differential introgression using Bayesian estimation of genomic clines (BGC) (Gompert & Buerkle, 2012). We aimed to identify loci with excess ancestry that deviated significantly from the genome‐wide average introgression based on estimates of two cline parameters, α i (designating an increase or decrease in the probability of ancestry from species 1 for locus i) or β i (an increase or decrease in the rate of transition from a low to high probability of ancestry from species 1), indicating genetic drift or selection.

Finally, we used the ABBA–BABA test, which relies on Patterson's D statistics (Durand et al., 2011), to investigate the presence of introgression between the hybrids and the parental species within the hybrid zones. Allopatric pure populations of the parental species, that is, outside the hybrid zone, were used as the nonintrogressed sister lineage (P1), while the parental individuals and hybrids from the hybrid zone were used as P2 and P3, respectively. If one of the parental species P2 was not present in the given hybrid zone, the geographically closest population was used instead (see Table 2). The block‐jackknife method was used to test for significant deviation from the null expectation of D = 0. The computations were run using the scripts written by Simon Martin, available at https://github.com/simonhmartin/tutorials/tree/master/ABBA_BABA_whole_genome.

Directionality of interspecific gene flow: Identification of maternal donors based on plastome variation

To infer the maternal progenitors of the hybrid individuals, SNPs localized on the plastid genome (KY562580.1) were extracted and analyzed separately. A ML tree was constructed in RAxML‐NG v.0.9.0 (Kozlov et al., 2019) with inputs in PHYLIP format. Lewis' method for ascertainment bias correction (Lewis, 2001) was applied. The VCF file was converted to PHYLIP format using the vcf2phylip.py script (Ortiz, 2019), and invariant sites were removed using the ascbias.py script (available at https://github.com/btmartin721/raxml_ascbias), following the recommendations of Leaché et al. (2015).

Clonality assessment

Since all parental species can reproduce vegetatively (see ‘Study species’ in the Materials and Methods section), we investigated the role that clonal growth may play in the persistence of hybrids as well as autotriploids. To assess clonality in both the hybrid zones and pure C. rivularis populations, we calculated Nei's genetic distances (Nei, 1972) from a VCF file, which was further filtered to retain only variants present in at least 95% of individuals with a minimum sequencing depth of 10×. The frequency distributions of Nei's pairwise genetic distances were then examined for the total dataset and each hybrid zone/population separately to determine a genet assignment threshold following the approach proposed by Tsujimoto et al. (2020). According to this approach, the first peak in the frequency distribution of Nei's pairwise genetic distances indicates somatic mutations and genotypic errors within genets (genetically identical individuals resulting from clonal reproduction), followed by distances representing variation between the genets. In addition, we calculated pairwise distances for five replicated samples (not included in the main VCF file) and plotted them on the frequency distribution graph to examine their positions with respect to the inferred genet assignment threshold.

Genomic in situ hybridization

GISH was performed in seven triploid individuals (2n = 24), one per hybrid zone (Table 1), representing different hybrid types according to the RADseq results (see the Results section), to determine their parental chromosome composition. Our previous study (Šlenker et al., 2021) showed that GISH is ineffective for distinguishing chromosomes of the closely related C. rivularis, C. matthioli, and C. acris, which diverged in the Pliocene, but is able to discriminate the chromosomes of the phylogenetically more distant C. amara (see Fig. 1a). Therefore, the present GISH analyses of the hybrids aimed to detect a subgenome(s) contributed by C. amara. The GISH procedure followed the protocol described in detail in Šlenker et al. (2021), using the total genomic DNA (gDNA) of diploid C. amara and C. rivularis auct. as probes (accessions from Mandáková et al., 2013). After immunodetection, chromosomes were counterstained with DAPI (2 mg ml–1) in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss AxioImager Z2 epifluorescence microscope equipped with a CoolCube camera (MetaSystems, Altlussheim, Germany). Individual fluorochrome images were captured using specific excitation/emission filters (AHF Analysentechnik, Tübingen, Germany), pseudocolored, merged, and processed in Photoshop CS (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA).

Pollen viability and pollen size assessments

To evaluate the potential fertility of the genetically identified hybrids, we estimated pollen viability and viable pollen grain size in 132 plants representing the parental taxa (both from pure populations and hybrid zones) and different hybrid types as determined by RADseq and GISH (see Tables 1, S1). This was done using a modified Alexander's stain, following the protocol of Peterson et al. (2010). All statistical computations and plottings were performed in R 4.1.1 (R Core Team, 2021). To test for differences in pollen viability and pollen grain size between the parents and hybrids, we used a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM; Bolker et al., 2009) and post hoc comparisons based on estimated marginal means with Tukey correction for multiple testing. The R packages glmmtmb (Brooks et al., 2017), DHARMa (Hartig, 2022) and emmeans (Lenth, 2024) were used. Details are given in Methods S4.

Results

Chromosome numbers and nuclear DNA content

Chromosome counting showed the presence of both diploid (2n = 16) and triploid (2n = 24) individuals in all seven hybrid zones (Tables 1, S2). Flow cytometric analysis revealed substantial variation in nuclear DNA content, ranging 2C = 0.26–0.85 (sample/standard ratio), with several discrete genome size classes within each hybrid zone (Figs 2, S2). Individuals of C. amara subsp. balcanica had the smallest genome (mean 2C = 0.27 ± 0.006, n = 22), followed by C. matthioli (mean 2C = 0.44 ± 0.014, n = 126), while C. rivularis had the largest genome (diploids: mean 2C = 0.56 ± 0.009, n = 32; autotriploids proved by RADseq: mean 2C = 0.82 ± 0.014, n = 29). The vast majority of individuals identified as hybrids by RADseq data (see below) were triploids with a mean 2C = 0.65–0.85 (n = 147; Figs 2, S2). Only two individuals (from two hybrid zones, YUN and ZEL) were identified as diploid hybrids, both with 2C = 0.57 (Fig. 2).

RADseq dataset

Sequencing yielded between 750 074 and 35 760 470 paired reads per sample, with an average of 5 127 491.94 reads retained after quality control and deduplication. Of these, 57.54%–93.85% were successfully mapped to the reference genome, resulting in an average mapping rate of 80.57%. The final VCF file of 610 individuals comprised 212 215 SNPs, distributed across 11 518 presumably unlinked genomic regions.

Evidence of interspecific gene flow, identification of hybrids, and their genomic composition

Bayesian clustering (STRUCTURE) based on SNP variation resulted in four clusters matching the four analyzed species, C. matthioli, C. rivularis, C. amara, and C. acris, sampled from the populations outside the hybrid zones (Fig. 3a). Within the hybrid zones, we identified genetically pure individuals corresponding to C. matthioli (in all hybrid zones except BEG), C. rivularis (in all hybrid zones except ZEL), and C. amara (in four hybrid zones from the West Rhodope Mts., BEG, JEB, BIT, and POB), as well as genetically admixed individuals found in each hybrid zone. The Bayesian clustering patterns were consistent with the results of NeighborNet (Fig. S3). Genetically pure individuals could be clearly distinguished from the hybrids, as the former had an ancestry proportion to one of the Bayesian clusters greater than 0.9, and their NeighborNet splits were positioned together with those of the respective pure parental populations. By contrast, the hybrid samples showed an ancestry proportion to any of the Bayesian clusters of < 0.71, and their NeighborNet splits appeared in conflicting positions. These clustering patterns were also supported by PCA ordination diagrams (Fig. 3b) and the distribution of pairwise relatedness values (PolyRelatedness; Fig. S4), suggesting the presence of four different hybrid types (Fig. 4d) and up to two hybrid types per hybrid zone.

The hybrids C. rivularis × C. amara subsp. balcanica were recorded in all five hybrid zones from the West Rhodope Mts. (i.e. BEG, JEB, KAT, BIT, and POB), while C. rivularis × C. matthioli were found in three of them (BEG, JEB, and KAT) as well as in YUN. The admixture proportion in the STRUCTURE analysis, supported by PCA plots (Fig. 3), indicates that both hybrid types from the West Rhodope Mts. are genetically closer to C. rivularis, suggesting the RRM (two rivularis‐derived subgenomes and one matthioli‐derived subgenome) and RRAb (two rivularis‐derived subgenomes and one amara subsp. balcanica‐derived subgenome) genomic compositions, the latter confirmed by GISH analyses (see below). At the Yundola site (YUN), located between the Rila and West Rhodope Mts., variable admixture proportions in STRUCTURE and the broader PCA ordination space occupied by the hybrids (Fig. 3) indicate the presence of both RRM and RMM hybrids, and some intermediate forms, including a single diploid RM hybrid individual (Fig. 2). Finally, at the foothills of the Vitosha Mts. (ZEL), we found diploid C. matthioli together with the C. rivularis × C. acris hybrids (both diploid RAc and triploid RRAc comprising two rivularis‐ and one acris‐derived subgenomes) and trigenomic triploid hybrids, C. rivularis × C. matthioli × C. acris (RMAc; Figs 2, 3, S4).

Introgression tests

The hybrid index values calculated in the hybrid zones demonstrated the presence of genetically pure parental individuals (h < 0.02 for C. rivularis, h > 0.94 for C. matthioli, and h > 0.99 for C. amara) and hybrids that exhibited a relatively narrow range of values in all hybrid zones (with the exception of YUN, see below): h = 0.30–0.48 (computed in GenoDive) and h = 0.40–0.46 (computed in Introgress). The vast majority of the hybrids showed high interspecific heterozygosity: 85% of RRM and 93% of RRAb hybrids exhibited the heterozygosity values above 0.8, and about two‐thirds of RRM and half of RRAb hybrids had the values above 0.85 (Fig. 4a; Table S2). These values of hybrid indices and interspecific heterozygosity suggest the presence of early‐generation (predominantly F1) hybrids. However, decreased interspecific heterozygosity was observed in some hybrids in the JEB (minimum 0.74) and ZEL (minimum 0.72) zones. In the Yundola (YUN) hybrid zone, hybrids showed greater variation in hybrid index values (h = 0.32–0.69 in GenoDive, h = 0.39–0.63 in Introgress) and were associated with lower heterozygosity values (with a minimum of 0.62; Table S2).

In BGC, we found loci with α excess ancestry that differed significantly from the genome‐wide average introgression in each hybrid type, suggesting genetic drift or selection (Fig. S5; Table S3). They were found in both directions, toward C. rivularis (detected at 4.11–8.48% of the loci, depending on the hybrid type) and toward the other parental species (found at 2.44–8.54% of the loci). We also identified α outlier loci with extreme introgression in each hybrid type, predominantly toward C. rivularis (detected at 2.40–17.48% of the loci), suggesting directional selection. For the β parameter expressing introgression rate, we found negative β excess ancestry as well as outliers in the RRM/RMM and RRAb hybrids (up to 11.91% of the loci in RRAb), indicating hybrid vigor (faster introgression than expected).

In the ABBA–BABA introgression tests (Table 2), we obtained significantly positive D statistics, indicating introgression between hybrids and sympatric parental individuals in all hybrid zones, although not for every hybrid–parent combination. While introgression with sympatric C. rivularis was detected in every hybrid type and hybrid zone, introgression with C. matthioli was nonsignificant or absent in three hybrid zones (YUN, KAT, and BEG), and that with C. amara subsp. balcanica was nonsignificant in two hybrid zones (KAT and JEB).

Directionality of interspecific gene flow: Identification of maternal donors based on plastome variation

The ML tree inferred from the plastome SNP data (1295 SNPs obtained after applying ascertainment bias correction) showed several species‐specific clades (Fig. S6). Individuals from the hybrid zones that were identified as parental ones based on STRUCTURE assignments and hybrid indices were placed in the clades together with conspecific individuals originating from pure populations. All triploid hybrids from the West Rhodope Mts. (BEG, JEB, KAT, BIT, and POB) were placed in a single clade together with C. rivularis. This pattern strongly suggests that C. rivularis served as the maternal parent for all of these triploid hybrids. By contrast, the hybrids from the YUN zone were placed in two distinct clades that comprised C. rivularis and C. matthioli accessions, respectively, indicating bidirectional crossing events. Finally, the hybrids from the ZEL zone were placed in a clade that included both C. acris and accessions of C. rivularis from the Vitosha Mts. (locality ALE), leaving the maternal contribution unresolved.

Abundance of parental vs hybrid individuals and spatial patterns in the hybrid zones

The hybrid zones differed both in the frequency of parental and hybrid individuals and in the spatial patterns (Fig. 1b). In most localities, parental and hybrid individuals were spatially intermingled, but patches with exclusively hybrid or parental individuals were also found, which was particularly true for C. matthioli. Cardamine matthioli mostly grew in wet meadows and damp sites adjacent to, but not invading, sites with running water, where C. rivularis, C. amara, and hybrids were more common. This pattern was particularly pronounced at two localities, POB and JEB (Fig. 1b). Hybrids were abundant at most localities, with their frequencies either matching (POB, KAT, JEB, and BEG) or exceeding (YUN and BIT) those of the parents. In some localities, one of the parental species was absent or found in very low numbers, although hybrids bearing their genomes were common (Table 1). The most striking case is the locality ZEL, where several C. rivularis × C. acris hybrids were found in the absence of both parental species. A similar pattern was also observed at the localities YUN, BIT (rare occurrence of C. rivularis), BEG (absence of C. matthioli), and at the localities POB, KAT, and BEG (rare occurrence or absence of C. amara).

Autotriploids of Cardamine rivularis, their abundance, and spatial patterns

The RADseq data confirmed the autopolyploid and in situ origin of the triploids of C. rivularis in the populations from the Southern Carpathians, Romania (Fig. S3). The triploids were rare in three populations, with only one or two individuals among the 21–32 individuals sampled per locality. However, in two populations in the Făgăraş Mts., triploids outnumbered diploids and grew in several sites along different streams or a lakeshore, while diploids formed a small patch only in a single stream (Fig. S1). In the Bulgarian distribution area, autotriploids of C. rivularis were not detected in pure populations despite dense sampling. However, they were found in three hybrid zones in the West Rhodope Mts. (JEB, BEG, and KAT), where both RRAb and RRM hybrids co‐occur (Fig. 1b; Tables 1, S2). These autotriploids clustered with the pure diploids of the respective populations and thus can be clearly distinguished from triploid hybrids (Fig. S3). The autotriploids were abundant and spatially widespread at these three localities (JEB, BEG, and KAT) and even outnumbered the diploids of C. rivularis at one locality (KAT; Fig. 1b; Table 1).

Clonality assessment

In the frequency distribution plot of Nei's pairwise genetic distances for the total dataset, we observed the first peak around the distance value of 0.003, which decreased to a value of 0.005 and reached stable low frequencies until 0.01, followed by a steady increase (Fig. S7). Importantly, the pairwise distances between five replicated samples were below 0.005. We assume that the genet assignment threshold lies between the distance values of 0.005 and 0.01, but since we did not observe a clear gap, the threshold could not be determined with certainty. When analyzing pairwise distances within the hybrid zones, we observed gaps either c. 0.005 or between 0.005 and 0.01 (with the exception of BIT, see below). Regardless of whether the 0.005 or 0.01 threshold is applied, C. amara, triploid C. rivularis, the RRAb, and RRAc hybrids appear to be highly clonal, as most individuals at the locality could be assigned to a single or only a few genets (Fig. S7). By contrast, less clonality was found in the RRM hybrids, and only exceptionally in C. matthioli (ZEL and POB) or diploid C. rivularis (POB). In the BIT hybrid zone, the frequency distributions of genetic distances showed no gap up to values of 0.025, indicating high similarities between (putatively sibling) individuals, extending beyond the genet assignment threshold. In the pure populations of C. rivularis, almost no genets (at a threshold of 0.005) were observed outside the Făgăraş Mts. By contrast, high clonality was inferred in both diploids and triploids in the ploidy‐mixed populations in the Făgăraş Mts. (Fig. S7).

Genomic in situ hybridization

DAPI staining of the mitotic chromosomes in the triploid hybrids revealed two distinct karyotypes. One consisted of 24 chromosomes of similar length, characterized by extensive pericentromeric heterochromatin (denoted as L chromosomes), a feature previously observed in C. acris, C. matthioli, and C. rivularis (Mandáková et al., 2013; Šlenker et al., 2021). This 24 L karyotype was confirmed in the RRM and RRAc hybrids, as determined by RADseq. By contrast, the second karyotype, found in the RRAb hybrids, comprised 16 L chromosomes and 8 smaller (S) chromosomes. The gDNA probe of C. amara hybridized specifically to 8 S chromosomes in the RRAb hybrids but did not bind to any chromosomes in the RRM or RRAc hybrids (Table S2; Fig. 2b). By contrast, the gDNA probe of C. rivularis hybridized to 16 L chromosomes in RRAb hybrids and to all 24 L chromosomes in RRM and RRAc hybrids (Table S2; Fig. 2b). Thus, GISH confirmed the presence of one C. amara‐derived subgenome (eight chromosomes) in the RRAb hybrids and disproved it in the RRAc and RRM hybrids.

Pollen viability and pollen size assessments

We found significant differences in pollen viability between the diploid parental individuals and the hybrids (Fig. 5, Tables S4, S5). The diploid parental species showed a high proportion of viable pollen (median above 90%, only in C. acris slightly lower with a median of 70.7%), while all hybrid combinations had a strongly reduced viability (median values in RRAb – 19.7%, RRAc – 12.1%, RRM/RMM – 9.16%, RMAc – 0%). In autotriploid C. rivularis, we observed decreased viability, but with more than half of the pollen grains being viable (median = 51.6%). Regarding pollen grain size (Fig. S8; Tables S6, S7), the diploid parental species produced viable pollen of homogeneous size corresponding to their species‐specific genome size (Šlenker et al., 2021 and this study; Fig. 2), with no evidence of unreduced male gamete formation at detectable rates. By contrast, the histogram of viable pollen grains in triploid C. rivularis showed a bimodal distribution. Pollen size in hybrids was more heterogeneous, but these results are based on a limited number of viable pollen grains (Fig. S8).

Discussion

Hybrid zones as a window to the evolutionary success of allopolyploidy in Cardamine

Despite sympatric distribution patterns of several Cardamine species adapted to similar habitats, spontaneous interspecific hybridization is relatively rare and geographically limited (Marhold et al., 2002; Lihová et al., 2006, 2007; Zozomová‐Lihová et al., 2014). Nevertheless, a high number of allopolyploid endemics recognized in the genus underline the evolutionary significance of historical hybridization events that have led to stabilized lineages over different time periods (Mandáková et al., 2014, 2019; Marhold et al., 2018; Šlenker et al., 2021; Kantor et al., 2023). In this context, the hybrid zones discovered and studied here represent an unprecedented case and offer unique insights into how allopolyploidy has become a major evolutionary force in this genus.

In five hybrid zones in the West Rhodope Mts., found at the Dospat, Ribna, and Semiza rivers (BEG, JEB, POB, BIT, and KAT; Table 1; Fig. 1), we observed similar hybridization patterns in terms of the presence of two to three parental species (C. rivularis, C. matthioli, and C. amara subsp. balcanica) and two types of triploid hybrids (RRM and RRAb; Fig. 4d). Although frequencies and spatial patterns varied among the localities (Fig. 1b), all these hybrids were inferred to represent early generations (Fig. 4a–b), with C. rivularis acting as the maternal parent (Fig. S6). The clustering patterns (Fig. S3) supported multiple, polytopic origins, consistent with observations in other allopolyploids (Soltis & Soltis, 2009). Similar multiple‐origin scenarios have also been demonstrated for the triploid Mimulus × robertsii (Vallejo‐Marín & Lye, 2013) and the well‐studied neopolyploids Tragopogon mirus, T. miscellus (Soltis et al., 1995, 2004a; Symonds et al., 2010), Mimulus peregrinus (Vallejo‐Marín et al., 2017), as well as in Salsola ryanii (Welles & Ellstrand, 2016). The unidirectional parentage of the RRM and RRAb hybrids likely reflects asymmetric production of triploids, with greater success recorded for ‘maternal‐excess’ crosses (Köhler et al., 2010; Vallejo‐Marín et al., 2016), which here corresponds to the involvement of unreduced female gametes of C. rivularis (see also below).

Evidence of local triploid–diploid introgression (Table 2) and some degree of male fertility in the hybrids (Fig. 5) suggest occasional cases of hybrid mating and backcrossing, but they persist and spread mainly by clonal growth (Fig. S7; Table S2). All three parental taxa can reproduce vegetatively, which is particularly pronounced in C. amara via stolons (Krásná, 2008; Tedder et al., 2015; Zozomová‐Lihová et al., 2015; Bartolić et al., 2025), as also shown here by clone estimates (Fig. S7). The high clonality of the hybrids, especially the RRAb ones (Fig. S7), is likely a trait inherited from the parents and a key factor for the persistence of the hybrids, similar to triploid Cardamine × insueta in the Alps (Sun et al., 2020). Persistence of viable but largely sterile hybrids through vegetative means has also been reported for other hybrids (Vallejo‐Marín & Hiscock, 2016), including the triploids Spartina × townsendii (Ainouche et al., 2003; Huska et al., 2016) and Mimulus × robertsii (Vallejo‐Marín & Lye, 2013).

In contrast to the sites in the West Rhodope Mts., greater variation of C. rivularis × C. matthioli hybrids was found at the Yundola site, where C. amara and the hybrids comprising its genome were absent. Here, we observed bidirectional crosses, both RRM and RMM hybrids, representing predominantly F1 but likely also some later‐generation or backcrossed hybrids, as well as a diploid RM hybrid (Figs 2, 3, 4). Repeated origins with reciprocal maternal parentage can lead to significant variation, as shown by the example of Tragopogon miscellus, which exhibits striking differences in floral morphology, possibly resulting in reproductive isolation and cryptic speciation (Soltis et al., 1995; Soltis & Soltis, 2009).

Finally, the Zheleznitsa site in the Vitosha Mts. represents a peculiar case of the co‐occurrence of C. matthioli with the hybrids comprising the genomes of C. rivularis and C. acris (RAc and RRAc) as well as trigenomic hybrids with an additional genome of C. matthioli (RMAc; Figs 1b, 4d). The RAc and RRAc hybrids growing there in the absence of their parental species may reflect historical sympatry and gene flow. While C. rivularis occurs in the upper parts of this mountain range c. 600 m above, see Table S1, C. acris has not been recorded there; the closest known localities are in the Stara Planina and Rila Mts. (Perný et al., 2004; Šlenker et al., 2024). Nevertheless, habitat suitability modeling of C. acris suggested that this species may have been more widespread in central and western Bulgaria, including the Vitosha range, in the recent past – during the mid‐Holocene (8300–4200 yr ago) and especially during the Last Glacial Maximum (Šlenker et al., 2024). Under these circumstances, the high interspecific heterozygosity (0.72–0.83) and a narrow hybrid index range of the RAc and RRAc hybrids, indicative of early‐generation hybrids, imply their long‐term persistence with only limited sexual reproduction. Still, the presence of sterile trigenomic RMAc hybrids and our results on pollen quality analyses indicate that RAc/RRAc hybrids occasionally produce viable gametes (Fig. 5). A notable example of the evolutionary success of an exclusively clonally reproducing species is the dodecaploid Cardamine bulbifera, which is thought to have originated in the Caucasian region and to have spread vegetatively throughout Europe during the post‐glacial period, preserving the genetic diversity of its early polyploid and reticulate evolutionary history (Ru et al., 2022). Similarly, the sterile triploid Mimulus × robertsii has spread vegetatively across the British Isles since its origin c. 150 yr ago, where it forms large, naturalized populations in the absence of the parental species (Vallejo‐Marín & Lye, 2013).

The formation of the studied hybrid zones was likely triggered by environmental disturbances and human activities (Ančev et al., 2013), which are known to promote hybridization (Soltis & Soltis, 2009), and also played an important role in the well‐studied natural neopolyploid systems (Vallejo‐Marín & Hiscock, 2016; Edger et al., 2025). Global warming combined with anthropogenic activities that disturb habitats or create dispersal corridors has the potential to alter species distributions (e.g. through latitudinal and altitudinal shifts) faster than ever before, especially in mountain ecosystems (Hlásny et al., 2016; Körner, 2021). In the West Rhodope Mts., intensified deforestation, livestock farming, grazing, and crop cultivation have been ongoing for the past 2000 yr (Marinova et al., 2023). These climatic and vegetational changes may have shifted Cardamine species (adapted to different niches defined by water and light availability and altitudinal range; Ančev et al., 2013; Melichárková et al., 2020; our personal observations) into spatial proximity, thereby facilitating interspecific gene flow. Moreover, dynamic riparian and wetland habitats, characterized by periodic disturbances and spatially and year‐round fluctuating water levels, provide a mosaic of niches that can be exploited by hybrids. These conditions also enhance the dispersal of vegetative propagules, promoting the spread of hybrids, as has been proposed for Mimulus neopolyploids (Vallejo‐Marín & Hiscock, 2016).

The central role of Cardamine rivularis and its unreduced female gametes

Even though four different parental species were involved in the hybridization events investigated here, not all possible hybrid combinations were recorded (Fig. 4d). All the hybrids we found contained at least one genome of C. rivularis, but predominantly two, and the majority had C. rivularis as the maternal parent. Thus, it appears that unreduced female gametes of C. rivularis play a pivotal role in the ongoing hybridization. The union of the unreduced female gamete of C. rivularis with the reduced pollen from co‐occurring C. matthioli or C. amara likely explains the formation of the triploid hybrids. This scenario is consistent with previous observations that unreduced eggs are more effective in triploid formation than unreduced pollen, probably related to more favorable embryo : endosperm and maternal : paternal endosperm ploidy ratios, necessary for the viable embryo development, than in reciprocal crosses (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998; Köhler et al., 2021; Morgan et al., 2021). Experimental crosses in polyploid Achillea borealis have also indicated that, despite the high production of unreduced pollen, unreduced eggs rather than pollen promote the neopolyploid formation (Ramsey, 2007). Recent estimates suggest that the frequencies of unreduced gametes in natural populations are typically low (0.1–2% on average, although mainly assessed from the screening of male gametes) and are probably maintained in a dynamic balance between the mutation rate and the selection forces acting against them, but can also be considerably increased in a small number of individuals, reaching up to 82.6% (Kreiner et al., 2017a, 2017b).

Although the 2n gamete production is genetically controlled, it is also known to be influenced by a variety of environmental factors (such as water and nutrient stress, temperature fluctuations, and herbivory), which could explain the quantitative variation of this trait within the species (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998; Mason & Pires, 2015; Kreiner et al., 2017a). The incidence of triploids depends not only on the production of unreduced gametes within populations, but also on their success in subsequent stages, including pollination, fertilization, seed germination, and the offspring's fitness and establishment (Kreiner et al., 2017a). Interestingly, the occurrence of autotriploids in C. rivularis is not evenly distributed, suggesting that intraspecific variation and ecological factors may affect the rate of unreduced gamete formation and persistence of triploid progeny. The triploids were more abundant in the Făgăraș Mts. than in other mountains of the Romanian range, and not a single autotriploid was detected in the pure Bulgarian populations, although we conducted extensive sampling within the populations. By contrast, autotriploids were frequent in the hybrid zones, that is, in the presence of other congeners. We can speculate that the sympatric occurrence of different species within a hybrid zone may in some way stimulate the successful formation of autotriploids, although the possible mechanisms behind this are unknown.

We found that the autotriploids have moderate pollen viability (Fig. 5); nevertheless, variation in pollen grain size and the absence of tetraploids in the studied populations suggest that most gametes are aneuploid due to the expected abnormalities in meiotic chromosome pairing and segregation (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998; Nyarko et al., 2025). This is accompanied by the high clonality of the autotriploids both in the pure populations in the Făgăraș Mts. and in the hybrid zones (Fig. S7), supporting previous findings that clonal propagation provides an escape route from reduced fertility (Vallejo‐Marín & Hiscock, 2016; Šingliarová et al., 2023). In the Făgăraș Mts., higher clonal growth can also be inferred for the diploids, which seems to explain the patchy distribution and persisting coexistence of these two cytotypes, in accordance with the conclusions drawn in the mixed‐ploidy Pilosella echioides (Chrtek et al., 2017).

The overwhelming dominance of triploid hybrids: evolutionary dead‐ends, nascent allopolyploid species, or threats to parental species?

Most triploid hybrids studied here could be assigned to the early generations (predominantly F1) based on the hybrid index and interspecific heterozygosity, following the estimates of Milne & Abbott (2008). These conclusions, together with the high hybrid frequencies at some localities (especially at YUN, KAT, and BIT), indicate recurrent polytopic origins and persistence ensured by perennial growth and clonal propagation, traits clearly inherited from their parents. Crosses between F1 hybrids or backcrosses with the parental individuals seem to be rare but are supported by evidence of diploid–triploid introgression (Table 2) and some degree of male fertility (Fig. 5). Despite meiotic irregularities, triploid hybrids may produce a certain proportion of viable gametes, generating euploid progeny in subsequent generations and serving as a bridge for interploidy gene flow (Bartolić et al., 2024). In the triploid hybrid Cardamine × insueta from the Swiss Alps, both male and female sporogenesis were found to be severely disrupted (Urbanska‐Worytkiewicz, 1977). Only 1.5% (5 out of 337) of the triploids investigated produced viable pollen (with a viability of 26–51%), generating aneuploid, unreduced, and polarized (8‐ or 16‐chromosomal) gametes. Open pollination resulted in very low seed fertility, but all the progeny were euploid – either triploid (after cross‐pollinations of triploids) or diploid to pentaploid (after backcrossing), confirming the triploid bridge as one of the common pathways of polyploid formation (Ramsey & Schemske, 1998; Husband, 2004; Mason & Pires, 2015).

In their study on triploids from the West Rhodope Mts., Ančev et al. (2013) reported deviations and degeneration processes during meiosis and development of both male and female gametophytes, but found no statistically significant differences in the fertility of pollen grains in the diploid C. matthioli and C. rivularis on the one hand and the triploid hybrids on the other. This is in contrast to our data, according to which the diploid parental species are highly fertile, while the hybrids have significantly reduced fertility (Fig. 5). This discrepancy may be due to taxonomic misassignments in the earlier study, where the presence of RRAb hybrids and autotriploids of C. rivularis was not recognized, with the latter probably being erroneously assigned to hybrids. Ančev et al. (2013) also suggested apomictic development of the embryo sac in some ovules of the triploids, but the character and importance of apomixis still need to be verified (Hörandl et al., 2024; Šarhanová et al., 2024).

The hybrids studied here have accumulated considerable genetic variation due to their recurrent and multiple origins (within and across different hybrid zones), increasing the chance of producing euploid gametes, potentially leading to the formation of tetra‐ or hexaploids with restored fertility (as observed in the neoallopolyploids Senecio cambrensis and Mimulus peregrinus; Abbott & Lowe, 2004; Vallejo‐Marín et al., 2017). In one of the hybrid zones (BIT), the distributions of genetic distances suggested high similarities between individuals that steadily extended beyond the genet assignment threshold (Fig. S7), which can be attributed to the combined effect of clonal and sexual autogamous reproduction. The transition from outcrossing to self‐fertilization appears to be a typical feature of polyploid evolution (Abbott & Lowe, 2004; Shimizu & Tsuchimatsu, 2015; Edger et al., 2025), possibly enhanced here through inheritance of self‐compatibility of C. amara (Tedder et al., 2015).

One of the possible consequences of interspecific hybridization is also the decline and (local or global) extinction of one or both parental lineages. Endemic high‐mountain species may be particularly threatened if they come into contact with more widespread congeners, as Gómez et al. (2015) pointed out in the Sierra Nevada Mts., where up to 25% of local endemics appear to hybridize with other, mostly lowland relatives. Either demographic (population decline due to wasted reproductive effort) or genetic swamping (replacement of the parental lineage by viable hybrids) has been described as a possible threatening mechanism (Todesco et al., 2016). A review of previous studies indicated that genetic swamping is a much more common process (Todesco et al., 2016), which also seems to operate in some of the hybrid zones studied here. In the YUN and BIT hybrid zones located at altitudes of 1270–1380 m, hybrids clearly dominate over very few individuals of C. rivularis, both growing along streams, while patches of pure C. matthioli remain in nearby meadows, mires, and bogs (Fig. 1b). These spatial patterns are consistent with the different phytosociological characteristics reported by Ančev et al. (2013) for C. rivularis and C. matthioli, and additionally show that the hybrids may occupy the habitat typical of C. rivularis, possibly displacing it. This is also supported by the results of BGC, which showed the presence of α outlier loci with extreme introgression, indicative of directional selection (Table S3). By contrast, pure C. rivularis is still abundant together with relatively few hybrids of both types (RRAb and RRM) at higher altitudes of 1500–1550 m at the sites of the Semiza river (JEB and BEG). This leads us to the conclusion that the (sub)alpine C. rivularis may be currently threatened at its lower altitudinal margin. However, climate warming, associated altitudinal shifts, and increasing parapatry could expand this critical zone to higher altitudes and intensify hybridization similarly as suspected or observed in other genera (Gómez et al., 2015; Konowalik et al., 2025).

Conclusions

Newly formed allopolyploids can follow divergent evolutionary trajectories, ranging from short‐lived dead ends to the emergence of successful lineages with long‐term persistence. Only a few well‐studied neo‐allopolyploids provide an opportunity to comparatively investigate and understand the earliest stages of allopolyploid speciation. Our study adds to this knowledge by discovering and describing a unique, highly variable, and dynamic system of seven hybrid zones involving four Cardamine congeners and allotriploids with polytopic and recurrent formations. We found that one parental species (C. rivularis), likely producing unreduced female gametes at higher rates, plays a pivotal role in the origin and establishment of the triploids. At the same time, this species may be threatened by genetic swamping, especially at lower altitudes, illustrating how hybridization can simultaneously act as a source of evolutionary novelty and a potential threat to parental lineages. We also conclude that the interplay of extrinsic (range shifts, diversity and dynamics of wetland niches) and intrinsic factors (elevated production of unreduced gametes, variation in fertility, and reproductive modes) promotes the emergence and persistence of allotriploids. We hypothesize significant evolutionary potential of the discovered allotriploids due to their diverse genomic compositions, accumulated diversity through recurrent origins, high abundance, partial male fertility, and efficient clonal propagation. These features may allow them to evolve into one or more additional stabilized neo‐allopolyploids.
