MicroRNAs as prognostic and predictive biomarkers among chronic myeloid leukemia patients in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Fekadu Urgessa, Isaac Jenkins, Aster Tsegaye, Helen Nigussie, Teklu Kuru, Amha Gebremedhin, Fozia Abdela, Fisihatsion Tadesse, Jerald Radich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Approximately 1.5 million people worldwide suffer from chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). MicroRNAs (miRs) are important regulators of gene expression and offer an attractive option as biomarkers for cancer detection, diagnosis, and prognosis assessment in solid and liquid tumors. To assess miRs as prognostic and predictive biomarkers among CML patients at the Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), Addis Ababa, Ethiopia from April 2021 to May 2023. Blood samples were collected from newly diagnosed CML patients before initiation of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), imatinib treatment, and while on therapy. The expression level of miRs were determined using the NanoString platform. LIMMA analysis was used to identify differentially expressed miR between TKI response groups and disease phases. Fifty-two study participants were enrolled in the study. From each sample, 798 hsa-miRs included on the Nanostring assay were measured. Comparing TKI naive new CML patients (n = 14) with those progressed or had blast crisis (BC) on TKI therapy (n = 12), 97 miRs were differentially expressed (|log2FC|, FDR, and P-value at > 1, < 0.001, and < 0.0001, respectively). Most miRs showed upregulation in BC CML patients compared to new CML cases except miR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975, and miR-630 which were downregulated in patients with BC. In addition, eight miRs were differentially expressed comparing poor molecular responder (n = 12) with good molecular responder (n = 28) patients (P < 0.05). MiR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975, and miR-630 were commonly deregulated in BC and poor molecular response groups. MiRs have significant potential as prognostic and predictive biomarkers for CML patients. MiR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975 and miR-630 could be considered as prognostic and predictive biomarkers for disease progression and treatment response if validated by other large studies.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative disorder of hematopoietic stem cells, triggered by t(9;22) chromosomal translocation coupling the 5’domains of the Break Cluster Region (BCR) gene from chromosome 22 to the 3’ tyrosine kinase domains of the Abelson (ABL) gene from chromosome 91–3. The chimeric protein product drives the inappropriate myeloproliferation in CML and is the target of TKI therapy. Untreated CML typically evolves from chronic phase disease to blast phase at a median time from 5 to 7 years. The only known curative modality for blast phase CML is allogeneic stem cell transplantation.

The advent of targeted small-molecule drugs has revolutionized CML treatment over the past decade. These drugs inhibit the constitutively active Breakpoint Cluster Region-Abelson (BCR::ABL1) tyrosine kinase, the primary driver of CML, by interfering with its ATP-dependent activity. While TKIs have significantly improved overall survival rates to more than 90%4, they are often limited by issues of intolerance or resistance. These two groups of patients are at higher risk blastic transformation (blast crisis), either because of the inability to effectively give drug (intolerance), or from the biological mechanisms that cause resistance, which are often independent of kinase blockade5. Blast crisis (BC) remains a major therapeutic challenge despite the major changes in management of chronic phase (CP) CML. The introduction of TKI has been significant for reducing the incidence of BC6.

Response to TKI therapy is determined by the measurement of hematologic, cytogenetic, and molecular assessments. Monitoring with quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRTPCR), standardized to an International Scale (IS) every 3 months is recommended for all patients after initiating TKI therapy. Treatment milestones have been established for BCR::ABL1 levels 3, 6, and 12 months (≤ 10% BCR::ABL1 IS at 3 and 6 months, ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1 IS at 12 months, and ≤ 0.1% BCR::ABL1 IS at > 12 months)7.

MicroRNAs (miRs) are an attractive target for biomarker research. MiRs are a class of small, non-coding RNA molecules approximately 22 nucleotides in length. They regulate gene expression through mechanisms such as translational repression, mRNA cleavage, and mRNA decay via microRNA-guided deadenylation8. MiRs suppress target mRNA expression through two primary mechanisms: (i) degradation of the target mRNA by ribonucleases within the multiprotein RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) in the cytoplasm or (ii) inhibition of translation without degrading the target mRNA. In both cases, the target gene is not translated. MiRs play critical roles in various cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and apoptosis, and they are implicated in cancer development and progression9.

The role of miRs in key oncogenic processes such as differentiation, proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and apoptosis has garnered significant attention as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in leukemia. Emerging evidence indicates that the mutational spectrum and dysregulated expression of miR genes are closely associated with the onset and progression of various cancers, including leukemia10. The specific expression profiles of miRs in leukemia enable their use as novel biomarkers for distinguishing leukemia subtypes, improving diagnostic accuracy, and predicting clinical outcomes to aid in individualized treatment planning11. Combinatorial miR expression profiles have the potential to serve as effective biomarkers for diagnosis, monitoring disease progression, and assessing drug response12.

Despite the promise of miR expression profiles as a new category of disease biomarkers, their prevalence and expression levels can vary due to population-specific genetic variations across diverse groups, including different demographic variables including ethnicity13,14. This variability may contribute to health disparities observed in various cancers, including leukemia. Further, the finding of this study could contribute for addressing this variability since the study focuses on an underrepresented population. Therefore, we aimed to assess the prognostic and predictive role of miR among patients with CML in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods

This study was conducted in hematology clinic TASH, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia from April 2021 to May 2023. The hospital serves as a referral center for most of the country, especially for cancer-related diseases. Cross sectional and case-cohort study design were used for assessing the prognostic and predictive role of miR among patients with CML, respectively. The study population includes newly diagnosed patients with CML who were treatment naïve and HU treated, and those who progressed to blast crisis. The blood samples were collected in the hematology clinic before the onset of treatment for those newly diagnosed CML patients prior to the initiation of TKI therapy. During the treatment period, blood samples were collected at 12 months for assessing hematological, cytogenetic, and molecular response. For this study we analyzed 52 samples for blast crisis (n = 12), and newly diagnosed CML patients (n = 40). We aimed to study the differential expression of miR in different CML states: newly diagnosed CML, CML cases with progression and/or blast crisis, and different categories of BCR::ABL1 response to TKI therapy.

Socio-demographic and clinical data were collected by nurses working at the hematology clinic. Whole blood samples were collected in BD vacutainer ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) Tubes and samples stored at room temperature (15–25 °C) or 4 °C and processed within one hour. To isolate circulating, cell-free nucleic acids from whole blood samples, we followed protocols recommended by Qiagen (Appendix I), which include an initial low g-force centrifugation step to separate cells from plasma followed by a high g-force centrifugation step to remove all remaining cellular debris. After collection and centrifugation, plasma was stored at –90 to –65 °C temperature in aliquots at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI). Finally, the sample was transported to Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, Seattle, WA, USA for miR extraction and profiling.

Total RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from plasma samples using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen catalog #217184, Germantown, MD, USA) with modifications. Total RNA was measured using a Nanodrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (Appendix II).

MicroRNA expression assay

We used 100 ng of purified total RNA as input into the NanoString Human v3 miRNA Code Set Kit (PN150629). The Code Set kit (PN150629) is the specific panel that contains 800 human miRs derived from miRbase plus 20 assay controls and 5 mRNA probes that we used. This panel contains the specific codeset, NS_H_miR_v3b, as well as the reagents needed for annealing, ligating, and purifying miR targets. Samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Target-probe complexes were processed and immobilized to nCounter cartridge on the nCounter Prep Station and then transferred to the nCounter MAX/FLEX system to quantify probe abundances (nCounter miRNA expression assay user manual). Sample QC and reporter probe counts were then analyzed using the nSolver Analysis Software 4.0. The miR panel, nCounter system, and nSolver software were manufactured by NanoString Technologies Seattle, WA, USA. All RNA extraction and miR expression determination procedures were performed in the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, Seattle, WA, USA.

Molecular response determination

BCR::ABL1 level was measured using the GeneXpert Ultra platform (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, Ca., USA). It was used to determine BCR::ABL1 result for molecular response15. The BCR::ABL1 transcript level is measured as a ratio of BCR::ABL1 1 to ABL1 on the International Scale (IS). The BCR::ABL1 level of < 1% (IS) was the cut off value for optimal molecular response after 12 months on imatinib per established treatment guidelines as failure to achieve molecular milestones represented by BCR::ABL1 < 1% at 12 months is associated with statistically significant worse survival7,16.

Determination of microRNA as prognostic and predictive biomarkers

To assess the prognostic and predictive values of miR, we studied the differential expression of miR in different CML states. For prognostic values we examined differentially expressed miRs between TKI naïve newly diagnosed patients CML patients (n = 40) and progression/blast crisis (n = 12) cases. MiR of both treatment naïve and cases who received HU before TKI were combined (TKI naïve newly diagnosed patients) and were compared with BC cases. To identify miRs that predict the TKI and molecular response, CML patients were followed on IM treatment for 12 months. Based on their molecular and TKI responses on 12 months, the patients were classified as poor (BCR::ABL1 result > 1%) or good (BCR::ABL1 result < 1%) molecular responses. MiRs of patients with good molecular response (n = 28) were compared with poor molecular response (n = 12) patients after 12 months on the first-generation drug, imatinib (IM). For top differentially expressed miRs, genes targeted by these miRNAs were determined.

Data processing and statistics

Raw miR expression data was normalized using R v4.4.317 and the nSolver method from the NanoString NC tools v1.12.018 package. Differential expression analysis was performed on the normalized data using simple, two-group limma v3.60.419 comparisons. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the method of Benjamini-Hochberg. Volcano plots with varying log fold change and FDR thresholds were used to identify interesting miRs across multiple pairwise comparisons. For the top differentially expressed miRs, molecular signaling pathway enrichment and gene ontology classification have been performed using the Mienturnet web tool20. Expression values highlighting differences between groups were visualized using standard box plots and heat maps.

Quality assurance mechanisms

To assure the quality of the study, different mechanisms were used starting from the sample collection up to data analysis, then report. Plasma preparation protocol for miRs was strictly followed to keep the integrity of the sample and to minimize errors during plasma preparation. To check the quality of plasma we sent non-critical samples using dry-ice to Fred Hutch and checked quality of the plasma. As quality was confirmed, we then transported the samples used for this study using dry-ice. During RNA extraction, the quality of the purified total RNA for each sample was measured by a Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer, Based on 260/280 and 260/230 ratios. Additionally, the expression of several internal control miRs were used as a measure of sample quality. No sample with poor quality or below the standard was detected.

Results

Sociodemographic variables

From 52 study participants, 40 were newly diagnosed and TKI naive and 12 were BC CML patients. The median age for the study participants were 35 (30–44) years and 53% were rural residents. Sixty-seven percent of patients were male.

Common microRNAs deregulated in BC CML patients

Most upregulated miRs were common among all cases of new CML (despite exposure to HU) compared to BC cases. MiR-30a-3p, miR-454-3p, miR-1200, miR-487b-3p, and miR-1296-3p were miRs that commonly upregulated and differentially expressed among the BC group compared to other comparison groups (P < 0.01). Conversely, miR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975 and miR-630 were downregulated in the BC and poor molecular response patients compared to others (P < 0.05). MiR-34a-5p was most networked with 107 target genes and upregulated in the BC cases (P < 0.0001). PCA plot was determined between BC and new CML patients miRs (Supplementary figure 1).

Discussion

MiR biology is essential to vital oncogenic processes such as differentiation, proliferation, migration, angiogenesis and apoptosis and thus miRs has attracted much attentions as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in leukemia10. MiRs specific expression in leukemia allows them to be used as novel biomarkers to differentiate the various types of leukemia, to make more accurate diagnoses and estimate clinical prognoses before developing an individualized treatment plan11. In this study we assessed changes of miR biology associated with CML progression and response to TKI therapy. We found a large differential miR expression signature in CML progression, with evidence of shared miR biology between progression and treatment response.

About 97 miRs showed differential expression using |log2FC| and p-value at > 1 and < 0.0001 cutoffs, respectively between new CML patient naive for HU and BC cases. All of them were overexpressed in BC compared to new CML cases, except miR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975, and miR-630, which had lower expression in BC compared to new CML cases. MiR-223 is crucial for hematological malignancies because of its main role in myeloid progenitor differentiation and immune response21–23. MiR-223 is downregulated in various blood cancers, including AML, where it targets E2F1 to inhibit cell cycle progression, establishing a negative autoregulatory loop. Among the primary target genes identified for miR-223 are FOXO3, ATM, STAT3, STAT5, and ABCB1, and these genes may be responsible for differentiation and drug resistance22,23. Thus, they provide a biological bridge between CML progression and TKI response seen in our patients.

MiR-630 was also significantly downregulated in the BC group compared to newly diagnosed CML patients. MiR-630 exhibits both oncogenic and tumor suppressor functions. It acts as an oncogenic miR in renal cell carcinoma, multiple myeloma, colorectal cancer, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, ovarian cancer, and prostate cancer. Conversely, in lung, cervical, breast, thyroid, and esophageal cancers it serves as a putative tumor suppressor miR24. Our data suggest that miR-630 may perform as tumor suppressor role in BC CML patients. Most of the target genes of miR-630 are dysregulated in cancers and are functionally associated with pathways such as BCL2, BCL2L2, AKT, P53, TGFβ, ERK/SP1, JNK/c-Jun, PI3K/AKT, and JAK2/STAT324.

MiR-148b showed an upregulation in BC CML patients compared to newly diagnosed CML patients. Previously miR-148b has been found to be associated with TKI response, with low expression levels in CML patients who had stable complete molecular responses after stopping IM treatment25, and elevated expression in treatment failure patients26. The target genes for miR-148b include DNMT1 and DNMT3, which are known to regulate FOXO3 expression. Low levels of miR-148b could lead to an upregulation of DNMT and a downregulation of regulatory T cell (Treg) activity, like how IM inhibits Treg activity. This again reinforces the miR link between progression and treatment response.

Another miR that demonstrated significant upregulation in the BC group compared to other groups was miR-30a-3p. This miR is also associated with response biology, as miR-30a enhances imatinib-induced cytotoxicity by downregulating Beclin-1 and autophagy protein 5 (ATG5) expression. These findings suggest that the dysregulation of miR-30a may interfere with the effectiveness of imatinib-mediated apoptosis through an autophagy-dependent mechanism, making it a potential novel therapeutic target in CML27. Other biologically relevant miR dysregulated in progression include miR-34a which has been implicated in leukemic stem/progenitor cell biology given its involvement in NOTCH, MYC, BCL-2, and CD44 expression28. Similarly, miR-142-5p levels were increased in BC CML relevant as reporter gene assays that identified survival, proliferation, and invasion-promoting CML-related genes, including ABL2, cKIT, MCL1, and SRI, as targets of miR-142-5p29.

Eight (8) miRs have been found to be differentially expressed between patients with poor and good molecular responses after one year on IM treatment at P < 0.05. Among the eight differentially expressed miRs, miR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975, and miR-630 were common with BC group which compared to newly diagnosed CML patients. These miRs showed downregulation in both BC and poor molecular response CML patients, suggesting they may contribute to disease progression and could predict the TKI response too. The biology of miR-223-3p and miR-630 have been discussed briefly above, including the putative link between progression and response. In contrast, little is known about miRs-4454 and -7975, and how they may relate to leukemia and therapy response.

Additionally, miRs such as miR-33a-5p, miR-568, and miR-574 were found to be downregulated in patients with poor molecular responses compared to those with good responses. One study highlighted that miR-33a-5p might help identify new targets and therapeutic strategies for managing IM resistance by inhibiting KG cell proliferation and reducing IM resistance through the suppression of HMGA230. MiR-568 has also been suggested as a tumor-suppressive miR among CML patients and is upregulated in patients during the chronic phase27. MiR-574-5p exhibits a context-dependent role in multiple cancers31 and is downregulated in IM-resistant patients compared to those who respond to IM32. There are no prior studies of miR-601 and miR-1285-5p with CML biology, but these miRs have tumor-suppressive roles in various solid cancers. For example, miR-1285-5p significantly inhibits cell proliferation in breast cancer cells, regardless of tumor subtype, by directly regulating the transmembrane protein 194A. Thus, miR-1285-5p acts as a tumor suppressor through the inhibition of TMEM194A in breast cancer33.

Similarly, miR-574-5p functions as a tumor suppressor in triple-negative breast cancer cells by repressing proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. The inhibitory effects of miR-574-5p in triple-negative breast cancer cells were at least partly dependent on SOX2 and BCL11A31, which are target genes for the differentially expressed miRs in this study. Additionally, plasma levels of miR-601 are significantly decreased in colorectal cancer (CRC) and advanced adenomas, suggesting miR-601 could serve as a novel biomarker for clinical CRC diagnosis and may be useful as a non-invasive biomarker for early detection34.

Molecular signaling pathway enrichment and gene ontology classification were analyzed using the Mienturnet web tool for the top differentially expressed miRs. Accordingly, we have identified target genes for top differentially expressed miRs. According to studies these identified genes had association with cancer, TKI resistance and disease progression. For instance, from identified target genes APC, BCL2, ERBB2, and TGFBR2 are well-known cancer-associated genes. Additionally, other miR target genes such as those involved in the MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and VEGF signaling pathways, play a crucial role in the progression of CML35. Further, the top miR target genes identified are associated with disease progression, drug resistance, and advanced CML cases. Identified target genes such as BTK, FOS, HIF1A, NFKB1, TNC, GAS2, ABCB1, JAK1, FOXO1, STAT3, STAT5, and CXCR4 are implicated in TKI resistance36,37.

Moreover, studies revealed identified target genes such as AKT1, ATM, BAP1, BCL2, CDKN2A, MYC, MYB, RUNX1, CTNNB1, CXCR4, EGFR, FYN, APC, JAK1, MAPK1, PTKB2, SRC, TGFB1, TGFB2, TP53, VEGFA, PTEN, CDKN1C, BCL6, and ERBB2 were overexpressed in blast crisis cases36–38. Study also highlighted that deregulation of identified target genes including EGFR, TP53, SRC, CTNNB1, SKIL, TGFB1, TGFB2, TNFA, and NFKB1 contribute for CML progression37. Furthermore, target genes identified were also involved in alternative pathways through genes such as RAS/MAPK, SRC, JAK/STAT, and PI3K/AKT as these genes contribute to TKI resistance according to study36. Genetic abnormalities of identified target genes MYC, RUNX1, IKZF1, PP2A, PTEN, p53, and GATA-2 are also linked to CML progression37.

Our study has some clear limitations. The study design did not involve time-to-event analyses, such as analysis of progression-free survival or disease-free survival because of frequently drug interruption among patients with CML. Moreover, our samples were also relatively small per groups which also impose the study findings to be cautiously interpreted to avoid making strong claims about clinical utility or applicability without external validation.

Conclusion

MiRs could hold significant potential as prognostic and predictive biomarkers for CML patients. MiR-223-3p, miR-4454, miR-7975, and miR-630 were downregulated in the BC and poor molecular response patients compared to others. Conversely, miR-30a-3p, miR-454-3p, miR-1200, miR-487b-3p, and miR-1296-3p were upregulated in the BC group compared to other comparison groups. If validated by external and other functional studies, these miRs could serve as valuable biomarkers for CML patients. Given the apparent overlap between the genetics of progression and resistance, these miRs biomarker may potentially be used to pick the most appropriate TKI therapy to maximize initial therapy and prevent progression. Lastly, miRs involved in both processes might be excellent targets of miR directed therapy.
