Safety and efficacy of percutaneous biopsy and microwave ablation in patients with pulmonary nodules on antithrombotic therapy: A study with rivaroxaban bridging
Authors: Nan Wang, Tianyu Xue, Wenwen Zheng, Zhongying Shao, Zhuang Liu, Faliang Dai, Qi Xie, Jing Sang, Xin Ye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the safety and efficacy of percutaneous biopsy and microwave ablation (B + MWA) in patients with pulmonary nodules (PNs) who are receiving antithrombotic therapy by rivaroxaban as bridging therapy.MethodsThe study comprised 187 patients with PNs who underwent 187 B + MWA sessions from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2021. The enrolled patients were divided into two groups: Group A, who received antithrombotic therapy five days before the procedure and received rivaroxaban as a bridging drug during hospitalization, and group B, who had no antithrombotic treatment. Information about the technical success rate, positive biopsy rate, complete ablative rate, and major complications were collected and analyzed.ResultsGroup A comprised 53 patients and group B comprised 134 patients. The technical success rate was 100% in both groups. The positive biopsy rates were 88.68% and 91.04%, respectively (p = 0.6211, X2 = 0.2443). In groups A and B, the complete ablative rates at 6, 12, and 24 months were 100.0% versus 99.25%, 96.23% versus 96.27%, and 88.68% versus 89.55%, respectively. There were no significant differences in bleeding and thrombotic complications between the two groups. No grade 5 complications occurred.ConclusionsIt is generally considered safe and effective that patients who are on antithrombotic therapy by rivaroxaban as bridging to undergo B + MWA for treating PNs.Keywords: antithrombotic therapy, efficacy, microwave ablation, pulmonary nodule, safety

INTRODUCTION

Pulmonary nodules (PNs), a certain proportion of which are malignant in nature, are attracting increasing attention following the wide application of low dose computed tomography (CT) for lung cancer screening. 1 , 2 Early treatment of malignant PNs can lead to a reduction in lung cancer mortality and prolong the survival time of patients. Unfortunately, due to the absence of scientific criteria, the false positive rate for detecting pulmonary nodules via low‐dose CT scanning is quite high. Overdiagnosis leads to excessive treatment, which in turn causes anxiety and economic burden for patients, reducing their quality of life. 3 Compared with temporary PNs, more attention needs to be given to persistent PNs. Generally, highly suspicious malignant PNs often appear to have a solid component of more than 6 mm or in cases where there is an increase in the solid component during a 6–9 months follow‐up. 4 , 5 It is difficult to distinguish malignant PNs from some benign nodules, such as tuberculoma or inflammatory pseudotumor. 6 , 7 In addition, advanced techniques such as positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT, liquid biopsy, and computer artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted diagnosis have been used for the differential diagnosis of PNs; nonetheless, issues related to low specificity and low sensitivity persist. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Currently, biopsy and surgical excision are the most widely used methods for managing highly malignant PNs. 12 , 13 , 14 However, puncture therapy and surgical excision tend to cause complications such as severe bleeding, especially in patients on antithrombotic therapy. 15 , 16 Antithrombotic agents, including antiplatelet agents and anticoagulants, are beneficial for preventing thromboembolism in older people and those with previous thrombotic diseases. Patients with PNs and a history of antithrombotic therapy may experience bleeding complications after a biopsy or surgery. Perioperative management, including the cessation of antithrombotic agents before invasive procedures, is commonly recommended; however, it can increase the risk of thromboembolism, 17 , 18 , 19 particularly in elderly lung cancer patients who are more susceptible to developing thrombotic complications due to their hypercoagulable state. 20 A previous study demonstrated the feasibility of treating patients with lung tumors via thermal ablation combined with biopsy and reported a lower incidence of bleeding complications compared to that seen with biopsy alone. 21 This might be attributed to the destruction of the vascular structure by thermal ablation, leading to an acceleration in thrombosis, which could reduce the risk of severe bleeding complications. 22 , 23 Studies on patients with PNs who received antithrombotic therapy and underwent biopsy and microwave ablation (B + MWA) are insufficient. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of synchronous CT‐guided percutaneous B + MWA for PNs in patients on antithrombotic therapy.

METHODS

Patients

This study consisted of 628 patients with highly suspicious malignant PNs who underwent 675 consecutive B + MWA treatments from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2021. These patients were unsuitable candidates for surgery or radiotherapy. This study was conducted in adherence with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received formal approval from the Ethics Committee of Shandong Province Qianfoshan Hospital, under the approval number YXLL‐KY‐2020 (S046). The nature of this study was retrospective, and the review board granted a waiver for the requirement to obtain written informed consent from the participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with lesions diagnosed as highly suspicious malignant PNs, (2) those with PNs measuring 8–30 mm and (3) those with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score of ≤2. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with other lesions treated at the same session, (2) those with metastatic lung tumor or primary lung cancer with lymph node and distant metastases, (3) those with abnormal liver and/or kidney function, (4) those with acute bleeding or thrombotic disorders during the past 30 days, (5) those with moderate or severe interstitial pulmonary disease, (6) those with uncontrolled malignant pleural effusion, (7) those who received other antitumor therapy during the periprocedural period, (8) those with history of other malignancies and (9) those who were lost to follow‐up. Overall, 187 patients (115 men and 72 women; mean age, 63.87 ± 13.56; range: 37–87 years) were enrolled. A flow chart of the patient selection process is shown in Figure 1. Determination of a history of antithrombotic therapy was based on searching the patient's medical charts and the electronic records of the prescriptions and medications in the ordering system. Those on antithrombotic medication up to five days prior to the procedure comprised the antithrombotic therapy group (group A), whereas those who did not receive antithrombotic agents comprised the control group (group B). Based on expert consensus, all treatment options were considered after a multidisciplinary team discussion, which involved members from thoracic surgery, oncology, respiratory diseases, radiotherapy, interventional radiology, imaging, and pathology departments. 24

Procedures and perioperative management of anticoagulant therapy

Patients who received antithrombotic therapy were required to stop before the procedure. Generally, aspirin, ticlopidine, and clopidogrel were discontinued five days before the procedure, and warfarin was stopped three days before. Anticoagulation with rivaroxaban (10 mg once a day) was initiated immediately after the discontinuation of the initial antithrombotic drug and was ceased one day prior to the procedure. Rivaroxaban was resumed three days post‐procedure based on the reduction of bleeding on the CT and the absence of bleeding complications. The initial antithrombotic therapy was resumed as soon as possible after confirming the absence of bleeding complications via a blood test and a post‐procedural CT scan after five days of rivaroxaban treatment. The status of the antithrombotic therapy was evaluated at each treatment.

CT (Lightspeed 64V, GE General Electric or NeuViz 64, Neusoft Co. Ltd. or uCT 760, United Imaging Healthcare Co. Ltd.) was used to guide the procedures. MWA was performed using the MTC 3C MWA system (Vison‐China Medical Devices R&D Center), the ECO‐100A1 MWA system (ECO Medical Instrument Co. Ltd.), with a frequency of 2450 ± 50 MHz and an adjustable continuous wave output power of 0–100W. The microwave antenna had an effective length of 100–180 mm, an outer diameter of 19‐G (19‐G antennas have high puncture accuracy and few complications), and a radiating tip of 1.5 cm (tapered end). After local anesthesia with 5–10 mL of 2% lidocaine, the 19 G‐antenna was inserted, followed by a coaxial guiding needle (17 G, GMT Medical) along the designated path or the ablation antenna. Ablation was performed for 1–2 min at 30–50 W power. CT was performed again to observe changes at the ablation site. The position of the needle tip was confirmed before the 18 G biopsy needle was inserted along the coaxial catheter, and samples were collected. If severe pulmonary bleeding was observed, ablation with a power of 30–50 W was applied for 1–2 min to promote hemostasis.

After performing the B + MWA, as described previously, 25 optimal circumferential ablated margins of ≥10 mm for solid lesions and ≥5 mm for subsolid lesions and ablative margins were achieved. The needle tract was ablated to promote hemostasis and reduce metastasis. Vessels with a diameter >2 mm distributed along the ablation antenna path were ablated for 1–2 min at the intersection of the path and the vessel to reduce bleeding. It was not advisable to have vessels >3 mm in diameter within the preprocedural designed puncture path.

Patients were confined to bed overnight and released from rest after appropriate blood test results were confirmed the next morning. Those in the anticoagulation group required closer attention. When hypotension (systolic pressure < 90 mmHg), tachycardia (heart rate > 100 beats/min), unusual hemoptysis, or other signs or symptoms of bleeding, like severe chest pain, dyspnea or vertigo were noted, the hemoglobin levels and chest CT scans were immediately checked while monitoring the electrocardiogram and blood pressure. Fluid replacement and hemostatic agents were commenced, and blood transfusion was considered when the hemodynamics could not be stabilized. Routine chest CT was performed 24 h post‐procedure. If the bleeding area was not reduced at 24 h, the CT scan was performed and re‐examined every 3 days post‐procedure until a reduction in the bleeding area was observed.

Once the bleeding‐related complications were controlled, the patient was recommended to take rivaroxaban for five consecutive days and observed for bleeding‐related symptoms. If the complications worsened, a CT examination was performed immediately. Patients were kept under observation if the CT scans confirmed an increase in the bleeding area. Antithrombotic agents were discontinued until bleeding was confirmed to be under control. If symptoms of bleeding did not worsen after an additional five days of rivaroxaban, the original antithrombotic drugs were provided as soon as possible.

Response criteria and follow‐up

Technical success was defined as completion of MWA of target lesions and collection of biopsy specimen according to the preprocedural plan. Positive biopsy results were defined as available pathological tissues, including atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), and malignant tissues. Complete ablation was defined as ablated area without enhancement or covered the target lesion area. Chest‐enhanced CT was performed in all patients to assess complete ablative rate one‐month post‐procedure as a baseline for follow‐up. Details of the efficacy evaluation after MWA of the pulmonary PNs are described in previous studies. 21 , 26 The patients were instructed to undergo a chest‐enhanced CT at 3‐, 6‐, 12‐, 18‐, and 24‐months post‐procedure. The mean follow‐up time was 17.2 ± 5.3 months (range: 11.2–27.4 months).

For complications, we focused only on bleeding and thrombotic complications. The incidence and mortality rates were evaluated according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE; version 5.0). Major complications were defined as ≥ grades 3. 27 Complications occurring before or beyond 1 month of procedures were not considered related to the procedure. The bleeding area was recorded from CT scans immediately and at 24 h post‐procedure. The blood coagulation parameters were assessed at 24 h and one‐month post‐procedure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test, whereas continuous variables were compared using the unpaired student's t‐test (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U test (nonparametric). Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the predictors of bleeding and thrombotic complications. The multivariable analysis with forwarding procedure includes the variables with p‐value ≤ 0.1 from the univariable analysis. A two‐tailed p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data used in this study were recorded for reference purposes.

RESULTS

The baseline characteristics of the patients on a treatment basis are shown in Table 1. There were 53 patients (30 men and 23 women; mean age, 68.83 ± 10.69 years) on antithrombotic therapy 5 days before the procedure in group A and 134 patients (85 men and 49 women; mean age, 62 ± 11.13 years) without antithrombotic therapy in group B, with no significant difference in age between the two groups (p = 0.0911). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, preprocedural coagulation function, preprocedural pulmonary function, basic medical history, target lesion location, distance from great vessels (>3 mm), size and proportion of the solid components, and pathological type of the target lesions. The procedural characteristics are shown in Table 2. No significant differences in patient position, puncture path length, puncture angle, number of cases puncture through vessels of more than 2 mm in diameter, mean maximum ablation power, mean ablation application duration, mean total procedure time, and hospitalization were observed between the two groups. In group A, 53 patients were treated with antithrombotic drugs five days before the procedure, including 41 treated with a single agent and 12 treated with two agents. The types and proportion of oral antithrombotic drugs used were as follows: 39 (73.58%) patients received aspirin; 15 (28.30%) patients received clopidogrel; four (7.55%) patients received ticagrelor; nine (16.98%) patients received warfarin. The indications for antithrombotic therapy in these patients included the following: ischemic heart disease, valvular heart disease, arrhythmia, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, pulmonary embolism, or deep vein thrombosis, and other or unspecified conditions.

Efficacy

All 187 lesions were completely ablated, and pathological tissues were obtained using a single procedure, with a 100% technical success rate. In group A, 11.32% (6/53) lesions were pathologically diagnosed as lung tissue or inflammation, considered pathologically negative, with a positive pathology rate of 88.68% (47/53). A total of 37 lesions were diagnosed as malignant tumors in group A, and the incidence rates of adenocarcinoma, squamous carcinoma, and adenosquamous carcinoma were 52.83% (28/53), 13.21% (7/53), and 3.77% (2/53), respectively. A total of 10 lesions were diagnosed as nonmalignant, including 13.21% (7/53) AIS and 5.66% (3/53) AAH. In group B, the positive pathology rate was 91.04% (122/134), and 8.96% (12/134) lesions were diagnosed as lung tissue or inflammation. The incidence rates of adenocarcinoma, squamous carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, carcinoid tumor, AIS, and AAH in this group were 63.43% (85/134), 10.45% (14/134), 1.49% (2/134), 0.75% (1/134), 13.43% (18/134), and 1.49% (2/134), respectively. At 6‐, 12‐, and 24‐months, the overall complete ablative rates in groups A and B were 53 (100) versus 133 (99.25), 51 (96.23) versus 129 (96.27), and 47 (88.68) versus 120 (89.55), with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.9999). Bleeding and thrombosis‐related complications

Bleeding and thrombotic complications are depicted in Table 4. The overall incidence of major complications was 26.41% in group A and 16.42% in group B. One patient in group A and two patients in group B experienced grade 4 bleeding complications. Two patients were cured after hemostatic medication and hospital observation, without needing interventional embolization or blood transfusion. No grade 5 complication was observed. Nine patients (3 in group A and 6 in group B) experienced a major bleeding complication that recurred more than 3 days post‐procedure and resolved rapidly with hemostatic therapy, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.6259). Among them, the onset time of three patients in group A was 9 ± 3.94 (range: 5–16) days and the onset time of six patients in group B was 7.5 ± 4.11 (range: 3–16) days. The D‐dimer levels at three days post‐procedure were significantly different between the two groups (p < 0.0001, t = 4.002). Analysis of prognostic factors

Figure 3 shows the results of the univariate and multivariate analyses of the major bleeding complications. Cox proportional hazards model showed that target lesion location (central location vs. peripheral location) (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.1425 [95% CI: 0.02891–2.578]; p < 0.0001) were independent prognostic factors for major bleeding complications. Figure 4 shows the results of the univariate and multivariate analyses of thrombotic complications. Cox proportional hazards model showed that age, years (<65 vs. ≥65) (HR = 0.2567 [95% CI: 0.05126–4.666]; p = 0.0668), Diagnosis of lesions (malignancy vs. not malignancy) (HR = 1.6626 [95% CI: 1.4226–2.116]; p = 0.0122), Postprocedural D‐dimer (abnormal vs. normal), (HR = 1.621 [95% CI: 0.7537–4.194]; p = 0.0047) were independent prognostic factors for thrombotic complications.

DISCUSSION

Surgical resection is the preferred treatment for early‐stage lung tumors. 28 , 29 , 30 However, a large proportion of patients cannot tolerate surgical treatment due to advanced age, poor cardiopulmonary function, or coagulation dysfunction. 31 , 32 , 33 Especially in patients with lung cancer because of hypercoagulable state, many require continuous antithrombotic therapy to reduce the incidence of thrombotic complications, which is one of the important reasons why they are not candidates for surgery. CT‐guided percutaneous thermal ablation provides an alternative treatment opportunity for these patients. 34 , 35 Previous studies have reported on the use of thermal ablation in patients with malignant tumors who were concurrently receiving anticoagulant drugs. However, these studies have focused on patients with liver cancer, and have mainly concentrated on the safety analysis of treatment modalities. 19

Performing CT‐guided percutaneous B + MWA of PNs in patients on antithrombotic therapy presents challenges. First, patients with coagulation abnormalities are at a higher risk of developing bleeding‐related complications. Second, puncture‐induced pulmonary parenchymal hemorrhage can readily alter the localization of target lesions, resulting in false negative biopsy, incomplete ablation, and even off‐target. Third, extended hospitalization due to persistent bleeding complications may raise the risk of secondary pulmonary infections. Nonetheless, withdrawing antithrombotic agents could result in severe thromboembolic events, particularly in patients with malignant tumors and a hypercoagulable state. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that patients with lung cancer are at a 100‐fold increased risk of thrombosis compared to the general population. This condition can lead to serious health complications, including but not limited to pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, visceral thrombosis, and catheter‐associated thrombosis. A report covering the period from 2008 to 2014 indicated that the incidence rates of thromboembolic events in patients with lung cancer ranged from 3% to 13.9%. 36 This finding underscores the high prevalence of thrombotic adverse events in patients with lung cancer during their disease and treatment. Consequently, judicious management of antithrombotic drug discontinuation and reinitiation during the periprocedural period is crucial.

In the present study, the baseline characteristics of the patients were generally similar in the two groups, with only a slightly greater average age observed in group B compared to group A, with no statistical significance. Yang et al. reported the experience of CT‐guided MWA in patients aged ≥75 years with early‐stage non‐small cell lung cancer. 37 Lu et al. claimed the safety and efficacy of radiofrequency ablation for early‐stage lung cancer in older adults. 38 Therefore, age should not be considered an independent factor affecting the findings of the present study. Additionally, no significant differences in the target tumor‐related characteristics, lung function, coagulation function, and underlying medical history were observed between the two groups in this study. Furthermore, the technique achieved a 100% success rate, with a positive biopsy rate of 90.37%. The local control rates at 6, 12, and 24 months were 99.47%, 96.26%, and 89.30%, respectively, which are comparable to those reported in previous studies. 21 , 25 The high efficacy of the technique and the favorable long‐term local control rates suggest the effectiveness of the treatment in the current study. The present study demonstrated that the incidence of bleeding complications was comparable between the two groups. Predominantly, the hemorrhage observed was pulmonary parenchymal hemorrhage. The bleeding area visible on the CT scans taken 24 h post‐procedure was more extensive in group A than in group B, indicating a slower absorption rate. The proportion of patients with increased CT bleeding area at 24 h post‐procedure was higher in group A than in group B. The D‐dimer levels in group A were significantly higher than those in group B 3 days post‐procedure (p < 0.0001). According to previous studies, factors such as the puncture path length, puncture angle, and number of punctures through vessels >2 mm increase the risk of bleeding complications. 39 , 40 Additionally, the power and duration of ablation can influence the risk of bleeding during B + MWA. In the current study, no significant differences in these factors were observed between the two groups. Therefore, the use of antithrombotic drugs is likely the primary reason for the poor coagulation function observed in group A three days post‐procedure. Upon conducting univariable and multivariable analyses of predictive factors, we identified that a central lesion location emerged as an independent predictor of bleeding complications. Concurrently, advanced age, malignancy, and an abnormal post‐procedural D‐dimer level were identified as independent predictors of thrombotic complications. This finding suggests that patients with advanced age, malignancy, a central lesion location, and an abnormal post‐procedural D‐dimer level, who undergo minimally invasive procedures, should be monitored with heightened vigilance. Furthermore, it may be prudent to extend the observation period appropriately to ensure the timely detection and management of any potential complications. In the whole observation period, the overall incidences of thrombotic complications were 1.89% and 3.73% in groups A and B, respectively, with no significant differences between the two groups. In a previous study, Anderson et al. described the high incidence of thrombosis in patients with lung cancer and the potential role of antiplatelet agents in the adjuvant treatment of cancer. 36 The low rate of thrombosis‐related complications in the present study might have benefited from bridging rivaroxaban during the discontinuation of antithrombotic therapy and the subsequent early resumption of the agents.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a single‐center study, and the results may not be reproducible consistently in other settings. Second, the study excluded patients with lung PNs measuring <8 mm and >30 mm, and patients who completed treatment of more than one lesion in the same procedure were also excluded, which may explain the high technical success rate and low complication rates. Third, no comparison was made with patients who directly discontinued antithrombotic therapy without bridging an oral anticoagulant therapy.

In conclusion, bridging oral anticoagulant therapy along with synchronous CT‐guided percutaneous B + MWA should be considered as an alternative treatment for PNs in patients receiving antithrombotic therapy. Although the potential for bleeding and thrombotic complications exists, these risks can be effectively managed through enhanced monitoring and the judicious extension of observation periods.
