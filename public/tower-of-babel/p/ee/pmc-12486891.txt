Interleukin‐6 induces cognitive impairment via toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4)‐mediated neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in mice with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Yangping Shentu, Shuqing Ma, Simin Ye, Yaozhe Ying, Luhui Wang, Yun Zhu, YunTing Wang, Nan Jiang, Zongyuan Zhao, Chenfei Zheng, Chaosheng Chen, Yongheng Bai, Ying Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsPast epidemiological and experimental studies in rodents have demonstrated that chronic kidney disease (CKD) leads to cognitive impairment. However, the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. Herein, a mouse model of CKD was established using conventional 5/6 nephrectomy. We aimed to examine the relationship between CKD and cognitive impairment and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.MethodsCognitive behavior was assessed using the Morris water maze, novel object recognition test, and fear conditioning test. Further experiments were also conducted to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsOur clinical data revealed a decrease in cognitive function among patients with CKD, accompanied by elevated plasma levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. A positive correlation between cytokine concentrations and serum creatinine levels, as well as a significant positive correlation with cognitive dysfunction, were observed. Correlation analyzes demonstrated that hippocampal cytokine levels were positively correlated with serum creatinine levels and cognitive dysfunction in CKD model mice. Furthermore, 20 mg/mL interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) significantly decreased HT22 cell activity in vitro. Further, HT22 cells treated with IL‐6 showed increased expression levels of toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) and myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88), thereby inducing the nuclear factor kappa‐B p65 inflammatory pathway and mitochondria‐dependent apoptosis. The CKD mouse model showed increased expression of TLR4 and cytokines in the hippocampus. TLR4 knockdown antagonized the IL‐6‐mediated pro‐inflammatory and pro‐apoptotic effects in HT22 cells. TLR4 knockdown in the CKD model mice decreased hippocampal inflammation and increased the number of neuron dendrites, thus ameliorated cognitive impairment.ConclusionThese results suggest that IL‐6 triggers TLR4 activation to induce neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in CKD, ultimately culminate in cognitive impairment.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by ongoing abnormalities in both the structure and function of the kidneys, persisting for more than 3 months, and potentially stemming from multiple factors. These abnormalities may include abnormal blood or urine composition, abnormal imaging examination, or unexplained glomeruar filtration rate (GFR) decreases (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2). 1 Over the past few decades, CKD has emerged as an escalating global public health concern, with a prevalence rate of 8%–15%. 2 Apart from diminishing quality of life and exacerbating pressures on healthcare resources, CKD imposes substantial economic burdens on both individuals and society. 3 Furthermore, a growing number of epidemiological and experimental studies have linked CKD to an elevated risk of cardiovascular diseases and mental health conditions such as anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment. 4 , 5 , 6 As such, it is imperative to delve into the intricate mechanisms driving the alterations induced by CKD in both the peripheral and central nervous systems.

In recent decades, the relationship between CKD and cognitive impairment has arisen as an extensively studied area of interest. Numerous studies have identified various pathways through which CKD may impact cognitive function, including uremic encephalopathy, neuroinflammation, metabolic disturbances, and vascular changes. 7 , 8 Clinical and experimental rodent studies have further revealed the critical involvement of the immune system and neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment in CKD. Moreover, the concurrence of morphological and functional alterations, such as ischemic cerebrovascular and white matter lesions, hippocampal atrophy, microbleeding events, and disruptions in synaptic plasticity, is widely acknowledged as contributing factors to cognitive impairment in patients. 9 , 10 , 11 Additionally, emerging evidence has highlighted a compelling link between blood‐brain barrier (BBB) disruption and cognitive impairment in animal models of CKD, with existing findings demonstrating a correlation between increased BBB permeability and renal dysfunction. 12 Studies have also suggested that inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‐α), interleukin 1 beta (IL‐1β), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) may play pivotal roles in BBB breakdown, subsequently contributing to neurodegenerative processes. 13

IL‐6 is a critical cytokine involved in the immune response, inflammatory regulation, and neurosystem modulation. 14 Recent studies have revealed that increased levels of IL‐6 in the brain precipitate neuronal damage, synaptic loss, and neuroinflammatory responses, culminating in cognitive decline. 15 Meanwhile, in patients with mild cognitive impairment, the levels of cerebrospinal fluid IL‐6 were significantly higher than those in controls without impairment. 16 Furthermore, studies have underscored the potential association between severe pulmonary infections and cognitive impairment, primarily attributed to the upregulation of systemic pro‐inflammatory mediators and cytokine‐induced perturbations of the BBB, concomitant with the manifestation of anxiety‐like behaviors in murine models. 17 More importantly, evidence suggests that the post‐peripheral administration of IL‐6 can breach the BBB, thus accessing the cerebrospinal fluid and brain parenchyma. 18 Hence, individuals with CKD may experience increased secretion of inflammatory factors due to chronic inflammation, coupled with the accumulation of inflammatory factors within the body, resulting in renal function damage. This, in turn, can trigger inflammatory cascades and neurodegenerative alterations within the central nervous system, ultimately lead to cognitive impairments.

Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) is a crucial immune receptor belonging to the TLR family which is widely expressed in immune cells, including monocytes and macrophages, and non‐immune cells, including renal tubular cells and cardiomyocytes. 19 , 20 Prior studies have shown that the activation of TLR4 triggers an inflammatory response and regulates the expression of various immune‐ and inflammation‐related genes through the activation of nuclear factor kappa‐B and mitogen‐activated protein kinase signaling pathways, promoting the activation and secretion of inflammatory factors by inflammatory cells. 21 In addition to immune responses, TLR4 participates in the regulation of various physiological and pathological processes, including apoptosis, cell proliferation, tissue repair, and neurodevelopment. 22 , 23 , 24 Particularly in the nervous system, the activation of TLR4 has been associated with neuronal damage, neuroinflammation, and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease. 25 , 26 For example, recent studies have indicated that safflower yellow can potentially treat Alzheimer's disease by inhibiting the interaction between TLR4 and myeloid differentiation protein‐2 (MD2), thus reducing the activation of microglia and Aβ aggregation in the hippocampus. 27 In addition, studies involving TLR4‐deficient mice have demonstrated a reduction in β2‐microglobulin‐induced cognitive decline by enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic function. 28 In summary, there is a close relationship between TLR4 and cognitive impairment, while TLR4 activation may negatively impact cognitive function.

In the present study, we investigated the release of pro‐inflammatory factors in patients with CKD as well as in mouse models in vivo, and further analyzed the correlation between CKD and cognitive impairment. We also explored the relationship between pro‐inflammatory factors and TLR4 expression. Our findings suggest that the elevated peripheral expression of IL‐6 induced by CKD results in the accumulation of pro‐inflammatory factors. This accumulation disrupts the BBB, triggering both inflammatory responses and upregulation of TLR4 within the central nervous system. These processes ultimately contribute to the cognitive decline.

METHODS

Animals

The study involved adult male C56/BL6 mice, aged between 10 and 12 weeks, and weighing 24 ± 1 g, sourced from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd in Beijing, China. Mice were kept in groups under specific‐pathogen‐free conditions, with environmental controls set to 22 ± 1°C for temperature, 55 ± 5% for humidity, and a 12‐h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7 a.m.). They had unrestricted access to food and water during the study. Ethical protocols approved by the Wenzhou Medical University Committee (approval number: WYYY‐AEC‐2022‐012), and aligned with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals were followed, with the efforts to minimize animal use.

Cognitive assessment and blood samples collection in CKD patients

This study protocol received approval from the Human Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (approval number: KY2022‐033). The research was carried out in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with stage 1 or 5 CKD for more than 3 months.

The criteria for exclusion encompassed individuals younger than 18 years, those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, active infections, uncontrolled heart failure, malignancies, a life expectancy of less than 6 months, or recent hospitalization within the past month. We used the computer to generate 100 random numbers for patients with CKD stages 1 and 5, respectively, with the smallest 50 being enrolled and the largest 50 not being enrolled. This generated two sets of random sequences, and we recruited patients in the order of the random sequences. All participants provided written informed consent. Detailed medical histories and medication information were collected from the participants’ medical records. Demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index, educational level, marital status, and occupational status, were also recorded. The data showed no significant differences (p> 0.05). Comorbidity information specifically related to diabetes and hypertension was obtained from the patients’ medical records. Laboratory parameters, including serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), hemoglobin, albumin concentrations, calcium, uric acid, blood β2 microglobulin, and cystatin C were collected. Blood samples of clinical uremia were collected from these patients to test the levels of the TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6.

Animal surgery of 5/6 nephrectomy (5/6 Nx)

The conventional 5/6 Nx procedure, involving the removal of the right kidney and resection of two‐thirds of the left kidney, was performed on adult male C57/BL6 mice. Mice were anesthetized with 3.0% isoflurane and 100% oxygen at an airflow rate of 1 L/min. After disinfection with povidone, a left laparotomy was conducted to expose the left kidney. The perihilar adipose tissue was then gently dissected to preserve an intact adrenal gland. The kidney artery bifurcation at the hilum was identified, and the upper branch was ligated with an 8‐0 polypropylene thread and a 6.5‐mm needle. The lower kidney pole was cauterized to a depth of 1 mm and subsequently sutured with non‐absorbable 4‐0 polyamide thread. After a recovery period of 7–10 days following left partial nephrectomy, a similar procedure was performed on the right kidney. This procedure involved exposing the right kidney, fully ligating the hilum, and removing the right kidney following the same steps as described earlier. Sham‐operated animals underwent a simple laparotomy. All animals received subcutaneous buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg) for analgesia, followed by daily anti‐inflammatory treatment for 7–10 days post‐surgery. The mice were euthanized 12 weeks after the surgical procedures, then blood and kidney tissues were collected for laboratory analysis. The severity of CKD was determined by assessing the serum creatinine levels in mice 1 week after the second surgery. In addition, renal histopathological assessments were performed in each experiment.

Adeno‐associated virus (AAV) vectors and vector delivery in vivo

Vigene Biosciences Inc. constructed AAV9 vectors targeting mouse TLR4 (Ad‐TLR4) and green fluorescent protein (Ad‐GFP). The viral titers were 5.94 × 1013 vg/mL for Ad‐TLR4 small interfering RNA (siRNA) and 1.5 × 1014 vg/mL for Ad‐GFP. The vectors were diluted with 0.9% saline. The vectors were diluted with 0.9% saline. To investigate the potential impact of hippocampal TLR4 on cognitive function, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6–8 per group) by random table: (1) Sham, (2) Sham + TLR4 siRNA, (3) CKD, and (4) CKD + TLR4 siRNA. In the Sham + TLR4 siRNA and CKD + TLR4 siRNA groups, mice received bilateral injections of 5 × 1011 vg/mL TLR4 siRNA vector in a 5 μL volume into the lateral cerebral ventricles. The positioning of the lateral ventricle starts from the midpoint of the interocular line, moves 3 mm vertically backwards from the interocular line, then moves about 2.0 mm laterally and about 3.5 mm downwards, entering the lateral ventricle. The injections were carried out using a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Guangdong, China) over 5 min, with a 5‐min wait period between injections. Mice in the Sham and CKD groups received an equivalent volume of the AAV‐GFP vector instead of AAV‐TLR4 siRNA. The body weights of the animals were recorded daily, and their general behavior was monitored throughout the study period. After a 3‐week post‐surgery recovery period, cognitive function tests were conducted as described below.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

The mice acclimated to the empty testing arena (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm, Techman Software Co., Ltd., Sichuan, China) for 5 min, 24 h before the experiment. On the first day, objects A and B were placed in the arenas, and the mice explored for 5 min. On the subsequent day, object B was replaced with object C, distinct from objects A and B. Mice explored the field for 5 min. On the third day, object C was replaced with object D, and the mice explored for 5 min. Behavior was recorded with a camera fixed 2 m above the arena. The frequency and timing of sniffing of each object (A, B, C, and D) were analyzed. The arena was cleaned with 75% alcohol between tests.

Morris water maze (MWM)

An opaque circular water tank (80 cm diameter, Techman Software Co., Ltd., Sichuan, China) with spatial cues was filled with water (23 ± 1°C) mixed with milk powder. The pool was divided into four quadrants, each with a starting position. Mice were trained for 5 days and daily sessions consisted of four trials with a submerged platform 1 cm below the surface. Mice swim in each trial for 60 s. If the mouse reached the platform or was guided to it after 60 s, it was returned to its cage for a 20‐min drying period. Escape latency was recorded. A probe test was performed at 1 and 48 h post‐training. Mice were placed in a quadrant opposite to where the platform was removed. A camera 2 m above the field recorded the movement. The crossing time and times in the target zone were recorded using video tracking system analysis software (Techman Software Co., Ltd., Sichuan, China).

Fear conditioning test (FCT)

The conditioning experiment took place in a 33 cm × 33 cm × 33 cm chamber with whiteboard walls, a speaker, and a grid floor. Mice explored the chamber freely for 2 min. Then, they were exposed to a 20 s tone (CS) at 80 dB and 2000 Hz, followed by a 2 s foot shock (US; 0.95 mA) through the grid floor. This sequence was repeated five times with 60 s intervals between each pairing. Contextual fear conditioning occurred on the second day, with each mouse spending 5 min in the chamber, during which freezing behavior in response to the context and stimulus‐free periods was recorded. On the third day, mice were placed back to the same chamber, but with a green wall. The freezing behavior of mice in the altered context was recorded. On the fourth day, after an initial 2 min exploration period, mice were exposed to the same CS tones used in training without foot shock. Freezing responses were recorded.

Western blotting assay

Mouse hippocampal tissues were homogenized. Protein was extracted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime). The homogenates were spun at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 20 min. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce, Massachusetts, America). A total of 40 μg protein was denatured in 5× loading buffer at 100°C for 10 min. The samples were then loaded onto 10% or 12% sodium dodecyl sulphate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and separated for 60 min at 25°C. Proteins were transferred to a 0.22 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, Massachusetts, America) for 90 min at 4°C. After blocking with 5% non‐fat milk for 120 min at 25°C, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse or rabbit monoclonal primary antibodies. Following three washes with tris buffered saline with Tween 20 at 25°C, the membrane was incubated for 1 h at 25°C with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse secondary antibodies (1:5000). Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Applygen, Beijing, China) and imaged using a computerized chemiluminescent gel image analysis system (Bio‐Rad, California, America). Band intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (Java 1.8.0_345, Media Cybernetic, Maryland, America), using β‐actin serving as an internal control. All the experiments were repeated at least in triplicate.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of hippocampal inflammatory cytokines

After completion of the behavioral tests, the mice were anesthetized and euthanized. Hippocampus tissues were carefully isolated, homogenized, and centrifuged at 3000 × g at 4°C for 15 min. The resultant supernatants were employed to measure TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels with mouse‐specific ELISA kits, following the manufacturer's instructions. All the experiments were repeated at least in triplicate.

Serum creatinine of mice

After completion of the behavioral tests, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Blood samples were collected. Serum creatinine levels were assessed using an enzymatic method (CREP2; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). All the experiments were repeated at least in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence staining

HT22 cells were cultured on glass slides in 12‐well plates. After washing with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Permeabilization was achieved by exposing the cells to 0.5% Triton/PBS, followed by incubation with 3% bovine serum albumin. Cells were washed with PBS after each step. The cells were then subjected to overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies, including TLR4 (1:100), MyD88 (1:100), p65 (1:100), and p‐p65 (1:100) at 4°C. After PBS washing, cells were exposed to DyLight 488 or 594 secondary antibodies (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were stained with 4',6‐diamidino2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (0.5 µg/mL, CST) for 10 min. To preserve the specimens, glass slides were sealed with an anti‐fluorescence quenching agent, and clear acrylic nail polish was applied to seal the edges. The images were taken with a fluorescence microscope.

Golgi staining

After being anesthetized and intracardially perfusion, brains were carefully sliced into 5 μm sections using a vibrating microtome (Leica, VT1000S, Shanghai, China) and immersed in the Golgi dye solution. Specimens were left to incubate in the dark for 3 days, then moved to a 1% silver nitrate solution for 3 days. The resulting images were examined using a microscope (Nikon, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

Data presented in graphs are expressed as means ± standard error of the mean, unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA, www.graphpad.com). Student's t‐test was used for two‐group comparisons, and two‐way analysis of variance with Bonferroni post‐tests for four‐group comparisons. Pearson's correlation analysis was used to analyze the correlations between cytokine levels, plasma creatinine levels, and Mini Mental State Examinatio (MMSE) scores. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Increased expression levels of inflammatory factors in CKD5 patients are positively associated with cognitive impairment

This study investigated the association between CKD and cognitive impairment. Fifty patients with stage 5 CKD (CKD5) and 50 patients with stage 1 CKD (CKD1) treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou, China) were enrolled. No statistically significant differences were observed in the key demographic characteristics, including age, sex, etiology, employment, body mass index, educational level, and comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, between the two cohorts (Table S1). As anticipated, CKD5 patients exhibited higher serum creatinine levels and lower eGFR than those with CKD1, indicating more severe renal impairment (Figure 1A). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining further confirmed greater kidney damage in CKD5 patients (Figure 1B). Additionally, the levels of inflammatory factors, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, were significantly elevated in CKD5 individuals compared to CKD1 patients (Figure 1C). To verify the relationship between renal function and cognitive impairment, we evaluated the MMSE scores. Our findings demonstrated notably diminished MMSE scores in CKD5 patients compared with CKD1 patients, suggesting a higher risk of cognitive impairment in the CKD5 group (Figure 1D). More importantly, our findings revealed a positive correlation between serum creatinine and cytokine levels, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. Conversely, eGFR and MMSE scores were negatively correlated with cytokine levels (Figure 1E and 1F). These results underscored the positive association between inflammatory factors such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, and cognitive dysfunction in CKD patients. Specifically, CKD5 patients exhibited higher production of inflammatory factors, which were positively associated with cognitive impairment.

Elevated levels of inflammatory factors are associated with cognitive dysfunction in the CKD model

To further investigate the relationship between CKD and cognitive impairment, we constructed a 5/6 Nx mouse model, with control group mice undergoing sham surgery. Our results revealed that 5/6 Nx mice exhibited elevated serum creatinine levels compared to sham group mice (Figure 2A). H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining further confirmed severe kidney damage in the 5/6 Nx mice (Figure 2B). Furthermore, the 5/6 Nx group mice showed significant increased levels of brain cytokine, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, in comparison to the sham group mice (Figure 2C). Notably, serum creatinine levels were positively correlated with cytokine levels (Figure 2D). The cognitive behaviors of the mice were examined using the NORT, MWM, and FCT. In the NORT, there were no significant differences in the recognition indices for objects A and B, A and C between the groups. Interestingly, the sham group demonstrated heightened interest in object D, as indicated by an increased recognition index. The discrimination index of the sham group displayed a marked increase compared with that of the 5/6 Nx group, confirming impaired object detection memory in the latter (Figure 2E). In the MWM, we allowed the mice 5 days to acclimatize to the environment and conducted 2 days of testing. During the initial 4 days, no significant difference in escape latency was observed between the two groups. However, on the fifth day, the sham group exhibited shorter escape latencies than the 5/6 Nx group. In addition, during the probe test conducted after 1 h, no significant difference was observed in the number of annulus crossings between the two groups. Nevertheless, during the probe test performed after 48 h, which assessed reference spatial learning and memory, the 5/6 Nx mice exhibited a decreased mean annulus crossing time compared with the sham group (Figure 2F). Furthermore, in the FCT, after 1 day of training involving sound cues followed by electrical stimulation, and 1 day of contextual training using green paper to alter the environment, both groups displayed similar freezing times on day 3. However, on day 4, when subjected to the sound cue without electrical stimulation, 5/6 Nx mice exhibited fewer freezing times than the sham group (Figure 2G). In summary, our results suggest that the CKD mouse model exhibits high cytokine expression levels in the hippocampus and cognitive impairment.

IL‐6 treatment activates the inflammatory pathway and induces cell apoptosis via TLR4 in vitro

To ascertain which inflammatory factor plays the most essential role in inducing cognitive impairment in CKD, we assessed the cell viability of HT22 cells exposed to varying concentrations of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. Interestingly, the HT22 cell viability displayed noticeable differences at concentrations of 400 pg/mL for IL‐1β and TNF‐α (Figure 3A and B). Conversely, even a concentration as low as 20 pg/mL of IL‐6 led to diminished cell viability (Figure 3C). Consequently, we inferred that IL‐6 may be a critical cytokine responsible for cognitive impairment in CKD. Subsequently, we cultured HT22 cells with IL‐6 concentrations of 0, 20, and 100 pg/mL, revealing elevated messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels of TLR4 and MyD88 in cells exposed to 20 and 100 pg/mL IL‐6 compared to those without IL‐6 treatment (Figure 3D). Western blot analysis also confirmed the increased expression levels of TLR4 and MyD88 in HT22 cells treated with IL‐6 (Figure 3E–G). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed increased expression of TLR4 and MyD88 in these groups (Figure 3H). Western blot analysis further revealed elevated levels of p65 and p‐p65 in the 20 and 100 pg/mL IL‐6 groups, respectively (Figure 3I,J). Immunofluorescence staining indicated that the expression levels of p65 and p‐p65 were elevated in the nucleus. Furthermore, exposure to higher concentrations of IL‐6 upregulated the expression of apoptotic genes, including Bcl‐2, Bax, FL‐Casp3, and CL‐Casp3 (Figure 3L–O). In summary, these findings suggest that IL‐6 promotes the expression of TLR4/MyD88, activating inflammatory signaling pathways and apoptosis in vitro.

TLR4 knockdown antagonizes the activation of inflammatory pathways and cell apoptosis in vitro

To validate whether a reduction in TLR4 could effectively inhibit the activation of inflammatory pathways and apoptosis, we employed siRNA knockdown of TLR4 in IL‐6‐treated HT22 cells. Subsequent evaluation of TLR4 mRNA expression confirmed the successful knockdown (Figure 4A). Further, western blot analysis revealed a marked reduction in TLR4 and MyD88 levels in the TLR4 siRNA group compared with those in the control and negative control (NC) groups (Figure 4B–D). Immunofluorescence staining further showed decreased TLR4 and MyD88 levels in the TLR4 siRNA group compared to those in the other groups (Figure 4E). Furthermore, the levels of p65 and p‐p65 were diminished in the TLR4 siRNA group, and the p‐p65/p65 ratio was lower than that in the other groups (Figure 4E–G). Immunofluorescence staining of p65 and p‐p65 showed reduced expression levels of p65 and p‐p65 in the nucleus (Figure 4H). Moreover, Bcl‐2, Bax, FL‐Casp3, and CL‐Casp3 levels were lower in the TLR4 siRNA group (Figure 4I–L). Our findings support the conclusion that TLR4 knockdown effectively suppresses MyD88 and p65 expression, thereby inhibiting inflammatory and apoptotic signaling pathways in HT22 cells.

TLR4 knockdown inhibits hippocampal inflammatory responses in CKD mice

To further investigate whether TLR4 influences inflammatory responses in mice, we administered intracerebroventricular injections of AAV‐TLR4 to knockdown TLR4 in 5/6 Nx mice. Western blot analysis further revealed that TLR4 expression was higher in the 5/6 Nx group than in the sham group. In the AVV‐TLR4 siRNA (AAV‐siTLR4) group, the TLR4 level was notably reduced compared to that in the AAV‐control (AAV‐con) group, which confirmed the successful knockdown of TLR4 in CKD mice (Figure 5A,B). We subsequently measured the expression levels of inflammatory factors in the hippocampus of mice. As expected, the expression levels of inflammatory factors, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, in the 5/6 Nx‐con group were notably elevated compared to the sham group. And IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α expression levels of the AAV‐siTLR4 groups were decreased compared with the AAV‐con group (Figure 5C–E). In summary, our findings indicate that the inhibition of TLR4 can effectively mitigate inflammatory responses in mice.

TLR4 knockdown rescues CKD‐induced cognitive dysfunction and increases the dendrites of neurons

After establishing the TLR4‐knockout mouse model, we further tested the cognitive function of the mice using behavioral tests. In the NORT, there was no difference in the recognition index for objects A and B, or A and C between the AAV‐con and AAV‐siTLR4 groups (Figure 6A,B). Similarly, no difference was observed in the recognition index of the AAV‐con group for objects A and D. However, the AAV‐siTLR4 group mice had a higher recognition index for object D than for object A (Figure 6C). On day 4, the discrimination index of the AAV‐siTLR4 group was higher than that of the AAV‐control group (Figure 6D). In the MWM, the escape latency time showed no difference during the first 5 days when the mice were allowed to adapt to the environment (Figure 6E). In the probe test performed after 1 h, the mean annulus crossing times showed no significant difference between the two groups (Figure 6F). However, in the probe test after 48 h, the mean annulus crossing times of AAV siTLR4 group were dramatically increased compared to the AAV‐con group (Figure 6G). In the FCT, freezing times did not differ after context alteration. On day 4, the AAV‐siTLR4 group had a longer freezing time than the AAV‐con group (Figure 6H). These results proved that knockdown of TLR4 improved cognitive function. Finally, we employed a Golgi staining assay to test for changes in the dendritic spines of hippocampal neurons. We observed that the 5/6 Nx group had fewer dendritic spines than the sham‐operated group. Treatment with AAV‐TLR4 siRNA resulted in a higher number of dendritic spines than in the AAV‐con group (Figure 6I). In summary, inhibition of TLR4 alleviates cognitive impairment induced by CKD in mice, increases the number of neuronal dendrites, and reduces neuronal degeneration.

DISCUSSION

An increasing number of studies have highlighted CKD as a significant risk factor for cognitive impairment. 29 , 30 While the determinants of CKD‐associated cognitive impairment in humans are multifactorial and include cerebrovascular disease, renal anemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, dialysis disequilibrium, and the accumulation of uremic toxins, the presence of inflammatory responses within both the peripheral and central nervous systems is one of the most prominent pathogenic features. 31 Neuroinflammation disrupts the morphology and function of brain structures, particularly the hippocampus, leading to disturbances in the homeostasis of neurons, glial cells, and microglia, and an imbalance in their regulation. In our study, serum creatinine level and eGFR served as indicators of renal function. CKD5 patients were also found to have higher serum creatinine levels and a lower eGFR filtration rate, indicating kidney damage. H&E and Masson's trichrome staining confirmed that kidney function damage was more severe in CKD5 patients than in those with CKD1. Additionally, the levels of serum creatinine and eGFR were correlated with the levels of inflammations (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α). The patients’ scores of the MMSE, a screening tool for cognitive impairment, displayed negative correlations with these cytokine levels. These findings were validated in a CKD mouse model induced by 5/6 Nx. Compared with the sham group mice, 5/6 Nx mice had remarkably increased serum creatinine and decreased inflammatory factors levels, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. H&E and Masson staining also revealed renal dysfunction in 5/6 nephrectomized mice. Based on the behavioral test results, 5/6 Nx mice displayed cognitive impairments compared to the sham group mice. Evidence has shown that CKD may lead to cognitive impairment. However, the underlying mechanisms by which CKD contributes to cognitive impairment remain unclear.

Studies have demonstrated that three inflammatory cytokines, namely TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6, play pivotal roles in disrupting the blood‐brain barrier, contributing to neurodegeneration. 13 Importantly, a recent clinical study showed that elevated IL‐6 levels were associated with cognitive function in elderly individuals in southern Italy. 32 In addition, a study involving 899 patients found that the expression of inflammatory factors was elevated in CKD patients. 33 Interestingly, in our study, we observed that a concentration of only 20 pg/mL IL‐6 effectively reduced the viability of HT22 cells. In contrast, nearly 400 pg/mL of TNF‐α and IL‐1β were required to induce a statistically significant decrease in HT22 cell activity. Moreover, both in CKD patients and CKD model mice in the present study, although the levels of IL‐6, TNF‐α and IL‐1β increased, they never surpassed 50 pg/mL. Therefore, we identified IL‐6 as a key factor in the association between CKD and cognitive function.

It has previously been reported that TLR4 is well‐known for its role in mediating innate immune responses and behavioral functions, including cognitive performance. The activated TLR signaling pathway interacts with the junction protein MyD88 in the toll‐interleukin 1 receptor domain. 34 , 35 We speculate that the increased expression of IL‐6 in the hippocampus of patients with CKD promotes the overexpression of the TLR4/MyD88 pathway, ultimately leading to cognitive impairment. To validate this hypothesis, HT22 cells were treated with IL‐6 at concentrations of 0, 20, and 100 pg/mL. The IL‐6‐treated group showed increased expression of TLR4 and MyD88. The levels of p‐65 and p‐p65, which are associated with the inflammatory response, were also increased in IL‐6‐treated HT22 cells. IL‐6 treatment induced the upregulation of apoptotic genes (Bcl‐2, CL‐casp3, and FL‐casp3) and increased the responsiveness of an anti‐apoptotic gene (Bax). Furthermore, TLR4 inhibition in HT22 cells was inhibited by TLR4 siRNA, as was TLR4 and MyD88 expression.

Moreover, the IL‐6 expression level was decreased in 5/6 Nx mice injected with AAV‐TLR4 into the lateral ventricle. The AAV‐siTLR4 group further showed improved cognitive function compared to AAV‐con mice. In addition, the AAV‐TLR4 group displayed a higher density of dendritic spines than the control group. This study has some limitations. Firstly, there is a need for further investigation into the mechanisms underlying BBB damage. Additionally, while we have proposed IL‐6 as a key factor contributing to cognitive dysfunction in CKD, it is essential to acknowledge the potential influence of other contributing factors.

In summary, we confirmed that CKD patients with renal dysfunction show increased peripheral inflammatory responses, particularly elevated expression levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐6. Increased IL‐6 levels compromise the BBB and activate the TLR4/MyD88 pathway, leading to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, ultimately resulting in cognitive impairment.
