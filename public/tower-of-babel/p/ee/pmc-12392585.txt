Brown tumor mimicking giant cell tumor in primary hyperparathyroidism: a case report
Authors: Luljeta Z. Abdullahu, Brunilda I. Haxhiu, Nimet H. Orqusha, Naser A. Gjonbalaj, Armend M. Jashari, Fisnik I. Kurshumliu, Vjollca I. Dedushaj Fazliu, Basri Z. Lenjani, Shemsi R. Veseli, Ilir N. Kurtishi, Ylli R. Kaçiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDifferentiating brown tumor due to primary hyperparathyroidism from a giant cell tumor remains a clinical challenge. Misdiagnosis may lead to inappropriate oncologic treatment and unnecessary surgical interventions.Case presentationWe report the case of a 52-year-old Caucasian white woman of Albanian ethnicity with multiple osteolytic bone lesions and a history of repeated orthopedic surgeries over 5 years. Initial histopathology suggested aneurysmal bone cyst and giant cell tumor, while imaging findings were interpreted as metastatic bone disease, and led to chemotherapy and radiotherapy in the absence of a confirmed primary malignancy. The patient was later referred to our Nuclear Medicine Unit, where a repeat bone scintigraphy indicated metabolic bone disease. A full biochemical evaluation revealed severe hypercalcemia and elevated parathyroid hormone. Parathyroid scintigraphy confirmed a hyperfunctioning parathyroid adenoma. Retrospective review of previous pathology reports supported the diagnosis of brown tumor. The patient underwent successful parathyroidectomy. Biochemical parameters normalized within 3 months postoperatively, and no complications such as hypocalcemia were observed.ConclusionThis case illustrates the importance of early consideration of primary hyperparathyroidism in the differential diagnosis of multifocal lytic bone lesions. Accurate diagnosis requires a multidisciplinary approach combining imaging, histopathology, and biochemical evaluation to prevent misdiagnosis and overtreatment.

Introduction

Giant cell tumors (GCTs), or osteoclastomas, are benign but locally aggressive primary bone tumors composed of multinucleated giant cells and mononuclear stromal cells. They usually present in the third or fourth decade of life and rarely undergo malignant transformation (< 10%) [1–4].

Paget’s disease of bone (PDB) is a metabolic disorder characterized by abnormal bone remodeling and commonly affects the pelvis, spine, and skull. Tc-99 m-methylene diphosphonate (Tc-99m-MDP) scintigraphy aids in assessing disease burden [5, 6].

Brown tumors are osteolytic manifestations of prolonged elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels in primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), commonly affecting the pelvis, ribs, clavicle, and mandible [7, 8].

Case report

Findings

We describe the case of a 52-year-old Caucasian white woman of Albanian ethnicity who experienced multiple skeletal lesions over a 5-year period. In early 2017, she underwent orthopedic surgery for a lesion in the distal left tibia, diagnosed histopathologically as an aneurysmal bone cyst. After 2–3 months, a second lesion appeared in the proximal right tibia and was diagnosed as a giant cell tumor (GCT/osteoclastoma). In 2019, recurrence at the same site in the proximal right tibia led to sequestration, curettage, and cortical reconstruction. Histopathological reassessment of the excised material raised three differential diagnoses: GCT, brown tumor due to primary hyperparathyroidism, and metaphyseal cortical defect (nonossifying fibroma) (Fig. 1). Treatment

After the diagnostic confirmation of primary hyperparathyroidism, the patient was initiated on a preparatory regimen including subcutaneous denosumab, oral calcium supplements, and cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) to correct metabolic imbalances prior to surgery. Denosumab was administered to reduce bone resorption and prevent further skeletal complications [20, 21].

Once her biochemical profile was stabilized, the patient underwent parathyroidectomy. The surgical procedure was uneventful, and no immediate postoperative complications such as hypocalcemia crises or hungry bone syndrome were observed [11].

Outcome and follow-up

Following treatment with denosumab, cholecalciferol (vitamin D3), and calcium supplementation, the patient underwent definitive parathyroidectomy. The procedure resulted in resolution of symptoms and normalization of biochemical markers. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of parathyroid adenoma. Serum PTH and calcium levels improved within days after surgery, with complete normalization achieved in 2–3 months. No episodes of postoperative hypocalcemia were observed. The patient was followed for 18 months by endocrinology and rheumatology teams without any reported complications.

Discussion

Histologically, giant cell tumors can resemble other bone lesions, including brown tumors associated with hyperparathyroidism, Paget’s disease, aneurysmal bone cysts, and reparative giant cell granulomas [5, 6, 14]. This overlap presents diagnostic challenges, requiring comprehensive evaluation beyond biopsy. Accurate diagnosis is critical owing to similarities in pathology and imaging and necessitates multidisciplinary collaboration [7, 8]. This case demonstrates the consequences of relying on histopathology and imaging without considering metabolic bone disorders. However, only brown tumors (BTs) are associated with hyperparathyroidism, making serum PTH and calcium crucial in the differential. Misdiagnosis led to overtreatment with chemotherapy and radiation.

Brown tumors, which may be multicentric, often mimic osteoblastic lesions with intense uptake on bone scintigraphy and osteolytic bone metastases, frequently the primary differential diagnosis [8–10, 15–17]. Polyostotic involvement and craniofacial and pelvic bone lesions raised suspicion for Paget’s disease, while biochemical, clinical, pathological, and imaging findings supported primary hyperparathyroidism, especially considering characteristic bone scan patterns [4]. It is important to note that magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in cases of primary hyperparathyroidism may present with multiple osteolytic bone lesions and marrow involvement that closely resemble metastatic disease. Hoshi et al. [8] described similar findings in a patient with brown tumors, underscoring the diagnostic challenge when radiological features are nonspecific.

Persistent elevated PTH resulted in nephrocalcinosis, renal failure, osteoporosis, and fractures, which are common clinical manifestations of primary hyperparathyroidism [18].

Zoledronic acid and bisphosphonates effectively reduce bone resorption and modestly improve clinical outcomes in PDB. In our case, despite reductions in alkaline phosphatase and calcium, zoledronic acid did not significantly improve 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels, PTH, bone density, fracture prevention, or quality of life [12].

Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody used for osteoporosis, administered for 3 months, showed superior clinical and biochemical improvements compared with zoledronic acid and chemotherapy [19]. Preoperative treatment with denosumab D3 led to marked improvements in bone markers, supporting favorable surgical outcomes in primary hyperparathyroidism with parathyroid adenoma [12]. The 25-hydroxyvitamin D coadministration was crucial, as deficiency can worsen hypercalcemia [20, 21].

Chemotherapy had no observable benefit in clinical or biochemical outcomes, and no randomized controlled trials currently guide its use in this context.

Radiotherapy is effective in halting tumor progression at doses between 40 Gy and 70 Gy [10]. Comprehensive evaluation of patients with bone lesions should include total body bone scans, DEXA scans, parathyroid scintigraphy, serum PTH, calcium, and other relevant biochemical markers to assess disease burden and predict outcomes [22].

This case underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and tailored therapy for giant cell-rich bone lesions.

Our patient’s solitary parathyroid adenoma was diagnosed late. Surgery remains the definitive treatment for primary hyperparathyroidism, with solitary adenomas being the most frequent cause (80–85%) [22].

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge in differentiating brown tumors secondary to primary hyperparathyroidism from giant cell tumors of bone. A multidisciplinary approach combining clinical, biochemical, radiological, and histopathological assessments is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. Early identification and surgical treatment of the underlying parathyroid pathology can lead to significant regression of brown tumors and prevent unnecessary aggressive orthopedic interventions. Awareness of this entity is particularly important in regions where access to advanced diagnostic modalities may be limited.

Learning points: our patient has experienced severe consequences due to wrong diagnosis for many years. When it comes to giant cell tumors in a histopathological preparation, it is necessary to take into account other possible entities in the framework of the differential diagnosis, always completing them with laboratory analyses with the parameters of bone markers and additional imaging.
