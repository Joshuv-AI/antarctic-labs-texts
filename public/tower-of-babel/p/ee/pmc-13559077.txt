Development and bootstrap-based internal validation of a dynamic model for predicting acute kidney injury after day-3 reassessment in children with severe community-acquired pneumonia
Authors: Jing Dong, Jing Yue, Hui Zhao, Ling Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren with severe community-acquired pneumonia (SCAP) are at risk of acute kidney injury (AKI), but creatinine-based recognition may be delayed, and existing pediatric AKI models are not SCAP-specific or trajectory-based. We developed a model for day-3 reassessment of subsequent AKI risk.MethodsThis single-center retrospective prediction model development study included children aged 1 month to 18 years with SCAP, defined by at least 1 major or 2 minor Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society (PIDS)/Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) severity criteria. Eligible patients had baseline and day-3 blood counts, C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, and serial creatinine measurements. Children with chronic kidney disease or congenital urinary anomalies, hospital-acquired or ventilator-associated pneumonia, transfer after >48 hours of treatment, major immunosuppression or chronic liver disease, missing core variables, or AKI before the day-3 index time were excluded. Incident AKI after day 3 was defined by Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) serum creatinine criteria. Candidate predictors were prespecified based on clinical relevance, prior evidence, availability, and sample-size constraints, with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) used as an auxiliary stability assessment. Bootstrap-based internal validation used 1,000 resamples.ResultsAmong 454 children [median age, 22 months; 56.4% male; 21.4% with Pediatric Critical Illness Score (PCIS) ≤70], 94 (20.7%) developed incident AKI. The 9-parameter model included age, PCIS ≤70, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive support, vancomycin-piperacillin/tazobactam exposure, fluid overload >10%, baseline creatinine, ΔPLR, and CAR clearance rate (CARc). The apparent area under the curve (AUC) was 0.872 (95% confidence interval: 0.834–0.910), the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.857, the corrected calibration slope was 0.940, and the corrected Brier score was 0.095. A 10% threshold yielded 90.4% sensitivity, 55.0% specificity, and 95.7% negative predictive value; a 30% threshold yielded 70.2% sensitivity, 80.3% specificity, and 48.2% positive predictive value.ConclusionsDynamic inflammatory changes improved day-3 AKI risk prediction within the development cohort. The model and proposed thresholds require independent external validation before bedside use.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Incident acute kidney injury (AKI) developed in 20.7% of children with severe community-acquired pneumonia (SCAP) after the day-3 inpatient assessment.

• A dynamic prediction nomogram incorporating early inflammatory trajectories and core clinical variables achieved an apparent area under the curve (AUC) of 0.872 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.857 within the development cohort.

What is known and what is new?

• AKI is a severe, life-threatening complication in pediatric SCAP. Current risk assessments typically rely on static admission baselines or single-time-point biomarkers, which often fail to capture the rapidly evolving nature of severe infections.

• Early dynamic changes in systemic inflammation over the first 72 hours—specifically the trajectory of the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio and the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio—provide significant incremental predictive value for subsequent AKI beyond standard baseline clinical models.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The nomogram provides a structured approach to day-3 AKI risk reassessment using routinely available clinical and laboratory data. A 10% predicted-risk threshold achieved 90.4% sensitivity and may support enhanced renal surveillance, whereas a 30% threshold achieved 80.3% specificity and may prompt a more focused review of fluid management and nephrotoxic medication exposure.

• The proposed thresholds should be used only as exploratory risk-stratification aids. They should not independently determine fluid or antimicrobial treatment, and external validation is required before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Background

Pediatric community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a major cause of hospitalization and acute respiratory compromise worldwide, despite progress in vaccination, antimicrobial therapy, and supportive care. Global analyses have shown that the morbidity and mortality burden of childhood pneumonia has declined over recent decades, but pneumonia continues to account for a substantial number of illnesses and deaths in young children (1). Severe community-acquired pneumonia (SCAP) is clinically heterogeneous and may rapidly progress from lung infection to hypoxemia, respiratory failure, sepsis, circulatory instability, and multi-organ dysfunction. In children requiring intensive care, severe CAP is frequently accompanied by extrapulmonary organ injury, which makes early organ-risk stratification an important part of clinical management (2).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is an important and potentially preventable complication in hospitalized and critically ill children. Pediatric AKI is associated with prolonged hospitalization, greater need for intensive care, and increased mortality, while recognition based on serum creatinine may occur only after kidney function has already deteriorated (3). These limitations have prompted the development of electronic health record-based prediction models intended to identify children at risk before conventional diagnostic criteria are fulfilled.

Several pediatric AKI prediction models have demonstrated that routinely collected clinical data can provide useful early risk estimates. Sanchez-Pinto and Khemani developed a seven-variable model using electronic health record data from the first 12 hours of pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission to predict AKI within 72 hours; the model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.84 in the development cohort and AUCs of 0.81 and 0.86 in two validation cohorts (4). Wang et al. subsequently developed separate models for intensive care unit (ICU) and non-ICU pediatric inpatients using age, medication exposures, platelet count, red blood cell distribution width, serum phosphorus, transaminases, and selected physiologic variables. Discrimination was more modest, with c-statistics of 0.74 in ICU patients and 0.69 in non-ICU patients (5). More recently, Zhang et al. developed a real-time ensemble model combining static characteristics with daily updated vital signs, laboratory results, and medication data. The model predicted AKI within 24 hours with areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.851 in internal validation and 0.869 in external validation (6). Although these studies established the feasibility of pediatric AKI prediction, they were developed in heterogeneous general inpatient or PICU populations, relied on broad electronic health record feature sets, and were not designed specifically for children with SCAP or for interpretation at a standardized 72-hour reassessment point.

Evidence specific to pneumonia is more limited and is derived predominantly from adults. In an adult CAP cohort, Chen et al. developed a nomogram incorporating cystatin C, albumin, white blood cell count, CURB-65 score, and acute respiratory failure, achieving an AUC of 0.870 and a Brier score of 0.129 (7). This model supports the relevance of inflammatory burden, disease severity, and renal biomarkers in pneumonia-associated AKI. However, its predictors were primarily admission or early single-time-point measurements, the cohort consisted largely of older adults, and the model did not assess whether short-term inflammatory trajectories improved prediction. Differences in renal physiology, comorbidity burden, pneumonia severity assessment, and treatment patterns also limit direct application of an adult CAP model to pediatric SCAP.

Children with SCAP represent a clinically distinct high-risk population in whom systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability, nephrotoxic antimicrobial exposure, and fluid accumulation may evolve concurrently during the first several days of hospitalization. Nephrotoxin-focused surveillance has reduced AKI in hospitalized children, underscoring the potential value of identifying modifiable risk before overt renal dysfunction develops (8). Nevertheless, existing pediatric AKI models have not specifically integrated SCAP-related severity, treatment exposure, fluid overload, baseline renal function, and serial inflammatory measurements within a single clinically interpretable model.

Inflammatory biomarkers are increasingly used to support severity assessment in pediatric CAP. Conventional markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin are associated with disease severity, although no single biomarker provides sufficient risk discrimination (9). Composite indices derived from routine blood tests may capture complementary components of the host response. Platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) reflects the balance between platelet-related inflammatory activation and lymphocyte depletion, whereas C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) integrates acute-phase inflammation with hypoalbuminemia. Pediatric pneumonia studies have reported associations of CAR and hematologic inflammatory ratios with sepsis, pneumonia severity, and intensive care-level illness (10,11). However, these studies have predominantly evaluated measurements obtained at admission or a single early time point.

Accordingly, the remaining gap is not simply the absence of another static AKI score, but the lack of a pediatric SCAP-specific model that evaluates whether early inflammatory trajectories add predictive information beyond clinical severity, treatment exposure, fluid balance, and baseline renal function. Changes in PLR and CAR from admission to the day-3 assessment may distinguish resolving inflammation from persistent or worsening systemic illness that is not fully represented by admission values. The present study therefore evaluated ΔPLR and CAR clearance rate as prespecified dynamic predictors of incident AKI occurring after the day-3 reassessment.

Objective

This study aimed to develop and perform bootstrap-based internal validation of a multivariable prognostic prediction model for incident AKI after the day-3 assessment in children with SCAP, incorporating routinely available clinical characteristics, treatment-related exposures, fluid overload, baseline renal function, and early changes in PLR and CAR. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0468/rc).

Methods

Study design and ethics

This was a single-center retrospective multivariable prognostic prediction model development study with bootstrap-based internal validation and optimism correction. The model was developed to estimate the probability of incident AKI occurring after the day-3 laboratory assessment among children hospitalized with SCAP, using clinical characteristics, treatment-related exposures, fluid balance, baseline renal function, and early dynamic changes in inflammatory markers. Comprehensive clinical and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic medical records (EMRs), laboratory information systems, and pediatric intensive care flowsheets of Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital for patients hospitalized between January 2019 and December 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital. Because this research utilized pre-existing, de-identified clinical data without imposing any additional interventions, the requirement for informed consent from the patients or their legal guardians was waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study population

The study cohort was identified from the institutional EMR system between January 2019 and December 2025. Potentially eligible cases were screened using ICD-10 codes for CAP, together with PICU admission records, mechanical ventilation orders, oxygen therapy records, and respiratory failure diagnoses. To reduce misclassification, all candidate records were reviewed by two independent clinical researchers to confirm the diagnosis of SCAP, eligibility criteria, and the temporal sequence between inflammatory marker assessment and AKI onset. Disagreements were resolved by a senior pediatric intensivist.

Children were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (I) age between 1 month and 18 years at hospital admission; (II) diagnosis of SCAP at admission, operationally defined as the presence of at least one major criterion or at least two minor criteria adapted from the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society (PIDS)/Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) pediatric CAP severity criteria (12). Major criteria included invasive mechanical ventilation, fluid-refractory shock requiring vasoactive support, an acute requirement for non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation, or hypoxemia requiring an inspired oxygen concentration or oxygen-flow level exceeding that routinely deliverable in a general pediatric ward. Minor criteria included age-adjusted tachypnea, apnea, increased work of breathing, a PaO2/FiO2 ratio <250, multilobar pulmonary infiltrates, altered mental status, hypotension, pleural effusion, a relevant comorbid condition, or otherwise unexplained metabolic acidosis. Only criteria documented at admission or within the first 24 hours were used for SCAP classification. For infants younger than 3 months, SCAP was adjudicated by two pediatric clinicians using the same prespecified physiologic and organ-support criteria because the PIDS/IDSA guideline was developed for children older than 3 months; (III) hospital stay longer than 48 hours; (IV) availability of complete blood count, CRP, serum albumin, and serum creatinine within 24 hours after admission and within the day-3 window, defined as 48–96 hours after admission, allowing calculation of PLR, CAR, ΔPLR, and CAR clearance rate (CARc); and (V) at least two serum creatinine measurements after admission, including one baseline measurement within 24 hours and at least one follow-up measurement within 7 days or before discharge, enabling AKI ascertainment according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria (13).

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (I) documented chronic kidney disease, congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract, end-stage kidney disease, maintenance dialysis, or previous kidney transplantation (14); (II) AKI present before admission or occurring before the day-3 laboratory assessment; (III) hospital-acquired pneumonia or ventilator-associated pneumonia; (IV) transfer from another hospital after more than 48 hours of inpatient treatment for the current pneumonia episode; (V) cardiac arrest, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or an irreversibly moribund state within 48 hours before admission; (VI) primary immunodeficiency, active malignancy receiving chemotherapy, post-organ transplantation status, or long-term systemic immunosuppressive therapy before admission; (VII) documented chronic liver disease, liver failure, or hepatic transplantation; and (VIII) missing baseline or day-3 core laboratory variables required to calculate PLR, CAR, ΔPLR, or CARc.

Data collection and outcome definition

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the institutional electronic medical records, including physician notes, nursing records or PICU flow sheets when available, electronic medication administration records, and structured laboratory reports. Data abstraction was independently performed by two trained pediatric clinical researchers using a standardized electronic case report form. Discrepancies regarding variable definitions, data interpretation, or temporal sequencing were resolved by a senior pediatric intensivist through review of the original medical records. Baseline variables were defined as data obtained within the first 24 hours after hospital admission. Day-3 laboratory variables were defined as values measured within 48–96 hours after admission, preferentially using the result closest to 72 hours. If multiple laboratory tests were available within the 48–96-hour window, the value closest to 72 hours after admission was used. The day-3 laboratory assessment was treated as the index time for prediction. All candidate predictors were defined using data recorded on or before this prediction index time. Information first documented after the index time was not used to determine predictor status. For variables with repeated measurements, the prespecified value or cumulative exposure within the relevant measurement window was used, as detailed below.

The primary outcome was incident AKI occurring after the day-3 prediction index time and before hospital day 7 or discharge, whichever occurred first. AKI was identified and staged according to the serum creatinine component of the KDIGO criteria: an increase in serum creatinine of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or an increase to ≥1.5 times the reference creatinine value known or presumed to have occurred within the preceding 7 days (13). The onset time was defined as the sampling time of the first serum creatinine measurement fulfilling either criterion.

The reference creatinine used for AKI ascertainment was determined using a prespecified hierarchical approach. When a clinically stable outpatient serum creatinine value obtained within the 3 months before admission was available, that value was used. When no eligible pre-admission value was available, the lowest serum creatinine measured between admission and the day-3 prediction index time was used as the reference value. Restricting the fallback reference value to measurements obtained on or before the index time prevented subsequent outcome information from contributing to AKI classification. The first serum creatinine measurement obtained within 24 hours after admission was retained separately as the baseline renal-function predictor in the prediction model.

Patients who fulfilled the KDIGO serum creatinine criteria at admission or at any time on or before the day-3 prediction index were classified as having prevalent or early AKI and were excluded from the primary dynamic prediction cohort. Urine-output criteria were not included in the primary outcome because complete hourly urine-output records were not consistently available outside the PICU. Urine-output criteria were evaluated in the prespecified PICU sensitivity analysis among patients with an indwelling urinary catheter and complete hourly documentation. AKI stages 1–3 were recorded as secondary descriptive outcomes, whereas the prediction model used incident AKI as a binary outcome.

Baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, admission body weight, major comorbidities, respiratory support, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, sepsis or septic shock, and disease severity at admission. Age, sex, admission body weight, major comorbidities, and disease severity were determined using information recorded within the first 24 hours after admission. Pediatric Critical Illness Score (PCIS) was calculated from the earliest complete set of required clinical and laboratory measurements within this period. Mechanical ventilation and vasoactive drug use were coded as present when initiated between admission and the day-3 prediction index time. Treatments first initiated after the index time were not counted as predictors. Disease severity was primarily assessed using the PCIS (15), categorized as >80 (non-critical), 71–80 (critical), and ≤70 (extremely critical). If complete physiologic data were available in PICU patients, the Pediatric Risk of Mortality III score was calculated as a supplementary severity measure (16). Sepsis or septic shock within the first 48 hours was recorded based on discharge diagnoses, progress notes, vasoactive drug use, lactate levels, and organ dysfunction documentation. When sufficient data were available, pediatric sepsis and septic shock were adjudicated using the Phoenix Sepsis Criteria (17,18). Vasoactive support was recorded as a binary predictor when at least one vasoactive drug was administered before or at the prediction index time. Vasoactive drugs included dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, milrinone, and vasopressin. The maximum Vasoactive-Inotropic Score within the first 48 hours was calculated as a supplementary variable when complete infusion-dose records were available (19). Nutritional status was primarily described using admission body weight and age category. Height- or length-derived nutritional indices were treated as supplementary variables.

Inflammatory predictors were derived from routine laboratory measurements obtained at baseline and during the day-3 window. Baseline measurements were defined as values obtained within 24 hours after admission. Day-3 measurements were defined as values obtained 48–96 hours after admission, with the value closest to 72 hours selected when multiple measurements were available. Platelet count and absolute lymphocyte count were obtained from the complete blood count, and CRP and serum albumin were obtained from the corresponding laboratory records.

PLR was calculated as platelet count divided by absolute lymphocyte count, and CAR was calculated as CRP divided by serum albumin. ΔPLR was calculated as day-3 PLR minus baseline PLR; positive values indicated an increase in PLR, whereas negative values indicated a decrease. CARc was calculated as ((baseline CAR − day-3 CAR)/baseline CAR) × 100%; positive values indicated a reduction in CAR, whereas zero or negative values indicated absent clearance or an increase in CAR. Both variables were retained as continuous predictors without truncating negative values.

ΔPLR and CARc were prespecified as the primary dynamic inflammatory predictors because they captured complementary aspects of the early inflammatory trajectory using routinely available laboratory measurements. Absolute change was used for PLR to quantify the direction and magnitude of change without division by potentially low baseline PLR values. Relative clearance was used for CAR to quantify the proportional reduction or persistence of the combined CRP–albumin signal relative to its baseline level. To limit model complexity and data-driven selection among correlated inflammatory measures, other inflammatory variables were not screened as interchangeable dynamic predictors.

Treatment-related exposures before the outcome window were collected from electronic medication administration records. Nephrotoxic medication exposure focused on concomitant vancomycin and piperacillin/tazobactam administration. VPT exposure was coded as present when at least 24 cumulative hours of overlapping administration had been completed between admission and the day-3 prediction index time. Exposure initiated after the prediction index time was not counted in the primary model. Other nephrotoxic medications, including aminoglycosides and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, were collected as supplementary covariates when documented and were not treated as mandatory predictors in the primary model. Diuretic exposure, particularly furosemide use on or before the day-3 prediction index time, was recorded as a supplementary covariate because it may influence urine-output and fluid-balance interpretation. Fluid overload was calculated over the first 48 hours after hospital admission using the formula: 100 × (cumulative fluid intake − cumulative fluid output)/admission body weight. The 48-hour window was fixed for all patients and ended before the day-3 prediction index assessment. Fluid overload >10% was considered clinically relevant (20). Fluid overload >10% was included as a prespecified predictor in the primary model and was additionally evaluated in the PICU subgroup. Antibiotic exposure before admission was recorded only when documented in referral notes, emergency records, or caregiver-reported medication histories. Major non-renal comorbidities, including congenital heart disease, were also recorded when available.

Sample size calculation

Because this retrospective study included all eligible patients during the predefined study period, a formal sample size framework was used to determine whether the available cohort was adequate for development of the multivariable prediction model and to constrain the number of candidate predictor parameters. The calculation followed the criteria proposed by Riley et al. for prediction models with a binary outcome (21,22).

The primary dynamic model included nine prespecified candidate predictor parameters: age, extremely critical illness defined as PCIS ≤70, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, VPT exposure ≥24 hours, fluid overload >10%, baseline serum creatinine, ΔPLR, and CARc. Each predictor required one model parameter. Based on previous pediatric CAP data, the anticipated incidence of AKI was set at 20%. In the absence of a directly comparable existing model, an anticipated Cox-Snell R2 of 0.17 was specified to represent moderate model fit, and a global shrinkage factor of ≥0.90 was targeted.

Using the pmsampsize framework, the minimum sample sizes required were 430 patients to limit overfitting and achieve a global shrinkage factor of at least 0.90, 255 patients to limit the absolute difference between the apparent and adjusted Nagelkerke R2 to ≤0.05, and 246 patients to estimate the overall outcome proportion with a margin of error of ±0.05. The largest requirement was therefore 430 patients, corresponding to approximately 86 AKI events at an anticipated event rate of 20%. The final cohort included 454 patients and 94 incident AKI events, exceeding the formal minimum requirement. The achieved events-per-parameter ratio of 10.44 was reported as a descriptive measure rather than as the primary justification of sample size.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the pmsampsize, mice, glmnet, rms, pROC, dcurves, and caret packages. Missingness was assessed separately for the outcome, primary model predictors, and supplementary covariates. The primary model used complete cases. The AKI outcome, baseline serum creatinine, baseline and day-3 platelet count, absolute lymphocyte count, CRP, and albumin were required for eligibility and were not imputed. Age, PCIS ≤70, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, VPT exposure ≥24 hours, and fluid overload >10% were also complete in the 454-patient cohort; therefore, all 454 patients, including 94 with incident AKI, contributed to the primary analysis.

Missing data were confined to supplementary variables: PRISM III score (9.7%), maximum Vasoactive-Inotropic Score (7.3%), height- or length-derived nutritional indices (12.8%), aminoglycoside exposure (4.4%), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug exposure (3.7%), and diuretic exposure (2.4%) (Table S1). Multiple imputation by chained equations was used only for sensitivity analyses involving these variables. Twenty datasets were generated using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic or multinomial logistic regression for categorical variables. The imputation model included the outcome, all primary predictors, variables undergoing imputation, and auxiliary variables related to missingness. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Convergence was assessed by trace plots, and observed and imputed distributions were compared for plausibility. Baseline characteristics of included patients were also compared with those excluded because of missing core day-3 laboratory data.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared using the independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as n (%) and compared using Pearson χ2 or Fisher exact tests. ΔPLR and CARc were compared between AKI and non-AKI groups. Linear mixed-effects models including group, time, and group × time interaction were used to assess changes in PLR and CAR from admission to the day-3 window; highly skewed variables were log-transformed or analyzed using robust methods.

ΔPLR and CARc were prespecified as the dynamic inflammatory predictors of primary interest and were not selected by univariable screening. The nine candidate parameters used for sample-size planning were age, PCIS ≤70, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, VPT exposure ≥24 hours, fluid overload >10%, baseline serum creatinine, ΔPLR, and CARc. Admission PLR and CAR were retained only in the comparator model. Collinearity was evaluated using Spearman correlations and variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF ≥5 indicating substantial multicollinearity. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used as an auxiliary assessment of predictor stability. The final model integrated prespecified clinical relevance, sample-size constraints, and LASSO results.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to predict incident AKI after the day-3 index time, and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs were reported. Three prespecified comparator models were evaluated: Model 1 included the seven clinical predictors; Model 2 added admission PLR and CAR to Model 1; and Model 3 added ΔPLR and CARc to Model 1. Discrimination was assessed by AUC and compared using the DeLong test. Overall accuracy was assessed by the Brier score, calibration by plots, slope, and intercept, and clinical utility by decision curve analysis (DCA). Two operating thresholds were additionally evaluated: a lower cutoff achieving sensitivity ≥90% and a higher cutoff achieving specificity ≥80%. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and the proportion classified above each threshold were reported with 95% CIs. NRI and IDI were treated as exploratory supplementary measures.

Bootstrap-based internal validation with 1,000 resamples was used to estimate optimism. The full model-development procedure was repeated in each bootstrap sample, and performance was evaluated in the bootstrap sample and original cohort. Optimism-corrected AUC, Brier score, calibration slope, and calibration intercept were reported, together with a bootstrap-corrected calibration curve. Sensitivity analyses included the expanded cohort with AKI before day 3, the PICU subgroup with urine-output criteria and fluid-overload assessment, exclusion of patients exposed to VPT, and multiple-imputation analyses of supplementary covariates. All tests were two-sided, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort selection and baseline characteristics

A total of 876 pediatric patients with SCAP were initially screened. Following systematic review, 422 patients were excluded (Figure 1): 135 for hospital stay ≤48 hours, 64 for transfer from another hospital after >48 hours of treatment, 58 for chronic kidney disease or congenital urinary anomalies, 47 for HAP/VAP, 42 for AKI prior to the day-3 assessment, 29 for severe immunosuppression or chronic liver disease, and 47 for missing day-3 core laboratory variables or insufficient serial serum creatinine records. The final dynamic prediction modeling cohort comprised 454 children, of whom 94 (20.7%) developed incident AKI after the day-3 laboratory assessment (non-AKI group, n=360). The AKI outcome and all nine predictors included in the primary dynamic model were completely recorded for these 454 patients; therefore, the primary model was fitted as a complete-case analysis without imputation of outcome or predictor values. No statistically significant differences in measured baseline characteristics were observed between the final cohort and those excluded due to missing core data (Table S2).

Baseline comparisons (Table 1) revealed that the AKI group was significantly younger (median 16 vs. 25 months; P=0.01) and had higher admission severity, with a greater proportion of extremely critical illness (PCIS ≤70: 43.6% vs. 15.3%; P<0.001). Frequencies of mechanical ventilation (68.1% vs. 28.3%), vasoactive drug use (46.8% vs. 14.4%), and sepsis or septic shock (55.3% vs. 22.8%) were significantly higher in the AKI group (all P<0.001). Regarding treatment exposures, concomitant VPT exposure for ≥24 hours (27.7% vs. 9.7%) and FO >10% (38.3% vs. 11.9%) were more prevalent in those developing AKI (both P<0.001). Laboratory parameters showed that the AKI group had higher baseline serum creatinine (median 0.46 vs. 0.34 mg/dL), PLR (168 vs. 118), and CAR (3.1 vs. 1.4) compared with the non-AKI group (all P<0.001).

Incidence, staging, timing, and clinical outcomes of AKI

Among the 454 children in the final dynamic prediction modeling cohort, incident AKI based exclusively on the KDIGO serum creatinine criteria was identified in 94 patients, yielding an incidence rate of 20.7% for incident AKI after the day-3 laboratory assessment. Regarding the severity distribution of renal impairment, the majority of the incident AKI cases were classified as KDIGO stage 1 (n=58, 61.7%), followed by stage 2 (n=24, 25.5%) and stage 3 (n=12, 12.8%). The median time from hospital admission to the onset of incident AKI—defined strictly by the sampling time of the first serum creatinine measurement fulfilling the KDIGO criteria—was 4.5 days (IQR, 4.0–5.5 days).

Comparisons of the clinical course and outcomes demonstrated that patients who developed incident AKI were more likely to have required PICU care during hospitalization (74.5% vs. 35.0%; P<0.001) compared to the non-AKI group. For patients requiring critical care, the median PICU length of stay was significantly prolonged in the AKI group (8.0 vs. 4.5 days; P<0.001). Furthermore, the AKI group exhibited a significantly longer median duration of mechanical ventilation (4.5 vs. 0 days; P<0.001) and total hospital length of stay (16.0 vs. 10.0 days; P<0.001). Renal replacement therapy, representing an advanced renal support intervention, was exclusively required by patients in the AKI group (6.4% vs. 0.0%; P<0.001). The in-hospital mortality rate was significantly higher among patients who developed incident AKI (8.5% vs. 0.8%; P=0.001) (Table 2).

Candidate predictor assessment and final model construction

Before multivariable modeling, Spearman correlation and VIF analyses showed no substantial multicollinearity among candidate predictors, with all VIF values <2.5. LASSO logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation was then used as an auxiliary procedure to assess predictor stability. Core clinical variables and both dynamic inflammatory indices, ΔPLR and CARc, were retained at the optimal λ, supporting their inclusion in the final model (Figure S1).

The final multivariable logistic model included nine parameters: age, PCIS ≤70, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, VPT exposure ≥24 hours, FO >10%, baseline serum creatinine, ΔPLR, and CARc. FO >10% was retained because fluid balance data were sufficiently complete in the modeling cohort.

In the final model, PCIS ≤70 (aOR 2.34; 95% CI: 1.21–4.53; P=0.01), vasoactive drug use (aOR 1.93; 95% CI: 1.05–3.55; P=0.03), VPT exposure ≥24 hours (aOR 2.15; 95% CI: 1.12–4.12; P=0.02), FO >10% (aOR 2.56; 95% CI: 1.35–4.85; P=0.004), and higher baseline serum creatinine (aOR 1.43 per 0.1 mg/dL increase; 95% CI: 1.05–1.95; P=0.02) were independently associated with incident AKI. Mechanical ventilation showed a positive but non-significant association after adjustment (aOR 1.70; 95% CI: 0.95–3.04; P=0.07).

Both early dynamic inflammatory indices remained independently associated with incident AKI. Each 10-unit increase in ΔPLR was associated with higher AKI risk (aOR 1.13; 95% CI: 1.06–1.20; P<0.001), whereas each 10% increase in CARc was associated with lower AKI risk (aOR 0.82; 95% CI: 0.75–0.90; P<0.001) (Table 4).

Incremental predictive value of early dynamic inflammatory indices

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of the dynamic inflammatory indices, three prespecified logistic regression models were compared. Model 1, the clinical baseline model, included seven parameters: age, PCIS ≤70, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, VPT exposure ≥24 hours, FO >10%, and baseline serum creatinine. Model 1 showed moderate discrimination, with an AUC of 0.765 (95% CI: 0.715–0.815) and a Brier score of 0.138.

Adding baseline PLR and CAR to Model 1 produced Model 2, which improved discrimination compared with Model 1 (AUC 0.806; 95% CI: 0.762–0.850; DeLong P=0.03) and reduced the Brier score to 0.115. Model 3, which added ΔPLR and CARc to Model 1, showed the best apparent performance, with an AUC of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.834–0.910), significantly higher than Model 2 and Model 1 (both DeLong P<0.001). Model 3 also had the lowest Brier score (0.089) and favorable apparent calibration (calibration slope 0.98; intercept 0.01) (Table 5; Figure 3A).

Exploratory reclassification analyses suggested that using early dynamic indices instead of baseline inflammatory indices improved risk stratification. To provide clinically interpretable operating points, two probability thresholds were evaluated for the final dynamic model. At a lower screening threshold of 10%, 85 of 94 children who subsequently developed AKI were correctly identified, corresponding to a sensitivity of 90.4% (95% CI: 82.8–94.9%), a specificity of 55.0% (95% CI: 49.8–60.1%), a positive predictive value of 34.4% (95% CI: 28.8–40.5%), and a negative predictive value of 95.7% (95% CI: 91.9–97.7%). At this threshold, 247 patients (54.4%) were classified as requiring enhanced renal surveillance. At a higher action threshold of 30%, sensitivity was 70.2% (95% CI: 60.3–78.5%), specificity was 80.3% (95% CI: 75.9–84.1%), positive predictive value was 48.2% (95% CI: 40.0–56.5%), and negative predictive value was 91.2% (95% CI: 87.5–93.8%). A total of 137 patients (30.2%) were classified above the higher threshold. Detailed classification performance is shown in Table S3.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary findings (Table S4). First, after adding 42 patients who developed AKI before the day-3 laboratory assessment, the expanded descriptive cohort included 496 patients. In this cohort, key baseline predictors, including PCIS ≤70 and baseline serum creatinine, showed directionally consistent associations with overall AKI. Because these early AKI events occurred before the day-3 index assessment, this cohort was not used for the primary dynamic prediction model.

Second, in the PICU subgroup (n=196), the model maintained favorable apparent performance, with an AUC of 0.865 and a calibration slope of 0.95 (Figure S2). FO >10% showed a stronger association with incident AKI in this subgroup (aOR 3.15; 95% CI: 1.55–6.42). Applying urine output criteria as a supplementary definition identified 6 additional transient AKI cases, but the dynamic inflammatory indices remained independently associated with incident AKI.

Third, after excluding patients with VPT exposure, the non-VPT cohort included 393 patients. ΔPLR and CARc remained independently associated with incident AKI, with effect sizes similar to the primary model (ΔPLR aOR 1.14 per 10-unit increase; CARc aOR 0.81 per 10% increase). Exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by mechanical ventilation and sepsis/septic shock status also showed broadly consistent performance, with apparent AUC values ranging from 0.852 to 0.880. No clear evidence of effect modification was observed, although these analyses were not powered for formal interaction testing.

In an additional missing-data sensitivity analysis, supplementary covariates with incomplete observations were imputed across 20 datasets using multiple imputation by chained equations. Imputation diagnostics showed stable trace plots, close agreement between observed and imputed distributions, and relative efficiencies exceeding 99%. Repeating the supplementary analyses after imputation did not materially alter the direction or magnitude of the associations involving the primary model predictors. The final nine predictors remained unchanged, the pooled apparent AUC was 0.871 compared with 0.872 in the complete-case primary analysis, the pooled Brier score was 0.090 compared with 0.089, and the pooled calibration slope was 0.97 compared with 0.98.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of children with SCAP, incident AKI after the day-3 laboratory assessment occurred in 20.7% of the final modeling cohort. Most AKI cases were KDIGO stage 1, but AKI was nevertheless associated with more intensive clinical care, longer PICU stay, longer mechanical ventilation, prolonged hospitalization, greater need for renal replacement therapy, and higher in-hospital mortality. These findings are consistent with prior pediatric CAP data showing that AKI is not uncommon among hospitalized children with pneumonia, as well as larger pediatric critical care cohorts in which AKI was associated with adverse short-term outcomes and mortality (23,24). The present study extends these observations by focusing on children with SCAP and by using an index-time design in which early inflammatory changes from admission to the day-3 window were used to predict subsequent AKI, rather than describing AKI occurrence alone.

The observed AKI incidence was close to the approximately 20% incidence reported in children hospitalized with CAP (23). However, direct comparison requires caution because our primary outcome was restricted to incident AKI after the day-3 assessment, and patients who had already developed AKI before that time point were excluded from the primary dynamic model. This design was necessary to preserve the temporal relationship between the predictors and outcome. From a clinical perspective, the model is therefore best interpreted as a tool for early inpatient risk reassessment after initial stabilization, not as an admission-only prediction model. This distinction is important because pediatric AKI is often under-recognized, serum creatinine rises late relative to kidney injury, and effective treatments after AKI has occurred remain limited (3). A model that identifies children at elevated risk before overt creatinine-defined AKI may help clinicians intensify renal monitoring, review nephrotoxic exposures, and optimize fluid management.

Children who developed AKI had higher disease severity at admission, with a higher proportion of PCIS ≤70, more frequent mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug use, and sepsis or septic shock. These findings are clinically plausible. Severe pneumonia can progress to systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability, and multi-organ dysfunction, all of which can impair renal perfusion and promote tubular stress. The use of Phoenix criteria to characterize pediatric sepsis and septic shock strengthens clinical interpretability because recent international consensus criteria emphasize organ dysfunction rather than systemic inflammatory response alone (17,18). In the multivariable model, PCIS ≤70 and vasoactive drug use remained associated with incident AKI, whereas mechanical ventilation showed a positive but non-significant association after adjustment. This pattern suggests that mechanical ventilation may partly reflect overall severity and circulatory instability rather than acting as an isolated predictor.

Fluid overload was another important predictor in the final model, and its association was stronger in the PICU subgroup. This result agrees with prior evidence that positive fluid balance and fluid accumulation are associated with worse outcomes in critically ill children (20,25). In SCAP, fluid overload may reflect capillary leakage, aggressive resuscitation, impaired renal excretion, myocardial dysfunction, or evolving septic physiology. It may also worsen pulmonary mechanics, prolong ventilatory support, and increase renal venous congestion. Because fluid balance data are more reliable in PICU settings than in general wards, our sensitivity analysis supports the view that FO >10% is clinically meaningful when measured under adequate monitoring conditions. This also explains why FO should be interpreted as a context-dependent predictor rather than as a universally robust variable across all pediatric ward settings.

VPT exposure for at least 24 hours was independently associated with incident AKI, and the dynamic inflammatory indicators remained predictive after excluding VPT-exposed patients. This finding has two implications. First, it is consistent with pediatric and broader observational evidence linking concomitant vancomycin and piperacillin/tazobactam with higher AKI risk, particularly among critically ill children and those with additional risk factors (26-28). Second, the persistence of ΔPLR and CARc associations in the non-VPT cohort suggests that the inflammatory dynamics were not merely proxies for broad-spectrum nephrotoxic antibiotic exposure. Nevertheless, this should not be interpreted as proof of causality. The decision to prescribe VPT is influenced by illness severity, suspected resistant pathogens, and clinician judgment. Therefore, the VPT finding should primarily be used to support careful renal monitoring and antibiotic stewardship rather than to infer direct nephrotoxicity in every case.

A central finding of this study was that early changes in PLR and CAR differed markedly between children who subsequently developed AKI and those who did not. Non-AKI patients showed decreasing PLR and marked CAR clearance by the day-3 window, whereas AKI patients showed increasing PLR and persistently elevated CAR. Previous pediatric pneumonia studies have suggested that inflammatory and albumin-based ratios, including CAR and PLR, may help characterize disease severity in children with CAP (29,30). Studies in AKI and critical illness have also linked PLR and related hematologic ratios with AKI risk or outcomes, although findings vary by population and timing (31-33). The novelty of the present study lies in emphasizing early dynamic changes rather than single baseline values. This is clinically relevant because SCAP is a rapidly evolving condition, and the direction of inflammatory resolution or persistence during the first 72 hours may provide more actionable risk information than admission biomarkers alone.

Several mechanisms may explain why ΔPLR and CARc were associated with subsequent AKI. An increasing PLR may reflect platelet activation, lymphopenia-related immune dysregulation, endothelial injury, and a prothrombotic inflammatory state. Persistent CAR elevation integrates ongoing CRP-driven inflammation with hypoalbuminemia, which may reflect capillary leakage, reduced hepatic synthesis, malnutrition, dilution, or systemic disease severity. In sepsis and severe infection, renal injury is increasingly understood as a consequence of inflammatory signaling, endothelial activation, microcirculatory dysfunction, oxidative stress, mitochondrial stress, and tubular cell maladaptation rather than reduced renal blood flow alone (34,35). Under this framework, failure of CAR to clear and continued PLR elevation may represent persistent systemic inflammatory burden and microvascular stress that precede overt creatinine-defined AKI.

The model comparison further supports the value of dynamic inflammation assessment. The clinical baseline model showed moderate discrimination, while adding admission PLR and CAR improved performance. The dynamic inflammatory model, which replaced baseline inflammatory indices with ΔPLR and CARc, achieved the highest apparent AUC, lowest Brier score, and favorable calibration. DCA suggested greater net benefit across clinically relevant risk thresholds. These results indicate that the early direction of inflammatory change adds risk information beyond baseline severity, treatment exposure, and baseline renal function. The optimism-corrected AUC remained encouraging after bootstrap adjustment, suggesting that apparent model performance was not entirely attributable to overfitting within the development cohort. However, bootstrap correction evaluates model optimism under repeated sampling from the same source population and does not establish performance in patients from different institutions, time periods, or clinical settings. The formal sample size assessment supported the adequacy of the development cohort, consistent with recommendations that prediction-model sample size should account for outcome frequency, anticipated model fit, predictor parameters, and overfitting control rather than rely primarily on events-per-variable rules (21,22).

The nomogram uses variables available from routine clinical records and is currently intended as a research tool for evaluating day-3 AKI risk reassessment rather than as a validated bedside decision instrument. At the 10% screening threshold, the model achieved a sensitivity of 90.4% and a negative predictive value of 95.7%, identifying 85 of 94 children who subsequently developed AKI. This threshold may therefore be suitable for triggering low-burden measures such as repeat serum creatinine testing, closer urine-output and fluid-balance surveillance, and review of concurrent nephrotoxic medications. Its moderate specificity of 55.0% means that approximately half of the cohort would undergo enhanced surveillance, which may be acceptable when the principal objective is to minimize missed AKI.

At the 30% threshold, specificity increased to 80.3%, while sensitivity remained 70.2%. This higher threshold may be more appropriate for prompting a structured multidisciplinary review, including reassessment of fluid management, closer evaluation of potentially modifiable nephrotoxic exposure, or consideration of nephrology consultation. However, the positive predictive value remained 48.2%, indicating that predicted high risk should not be interpreted as confirmation that AKI will occur. Children with predicted risks between 10% and 30% should continue to undergo routine clinical reassessment based on disease severity, renal trends, fluid status, and treatment exposure. These operating thresholds are exploratory and require external validation and prospective impact evaluation before routine clinical implementation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and local PICU admission criteria, antibiotic protocols, laboratory testing patterns, and fluid documentation practices may limit generalizability. Second, the primary AKI definition was based on serum creatinine criteria. Urine output criteria were evaluated only in PICU patients with complete hourly records, so some transient oliguric AKI events may have been missed in the main analysis. Third, day-3 laboratory availability may introduce selection bias because children who improved rapidly may have undergone fewer repeat tests, although measured baseline characteristics were similar between the final modeling cohort and those excluded for missing core data. Missing data did not affect the AKI outcome or the nine predictors included in the primary model, which was therefore estimated using all 454 complete cases. Multiple imputation was restricted to supplementary covariates and used only in sensitivity analyses. Although the close agreement between complete-case and imputed analyses reduces concern that the findings were driven by the imputation model, the missing-at-random assumption for supplementary variables cannot be verified and residual bias remains possible. Fourth, VPT exposure, fluid overload, vasoactive support, and mechanical ventilation are closely related to illness severity; residual confounding cannot be eliminated despite multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses. Fifth, model development and performance assessment were conducted in the same single-center cohort. Bootstrap resampling was used to estimate optimism and obtain internally corrected performance measures, but this procedure cannot assess temporal transportability or external generalizability. No separate temporal validation sample or independent external cohort was available. The reported optimism-corrected AUC and calibration therefore reflect expected performance after adjustment for overfitting within the source population rather than validated performance in new clinical settings. Independent multicenter external validation, followed by prospective impact evaluation, is required before the nomogram or its proposed operating thresholds can be used to guide bedside decisions. Sixth, the model predicts short-term inpatient AKI after the day-3 assessment and does not address long-term renal recovery, post-discharge kidney function, or chronic kidney disease risk. Future studies should evaluate whether integrating this model into EMR-based decision support improves AKI surveillance, nephrotoxin stewardship, fluid management, and patient outcomes.

Conclusions

In children hospitalized with SCAP, incident AKI after the day-3 laboratory assessment was common and associated with more severe clinical course and worse in-hospital outcomes. A nomogram incorporating clinical severity, treatment exposures, fluid overload, baseline renal function, ΔPLR, and CARc showed encouraging optimism-corrected discrimination and acceptable calibration within the development cohort. Although the model is based on routinely available variables, independent external validation and prospective impact evaluation are required before it can be considered for bedside risk stratification or treatment-related decision support.
