Prediction of microvascular invasion in hepatocellular carcinoma using contrast-enhanced ultrasound and deep learning
Authors: Chuan Pang, Jinyu Ru, Yue Liu, Wenzhen Ding, Suwan Chai, Jundong Yao, Shuhong Liu, Hui Feng, Jing Liu, Min Chen, Ming Kuang, Shuling Chen, Minghua Ying, Jinghan Yang, Chaonan Chen, Xiaoling Yu, Haoyan Zhang, Xiaopeng Gao, Jie Tian, Kun Wang, Jie Yu, Ping Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microvascular invasion (MVI) is a key prognostic factor in hepatocellular carcinoma but is currently only detectable after surgery. Here, we develop MAPUSE, a deep learning model using contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) to predict MVI non-invasively. We train and test the model on 5148 CEUS videos from 1716 patients across multiple centers. Results show that MAPUSE achieves accurate MVI prediction (AUCs 0.835-0.978) across different tumor sizes, contrast agents, and prospective validations. Transcriptomic analysis links the model’s predictions to CD8 + T cell immune infiltration, confirmed via the model’s attention maps. In a clinical cohort, patients predicted as MVI-positive can benefit from post-ablation immunotherapy. MAPUSE thus enables preoperative, non-invasive MVI assessment and provides insights into the tumor immune microenvironment, offering a valuable tool for clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide1. The presence of tumor cells within vascular lumens outside the tumor margin and detectable only by histopathological examination, namely microvascular invasion (MVI), is a well-established risk factor associated with early recurrence and poor prognosis after local treatment2. Accurate preoperative diagnosis of MVI based on imaging is crucial for clinical decision-making, but remains challenging with conventional modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computerized tomography (CT), or ultrasound (US) because the microvascular lumens where MVI exists are difficult to be directly visualized3.

Compared with the still images of CT or MRI, ultrasound enables real-time scan and can provide dynamic videos. Especially, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) can visualize the dynamic microvascular flow by tracking the movement trajectory of 2–5 μm contrast agent microbubbles, which can accurately reflect the microcirculation of region of interest4. The advantage of high temporal and spatial resolution with abundant microvascular information makes CEUS potentially a promising imaging modality to detect MVI or even locate its position. Nevertheless, it is challenging to manually capture MVI-associated CEUS features, particularly dynamic ones.

Deep learning (DL) based artificial intelligence (AI) is capable of extracting high throughput radiomic characteristics from medical images and has shown similar or superior performance to human radiologists5–7. However, the enormous amount of information in CEUS videos require a highly efficient AI algorithm for training and learning without using too expensive computing power8. TimeSformer is the first fully Transformer-based video architecture with self-attention mechanism. Networks based on Transformer was proven to be more efficient in training and inference for natural video recognition tasks comparing with conventional convolutional neural networks, especially for long video clips9,10. Therefore, we hypothesized that it would be ideal for learning CEUS videos.

Due to the “black box” nature of DL-assisted radiomics models, the biological and pathological explainability of DL features has been challenging11. Recently, increasing studies have indicated that radiomic signatures (or imaging biomarkers) are correlated with certain biological activities at molecular and cellular levels, which enhanced their reliability and clinical translational potential12–14. It is reasonable to believe that the microscopic biological characteristics of MVI may be reflected in the hidden features of macroscopic CEUS videos that are recognizable by the well-trained TimeSformer model. Investigating and understanding such cross-scale correspondences would be important for us to interpret the DL model and provide greater confidence for applying it in real clinical scenarios.

In this study, we collect a large nationwide multicenter sample in China to train a dynamic CEUS-based DL model, named MAPUSE (MVI AI prediction via CEUS with explainability), to predict the presence of MVI in HCC. Its performance is validated in multiple scenarios, including in different contrast agent cohorts [Sonovue (Bracco, Italy) and Sonazoid (GE Healthcare, US)] and in different tumor size subgroups. The specific correlation between our model and the data of bulk RNA sequencing (bulkRNA-seq) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is analyzed to establish the biological explainability. Moreover, this study prospectively examines the histopathological finding from surgically acquired specimens based on strong AI attention areas in CEUS images to explore whether MAPUSE could locate MVI or not.

Results

The MAPUSE network

We constructed the MAPUSE model based on preoperative CEUS videos with spatiotemporal attention mechanism to predict the presence of MVI in HCC patients. From July 2017 to May 2024, a total of 1716 HCC patients with 5148 CEUS videos (>3,300,000 frames) from 23 hospitals were included in this study, involving seven primary cohorts with no patient overlap and three additional cohorts extracted from primary cohorts (Fig. 1a, d). Each patient contributed three CEUS videos corresponding to the hepatic artery phase (0–120 s), portal vein phase (180–190 s), and delay phase (240–250 s). The seven primary cohorts: (1) a 23-center training cohort of 495 patients using two representative contrast agents [Sonovue (Bracco), a sulfur hexafluoride pure intravascular microbubble contrast agent, n = 397; Sonazoid (GE Healcare), a perfluorobutane microbubble contrast agent that can be phagocytosed by Kupffer cells and provide a prolonged enhancement of liver parenchyma, n = 98], on which the deep learning model was built; (2) a 23-center internal validation cohort of 213 patients (Sonovue n = 171; Sonazoid n = 42), on which the model performance were assessed; (3) a 23-center Sonovue hold-out test cohort of 98 patients, on which the model performance on images using sulfur hexafluoride contrast agent were assessed; (4) a 23-center Sonazoid hold-out testing cohort of 92 patients, on which the model performance on images using perfluorobutane contrast agent were assessed; (5–6) two prospective testing cohorts of 250 patients (prospective cohort 1: the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University in southern China, n = 120; prospective cohort 2: Chinese PLA General Hospital in northern China n = 130), on which the model performance were prospectively assessed; (7) an immunotherapy cohort of 568 patients who underwent curative-intent ablation, on which the clinical implication of the model on those who cannot achieve pathological MVI diagnosis were assessed. The three additional cohorts: (1) a cohort of 203 patients from the training and internal validation cohorts with bulkRNA-seq data including (2) 12 patients with scRNA-seq data, on which the biological correlation of the model was further validated; (3) 64 patients from the prospective testing cohort 2 (Chinese PLA General Hospital) who were predicted to be MVI true-positive by MAPUSE, on whom the histopathology corresponding to the strong AI attention areas of CEUS images were examined to explore potential MVI locating or tumor microenvironment visualization capability of the model. The process of CEUS data preprocessing and model construction was presented in Fig. 1b, c. Based on TITAN Xp graphics processing unit (NVIDIA, USA), CUDA version 12.2, the equal interval downsampling strategy of 48 frames at 6:1:1 ratio in the three-phase videos achieved the best balance between area under operating characteristic curves (AUC) and computation time, compared with other data process strategies (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The enlarged 1.2-fold ROI outperformed the original ROI with better AUCs, implying the importance of peritumoral image information (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, the 1.2-fold ROI and 48-frame downsampling strategy were adopted in the model training and evaluation. Finally, 247,104 frames of 5,148 CEUS videos were used in this study. For each patient, the MAPUSE model outputs an MVI risk score ranging from 0 to 1, representing the predicted probability of MVI. For prediction of the presence of MVI, MAPUSE achieved AUCs of 0.986 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.982–0.989] and 0.955 (95% CI 0.933–0.972), and accuracy (ACC) of 0.923 (95% CI 0.927–0.947) and 0.923 (95% CI 0.933–0.972) in training and internal validation cohorts, respectively. Sonovue and Sonazoid hold-out testing

After the internal training and validation, we set up the hold-out multicenter Sonovue cohort (n = 98) and Sonazoid cohort (n = 92) separately to test the performance of MAPUSE on the two widely used ultrasound contrast agents. It achieved AUCs of 0.835 (95% CI 0.792–0.869) and 0.858 (95% CI 0.786–0.912), and ACC of 0.714 (95% CI 0.706–0.789) and 0.739 (95% CI 0.677–0.945) in Sonovue and Sonazoid cohorts, respectively (Fig. 2b, all P > 0.05). The consistency of performance on different contrast agents demonstrated MAPUSE had good robustness and generalizability.

Tumor size subgroup study

Small tumors have less characteristic information and a lower incidence of MVI, making the prediction of MVI more challenging. Therefore, we tested MAPUSE in subgroups with tumor sized >5 cm, 3–5 cm, and <3 cm (Fig. 2d). For the internal validation cohort, the AUCs reached 0.907 (95%CI 0.786-1.0), 0.997 (95%CI 0.986–1.0), and 0.974 (95%CI 0.913–1.0) in >5 cm, 3–5 cm, and <3 cm subgroups respectively (P > 0.05). However, for the hold-out testing cohort, the AUCs were 0.978 (95%CI 0.9–1.0), 0.814 (95%CI 0.710–0.894), and 0.756 (95%CI 0.669–0.913) in three subgroups (P < 0.05). As expected, MAPUSE demonstrated a significant better performance on larger tumors in the hold-out testing cohort, but its prediction was still acceptable on <3 cm tumors (Supplementary Table 4).

Biological explainability of MAPUSE

To explore the potential biological relevance of MAPUSE, we analyzed the quantitative association between its outputs—including the MVI risk score (the model’s predicted probability of MVI) and the extracted radiomic features—and the bulk RNA-seq data for 203 patients (Fig. 3a). The differential genes between MAPUSE predicted MVI high-risk and low-risk groups were shown in Fig. 3b. For MVI high-risk group, high expression of cystatin SN (CST1) is observed, which is commonly related with tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion15. Low expression of metallothionein 1 G (MT1G) and metallothionein 1 M (MT1M) are associated with HCC progression and poor prognosis16. Furthermore, we performed a gene-set enrichment analysis to examine genes associated with MAPUSE. The results showed that many individual radiomic features were also negatively correlated with antitumor immune process, such as T cells, NK cells, etc., while positively correlated with immune suppressors and tumor invasive process, such as cell proliferation, T cell exhaustion, etc. (Fig. 3d). Prospective testing and exploration of strong attention area

To test the performance of MAPUSE model in clinical practice from different geographical areas, we used MAPUSE in two prospective cohorts from two centers from southern and northern China (prospective cohort 1: FAHSYU, the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, southern China, n = 130; prospective cohort 2: CPGH, Chinese PLA General Hospital, northern China) n = 120. The AUC is 0.886 and 0.847. ROC, ACC, specificity, sensitivity, and F1-score were also presented in Fig. 2c.

MAPUSE has a self-attention mechanism that can be visualized by heatmaps. The average computation time for 48 frames of heatmap is 0.5 seconds with TITAN Xp graphics processing unit (NVIDIA, USA), CUDA version 12.2, Python 3.8 (https://www.python.org), and PyTorch (https://pytorch.org). To validate whether strong attention areas on CEUS images can locate MVI or not, the 64 HCC patients from prospective cohort 1 who were predicted to be MVI high-risk by MAPUSE and proven to be MVI-positive by pathological diagnosis (MVI true-positive) formed the attention exploration cohort. Preoperatively, we used MAPUSE to generate attention heatmaps on their CEUS images. Corresponding to 160 peritumoral strong attention areas, we examined their histopathology findings. The selection of attention areas should meet the following criteria: (1) located at peri-tumor areas; (2) consistently and stably present throughout the entire video; (3) clear anatomical markers are visible adjacent to the area, enabling relatively precise correlation with the pathological sections. Results showed that although all patients were confirmed to be pathologically MVI-positive, only 24/160 (15%) strong attention areas “coincided” with the real location of MVI (Fig. 5a–c). This suggested that MAPUSE can diagnose the presence of MVI but is not yet capable of locating MVI. However, through further immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, we found that the strong attention areas exhibited significantly less CD8 + T cells compared with weak areas (Fig. 5d, P < 0.001). Implication of MAPUSE for HCC treatment

Among the 568 patients included in the immunotherapy cohort, 234 were classified by MAPUSE as MVI high-risk and 334 as MVI low risk. There were 30 MVI high-risk patients and 45 MVI low-risk patients had used immunotherapy (20 and 31 with combination of targeted drugs, respectively). After overlap weighting (OW), patient baseline characteristics between immunotherapy and non-immunotherapy groups were precisely balanced with standard mean deviations (SMDs) approaching zero (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7).

Among MVI high-risk patients, those who used immunotherapy has better disease-free survival than those who did not (5-year DFS 30.8% vs. 14.6%; HR 0.61, 95%CI 0.42–0.90, P = 0.027) (Fig. 5e). However, MVI low-risk patients have similar disease-free survival whether they used immunotherapy or not (5-year DFS 33.7% vs. 30.4%; HR 0.75, 95%CI 0.50–1.13, P = 0.21) (Fig. 5e). These results suggested that patients with positive MVI predicted by MAPUSE may benefit from postoperative immunotherapy. This further validated the correlation between the MAPUSE model and tumor immune microenvironment, and also implied that MAPUSE could be helpful for treatment decision-making of HCC patients who cannot achieve pathological MVI diagnosis previously.

Discussion

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) allows for real-time visualization of microvascular flow within and around tumors and enables deep learning radiomics technique to extract rich spatiotemporal information. This makes preoperative non-invasive diagnosis of MVI in HCC based on CEUS and DL a feasible potential means17,18. Therefore, we collected more than 1700 patients with 5000 CEUS videos (>3,300,000 frames of images) of HCC from 23 centers across China to develop the MAPUSE model based on TimeSformer strategy with the spatiotemporal self-attention mechanism. Multiple independent validations across various scenarios were performed, making this a large-sample study to date for the MVI diagnosis based on CEUS videos. MAPUSE achieved AUCs of 0.986, 0.955, and 0.835–0.886 in the training, internal validation, multiple hold-out, and prospective testing cohorts, respectively. Previously, a few studies used CEUS-based radiomics to predict MVI. Dong’s study19 of 100 HCC lesions with non-DL machine learning reported an AUC of 0.804, and Qin’s study20 of 252 patients with DL reported an AUC of 0.856 in their internal testing, both without external testing or enrolling temporal information of CEUS videos during model construction. Zhang’s study with deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) based on CEUS videos and clinical variables of 436 patients achieved an AUC of 0.865 in a small external test set of 30 patients21. In our study, the MAPUSE model was validated in four independent testing cohorts (Sonovue, Sonazoid, and two prospective cohorts from different geographical areas), all proved that CEUS and MAPUSE provided a robust and promising way for diagnosing MVI of HCC, which would be of great significance for surgical or interventional strategy formulation, postoperative management, and patient follow-up3,22.

Sonovue and Sonazoid are the two widely used ultrasound contrast agents in clinical practice. MAPUSE demonstrated accurate and robust preoperative prediction of MVI, no matter CEUS was performed with Sonovue or Sonazoid. Compared with Sonovue-CEUS, the most difference of Sonazoid-CEUS is its prolonged hepatobiliary phase. However, the value of hepatobiliary phase in predicting MVI is uncertain. A prospective study of our team investigated imaging features of Sonazoid-CEUS by logistic regression to predict MVI but found no related hepatobiliary phase-specific feature18. Another study also reported that Sonovue-CEUS and Sonazoid-CEUS had similar accuracy (AUC 0.810 vs. 0.786) in predicting MVI using logistic regression and machine learning23. These results supported our approach to combine these two representative contrast agents in our model and thus to meet the needs of multiple clinical scenarios. In addition, the prediction of MVI is more challenging for smaller tumors due to the low incidence and limited image features24,25. MAPUSE achieved AUCs of 0.978, 0.815, and 0.767 in tumor size >5 cm, 3–5 cm, and <3 cm subgroups in hold-out testing, respectively, showing reduced but still acceptable performance in small HCCs.

The clinical use of this model is simple. The ultrasound radiologist only needed to select one frame from a CEUS video to crop the lesion. MAPUSE then automatically generated 1.2x enlarged regions of interest (ROI) for each frame and performed equal interval downsampling. This data processing approach reduced memory consumption per training sample while enabling large-scale network training with affordable computational cost. Integrating it with TimeSformer architecture ensured that long-term dynamic changes within and around tumors could be effectively and efficiently captured from each sample. Therefore, MAPUSE has minimal impact on the routine process of CEUS examinations and can provide predictions during the procedure, while every hospital that can perform CEUS examinations can easily afford the labor and financial cost of running it. Traditional radiomics approaches, whether based on CT, MRI or ultrasound data for MVI prediction, are difficult to achieve the same ease of use as MAPUSE, because they largely rely on precise segmentations of HCC for engineered feature extractions17,20,26,27. The heavy burden of annotations makes engineered radiomics only applicable post-imaging examinations, which is unacceptable for clinical ultrasonography, especially in a country like China where the number of ultrasound radiologists is far from meeting the demand for medical care.

Ever since the deep learning radiomics model was proposed, its biological explainability has always been questioned, affecting its credibility and reliability in clinical applications28,29. Increasing efforts have been devoted to addressing this challenge by seeking the insight of underlying associations between critical DL features from medical images and certain cellular and molecular activities across scales. Our team previously constructed a radiomics model to associate whole-lung CT imaging with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and achieved good performance for predicting this genotype and targeted therapy response in lung cancer with AUCs ranging from 0.748 to 0.813 in the six retrospective and prospective testing cohorts30. Sun et al. proposed a contrast-enhanced CT based radiomics approach to assess tumor-infiltrating CD8 cells and response to anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy31. Another CT radiomics survival prediction model was found to be associated with defective humoral immunity involving B-cell infiltration and immunoglobulin synthesis in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma13. Li et al. developed an MRI-based radiomics model for predicting the survival of gliomas and found the model was associated with tumor macrophage infiltration12.

Compared with those investigations, our study provided a deeper and more comprehensive exploration of the biological explainability of the DL radiomics model. We compared and analyzed data from bulk RNA sequencing, scRNA sequencing, and IHC staining. The triple mutual verification confirmed that the model risk score and DL features significantly corresponded to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration. It is well-known that CD8 + T cells play a crucial role in the anti-tumor immune response and immunotherapy effect32. Our findings also align with a recent study that reported MVI-positive tumors being associated with an immunosuppressive and pro-metastatic microenvironment using scRNA sequencing33. The cellular level activity of CD8 T cell infiltration was actually visualized by CEUS videos, and the related macroscopic phenotype and key imaging features were effectively recognized and learned by MAPUSE, which might be the intrinsic mechanism that allowed the model to predict MVI accurately. Therefore, even if CEUS did not capture the actual location of MVI, MAPUSE might still enable accurate diagnosis by evaluating the enrichment of CD8 + T cells around the HCC peripheral areas in the video. This explains why MAPUSE can predict but not precisely locate MVI. More importantly, it sheds light on its potential clinical utility: it could inform critical treatment decisions, such as planning extended resection into immunosuppressed regions, guiding immunotherapy, or optimizing targets for precise locoregional therapy and biopsy. In summary, these results revealed the biological explainability of MAPUSE, enhanced its reliability and generalizability, and were very conducive to promoting its clinical translation.

In the immunotherapy cohort of this study, the MAPUSE model helped detect the presence of MVI for patients after ablation, for whom the MVI status could not have been obtained. After overlap weighting to mimic RCT, we found that patients with a prediction of positive MVI benefited from immunotherapy. There is a rigorous randomized controlled trial demonstrates that HCC patients with positive MVI could benefit from adjuvant immunotherapy34, which aligns with the results of our study. This indicates that the MAPUSE model offers significant insights that can enhance clinical decision-making processes. The correlation between MAPUSE prediction and CD8 + T cells infiltration identified in this study may also represent a biological factor influencing the efficacy of immunotherapy, though this requires further investigation.

Our study had several limitations. First, despite the collection of a large amount of multicenter CEUS video data across China, further validations on a broader scale and larger prospective cohort are required before the model can be applied in clinical practice, which will be our future endeavor. Second, it should be noted that all enrolled patients were Chinese individuals predominantly affected by viral hepatitis B. The reliability and reproducibility of the model across diverse demographic populations with various liver diseases may be limited and needs further evaluations. Third, the precise correlation between imaging and histological slides in attention area validation is challenging. Although we used the optimal approach achievable under the current conditions, the results are still subject to potential spatial mismatches and sampling differences. Fourth, the challenge of the “black box” nature of DL based radiomics models remains significant. Although our study has taken a small step forward in HCC compared with other reported studies, it is still in the preliminary stage and requires more in-depth and comprehensive investigations to achieve a solid understanding of its biological explainability. Furthermore, the potential presence of biases within the training dataset could inadvertently influence model behavior and limit its generalizability to diverse populations or settings.

In conclusion, our MAPUSE model offers an accurate and robust prediction of MVI based on preoperative CEUS videos of HCC, regardless of whether Sonovue or Sonazoid is applied as the contrast agent. It is simple and fast to use, with low costs of human power and computing power, and is very suitable for use during CEUS examinations. Its risk score and DL features significantly and inversely corresponded to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by CD8 + T cell infiltration, suggesting potential biological relevance. More importantly, the MAPUSE model can help detect the presence of MVI for patients whose MVI status could not have been obtained and exhibits significant clinical implication for treatment decision-making.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study has received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Chinese PLA General Hospital and was carried out in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. The retrospective and prospective collection of the patient datasets in each cohort was approved by the institutional ethical committee at each institution with a waiver of informed consent for retrospective collection. The criteria are: (1) The research involves no more than minimal risk to the subjects; (2) The waiver will not adversely affect the rights and welfare of the subjects; (3) Conducting the research would be impracticable without the waiver; (4) There is an adequate plan to protect the confidentiality of private information; Additionally, the prospective component of this study is officially registered with the ClinicalTrial Registry, under the identifier NCT06760494.

Study design and participants

The design of this study did not consider both sex and/or gender. The CEUS videos of HCC were collected from a liver lesion ultrasound imaging database previously constructed by our team (http://www.usliver.org), which has prospectively collected ultrasound images of focal liver lesions of 27,500 patients from 105 hospitals throughout China to date. Details for the collection of CEUS videos were presented in supplementary methods 1. For patients with multiple lesions, only the tumor with the largest diameter was analysed. The inclusion criteria of this study were as follows: (1) the HCC diagnosis and the presence of MVI were confirmed by surgical pathology; (2) complete and clear CEUS videos obtained within two weeks preoperatively; (3) age >18 years old. The exclusion criteria: (1) unqualified CEUS images (image collection criteria were described in the following “Collection” section); (2) missing surgical pathological diagnosis; (3) lesions underwent local treatments. MVI was defined as the presence of tumor emboli in a vascular space lined by endothelium that was visible only on microscopy. All the specimen slices were reviewed by pathologists with at least 10 years of experience in pathology.

From July 2017 to May 2024, a total of 1716 HCC patients with 5148 CEUS videos (>3,300,000 frames) from 23 hospitals were included in this study, involving seven primary cohorts with no patient overlap (training, internal validation, Sonovue testing, Sonazoid testing, prospective testing 1, prospective testing 2 and immunotherapy cohorts). In addition, patients with bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data from training and validation cohorts formed two additional cohorts for biological explainability investigation, and patients from the prospective testing 2 cohort who were predicted to be MVI true-positive by MAPUSE formed the third additional cohort to explore the potential MVI locating capability of AI attention (Fig. 1a). Data preprocessing

Before training and validating the deep learning network, preprocessing was performed on the included CEUS videos (Fig. 1b). For each patient, three videos of arterial phase (0–45 s), venous phase (45–120 s), and delay phase (180–190 s) were used. For each video, a radiologist (more than 5-year experience on CEUS) selected a frame with largest tumor size and outlined a rectangular box as the region of interest (ROI) containing the entire tumor area and some surrounding liver parenchyma. Then the bounding box was automatically generated on each frame of the CEUS video. Considering the presence of MVI in the surrounding area of the tumor, we attempted to increase the side length of original ROI box to 1.2 times and tested its performance. Due to the large number of video frames and the small difference between consecutive frames, we downsampled the video frames by using two strategies, namely time intensity curves (TIC) based downsampling and equal interval downsampling. The former strategy firstly drew a TIC for a video and extracted frames during the overall rising period, plateau period and falling period of the curve, respectively. The latter strategy was a process of averaging frames taken at different densities in arterial, venous, and delay phrases. Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis of the model performance across the three phrases using varying sampling ratios and different numbers of retained ROI frames. The specific data preprocessing description can be found in Supplementary methods 2.

Model construction

The MAPUSE network was designed to extract MVI-related spatiotemporal radiomic features from CEUS videos. This model preserved the long-distance dependency features captured by the Vision Transformer through its self-attention mechanism, offering superior learning capabilities and efficiency35. It integrated features across diverse independent frames to jointly construct temporal features. MAPUSE comprised two integral parts: spatial attention calculation for individual video frames and temporal attention calculation across all video frames. Initially, it applied the self-attention mechanism to all image blocks within the same frame. Subsequently, it employed an attention mechanism to corresponding image blocks across different frames. This spatiotemporal attention mechanism enhanced the model’s ability to discern key features in videos, further bolstered by the increase in attention heads and layers, ultimately improving performance. MAPUSE mainly included an image embedding module, a time-space dimension feature extraction module, and a classifier module (Fig. 1c). First, the selected video frames are fed into the embedding layer for temporal and spatial position embedding and these one-dimensional vectors are transformed into fixed dimensional embedding vectors through a linear projection. Second, spatiotemporal features were extracted by the TimeSFormer based network. Third, the obtained class token was inputted into the multilayer perceptron (MLP) classification layer for categorization, yielding a MVI risk score. This score was normalized through the softmax function, resulting in a probability value ranging from 0 to 1, representative of the model’s predicted probability of positive MVI. A risk score >0.5 was considered as MVI high-risk. For the comprehensive description of the deep learning-based radiomics model, please refer to the Supplementary methods 3.

Sonovue and Sonazoid hold-out testing

In training and validation sets from Jul 2017 to Oct 2022, two widely used and different contrast agents, Sonovue and Sonazoid, were both included. From Nov 2022 to Oct 2023, 98 patients using Sonovue and 92 patients using Sonazoid from the same 23 centers were set as Sonovue and Sonazoid hold-out test cohorts, respectively.

Tumor size subgroup validation

Since MVI is more difficult to diagnose in small tumors because of lack of feature information and low MVI incidence36,37, we did a tumor size subgroup analysis in tumors >5 cm, 3–5 cm, and <3 cm to test the model performance on large and small tumors.

Biological explainability study

To explore the biological basis and explainability of MAPUSE, we analyzed transcriptomic data from 203 patients in the training and internal validation cohorts, including 12 patients with paired single-cell RNA-seq data. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.0) using standardized pipelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

We performed enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes based on gene sets of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and the gene expression were converted into the abundance of immune cells and stromal cells infiltration using “MCPcounter” R package. The biological process and pathway activation scores of each patient were calculated with Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), using gene sets from IOBR R package. Then, the correlation among biological process, cell infiltration, MVI risk score and extracted radiomic feature values were calculated. (Details in Supplementary methods 4),

To further validate and strengthen the biological correlation of MAPUSE, we conducted scRNA-seq analysis of 12 patients. We used the seurat package to perform quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, and cluster analysis on single-cell data. Cell subpopulations were named using recognized cell markers. The twelve patients were categorized into groups with low (n = 8) and high (n = 4) MVI risk and compared the differences in cell ratios between the two groups using the chi-square test. (Details in Supplementary methods 5).

Prospective testing and exploration of strong attention area

We tested the performance of MAPUSE in two prospective testing cohorts from two centers in southern and northern China (cohort 1: FAHSYU, the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University n = 120; cohort 2: CPGH, Chinese PLA General Hospital n = 130). The protocol is presented in supplementary materials (ClinicalTrial: NCT 06760494).

In the prospective cohort 2 from CPGH, to explore if the strong attention area generated by MAPUSE potentially locates the MVI presence, we validated the histological finding under strong attention area of CEUS images that was predicted to be MVI true-positive (the prospective attention exploration cohort). Firstly, CEUS images were obtained preoperatively and were analyzed by MAPUSE to predict the presence of MVI and generate a heatmap of attention using Grad-CAM38 (Details in Supplementary methods 6). Then we randomly selected strong attention areas located at peritumor regions. After the tumor was resected, we refer to the CEUS image plane and anatomical marks to accurately section corresponding gross specimen plane of the tumor. Subsequently, we selectively sample the specimen area corresponding to strong attention area to validate the presence of MVI or other histopathological findings within that specific region by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining and IHC staining including CD8. Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to manufacturer’s protocol with recommended concentration. The positive cells were counted using ImageJ (v1.54) software. To ensure the precise correlation between imaging and histologic slides, we have taken the following measures: First, senior ultrasonographers were asked to capture images, focusing on areas with clear local anatomical landmarks. Then, experienced surgeons performed cross-sectional dissections of the tumors based on the image planes and anatomical landmarks. Approximately 10–15 pathological sections were obtained from each selected area. Finally, the result was validated in over 160 areas—a sample size sufficient to support statistical analysis.

Implication of MAPUSE for HCC immunotherapy

HCC patients who underwent thermal ablation cannot achieve MVI diagnosis, so we used MAPUSE and their preoperative CEUS videos to detect the presence of MVI and test its implication for immunotherapy response.

From May 2017 to May 2023, 568 primary HCC patients who underwent thermal ablation (radiofrequency ablation or microwave ablation) from Chinese PLA General Hospital were included. Their preoperative CEUS videos, postoperative use of immunotherapy (PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors, including Pembrolizumab, Nivolumab, Durvalumab, Atezolizumab, Toripalimab, Sintilimab, Camrelizumab, Tislelizumab, Sugemalimab), and baseline characteristics were retrospectively collected. First, we used MAPUSE to assess the MVI risk, and then in MVI high-risk and low-risk groups, respectively, we compared the disease-free survival (DFS, defined as the time interval between the ablation and any type of recurrence) between patients who used postoperative immunotherapy or not.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data are presented using numbers and percentages, mean and standard deviation for symmetrically distributed variables, and median and interquartile range for unsymmetrically distributed variables. Baseline variables were compared by Kruskal Wallis test for continuous variables, and Pearson χ2 test, or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The correlation between cell infiltration and MVI risk score was analyzed using Spearman analysis. The correlation between pathway activation score and radiomic feature values was conducted using Pearson correlation analysis.

For the analysis of immunotherapy cohort, we used overlap weighting (OW) method that attempts to mimic important features of randomized clinical trials (RCT) (details in Supplementary methods 7). Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate cumulative rates of survival, utilizing log-rank test for survival comparison. The proportional hazards of survival outcomes were evaluated by Cox proportional hazard regression modeling with backward stepwise selection. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by Schoenfeld residuals and linearity assumption was verified by restricted cubic splines. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed by R software (v 4.2.0).
