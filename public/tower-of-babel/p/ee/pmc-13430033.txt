Quality of life in Australian patients with central disorders of hypersomnolence
Authors: Stephen Kinder, Sara Winter, Brett Duce, Claire M Ellender
Published: 2026

Central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH) represent a subset of sleep disorders with a core element of pathological sleepiness, which can have a multi-faceted impact on the quality of life of those living with the condition [1]. A systematic review of 30 studies investigating the quality-of-life (QOL) of patients with narcolepsy, reports a significantly reduced health-related quality of life compared to the general population, and to a greater extent than those with epilepsy or diabetes [2]. No studies from Australia were included in this large systematic review. This reduction in quality of life extends across all elements of the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) [3], which covers a breadth of domains from limitations of physical activity due to health problems, social activities due to physical or emotional problems, usual role activities due to physical health problems, bodily pain, general mental health, in usual role activities due to emotional problems, vitality, and general health perceptions [3, 4]. Of note, medications for the treatment of narcolepsy, such as wakefulness promoting agents and sodium oxybate, improved quality of life, but not to the normal range [4].

Idiopathic hypersomnia is suggested to result in persistent moderate daytime symptoms, with one study finding a mean Epworth Sleepiness Score of 14.5 (SD ±3.5) despite 89.3% of the study population taking medication for the condition [5]. Work impairment is additionally more difficult for people with CDH, with a cohort of 75 patients reporting 12.3% (SD ±23.6%) absenteeism and 47.6% (SD ±22.7%) presenteeism with an overall work productivity of 51.4% (SD ±24.7%) [5].

This study describes the quality-of-life in patients with CDH, using the SF-36 in a cohort with a secure diagnosis, as confirmed by sleep physician consultation and appropriate confirmatory testing [6]. We hypothesized that the baseline quality of life in patients with CDH would be worse than the general population norms. Documenting this is an important part of future applications for government funded pharmaceutical reimbursement in Australia.

Materials and methods

Following relevant ethical approvals (HREC EX/2023/QMS/100202), a retrospective cohort study of adult (age 18 years and older) patients seen as new cases between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2022 at a tertiary Australian sleep disorders service was undertaken. The cohort was formed from consecutive patients, identified from a standard-of-care (prospectively entered) clinical database. Both incident and prevalent cases were included who had a secure diagnosis of narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), narcolepsy type 2 (NT2), or idiopathic hypersomnia (IH), as per ICSD-3TR criteria [6]. Cases were excluded if there was a diagnosis of: hypersomnolence disorder secondary to a medical disorder, psychiatric disorder, or medication, or Kleine-Levin Syndrome.

The primary outcome was SF-36 score at baseline assessment, including subscales: Physical Functioning (PF), Role Physical (RP), Bodily Pain (BP), General Health (GH), Vitality (VT), Social Functioning (SF), Role Emotional (RE), and Mental Health (MH) [3]. The SF-36 is scored 0-100, with higher scores indicating better health [3]. Secondary outcomes were FOSQ [7] score at baseline, including subscales: Activity Level (A), Vigilance (V), Intimacy (I), General Productivity, and Social Outcome (S), Epworth Sleepiness Scale [8] at baseline, and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [9] score at baseline. For graphical comparison, normative data of these subscales was obtained Jenkinson et al [3] and shown as General Population in Figure 1.

Descriptive statistics were performed, with normally distributed data described as mean and standard deviation and non-normally distributed data described as median and interquartile range; normality was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk test using SPSS (version 30 for Macintosh, IBM). Normally distributed variables were compared with t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) and non-normally with Kruskal–Wallis. Categorical variables were compared with Chi-square tests as appropriate.

Results

A total of forty-three people were included, with demographics shown in Table 1. Within the cohort 7(16%) were diagnosed with NT1, 11 (26%) with NT2 and 25 (58%) with IH. Mean sleep latency for the cohort was 4.8 (±SD 2.12) minutes. The mean ESS for the cohort was 15 (±4), mean PSQI was 8 (±3), and mean FOSQ was 12 (±3). The mean SF36 scores of the whole cohort for each domain were: PF 39 (±24), RP 42 (±13), BP 44 (±9), GH 35 (±10), MH 35 (±13), RE 42 (±13), SF 35 (±14), and VT 33 (±10).

Eleven people (26%) were already established on pharmacotherapy for hypersomnolence at the time of initial assessment (prevalent cases). There was no significant difference in SF-36 physical component summary or mental component summary for patients without stimulant treatment versus on treatment; mean SF-36 PCS without medication 41 (±14) versus 43 (±11) p = .52 and SF-36 MCS 33 (±14) versus 29 (±12) p = .51.

There were no significant between group differences in ESS, PSQI, or FOSQ, when comparing NT1, NT2, and IH cohorts. This is shown in Figure 1, alongside normative data [3] shown as “General Population.” The only differences in SF-36 subdomains between the cohorts was Role Physical (RP) with a significantly higher self-reported function in those with NT2 (p = .008). No significant difference was seen in ESS, FOSQ, PSQI, or SF36 domains in those established on previous pharmacotherapy.

Discussion

The results of this study are consistent with the hypothesis that this cohort with central disorders of hypersomnolence unfortunately reports poor QOL. The degree of sleepiness was comparable to past literature [10]. The overall physical component score in this cohort (42 ± 14) is in keeping with a large systematic review, finding a pooled mean score for physical component 45.91 [2]. However, this study found a lower overall mental component score of 33 (±14), compared with 42.98 in the pooled analysis [2]. It should be noted when comparing data though, the mean age of this cohort was lower than in much of the existing global data. In contrast to the findings of the present study, Galušková et al. [10] found higher physical and mental component QOL scores across all CDH disorders; in this cohort 22% of NT1 patients were noted to be prescribed sodium oxybate. Advanced therapies such as sodium oxybate have been shown to improve QOL [11].

Compared with historic normative data, this cohort of both incident and prevalent hypersomnolence patients demonstrated considerably lower QOL across all domains of the SF-36. This is especially concerning given the young age of this cohort (29 years). No significant differences in QOL were found between individuals on therapy or at diagnosis; whilst the sample size is small, this is hypothesis-generating and may suggest that pharmacotherapy maybe suboptimal, due to limited availability of advanced therapies such as sodium oxybate, pitolisant, and solriamfetol in Australia.

A strength of this study is its cohort being defined by a secure hypersomnolence diagnosis. SF36 as a health-related quality of life measure is well validated and allows comparison between diagnoses with normative, validated assessment across a range of domains.

Limitations of this study include the retrospective nature, and its mixed cohort of incident and prevalent cases. Noting that such central disorders of hypersomnolence are uncommon, there are relatively small numbers within each subgroup -thus, comparisons of individual medications was not possible, limiting conclusions. Given the small sample sizes, the comparisons between diagnostic groups (NT1, NT2, and IH) should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

The results of this study provide Australian context to the impact of central disorders of hypersomnolence in the lives of individuals and their QOL. Narcolepsy and Idiopathic Hypersomnia are highly impactful sleep disorders that reduce QOL across all domains, and this cohort had particularly lower mental component summary compared with the international dataset. Given the limited availability of advanced pharmacotherapy in the Australian context, larger pooled data may be useful to lobby for funding of these advanced therapies.
