Downregulation of chloride voltage-gated channel 7 contributes to hyperalgesia following spared nerve injury
Authors: Yunyun Cai, Jiajie Li, Kewei Fan, Dongmei Zhang, Hongjian Lu, Gang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Alterations in anion balance potential, along with the involvement of cation–chloride cotransporters, play pivotal roles in the development of hyperalgesia after peripheral nerve injury. Chloride voltage-gated channel seven (CLCN7) is the predominant member of the CLC protein family. Investigations on CLCN7 have focused primarily on its involvement in osteosclerosis and lysosomal storage disorders; nevertheless, its contribution to neuropathic pain has not been determined. In this investigation, we noted high expression of CLCN7 in neurons situated within the spinal dorsal horns and dorsal root ganglions (DRGs). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that CLCN7 was predominantly distributed among IB4-positive and CGRP-positive neurons. Furthermore, the expression of CLCN7 was observed to be mainly reduced in neurons within the spinal dorsal horns and in small- and medium-sized neurons located in the DRGs of spared nerve injury mice. Knockdown of CLCN7 via siRNA in the DRGs resulted in increased mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia in naïve mice. Furthermore, the excitability of cultured DRG neurons in vitro was augmented upon treatment with CLCN7 siRNA. These findings suggested that CLCN7 downregulation following SNI was crucial for the manifestation of mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia, highlighting potential targeting strategies for treating neuropathic pain.

Neuropathic pain (NP) refers to pain resulting directly from injury or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system and is characterized by spontaneous pain, heightened sensitivity to painful stimuli (hyperalgesia), perception of nonpainful stimuli as painful (allodynia), and abnormal sensations (paresthesia) (1). Classical pain-related theories, such as the Gate Theory of Pain, propose that spinal cord's interneurons have a gate control function in controlling the generation of pain (2). It is widely recognized that the overexcitability of primary afferent neurons caused by peripheral nerve injury (PNI) contributes to the development of neurological disorders (3). Increasing research indicates that the functions of ion channels, including potassium channels (4), voltage-gated calcium channels (5), voltage-gated sodium channels (6), acid-sensing ion channels (7), hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide gated cation channels (8), and transient receptor potential ion channels (9), are pivotal for the initiation, progression, and persistence of NP. Ion channel blockers, such as tetrodotoxin (9) and conotoxin (10), have potent analgesic effects; however, they also have a narrow therapeutic window. Previous studies have focused primarily on cation channels in the context of pain and ion channels, wherein alterations in ion channel activity result in modifications to the characteristics and expression levels of various categories of ion channels, including sodium channels, potassium ion channels, and calcium channels (11, 12, 13). Published data suggested that anion channels were implicated in pain perception; however, the precise role of specific Cl− channels remains elusive.

Chloride (Cl−) is undoubtedly a highly important neurophysiological anion that plays a crucial role in the inhibition of synaptic activity and the control of neuronal excitability within the fully developed nervous system (14). The generation and maintenance of Cl− gradients through various ion channels, exchangers, and cotransporters facilitate essential functions in neurons and glial cells. Moreover, Cl− itself can act as a downstream signaling molecule that triggers phosphorylation events to finely regulate neuronal inhibition in response to changes in neuronal activity (15, 16, 17). A reduction or impairment in the neuron-specific transporter KCC2, which is responsible for removing Cl− ions, is associated with the occurrence of seizures (18), NP (19, 20, 21), and autism spectrum disorders (22). The Cl− concentration within the neurons of dorsal root ganglions (DRGs) has been found to increase after either sciatic nerve division or inflammation, as evidenced by several investigations (23, 24). Mechanical hypersensitivity occurs following PNI due to the downregulation of chloride channel proteins in DRG neurons (25). In addition, the application of chloride channel inhibitors exacerbates mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia in a rat model of NP (26). These results suggest that chloride channels may play a critical role in the progression of NP.

Chloride voltage-gated channel seven (CLCN7) is an 89-kDa protein-coding gene with 803 amino acids located at the chromosomal locus 16p13.3 (27, 28). CLCN7 is structurally conserved across species, and no homologous protein has been identified in mammals. CLCN7 is exclusively associated with the single transmembrane domain (TMD) protein Ostm1 (29). This protein complex aids in the movement of 2Cl-/H+ ions across lysosomes and the ruffled border of osteoclasts (29, 30). Genetic mutations affecting the CLCN7 and Ostm1 genes have been associated with the development of osteopetrosis and neurodegenerative disorders (31, 32). The involvement of CLCN7/Ostm1 within the ruffled border of osteoclasts is highly likely to play a pivotal role in the acidification of resorption lacuna, facilitating efficient bone resorption by osteoclasts (32). The presence of a 2Cl-/H+ antiporter in lysosomes is crucial for the precise regulation of chloride concentration within the lumen (33). CLCN7 mRNA exhibited high expression levels in various regions, such as the brain, eye, spinal cord, DRG, and trigeminal ganglion, in mouse embryos (32). Furthermore, adult mice exhibit notable expression of CLCN7 mRNA in multiple neurons within the central nervous system (CNS), hepatocytes, the external medulla, lung tissue, the pancreas, and the testis (34). Jentsch et al. demonstrated that CLCN7-deficient mice exhibit a lysosomal storage disease resembling neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (35), further contributing to the neurodegeneration of both the brain and retina. The presence of significant inflammatory processes within the brain coincided with the observed neuronal loss in CLCN7 KO mice (36). Dr Malicdan’s research group has investigated the functional impact of the Y715C variant and revealed its ability to increase the current amplitude of CLCN7/Ostm1. As a result, affected individuals may experience delayed myelination in the brain and organomegaly, with a particular focus on kidney involvement (37).

The diverse properties of CLCN7 are evident, as it not only causes osteopetrosis with or without extraskeletal consequences but also has the potential to induce a pleiotropic syndrome. Despite numerous investigations into the pathological role of CLCN7 in diseases primarily characterized by osteopetrosis, there is currently a lack of research exploring the involvement of CLCN7 in the pathophysiology of NP. This study presented evidence demonstrating that CLCN7 contributes to NP development following spared nerve injury (SNI) in mice by modulating DRG neuron excitability.

Results

The expression of CLCN7 in the spinal dorsal horns was attenuated by SNI

At the beginning of this study, we employed an anti-CLCN7 antibody for immunohistochemical staining of spinal lumbar enlargement samples from mice, which revealed the expression of CLCN7 in the spinal dorsal horns (SDHs) (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we evaluated the expression of CLCN7 in the SDHs of the L4-L6 injured side at various time points post-SNI. The images shown in Fig. 1A indicated that the expression of CLCN7 in the affected SDHs gradually decreased as the injury progressed; the quantitative data for CLCN7 obtained by analysis were shown in Fig. 1B. Thereafter, we extracted SDHs from the sham and SNI groups on days 3, 7 and 14 and subjected them to reverse-transcription PCR (RT‒PCR) and Western blot analysis to quantitatively evaluate the changes in CLCN7 mRNA and protein expression levels within the SDHs of SNI model mice (Fig. 1, C and D). The CLCN7 level in the DRGs exhibited a significant decrease following SNI

During the process of pain perception, DRG neurons, which serve as primary nociceptive neurons, assume a crucial function in the fundamental processes of NP (38, 39). Ion channels closely associated with pain mechanisms are crucial for achieving targeted analgesia, specifically at the level of DRGs. Consequently, there has been significant interest in investigating the physiological characteristics of DRG neurons following nerve injury. Therefore, we subsequently examined CLCN7 expression following SNI in the DRGs. Among the L4-6 DRGs on the same side of the mice, at various time intervals after SNI, a notable reduction in the expression of both CLCN7 mRNA and protein was observed compared to that in the sham-operated mice, starting at 7 days post-surgery (Fig. 4, A and B). After SNI, the expression of CLCN7 continued to progressively decrease, exhibiting the most prominent reduction at 2 weeks post-injury among the examined time points. Consequently, we employed immunofluorescence staining to further elucidate the alterations in CLCN7 expression after SNI (Fig. 4C). Morphological distribution of CLCN7 in the DRGs

Subsequently, our objective was to ascertain the specific cellular subtype expressing CLCN7 in the DRGs. Initially, we performed co-labeling experiments employing a CLCN7 antibody along with NeuN (a neuron-specific marker) or GS (a satellite glial cell-specific marker) to determine the expression pattern of CLCN7 within DRGs. Surprisingly, DRG neurons exhibited high levels of CLCN7 (Fig. 5A), whereas no CLCN7 was detected in satellite glial cells (Fig. 5B). Double immunofluorescence staining revealed that CLCN7 co-expressed with NF200 (a marker for A-type neurons), CGRP (a marker for peptidergic C-type neurons), and IB4 (a marker for nonpeptidergic C-type neurons) (Fig. 5, C–E). The proportions of neurons positive for CLCN7 and NF200, CGRP, or IB4 were determined to be approximately 16.6%, 29.4%, and 47.6%, respectively (Fig. 5F). Based on our experimental findings, it has been demonstrated that CLCN7 exhibits predominant expression within DRG neurons across various size categories including small, medium, and large cells. Moreover, we observed a reduction in its expression levels after SNI. Nevertheless, the precise subtypes of DRG neurons primarily impacted by this phenomenon remain elusive. To address this knowledge gap, in our study, we conducted an examination of both ipsilateral and contralateral sides of DRGs in SNI mice. Silencing CLCN7 in DRG neurons resulted in a reduction in the mouse basal paw withdrawal threshold

Allodynia (pain in response to typically nonpainful stimuli) and hyperalgesia (heightened response to painful stimuli) are prominent symptoms observed in individuals suffering from NP. To investigate the potential impact of altering CLCN7 expression in sensory neurons on mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia, we evaluated the effect of reducing CLCN7 levels in DRG neurons on pain sensitivity through the administration of CLCN7 siRNA via DRG injection. Notably, only the group treated with CLCN7 siRNA exhibited a notable decrease in responsiveness to mechanical and thermal stimuli on the injected side (Fig. 7, A and C), while no discernible effect was observed on the contralateral (uninjured) side (Fig. 7, B and D). Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 7E, substantial suppression of CLCN7 mRNA expression was evident in the CLCN7 siRNA group, whereas the NC (negative control) siRNA group did not exhibit any noticeable difference. Reconfirmation of the efficacy of CLCN7 knockdown was achieved through protein-level validation (Fig. 7, F and G). In this study, we employed three distinct sequences of CLCN7 siRNAs to induce knockdown of CLCN7 in the DRG in vivo. All three CLCN7 siRNAs effectively downregulated CLCN7 expression in the DRGs to varying extents and resulted in increased mechanical sensitivity and thermal pain responses in mice (Fig. S2). This further substantiates the role of CLCN7 in modulating pain sensation in mice. However, for subsequent main experiments, we selected only one CLCN7 siRNA for knockdown, thereby optimizing economic and time resources. CLCN7 deficiency increased the excitability of small- and medium-sized DRG neurons

To explore the specific mechanisms responsible for the effects of CLCN7 knockdown in DRGs on nociception, we analyzed the action potential (AP) and resting membrane potential (RMP) properties of cultured DRG neurons treated with NC siRNA or CLCN7 siRNA. First, we assessed the knockdown efficiency of CLCN7 siRNA (at concentrations of 5 nM and 50 nM) in DRG neurons. Compared to that in the NC siRNA treatment group, CLCN7 mRNA expression was reduced by 64.8% (5 nM) and 82.0% (50 nM) following the administration of CLCN7 siRNA (Fig. 8A). The successful knockdown of CLCN7 was also confirmed at the protein level (Fig. 8B). Therefore, we selected a concentration of 50 nM for CLCN7 siRNA interference in subsequent experiments, as this concentration had the highest knockdown efficiency and ensured optimal cellular conditions. In current clamp mode, APs were induced by applying a depolarizing current of 30 pA. The basal current (current threshold), which refers to the minimum amount of current needed to elicit action potentials, was measured as an indicator of excitability. Additionally, the RMPs of DRG neurons treated with CLCN7 siRNA were significantly increased (Fig. 8E). Discussion

Our study demonstrated that CLCN7 was predominantly expressed in neurons located in the SDHs and DRGs, with a notable decrease in its expression following SNI. Importantly, knockdown of CLCN7 in the DRGs resulted in mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia on the injection side. Furthermore, downregulation of CLCN7 expression augmented the excitability of small- and medium-sized DRG neurons in vitro. These findings suggested that downregulation of CLCN7 led to enhanced excitability of DRG neurons upon mechanical and thermal stimulation.

The mammalian CLC family comprises a total of nine members, specifically denoted as CLCN1-CLCN7, CLCNKA, and CLCNKB (40). Among these genes, only CLCN3 has been identified as a key regulator of pathological pain (25, 40, 41, 42). The involvement of other CLCs in pathological pain remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Gene expression profile analysis following PNI revealed that CLCN7 was expressed at lower levels in DRGs after SNI (43), which offered novel insights into the potential involvement of CLCN7 in the NP. CLCN7 is ubiquitously expressed across various regions in mice, including the proximal tubules of the kidney (44), Purkinje fibers within the cerebellum, medulla oblongata in the brain; CLCN7 is also expressed in hepatocytes and testicular cells (34). CLCN7 exhibits predominant subcellular localization in lysosomes and on the surface of osteoclast folds (45). It is evident that CLCN7, a member of the family of CLC chloride channel proteins, is widely expressed throughout various regions of the body. To better explore the role of CLCN7 in NP sensitization, an investigation was conducted to determine the cellular localization of CLCN7 within the spinal cord and DRGs. In this investigation, it was noted that the expression of CLCN7 was not limited to the spinal cords but also extended throughout the DRGs. Interestingly, CLCN7 exhibited high expression levels in neurons, particularly in medium/small-volume neurons. The presence of pain-related ion channels in small DRG neurons located within the endoplasmic reticulum of the plasma membrane suggests their involvement in regulating nociception (46). Small- and medium-sized DRG neurons may transmit nociceptive signals triggered by thermal and mechanical stimuli (47). Furthermore, our findings demonstrated for the first time that SNI significantly downregulated CLCN7 expression in both SDH and DRG neurons. The superficial neurons in the SDHs play a crucial role in regulating pain perception through both excitatory and inhibitory interneurons and circuits, and when these circuits fail to function properly, individuals may experience heightened pain sensations (48, 49, 50). DRG neurons express a diverse array of molecules and pathways associated with pain mechanisms. Nerve injury induces modifications in DRG neurons, including changes in excitability, which are crucial in NP development (51). In conclusion, CLCN7 plays an important role in pain sensation. Knockdown of CLCN7 in the DRGs by siRNA resulted in mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia exclusively on the injected side, while no such effects were observed on the control side. These findings also eliminated any potential influence of our experimental procedure itself on the pain threshold. These findings suggested that CLCN7 serves as a negative regulator of NP development, and its downregulation is necessary for the induction of SNI-mediated nociceptive responses.

The primary sensory neurons in DRGs are responsible for sensing external stimuli and transmitting signals to the CNS (52). Following nerve injury, DRG neurons exhibit spontaneous hyperactivity (53), potentially attributed to abnormal signaling and adaptive alterations in receptors and ion channels at the injury site (54, 55). Subsequent neuroplastic changes in the ascending pain pathway may be triggered by increased neuronal activity in the DRGs, potentially playing a role in the development of chronic pain (56). The patch clamp experiments exclusively utilized small- and medium-sized DRG neurons. We noticed an increased occurrence of APs and RMPs in DRG neurons treated with CLCN7 siRNA when the same current injection was applied. These findings indicated that the heightened sensitivity of primary afferent neurons to mechanical and thermal stimuli in CLCN7-deficient mice may be attributed to an increase in DRG neuronal excitability.

When expressed at the cell plasma membrane, CLCN7 exhibits anion channel activity or acts as a Cl-/H+ antiporter at the lysosomal membrane (57). Research on the role of CLCN3 in NP has indicated that elimination of the Cl-/H+ exchanger CLCN3 through genetic methods leads to modifications in nociception in mice (58). We postulated that the potential mechanism underlying CLCN7-mediated pain could involve the modulation of the Cl¯ current in DRG neurons, leading to a reduction in neuronal excitability and pain hypersensitivity. Notably, defects in CLCN7 led to an increase in APs within DRG neurons, a parameter primarily regulated by Na+ channels. Furthermore, loss of CLCN7 elevated RMPs in DRG neurons, which are influenced mainly by voltage-dependent K+ channels. Therefore, modifications to Na+ or K+ channels could contribute to the heightened excitability and enhanced sensitivity to pain stimuli observed in mice lacking functional CLCN7 expression. However, further investigations are needed to confirm these potential mechanisms. Moreover, synaptic inhibition plays a pivotal role in the regulation of neuronal excitability, thereby exerting diverse effects on various functions of the nervous system, such as NP (59). The impact of this inhibitory process depends on the chloride gradient, which is established by cation–chloride cotransporters, such as NKCC1 and KCC2. KCC2 expression in SDH neurons was decreased following PNI. Moreover, in the trigeminal neuralgia model, NKCC1 was upregulated, while KCC2 was downregulated in neurons at the injury site, leading to an excitatory GABAergic response (20). Additionally, compelling evidence suggested that restoring KCC2 expression or function after injury could rebalance excitation and inhibition in the spinal cord, ultimately promoting recovery (60). However, further investigations of the molecular mechanism underlying the impact of CLCN7 on NP are needed, and all the related discussions have provided us with valuable insights. It was noteworthy that female mice were not included in our study, as investigating sex differences was not the primary objective. However, further investigation into the impact of CLCN7 on male and female disparities in pain models would be a valuable avenue to explore.

In conclusion, our current investigation presented preliminary findings indicating the presence of CLCN7 expression in SDH and DRG neurons, with a concomitant reduction observed after SNI. CLCN7 deficiency led to increased excitability of small- and medium-sized DRG neurons, thereby augmenting pain sensitivity. These findings suggested that targeting CLCN7 could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy for managing NP.

Experimental procedures

Animals

All ICR mice used in the study were sourced from the Experimental Animal Centre of Nantong University and exclusively consisted of male individuals. Throughout the entire experiment, the animals were accommodated in a facility with a 12-h light and dark cycle and free access to water and food. All animal experiments were approved by the Society for Animal Ethics of Nantong University (permission no. S20220303-005).

Surgery

All in vivo animal experiments were conducted using a specialized compact device designed for administering isoflurane anesthesia to small animals (RWD Life Science). An SNI model was established as previously described (61). The skin was cut at the upper edge of the hind limb, and the muscle was separated. The main trunk of the sciatic nerve and its lower branches (tibial nerve, common peroneal nerve, and sural nerve) were exposed. The tibial and common peroneal nerves were meticulously tied with 6-0 silk (Jinhuana). This step was crucial because it was imperative to preserve the complete integrity of the sural nerve. A tightly secured surgical knot was used, as limb contraction could be observed when the initial knot was firmly tied.

Behavioral assay

Von Frey test

The assessment of mechanical sensitivity was conducted by employing von Frey filaments and the up-down method following a previously described protocol (62). Briefly, after three consecutive days of acclimatization to the test environment, the mice were placed in a separate transparent test chamber situated on a barbed wire floor. Von Frey filaments of various strengths (0.16 g, 0.40 g, 0.60 g, and 1.00 g 2.00 g) were used to stimulate the plantar skin of the mice from low to high intensities. The stimulation was applied just enough to flex the filaments and was maintained for 1 to 2 s. A positive response was characterized by rapid paw withdrawal, paw licking, or paw flinching.

Hargreaves test

A Hargreaves assessment was conducted utilizing a Hargreaves Bolometer (manufactured by IITC Life Science). The experimental mice were housed in a glass enclosure that was furnished with a glass plate measuring 2 mm in thickness at the base. After acclimating to the testing surroundings and achieving a reasonably consistent condition, we focused an intense thermal ray towards the hind paw for the purpose of detection. To prevent potential harm caused by high laser intensity on the mouse's foot sole, we established a baseline paw withdrawal latency of 8 to 12 s, with a maximum cut-off time of 20 s. At least 5 min elapsed between each exposure, and we calculated an average based on three exposures.

The behavioral experiments were conducted in a double-blind manner, ensuring that the individuals responsible for testing and placing the mice were different from each other and that the tester remained unaware of the specific grouping of the mice.

DRG injection

The hair was shaved, and the skin was disinfected, followed by a small incision made above the posterior superior iliac spine to expose the subcutaneous muscle. The first junction of the spinous process and aponeurosis served as a marker for localization. A muscle incision near the left spinous process was made to access the conus, revealing the left intervertebral joint and lamina. In the prone position, we located the DRG below this joint. Using eye scissors, we carefully removed part of the articular eminence along the lumbar spine until it reached the lower border of the previous cone and intercepted it with the superior articular eminence of the next cone in the horizontal direction. This osteotomy exposed an accessory process where we found the DRG nested within its axilla after gently separating the surrounding connective tissue. All injections were conducted using a microprocessor-controlled injection system (RWD Life Science) with a glass capillary injection needle gently inserted into the L5 DRG core. Subsequently, an accurately measured quantity of siRNA was administered at a rate of 3 μl/min through a calibrated glass microelectrode connected to an infusion pump. All siRNAs were obtained from RiboBio (https://www.ribobio.com). The sequence of the mouse NC siRNA was 5′-GGCUCUAGAAAAGCCUAUGC-3′. The sequence of the mouse CLCN7 siRNA was 5′-CACAGCCACAGTTGCATTT-3′. The sequence of the mouse CLCN7-2 siRNA was 5′-GACAACATTGACAAGTTCA-3′. The sequence of the mouse CLCN7-3 siRNA was 5′- CAAGATACCGCCTAGGAAA-3′. The provided NC siRNA was a universal nonsense sequence designed for humans, mice, and rats. All siRNAs were meticulously designed and rigorously tested for targeting and off-target using NCBI Blast by Ribobio. Briefly, 1 nmol of CLCN7 siRNA or control siRNA was administered once in a volume of 1.5 μl. The dosage of siRNA administered into the DRG in vivo was manipulated following the protocol provided by Ribobio. Additionally, a comprehensive literature review was conducted (63), and our research group previously demonstrated successful knockdown by intradermal injection of siRNAs into the DRG of mice at this specific concentration.

Primary DRG neuron culture

The experimental subjects utilized in this investigation were 6-week-old male ICR mice. All DRGs from both sides of the spinal cord were meticulously extracted under sterile conditions. After extraction, the DRGs were washed with dissection solution (PBS (HyClone, SH30256.01) with streptomycin (100 mg/ml) and penicillin (100 U/ml) (PS) (Nacalai Tesque)). Subsequently, 500 μl collagenase A (Roche, COLLA-RO) (2 mg/ml) was used to digest the DRGs for 60 min in 37 °C. This was followed by treatment with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Sigma‒Aldrich, T4049) for 10 min in a controlled environment, after which the temperature was maintained at 37 °C. During this process, the centrifuge tube was gently inverted every 10 min to ensure thorough digestion. The digestion process was terminated by incubating the samples in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). The digestion was concluded by gently blowing the tip of a 1 ml pipette until no visible precipitation was observed. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was carefully collected. Subsequently, Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin was added to resuspend the cells, which were then cultured in medium Petri dish at 37 °C for at least 1 h to collect the high-purity neuronal supernatant. The cells obtained from this process were then cultured on small Petri dishes precoated with poly-D-lysine (Sigma‒Aldrich, A-003-M). These cells were cultured in neurobasal A medium (Gibco, 2192595) supplemented with B27 (Gibco, 2415396) at a concentration of 2%, glutamine (Sigma‒Aldrich, 1294808) at a concentration of 1%, and Recombinant Mouse beta-NGF Protein (RD systems, 1156-NG). The transfection of siRNA was performed concurrently with the seeding of DRG neurons, followed by medium replacement after 12 h. The culture was then continued for an additional 36 to 48 h before sample collection for assessing the efficiency of siRNA transfection and conducting patch-clamp recordings.

Immunohistochemistry

The animals were administered isoflurane for deep anaesthesia, followed by sequential perfusion with saline and precooled 4% paraformaldehyde. The excised L4-L6 spinal cords and DRGs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight or for 6 h, respectively. Afterwards, the tissues were transferred to a solution of 30% sucrose until they sank. Transverse sections of the spinal cord measuring 30 μm in thickness were obtained, while a freezing microtome was used to obtain planes of DRGs measuring 12 μm in thickness. Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining was conducted. All sections were blocked using an immunohistochemical blocking solution (Beyotime, P0102) at a room temperature for 2 h. The sections were subsequently incubated with the primary antibody for a duration of approximately 24 h at a temperature of 4 °C and subsequently incubated with Cy3- or 488-labeled secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:1000) overnight at the same temperature. The used primary antibodies included Clcn7 (Novus, catalog: NBP1-91791; Research Resource Identifier [RRID]: AB_11029200, 1:250); NeuN (MILLIPORE, catalog: MAB377, [RRID]: AB_2298772, 1:500); GFAP (MILLIPORE, catalog: MAB360, [RRID]: AB_11212597, 1:1000); Iba1 (Wako, catalog: 019-19741, [RRID]: AB_839504, 1:100); A2B5 (Abcam, catalog: ab53521, [RRID]: AB_1209137, 1:500); PDGFRα (RD systems, catalog: AF1062-SP, 1:50); GS (BD Biosciences, catalog: 610517, RRID: AB_397879, 1:100); NF200 (Sigma‒Aldrich, catalog: N0142, RRID: AB_477257, 1:200); CGRP (Abcam, catalog: ab81887, RRID: AB_1658411, 1:100); MAP2 (MILLIPORE, catalog: MAB3418, RRID:AB_94856, 1:200); GAD65 (Abcam, catalog: ab26113, RRID:AB_448989, 1:200), and FITC-conjugated IB4 (Sigma‒Aldrich, catalog: L2140, RRID: AB_2313663, 1:100). The slides were subsequently sealed with 30 μl of Antifade Mounting Medium containing Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, P0133). The stained sections were examined using a Nikon fluorescence microscope and a Leica confocal microscope.

Quantitative analysis of CLCN7 immunofluorescence intensity in mice

The microscope settings for spinal cord and DRG images, employed to investigate the same phenomenon (e.g., changes in CLCN7 fluorescence intensity before and after SNI, as well as knockdown efficiency following DRG injection with CLCN7 siRNA), were consistently maintained throughout the duration of this study, respectively. Each group consisted of four or five mice. For the spinal cord tissue, approximately 80 sections (30 μm thick) were prepared per mouse. From these sections, one slice with a similar planar position was selected in each animal, followed by the selection of two or three random slices for analysis. A region measuring 1200 × 600 pixel2 was positioned in the SDHs, and fluorescence intensity calculations were conducted using thresholds through the analysis-measure plug-in in Image J. Regarding DRG tissue, six slices per mouse were prepared, encompassing 4 to 6 serial sections each (12 μm thick). Like the analysis of spinal cord tissue, one slice with a comparable planar position was chosen from each animal along with two or three random slices for subsequent analysis. Fluorescence intensity calculations were performed based on thresholds utilizing the analysis-measure plug-in in Image J. To account for potential errors caused by background staining intensity, manual counting was employed as the determination standard for co-standardization, with consideration given to cells exhibiting similar morphology and complete staining overlap. Image analysis was carried out by individuals blinded to the treatment conditions.

Co-expression analysis

Three to six mice were selected from each experimental group, and two to three random tissue sections were chosen from each mouse for co-expression analysis. The values obtained from the randomly selected sections were subsequently averaged, yielding a representative value denoted as a “n.” We employed the following methodologies to assess the co-expression of CLCN7 with various cells in SDH and DRG, respectively. The outcomes obtained from each approach were consistent, and only the results derived from one method were presented in the results section. The percentage change before and after SNI was individually calculated for each mouse using the formula "(percentage of CLCN7 co-expression in each cell (Contra.) - percentage of CLCN7 co-expression in each cell (Ipsi.))/percentage of CLCN7 co-expression in each cell (Contra.)" and subsequently averaged.

Automatic and manual counting of cells co-expression (SDH & DRG)

Automatic counting of cells co-expression was performed using Image J (SDH)

Next, apply the appropriate threshold by adjusting the slider to accurately depict the target element (Image → Adjust → Threshold). Remove any background interference by clearing it using Image Adjust Threshold function (0-Infinity) (note that consistent staining samples should have the same threshold setting). Finally, automatically generate and extract the desired value of the target element (Analyse → Analyse Particles). The co-expression percentage was determined by dividing the number of cells positive for both CLCN7 and other cell markers (numerator) by the total number of CLCN7+ cells (denominator).

Pearson's correlation coefficient in Image J (SDH & DRG)

Electrophysiological recordings

All measurements were performed on neurons exhibiting diameters between 17 and 25 μm, which fall within the small to medium range. The electrophysiological recordings were conducted on cells derived from three distinct cultures. Patch-clamp recording experiments were carried out using an EPC10 amplifier (HEKA) and patch master software following the previously described protocol (64). Pipettes were created from borosilicate capillaries and polished until they reached a resistance of 4 to 6 MΩ. The recordings underwent a filtering process at 2 kHz and were sampled at a rate of 10 kHz. The resistance of the cells following membrane rupture ranged from 300 to 800 mΩ. For the recordings of AP and RMP, the extracellular solution was composed of 140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Hepes, and 10 mM glucose. To generate APs and RMPs, we employed the current-clamp mode and applied a range of current pulses to induce AP firing. The current pulses were incrementally increased from 0 to 70 pA in steps of 10 pA, with a total of seven sweeps. Each sweep lasted for 700 ms, followed by a 2-s interval before the next one. The pH was adjusted to approximately 7.4 using NaOH. The internal solution inside the electrode contained 136 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EGTA, 10 mM Hepes, and Mg-ATP2. The pH was adjusted to a value of 7.3 with KOH.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the mean ± SD obtained from a minimum of three independent experiments, and statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software. All data were tested for a normal distribution. When comparing two groups, we employed the student’s t test. One-way ANOVA was employed to compare multiple categories at a single time point, while two-way ANOVA was used to assess multiple categories across multiple time points. Subsequent analyses were conducted using Dunnett or Bonferroni correction methods. The significance level (p) was categorized as follows: n.s. (no significance), p > 0.05; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001. No data were excluded from the quantitative analyses conducted in this study.

Conclusions

In this study, we have presented novel evidence demonstrating the high expression of CLCN7 in both SDH and DRG neurons. Furthermore, there was a noticeable decrease in the expression of CLCN7 in both SGHs and DRGs after SNI. CLCN7 deficiency results in increased DRG neuron excitability, thereby enhancing pain sensitivity. These findings underscore the pivotal role of CLCN7 in modulating DRG neuron excitability as a potential mechanism underlying SNI-induced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity.
