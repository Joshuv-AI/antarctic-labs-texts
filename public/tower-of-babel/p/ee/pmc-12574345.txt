Development and pilot of the BC Wildfire Smoke and Extreme Heat Action Plan: empowering patients with climate health readiness
Authors: Rose He, Erin Shellington, Prabjit Barn, Karen Rideout, Agustin Bueso, Isha Joshi, Stacey Maddocks, Pat G Camp, Mary Crocker, Eric Coker, Tina Afshar, Jacqueline Turvey, Emily Brigham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobally, wildfire smoke and extreme heat events are increasing in frequency and intensity. Western Canada, including the Province of British Columbia (BC), is impacted annually by these events, resulting in the accelerated development of public health messaging and emergency preparedness. It is particularly important to reach, educate and empower individuals who are highly susceptible to climate events, such as those with respiratory diseases, through targeted communication strategies delivered by trusted sources. We aimed to develop an evidence-informed action plan (AP) tool and pilot integration into clinical encounters with patients living with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).The project team developed a draft tool—a BC Wildfire Smoke and Extreme Heat AP document inspired by the concept of an Asthma AP—along with a guide to support healthcare providers in addressing questions during patient counselling sessions. Iterative feedback from trained patient partners, clinicians and knowledge translation specialists was incorporated to refine messaging and delivery. Use of the tool was piloted in clinical encounters between certified respiratory educators (CREs) and patients living with asthma and COPD in two regional health authorities. Additional process and content feedback was gathered via questionnaires and focus groups.Patients (project participants) reported that AP tool use increased their understanding and preparedness for wildfire smoke and extreme heat events. While the plan was positively received by providers in a CRE role, time constraints and staffing capacity were highlighted as barriers to implementation. Suggested improvements included strengthened public awareness, preseason deployment and enhancement of content and delivery. Additional quality improvement cycles are needed to increase readability, accessibility and actionability.Keywords: Asthma, Communication, Healthcare quality improvement

Problem

Climate change is driving drier and hotter conditions, leading to more frequent, prolonged and intense wildfires and extreme heat events internationally. 1 2 Among the Canadian provinces, British Columbia (BC) has been one of the most significantly impacted by these conditions, with record-breaking wildfire activity in 2017, 2018 and 2023.3 In 2021, an unprecedented extreme heat event struck BC. With outdoor temperatures soaring up to 49.6°C, this event exposed key vulnerabilities in extreme heat preparedness and 619 deaths were directly attributed. 4

Climate projections indicate a quadrupling of extreme fire year potential in Canada by the end of the century and increasing frequency of extreme temperatures such as the 2021 heat dome. 5,7 Given BC’s climate vulnerability, maximising public health outreach is critical to reducing population mortality and morbidity. Public health organisations, including the BC Centre for Disease Control, National Collaborating Centre for Environmental Health, regional health authorities, and others, have been active in launching extensive public-facing health messaging within BC. 8,10 However, tools facilitating communication between healthcare providers and patients are lacking.

Certified Respiratory Educators (CREs) work directly with a known susceptible population; individuals living with chronic respiratory disease. CREs are trained respiratory therapists or registered nurses who work closely with physicians to provide wrap-around care to patients with asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in home or clinical settings. Of note, asthma and COPD are highly prevalent respiratory diseases (~9% age-standardised population prevalence for each disease in BC, applying only among those over 35 years of age for COPD), 11 representing a key demographic in which to prioritise preparedness. While CREs typically provide environmental counselling to clients, there is currently no formal pathway for providing wildfire smoke and extreme heat (WSEH)-related education.

This project aimed to develop and pilot a health education tool that could be tailored to patient preferences and resources. Inspired by the growing health risks from climate-related events and the susceptibility of patients with chronic respiratory disease to WSEH, the tool was developed and then implemented amongst patients with asthma and COPD and CREs in two regional health authorities (Vancouver Coastal Health and Interior Health). Vancouver Coastal Health serves metropolitan Vancouver and remote coastal areas, while Interior Health covers urban, rural and remote communities in the south-central province and historically experiences more frequent and severe wildfire and heat events. 7 12 13 Both health authorities work to align and disseminate public health messaging, serving a total of 2 million patients. Measurable outcomes of the project included facilitators and barriers to implementation, perception of usefulness and patient-reported preparedness for the upcoming season.

Background

Wildfire smoke is a complex mixture of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and gaseous components. 14 It is widely acknowledged that PM2.5 is a leading environmental health risk factor globally, causing local and systemic oxidative stress and inflammation when inhaled and resulting in an estimated 4 million deaths annually. 15 Within Canada, wildfires are the most significant contributor to ambient (outdoor) PM2.5, which can travel thousands of miles from the source to impact outdoor and indoor air quality over extensive regions. 16,19

Air pollution and temperature are key environmental triggers for patients living with asthma and COPD—the two most common chronic respiratory conditions worldwide. 20,23 Exposure to wildfire PM2.5 is linked to increased medication use and dispensations, emergency department visits, hospitalisations, respiratory morbidity and all-cause mortality. 24,27 Patients with chronic respiratory disease also demonstrate heightened susceptibility to heat-related illnesses such as heat exhaustion and heat stroke, which can lead to malaise, altered mentation, multiorgan failure and, eventually, death. 28,31 Co-exposure to heat and wildfire smoke may pose greater health risks than exposure to either condition alone, compounding public health concerns and complicating the delivery of tailored recommendations to susceptible populations and/or communities at risk of co-exposure. 32 33

Current asthma and COPD management strategies emphasise using disease-specific action plans (APs), along with regular clinician-guided review, to help patients understand self-monitoring, implement self-management and recognise when to seek help. 34,36 APs have been shown to reduce hospitalisations, reduce emergency room visits and improve quality of life in patients with asthma and reduce the duration of exacerbations and the risk of hospitalisations in patients with COPD, 37 38 but do not contain specific recommendations for mitigating exposure to WSEH.

Although healthcare providers are already engaged with counselling patients regarding WSEH, many have expressed a desire for additional resources to better meet their patients' needs. 39 Diverse methods of communication and personalised messaging have been identified as strategies to optimise receipt, understanding and action of WSEH public health recommendations. 40,42

We therefore applied the process elements of the familiar asthma-specific and COPD-specific APs to the creation, refinement and dissemination of the ‘BC Wildfire Smoke and Extreme Heat AP.’ The initial concept of adapting the AP format for WSEH was derived from a 2022 University of BC Continuing Medical Education workshop, ‘Climate Change: Understanding and Managing the Significant Health Impacts of Wildfire Smoke.’ 43 Participants, including public health experts and clinicians, identified the potential usefulness of an AP to be used on smoky days.

Measurement

Measurement took various forms, depending on the needs associated with the project’s stage (AP development and refinement and implementation/postimplementation).

Development and Refinement (pertaining to plan-do-study-act cycles 1 and 2)

Unstructured feedback was solicited and incorporated from trained patient partners (members of the Legacy for Airway Health Community Partner Committee44, contributing to project design and ensuring meaningful outcomes), clinicians and knowledge translation specialists via group feedback sessions or individually by email during initial development and refinement.

Implementation/Post-implementation (pertaining to plan-do-study-act cycle 3)

The AP was introduced and provided by the CRE at an initial education session with patients in the community (clinic visit or home visit, dependent on CRE workflow). After this interaction, intended to be a shared counselling session for AP completion, patients were invited to complete an optional, short survey (online supplemental file 1) with space for additional (open-ended) comments. The surveys were designed by the study team to specifically evaluate perceived understanding and preparedness in response to the AP. Post-implementation, 90-minute focus groups were planned and held with CREs from Vancouver Coastal Health and Interior Health. One Vancouver Coastal Health CRE could not attend the scheduled focus group and was interviewed separately by the project physician. Focus groups were selected as an opportunity to receive detailed, contextualised feedback after a season of deployment.

The focus groups were conducted over Zoom video conferencing by a dedicated facilitator using a semistructured interview guide (online supplemental file 2). Prepared guiding questions, created by the project team and focused on RE experiences providing the AP to patients, were provided to facilitators and organised into specific Mechanisms of Action based on the Theory and Techniques Tool as well as implementation strategies. 45 Mechanisms of Action included environmental context and resources, knowledge, skills, beliefs about capabilities, beliefs about consequences and implementation strategies. Facilitators were encouraged to elicit open-ended feedback from participants. Audio recordings were transcribed. Transcripts were then de-identified, openly coded by two reviewers for thematic analysis and reconciled by a third party.

As all assessments were focused on the tool and its implementation (quasi-experimental design), all observed outcomes were inferred to be a direct result of the intervention. The design of the survey and interview guide was customised to the project, ensuring content validity.

Design

The original project team included a physician (practising respirologist focused on asthma and COPD care in Vancouver Coastal Health), knowledge translation experts embedded within the Legacy for Airway Health Program at Vancouver Coastal Health Research Institute, trained patient partners and a core group of CREs. Given existing CRE involvement in environmental counselling as part of respiratory care, and familiarity with individualised AP deployment, CREs were identified as ideal partners for the pilot process. Additional project members joined during the course of the project, including several students and a project coordinator.

The overarching concept of the AP tool was to support discussion and education regardless of health or disease state (e.g., not specific to respiratory recommendations). Patients living with asthma and COPD were considered an ideal population in which to pilot the intervention. Rationale for study population choice included: (1) familiarity with the concept of APs, (2) existing clinical infrastructure and personnel dedicated to asthma and COPD AP dissemination, (3) recognised susceptibility, as well as hallmark environmental sensitivity in populations living with asthma and COPD and (4) expertise of the project team.

A provider guide was created and paired with the AP tool, documenting not only recommended steps for preparedness but also common questions and links to more information (including from public health resources informing the plan, e.g., BC Centre for Disease Control, Health Authorities and the National Collaborating Centre for Environmental Health). The provider guide was also intended to facilitate provider learning and comfort with counselling patients through the AP implementation process.

Prior to the AP project, the project team was already engaged in multiple collaborative initiatives, including with the trained patient partners in the Legacy for Airway Health programme. 44 Pre-existing relationships, mandates and resources within this network across the continuum of care and discovery supported sustainability of the intervention if well-received. Specific factors included: (1) project physician leadership in wildfire and climate health research, supporting updates to evidence and guidance when relevant, (2) opportunity for ongoing, seamless use of the tool by CREs within each health authority post-project if successful and (3) a centralised website for sharing the plan and updates therein with appropriate context for use.

Strategy

The initial BC WSEH AP was drafted by the project physician and knowledge translation specialists and iteratively improved and piloted through the plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles (figure 1). Early cycles were loosely structured to allow for flexibility in process and rapid development. The AP was officially launched in the winter of 2022, and the third PDSA cycle was completed in summer of 2023. A fourth PDSA cycle is ongoing.

PDSA cycle 1

The initial draft of the AP was presented to four trained patient partners from the Legacy for Airway Health Community Partner Committee 44 for detailed feedback via a 1-hour Zoom meeting. Three knowledge translation specialists and the project physician were present. Patient partners suggested expanding the topics covered in the plan beyond wildfire smoke alone to both WSEH. After incorporating these suggestions, the revised plan was sent back for further comments, which were integrated as feasible.

PDSA cycle 2

The updated AP and the developed provider guide were circulated via email to three CREs and an additional respirologist. Within the content of the email, respondents were requested to comment on content and clarity, length, practicality of printing the AP and practicality of tracking which patients received the AP. Responses were received from the three CREs, and feedback was incorporated into the AP, the provider guide and structure for implementation.

PDSA cycle 3

For project implementation, following operational and privacy approval from Vancouver Coastal Health and Interior Health, each RE was provided with a folder containing preprinted copies of: (1) the AP (onlinesupplemental files 3 A B), (2) companion provider guide (onlinesupplemental files 4 A B), (3) optional patient survey (online supplemental file 1) and (4) study flyer.

CREs conducted a counselling session with patients and offered the AP to provide individualised guidance and education on WSEH preparedness. CREs were requested to offer the AP to patients over 18 years of age with asthma or COPD. Patient selection beyond these criteria was at the discretion of the individual CRE, based on time available within the CRE’s schedule, patient availability and perceived benefit of the AP for the individual patient. This approach was chosen to respect the professional discretion of the CREs, optimise the convenience of the pilot and understand the feasibility in workflow. Similarly, CREs were not asked to keep a formal tracking of the number of patients that received the AP; while doing so may have provided an opportunity for expanded understanding of feasibility and acceptance, this was viewed as potentially burdensome for the CRE.

Patients who received the AP were subsequently given a flier describing the project and the option to participate via completion of the project survey. Patients were informed that their participation was voluntary, and the flier outlined that choosing to provide feedback served as implied consent. To facilitate completion and reduce patient burden, the survey was limited to six questions and a single space for optional, written commentary. A stamped envelope addressed to the study team was provided for anonymous completion, allowing for feedback delivery without the need for additional CRE time or support.

PDSA cycle 4

This cycle is ongoing and involves dissemination of the AP for customisation to local contexts. Knowledge translation is incorporated through webinars and public dissemination, in addition to development of implementation support, with a focus on adaptation and implementation of the AP.

Results

WSEH action plan

Briefly, the version of the AP (see onlinesupplemental files 3A B for drafts launched with each health authority) resulting from the feedback obtained in PDSA cycles 1 and 2 was a two-sided document (figure 2) that could be placed in a prominent, accessible area within the patient’s home (eg, on the refrigerator) for ready reference. On side 1 was space to note the patient’s name, date of completion of the AP, care professional and emergency contact name and contact number, and three sections to complete with the provider.

Content of the single-page action plan document. AQHI, Air Quality Health Index; PM2.5, fine particulate matter.

Within the first section, ‘I know my air quality and temperature,’ the patient was provided with options and asked to identify which publicly accessible air quality and temperature data source they would check routinely. The provider guide (see onlinesupplemental files 4 A B) contained further details regarding the Canadian Air Quality Health Index (AQHI), 46 the national communication tool developed to index relative health risk of air pollutants (specifically ground-level ozone, PM2.5 and nitrogen oxide). Real-time AQHI data are continually updated at the national level by Health Canada and Environment and Climate Change Canada and are available to the public via links provided in the AP. The AP further directed patients to check smoke and weather conditions at least daily.

Within the second section, ‘My home and supplies are ready,’ the patient was provided with a supply checklist for readiness for WSEH. Concerns were raised with early feedback (PDSA cycle 2) that seeing a checklist could contribute to patients feeling inadequately prepared if they were unable to obtain all items or complete all steps; in response, providers were guided to reassure and empower patients with the messaging that each additional action they could do improved their preparedness. Listed steps and items included: preparing emergency medications, extra food, acquiring a home thermometer, window coverings, air conditioner/fan/heat pump, air cleaners, masks and designating a clean and cool air room. Specifics for each step, including qualifications for tax credits for air cleaners and air conditioners, were included in the accompanying provider guide. Suggestions for advanced discussions with employers and service suppliers for built-in heating/cooling/filtration systems when already present were also provided.

Within the third section, ‘I have a plan for during an event,’ the patient was asked to identify and record emergency and safe transport contacts alongside locations where they could and would plan to relocate if cleaner or cooler air is needed. This section also provided space to identify a buddy and daily contact time, for safety checks during events.

On side 2 (see onlinesupplemental files 3 A B), blank spaces were provided to allow CREs and other clinicians to record personalised health messaging in accordance with hourly PM2.5 and AQHI risk profiles. Within the provider guide, a copy of the provincial recommendations for health messaging based on risk was available as a point of reference, though providers could choose to customise based on prior or projected reactions to smoke. Additionally, a table with locations for individualised messaging based on body temperature and indoor temperatures was provided, with examples in the provider guide, as well as guidance for cooling the body and the home.

Both sides of the plan contained the phone number of the health information line (811) and for medical emergencies (911).

Patient surveys

Six completed surveys were received by mail from patients who had completed a session with the plan. All respondents agreed that the AP increased understanding and preparation for WSEH, that they understood the information in the AP, and that they believed the tool would help them better prepare for both WSEH emergencies.

Participants were given the opportunity to leave comments. One participant remarked that “[the] AP is excellent. [They were] learning a great deal and [had] things planned to move on forward with making [their] life better.” Another participant acknowledged that although the tool was helpful for emergency education, they expressed concerns about feeling ill-equipped due to the lack of a personal support network. They relied on their CRE as a primary resource and noted that they felt lost and unsure of whom else to reach out to during a WSEH.

Respiratory educator focus groups

Post-implementation, two, 90-minute focus groups were planned and held with CREs from Vancouver Coastal Health (n=3, 8 May 2023) and Interior Health (n=2, 16 June 2023). One Vancouver Coastal Health CRE could not attend the scheduled focus group and was interviewed separately by the project physician.

Themes from the two CRE focus groups and one interview have been organised into two categories: (1) AP information and rollout/administration and (2) recommendations for tool enhancement (table 1). Facilitators to implementation: The inclusion of the provider guide with inline comments was highlighted as helpful. “I think it was very thorough, the packet that was put together was very helpful, very thorough.” Previous experience with WSEH, along with timing of receipt closer to the high-risk season, was perceived as factors that motivated patient interest in the AP: “In the summer when we’re talking to our clients and there’s active fires, smoke going on … it’s more pertinent and we’re asking them more like, oh, how are you doing in the house, like are you staying in, are you going out, … have you got a fan, have you got a HEPA filter like we’re asking that kind of stuff so but in the winter we’re not asking … because then they’re having [respiratory] flare-ups in the winter, so we’re talking flare-ups all winter.”

Barriers to implementation: Time constraints were noted as a barrier, particularly when CREs spoke with patients with limited English language skills. “I try to briefly go through this with somebody where we use a translator. It took a lot of time and I think about halfway through we kind of gave up and then we started talking about something else and then they’re like we’ll just take this and then they just took it with them so we did not go through the whole thing because it would take so long.” CREs expressed concerns about staffing capacity and competing work priorities and demands. “It just came up that we had so many other things that we needed to get done that it was an extra thing that was important and good to do and good to know…, we kind of had to pick and choose what to do for that visit.” Additionally, they highlighted difficulties navigating multiple web links during the timeframe of a patient visit.

Additional challenges included disinterest among some patients, perceived lack of novelty of information from others and restricted autonomy/ability to act on recommendations: “the ones that are the most disadvantaged that would need someone or their family,” “I mean some of this stuff or a lot of it might just be completely irrelevant to their life, they can’t even foresee themselves…doing any of it and maybe the only one thing that they would get from it is like here’s where you find a cooling station,…some of our patients don’t have phones or they couldn’t even possibly…pull up any of these links.”

Suggested content and delivery enhancements: CREs recommended offering both paper and electronic versions of the plan to broaden accessibility. Other suggestions included reducing written content, using graphics for visual simplicity, using quick response codes instead of links for ease of access, developing fridge magnets for simplified reminders and distributing relevant sections of the AP based on patient needs. Translating the plan into multiple languages was considered an important step in broadening the accessibility and readability of the tool.

Increasing provider education and information access: CREs suggested strategies such as developing accredited training webinars and presentations or creating a central hub of information for ease of access during consultations.

Dissemination: CREs suggested developing specific patient selection criteria to identify those at highest risk and streamline delivery. Taking into consideration previous challenges, CREs suggested developing a separate AP tailored for populations with limited technological access. Recognising limited time and capacity, CREs also suggested creating a seasonal team dedicated to implementing the tool.

Creating supplementary resources: CREs indicated a potential benefit from creating educational material for families and caregivers of patients. Suggestions included developing pamphlets with lay-language information, videos for individuals seeking virtual care and more opportunities for follow-up. To enhance the reception of supplementary resources, REs suggested exploring the possibility of creating a provincial AP registration system for individuals with chronic disease and facilitating collaborations between government and healthcare providers to share resources.

Improving partner awareness: CREs proposed partnerships with community-based organisations and social workers, along with publishing the AP on online educational platforms and offering presentations on public media like television and radio to increase awareness. They also suggested supplying educational posters at primary health centres, organising community pop-ups and presenting the tool at conferences and talks with local groups.

Lessons and limitations

Strengths of the project include use of a pre-existing, proven strategy (asthma and COPD APs) for dissemination and personalisation of preparedness and action planning for WSEH. Implementation in a patient population and by CREs familiar with APs expedited the project timeline. Due to BC’s history of wildfire events, there was a wealth of public-facing resources for exposure mitigation, which were leveraged in the evidence-informed development of the BC WSEH AP. Finally, a collaborative network of clinicians, patient partners, knowledge translation experts and CREs focused on improving respiratory health in BC provided an ideal foundation for developing this initiative.

As a grassroots initiative, staffing availability for the development, implementation and maintenance of the plan was variable. To overcome this, meticulous documentation was maintained, and a larger study team was established to allow for flexibility in task allocation, ultimately expanding opportunity, creativity, and growth of the initiative.

The AP included many links to public health resources, but these were intermittently updated or relocated, rendering some links non-functional. Recurrently checking and updating links was time-consuming for the project team and reduced resource accessibility; a potential solution would be centralisation of relevant resources on a public site. Since the AP’s inception, there has been greater consolidation of resources within and across regional health authorities, reducing the need for frequent updates.

Provider time was anticipated and confirmed to be a barrier during implementation. Several creative suggestions emerged from the focus groups (see Results) and afterwards, including a suggestion from public health officials to create a version that could be implemented largely by community-based organizations, excluding specific medical advice. Indeed, a component of PDSA 4 is likely to involve collaboration with community-based organizations. Ideally, formal sustainability assessments will be integrated at each future stage of evolution/adaptation of the plan to guide and support ongoing efforts within each system.

With regard to health messaging and implementation, our team developed a greater appreciation for the importance of local and individual context. Vancouver Coastal Health and Interior Health encompass metro, urban, rural and remote regions, each with unique challenges and characteristics that influence healthcare access and community support. 47 Specific CREs within these health authorities serve varying patient populations (caseload mixes of asthma and COPD may vary widely), in different locations (home versus clinic versus hospital) and different regions with different levels of individual and community resources. Creating an AP that is applicable to all these contexts while maintaining streamlined messaging and maximising efficiency is a significant challenge and raises the possibility of the need for context-dependent variations in the AP. While our initial goal was to create an AP that could translate across these contexts, this approach may ultimately be less successful than several customised versions. Indeed, we had already conceived that customisation might be optimal for specific health groups (children, pregnant persons, non-English speaking), and this conclusion follows a similar logic.

Our sample size was relatively small, and we were not able to gather data on response rates among those who received the plan due to limitations in design aimed at reducing burden. Additionally, due to our recruitment methodology, those who lack access to a regular health care provider were likely not well represented; 13% of British Columbians do not have access to a regular health professional, and therefore this represents a source of selection bias. 48 It is further possible that selection bias resulted in more favourable survey results if participants were more inclined to be receptive to health interventions or were more likely to complete the survey if they had a positive experience with the AP. Response bias is also a potential contributor. Any such biases are potentially amplified by the small sample size. Further, as the intervention was only piloted among CREs and patients with asthma and/or COPD, results are not applicable to the workflow of other healthcare providers or other patient populations.

While the feedback from the CREs was insightful and highly informative, ideally, further narrative feedback would have been obtained directly from patients. Additionally, to expedite the plan’s distribution and alleviate the workload of CREs, we did not formally track the number and characteristics of patients who were engaged with the AP. Consequently, we did not have contact information to reach out to patients beyond those that had filled out the questionnaire and consented to contact. As such, we cannot provide further characterisation of plan uptake or follow-up for effectiveness during the ensuing WSEH season.

Conclusion

Wildfire smoke and extreme heat events are a growing public health concern. The volatility of extreme weather events and the susceptibility of populations with chronic health conditions underscore the importance of proactive education and support.

Our project provides evidence that a targeted AP may be a useful tool to educate and prepare patients for WSEH. Although results demonstrate the implemented version of the AP was well-received by patients and providers, we acknowledge potential selection bias and the need for refinements to make the tool more accessible, readable and actionable. Indeed, these refinements are ongoing and updated versions of the AP and provider guide are publicly available for adaptation and use.

Future directions include incorporating feedback from CREs, tailoring of the plan for distinct patient populations and associated needs, and incorporating formal sustainability assessments for each iteration of the AP. Further adaptation of the plan to support time constraints of healthcare providers is ongoing, including the creation of documentation that can potentially support community organisations in completing side 1 of the AP with clients. We continue to freely share this resource in development on the Centre for Lung Health page (https://centreforlunghealth.ca/wildfire-smoke-and-extreme-heat-action-plan/) for adaptation and use, with dated versions noted. We have been fortunate to share the resource with public health officers who have provided additional feedback regarding messaging and will continue to post updated versions as they become available to support patients and providers in preparing for WSEH.
