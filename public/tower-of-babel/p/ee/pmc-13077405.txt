Prehabilitation moves from theory to practice: time to act?
Authors: Dagmar Goodijk, Diethard Monbaliu, Mara A. McAdams-DeMarco, Robert A. Pol
Published: 2026

Patients with poor ventilatory efficiency undergoing lung resection face a substantially increased risk of postoperative pulmonary complications, yet evidence on strategies to modify this risk has been limited (1). Because lung resection places considerable cardiopulmonary stress on patients with reduced ventilatory efficiency, postoperative pulmonary complications continue to be a major cause of morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs (2). In a multicenter randomized controlled trial of 122 high-risk patients, identified by a minute ventilation/carbon dioxide production (VE/VCO2)-slope ≥33, Brat et al. investigated whether a brief, 14-day multimodal prehabilitation program could improve surgical outcomes (3). Their intervention, combining high-intensity respiratory muscle training with smoking cessation, nutritional optimization, and psychological support, significantly reduced postoperative pulmonary complications and shortened hospital length of stay compared with usual care. Notably, ventilatory efficiency and patient-reported quality of life also improved, providing strong evidence that targeted respiratory conditioning before lung resection can meaningfully reduce postoperative risk.

While these results are promising, careful interpretation is warranted. As in many pragmatic surgical trials, some imbalance in perioperative variables may have influenced outcomes: a higher proportion of patients in the usual care group underwent open thoracotomy compared with the prehabilitation group (45% vs. 33%). Given the well-established association between open surgical approach and increased pulmonary morbidity, this imbalance introduces potential residual confounding (4). Although randomization limits systematic bias, differences in surgical approach may have partially contributed to the observed reduction in postoperative pulmonary complications and length of stay and should be considered when interpreting the benefits attributed to prehabilitation alone. Nevertheless, even with minimally invasive thoracic surgery, which is associated with reduced surgical trauma and faster recovery, patients with limited physiological reserve, particularly those who are older or frail, may still benefit from prehabilitation by further enhancing functional capacity and resilience.

The observed reduction in VE/VCO2 slope highlights the mechanistic rationale of targeted prehabilitation: short-term, high-intensity training can induce measurable improvements in cardiopulmonary performance even within a limited preoperative window. The VE/VCO2 slope, derived from cardiopulmonary exercise testing, reflects ventilatory efficiency. Although it is a robust prognostic marker in thoracic surgery, it is not routinely available in standard preoperative assessment, and future research should explore whether simpler, more widely accessible physiological markers can similarly capture treatment response and risk stratification. Furthermore, the rationale for multimodal prehabilitation extends beyond isolated physiological optimization. Surgical outcomes reflect complex interactions between physical fitness, nutritional status, psychological resilience, and health behaviors such as smoking (5). This study used a multimodal design to reflect clinical complexity and align with current enhanced recovery principles. However, this approach limits causal inference about the individual effects of respiratory muscle training, nutrition, or psychological support. In addition, it remains unclear whether physiological markers such as the VE/VCO2 slope translate into sustained postoperative functional recovery, reduced complication severity, or improved long-term survival following lung resection.

Prehabilitation is increasingly relevant in the context of an aging and increasingly frail surgical population. The geriatric population in the United States alone is expected to double from 46 to 98 million by 2060, with major implications for all surgical specialties (6). Older adults are far from homogeneous, with widely varying vulnerabilities requiring nuanced perioperative strategies. Frailty, a multidimensional decline encompassing physical, cognitive, and psychological domains, is a key predictor of adverse outcomes (7). However, in our previous prospective study, we demonstrated that frail patients may maintain functional and cognitive stability after major surgery, challenging the assumption that frailty inevitably results in poorer outcomes (8). These findings show that vulnerability should lead to tailored optimization rather than exclusion, a principle directly relevant to high-risk thoracic surgery patients.

Prehabilitation has demonstrated feasibility, safety, and benefit in major abdominal and transplant surgery (9-11). An increasing body of evidence also shows that structured prehabilitation improves functional status and quality of life in lung transplant candidates (11-15). Across various prospective and retrospective studies, programs incorporating aerobic training, resistance exercises, stretching, breathing exercises, and, in some cases, respiratory muscle training consistently enhanced exercise capacity, reduced dyspnea, and improved patient-reported outcomes. Whether delivered as inpatient, hybrid, or home-based models, these interventions yielded measurable gains in 6-minute walk distance, muscle strength, inspiratory function, and overall well-being. Although program designs varied, the collective findings support prehabilitation as a valuable tool for preparing high-risk patients for major pulmonary surgery, including lung transplantation (5). A recent overview further reinforced the safety and growing promise of multimodal prehabilitation in elective cardiac surgery, noting improvements in postoperative disability, respiratory health, and longer-term cardiovascular outcomes (16).

The central premise of prehabilitation is to enhance physical and physiological reserves before surgery, particularly in high-risk patients such as those who are elderly, frail, or malnourished (17). Multimodal prehabilitation, combining exercise, nutritional optimization, and psychological support, strengthens functional capacity and improves postoperative outcomes (18). Frail patients, who often have multiple chronic conditions, may benefit most from this targeted approach. Sarcopenia, a key component of frailty, is strongly linked to adverse outcomes (18,19). Because skeletal muscle buffers the body’s response to stress, maintaining adequate muscle mass and function is essential for recovery.

Ideally, multimodal prehabilitation follows a structured pathway that begins with early identification of high-risk patients using validated screening tools and multidisciplinary assessment (Figure 1). This is followed by comprehensive baseline evaluation of functional capacity, pulmonary function and respiratory muscle strength, nutritional status, and cognitive-psychosocial status. Patients then enter a cyclical prehabilitation phase comprising individualized exercise training, targeted inspiratory muscle training, nutritional optimization, lifestyle modification, and cognitive-psychosocial support. Progress is reassessed at regular intervals, with a feedback loop allowing interventions to be intensified or adjusted if predefined targets are not achieved. The proposed 2–4-week timeframe should be viewed as a flexible window, dependent on oncological urgency, underscoring that even brief, short-term prehabilitation interventions can be feasible and clinically relevant. Prehabilitation concludes at hospital admission and transitions into postoperative rehabilitation and enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS)-based care, ensuring continuity across the perioperative trajectory.

Multimodal prehabilitation pathway prior to major thoracic surgery. Overview of the prehabilitation pathway. Following structured risk and frailty screening and MDT discussion, patients undergo baseline functional (6MWT, CPET), pulmonary (spirometry, MEP), nutritional (BMI, MNA-SF), and cognitive-psychosocial (MoCA, EQ-5D-5L) assessments. Eligible patients then participate in a cyclical, multimodal prehabilitation program with individualized exercise training, inspiratory muscle training, nutritional optimization, lifestyle and psychosocial interventions, incorporating regular reassessment and feedback-driven adjustments. Despite its promise, prehabilitation remains difficult to standardize while also being tailored to the individual needs of patients. Although it has the potential to improve surgical outcomes, real-world implementation is challenging and may unintentionally create intervention-based inequalities, as patients’ ability to engage is shaped by socioeconomic status, health literacy, comorbidity burden, and access to resources (17). Without deliberate, inclusive design, prehabilitation risks preferentially benefiting more advantaged patients and reinforcing existing disparities in cancer surgery care. The marked heterogeneity of surgical patients raises questions about whether multimodal programs can serve as a universal solution. Program duration varies widely, delivery formats differ, and both unimodal and multimodal approaches show benefit (18). Although combining exercise with nutritional and psychological support is most likely to address complex patient needs, this comprehensiveness poses practical challenges. Ideally, specific domains requiring intervention would be identified for each patient, complementing standard recommendations such as smoking cessation. However, current risk scores and nomograms offer only moderate predictive value and are insufficient for personalized prehabilitation in lung-surgery candidates (20). Nutritional and frailty screening is recommended for all patients, yet these assessments identify risk without specifying how to tailor interventions. Consequently, many centers rely on standardized programs that are easier to implement, measure, and fund (21). The timing of prehabilitation is another critical consideration. The 14-day intervention period used in this study challenges the traditional assumption that meaningful physiological adaptations require prolonged training (3,22). It underscores that even brief, intensive, and well-targeted interventions can yield clinically significant benefits for carefully selected high-risk patients. This short duration improves feasibility within oncological care pathways, where delays to surgery are undesirable. Respiratory muscle training is relatively low-cost, scalable, and can be delivered in outpatient or home-based settings, making it an attractive component of future thoracic prehabilitation programs. Current evidence remains constrained by small sample sizes, non-randomized designs, and a shortage of adequately powered trials. Yet, these limitations do not detract from the broader message: vulnerability is not fixed, and targeted preoperative interventions can alter postoperative trajectories. The study by Brat et al. provides a strong basis for wider implementation of multimodal prehabilitation in lung surgery and potentially beyond (3). However, real-world adoption remains slow, in part due to limited reimbursement and the substantial resources required to mobilize multidisciplinary teams (2). In this context, robust cost-effectiveness data are critical to justify investment, guide policy decisions, and support sustainable implementation across diverse healthcare systems. As the geriatric surgical population grows, scalable strategies to improve resilience and postoperative outcomes are increasingly urgent. Addressing financial and organizational barriers is essential for equitable and accessible prehabilitation. Ultimately, consensus on the optimal structure and goals of prehabilitation will be essential for integrating it into routine surgical care. The question is no longer whether prehabilitation works—it does—but whether we are ready to embrace and support it. If we aim to translate evidence into practice, the time to act is now.
