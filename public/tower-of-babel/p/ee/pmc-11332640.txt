Decoding epigenetic markers: implications of traits and genes through DNA methylation in resilience and susceptibility to mastitis in dairy cows
Authors: Lotfi Bouzeraa, Helene Martin, Clement Plessis, Pascal Dufour, Jessica C. S. Marques, Sydney Moore, Ronaldo Cerri, Marc-Andre Sirard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCattle farming faces challenges linked to intensive exploitation and climate change, requiring the reinforcement of animal resilience in response to these dynamic environments. Currently, genetic selection is used to enhance resilience by identifying animals resistant to specific diseases; however, certain diseases, such as mastitis, pose difficulties in genetic prediction. This study introduced the utilization of enzymatic methyl sequencing (EM-seq) of the blood genomic DNA from twelve dairy cows to identify DNA methylation biomarkers, with the aim of predicting resilience and susceptibility to mastitis. The analysis uncovered significant differences between cows resilient and susceptible to mastitis, with 196,275 differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs) and 1,227 Differentially Methylated Regions (DMRs). Key genes associated with the immune response and morphological traits, including ENOPH1, MYL10 and KIR2DL5A, were identified by our analysis. Quantitative trait loci (QTL) were also highlighted and the body weight trait was the most targeted by DMCs and DMRs. Based on our results, the risk of developing mastitis can potentially be estimated with as few as fifty methylation biomarkers, paving the way for early animal selection. This research sets the stage for improved animal health management and economic yields within the framework of agricultural sustainability through early selection based on the epigenetic status of animals.

Introduction

In a world grappling with climate change and a growing global population (FAO, 2009), questions about the sustainability of industrial farming and food security are vital. Agriculture, particularly livestock farming, faces challenges from extensive exploitation and climate change impacts. Livestock animals are subjected to extrinsic factors, such as heat stress and diseases, and intrinsic factors, such as physiological stages and aging. All these pressures can impact the health and well-being of animals, thus influencing the level of meat and milk production, and consequently the economy.

Traditionally, dairy farmers have favored a specialization strategy, aiming to maintain or increase milk production by controlling the health and well-being of the animals through environmental management and reduction of disturbances [1]. However, given the pressures mentioned above, another strategy becomes necessary: the optimization of resilience in animals and the use of cows which exhibit a low level of environmental sensitivity.

Resilience in animals is defined as the ability to recover from a challenge and regain good health [2,3]. The overall resilience is determined by the resilience of the subsystems that oversee essential parameters such as blood pressure, temperature, and glucose levels [3]; however, the resilience of these subsystems can be challenged by intrinsic factors like gestation, calving, or milk production, or externally generated factors such as pathogens, stress from grouping, overcrowding, inadequate housing, or farm management [4].

The livestock industry holds significant interest in adopting strategies of genetic selection and genomic management to enhance resilience [5] and this involves identifying cows capable of resisting specific diseases. Variability in terms of severity, duration, and recovery from a disease can thus be used as a measure of the resilience of a dairy cow [6].

Research in animal genetics has increasingly emphasized the importance of the relationship between genetics and adaptation. Innovative selection schemes are now expected to include hereditary resilience biomarkers that can identify animals potentially able to overcome the foreseeable adverse effects of climate change [7]. Two genotypes can be identified: specialists, who evolve in a homogeneous and stable environment, and generalists, who adapt to a heterogeneous environment [8]. Several studies identified genetic biomarkers that can be used as disease predictors [9–11].

While genetics offers valuable insights into heritability and potential disease predictors through genetic biomarkers, it covers only a part of the complex picture of genomic regulation. How these genes are subsequently regulated and expressed is largely influenced by epigenetics [12] which is defined as the study of hereditary molecular modifications carried by DNA but without altering its sequence. These modifications are responsible for regulating genome activity, particularly gene expression, resulting in phenotypic differences [13,14].

Thus, by combining the understanding of genetic biomarkers with epigenetic modifications, it is possible to get a more thorough and nuanced picture of the health and resilience of livestock animals.

Mastitis is a very common disease in dairy cows [15] and it has significant consequences not only for their health but also economically, as it’s very costly. Losses result from a significant decrease in milk production, additional veterinary treatments, and degradation of welfare leading to early culling of the animal. Mastitis is an inflammation of the mammary glands caused by physical injuries and/or pathogenic microorganisms. When pathogens enter the body, they trigger the immune system in the mammary glands, leading to inflammatory changes that result in mastitis [16]. Pathogens responsible for clinical mastitis can have detrimental effects on health but also on the reproductive performance of dairy cows, underscoring the importance of mastitis control programs in dairy herds [17]. Strategies based on genetic selection were adopted to improve resistance to mastitis [18] and to identify various potential biomarkers [19,20]. As technological advancements progressed, numerous studies highlighted the possible roles of epigenetic processes, such as DNA methylation, in mastitis susceptibility [21].

Early identification of cows lacking resilience could allow early intervention with preventive or supportive measures to avoid economic losses. In this context, we sought to determine if blood cell methylome biomarkers during the early adult life of dairy cows could be used as resilience indicators to identify animals more likely to suffer from mastitis in the following years. We tested the hypothesis that methylation profiles at specific DNA loci in blood cells can be used as predictors to distinguish resilient cows from susceptible ones at an early age. We explored whole-genome methylation and evaluated various potential sites involved in the immune system as predictors of resilience.

Material and methods

Experimental design

Ethical approval and consent

Ethical approval to conduct the study was provided by the Animal Protection and Ethics Committee of Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (approval number [A20–0196]). The study adhered to the guidelines set by the Canadian Council on Animal Care.

Animal selection and sample collection

Blood from cows raised under the same conditions at a Vancouver farm, was collected from November 2020 to January 2021. A year later, we selected six cows that were culled during the year due to complications related to mastitis that were not identified at the time of sampling, thus forming the Mastitis Susceptible Group (MSG). We selected a second group of six cows that were still in the herd one year after blood collection and had a similar age distribution as the cows from the MSG. This second group represented the Mastitis Resilient Group (MRG). The age of the two groups ranges between three and eight years (Table S1).

Enzymatic methyl sequencing

One mL of blood was sent to the Génome Québec sequencing platform for DNA extraction, enzymatic methyl-seq (EM-seq) library preparation [22], and whole-genome sequencing. Libraries were pooled at equimolar concentrations and sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq technology, generating approximately 450 million 150-bp paired-end reads per library.

During library preparation, there is a DNA conversion step that involves three primary enzymes: TET2 and T4-BGT detect and transform 5mC and 5hmC into a product that cannot be deaminated by the APOBEC3A enzyme. Subsequently, APOBEC3A deaminates unmodified cytosines by converting them to uracils. As a result, unmethylated cytosines are transformed into uracils and then to thymines during PCR amplification, while methylated cytosines remain as cytosines. To verify the extent of DNA conversion of each sample, two internal controls were added before the conversion step: unmethylated lambda (Table S2) and CpG methylated pUC19. By estimating the methylation levels of lambda and pUC19, we can verify the quality of DNA conversion.

Methyl calling

Reads were filtered, trimmed, and deduplicated using fastp version 0.23.1 with default parameters [23]. Read conversion, alignment to the Bos taurus reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2.104), and methyl calling were performed using Bismark [24]. This process was implemented in the DRAGEN Methylation Pipeline, which was run on an Illumina DRAGEN server using default parameters. In brief, reads from the EM-seq library were converted into both a C-to-T and G-to-A version and then aligned to a correspondingly converted reference genome. Methyl calling was achieved by comparing the originally sequenced read with its corresponding original reference genome.

Clustering analysis

A descriptive analysis of methylation data, including hierarchical clustering based on Ward.D correlation and principal component analysis (PCA) of cellular methylomes, was performed. These analyses were conducted using the R package MethylKit (version 1.20.0 [25].

Markers to extrapolate the proportion of white blood cell populations

The cows selected for this study showed no pathological signs at the time of blood sampling. This assessment was based on a clinical examination, which included checking for visible signs of illness, abnormal vital signs, and udder health. To validate that our blood samples harbored similar proportions of white blood cell populations, we used markers from the Powell paper [26]. These markers were obtained through a deconvolution CIBERSORTx approach from hematopoietic DNA methylation to predict blood count in the Holstein Friesian breed and other breeds. They allow the estimation of the proportions of seven immune cells: B cells, CD4 αβ T cells, CD8 αβ T cells, γδ T cells, NK cells, monocytes and granulocytes from 646 blood biomarker CpGs.

Differential methylation analysis

Differential methylation analysis was conducted for individual cytosines (sites) and for regions using the R package MethylKit (version 1.20.0 [25]. Methylation calls from Bismark were filtered to retain CpG sites covered by at least six reads in a minimum of five samples per group. The regional analysis used a 500-bp sliding window with a 250-bp overlap and a minimum of five CpG per regions.

For each CpG site (and region), a logistic regression model was fitted to calculate p-values. These p-values were then adjusted to q-values using the SLIM method without making corrections for overdispersion. The methylation level of lambda in the CpG context was included as a covariate in the analysis to adjust for conversion rate disparities between samples. The age of the cows at the time of sampling was also used as a covariate to distinguish the influence of age from the effects of mastitis susceptibility.

Methylation differences were calculated as: mean methylation level in MSG minus mean methylation level in MRG.

Our analysis focused on CpG sites and regions with strict thresholds: q-values less than 0.01 and methylation differences greater than the absolute value of 25. These cutoffs were chosen based on commonly accepted thresholds to balance false discovery rates and biological significance [27,28].

Annotation

Differentially methylated sites or regions were linked to adjacent genes or the closest genes using the annotatePeak function in the default mode of the R package ChIPseeker (version 1.32.1 [29]. The annotation considered regions spanning ±3000 kb around the transcription start site (TSS), designating them as promoters. Depending on the location relative to the closest gene, each annotated site or region was categorized as ‘Promoter,’ ‘5’UTR,’ ‘3’ UTR,’ ‘Exonic,’ ‘Intronic’ ‘Downstream’ or ‘Distal intergenic.’ For these analyses, the reference genome used was Bos_taurus.ARS-UCD1.2 from Ensembl (version 108) with supplementary genomic annotations provided by the org.Bt.eg.db package (version 3.8.2).

Differentially methylated sites or regions were also annotated with a total of 167,849 QTLs for Bos taurus that were extracted from the website https://www.animalgenome.org/, version ARS_UCD1.2.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene clusters, based on their functional similarity, were examined with The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID). This tool uses the hypergeometric test algorithm. This analysis was performed with Bos_taurus.ARS-UCD1.2, Release 106. The twenty most significant categories were selected. A p-value threshold of < 0.05 and the presence of at least five genes per class were required for the analysis.

We tested five different genomic ontology resources: GOTERM_Biological Process (BP), GOTERM_Cellular Component (CC), GOTERM_Molecular Function (MF), the KEGG_pathway database (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), and the Reactome Pathway database.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed on DMCs in promoter regions.

Statistical analyses and visualization methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.1.2). To identify the most distinguishing variables, we employed Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and sparse Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA). Additionally, a clustered image map (CIM) was generated. These methods are implemented in the mixOmics package (version 6.18.1) [30]. Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) is a linear classification model that can predict the class of samples [31]. The background.predict function illustrates the separation of class labels. For validation, we used parameters: validation = ‘Mfold,’ folds = 5, nrepeat = 100, dist = ‘max.dist’ (employing the max.dist measure), and measure = ‘BER’ (leveraging the balanced error rate from the dist measure).

The Manhattan plot was drawn with the qqman package (version 0.1.8) [32] and heatmaps were created with the gplots package (version 3.1.3).

Results

Good conversion rates and deep sequencing

The complete set of sequence quality control parameters is presented in Table S2. All samples were deeply sequenced with an average of 651 million reads, covering on average 97.32% of the genome, with an average depth coverage of 34.79X. During library preparation each sample was associated with an unmethylated lambda sequence as a control for the enzymatic conversion rate. The methylation level of lambda in all samples ranged from 0.1 to 0.2% indicating a conversion rate ranging from 99.8 to 99.9%.

White blood cell population proportions were homogenous across categories

Blood contains a mixture of several cell populations in proportions that may vary according to the health status of the individual. To verify that our samples exhibited similar proportions of each cell population, we looked at the methylation level of 646 biomarkers in our samples (see methods). A clustering analysis using these markers revealed no clustering overlapping our resilience categories (Figure S1).

Arrangement of individuals according to their methylation profile

The descriptive analysis was conducted with the methylation information from 49,168,956 CpGs covered by at least six reads. The principal component analysis as well as Ward.D correlation clustering did not cluster samples according to our two groups.

The bifurcation of the Ward.D correlation clustering showed an uneven distribution of individuals between the two branches. The first branch included three individuals from the mastitis resilient group (MSG) and two from the mastitis susceptible group (MRG). Conversely, the second branch consisted of four MRG individuals and three MSG individuals. Although there is no distinct group effect, a slight age effect was observed: the first cluster encompassed cows aged over ~2100 days, except for cow number 8010 which was 1036 days old; while the second cluster included younger cows aged under ~1900 days (Figure 1, A). Regarding the principal component analysis, the first two axes explained 21.3% of the total variability and did not reveal a clear separation between our groups. Differentially methylated positions target several regions

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify sites exhibiting significant methylation level differences between the two resilience categories. This approach identified 196,275 DMCs (q-value ≤ 0.01, methylation difference absolute (25%)), which accounted for 0.4% of the total CpG analysed. More than half (58.23%) of DMCs were located in distal intergenic regions, while 41.77% were in genic regions. Differential analysis of gene functions targeted by DMCs

Only 5.4% of DMCs were located in promoter regions. To understand the biological significance of the genes targeted by DMCs located in promoters, enrichment analyses were conducted for 4035 genes using DAVID and 89.25% of the genes were enriched. However, KEGG did not provide any information for the twenty most significant categories. The most significant categories had p-values ranging from 1.37E–04 to 4.30E–03, with a variable number of genes per category between 7 and 202.

These categories appeared to be associated with biological functions such as cellular processes, and signaling pathways, including trace amine receptors and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) (Figure S2).

Our analysis revealed that 119,211 QTLs overlapped promoters with DMCs. The presented data highlight the number of DMCs annotated in different known quantitative trait loci (QTL) (Figure 5). The QTL associated with ‘Body Weight’ contained the highest number of DMCs, totaling 16,872 DMCs. The QTL ‘Milk Fat Yield’ held the second highest number of DMCs, with 7,948, followed by ‘Milk Protein Yield’ with 6,231 DMCs. Notably, the QTL ‘Clinical Mastitis’ contained 1,728 DMCs. Our sliding windows analysis identified 1,227 Differentially Methylated Regions (DMRs) with q-values < 0.01, and methylation differences > absolute (25%). Among these, 67.81% were located in distal intergenic regions and 32.19% in genic regions, impacting a total of 685 genes (Figure 6). Discussion

This study is the first one, to our knowledge, to investigate the potential predisposition to a disease using the DNA methylation profile of adult cows. During this study, we used whole genome data related to DNA methylation in cows with divergent future phenotypes. The analysis covered 49,168,956 CpG. Among the differentially methylated positions and regions, 196,275 DMCs and 1,227 DMRs showed a methylation difference of at least 25% between the two groups. Focusing on promoters (5.44% of DMCs and 4.24% DMRs), we were able to distinguish the most significantly affected genes and those carrying multiple highly significant DMCs. Using QTL annotation, we found that body weight was the most notably affected.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to find an association between cows’ resilience to mastitis and methylation data. We compiled a list of specific methylation positions that could be used as biomarkers to predict a cow’s ability to resist mastitis.

The ENOPH1 gene emerged as a key epigenetic player in our resilience study in cattle as 19 DMCs were identified, of which two were highly significant. In the discriminant analysis, four DMCs out of 50 were associated with ENOPH1, two in the promoter and two in introns. According to previous findings in humans (database Human Protein Atlas, HPA), this gene is also expressed in the immune system, with an average pTPM of 20.62 across the 17 immune cell types studied. Additionally, the analysis revealed that ENOPH1 was hypomethylated in the MSG. These findings suggest that ENOPH1 may play a role in the immune response, a pivotal element in understanding susceptibility or resilience to mastitis. Methylation levels of this gene varied significantly between MRG and MSG. For MRG, methylation ranged between 28 and 57%, while for MSG, it was significantly reduced, varying between 0 and 8% (Figure 11). The gene ENOPH1 was associated with the udder morphology QTL and was previously identified as a candidate gene associated with Heart Girth in Chinese Holstein heifers. These measurements are often used as predictors of body weight [33], and most of our DMCs and DMRs overlapped QTL associated with body weight. Some studies suggested that cows with large funnel-shaped teats or a drooping udder after calving were at higher risk of subclinical mastitis [34]. Hence, the presence of DMCs in ENOPH1, which could be an indicator of morphology, might also indicate a link with resilience to mastitis, suggesting that udder size and/or body weight could be indirect indicators of resilience. In addition, the promoter of a second gene, MYL10, was targeted by 6 highly significant DMCs and a DMR with five DMCs. This gene is involved in immune cell transmigration and was associated with a QTL related to the width of the rear teat [35]. Furthermore, this discovery reinforces the significance of rear teat width as a potential indicator of susceptibility to mastitis. This adds a dimension to the potential regulatory network governing traits related to resilience to mastitis.

The identification of the KIR2DL5A promoter as being targeted by three highly significant DMCs adds a layer of significance to this gene. In the study conducted by Zhang et al., 2021 [36], the association of KIR2DL5A with longevity traits in Chinese Holsteins brought attention to its potential role in influencing the lifespan of these cattle. In the broader context of our research, this finding could offer a valuable perspective, potentially establishing a connection between the identified resilience factors and the overall longevity of the Holstein population. Understanding the genetic markers associated with both resilience and longevity may provide insights into the underlying mechanisms (including mastitis resistance) that contribute to the robustness and longevity of these animals.

In discriminant analysis, we observed that the intronic region of MGAT5 was targeted by twelve DMCs, among which 11 DMCs overlapped annotated SNPs from Ensembl. In the study by Zhong et al. (2023) [37], a link between certain SNPs of MGAT5 and mastitis traits in the milk somatic cells of Xinjiang brown cattle was establish by Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS), suggesting a potential role in mastitis resistance. Moreover, in human studies, MGAT5 was implicated in inhibiting the growth of breast cancer cells, with simultaneous activation of CD4+ T cells and macrophages after Mgat5 inactivation, as reported by Li et al. (2008) [38]. This dual association underscores the versatile nature of MGAT5 across different breeds, species, and physiological contexts.

During gene enrichment analysis, we encountered a methodological challenge. Due to the limited number of genes targeted by DMCs in exons and promoters, it was not efficient to use a traditional adjusted p-value. This constraint may have implications for interpreting results, underscoring the importance of caution when generalizing conclusions drawn from this analysis.

The DMCs targeting the promoters of the Trace Amine Activity Receptor (TAAR) gene family were enriched with the most significant p-value. Among them, the TAAR3 promoter was targeted by a DMR with three DMCs. In humans, the expression of five TAAR genes, each with varying abundance, was demonstrated in five isolated types of blood leukocytes: Neutrophils, T cells, B cells, Natural Killer (NK) cells, and monocytes. Specifically, the mRNA of TAAR1 and TAAR2, were most abundantly expressed in Neutrophils and activated T and B leukocytes [39]. According to Christian & Berry (2018) [40], alterations in the functioning of TAAR genes can potentially disrupt the chemotaxis, differentiation, and activation of leukocytes, leading to a loss of homeostasis. In our study the mean methylation levels of the 40 DMCs targeting TAAR promoters in the MSG and MRG were 50.62 and 35.41%, respectively. This observation suggests an association of TAAR genes with immune cell activation in MRG, potentially indicating that resilient cows were better prepared for infection.

The risk of developing mastitis can potentially be estimated by using as few as 50 biomarkers with their methylation percentages (Figure S3), opening the way for early selection of animals based on the methylation status of these sites.

We acknowledge that the sample size might be a potential limitation of this study. A larger sample would have rendered a more robust statistical power to our analyses. To reinforce the validity of our findings, it is imperative to conduct biological validation on a new cohort of cows. This step would not only test our markers, but also assess their applicability to a broader population.

When dairy cows are selected, typically at an early age, the primary criteria are based on the genetic value of their parents and early postnatal health. This practice often leads to the rearing of a larger population of animals than necessary to ensure an adequate replacement rate in the herds. However, if the selection process were to incorporate information related to disease resistance, which is poorly predicted by genetics due to its low heritability, it could lead to a reduction in the number of animals raised and, indirectly, an increase in their longevity. The present study represents an initial step towards establishing an improved selection scheme based on early-life blood DNA modification patterns. Indeed, if the markers identified in this study are already present and stable in the early stages of life, analyzing their profiles could significantly enhance the selection process. While our findings are promising, further validation studies are required before these markers can be confidently used for early selection.
