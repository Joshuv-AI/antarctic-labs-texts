Authors: Shaakir Hasan, Xinglei Shen, Huan Giap, Sagar Patel, Christian Hyde, Curtiland Deville, James Gray, Charles B Simone, Nancy P Mendenhall, Seungtaek Choi, Curtis M Bryant
Published: 2026

Introduction

Up to half of prostate cancer recurrences following definitive external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) occur exclusively within the prostate and are thus potentially amenable to curative salvage treatment.1, 2 A meta-analysis of 6 randomized prostate cancer radiotherapy trials suggested that untreated local recurrences may lead to quicker distant metastases and, therefore, worse survival.3 Despite these prognostic implications, palliative measures such as androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) are most commonly employed to treat locally recurrent disease, whereas local salvage therapies are utilized in just 2% of cases.4 This may be because surgical procedures such as salvage prostatectomy, cryotherapy, and high intensity focused ultrasound carry a substantial complication risk after radiation5, 6 Likewise, re-treatment with conventionally fractionated photon EBRT is limited by high cumulative doses to surrounding organs at risk (OARs) and can result in more than 20% genitourinary (GU) and 40% gastrointestinal (GI) grade 3+ toxicity, respectively.7 Alternatively, highly conformal radiotherapy techniques, such as brachytherapy and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), have more acceptable results in the reirradiation setting.

Even though phase III clinical evidence is lacking, multiple phase I-II trials have shown that carefully planned reirradiation can offer a chance of cure in select patients. In a systematic review from the Re-Irradiation Working Group of the Italian Association of Radiotherapy and Clinical Oncology, which included 38 studies with non-proton radiation techniques with a total of 1194 patients and a median follow-up of 30 months, brachytherapy was the most used technique with 27 studies, followed by SBRT with 9 studies and IMRT with 2 studies. The pooled 4-year overall mortality rate and biochemical failure rate were 12.5% (95%CI:8.1%-19.5%; P < .001) and 35.6% (95% CI: 28.7%-44.3%, P < .001), respectively. The pooled grade 3+ acute toxicity was 1.4% (95%CI: 0.7%-3%, P < .001). The pooled grade 3+ late toxicity was 8.7% (95%CI: 5.8%-13%, P < .001). The authors concluded that prostate reirradiation showed promising survival rates and biochemical control rates with a safe toxicity profile.8

Salvage low-dose-rate (LDR) and high-dose-rate (HDR) brachytherapy have been shown by several prospective and retrospective series to be associated with favorable disease-related outcomes and side effect profiles.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 NRG/RTOG 0526 is the largest prospective, multi-center, phase II trial that evaluated salvage LDR prostate brachytherapy in 100 patients with local recurrences after EBRT. With 4.5 years of median follow-up, late grade 3+ combined GU/GI toxicity was observed in 14% of patients.14 Salvage HDR brachytherapy has several possible advantages over LDR brachytherapy, given the radiobiology of prostate cancer and the ability to manipulate dose distribution more easily with HDR treatment planning techniques. Chen et al showed a 5-year survival of 92% and biochemical control of 51% with salvage HDR brachytherapy. There was only 4% grade 2 GI late toxicity seen.12 Both LDR and HDR brachytherapy, however, are highly user dependent, invasive and, therefore, not widely used.

Technical advances in image guidance and organ motion management have facilitated the development of extraordinarily precise EBRT techniques, such as SBRT. Furthermore, the high dose per fraction delivered in SBRT takes advantage of the lower alpha/beta ratio of prostate cancer cells relative to normal tissue, which should result in better tumor control than conventionally fractionated EBRT.15 In the earliest SBRT reirradiation study, Fuller et al described 29 patients who were treated to 34 Gy in 5 fractions targeting the prostate. The 2-year grade 2+ and 3+ toxicity rates were 18% and 7%, respectively.16 Earlier this year, Ekanger et al reported a 58% biochemical control rate for 38 patients prospectively treated to 35 Gy in 5 fractions with partial gland SBRT and noted 16% grade 3 late GU toxicities.17 Several retrospective studies of salvage prostate SBRT have also been published that employed doses from 25 Gy in 5 fractions to 34 Gy in 5 fractions. The largest series was reported by the GETUG group, where 100 patients were treated with salvage prostate SBRT and 12 months of ADT with a reported 3-year biochemical recurrence-free survival of 55%, late grade 2 GU toxicity of 20%, and only 1 grade 3 event.18 These data appear to reflect similar results published in smaller retrospective reports.19, 20, 21 A summary of prospective prostate reirradiation trials is depicted in Table 1.

Isolated pelvic nodal relapses following radiotherapy appear to be less controversial, where one standard of care is salvage radiation therapy to the pelvis. This is supported by the NCCN Guidelines.25 The prospective phase II GETUG OLIGOPELVIS trial and other retrospective series have reported the safety and effectiveness, with biochemical control up to 60%-75% at 3 years with salvage photon reirradiation to the pelvis.26, 27

Devoid from the literature, however, is the use of salvage proton beam therapy (PBT) for local or regional radio-recurrent prostate cancer. PBT allows for precise dose deposition within the target volume with the advantage of a lower integral dose to surrounding normal tissues compared to photon radiation. In the reirradiation setting, PBT has been shown to provide improved efficacy and reduced toxicity compared to photons for recurrent thoracic and head and neck (H&N) tumors.28, 29, 30 Such dosimetric advantages may also be valuable in the re-treatment of prostate cancer, which is routinely treated to doses approaching or surpassing 80 Gy.31 Furthermore, some studies suggest that proton therapy is associated with greater radiobiologic damage to malignant cells, which may be important in disease that has already proven to be resistant to treatment with photons.32 Herein, members of the Particle Therapy Co-Operative Group (PTCOG) GU subcommittee critically evaluate the potential advantages and challenges of proton therapy for radio-recurrent prostate cancer and propose appropriate candidates for proton reirradiation.

Potential clinical advantages of proton reirradiation

Current and mounting literature has demonstrated the potential of PBT in the reirradiation setting to deliver additional dose to challenging disease sites across the body, ranging from H&N, breast, thoracic, GI, sarcoma, and other complex scenarios, particularly where photon therapy was deemed unsafe to deliver.33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Currently reirradiation cases comprise a notable proportion of cases treated at proton centers, including up to 40% in some institutions.38, 39 In accordance with such evidence, the 2023 ASTRO model policy for PBT lists reirradiation as a common consideration or indication for proton therapy.40

Within the pelvis specifically, several studies have demonstrated a benefit to the utilization of proton therapy, mostly in non-prostate sites, such as the rectum, gynecologic malignancies, and soft tissue sarcoma.37 Specifically for rectal reirradiation, reported advantages to proton therapy over photon therapy for commonly discovered presacral or pelvic sidewall relapses include the lack of exit dose, which permits sparing of the bladder and bowel without having to sacrifice the hip joints and pelvic bone marrow.37 The largest single institution experience of 28 patients with rectal cancer treated with PBS proton therapy having a 29 months median follow-up found low acute toxicity rates and acceptable late toxicity, supporting pencil beam scanning PBT as an option for this high-risk patient population.41 Similarly, a single institution experience of 15 patients treated with hyperfractionated, accelerated proton reirradiation to the pelvis for recurrent rectal or anal cancers with a 14-month median follow-up found that proton reirradiation affords improved sparing of pelvic bone marrow compared with photon-based reirradiation and was well-tolerated.42

Among gynecologic malignancies, PBT has been utilized in an effort to minimize the risk of bladder damage, fistulas, lymphedema, and bone marrow toxicity in the re-irradiation setting.37 For soft tissue sarcoma, a prospective study of proton reirradiation, including sarcomas of the pelvis, similarly found that proton reirradiation was well tolerated and that, while longer follow-up was needed, early survival outcomes in this high-risk population were encouraging.43

To date, no studies have reported outcomes on the use of proton therapy for reirradiation of intraprostatic cancer failures, although other radiation modalities such as SBRT and LDR and HDR brachytherapy have been safely utilized and reported as mentioned above. In addition to the heightened conformality of these techniques, SBRT and HDR brachytherapy exploit the radiobiologic effects of delivering a higher dose per fraction, which may confer improved tumor control in the face of radioresistant disease. Proton therapy has the potential to not only deliver high doses per fraction, but it also generates more DNA double-strand breaks in vitro relative to photon therapy, which may further strengthen the fight against radioresistant cancer cells.44, 45 Furthermore, the aforementioned clinical and dosimetric benefits in other pelvic sites would be similarly anticipated in the setting of local and regional recurrences following prior prostate radiation therapy, which are increasingly detected in the era of prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) imaging and multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI).46, 47

Proton techniques and sample cases

Workup

In addition to monitoring PSA for biochemical failure, modern imaging is essential to identify the location and extent of relapsed disease, where the sensitivity of detecting intraprostatic recurrences is over 90% for both multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging and PSMA and sensitivity for distant recurrence is over 90% with PSMA.46, 47, 48 Despite the impressive sensitivity, a biopsy is also strongly encouraged, as the most common site of false positive for PSMA is the previously irradiated prostate, and approximately 70% of re-biopsies are positive outside the visible MRI lesion, making it unreliable to delineate the entire extent of recurrence.49 Nevertheless, modern imaging quite accurately rules out distant metastases, so one can feel confident about offering anatomically targeted salvage treatment for local or regional relapse.

Protons versus photons

Photon-based prostate treatments use multiple beams (5-9 static beams for IMRT, or complete rotational arcs for VMAT) of non-uniform intensity around the pelvis to deliver radiation to the prostate. Photons unavoidably deposit radiation in normal tissues beyond the tumor, namely the bladder and rectum. In contrast, PBT directs a beam of protons at the prostate, where they deposit the bulk of their energy in the last few millimeters of their range; tissue beyond the tumor receives very little radiation dose because of the absence of an exit dose (Figure 1). Both techniques aim to maximize the dose of radiation to the prostate gland while sparing surrounding normal tissues like the rectum and bladder. Given these advantages, PBT can facilitate dose escalation to radiographically involved pelvic lymph nodes adjacent to the bladder or bowel, or dose escalation to a prostatic dominant intraprostatic lesion (DIL), beyond that achievable with photons, while still optimally sparing immediately adjacent surrounding normal tissue.50, 51

Prostatic whole gland reirradiation with dominant intraprostatic lesion boosting with PBT

Studies have shown that the DIL is the most common site of recurrence after radiation therapy.52, 53 The physical characteristics of proton therapy, compared with photon therapy, are particularly advantageous in the prostate-directed reirradiation setting, where the goal of treatment is to maximally target the intraprostatic recurrence while avoiding the surrounding normal tissue, such as the bladder, rectum, and urethra. A comparative planning study of protons versus intensity-modulated photons for a representative patient with locally recurrent prostate cancer undergoing reirradiation with DIL boost is shown in Figure 2. In this comparative plan analysis, 30 Gy in 5 fractions was prescribed to the whole gland, and 40 Gy was prescribed to the DIL via a simultaneous integrated boost technique. The DIL was located at the junction of the prostate base and proximal seminal vesicles. In this case, proton therapy was able to optimally treat the prostate gland and DIL to prescription dose while maximally sparing the bladder neck and anterior rectal wall.

These comparative plans demonstrate the superior dose distributions with proton over photon therapy. The data supporting protons over photons (ie, intensity-modulated RT) for prostate cancer are nonetheless conflicting.54, 55 However, these studies predominantly included men treated with first-generation passive scatter proton therapy. The dosimetric advantages of modern pencil beam scanning proton therapy, as well as the widespread uptake of online cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) image guidance, may maximally reduce urinary/bowel dose and subsequent toxicity even further.56 Furthermore, with the advent of pencil beam scanning proton therapy and CBCT image guidance, studies have shown the feasibility of implementing dominant intraprostatic lesion (DIL) boosts, as shown in Figure 2, and perhaps the ability to further dose-escalate beyond that achievable with photons, thereby improving tumor control probability.57, 58

Dosimetric uncertainties with protons compared with photons and mitigation strategies

While proton therapy provides superior dose distributions and optimal avoidance of surrounding normal tissue compared with photons, protons are more sensitive to setup uncertainties and inter- and intra-fractional anatomy changes, such as rectum and bladder filling, femur rotation, and lateral tissue thickness variation.59, 60 In the reirradiation setting, especially when implementing pelvic lymph node or intraprostatic boosting with a simultaneous integrated boost technique, any uncertainties in patient setup or anatomy changes may pose a significantly higher risk of overdose and/or toxicity to critical organs in close proximity.61 This effect may be more pronounced in treatment plans utilizing multiple beam arrangements, such as anterior and/or posterior oblique beams. These techniques are often optimal in the prostate-directed reirradiation setting, as the relative biological effectiveness uncertainties can be spread out, and plan conformality can be increased.61 Techniques to minimize interfractional variations and setup uncertainties, such as fiducial insertion, pre-treatment enema administration, visible rectal spacer or balloon (provided adequate rectal sparing), or strict bladder filling protocols, should be employed. Future work towards identifying a multilayer perceptron neural network, which may model target coverage and avoidance structure dose changes using critical parameters derived from CBCT images and initial treatment plans, may improve the ability to predict proton reirradiation safety, help with patient selection, and/or support the use of online adaptation protocols in patients at high risk of significant uncertainty and/or setup changes.62, 63, 64

Dose and constraints

Given the paucity of proton reirradiation data for prostate cancer, the committee does not endorse a particular dose and fractionation scheme, as this should be determined based on patient and clinical characteristics, technology available, and physician comfort/familiarity. One cannot ignore, however, that the prospective photon SBRT data include a range of 30-36 Gy in 5-6 fractions to either partial or whole gland. Notably, none of the trials required the DICOM dosimetry data from the initial course of radiation, presumably due to limited availability, and, therefore, all constraints mentioned were only for the retreatment course.

Low GI toxicity rates were reported overall, and the maximum dose to the rectum was limited to 25-35 Gy in 5 fractions.17, 65 Most studies did not employ a maximum dose constraint to the urethra and/or bladder; however, it should be noted that allowable dose inhomogeneity ranged from 7% to 20%. Urinary toxicity was reported at a higher rate for studies with greater inhomogeneity (higher “hot spots”) and those with whole gland irradiation.17, 22, 23, 65 The currently enrolling proton reirradiation trial, NCT05313191, treats the entire prostate (and seminal vesicles, if disease is suspected to extend into them) to 30 Gy in 5 Fx and DIL to 35 Gy in 5 Fx, with an overall Dmax <105%, and rectal, bladder, and urethral Dmax all <35 Gy.66

Rectal spacers

Treating prostate cancer with any modality of radiation therapy involves risk to the rectum, since it is immediately posterior to the prostate and may be damaged by the typical dose levels required to sterilize prostate cancer, particularly in the setting of prior irradiation. PBT can potentially deliver the radiation dose exclusively through lateral beams, excluding the uninvolved rectum from most of the dose delivery volume. This geometric advantage can be further enhanced with rectal spacer implantation.

SpaceOAR, a biodegradable polyethylene glycol hydrogel that is inserted through the perineum between the prostate and Denonvillers’ fascia following hydrodissection, was tested and FDA-approved in 2015 after demonstrating safety and improvement in both dosimetric criteria and toxicity outcomes through a randomized trial.67 Positive toxicity benefits included a reduction in 3-year bowel changes from 12.5% to 2.5%. Several subsequent studies have been published validating the safety and efficacy of this product with protons, where the dosimetric and toxicity improvement appear to be even more pronounced.68, 69 The advantages of SpaceOAR are the extensive clinical usage and recent introduction of new compounds that can be seen on CT scan (SpaceOAR Vue Hydrogel). The disadvantage is lack of adjustment once the compound is implanted since it requires mixing of 2 products and polymerization, such that if the final spacer gel is not in the ideal space or distribution, no further adjustment can be performed.

Barrigel, a non-animal stabilized hyaluronic acid, received FDA clearance in 2022, and CE mark in Europe 10 years earlier. Similar to SpaceOAR, grade 2+ GI toxicity was reduced from 13% to 3% with Barrigel in a randomized trial.70 The implant of Barrigel does not require hydro-dissection or mixing of compounds; therefore, one can potentially achieve a more uniform distribution. There are additional rectal spacer products on the market, such as collagen-based gels and implantable biodegradable balloon spacers; however, the data for these are more sparse.71, 72

In the reirradiation setting, the tissue space posterior to Denonvilliers’ fascia and anterior to the rectal serosa, where the gel is placed, may become fibrosed and, therefore, less elastic and distensible, rendering placement of a rectal spacer more difficult. The data for rectal spacers following prior radiotherapy are limited. One study using hyaluronic acid spacers in 11 patients reported 1 patient with a grade 3 acute rectal toxicity and 1 patient with a grade 2 late urinary toxicity. They did not observe any acute or late toxicity due to hyaluronic acid spacer injection.73 Another series of 11 patients using PEG hydrogel followed by I-125 brachytherapy as salvage therapy for radio-recurrent disease reported that rectal spacing was achieved in 8 of the 11 (73%) patients but was not possible in 3 (1 had prior brachytherapy and 2 had prior EBRT) owing to fibrosis and adhesions. For the 8 patients in whom spacing was accomplished, the median space between the prostate and rectum was 10.9 mm (prior EBRT) versus 7.7 mm (prior brachytherapy), P = .048. One patient developed a prostato-rectal fistula requiring a diverting colostomy. There were no other significant changes throughout the study period in bowel or sexual QoL. The authors conclude that hydrogel spacer placements may be feasible in most patients with prior pelvic radiation.74, 75

Considering all the above, the committee believes an attempt can be made by well-experienced physicians to install a rectal spacer prior to prostate reirradiation with PBT to further optimize dose sparing to the rectum, which very likely received the prescription dose during the first radiation course. It should be noted, however, that since the risks for rectal wall infiltration and infection appear to occur at a higher rate in the setting of prior irradiation, insertion should be approached with caution, and practitioners should not “force the issue” should they be met with too much resistance from tissue fibrosis during the procedure.

Systemic therapy

The role of systemic therapy is beyond the scope of this consensus statement and will not be detailed here. Briefly, it should be noted that most prospective reirradiation protocols recommended 6 months of ADT with reirradiation, and, therefore, the committee believes it warrants strong consideration, especially for pelvic nodal relapses.9, 14, 17, 18 Several disease characteristics may also dictate the risk of distant recurrence and should be considered when evaluating the role of systemic therapy. We also acknowledge that the landscape may change in the future, particularly with the emergence of novel antiandrogens and genomic risk assessments of metastatic disease. Still, the goal of some patients undergoing salvage reirradiation may be to delay the need for system treatment, and this should be considered as well. Overall, the role of systemic therapy should be discussed with the patient and medical oncology colleagues to evaluate the side effect profile weighed against the risk of distant relapse. Based on the currently available evidence, however, most patients in the recurrent setting will most likely benefit from at least 6 months of androgen deprivation.

Genomic testing

Recent advances have increased our understanding of prostate cancer biology via molecular information obtained from fresh-frozen, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue using biomarker tests, which potentially can determine prostate cancer prognosis and guide treatment decisions.76 Decipher is one such test used after biopsy to help guide definitive management or after prostatectomy to help guide adjuvant/salvage radiotherapy. While Decipher currently contains the most prospective data, several other genomic tests exist to assess prognosis beyond conventional risk categorization, such as Onctoype Dx and Prolaris.77, 78, 79

The current data suggest that molecular tests can give additional, independent prognostic information that may support risk stratification and treatment selection for patients with localized prostate cancer. However, not all tests have been prospectively validated to improve long-term outcomes (survival, quality of life, need for treatment), but with increasing time and validation are expected to become part of care pathways.80 Ongoing clinical trials such as NCT03312972 will incorporate genomic biomarkers of re-biopsied tissue with their analysis of salvage reirradiation.81 Currently, the committee believes that it would be reasonable to include genomic risk assessment to determine the patient’s overall prognosis and risk of distant recurrence when considering various salvage treatments; however, it should not be considered a mandatory component of management. Hopefully, future studies will help dictate which pathologic variants, tumor mutations, or genomic scores may be associated with higher rates of local failure and thereby help elucidate susceptibility to salvage radiotherapy.

Patient selection

Reirradiation is a trade-off between toxicity and cancer control. A second (or later) course of treatment will typically result in cumulative doses to normal tissues that exceed accepted initial course constraints. The dosimetric superiority of PBT has the potential to allow reirradiation more safely than with photons. Patients who are good candidates for photon reirradiation are also good candidates for proton beam reirradiation. In addition, there may be scenarios where reirradiation may be more safely delivered with PBT.

Intraprostatic recurrence and partial versus whole gland reirradiation

Full dose reirradiation of the prostate is a standard of care option for patients who have local recurrence following prior fractionated radiation, SBRT, or prostate brachytherapy. Historically, the preponderance of data have been using salvage prostate brachytherapy. However, external beam options, including ultrahypofractionated and fractionated approaches, have also been delivered with similar reported outcomes.7, 82, 83, 84, 85 Published data for PBT in this setting are more limited. Based on comparison of outcomes in the initial treatment of prostate cancer, PBT would be expected to offer results favorable to photon external beam treatments. There is even stronger potential advantage to PBT, as moderate and low dose reirradiation to normal tissues, which is profoundly reduced with PBT, may have greater impact than seen in primary therapy due to overlapping prior dose.

Regardless of radiation modality, perhaps the most controversial question within this space is whether or not the retreatment volume should incorporate the entire prostate or only visible disease on imaging. At the American Brachytherapy Society meeting in 2019, an informal poll of the audience revealed that about half of specialists attending typically implant the whole gland and about half implant only the involved area for salvage of local recurrence.86 The same question was asked for SBRT in the 2024 European Society of Therapeutic Radiation and Oncology (ESTRO), with similar results.87 Interestingly, although most PTCOG members with prostate reirradiation experience stated that they typically treat the entire prostate gland in the reirradiation setting, approximately half of the group agreed that focal reirradiation may be an optimal approach.88 Of note, at the time of the poll at the 2023 PTCOG-NA meeting, PSMA scans had just been approved in the recurrent setting, and nearly all prospective external beam reirradiation data to that point had included the entire gland in the treatment volume.

Surgical data, namely 2 studies of salvage prostatectomies after prostate radiotherapy, reveal that truly isolated recurrences occur in 50%-60% of cases, and the areas at highest risk of recurrence occur at the apex (90%) and within 5 mm of the urethra (69%).89 Of note, these salvage procedures were performed without the benefit of PSMA but nevertheless suggest that a substantial portion of intraprostatic recurrences are not confined to 1 lesion. In the modern imaging era, a repeat-biopsy study following biochemical failure revealed that the tumor was detected beyond the identifiable target in 54% of PSMA-avid lesions and 68% of MRI-detected lesions.49 Despite these patterns of relapse, 1 reirradiation brachytherapy study comparing 15 whole gland procedures to 73 focal procedures reported that whole gland reirradiation was associated with higher rate of grade 2 GI (20% versus 0%) and GU (60% versus 15%) adverse events, without a difference in failure-free survival.89

Data are limited for prostate reirradiation and even more limited for an authoritative answer to the question of focal versus whole gland reirradiation. As is often the case, there are likely some patients who are ideal candidates for focal salvage therapy and others who may benefit from retreatment to the whole gland. In the absence of prospective studies to help select these patients, and in light of previously mentioned surgical and biopsy data, the committee agrees that most patients would likely benefit from a “microscopic disease dose” to the whole gland with an escalated dose to known gross disease. A strong case for partial gland treatment alone can be made if non-targeted biopsy cores yield no evidence of tumor, or if the patient is at high risk for urinary toxicity.

Although the whole gland SBRT trial by Ekanger et al, demonstrated late grade 3 urinary toxicity in 16% of patients, the 5-year metastasis-free survival rate was an impressive 82% and potentially “worth” the toxicity tradeoff.17 There may be an opportunity to further improve these results with salvage proton SBRT. Modest but statistically significant improvements in DIL dose escalation and target coverage homogeneity, with significant improvements in volumetric rectal and bladder dose sparing, have been demonstrated in dosimetric studies.90 It remains unclear whether or not these dosimetric improvements translate to clinical benefits, but attempts to spare should be made given the known sensitivity of OAR to even slight increases in dose.91 Taken together, patients with intraprostatic recurrence should weigh potential local therapy options and should be considered suitable candidates for proton reirradiation.

Pelvic nodal recurrence after prior prostate-only or prostate bed radiation

In patients who experience nodal recurrence after prior prostate or prostate bed irradiation, one standard of care is salvage radiation therapy to the pelvis. Multi-institutional retrospective data suggest that elective nodal irradiation confers superior metastasis-free survival compared to metastasis-only directed therapy using SBRT in the setting of a pelvic relapse.92 However, it is important to note that late toxicity is higher with elective nodal irradiation (16% versus 5%), suggesting a potential opportunity for improvement with PBT.92 The PEACE V (STORM) trial was a randomized phase II study of gross-node-only SBRT of 30 Gy in 3 fractions, versus nodal IMRT in 25 fractions, with 45 Gy to elective nodes and 65 Gy SIB to gross nodes; for patients with up to 5 visible oligo-recurrent nodes. At 4 years, the primary endpoint of metastasis-free survival was met in 63% of SBRT versus 76% of patients with elective nodal IMRT remaining metastasis free. Both arms received 6 months of ADT concurrent with radiation.93 When treating larger volumes of elective nodes, protons may create an opportunity for improved dosimetric sparing of the bladder beyond that achieved with IMRT.

A challenge in reirradiation is the gap between the prior radiation fields and reirradiation fields. The GETUG OLIGOPELVIS trial intentionally left a 1 cm gap between treatment regions. Despite this, the summation of the low-dose contribution from both courses may cause excess risk of long-term toxicity. PBT has an advantage in avoiding excess cumulative dose by reducing the low-dose exposure with reirradiation. To our knowledge, there are no studies reporting clinical or dosimetric differences between protons and photons in this setting.

Nevertheless, there have been several dosimetric studies examining pelvic radiation therapy that demonstrate a significant reduction in excess radiation to small bowel, large bowel, and bladder with proton therapy, and these discrepancies widen in the reirradiation setting for gynecologic and GI tumors.94, 95, 96 Small bowel sparing may be of particular importance given its sensitivity to low and integral doses of radiation, both of which are considerably improved with proton therapy, especially in the setting of prior pelvic irradiation.97 Specifically, clinical correlations between late grade 2+ GI toxicity and small bowel V15 >120-150 cc have been demonstrated, and severity of late GI toxicity was most strongly associated with the volume of small bowel receiving at least 5 Gy.98, 99 Furthermore, pelvic nodes may warrant dose escalation for optimal disease control, and at least 2 studies have used doses of 56 Gy EQD2 or more,100, 101 and as mentioned previously with DIL escalation, nodal dose escalation in the pelvis can be more optimally delivered with protons given the Bragg Peak effect from posterior beams, as has been frequently demonstrated with sacral chordomas.102, 103 One should take caution with dose escalation, however, if gross disease is flush with small bowel given the end-range uncertainty of the Bragg Peak. This can usually be taken into account with robust optimization to prioritize sparing of dose to the small bowel and still allow for superior dosimetry overall relative to photons.

Non-regional nodal recurrence after prior pelvic radiation therapy

Salvage radiotherapy is also recommended for non-regional (para-aortic) relapses, which by definition would occur outside the previous radiation field. However, several investigators recommend, and are currently prospectively evaluating the role of, full pelvic and paraortic (“extended field”) radiation for pelvic/para-aortic recurrences, which requires a treatment volume extending down to the previous treatment field.100 One study found para-aortic reirradiation after prior pelvic radiation to be effective, with >80% progression-free survival at 2 years.104 Similar to pelvic reirradiation, proton beam reirradiation to the para-aortic lymph nodes has an advantage to reduce cumulative excess dose to the bowels, possibly even more pronounced given the larger treatment volume and OAR. Dosimetric studies illustrate improved integral dose sparing for small bowel, large bowel, kidney, liver, and pelvic bones with proton therapy compared to IMRT for patients receiving extended field radiotherapy.95 These patients are also ideal candidates for proton beam reirradiation and included in the ASTRO 2023 proton coverage guideline.40

Local recurrence within the prostate bed following prior prostate bed radiation therapy

Reirradiation to the prostate bed may be associated with higher risks of toxicity. Brachytherapy is challenging in this setting. Nearly all data for repeat radiation of local recurrence in the prostate bed have used a SBRT approach.105 There also may be challenges with precision using proton therapy for small-volume recurrences given Bragg Peak uncertainties. These concerns, however, can be mitigated with fiducial placement and/or CT-based image guidance. In such cases, one would still expect improved dosimetry, although to a lesser degree, with protons, especially in the setting of prior postoperative radiation. Given the lack of evidence (including dosimetric studies) in this scenario, these patients may be less suitable for proton beam reirradiation.

Regional recurrence in the pelvis after prior pelvic nodal radiation therapy

Very limited data exist for reirradiation to extra-prostatic, in-field recurrences, such as pelvic nodal relapses in the setting of prior elective nodal irradiation. SBRT to the grossly visible lymph node recurrence, typically 30-35 Gy in 5 fractions, has been most commonly utilized in this scenario with a wide range in disease control but consistent reports of safe delivery.20, 106, 107 There are no data on reirradiation to the entire nodal basin in the setting of previous elective nodal radiation and, therefore, the committee does not recommend it. As mentioned previously, there are some potentially benefits to proton SBRT in this scenario, such as optimal dose escalation to the gross node (unless flush with the small bowel as previously discussed) and improved intermediate/low dose sparing to adjacent OARs, which may or may not be as important based on the location of recurrence. As such, these patients may be suitable candidates for proton SBRT, although photon-based SBRT may be preferred for lesions in direct contact with adjacent bowel.

Patient selections factors

Reirradiation, regardless of modality, carries higher risk than the initial course of treatment. Patients should be counseled on potential risks as well as alternative options, such as observation, systemic therapy, and alternative local therapy options. Treatment decisions should take into account goals of care as well as potential short- and long-term toxicities of each option. Conclusions

A growing body of evidence indicates that salvage reirradiation for locally or regionally recurrent prostate cancer can result in durable disease control and potentially delay permanent systemic therapy use, distant progression, and even death.82 Detection of salvageable recurrences will increase in the years to come given the modern era of advanced imaging. The role of proton reirradiation is currently not well established but is promising given both dosimetric and potential radiobiologic advantages compared to photons in reirradiation setting, as outlined in this consensus statement. That said, not all patients will be ideal for reirradiation with PBT, and the committee, therefore, encourages enrollment on prospective proton reirradiation trials, if possible, to further identify the most appropriate candidates. NCT05313191 is one such trial that is currently evaluating the safety and efficacy of reirradiation with protons and will hopefully shed light on some of the many unanswered questions in this space.
