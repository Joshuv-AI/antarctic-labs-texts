Painful gastric glitch: an acute epigastric pain triggered by specific food as a new phenotype of postprandial epigastric pain syndrome
Authors: Gabriela Estacia Ambros, Luiz Carlos Kreutz, Iyad Naim, Rubens Rodriguez, Alessandra Loureiro Morassutti, Ricard Farré, Fernando Fornari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimPostprandial epigastric pain syndrome was recently suggested as a disorder of gut-brain interaction. We assessed gastroduodenal motility and histology in patients with acute epigastric pain triggered by specific foods.MethodsIn this case-control, exploratory study, patients with epigastric pain and matched controls underwent upper endoscopy to exclude organic disease and gastroduodenal biopsies for histology. All participants underwent a dynamic gastroduodenal MRI, with a baseline acquisition followed by a food-trigger intake and three MRI acquisitions at 15-minute intervals. ResultsWe studied seven pairs of participants (57% women, 33.3 ± 10.7 years old), all with unrevealing endoscopy. During the MRI, epigastric pain patients had pain crises that scored 6.6 ± 2.4 of a maximum of 10 points, triggered by caipirinha, red wine, banana, and coffee. No control subject had a pain crisis. Epigastric pain patients and controls did not differ in gastric motor parameters (P > 0.05), including accommodation, wall motion, and gastric emptying. Epigastric pain patients showed a negative correlation between pain score and wall motion time to peak (r = -0.81, P = 0.027). The duodenal mast cell count was higher in epigastric pain patients than in controls [median 140 (interquartile 25–75%: 117–153) vs. 86 (76–117); P = 0.020]. ConclusionsIn patients presenting with acute epigastric pain following specific food intake, pain intensity might correlate with gastric wall motion parameters and duodenal mast cell infiltration. After confirmatory studies, such a postprandial epigastric pain syndrome might be recognized as a painful gastric glitch.

Introduction

Dyspepsia is a prevalent gastrointestinal (GI) condition manifested by epigastric pain, postprandial fullness, and early satiety [1]. This condition reduces patient quality of life and generates substantial costs for healthcare systems [2] due to the need for medical consultations, complementary exams, and work absenteeism. Most patients with dyspeptic symptoms have postprandial distress syndrome (PDS) and/or epigastric pain syndrome (EPS), belonging to the disorder of gut-brain interaction (DGBI), while a minority harbor an organic condition such as a peptic ulcer. These presentations can be related to Helicobacter pylori infection [3]. A recent study suggested that the EPS might be expanded to phenotypes such as postprandial epigastric pain and meal-unrelated epigastric pain [4], as provisional criteria in Rome V [5].

A new entity was recently proposed within the spectrum of dyspepsia, resembling food-induced immediate response in the oesophagus [6], named painful gastric glitch (PGG) [7]. It presents acute epigastric pain of medium to severe intensity that occurs in adults who ingest specific foods/drinks, such as alcohol, coffee, and bananas, on an empty stomach and during stressful periods. PGG patients show unrevealing endoscopic evaluation, with the reported case presenting a pain crisis after Helicobacter pylori eradication. Hitherto, data assessing food-related symptoms in patients with DGBI are scarce [8].

The classic pathophysiology of DGBI with dyspeptic symptoms relies on changes in gastric accommodation, gastric emptying, and visceral hypersensitivity [9]. However, such dysfunctions have a weak association with different patterns of dyspeptic symptoms, and mechanisms mediated by the immune system and intestinal dysbiosis have recently been postulated as relevant [10]. Patients with dyspepsia present gastroduodenal hypersensitivity to chemical stimuli such as hydrochloric acid, increased mucosal permeability, and low-grade duodenal inflammation [11–13], characterized by eosinophilic and mast cell infiltrate, which can lead to neuronal hyperexcitation [14, 15].

Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can evaluate gastroduodenal motility, allowing the calculation of parameters such as accommodation, wall motility, gastric volume, and emptying [16, 17], with acceptable interobserver variability [18]. MRI does not use ionizing radiation and assesses real-time gastric motility [19]. The evaluation of duodenal physiology is in development, with some studies in humans [20–22].

The present study used dynamic gastroduodenal MRI to assess gastric motility before and during epigastric pain crisis induced by specific food intake in patients with PGG. We also described the microscopical findings of the gastroduodenal mucosa in patients with this condition and healthy controls.

Methods

STROBE statement recommendations were followed in the design and reporting of this study.

Study design and setting

In a case-control, exploratory study, we investigated the pathophysiology of PGG, focused on motility and mucosal changes of the gastroduodenal segment. We examined the participants at the São Vicente de Paulo hospital for dynamic MRI and the Endopasso clinic for upper digestive endoscopy. Microscopical analysis of the gastroduodenal biopsies occurred at the DASA-Mãe de Deus hospital.

Participants and ethics approval

We invited adult patients with PGG who were seen in the twelve months before allocation. For each patient, we selected a control subject matched by sex, age (± 5 years), and body mass index (± 5 kg/m2), who denied any gastrointestinal condition. Patients with PGG had to report moderate to severe epigastric pain shortly after (up to 15 min) the ingestion of a specific food or beverage on an empty stomach. For patients, exclusion criteria were nausea, vomiting, or headache during the crises, indicative of migraine, and organic conditions at endoscopy, such as moderate to severe reflux oesophagitis, hiatal hernia (≥ 3 cm), and peptic ulcer disease. Patients with PGG were included irrespective of Helicobacter pylori infection. Other conditions that can mimic epigastric pain were excluded after clinical evaluation and abdominal MRI, such as biliary tract disease, chronic pancreatitis, and abdominal wall pain.

No included participant had gastro-oesophageal surgery, use of proton pump inhibitors or prokinetic medications, chronic diseases such as arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, or atopic conditions. Any finding of abdominal disease on MRI that could be associated with epigastric pain was used as an exclusion criterion for both patients and controls. Dyspepsia was evaluated with the Rome IV diagnostic questionnaire for adults (R4DQ) [23].

A local Research Ethics Committee approved the study (number 6.036.242). Before entering the study, each participant signed an informed consent form. The procedures followed the rules of the Helsinki Declaration.

Upper digestive endoscopy

The participants performed upper digestive endoscopy to exclude organic diseases. An expert endoscopist (FF) performed the exams using an Olympus apparatus. After sedation with intravenous midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), the endoscopist described the mucosal pattern in the oesophagus, stomach, and duodenum. We collected biopsies from the descending duodenum (three specimens) and stomach (two specimens from the antrum and two from the corpus).

Dynamic gastroduodenal MRI

Dynamic gastroduodenal MRI was performed with a 1.5T device (Siemens AERA Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany), with the participants in the supine position, using a 16-channel radiofrequency coil, with a gradient performance of 40 mT. /m and a variation rate of 200 T/m/s after an average period of 6 to 10 min. We attached a 16-element body coil around the participants’ abdomen for adequate gastric coverage. With the help of Motilent (London, UK), gastric accommodation, gastric emptying, and motility of the gastric wall were calculated before and after ingestion of food triggers.

Each participant underwent an MRI examination (Fig. 1) to calculate dynamic MRI parameters using 3DCIS Gradient sequences and dynamic CINE sequences (True FISP, temporal resolution 3,4 ms), Matrix 256 × 256). The exams occurred in the morning, at a uniform time (between 8 and 9 am). The first scan lasted six minutes and was performed with the patient fasting, considered the baseline scan (minute zero). After this scan, the participant left the machine and ingested the trigger food, returning for the second scan 10 to 15 min after ingestion, reporting the degree of epigastric pain immediately before and after each scan. Pain scores were rated based on a visual analog scale, in which 0 corresponds to no pain and 10 to maximal pain. Three scans were performed after the trigger, with the patient lying on the machine, with a 10 to 15-minute interval between them, totaling 60 min of dynamic MRI. Each PGG patient defined its food trigger, including the intake of caipirinha (sugarcane brandy, 100 ml) mixed with strawberries, dry red wine (230 to 250 ml), black coffee (Coffea arabica, 150 ml), and a banana prata anã (Musa sapientum, pulp weighing 72 g x 0.8 density = 90 ml). Control subjects performed dynamic MRIs under the same protocol as their glitch counterparts.

MRI images analysis

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were anonymized, imported, and processed using the Entrolytics® platform (Motilent, London, UK). The images were corrected for respiratory motion to obtain detailed information about the gastrointestinal tract wall motion, enabling subsequent automatic image analysis [24]. The data were analyzed using a spatio-temporal motility MRI (STMM) technique [25]. The first author (GEA) analyzed the images, supervised by an Entrolytics® platform expert (IN).

Initially, the boundaries of the stomach wall were manually delineated using a reference image. These boundaries were automatically propagated throughout the dataset, coordinating the wall boundaries in all images. The axis of the stomach was then manually drawn along the visible part of the lumen. Subsequently, the software automatically measured the lumen diameter as a function of time at defined nodal points along the stomach axis, generating plots of stomach diameter versus time. We calculated the area under the curve (AUC) over the 3.2-minute acquisition block, with the result expressed in mm × second.

The gastric wall motion peak, measured by the STMM tool, represents the maximum level of gastric motility observed after a food trigger. It indicates the stomach’s most intense contractile response. Time to peak refers to the duration from food trigger ingestion to the gastric motility peak.

We performed a semi-automated calculation of the gastric content volume, and the total gastric volume based on T2-weighted acquisitions taken during fasting and the three acquisitions taken after the trigger ingestion. The calculations of total gastric volume, gastric content volume, and gastric motility allowed the generation of a report of the identified changes over time. Gastric volume % change is the post-meal maximum volume /pre-meal volume.

Fundus accommodation in cm3 was calculated separately from total gastric accommodation, using data from all the MRI acquisitions (basal, 15, 30, and 45 min). For this purpose, we measured the gastric fundus as the part of the stomach located posteriorly in the splenic vein in coronal images, considering the fundus as the posterior-superior part of the organ [26].

We estimated the proportion of ingested volume emptied into the duodenum using the first two MRI acquisitions (baseline and post-trigger). Knowing the ingested volume of each trigger (x-value), we calculated the baseline gastric volume (y-value) and the post-trigger gastric volume (z). The duodenal volume (dv) in the first post-trigger acquisition was estimated using the formula dv = x + y-z, allowing the calculation of the percentage of gastric volume emptied into the duodenum in the first 10 to 15 min after trigger ingestion.

Gastroduodenal histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Gastric and duodenal biopsies allowed eosinophils counting with hematoxylin-eosin stain, mast cells with CD117 antibody, and Helicobacter pylori infection with Giemsa stain [27, 28]. The samples were fixed in buffered formalin for histopathological Sect. (5 μm). The sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE), and Giemsa, and observed using optical microscopy (Olympus CX23, Japan) using an X40 objective with an X20-mm Ø ocular. For immunohistochemistry, slides containing tissue sections were processed with the Envision Flex Target Retrieval for antigen recovery, using a high-pH solution for 80 min. The slides were then prepared with peroxidase-blocking buffers for 20 min, mixed with the polyclonal rabbit anti-human CD117, and diluted as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. The final stain was performed in Autostainer Link 48 (DAKO, Via Real, USA). We used an Olympus CX23 optical microscope for cell counting.

Variables and study protocol

Concerning PGG data, we reported food triggers quality and volume, and pain crisis characteristics such as latency, pain intensity between 0 and 10 (maximal pain), duration in minutes, and need for analgesics. We assessed gastroduodenal motility using dynamic MRI in both fasting conditions (baseline) and post-trigger intake. The corresponding MRI variables covered total and compartmental gastric volume (fundus/body/antrum), gastric emptying, duodenal motility parameters, and movement-breathing artifacts.

Regarding gastroduodenal microscopic parameters with the analysis of endoscopic biopsies, we counted eosinophils per mm2 using standard histopathology and mast cell quantification using the CD117 immunohistochemistry [28]. Mast cells and eosinophils counts in the lamina propria and the epithelium were expressed in five consecutive high-power fields (HPF) corresponding to 1mm2.

Participants signed an informed consent before entering the study. MRI procedures were carried out at the HSVP facilities, while the endoscopies were in the Endopasso clinic. The first visit occurred for upper digestive endoscopy with biopsies, and the second for MRI, with a minimum interval of 7 days between assessments.

Sample size

The parameter that guided the sample calculation was the change in gastric volume (delta) between the baseline and provocative MRI in a patient with PGG and a control subject [7]. To detect a difference of at least 150 ml between the deltas, with a standard deviation of 140 ml (alpha = 5% and power of 80%), 15 pairs of PGG patients and control subjects were necessary. A preliminary analysis with seven pairs of participants revealed no trend in changes in gastric volume between patients and control subjects, justifying the termination of the study.

Statistical analysis

We first analyzed continuous variables for symmetry using the Shapiro-Wilk test, using mean and standard deviation for symmetric data and median and interquartile range 25%-75% for skewed data. We used the Student t-test and ANOVA with the Tukey test and their non-parametric equivalents to analyze continuous data. Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies and analyzed with Fisher’s exact test. The correlations between motility parameters and glitch pain scores were analyzed with the Pearson test. We used GraphPad Prism version 8 for data analysis, assuming a P-value ≤ 0.05 for statistical significance.

Results

Participants

We studied seven patients with PGG (57% women, 33.3 ± 10.7 years old, BMI 24.4 ± 2.8 kg/m2) and seven control subjects [57% women, 32.1 ± 12.3 years old (P = 0.231 vs. PGG patients), BMI 23.0 ± 2.7 kg/m2 (P = 0.283 vs. PGG patients)]. No participant was using PPIs in the last six months. Three participants used serotonin reuptake inhibitors regularly (two PGG patients and one control). One participant with PGG rated positively for dyspepsia by the R4DQ, characterized as epigastric pain syndrome with weekly symptoms in the last three months, but different from PGG crises. All participants performed an upper digestive endoscopy (twelve prospectively with gastroduodenal biopsies and two in the past year with gastric biopsies) and dynamic gastroduodenal MRI with a provocative test. Endoscopy was unrevealing in most cases (9 out of 14, Table 1), with non-relevant findings such as reflux oesophagitis Los Angeles grade A, and enanthematous gastritis in the remaining five cases.

Dynamic gastroduodenal MRI

All participants successfully performed a dynamic MRI with a baseline acquisition and three post-trigger scans. All PGG patients reported epigastric pain after trigger ingestion, whereas no control subject signaled pain (Fig. 2). In the seven PGG patients, the pain severity and scores (range 0–10: maximal pain) after the trigger were severe with caipirinha (score 10), moderate with banana (scores 5, 6, and 7), moderate to severe with red wine (scores 6 and 9), and mild with coffee (score 3). During the PGG crises, the maximal pain averaged 6.6 (range 3–10), with pain starting at 7.7 min (range 2–15 min) after trigger ingestion and lasting 35.8 min (range 17–57 min). No participant presented nausea or vomiting during the tests. Those who consumed alcoholic beverages had transient drunkenness, especially with caipirinha. One PGG patient required analgesia with oral dipyrone after a test with wine ingestion.

There was no difference between PGG patients and control subjects regarding gastric motor parameters (P > 0.05, Table 2), including total gastric accommodation (% change after the trigger, maximal volume, and post-trigger delta), gastric wall motion (baseline, peak, and time to peak), and gastric emptying residual. In patients with PGG (Fig. 3), there was a negative correlation between pain score and wall motion time to peak (r = -0.81, P = 0.027) and a trend for positive correlation between pain and wall motion peak (r = 0.75, P = 0.053). Regarding total gastric accommodation, there was a trend for a positive correlation between pain score and both compliance change (r = 0.72, P = 0.067) and post-meal delta (r = 0.72, P = 0.066).

Discussion

According to Rome V, the DGBI definition requires the presence of GI symptoms related to any combination of criteria, including motility disturbance, altered mucosal and immune function, and altered central nervous system processing [3]. Although caution is necessary in interpreting the results of this study, PGG might be a new phenotype of EPS [4] characterized by acute epigastric pain following ingestion of a specific food trigger. Further studies are needed to clarify and confirm the gastric motility changes and duodenal mast cell infiltration suggested in the present study.

The first reported case of PGG had a severe epigastric pain crisis following the ingestion of red wine, which responded to buspirone in an N-of-1 double-blinded trial [7]. Although the disease seems undangerous, PGG clarification may be an opportunity to elucidate food-induced immediate mucosal and immune responses, as described in patients with eosinophilic oesophagitis and FIRE complex symptoms [6]. Up to now, PGG has not fit either the Rome IV criteria or any organic conditions causing epigastric pain. Probably, PGG represents a specific subgroup of postprandial EPS, contemplated in Rome V as a provisional criterion [5].

The diagnosis of PGG requires the elimination of GI conditions such as GORD, peptic ulcer, biliary stones, and migraine. PGG is not rare in our clinical practice (data not published), occurring in apparently healthy subjects who report avoiding ingesting triggers after experiencing severe epigastric pain, always on an empty stomach and during emotional stress. The food trigger is not dose-dependent, including a single banana, a glass of red wine, or a few caipirinha sips. All these foods have tannins, catecholamines, and flavonoids, which can interact with the gastroduodenal mucosa sensors [29, 30]. Specialized receptors detect the presence of nutrients in the GI mucosa, with taste and nutrient sensors activating entero-endocrine and neural pathways, resulting in a myriad of functional responses [31]. Such responses may include microinflammation with the recruitment of eosinophils and mast cells, increased mucosal permeability, and visceral sensitization [32]. Aguilera-Lizarraga et al. unveiled and characterized a novel and complex mechanism in which local immune response to food antigens drives meal-induced abdominal pain [33].

In the present study, the patients with supposed PGG successfully reproduced their gastric glitch crises during the provocative MRI, reporting moderate to severe pain in most cases. Among seven PGG patients, one experienced mild epigastric pain after coffee intake. Severe pain occurred with caipirinha and red wine, whereas moderate pain was related to banana ingestion, notably banana prata anã. Interestingly, the confrontation of fasting gastric content, trigger volume, and immediate post-trigger gastric volume indicated the presence of chyme in the duodenum at the beginning of pain crises, suggesting that PGG pathophysiology may have duodenal involvement, as occurs in functional dyspepsia [10, 34]. One PGG patient had epigastric pain syndrome according to Rome IV criteria, suggesting an overlapping condition. The recent categorization of EPS into postprandial EPS and meal-unrelated EPS phenotypes [4], although as provisional criteria in Rome V [5], opens the possibility of including PGG within the postprandial EPS subgroup.

An initial hypothesis to explain the painful crisis would be motor-gastric alterations, including atony with gastric dilation and delayed emptying, as suggested by the reported case of PGG with crisis attenuation using buspirone, a fundus-relaxing drug [7, 35]. However, in a limited sample of 7 patients, we did not identify any changes in gastric motility compared with control subjects. We used dynamic MRI, comparing baseline and post-trigger parameters, including total gastric accommodation, wall motion, and gastric emptying [16, 36]. Nevertheless, a parameter that caught attention due to the numerical difference was the wall motion time to peak, which might be longer in patients with PGG, suggesting that an atonic stomach takes longer to recover its motor pattern during the painful crisis. In agreement, a separate analysis of fundus accommodation indicated that the compliance of the gastric fundus was more prolonged in PGG patients than in control subjects. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify the involvement of these gastric motility parameters in PGG pathophysiology.

PGG patients showed a negative correlation between pain score and wall motion time to peak. Considering a state of gastric hypomotility characteristic of fasting, the food-trigger ingestion provoked a rapid intensification of the gastric wall motor activity that appears to be related to pain severity. This finding is concordant with a positive correlation between pain severity and wall motion peak measured in STMM [36]. Further studies are needed to confirm these observations.

The assessment of low-grade inflammation is a hot topic in DGBI, especially eosinophils and mast cells in the duodenum [37–39]. However, most studies address gastric mucosa responses to Helicobacter pylori infection in functional dyspepsia [40]. In the present study, with a limited sample size, we found an increased number of mast cells in the descending duodenum of PGG patients compared to control subjects, identified with anti-CD117 antibody by immunohistochemistry and counted following international rules [28]. However, inflammatory cell count from gastric biopsies did not differ between PGG patients and control subjects, either for eosinophils or mast cells. The studies addressing eosinophil involvement in DGBI are still controversial, demanding further research [37, 41].

This study has shortcomings. The main one was the limited number of participants, which reduced the study’s power. The trigger food seems to be different for each patient. This may lead to a difference in accommodation and gastric motility between the participants, which adds to the variability of the results in this series. It might be interesting to assess whether gastric motility is different after a triggering food compared to after a non-triggering food. Collecting gastroduodenal biopsies during a painful crisis could identify dynamic changes, such as the recruitment of mast cells and eosinophils as symptom mediators. The strengths of this study were the reproduction of moderate to severe pain in most PGG patients, the comparison with control subjects, the use of dynamic gastroduodenal MRI, and prospective endoscopy with gastroduodenal biopsies.

In conclusion, this preliminary study suggests that PGG might be included as a phenotype of postprandial EPS, provided that further studies confirm the motility disturbance and mucosal immune changes potentially related to PGG.
