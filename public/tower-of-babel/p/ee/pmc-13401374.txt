Academic Psychological Capital and Mental Health: The Mediating Role of Coping Strategies
Authors: Chung Ho Chan, Mengying Wang, Qiaoqiao Liang, Henry C Y Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic pressure represents a primary source of stress for young people. While psychological capital, encompassing hope, efficacy, resilience, and optimism, benefits working populations, its significance in academic contexts remains less understood, particularly regarding student mental health. Drawing from the conservation of resources theory, it was hypothesized that academic PsyCap is prospectively associated with reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms through increased adaptive coping and decreased maladaptive coping strategies.MethodsA two-wave prospective study was conducted on a sample of 507 undergraduate students in Hong Kong, China. Structured questionnaires were administered at the beginning (T1) and the end (T2) of the academic semester. The measures included academic PsyCap, coping strategies for academic stress, and anxiety and depressive symptoms. Structural equation modeling was conducted to test the hypothesized mediation model.ResultsThe results indicated that academic PsyCap at T1 had a negative indirect effect on anxiety and depressive symptoms at T2 through reduced maladaptive coping strategies at T2. Conversely, adaptive coping strategies at T2 did not serve as an underlying mechanism for the effect of academic PsyCap at T1 on anxiety and depressive symptoms at T2.ConclusionThis study highlights the role of academic PsyCap as a valuable set of personal resources associated with reduced maladaptive navigation of academic stress and better mental health outcomes among university students. Working toward the development and evaluation of intervention programs designed to cultivate academic PsyCap may represent a potential avenue for supporting student well-being.Keywords: psychological capital, coping strategies, anxiety, depression, students

Introduction

The mental health of adolescents and youth has become a growing concern worldwide, with recent estimates indicating that one in seven young people experiences mental health disorders, predominantly depression and anxiety.1 Adolescents and youth face challenges as they develop and transition into independence; these include academic pressures, family expectations, peer influences, issues related to identity and self-worth, and assumption of responsibilities.2 Young people may resort to risk-taking behaviors, such as substance abuse, violent behaviors, acts of self-harm, and delinquency as ways of coping, potentially leading to adverse effects on their overall well-being.3 The concerning mental health situation among adolescents and youth in Hong Kong, China, is exacerbated by its high-pressure educational environment, where over 60% of stress stems directly from academic activities.4,5 Within this highly competitive educational landscape, performance is heavily linked to perceived future prospects, generating significant academic pressure and stress among local students.6 Consequently, prevalence data over the past decade reveals a steady and concerning increase in suicide rates among individuals aged 15–24.7 These trends underscore the urgent need to identify the key factors that enable adolescents and youth to be resilient and cope adaptively as they navigate life’s challenges, in order to devise effective strategies to enhance their mental health.

Research on coping behaviors among adolescents and youth can be traced back to the literature on stress and coping. Previous studies have predominately focused on how young people cope with different life stressors, ranging from natural disasters such as earthquakes8 and public health risks such as COVID-19,9 to discrimination and violence,10,11 and physical health conditions.12 The findings from these studies revealed that: 1) Adaptive coping strategies benefit the mental health and well-being of young people, while maladaptive coping strategies are detrimental;8,9,12–16 2) exposure to various stressors can promote maladaptive behaviors, ultimately resulting in ill-health and ill-being.11,17,18 Although the challenges faced by adolescents and youth in dealing with stressful life events and their associated negative consequences have been extensively researched,19 a substantial knowledge gap remains regarding the protective factors that contribute to successful coping and mental health.

Based on the conservation of resources (COR) theory,20 the present study proposes that students who possess greater psychological resources are better equipped to face stressful life events, thereby utilizing more effective coping strategies and exhibiting better mental health. Utilizing a two-wave prospective design, this study aimed to examine the role of academic psychological capital (PsyCap) in the coping process and to elucidate its association with student mental health. By simultaneously examining adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies as parallel underlying mechanisms, this research contributes to theoretical advancements in the stress and coping literature and provides practical implications for educators, school counsellors, and educational psychologists. The findings of this study can be useful for developing effective intervention techniques to strengthen the resilience of the student population.

Psychological Capital as Personal Resources for Coping

Psychological capital (PsyCap) is a higher-order construct that represents four core psychological resources: hope, efficacy, resilience, and optimism.21 Originating from the positive organizational behavior literature, there is substantial evidence on the effects of PsyCap on employees’ capacity to deal with work challenges, promoting their well-being, commitment, and performance in the workplace.22–25 Meanwhile, academic PsyCap refers to the psychological resources relevant to students’ educational endeavors. These resources have recently been shown to be associated with beneficial academic outcomes, including study satisfaction, commitment, engagement, and performance.26–29 To expand the literature beyond academic-specific outcomes and identify protective mechanisms for the concerning mental health situation of adolescents and youth, the present study aims to illustrate how academic PsyCap influences the utilization of coping strategies and, in turn, their impact on mental health outcomes.

The hypothesized associations between the academic PsyCap of adolescents and youth and their coping behaviors can be explained by the COR theory. According to this theory, individuals strive to preserve, acquire, and protect valuable resources across diverse contexts.20 Therefore, the primary source of stress that threatens individual well-being is the loss of resources.20 In times of stress and resource loss, the motivation to preserve resources drives individuals to cope by employing their remaining resources to counteract or prevent further losses.20 However, engaging in coping behaviors may lead to self-defeating outcomes if the resources expended exceed those gained from coping.20,30 Meta-analyses have indicated that a range of coping strategies can contribute to either adaptive or maladaptive adjustment.31,32 Individuals lacking personal resources—whether psychological, physical, or social—are more likely to resort to maladaptive coping strategies because these are often perceived as ways to avoid confronting reality, even if only temporarily, acting as a last resort to preserve limited resources.20 This explanation aligns with empirical findings. A multi-national study revealed that individuals lacking personal resources (ie, having a low resilience level) were more likely to engage in alcohol and substance use, as well as unhealthy and antisocial behaviors as maladaptive strategies.33 A systematic review also showed that individuals lacking social resources and exposed to environmental risk factors were more likely to engage in maladaptive coping. By contrast, those with higher levels of resilience were less likely to adopt these strategies.34

Additionally, the COR theory posits that individuals with resource surpluses are better equipped to gain additional resources because they have greater capacity to meet specific demands, enabling them to exhibit adaptive behaviors.35–37 In line with this perspective, PsyCap functions as a cohesive “resource caravan”, wherein individual personal resources aggregate to facilitate effective coping behaviors.24,36,38,39 Extant research suggests that the four components of PsyCap are crucial when navigating stress. Specifically, hope provides the agentic thinking and determination needed to identify and pursue viable pathways for proactive coping.40 Efficacy fosters confidence and perceived capability required to execute these coping strategies successfully.41 Resilience acts as a crucial buffer during the initial appraisal of a stressor, orienting individuals toward adaptive adjustment and recovery.42 Finally, optimism determines behavioral persistence whereby positive outcome expectations sustain active coping efforts.43 Applied to the academic context as a whole, academic PsyCap would enable students who face the stressful experience of receiving a poor grade to maintain a positive mindset despite failure (resilience), cultivate a positive outlook for improved academic performance in the future (optimism), exert the necessary effort to enhance their performance on subsequent assessments (efficacy), and seek alternative study strategies, such as engaging in peer learning for instrumental support (hope). Emerging research on PsyCap and coping behaviors has found that PsyCap is positively associated with adaptive coping strategies, such as problem-solving, emotional expression, social support, and cognitive restructuring, but negatively associated with maladaptive coping strategies, such as self-criticism, desiderative thinking, and social withdrawal.38 However, the literature exhibits several major research gaps and discrepancies that require further investigation.

First, mixed findings have been reported, with some studies arguing that PsyCap is associated with both adaptive and maladaptive coping,38,44 others indicating that PsyCap is associated only with adaptive coping but not with maladaptive coping,45 and some focused solely on adaptive coping when examining its association with PsyCap.46,47 Second, most relevant studies have examined PsyCap as a general resource,46,48,49 and only a few have investigated the effects of students’ PsyCap in academic contexts.38,50 Thus, although much is known about general and work-specific PsyCap and the well-being of general populations, there is a lack of knowledge about academic PsyCap and its impact on the mental health of students. This is an important area of interest because academic pressure is the primary source of stress during this developmental stage. Third, the directionality of the associations between PsyCap and coping behaviors remains unclear, with marked variations in how their pathways are modeled. Some studies have tested the effects of PsyCap on coping strategies, aligning with the postulations of COR.38,44,48 However, others have examined the reversed associations, using coping strategies to predict PsyCap.51,52 A major limitation of existing literature is the reliance on cross-sectional designs, which limit the ability to determine directionality in the examined models and relationships. The mixed findings, research gaps, and limitations restrict theoretical advancements in understanding the coping process in academic contexts. Therefore, the present study adopted a prospective design to achieve a more robust and comprehensive understanding of the effects of academic PsyCap on students’ use of adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies, and ultimately on their mental health.

Coping Strategies and Mental Health

Coping refers to the deployment of cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage stressors that tax or even exceed individuals’ available resources.53,54 Various taxonomies of coping strategies have been proposed for empirical investigation, such as problem-focused versus emotion-focused54 and engagement versus disengagement strategies.55 However, theoretical and empirical evidence suggest that emotion-focused strategies, such as seeking emotional support, can be adaptive in the context of health-related outcomes.56 Other coping classifications have been criticized for being unable to comprehensively capture the range of strategies.57,58 Therefore, in line with the empirical practices of two recent meta-analytic studies,57,59 the present study adopted a taxonomy of adaptive versus maladaptive coping. This approach aims to more effectively capture and illustrate the positive and negative aspects of coping strategies in academic settings, providing a theory-driven, empirically supported, and parsimonious interpretation of the impact of coping strategies on students’ mental health.57 Adaptive coping strategies involve reallocating resources and taking actions to overcome stressors.57 These strategies include active coping, instrumental support, planning, emotional support, positive reframing, humor, acceptance, and religion.57,59 By contrast, maladaptive coping strategies refer to avoiding or diverting attention away from stressors or excessive fixation without viable solutions.57 These strategies include self-distraction, denial, substance use, behavioral disengagement, venting, and self-blame.57,59

The COR theory posits that psychological stress stems from a lack of resource gain following resource investment.20 This concept aligns with the use of maladaptive strategies, where individuals rely on limited resources to offset further losses, often failing to achieve the objective of overcoming stressors and leading to counterproductive outcomes.20,36 For instance, a student facing academic difficulties might engage in avoidant behaviors, such as skipping classes. This form of avoidance coping tends to exacerbate academic underperformance and elevate psychological distress, a pattern consistent with the onset of a downward spiral of maladaptive adjustment.60,61 In contrast, a student who maintains a positive mindset and actively seeks social support, such as joining a study group to obtain learning tips and a structured plan, is more likely to experience adaptive adjustment, potentially preventing further declines in mental health.60 Empirical evidence has demonstrated that maladaptive coping strategies are positively associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms,34,62–64 whereas adaptive coping strategies are positively associated with psychological well-being.39,65 However, there is limited evidence of the interplay among academic PsyCap, coping strategies, and mental health in adolescents and youth. One exception is a recent cross-sectional study on university students that showed academic PsyCap was indirectly associated with flourishing through adaptive coping strategies.38 Based on these preliminary results and given that psychological well-being is negatively associated with mental illnesses, it can be inferred that academic PsyCap may influence anxiety and depressive symptoms indirectly by impacting the use of adaptive and maladaptive strategies.

Taken together, based on the COR theory, possessing a higher level of academic PsyCap provides students with a greater capacity to deal with academic pressures, meet educational goals, overcome failures, and maintain a positive outlook. Students’ resourcefulness enables them to approach their academic endeavors in an adaptive rather than maladaptive way. This adaptive adjustment in the academic context in turn reduces the likelihood of developing mental health problems, including anxiety and depression. Therefore, academic PsyCap is expected to reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms by promoting adaptive coping strategies and reducing maladaptive coping strategies.

The Present Study

The present study aims to investigate whether and how academic PsyCap, as a set of personal resources, influences the coping process, particularly the adoption of adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies, and ultimately affects mental health. Although this study proposes that academic PsyCap influences students’ levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms through its impact on coping strategies, prior research has suggested direct linkages between PsyCap and mental health outcomes.22,66 Therefore, the present study also anticipates that academic PsyCap is directly associated with students’ anxiety and depressive symptoms. Drawing from the COR theory and the aforementioned literature, it is hypothesized that (H1) academic PsyCap is positively associated with subsequent adaptive coping strategies and negatively associated with maladaptive coping strategies; (H2) adaptive coping strategies are negatively associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms; (H3) maladaptive coping strategies are positively associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms; (H4) academic PsyCap is negatively associated with subsequent anxiety and depressive symptoms; and (H5) the prospective associations between academic PsyCap and anxiety and depressive symptoms are mediated by both adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies.

Methods

Participants and Procedures

The current study adopted a two-wave prospective survey design with a four-month time lag. A total of 507 undergraduate students in Hong Kong, China, were recruited to complete a survey at Time 1 (T1) at the beginning of the semester. At Time 2 (T2), at the end of the semester, these initial participants were invited to complete a survey again. A total of 396 participants responded at T2, yielding a retention rate of 78.1%. Most participants were female (77.9%) and aged between 18 and 24 years (95.5%). A priori power analysis indicated that the sample size was sufficient to detect medium-sized effects (R2 =0.32 to.34) with a statistical power of 90% and a significance level of.05.67,68

A mass email and an advertisement containing a survey link were distributed through the intranet of universities. Interested students were directed to a secure website where they provided informed consent and completed a structured questionnaire consisting of the variables of interest and demographics. Participants who completed the T1 questionnaire were then invited to complete the T2 questionnaire. Academic PsyCap was measured at T1, and adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies, anxiety symptoms, and depressive symptoms were measured at T2. The instruments were translated from English into traditional Chinese by two independent research support staff using a forward and backward translation method. As an incentive, eligible participants received a $100 Hong Kong dollar supermarket gift coupon (approximately $13 US dollars) upon completing the two questionnaires.

Measures

Academic Psychological Capital

Academic PsyCap was measured by adapting the original Psychological Capital Questionnaire21 for the academic context. The scale consists of 24 items assessing hope, efficacy, resilience, and optimism. In line with existing practice, the items were modified for the student sample, replacing contextual terms such as “work” and “colleagues” with “academic” and “classmates”.69 A sample item was: “I can think of many ways to reach my current academic goals.” The scale was rated using a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). A composite score was generated by averaging the items.70 In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was high (α =0.92).

Coping Strategies

Coping strategies were measured using the 28-item brief Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced (COPE) Inventory.71 It assesses the usage of 14 coping strategies representing two categories: adaptive coping strategies and maladaptive coping strategies.72,73 The scale was adapted to the academic context by modifying the instruction of the scale: “To access your responses in the face of academic difficulties and stressful events”. Sample items included: “I’ve been getting emotional support from others” (adaptive coping) and “I’ve been criticizing myself” (maladaptive coping). The scale was rated using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (I have not been doing this at all) to 4 (I have been doing this a lot). In the present study, the internal consistencies of the adaptive and maladaptive subscales were good (α =0.79 and.82, respectively).

Anxiety Symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale.74 It assesses the severity of generalized anxiety disorder according to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. A sample item was: “Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge”. The scale was rated using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (nearly every day). In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was high (α =0.95).

Depressive Symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire.75 It assesses the severity of depression according to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. A sample item was: “Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless”. The scale was rated using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (nearly every day). In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was high (α =0.89).

Data Analysis

SPSS (version 26) and Mplus (version 7.4) were used for data analysis. Harman’s single factor test and single-factor CFA model were used to examine the impact of common method bias on the research results.76,77 Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations among all variables were examined before testing the proposed model. Maximum likelihood estimation for a structural equation model (SEM) was then employed to assess the model fit. Missing data was handled with full information maximum likelihood.78 The model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). A model with CFI ≥.95, TLI ≥.95, SRMR ≤.05, and RMSEA ≤.05 was considered to have good model fit.79 Using the factorial algorithm suggested,80 each of the latent variables was indicated by three parcels of manifest variables to deal with non-normality, sampling error, cross-loadings, and correlated residuals. The indirect effects of T1 academic PsyCap on T2 anxiety and depressive symptoms via T2 adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies were tested using age, sex, and year of study as control variables. Bootstrap analysis with bias-corrected confidence intervals was conducted by drawing 10,000 random samples with replacement from the full sample. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05 for all analyses.

Results

An exploratory factor analysis was conducted using Harman’s single factor test. The results showed that the first factor explained 21.50% of the variance (eigenvalue = 14.62), which was lower than the commonly used critical value of 50%, indicating that the issue of common method bias was minimal.76 A single-factor CFA model was then constructed to validate the results. The goodness-of-fit indices showed that the single-factor model was not supported (RMSEA =0.06, CFI =0.71, TLI =0.70, SRMR =0.10). This outcome confirmed that there was no dominant single-factor structure in the data, further ruling out the problem of common method bias in this study.

Discussion

Using a two-wave prospective design, the present study demonstrated the mediating effects of adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies on the associations between PsyCap and mental health outcomes within a student population. In line with the COR theory,20 which posits that personal resources enable individuals to be resilient against daily stress, our findings reveal that academic PsyCap plays an important role as a protective factor specifically against depression and anxiety in academic settings. Academic PsyCap encompasses four core psychological capacities that help students face academic challenges (hope), maintain confidence in overcoming educational obstacles (efficacy), bounce back from failures (resilience), and hold a positive outlook (optimism).21 Beyond current evidence on the beneficial effects of academic PsyCap on academic outcomes,26–29 this study shows that university students possessing a higher level of academic PsyCap at the beginning of the academic semester exhibit lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms by the end of the semester. Extending beyond recent knowledge about the contributions of academic PsyCap to positive cognitive change,81 our results reveal that coping behaviors, specifically reduced levels of maladaptive coping, serve as an underlying mechanism for explaining the effects of academic PsyCap on mental health.

The first hypothesis, predicting a direct association between academic PsyCap and the utilization of adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies, was supported. Consistent with the COR theory, academic PsyCap was positively associated with subsequent adaptive coping strategies and negatively associated with maladaptive coping strategies. Our findings indicate that a student’s utilization of adaptive or maladaptive coping in response to academic stress may vary according to their baseline level of academic PsyCap. These results align with prior cross-sectional studies conducted in student populations,38,44 but are inconsistent with those that supported only the relevance of adaptive coping, not maladaptive coping.45–47 This outcome suggests that academic PsyCap may function as a personal resource associated with effective coping choices.20 Students who report being hopeful, efficacious, resilient, and optimistic demonstrate a tendency toward better academic adjustment by engaging in adaptive coping strategies, such as seeking instrumental and emotional support from peers and reframing failures as opportunities for learning. By contrast, those reporting lower initial resources appear more vulnerable to experiencing difficulties adapting to academic challenges, showing a higher likelihood of utilizing maladaptive coping strategies such as denial, substance use, and self-blame. These findings encourage future research on academic PsyCap and coping to comprehensively examine both adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies to provide further theoretical advancements and address the inconsistent results across studies.

The second hypothesis regarding the direct negative association between adaptive coping strategies and anxiety and depressive symptoms was not supported, while the third hypothesis concerning maladaptive coping strategies was supported. Specifically, adaptive coping strategies did not show a significant direct association with either anxiety or depressive symptoms. In contrast, maladaptive coping strategies demonstrated a direct positive association with anxiety and depressive symptoms. These results are not contradictory to previous research findings, which primarily focused on enhancing psychological well-being rather than reducing mental illness.38,65 One plausible explanation could be that adaptive coping strategies may be more effective for promoting well-being but less robustly linked to the alleviation of symptoms of mental illnesses. The evidence consistently supports the effectiveness of positive interventions that promote a positive mindset, proactivity, and adaptive adjustment in enhancing well-being; however, their association with reduced negative symptoms remain inconclusive.82,83 On the other hand, maladaptive coping strategies frequently co-occur with feelings of helplessness and may hinder effective coping, potentially tracking with impaired mental health over time.57,84 For example, a meta-analysis suggested that maladaptive coping, rather than adaptive coping, was significantly associated with higher stress among individuals with chronic pain conditions.57

This explanation aligns with the COR theory, which posits that individuals who employ maladaptive strategies may experience self-defeating outcomes, putting them at risk of entering a detrimental cycle.36 Supporting this, previous studies have shown that the association between maladaptive coping strategies and negative outcomes is stronger than the association between adaptive coping strategies and negative outcomes.85,86 Therefore, the findings of the present study suggest that minimizing the use of maladaptive coping to manage stressful events may be a crucial focus for reducing the risk of anxiety and depression among student populations.

The fourth hypothesis on the direct negative association between academic PsyCap and anxiety and depressive symptoms was partially supported. Academic PsyCap was negatively associated with subsequent anxiety symptoms, as expected, but not with subsequent depressive symptoms. These results align with previous meta-analyses, which also showed significant negative associations between PsyCap and stress and anxiety in working populations.87 The non-significant direct pathway to depressive symptoms may be explained by the mediational assumption that introducing a mediating variable can diminish the magnitude of the direct associations between independent and dependent variables.88 Specifically, the mediating effects of coping strategies may account for the relationship between academic PsyCap and depressive symptoms in the tested model, resulting in a non-significant direct effect.88 An examination of the zero-order correlations revealed that academic PsyCap was negatively associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms at a similar magnitude, suggesting that the temporal relationships between academic PsyCap and mental health were fully accounted for by coping strategies, as elaborated below.

The fifth hypothesis regarding the mediating roles of adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies on the prospective associations between academic PsyCap and anxiety and depressive symptoms was partially supported. Our findings revealed that students with a higher level of academic PsyCap were less likely to engage in subsequent maladaptive coping strategies; in turn, lower engagement in these strategies was associated with lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms. Adaptive coping strategies, on the other hand, did not emerge as a significant mediator between academic PsyCap and mental health outcomes. These results suggest that students with elevated initial levels of academic PsyCap may navigate challenges by reducing their reliance on maladaptive behaviors, which tracks with better subsequent mental health. This observation aligns with the COR theory, which posits that the harmful impact of resource loss (ie, lower levels of academic PsyCap) is greater than the benefits of resource gain (ie, higher levels of academic PsyCap).36 Students with limited resources may struggle to cope with stressful challenges, showing a vulnerability to resort to maladaptive coping strategies to temporarily escape reality. While a true “loss spiral” cannot be definitively established within a two-wave prospective design, our data are consistent with the framework that these negative behaviors correspond with further resource loss, characterized by feelings of hopelessness and self-defeat.20,30 Students caught in this stress spiral will have fewer psychological resources to offset resource loss as they continue to face academic stress, and the resulting loss spiral will ultimately contribute to the development of anxiety and depression.36

The present study highlights maladaptive coping strategies as a negative mechanism linking lower PsyCap to mental health problems, complementing prior research on adaptive coping strategies as a positive mechanism of PsyCap towards well-being outcomes.38,45 This provides a holistic model that aligns with the resource gain and resource loss pathways of the COR theory. Contributing to PsyCap theory,21 the supported indirect effect model of academic PsyCap on anxiety and depressive symptoms through maladaptive coping with academic stress enhances our understanding of context-specific PsyCap in educational settings. Therefore, the present study raises the theoretical and practical value of PsyCap beyond traditional work contexts to other important life domains, warranting further investigation in this line of research.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, reliance on voluntary responses is a common challenge in empirical research. This dependence can introduce self-selection bias because students who willingly participated in this two-wave study from the beginning to the end of the semester may possess more psychological resources. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to those with limited psychological resources. Second, this study used self-report measures, which may make the findings susceptible to social desirability bias. However, because the constructs examined in the proposed model are intertwined with individuals’ subjective views and perceptions, assessing participants’ self-reports is arguably the most reliable way for evaluating their personal resources, coping behaviors, and mental health symptoms. Third, while the two-wave prospective design offers stronger empirical insights than cross-sectional data, it cannot definitively establish causality.89 Consequently, the observed direct and indirect associations warrant cautious interpretation. Future research employing fully cross-lagged panel models, baseline-controlled specifications, experimental designs, or randomized controlled trials is necessary to rigorously isolate causal mechanisms and control for unmeasured confounding factors in students’ coping processes.

Conclusion

This study highlights the role of academic PsyCap as a valuable set of personal resources associated with reduced maladaptive navigation of academic stress and better mental health outcomes among university students. Specifically, higher initial levels of academic PsyCap predict a lower likelihood of relying on maladaptive coping strategies, which in turn account for a lower risk of subsequent anxiety and depressive symptoms. These findings underscore the pivotal role of academic PsyCap in relation to reduced engagement in maladaptive coping strategies. While the present study design cannot firmly establish causal relationships, these prospective patterns support the potential utility of institutional programs designed to cultivate students’ academic PsyCap. Future research employing fully cross-lagged or experimental designs is warranted to rigorously evaluate the causal impact of such psychological resource interventions within higher education settings.
