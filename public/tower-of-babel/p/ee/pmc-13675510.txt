Water temperature and river discharge drive age‐dependent changes in brown trout (Salmo trutta) growth
Authors: Patricia Y Koh, Jarod P Lyon, Matt West, John R Morrongiello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRivers and streams exhibit inherent variability in environmental conditions, particularly in water temperature and stream discharge. These factors can impact fish growth through both direct (i.e., physiological) and indirect (i.e., ecosystem productivity) mechanisms. Additionally, the effects of temperature and stream flow on the growth of an individual fish may depend on characteristics of the fish itself. Given this complexity, predicting how individual fish, and by extension fish populations, respond to environmental variation is challenging. We aimed to explore how spatial and temporal environmental variation in water temperature and stream discharge affects the growth of brown trout (Salmo trutta) in south‐eastern Australia and if this was modulated by inter‐ or intraspecific interactions. We used otoliths from fish sampled from self‐sustaining populations to estimate somatic growth. Our findings reveal that S. trutta growth was influenced by both short‐term and longer‐term water temperature and streamflow variation, with these responses being dependent on fish age. Overall, S. trutta grew faster at warmer sites and in years with higher annual mean stream discharge. Younger fish did, however, grow more slowly while older fish grew faster in years that were warmer than the site's average. We found that summer was a critical period for S. trutta growth, with evidence suggesting that water temperature in the warmest part of the year directly affects growth. Furthermore, antecedent stream conditions indirectly affected growth, likely through their impact on stream productivity and thus the provision of trout prey. Our study suggests that Australian S. trutta may have a higher tolerance to warmer conditions than previously thought, potentially facilitated by a combination of local adaptation and behaviour. This resilience appears to be age‐dependent, with older fish showing increased growth in warmer years, possibly due to their ability to monopolise optimal habitats and resources. Our results highlight the need to understand the drivers of growth variation while considering age‐dependent response, which is crucial for assessing the persistence of fish populations under changing environmental conditions.Keywords: environmental variability, fish growth, freshwater, ontogeny, otolith, salmonid

INTRODUCTION

Rivers and streams, like most natural environments, are inherently variable through space and time due to the vagaries of climate, topography and local ecological dynamics such as competition and prey availability (Caissie, 2006; Poff et al., 2006; Ward, 1989). Freshwater fish, restricted as they are to ribbons of aquatic habitat within an inhospitable terrestrial landscape (Fausch et al., 2002), are subjected to this inherent variability, which can have profound effects on the degree of stress an individual experiences, as well as its access to food, habitat and ultimately its fitness (Lennox et al., 2019). Fish growth is particularly sensitive to changes in water temperature and stream discharge (Al‐Chokhachy et al., 2022; Morrongiello et al., 2014; Stoffels et al., 2020). Additionally, intraspecific interactions and prey availability play an important role (Sánchez‐Hernández & Cobo, 2019), with all these factors influencing growth through both direct and indirect mechanisms (Al‐Chokhachy et al., 2022). Understanding how fish growth varies, and thus the size they can reach at a given age, is critical for evaluating the risks and opportunities of environmental change on fish populations and signposting options for adaptive management of fisheries.

Water temperature and stream discharge can directly influence fish growth through their influence on fish physiology and energetics. As ectotherms, a fish's metabolism, and hence its energy budget and cellular processes, are directly influenced by the surrounding water temperature (Audzijonyte et al., 2019; Wootton et al., 2022). The temperature sensitivity of a fish's physiology can then directly impact its growth (Al‐Chokhachy et al., 2022). Cooler temperatures generally decrease metabolism and hence growth rate, whereas warmer temperatures can have the opposite effect until some critical upper thermal limit is reached, after which fitness rapidly declines (Clark et al., 2013). Likewise, changes in stream discharge can also directly impact fish growth (Al‐Chokhachy et al., 2022; Freeman et al., 2022). Reduced flows can cause water to stagnate and stratify, which deteriorates water quality by increasing temperature (Booker & Whitehead, 2022) and decreasing dissolved oxygen (Turner & Erskine, 2005). These shifts in the physicochemical properties of water can induce stress on fish and lead to poor growth or even mortality (Australian Academy of Science, 2019; Harvey et al., 2006). In contrast, high stream flow may increase the energetic demands of movement, forcing fish to divert energy away from growth (Enders et al., 2003; Whiterod et al., 2018). Thus, both water temperature and stream flow act as fundamental environmental drivers that can significantly alter fish growth through their direct effects on physiological processes and energy allocation.

The indirect effects of changing temperature and flow on growth, wrought by shifts in ecosystem productivity or competitive dynamics, can be just as profound as those induced by direct mechanisms. Water temperature can indirectly alter fish growth by influencing the growth, phenology and community composition of primary producers and associated macroinvertebrate assemblages, an important food source for freshwater fish (Bonacina et al., 2023). It can also determine the outcome of both intra‐ (Alanärä et al., 2001) and inter‐specific competition, which affects how an individual can access food and habitat (Taniguchi et al., 1998; Taniguchi & Nakano, 2000). Similarly, drought, flow regulation and even altered catchment runoff due to land use change all impact the amount of water in a system. Reduced flow can decrease the availability of preferred habitat for individuals, causing increased competitive interactions (Elliott, 2006), or conversely prompt individuals to disperse away to more favourable locations (Magoulick & Kobza, 2003). Variations in flow can also affect macroinvertebrate communities (Boulton, 2003; Dewson et al., 2007; Mackie et al., 2013), which can decrease ecosystem productivity by reducing abundances and species richness of potential prey items that ultimately influence the growth and abundance of predators such as fish (Boulton & Lake, 1992; Mackie et al., 2013; Reed et al., 1994).

The effects of temperature and stream flow on the growth of an individual fish may depend on the fish's ontogeny in conjunction with its phenotype (Sánchez‐Hernández & Cobo, 2019). Compared to adult fish, newly hatched and juvenile fish often have a decreased physiological tolerance of warmer temperatures, presumably due to the lack of developed cardiovascular organs (Dahlke et al., 2020; Pörtner & Farrell, 2008). Likewise, reproducing adults may also have decreased physiological tolerances due to the development of reproductive organs (Dahlke et al., 2020). Reproducing adults also have to balance energy allocation among basal metabolism and routine behaviour, somatic growth and reproduction (Elliott & Allonby, 2013; Sibly et al., 2015). Lastly, ontogenetic niche shifts, where juveniles and adults may inhabit environments of different quality that themselves fluctuate through time, can result in spatio‐temporal and among‐individual growth variation in growth (Sánchez‐Hernández et al., 2019).

Periods of peak growth may occur during certain temporal windows when both optimal environmental conditions and high prey availability coalesce (Durant et al., 2007). For example, faster growth in summer may occur if optimal thermal conditions for physiological processes align with elevated prey availability (Chappaz et al., 1996; Elliott, 1973). Likewise, antecedent conditions may prime an environment for fish growth. Here, water temperature and stream flow can cause changes in water chemistry, such as nutrient levels and dissolved oxygen, which may stimulate ecosystem productivity (Biggs et al., 2005). Such changes can potentially drive bottom‐up trophic cascades that become manifest at higher trophic levels (Barrow et al., 2018; Heath et al., 2014; Kagata & Ohgushi, 2006), or even in age‐dependent ways through ontogenetic diet preferences (King, 2005; Sánchez‐Hernández & Cobo, 2012). Conversely, periods of poor growth may occur when sub‐optimal conditions occur. For example, during cooler winters, metabolic rates, feeding activity levels and prey availability are all typically reduced, resulting in slower growth (Shuter et al., 2012). Additionally, during summers where temperature limits are exceeded or stream flow is decreased, growth may be reduced (Korman et al., 2023).

Brown trout Salmo trutta L., a freshwater salmonoid native to Europe and western Asia, has been widely introduced globally, including to Australia in the mid‐late 19th century (Wilson, 1879). While S. trutta growth generally increases with temperature until reaching an upper thermal limit (Elliott, 2000; Elliott et al., 1995), it can vary due to adaptation to local conditions (Jensen et al., 2000; Jonsson et al., 2001; Lobón‐Cerviá & Rincón, 1998). In Australia, S. trutta are non‐native but form self‐sustaining populations that inhabit cooler mountainous regions. However, environmental conditions often differ from those of their source populations in England. In general, southeastern Australian streams are characterised by lower precipitation and higher inter‐annual variability in runoff (Peel et al., 2004) compared to English streams, and water temperatures often exceed the incipient lethal temperature for S. trutta (Koh et al., 2025). Thus, there is also considerable spatial variability in the environmental conditions of Australian streams, with eastern areas having a cooler and wetter climate and mountainous terrain compared to the western areas. Extreme heat days, bushfires and droughts occur throughout southeastern Australia and are known to be lethal to S. trutta (Lyon & O'Connor, 2008; Morrongiello et al., 2006). These climatic disturbances are increasing in both frequency and severity (State of the Climate 2024, CSIRO and Bureau of Meteorology, 2024), and S. trutta are consequently predicted to decline in south‐eastern Australia as conditions move beyond their tolerances (Bond et al., 2011).

This study aimed to explore how spatial and temporal environmental variation in water temperature and stream discharge affects the growth of S. trutta in south‐eastern Australia, as recorded in their otoliths. We also explored whether these effects are modulated by intra‐ and interspecific interactions, as proxied by local trout density and the presence or absence of three groups of fish that co‐occur with S. trutta in southeastern Australia: Galaxias sp., Gadopsis sp., and the short‐finned eel, Anguilla australis. We hypothesised that summer temperature and summer stream discharge would be the most important predictors for S. trutta, as temperatures and stream discharge will likely approach or exceed critical physiological and ecological thresholds. We predicted that older individuals would exhibit higher growth compared to younger, and likely smaller, individuals during these conditions as they can competitively monopolise optimal habitat (Ayllón et al., 2010; Höjesjö, 2017). We expected that the effect of local S. trutta density and competition with other species would be greatest when fish experience high temperatures during summer.

METHODS

Study region and sample collection

This study focused on freshwater rivers and streams in Victoria, Australia (Figure 1a and Figure S1). In April–June of 2021 and 2022, we conducted backpack electrofishing (using a SmithRoot LR‐20B Electrofisher; Smith‐Root, Vancouver, WA, USA) to capture 380 S. trutta from 24 sites across 12 river basins. We collected between one and 39 S. trutta per site, with sample sizes determined by local abundance. We also performed surveys to produce estimates of the fish community at each site. We estimated trout density as well as the presence or absence of three co‐occurring groups of fish: Galaxias spp., Gadopsis spp. and the short‐finned eel, A. australis. Trout density was categorised into tertiles based on the 33rd and 66th percentiles of brown trout caught during electrofishing surveys at each site. This corresponded to low (<2 individuals caught in 15 min of electrofishing), medium (2–7 individuals caught in 15 min of electrofishing) and high (≥8 individuals caught in 15 min of electrofishing) densities of trout. Initial sampling sites were selected to represent a broad range of environmental conditions. However, only sites at which S. trutta were successfully captured were included in subsequent data analysis. Our sites differed in elevation, drainage area and size (Table S1) and their long‐term environmental conditions (Figure 1b), including cool and fast‐flowing streams through to warmer, slow‐flowing streams (Figure 1c,d). We assume that our fish are all wild spawned, given that official stocking at most sites ceased in the 1970s to early 1990s (Victorian Fisheries Authority, 2025b). We targeted S. trutta individuals over 100 mm as these were most likely to be at least 2 years old (Morrongiello & Thresher, 2015) and thus provide us with a growth increment (see below) for analysis.

Ethics statement

All collection and care of specimens and field sampling were carried out with the permission of the Department of Environment and Primary Industries Fisheries Act 1995 Permit (permit no. RP1436) and under Animal Ethics Approval (ethics no. 2021‐20793‐13682‐4).

Otolith preparation and growth increment measurements

Sagittal otoliths were dissected from each fish and cleaned. Otoliths were embedded in epoxy resin within silicon moulds (15 × 5 × 5 mm), and a ~300 μm transverse section through the core of each otolith was taken using a South Bay Technology Model 650 low‐speed diamond saw (South Bay Technology, San Clemente, CA, USA). Sections were then mounted on a glass microscope slide using Crystalbond, and each section was imaged using a Canon EOS 60D digital camera (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) mounted to a compound microscope at 40x magnification. We used ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012) with the macro, ObjectJ (Vischer and Nastase, 2018), to measure the width of otolith growth increments, following the standardised protocol stipulated by Denechaud et al. (2018). The relationship between the width of an otolith and somatic growth has been previously validated in S. trutta within its native (Jonsson, 1976) and introduced range (Aymes et al., 2016; Graynoth, 1996; Haglund & Mitro, 2017), supporting the use of otolith increment width as a proxy for annual somatic growth (Campana, 1990).

To estimate fish age, two readers counted the number of narrow opaque zones, or annuli, on the otolith from the core to the outer edge on the dorsal side of the sulcus (Figure S2). These annuli represent periods of slow growth, and S. trutta have been shown to lay a complete annulus at the end of winter (Aymes et al., 2016; Graynoth, 1996). We measured the width of each increment along the ageing axis, between the outer edge of subsequent narrow opaque zones, to estimate the relative growth within a year. In Victoria, S. trutta typically spawn in autumn‐winter (Victorian Fisheries Authority, 2025a) with fry emerging after 4–14 weeks (Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development, 2025). Thus, we nominated an arbitrary birthdate of 1st October to encompass the spawning period and fry emergence. Each annulus hence represents the period, 1 October to 30 September. We assigned an Age and year of formation to each otolith increment based on the date of capture and estimated birth year. We excluded the first increment from each fish's otolith as this may not represent a comparable period of growth among early and late spawned individuals (Morrongiello & Thresher, 2015).

Statistical analysis

We used hierarchical linear mixed models to relate otolith increment width and, by proxy, somatic growth rate of each S. trutta to a series of intrinsic (within‐individual) and extrinsic (environmental and fish community) predictors. This approach allowed for the exploration of the drivers of fish growth while accounting for the hierarchical nature of measurements, that is, repeated measures of annual growth within the same individual or across individuals within the same site and year (Morrongiello & Thresher, 2015). Our modelling followed a three‐stage process. First, we compared a set of candidate intrinsic effects models to identify a base model that produced annual growth chronologies for each site. Second, we expanded this base intrinsic growth model to include different combinations of extrinsic environmental predictors. Third, we added fish community predictors to the best intrinsic‐environment model. Models were fit using the package lme4 version 1.1‐34 (Bates et al., 2015) in the program R version 4.3.1 and RStudio 2023.09.0. Competing extrinsic models were fit with maximum likelihood (ML) and ranked using Akaike's information criterion corrected for small sample sizes (AICc). The best model was refit with restricted ML to produce unbiased parameter estimates (Zuur et al., 2009), and residuals were checked for normality and heteroscedasticity. Additionally, we produced conditional and marginal R 2 values (Nakagawa & Schielzeth, 2013) for the highest‐ranked environmental model and the highest‐ranked fish community and environmental model.

Intrinsic predictors

We fit a series of models with different combinations of random effects structures to determine which structure would be used for subsequent fixed effects analyses. Each model included the maximal fixed intrinsic effects structure of age at the time the growth increment was formed (Age) and age at capture of each fish (AAC) to account for any age‐dependent growth selectivity (Morrongiello et al., 2012) (Table 1). We included random intercepts for fish identity (FishID), the river from which the fish was collected (site), and year of collection nested with site (year_site). The fishID random intercept allowed for each individual to deviate in its growth from the global average. Likewise, the site random intercept allowed each site to have its own average growth baseline to account for location‐specific differences in growth. The year_site random effect allowed for each site and year combination to have its own average growth to account for spatio‐temporal differences in growth. We also tested whether model fit was improved by the inclusion of a random slope for age at the time the growth increment was formed (Age), fishID and site. Extrinsic predictors

To explore how the environment affected somatic growth, we incorporated different combinations of extrinsic predictors (Table 1) and their interaction with Age. These were added to the best intrinsic fixed and random effect structure determined above. We developed two suites of environmental predictors based on modelled water temperature and stream discharge. Water temperature and discharge data were each separated into four temporal windows: annual (1 October–30 September), summer (December–February), winter (June–August) and the preceding annual (1 October–30 September from the previous year). Daily stream discharge (ML/day) was sourced from the closest water gauges available to each site along the same river from 2008 to 2022 (Bureau of Meteterology, 2022) (Table S2 for gauges used). Small temporal gaps in stream discharge data were interpolated using multiple regression of data from nearby gauges using the River Analysis Package (Stewardson & Marsh, 2003). We used the model of Koh et al. (2025) to estimate daily water temperature from air temperature (Hersbach et al., 2023) and elevation (Hollister et al., 2023).

We decomposed environmental data into its spatial and temporal components to capture spatial dynamics across sites and temporal dynamics across years. This involved estimating the long‐term average (2008–2022) for each temporal period (e.g., annual or summer) at each site to describe spatial dynamics and then scaling the annual observations within each site from this long‐term average to describe the temporal dynamics. All extrinsic predictors were normalised to improve model fit and aid parameter estimation. We fitted both linear and quadratic environmental predictors to explore any potential curvilinear responses of growth to environmental conditions.

We compared models that contained combinations of our extrinsic predictors, allowing for one predictor for stream flow and one predictor for temperature, that is, summer temperature and annual flow, but not summer temperature and winter temperature. All predictors were included either with or without an interaction with age, in separate models, to assess whether the inclusion of environmental effects on age‐specific growth improved model fit. RESULTS

We measured 893 otolith increments from 380 individual S. trutta collected across 24 sites. Fish included in the analysis were between 2 and 14 years of age, with growth occurring in the years 2008–2021. The majority of our fish were relatively young in age, with 67.89% aged <4 years, while just 32.11% were aged ≥4 years. There was a significant positive correlation between body length at capture and total otolith increment width (R 2 = 0.304, df = 378, p < 0.001) (Figure S3), confirming that otolith increment is a proxy for S. trutta somatic growth. We also detected that this relationship was site‐dependent and for sites with at least 5 fish, R 2 ranged from 0.279 to 0.928 (Figure S4).

Intrinsic predictors

The best intrinsic effect model explaining S. trutta growth included both AAC and Age of the fish at the time growth increment was formed, with random Age slopes for each fish (Age|FishID) (Table S4). Annual growth rate declined as fish aged (Figure S5), and fish caught at younger ages had relatively higher average growth rates compared to fish that were caught at older ages (Figure S6). There was considerable spatiotemporal variation in growth (Figure S7).

Extrinsic predictors

Variation in annual growth across all sites was best explained by the combined effects of the average summer temperature (December–February) and the preceding year's average stream discharge (Table 2). All models that included at least one environmental predictor performed better than the model which included intrinsic predictors only (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we show that the growth of brown trout, S. trutta, was influenced by a combination of contemporary and antecedent environmental conditions, which were modulated by a fish's age. Generally, growth was highest in sites with warmer average summer temperatures and across years with higher preceding stream discharge. We also found that younger fish experienced relatively slower growth, and older fish experienced an increase in growth in years that were warmer than average. Our findings identify summer as a critical period for S. trutta growth, driven by both direct temperature effects and indirect influences of antecedent stream conditions on stream productivity. Furthermore, S. trutta growth was higher at sites where they co‐occurred with Gadopsis spp., likely reflective of similar habitat requirements (Jackson, 1978).

The growth of ectothermic fish is impacted by their surrounding temperature (Al‐Chokhachy et al., 2022; Elliott et al., 1995; Lobón‐Cerviá & Rincón, 1998). In our study, S. trutta residing in sites with warmer average summer temperatures exhibited higher growth compared to those in cooler than average sites. Our sites encompassed areas with average summer temperatures ranging from 14.6 to 22.0°C (Table S6), falling within the established 5–23°C temperature range for S. trutta growth (Forseth et al., 2009) and below the incipient lethal temperature of 24.7°C (Elliott, 1981). This suggests that, on average, our sites represent areas where summer temperatures may be favourable to S. trutta growth. The study sites cover a large area encompassing different climatic zones with different spatial and temporal environmental dynamics (Lake, 2003; Morrongiello et al., 2011). The observed higher growth rates at warmer sites may result from a combination of optimal thermal conditions for growth aligning with increased prey availability (Alcaraz et al., 2015; Bonacina et al., 2023). However, it is important to note that daily summer temperatures within warmer sites may occasionally exceed optimal thermal conditions for growth (during the summer of 2018, 13 days at Mackenzie River exceeded the incipient lethal temperature for S. trutta while stream discharge varied from 6.68 to 47.41 ML/day or 29th‐89th percentile). While our water temperature estimate provides a coarse measure for each location, it may not capture the microclimates of various microhabitats available to fish. S. trutta can potentially use thermal refugia, such as deep pools or shaded habitat, to thermoregulate to achieve optimal or preferred thermal conditions (Elliott, 2000). The presence of these thermal refugia could contribute to S. trutta survival and growth even in areas where average temperature approaches physiological limits (Al‐Chokhachy et al., 2022; Rahel & Nibbelink, 1999), although the capacity to use these refugia may vary with age or be influenced by inter‐ and intra‐specific interactions.

Juvenile fish often have a wider thermal tolerance than older adult fish, presumably due to their increased aerobic capacity (Dahlke et al., 2020; Lindmark et al., 2022; Pörtner & Farrell, 2008). However, some studies have shown that there are no or weak relationships between intraspecific size and thermal tolerance (Recsetar et al., 2012) or that increasing size confers increased thermal tolerance (Kraskura et al., 2023). S. trutta have been shown to have different thermal limits across their life stages, with thermal tolerance being highest for yearlings (1+ year olds) and 2+ year old fish compared to eggs and newly‐hatched young (Elliott & Elliott, 2010). While our analyses focussed on fish responses to annualised rather than acute thermal exposure, we believe physiological mechanisms can still be invoked. In particular, we found that in warmer years, younger, smaller fish experienced reduced growth, while larger, older adults experienced increased growth. The growth of older S. trutta is consistent with previous studies that show S. trutta grow optimally between 5 and 23°C (Forseth et al., 2009) and that the growth rate of fish in general increases as the temperature approaches their upper thermal limit (Clark et al., 2013). While these findings align with expectations for older S. trutta growth, the observed reduced growth in juveniles during warmer years suggests that factors beyond direct temperature effects, such as ontogenetic niche shifts, may play a role in determining growth across different life stages.

Many species undergo ontogenetic shifts in habitat due to changes in body size, which influences their diet, exposure to predation pressure and the outcomes of both intra‐ and inter‐specific competition (Sánchez‐Hernández et al., 2019; Werner & Gilliam, 1984; Werner & Hall, 1988). Habitat shifts, in turn, could lead to the elevated fitness of older and larger individuals compared to younger, smaller individuals if they can occupy more favourable locations (Sánchez‐Hernández & Cobo, 2019). We hypothesise that larger adult S. trutta likely occupy better quality habitats composed of deeper pools compared to smaller, younger S. trutta. Previous studies have shown that S. trutta show a clear ontogenetic shift in habitat use with larger individuals occupying deeper, slower‐moving pools and smaller S. trutta using shallower, faster‐flowing riffles (Armstrong et al., 2003; Ayllón et al., 2010). This habitat separation could be due to intraspecific competition or predation pressure displacing younger S. trutta into less favourable shallow habitats (Ayllón et al., 2010) or ontogenetic differences in diet composition (Sánchez‐Hernández & Cobo, 2012, 2016). Large S. trutta may forage on a more diverse suite of prey compared to small S. trutta because of their wider mouth gape (Griffiths, 2013). Larger individuals may also be more likely to cannibalise smaller trout that venture into larger pools (Jensen et al., 2012; Vik et al., 2001). A greater range of potential food sources, including other fish, may thus provide a growth benefit for large S. trutta. Furthermore, S. trutta feeding on a high‐nutrient, fish‐based diet tends to grow faster at higher temperatures compared to those relying on a lower‐nutrient, invertebrate‐based diet (Elliott & Elliott, 2010). Thus, older S. trutta may have a growth benefit by occupying large deep pools, which offer more stable thermal microclimate as well as potentially increased frequency of encounters with prey species. These ideas remain speculation as we did not record the environmental characteristics of fish capture locations, such as water depth and river width, but offer a fruitful opportunity for future studies.

Stream flow can directly impact the growth of fish through changes to water quality and energy expenditure (Freeman et al., 2022), but also indirectly influences growth through its impacts on ecosystem productivity (Stoffels et al., 2020). We found that S. trutta growth increased following years of higher‐than‐average stream discharge, suggesting that flow‐induced indirect effects on the food web, rather than direct physiological responses to flow variability, were the primary drivers. High discharge events can introduce pulses of nutrients and organic matter into river systems (Raymond et al., 2016), potentially stimulating aquatic invertebrate productivity (Palmer & Ruhi, 2019). However, a temporal lag can occur before this translates into increased prey, as these invertebrate species themselves need time to grow and reproduce in response to enhanced resource availability (Boulton & Lake, 1992; Le et al., 2020). Since aquatic invertebrates constitute a major component of S. trutta's diet, this time lag between stream discharge and increased S. trutta growth may reflect this trophic cascade. The availability of terrestrial invertebrates may also contribute to S. trutta growth. Previous findings show an increased reliance on terrestrial invertebrates compared to aquatic invertebrates in older individuals (Sánchez‐Hernández & Cobo, 2016, 2018). Riparian zones are important sources of invertebrate prey, with the abundance and type of invertebrate prey being dependent on riparian vegetation (Baxter et al., 2005; Nakano & Murakami, 2001). However, our study was unable to account for riparian vegetation at our sites, and thus, we were unable to characterise the potential effects of terrestrial invertebrates on S. trutta growth.

We found that the growth of S. trutta increased in the presence of the native two‐spined blackfish (Gadopsis bispinous) or the river blackfish (Gadopsis marmoratus). Larger S. trutta are known to prey upon Gadopsis spp. (Cadwallader, 1996) and so we expected to see an age‐dependent increase in S. trutta growth at sites where the species co‐occur. This relationship, however, was not evident. Both Gadopsis sp. are medium‐sized and consume invertebrates as well as small fish, a diet similar to S. trutta (Jackson, 1978; Lintermans, 1998). Thus, increased S. trutta growth in the presence of Gadopsis spp. might reflect a shared response to favourable conditions (Jackson, 1978; Jackson & Williams, 1980) rather than increased growth due to piscivory. This conclusion requires further exploration, as our predictors of fish community composition are coarse. It is possible that abundances of smaller Gadopsis spp. that are more readily eaten by S. trutta, rather than the presence of the species per se, are linked to S. trutta growth, which would then suggest predation rather than shared habitat requirements as the underlying mechanism.

Salmo trutta growth across Victoria was also negatively related to ACC. Fish caught at younger ages had overall faster growth rates than those caught at older ages. This pattern suggests that fast‐growing fish are more likely to have been recruited into our sampling regime, which only retained fish above a certain size threshold. Older age groups were comprised of both fast and slow growing phenotypes as individuals were more likely to meet our size threshold, an example of the Rosa Lee phenomenon (Kraak et al., 2019). There may be selection pressure against fast growth, and hence slower‐growing fish were more likely to survive to an older age (Morrongiello et al., 2012). The mechanism for this growth‐based selection in these populations remains unknown and is difficult to discern due to the unavailability of catch numbers. However, S. trutta within southeastern Australia are subjected to harvest pressure from recreational fisheries, which could contribute to the removal of faster‐growing younger fish due to size‐selectivity or individual boldness (Flink et al., 2024). Alternatively, slow‐growing fish adopt a ‘slower pace of life’ strategy, reaching older ages, while fast‐growing fish may have shorter lifespans (Réale et al., 2010; Smith & Blumstein, 2008). Consequently, the young fish in our sample size may be comprised of both fast and slow growing fish, whereas the older fish may predominantly consist of slower growers, thus explaining the observed negative relationship between growth rate and AAC.

All animals have an upper critical thermal maximum at which they cannot survive (Clark et al., 2013); however, the realised thermal niche of individuals may be narrower than expected from experimental studies (Santiago et al., 2016). We observed increased S. trutta growth at warming temperatures, which is consistent with some empirically derived field estimates of growth rate (Lobón‐Cerviá & Rincón, 1998). However, we do not observe either a plateau or a decline in growth rate at higher temperatures, as has been previously reported for this species (Elliott et al., 1995). It is likely that we were unable to parameterise the upper critical thermal maxima as individuals were already excluded from living in overly thermally stressful environments. Additionally, our study does not explicitly account for microhabitat use, but we suggest that microclimates such as deep pools and shaded habitat may provide a buffer to individuals from extreme temperatures (Ebersole et al., 2001; Elliott, 2000). The prevalence of these thermal refugia is unknown, but it is likely they exist in even our warmest sites because S. trutta continues to persist in conditions that, at the macro‐scale, appear too hot.

CONCLUSION

Salmo trutta, despite its classification as a cold‐adapted salmonoid, demonstrates surprising tolerance to Australian conditions (Hansen et al., 2025). Our study reveals that S. trutta growth increases in response to warmer sites and years, likely due to a combination of thermal tolerance and behavioural modulation. However, this positive growth response is unlikely to continue indefinitely, as it will ultimately be constrained by the species' critical thermal limit. Additionally, we demonstrate the importance of stream flow conditions in priming of the food web, thereby increasing energy availability for growth. While our findings suggest the potential for S. trutta persistence under warming conditions, the predicted future decreases in rainfall and increases in water temperature across southeastern Australia are likely to impede fish growth and contract the availability of suitable habitat. As aquatic ecosystems continue to be impacted globally, the long‐term viability of S. trutta remains uncertain.
