Reassessing oncologic adequacy and methodological rigor in minimally invasive surgery for remnant gastric cancer
Authors: Noor Un Nisa, Muhammad Mohsin, Maria Baig, Habib Ur Rehman, Eisha tur Razia
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Iwasaki et al. [1], entitled “Minimally invasive surgery for remnant gastric cancer: a single-center retrospective study of treatment outcomes in Japan.” The study is commendable for its timely clinical question, its real-world single-center experience, and its comparative propensity-score-matched analysis of minimally invasive and open approaches. However, several issues merit further discussion. If clarified, these points would strengthen the interpretation of the findings and improve their generalizability and applicability to routine surgical practice.

A key limitation is the lack of clear reporting and comparative analysis of oncologic adequacy, particularly resection margin status and harvested lymph node yield, both of which are essential for judging whether minimally invasive surgery provides oncologic outcomes comparable to open surgery rather than only short-term perioperative advantages [2].

Despite the application of propensity-score matching, the manuscript does not provide crucial post-matching balance diagnostics such as standardized mean differences, variance ratios, or an assessment of common support, making it difficult to confirm baseline comparability. Therefore, residual imbalance remains an alternative explanation for the observed differences [3,4].

A further limitation is the lack of a clearly stated primary endpoint and the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons despite the evaluation of several perioperative outcomes in a small matched cohort. In this exploratory setting, the reported significant findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as they may not be robust to multiplicity [5]. In addition, the absence of long-term oncologic outcomes, such as recurrence and survival, limits assessment of the durability of the reported findings.

In summary, this study adds valuable observational evidence to the growing literature on minimally invasive surgery for remnant gastric cancer. Nevertheless, further clarification on oncologic adequacy, post-matching balance diagnostics, long-term oncologic outcomes, and adjustment for multiple comparisons would strengthen the validity and applicability of the results.
