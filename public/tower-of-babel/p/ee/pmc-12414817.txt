Epithelioid glioblastoma mimicking metastatic brain tumor: A case report and literature review
Authors: Chihiro Watanabe, Tomokazu Sekine, Kazuhiro Nakamura, Ai Muroi, Eiichi Ishikawa, Yoji Komatsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpithelioid glioblastoma (eGBM) is a rare subtype of glioblastoma, generally associated with a poorer prognosis than conventional GBM despite maximum resection and standard chemoradiotherapy. Here, we report a case of a 78-year-old man who presented with left hemiplegia and a well-circumscribed right frontal lobe lesion on imaging, initially suspected to be a metastatic brain tumor. Surgical resection revealed a firm, clearly demarcated mass. Histopathological examination showed features consistent with eGBM, including epithelioid and rhabdoid cells, necrosis, and high proliferative activity (Ki-67 index: 41%). Immunohistochemistry indicated IDH-wildtype GBM with negative BRAF V600E. Despite postoperative radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy, the tumor recurred, and the patient died 1 year and 10 months after surgery. Even if a tumor appears well-circumscribed with minimal edema, a diagnosis of eGBM should be considered to avoid treatment delays if no primary lesion is detected. Following a diagnosis of eGBM, prognosis remains variable; therefore, careful pathological evaluation, including immunohistochemistry and molecular analysis, is essential to guide treatment decisions.Keyword: Epithelioid glioblastoma, Magnetic resonance imaging, Metastatic brain tumor

Introduction

Epithelioid glioblastoma (eGBM) is a primary brain tumor first classified in the 2016 World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, and later listed as a variant of GBM in the World Health Organization 2021 revision. It has been reported to account for approximately 3% of all glioblastoma cases [1]. Unlike conventional GBM, eGBM is more common in young adults and exhibits aggressive behavior, including dissemination and distal metastases, with a poor prognosis and median survival of less than 1 year [2,3]. Due to its rarity and relatively recent classification, its clinical, pathological, and imaging features are not yet fully understood, with most available evidence coming from case reports or small case series. eGBM features distinct pathological characteristics, and approximately 50% of cases harbor BRAF 600E mutations. Diagnosis can be challenging due to overlap with other tumor types. Herein, we describe a case of eGBM mimicking a metastatic brain tumor and discuss the associated diagnostic pitfalls.

Case report

A 78-year-old man with a history of hypertension and diabetes mellitus was brought to the emergency room after becoming immobile in his bathroom. He had no history of malignancy. Neurological examination revealed alert, with mild left hemiplegia involving the face and dysarthria. Blood tests and chest X-rays showed no abnormalities.

A head computed tomography scan revealed a mass lesion with slight hyperdensity in the right frontal lobe and marked edema around the lesion (Fig. 1). Further, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a well-circumscribed lesion containing some cystic components at the corticomedullary border of the right frontal lobe, with hypointensity on T1-weighted images and hyperintensity on T2-weighted images. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images showed ring enhancement, and diffusion-weighted images showed hyperintensity. The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map revealed a low minimum ADC value of 764.6 × 10−3 mm2/s. The differential diagnosis included a metastatic brain tumor or high-grade glioma. To identify metastatic brain tumor, contrast-enhanced computed tomography scans of the thorax and abdomen and serum tumor markers measurements were performed; both were negative. After admission, the left hemiparesis deteriorated, and tumor resection was performed, and total gross removal achieved. The tumor was clearly demarcated, firm, and elastic, with findings suspicious of a metastatic brain tumor. Postoperative MRI showed no obvious residual tumor. Stereotactic radiotherapy with total of 30 Gy in five fractions was administered, after which symptoms improved.

On pathological examination, the tumor consisted of polymorphic glial cells with increased density (Fig. 2). The cytoplasm was acidophilic, with conspicuous rhabdoid or epithelial cells. There was extensive necrosis, and a relatively clear boundary between the tumor and brain tissue. Immunohistochemical staining showed positive glial fibrillary acidic protein, negative p53, negative BRAF V600E, negative ATRX, negative IDH1 R132H, and a Ki-67 labeling index of 41%. Molecular analysis was not available. Based on these findings, glioblastoma not otherwise specified, particularly eGBM, was strongly suspected.

(C) (100×), tumor cells partly expressed GFAP by immunohistochemistry, supporting glial origin. (D) (100×), negative for BRAF V600E.

Accordingly, maintenance chemotherapy with temozolomide was initiated after radiotherapy. Nine months after surgery, the patient developed a severe drug-induced skin rash, and temozolomide was discontinued. One year and 5 months after surgery, local recurrence and dissemination appeared, and the patient died 1 year and 10 months after surgery.

Discussion

Here, we present a case of eGBM mimicking a metastatic brain tumor. Reported median survival for eGBM is less than 1 year, which is significantly shorter than that of conventional GBM [2,3]. Pathologically, eGBM is characterized by sharply demarcated, loosely cohesive aggregates of large epithelioid to rhabdoid cells with abundant cytoplasm, large vesicular nuclei, and prominent macronucleoli, in addition to the typical pathological findings of GBM. Differentiation from pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (PXA), metastatic brain tumors, melanoma, and giant cell glioblastoma is a matter of debate; however, differential diagnosis is often difficult because of the lack of specific immunohistochemical or molecular markers. Another unique feature of eGBM is the presence of BRAF V600E mutation in almost 50% of cases, being rare in conventional GBM [4,5]. Recent advances in molecular analysis have identified three molecular subclasses, including (1) PXA-like tumors with a favorable prognosis, predominantly in children and young adults; (2) IDH-wildtype GBM-like tumors with poor prognosis, mainly in older adults but with more frequent BRAF mutations; and (3) RTK1 pediatric GBM-like tumors with intermediate prognoses in children and young adults, associated with chromothripsis and frequent PDGFRA amplifications [6]. These findings suggest that the eGBM cohort has clinical and molecular heterogeneity.

Our case could be categorized as (1) IDH-wildtype GBM-like eGBM. Although a molecular diagnosis was not performed, immunohistochemistry considers an IDH-wild-type in tumors composed of epithelioid cells. Preoperatively, the primary concern was a metastatic brain tumor because of its location at the corticomedullary boarder and clear boundaries, unlike conventional GBM. The surgical findings, which included clear boundaries and little adhesion to the surrounding brain parenchyma, also supported this idea. However, since only few imaging characteristics of eGBM have been reported, and specific findings are not clear and may lead to misdiagnosis as other tumors.

Similar to conventional GBM, eGBM often shows iso- to hypointensity on T1-weighted images. However, unlike the typical ring-shaped enhancement pattern of conventional GBM, contrast enhancement in eGBM is inconsistent. Huang et al. [11] reported that some cases exhibit a dural tail sign or capsular sign, which are well-known characteristic findings of meningiomas. In addition, eGBM may be associated with cystic lesions within the tumor. Further, peritumoral edema is often mild, unlike in conventional GBM. The ADC value is known to be useful for determining tumor grade and prognosis in GBM. In our case, the ADC value of the contrast-enhancing region was low, suggesting high cell density and high malignancy. Similar findings were seen in the case series by Zhao et al. [14]. Although the association between ADC values and prognosis in eGBM has not been fully studied, it may correlate similarly to other types of gliomas. Regarding tumor location, conventional GBMs are mainly situated in the cerebral white matter, whereas eGBMs are more commonly found in the cortex or limbic system. Furthermore, multiple or multicentric lesions have been reported in eGBM [8,13]. Well-circumscribed, cortical, or multiple lesions may lead to misdiagnosis as meningioma, solitary fibrous tumor, malignant lymphoma, cerebral metastasis, or other localized gliomas, including PXA, ganglioglioma, and pilocytic astrocytoma. There is some overlap between eGBM and PXA, and PXA may potentially transform into eGBM [[16], [17], [18]]. Thus, establishing a differential diagnosis can be challenging. Metastases are commonly located at the gray-white matter junction and are well-circumscribed, similar to our case. Therefore, diagnosis should be made carefully, with simultaneous systemic screening.

Similar to conventional GBM, total gross resection is recommended for eGBM, and feasible due to their well-circumscribed form and cortical location [19]. The prognosis of eGBM is reportedly poor; however, the diagnosis of eGBM could include various subtypes, indicating a possibility for treatment. There have been several reports suggesting BRAF V600E-inhibitors as a potential treatment option for eGBMs, and combination therapy with BRAF and MEK inhibitors has been reported to produce a good response in eGBM harboring BRAF mutations [20,21]. Therefore, molecular findings are an important consideration in determining the treatment strategy.

In conclusion, we present a case of eGBM mimicking a metastatic brain tumor. Both preoperative and intraoperative findings suggested metastasis rather than GBM. Even if a tumor has clear boundaries and little edema, a diagnosis of eGBM should be considered to avoid treatment delays if a primary lesion is not detected. Even after a diagnosis of eGBM, the prognosis is variable; therefore, careful pathological examination, including immunohistochemistry and molecular analysis, is necessary to determine treatment options.
