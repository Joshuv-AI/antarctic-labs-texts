Association of the triglyceride–glucose index with all-cause and cause-specific mortality in patients with comorbid hypertension and diabetes: A population-based cohort study
Authors: Jiahao Zou, Song Lu, Kaishen Cao, Ruihong He, Xia Feng, Shuang Yang, Wen Liu, Haochen Liu, Zhichao Wang, Ruichun Liao, Jin Chen, Xiaoting Jiang, Xiaoping Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Study objectiveAlthough the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been shown to be an independent risk factor for either hypertension or diabetes, its association with the coexistence of both conditions remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between the TyG index and all-cause and cause-specific mortality among patients with comorbid hypertension and diabetes.DesignTo elucidate the correlation between the TyG index and various modes of death, three multifactorial Cox proportional risk regression models were constructed. Restricted cubic spline curves are utilised to fit dose–response associations with the TyG index and various mortality rates. The effect of the TyG index threshold on different types of mortality was assessed via a two-stage Cox regression model.ParticipantsThe current study included 3222 patients from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey with hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus.Main outcome measuresThe endpoints of interest were all-cause death, cardiovascular death, and diabetes death.ResultsDuring the median 13.7-year follow-up, 1029 (31.9 %) all-cause deaths were observed, including 308 (9.6 %) cardiovascular deaths and 99 (3.1 %) diabetes deaths. Multivariate Cox proportional risk regression models revealed a positive correlation with TyG for all three outcome deaths. A restricted cubic spline demonstrated that the TyG index was linearly and positively associated with diabetes mortality, with U-shaped associations between cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality, with thresholds set at 8.87 and 9.37, respectively.ConclusionsMonitoring and maintaining appropriate TyG levels may help reduce the risk of mortality in patients with diabetes and hypertension.

Introduction

Diabetes represents one of the major public health challenges facing the world. Although its incidence has been controlled to some extent through a range of treatment and management approaches [1], the extended survival of patients has led to an increasing prevalence of multiple coexisting conditions. Among these, hypertension combined with diabetes stands as one of the most common forms of comorbidity [2], significantly increasing the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality [3]. Nevertheless, current healthcare strategies predominantly rely on single-disease management models, leaving patients with comorbidities lacking targeted guidance for risk assessment and intervention.

Insulin resistance is recognised as the shared pathophysiological basis for both diabetes and hypertension [4,5]. Early identification and monitoring of insulin resistance may prove crucial in reducing the risk of metabolic-related diseases among individuals with hypertension and diabetes. Although the hyperinsulinemic euglycaemic clamp test is the gold standard for assessing insulin resistance, its complex operation and high cost limit its widespread application in routine clinical practice and population studies. In recent years, the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) has gained widespread use as a surrogate marker for insulin resistance due to its simplicity of calculation and low testing costs [6,7], with its validity confirmed in multiple epidemiological studies [8,9]. However, existing evidence primarily originates from general populations or patients with single diseases, with relatively scarce research on the prognostic value of the TyG index in individuals co-affected by hypertension and diabetes. Consequently, this study utilises data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey to investigate the association between the TyG index and all-cause mortality as well as major specific mortality in patients with hypertension and diabetes. This aims to provide evidence-based support for early risk stratification and precision interventions in this population.

Material and methods

The NHANES is a continuous cross-sectional survey of a nationally representative and complex multiphase sample intended to assess the nutritional and health conditions of the deinstitutionalised U.S. population [10] to contribute to the scientific underpinnings of public health policy, disease prevention, health promotion, and medical research. The results of the NHANES study are reported following the STROBE guidelines; the entire study process adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for the NHANES study plan was granted by the Ethics Review Board of the National Centre for Health Statistics, with each participant providing written informed consent. This analysis did not involve the use of any personally identifiable information and did not require specific ethics approval or consent to participate. The datasets generated and analyzed in this study are readily accessible on the website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html).

Study population

To probe the connection that the TyG index has with the three types of mortality in a population with hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus, the current cohort included information on subjects from the NHANES dataset from 1999 to 2018, and according to the research aims of the current cohort, we removed participants who did not have hypertension (n = 77,937) or diabetes (n = 20,157), participants who were ineligible (n = 8) and participants who were under 18 years old (n = 7) and lacked TyG data (n = 3497). Diagnosis of diabetes mellitus and hypertension

Diabetes mellitus was defined as any of the following: self-reported or physician-diagnosed diabetes mellitus, HbA1c levels of 6.5 % or greater, FPG levels of 7.0 mmol/L or greater, plasma glucose measurements of 11.1 mmol/L or greater in an oral glucose tolerance test [11], or the use of insulin or oral hypoglycaemic drugs. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension or the use of antihypertensive medication [12].

Assessment of the TyG index

TyG was calculated via the formula Ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2].

Assessment of mortality

All-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality (cardiovascular mortality and diabetes mortality) were used as endpoints in this study. The mortality information for the subjects was obtained from the NHANES dataset by connecting it to the National Death Index through social security numbers. Causes of mortality were identified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10). All-cause mortality was defined as death due to any cause. Cardiovascular mortality includes mortality resulting from cardiac disorders (ICD-10 codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51) and cerebrovascular disorders (ICD-10 codes I60-I69). Diabetes mortality is death attributed to diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 codes E10-E14).

Covariates

We collected the participants' demographic and medical history information, as well as physical and laboratory examination data from the NHANES database, to comprehensively assess their health status and associated risk factors. Demographic information included age, sex (female/male), poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), and ethnicity (non-Hispanic white/ Mexican American/ non-Hispanic black/ other Hispanic/ other races), which were obtained through standardised questionnaires. Medical history information covering smoking status (never/ former/ now), alcohol use (never/ former/ mild/ moderate/ heavy), and use of lipid-lowering medications (yes/no) was collected by professional investigators through face–to-face interviews or self-administered questionnaires. Physical and laboratory examination data were measured by medical professionals or obtained through standardised laboratory tests. The physical data included body mass index (BMI), which was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). The laboratory parameters included lipid and glucose metabolism-related parameters (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL—C], total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], fasting blood glucose [FPG], and uric acid [UA]) and liver function-related parameters (gamma-glutamyl transferase[GGT], aspartate aminotransferase[AST], and alanine aminotransferase [ALT]). The collection of samples was carried out by trained professionals at the Mobile Examination Centre, and the tests were performed via standardised laboratory analytical methods. Specifically, lipid indices were assessed via an enzymatic colorimetric assay, while FPG levels were determined via a hexametaphosphate glucose dehydrogenase assay, and liver enzymes (ALT, AST, and GGT) were analyzed via the kinetic rate method, all of which were performed via designated automated biochemistry analysers (e.g., Beckman Coulter or Roche BioChem Analyser).

Statistical analysis

The study subjects were categorised into four groups on the basis of quartiles of the TyG index, with baseline characteristics presented as medians (25th to 75th percentiles), means±standard errors (SEs), or absolute numbers (percentages). Between-group differences were contrasted via weighted one-way ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test, Fisher's exact test, or the weighted chi–square test. Before exploring the associations between the TyG index and both all-cause and cause-specific mortality, we first performed covariate selection. Specifically, covariates were determined based on prior studies and clinically relevant factors considered likely to influence outcomes. We then calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs) for each covariate using multiple linear regression models and excluded those with a VIF greater than 2 (Supplementary Table 1: Collinearity screening TyG and other variables). Finally, the proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, which indicated that the assumption was not violated (Supplementary Fig. 1: Schoenfeld residual test for mortality).Ultimately, we constructed 3 multivariate Cox proportionate risk regression models according to the STROBE guidelines to assess associations among TyG and various mortality rates. Model 1 accounted for age, sex, race, PIR, and BMI. Model 2 included additional adjustments for HDL—C, TC, AST, GGT, and UA based on Model 1. Model 3 was further expanded by including alcohol consumption status, smoking status, and the use of lipid-lowering medications based on Model 2. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to establish a dose–response association between the TyG index and mortality. The selection of RCS curve nodes is guided by minimising the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). RCS curves were fitted using knots at varying numbers and positions, with the AIC value calculated for each model. The final knot configuration was selected based on the model exhibiting the lowest AIC value, indicating the optimal balance between model fit and complexity. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with three knots (at 10 %, 50 %, and 90 %) was ultimately employed. When a nonlinear association was detected, a two-stage Cox proportional hazards model was applied to estimate the potential threshold effect. The turning point was determined using maximum likelihood estimation. This model assumes that the exposure–outcome relationship can be approximated by two linear functions on either side of the threshold, allowing hazard ratios to be estimated separately below and above the inflection point, thereby quantifying the threshold effect. Moreover, we conducted subgroup analyses considering key demographic and social factors to explore whether associations among TyG indices for mortality were stable across subgroups. Likelihood ratio tests were used to assess the differences among various strata, and multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to confirm the robustness of the results. Bilateral P values less than 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. All analyses were performed via R version 4.4.2.

RCS analysis

The results of the restricted triple spline analysis(Fig. 2) showed a U-shaped association between the TyG index and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and a linear association with diabetes mortality. Threshold effects were further assessed via two segmented Cox proportional risk regression models among TyG indices with different mortality risks. The analysis revealed that the thresholds for cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality were 8.87 and 9.37, respectively (Table 3). For the completely adjusted models, at greater than threshold values, the TyG index was positively correlated with cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.52, 95 % CI 1.17–1.97) and all-cause mortality (HR 1.45,95 % CI 1.22–1.74). Sensitivity analysis

To confirm the reliability of the link between the TyG index and mortality, three sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, participants within the previous 2 years were excluded (n = 326)to minimise the potential reverse causality effect of exposure and outcome. Second, participants with extreme BMIs, pregnancies and cancer were excluded separately (n = 530). The results obtained were generally in line with the main analyses (Supplementary Table 2: Sensitivity analysis). Third, to assess the potentially influential effects of confounders that were not measured in the study, we calculated E values [13]. The E-values calculated in this study ranged from 1.88 to 2.75, indicating that an unmeasured confounder capable of producing approximately 1.9- to 2.8-fold associations with both TyG levels and mortality risk would be required to fully explain the observed results. Given that unmeasured confounding of this magnitude is uncommon in practical research, we consider the findings of this study to possess a degree of robustness.

Discussion

This longitudinal cohort study is the first to explore the associations between the TyG index and all-cause mortality as well as cause-specific mortality in individuals with hypertension comorbid with diabetes mellitus. The key findings were as follows: (1) The TyG index was positively correlated with cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality, and diabetes mortality among patients suffering from hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus, with a linear correlation with diabetes mortality. (2) TyG showed a U-shaped correlation with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, with thresholds set at 8.87 and 9.37, respectively. (3) The correlation of the TyG index with all-cause death was more pronounced among the cohort of patients under 75 years of age.

The prevalence of hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus continues to rise globally as the population ages [14]. Research has shown that insulin resistance is more severe in patients with hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus than in patients with a single disease and is often accompanied by more significant metabolic disturbances and circulatory dysfunction than in patients with a single disease, which increases the risk of renal complications, cardiovascular disease, and premature death [15]. Therefore, exploring effective methods for the early identification of disease risk in patients with hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus has become the focus of current research.

The TyG index, a straightforward and dependable biochemical marker used in clinical settings to evaluate insulin resistance, has been extensively demonstrated to be significantly correlated with mortality risk [16]. Notably, these studies have been conducted mainly in the general population and in single disease populations, and there is a lack of evidence on the role of the TyG index in assessing disease risk in people with hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus. Therefore, we conducted this cohort study and reported a U-shaped association between the TyG index and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in this cohort (inflection points of 8.87 and 9.37, respectively), which was linearly and positively correlated with diabetes mortality. TyG can be identified as a simple and effective indicator of mortality risk assessment in this population.

The results of this study align closely with those of prior studies, including a prospective cohort study that identified a U-shaped association between the TyG index and both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the general population, with thresholds of 8.5 and 8.7, respectively [17]. A study conducted by Chang Liu et al. [18] revealed a U-shaped correlation between the TyG index and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among young diabetic patients in the United States, with thresholds of 9.16 and 9.18, respectively. The differences in thresholds may stem from differences in the disease selection of the study populations. Notably, earlier studies did not incorporate age stratification. In contrast, our research categorised patients by age, revealing that the correlation of the TyG index with all-cause mortality was more pronounced in younger patients <75 years of age. It is noteworthy that previous studies have rarely explored the relationship between the TyG index and diabetes mortality. This study found that, unlike the U-shaped relationship observed in all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, the TyG index exhibits a linear positive correlation with diabetes mortality. The potential mechanism may lie in the following: lower TyG levels often indicate malnutrition, impaired metabolic function, or chronic wasting diseases. Concurrently, hypoglycaemia can induce arrhythmias, thrombosis, vascular inflammation, and vasoconstriction, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality [19], constituting the left branch of the U-shaped curve. Conversely, elevated TyG levels reflect pronounced insulin resistance and lipid-glucose metabolism disorders, which activate chronic inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, thereby promoting atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events, forming the right branch of the U-shaped curve. In contrast, diabetes-related mortality is primarily driven by complications arising from prolonged hyperglycaemic burden and insulin resistance (such as renal failure, infections, and hyperglycaemic crises), with risk increasing linearly with rising TyG levels. This finding suggests that different mortality endpoints may be governed by distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. Clinical interpretation of the TyG index should therefore account for specific outcome types, avoiding blanket generalisations.

In addition, some of the findings were inconsistent with those of the present study. A cohort studies carried out in Korea [20] did not identify a significant connection between the TyG index and mortality, a finding supported by the meta-analysis by Liu et al. [21]. The reasons for these inconsistent findings may be as follows. First, there are differences in metabolism according to race. For example, African Americans have reduced mitochondrial oxidative capacity and poorer metabolic flexibility than Caucasians do, leading to increased susceptibility to insulin resistance, which in turn increases the risk of poor prognosis [22]. Furthermore, certain studies failed to observe a significant link between the TyG index and mortality, which may be related to insufficient statistical power. Since the TyG index is notably linked to increased mortality risk only when it surpasses a certain threshold, it is difficult to detect subtle risk differences if the proportion of individuals with high TyG levels in the included population is low or the sample size is limited. In addition, differences in follow-up time and socioeconomic status may introduce variability in study results by acting as potential confounders.

Interestingly, in the current longitudinal cohort, the TyG-associated all-cause mortality risk was greater in the <75-year-old group. Previous studies have also reported this age-related difference, suggesting that the TyG index serves as a more robust predictor of all-cause mortality in younger individuals [6]. Possible explanations: First, it is possible that age may influence the degree of insulin resistance. Research has indicated that younger patients tend to be more prone to developing insulin resistance and generally exhibit higher TyG index values [23]. Higher TyG levels in younger individuals may be associated with greater risk for insulin resistance-related diseases, which in turn may lead to increased mortality. Second, in elderly patients, the TyG index may be more influenced by confounding factors, including the presence of multiple comorbidities and the use of various medications, which may reduce the accuracy of TyG index measurements and consequently affect the study results. Given the age-related differences in the relationship between the TyG index and mortality risk, it is essential to incorporate age into risk assessments for this comorbid population, particularly for young comorbid patients, where TyG levels should be monitored and controlled more aggressively to reduce the risk of poor prognosis.

The underlying mechanism linking the TyG index to mortality remains incompletely understood, but IR might be a crucial factor in this association. First, IR leads to a hyperglycaemic state and triggers the release of inflammatory factors, which contribute to the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells and collagen deposition. This process ultimately results in vascular sclerosis and accelerated ageing [24]. Moreover, chronic inflammation further exacerbates impaired insulin signalling, creating a vicious cycle and increasing the risk of death [25]. Additionally, IR induces lipotoxicity and dyslipidaemia, which are characterised by elevated triglycerides, increased Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol, and decreased HDL-C levels. These changes contribute to the development of atherosclerosis, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular events [26]. Second, IR significantly enhances oxidative stress. Under hyperglycaemia, the overproduction of reactive oxygen species results in damage to vascular endothelial cells, which accelerates the process of atherosclerosis [27]. IR also inhibits the production of NO, which is a key regulator of vasodilatation, and a reduction in NO directly leads to a decrease in vasodilatation, which considerably increases the likelihood of developing cardiovascular disease [28].

The findings of this study suggest that the TyG index holds significant clinical and public health value in assessing mortality risk among patients with hypertension and comorbid diabetes. Based on the identified thresholds (all-cause mortality: 9.37; cardiovascular mortality: 8.87), we propose the TyG index as a potential reference indicator for risk stratification: TyG > 9.37 denotes high risk, 8.87 < TyG ≤ 9.37 indicates moderate risk, and TyG ≤ 8.87 signifies low risk. For high-risk individuals, enhanced management strategies may be explored, including rigorous lifestyle interventions (dietary control, regular exercise, and weight management), alongside further investigation into the potential value of prioritizing insulin-sensitizing therapies (e.g., GLP-1 receptor agonists) in pharmacological treatment. Moreover, the TyG index relies on routine blood glucose and lipid profile measurements, offering straightforward calculation and low cost. This characteristic makes it particularly suitable for primary healthcare settings and large-scale population screening, thereby facilitating early identification of high-risk individuals and timely initiation of interventions. Importantly, our study also revealed heterogeneity in the association between TyG and mortality risk across different age cohorts, suggesting that clinical practice should incorporate individualized risk assessment and dynamic management tailored to patient characteristics. In conclusion, the TyG index demonstrates promising feasibility and clinical utility. Nevertheless, given the retrospective cohort design of this study, the findings warrant further validation through prospective investigations. Future studies should aim to establish applicable thresholds across diverse populations and evaluate whether TyG-guided intervention strategies can improve long-term outcomes. Such evidence will strengthen the basis for precision prevention and management of patients with hypertension and comorbid diabetes.

Study strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study are as follows: (1) As a cohort study, it utilises the nationally representative NHANES database of 3222 patients with hypertension combined with diabetes mellitus, which is an adequate and representative sample size. (2) This study is the first to investigate the association between the TyG index and mortality risk in individuals with both hypertension and diabetes mellitus in the United States. (3) A U-shaped correlation was observed between the TyG index and both cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality, which is clinically important and suggests that reasonable control of the TyG index can effectively reduce the risk of death in patients with hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

Our study has certain limitations. (1) This study exclusively included patients from the United States who had both hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Therefore, future studies need to verify the generalizability of these results to patients from other countries. (2) The partial reliance on respondent recall in the NHANES data collection methodology inevitably leads to recall bias, which has some impact on the study results. (3) Since the NHANES data design did not explicitly differentiate between type 1 and type 2 diabetes, this study was unable to further assess associations between subgroups based on stratification by type of diabetes. (4) The causes of death in this study were determined based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition, rather than more refined clinical data, and were therefore subject to a degree of diagnostic uncertainty and possible misclassification. (5) While we controlled for potential confounders where possible, the effect of residual confounding could not be fully eliminated. However, the E value analysis results suggest that potential confounders are unlikely to have a substantial effect on the findings of this study.

Conclusion

This cohort study of hypertensive comorbid diabetic populations in the United States demonstrated that TyG was strongly associated with all-cause mortality as well as cause-specific mortality rates (cardiovascular mortality, diabetes mortality). TyG showed a U-shaped correlation with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, with thresholds set at 8.87 and 9.37, and exhibited a linear positive correlation with diabetes mortality. These results indicate that surveillance and maintenance of appropriate TyG levels may help reduce the risk of mortality in this population.
