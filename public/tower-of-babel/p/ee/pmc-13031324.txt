A qualitative exploration of patient experiences living with autoimmune encephalitis
Authors: Robb Wesselingh, Nabil Seery, Katherine Ko, Christina Kazzi, Tiffany Rushen, Tracie Tan, Michelle Mykytowycz, Amanda Wells, Daria Muir, Tabitha Orth, Mastura Monif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune encephalitis (AE) can result in significant long-term sequelae including cognitive dysfunction, leading to a reduction in quality of life. While cohort studies have highlighted these difficulties broadly, we require a deeper understanding of the challenges facing these individuals in their day-to-day lives to provide better care and support. This qualitative descriptive study aimed to accurately capture the experiences of people living with AE across Australia and understand potential gaps in medical care. Inductive qualitative content analysis identified four key categories that have influenced participants and their carers experiences living with AE: (1) Access to quality medical care and factors that led to delays in diagnosis and treatment, (2) Chronic symptoms and their impact on their day-to-day lives, (3) Re-integration into premorbid life, and (4) Carer burden. Appropriate timely diagnosis and treatment was influenced by variation in clinician knowledge and available resources. Disparities in availability of medical care were observed between rural, metropolitan, primary, and tertiary care providers. Cognitive dysfunction, fatigue, and treatment-resistant seizures were common chronic symptoms that drove poor quality-of-life, changes to pre-morbid roles and social isolation. Introduction

The autoimmune encephalitides (AE) are a collection of heterogeneous disorders characterized by immune mediated inflammation of the brain parenchyma and disruption of neuronal circuitry1. Acutely, AE can present with a range of symptoms including drug refractory seizures2, change in behaviour, hallucinations or delusions3, and cognitive dysfunction4. Approximately 50% of AE cases are identified by an antibody targeted against specific neuronal cell-surface antigens such as the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) or the leucine glioma inactivated-1 (LGI-1) protein5,6. A second subgroup demonstrates antibodies targeted against specific intracellular antigens and are commonly associated with malignancy (paraneoplastic)7. The remaining cases are referred to as seronegative AE and represent cases of AE without an identifiable antibody8.

Despite available treatments, people with prior AE (pwpAE) can experience significant long-term disability. There is a high prevalence of significant cognitive impairment9 and poorly controlled seizures10, and 37% of people with AE are unable to return to work or schooling11. While the magnitude of the impact of these long-term sequelae has been previously well quantified in several studies, the lived experiences of pwpAE are rarely captured and may provide greater insight into key gaps in their ongoing care.

Although AE is generally treatable with immunotherapy, delay in commencement of effective treatment has been linked with poor long-term functional outcomes (as defined by the modified Rankin Scale score) in NMDAR AE12 and LGI-1 AE13, and cognitive outcomes on neuropsychological testing in LGI-1 AE14. While many people with AE suffer from delays in diagnosis and treatment, the patient perspective on the processes leading up to diagnosis and treatment has not been well described. Understanding what patients and their caregivers go through in the acute and chronic phase of the illness is imperative to accurately understand the disease, institute timely investigations and appropriately manage AE and its multifaceted consequences. By hearing lived experience voices we can help improve care for pwpAE.

Our ‘Living with AE’ study provided people with AE and their carers an opportunity to share their experiences of AE in the acute phase of the illness and its chronic sequelae. Capturing this in depth and the disease’s personal impacts provides healthcare workers and researchers an insight into the challenges of living with the sequalae of AE. Through these interactive lived experience reports, we can identify potential gaps in the care of people with AE. Overall, the knowledge gained can be used for improvements in research protocols as well as clinical care to help improve AE related outcomes for our patients and their care givers.

Methods

Design

This qualitative descriptive study explored the lived experiences of pwpAE and their carers during their acute illness and beyond. We used a ‘free form’ long answer questionnaire to allow the participants the ability to report what is most pertinent to them, without being restricted into particular subject domains.

Study team

RW and MM are both neurology specialists with significant expertise (> 10 years experience) in managing people with neuroimmunological conditions. CK and KK are neuropsychologists who work clinically with people with neuroimmunological conditions. RW, MM, CK and KK have been involved in the clinical management of some but not all the participants. TO, DM and AW are lived experience consultants who have personal experience with AE and who also provide resources and support to pwpAE. TT is a neurology specialist with experience in managing people with epilepsy and AE.

Participant recruitment and ethics

Purposive sampling was performed from a pool of 143 pwpAE from the Australian Autoimmune Encephalitis Consortium (AAEC) database. All patients sampled from the database were diagnosed by a neurologist as either seropositive AE, with confirmatory antibody testing and the appropriate clinical phenotype, or met the criteria for definite autoimmune limbic encephalitis or probable autoimmune encephalitis as established by Graus et al.15 Participants were selected to provide a variety in AE subtypes, a broad age range, geographical location, time period, and disability. The definition of metropolitan, regional and rural were as per the Australian Standard Geographical Classification System16. Individuals were contacted by the study investigators by telephone to explain the study and its purpose and then sent a questionnaire by email. Study recruitment was ceased once data saturation was achieved: defined as the point at which no novel themes were identified during coding for four consecutive participants.

Data collection

Data was collected using a written long-answer questionnaire (See Supplementary Fig. S1 online) containing six questions. Three questions were addressed to the participant with prior AE covering diagnosis and current day-to-day life. Three questions were addressed to a carer or family member of that participant covering current day-to-day life. The questions were developed through a working group containing two experienced neurologists (RW, MM) and two lived experience consultants (DM, AW). The questionnaire has three key components: (1) open ended question about disease onset and disease trajectory, (2) impact of disease on the participants life, (3) targeted questions examining access to healthcare. The purpose of the focus on both the acute and chronic stages of disease was so both the severity, breadth and longitudinal impacts of the illness can be captured. Symptoms reported by the participants are subjective and not specifically supported by objective evidence (e.g. cognitive deficits are based on the participant reports and not formal psychometric testing). Focus on health care resources was to determine the potential gaps in care delivery in various settings of the Australian health sector.

Data analysis

Data were analysed by qualitative thematic analysis by investigator RW using an inductive approach to generate themes. The process outlined by Vaismoradi17was followed: (i) transcribe data and read/reread, (ii) code and collate features of the data, (iii) develop and review themes, and (iv) define and name themes. All steps were repeated by investigator MM, discussed with RW and finalized for reporting. After identifying emergent themes, these findings were then compared with existing theories, models, or frameworks to further understand the identified themes, potentially offering explanations, connections, or broader implications. A framework on health care accessibility developed by Levesque at al18. was used inform themes of healthcare access. Theories postulated by Jaswal et al.19 and Lindt et al.20 on the determinants of happiness and carer burden respectively were used to inform themes relating to participant quality of life and carer burden. Microsoft Office suite (Word/Excel) was used for collation and coding. Illustrative quotes from participants have been used to highlight themes.

Ethics approval

The Human Research Ethics Committee at Alfred Health (HREC/17/Alfred/168) approved the study. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Experiences living with AE

Inductive qualitative content analysis identified four key categories that have influenced participants and their carers experiences living with AE (Fig. 1). The first category identified was access to quality medical care during their initial illness and highlighted many factors that participants felt that led to delays in diagnosis and treatment. The other three categories pertained to their long-term experiences with AE with intertwining themes. Two of these categories highlighted the long-term sequelae of AE: chronic symptoms, and re-integration into premorbid life. The final category endorsed by both the participants and their caregivers was carer burden, with significant overlap with the other three categories.

There were also disparities between participants’ descriptions of health practitioners’ knowledge depending on facility/care type (primary care compared with tertiary care) and location (rural compared with metropolitan). Participants largely endorsed positive views of their medical care when they were being managed by a tertiary specialty centre, largely driven by a perception of knowledge and expertise in managing AE (Fig. 2).

Participants also expressed disappointment with a lack of patient education (Fig. 3), limiting their understanding of the disease, their awareness of what was occurring during their acute illness, and their ability to plan for the future.

These reported instances of poor communication were perceived by participants to reduce the quality of their clinical care and in some instances delay diagnosis or treatment. In addition, they were associated with increased dissatisfaction with medical care for some participants.

Available supports and resources

When asked specifically about available resources or support their caregivers were able to name only a few supports and, in some cases, were unaware of any (Fig. 4). Only one caregiver was able to identify a resource that provided information or support specifically for AE.

Participant quotes, Available supports and resources.

With regards to support services caregivers reported having knowledge of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS, an Australian Government scheme to support people with disability), Australian Government Aged Care services and in one case the Australian Government’s Department of Veteran’s Affairs. Caregivers endorsed a high degree of uncertainty on whether participants were eligible for these services and what specifically these services were able to provide. Those who were able to access these resources felt they were inadequate for the specific needs of pwpAE (Fig. 4).

Chronic symptoms

All participants reported ongoing symptoms of AE that impacted their activities of daily living. The most reported chronic symptom was fatigue. Fatigue was typically described as a reduction in energy levels and reduced resilience to performing ordinarily routine tasks and ranged from fluctuating to persistent and pervasive. Participants reported that fatigue had a significant impact on their ability to return to work, study, or in some cases perform personal care activities (Fig. 5).

Participant quotes, Chronic symptoms.

The second most common chronic symptom was cognitive dysfunction. Descriptions of cognitive symptoms fell into two main categories:

Retrograde amnesia with islands of memory loss. In the most affected participants, their caregivers also described the participant to have anterograde amnesia with an inability to form new memories.

Half the participants described post-encephalitic epilepsy with frequent and difficult to control seizures. Seizures were reported more commonly by care-givers than the participants themselves. Concerns around ongoing seizures were the unpredictable nature of their appearance and the potential for physical injury when they did occur.

Reintegration into pre-morbid life

All participants reported that their lives had been significantly changed by their illness. Participants described three key barriers in their attempts to return to their pre-morbid lives: social isolation, loss of role or identity and loss of independence.

A change or loss of their relationships with family, friends, or colleagues was expressed by participants. Most changes were reported negatively and created a sense of social isolation for many participants. However, some participants acknowledged that these relationships often evolved to provide additional support rather than isolation.

Return to work or study was not possible for seven out of ten of the participants. In addition, some participants felt the illness had changed who they were (Fig. 6). This occurred through loss of memories of previous events, inability to perform tasks they had previously been able to do or perform roles they had previously filled.

Participant quotes, Re-integration into pre-morbid life.

Finally, the strongest negative impact AE had made on participants’ lives was a significant change in their independence. Eight out of ten participants reported requiring day to day support from their carers for personal, domestic, and community activities including getting dressed, cleaning the house or preparing food, and driving to medical appointments (Fig. 6).

Carer burden

In designing the ‘Living with AE’ study, it was apparent through our experiences as clinicians and lived experienced consultants that it was vital to explore the impact AE had on carers. Our study therefore sought a carer’s perspective, which has previously been limited within research in AE. The carers in our study are defined as informal carers. Informal carers are people who provide care within the context of an existing relationship such as a family member or friend. The reported experiences of the participants were also reflected in the experiences of the carers.

Seven of the carers who participated expressed a significant impact of AE on their loved one (Fig. 7). The level of care required varied from psychological and emotional support only, to 24-hour care. To provide this support, most of the carers described making changes to their own lives and their broader support networks to provide the necessary support and in some cases expressed a mental or physical toll on themselves. Aspects of the other themes were reported as key contributors to this carer stress: difficulty accessing medical care, unavailable resources and supports or poor knowledge of resources and supports, ongoing symptoms, and changes in their relationship with their loved one.

Discussion

This study aimed to capture the experiences of pwpAE and identify key gaps in medical and community care in Australia for AE. Our findings indicate that clinician knowledge regarding AE, communication between health practitioners, and the lack of clearly available resources or supports are areas of concern for pwpAE. There was also a significant disparity in the perception of the quality of care between rural or primary care and tertiary or metropolitan care. In addition, in our study pwpAE were significantly impacted by their chronic symptoms and the long-term effect of AE on their position in society. Finally, we identified that all these factors contribute to the overall care burden placed upon their carers.

For pwpAE, delay in diagnosis and appropriate treatment has been linked with poor outcome21. Barriers that limit access to quality health care can therefore have significant impacts on pwpAE. Levesque et al. conceptualized five dimensions of accessibility for services: Approachability, Acceptability, Availability and accommodation, Affordability, and Appropriateness18. We identified Appropriateness and Availability as the two key dimensions affecting access to quality healthcare for pwpAE. Lack of awareness of AE amongst clinicians experienced by the participants of our study was a prominent barrier to quality treatment. Possible contributors to this clinical gap are that: (1) AE is a rare disease, so most clinicians would have limited experience with AE and (2) poor awareness of existing position papers on diagnosis15 and treatment22 in AE by clinicians. It would be important to understand from clinicians whether this reflects issues with accessibility of these resources or gaps in dissemination, particularly for non-neurologist clinicians. This also had a secondary effect on clinicians’ ability to provide adequate education to pwpAE and their carers. Patient education is an important aspect of long-term care and has been demonstrated that improved health literacy can reduce the burden of disease on patients and their carers23.

We also identified differences in the participants’ experiences within the healthcare system depending on location and specialisation. This disparity between primary care compared with tertiary care and rural care compared with metropolitan care highlights the inequalities of healthcare in a large country such as Australia where many have limited access to specialty neurology care. Other disciplines within healthcare, such as stroke medicine, have highly visible and utilized evidence-based living guidelines, endorsed by key professional societies and government agencies, which can reduce individual disparities in clinical knowledge and improve the delivery of quality of care24. Given clinician knowledge was a key aspect of care that impacted the experiences of pwpAE, improved visibility and use of a consensus evidence-based guideline for diseases such as AE provide one potential avenue for correcting systemic inequalities.

Availability or accessibility of resources or supports in AE are also likely affected by knowledge gaps and the rarity of the disease. AE is often considered a monophasic illness (although 15–40% will have relapses)25,26. Compared with the acute illness, the chronic sequelae of AE have been less well described or publicized and are therefore often not considered in provision of services unlike other neuroinflammatory diseases such as Multiple Sclerosis (MS). The inability of participants and their carers to access or identify these same resources or supports suggests that either support organisations do not recognize this aspect of AE or pwpAE are not being provided with education or the means to access these services.

In our study, patients who experienced cognitive dysfunction and fatigue also reported difficulties returning to their pre-morbid lives. Yokota et al. previously identified that 29% of pwpAE are unable to return to work or study11. This increased to 67% in those who had chronic sequelae and correlated with poorer social quality of life scores11. Fatigue in AE has previously been identified as highly prevalent across many AE subtypes27 and in a study by Bink et al. was identified as a strong predictor of poor quality of life in LGI-1 AE28.

The secondary impact of these sequalae were the themes of loss captured in our study: loss of role, loss of identity, loss of independence and loss of relationships (or social isolation). A sense of belonging and contributing is a key determinant of levels of happiness and life satisfaction and contributes to our sense of identity19. Illness may impact on this either through the trauma of an abrupt change in health state or through long-term irreversible changes during chronic disease. Our findings identified that pwpAE in our study experienced both the former during the acute illness and then suffered from the latter due to chronic sequelae such as cognitive changes.

Caregiver burden is “a multi-dimensional response to the negative appraisal and perceived stress resulting from taking care of an ill individual”29. In their systematic review, Lindt et al. identified several key determinants for carer burden including the duration of care, the patient’s functional status (and specifically cognitive capacity), the presence of role conflicts and the degree of social supports20. Specific diseases such as Alzheimer’s Dementia also posed increased risk20. The increased cognitive, emotional and physical load detailed by carers in our study suggest that AE is likely to be a high burden disease in a similar vein. Carers for pwpAE can be influenced by the themes already discussed: diagnostic uncertainty, chronic sequelae including cognitive dysfunction and changes in social networks and identity that increase social isolation. A study examining carer burden specifically in epilepsy identified that carer education and medical literacy decreases the risk of carer burden30. Providing clear and available information about AE for informal carers could play a significant role in reducing their burden.

There are several limitations to our study. Given AE is a heterogenous disease, the small cohort utilized in this study limits the generalisability of the findings and the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn. However, although different subtypes of AE have different manifestations and varied mechanisms of disease, this study focuses on the commonalities experienced by people who develop a rare neuroinflammatory condition that can result in an acquired brain injury. There is a risk of bias in the recruitment of participants. PwpAE who are symptom free are less likely to have ongoing follow-up or participate in research. Equally, those with AE with extremely poor outcomes (mRS = 5–6) are unlikely to be recruited into this study due to physical and cognitive limitations. Sampling from a large Australian database of people diagnosed with AE rather than through clinics/hospitals minimizes the risk of bias. The utilisation of written questionnaires and inclusion of carers minimized the risk of excluding those with cognitive impairment but may still exclude those with language difficulties or those for whom English is a second language. Finally, the study team’s own personal experiences and expertise has an impact on the interpretation of the data. The inclusion of a multi-disciplinary study team including specialty clinicians with experience in managing AE, allied health professionals and lived experience consultants provided a multi-faceted, balanced assessment of the themes generated from the participant responses.

Conclusion

The ‘Living with AE’ study sought to accurately capture the experiences of pwpAE in five states of Australia and understand potential gaps in medical care. Appropriate timely diagnosis and treatment was influenced by variation in clinician knowledge and available resources. Disparities in availability of medical care were observed between rural, metropolitan, primary, and tertiary care providers. Cognitive dysfunction, fatigue, and treatment-resistant seizures were common chronic symptoms that drove poor quality of life, changes to pre-morbid roles and social isolation. Together, access to quality care, high burden of chronic symptoms and poor social and healthcare supports contributed to carer overburden. Education, for both clinician and patient or carer, can play a vital role in improving access to care and carer overburden. Readily available guidelines for clinicians, pwpAE and their carers would provide an avenue to meet this need.
