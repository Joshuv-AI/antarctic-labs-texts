Impact of non-pharmaceutical interventions of the COVID-19 pandemic on the trend of seasonal influenza in Kingdom of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulilah Alqarny, Maged El‑Setouhy, Majed A Alotayfi, Mahmoud M Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly affected the transmission dynamics of respiratory viruses globally. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of COVID-19 related NPIs on the trends of seasonal influenza in the Jazan region of southern Saudi Arabia.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted from January 2017 to December 2023. Data on laboratory-confirmed cases of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 were obtained from the Saudi Ministry of Health databases. The analysis compared influenza activity before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, focusing on incidence fluctuations, demographic characteristics, and the timing relative to the implementation and relaxation of NPIs.ResultsInfluenza cases declined from 449 in 2020 to 46 in 2021 (p < 0.001) during periods of strict NPI enforcement before rebounding to 430 in 2022 and 1,001 in 2023 (p < 0.001). COVID-19 cases peaked between 2020 and 2022 and showed a significant inverse correlation to influenza activity (p < 0.001). Influenza predominantly affected Saudis (88.7%) versus non-Saudis (11.3%) (p < 0.001), while COVID-19 cases were mainly among Saudis (84.3%) (p < 0.001). Age and gender distributions showed significant shifts post-NPI relaxation (p < 0.01), with influenza affecting 5–14-year-olds and 25–64-year-olds more prominently, and a gender shift from male to balanced or female predominance in recent years (p < 0.05).ConclusionCOVID-19 NPIs substantially reduced seasonal influenza transmission in the Jazan region. These findings highlight the collateral benefits of public health measures during pandemics and suggest that continued surveillance and targeted NPIs can be valuable in controlling seasonal influenza. Future strategies should integrate influenza vaccination campaigns with pandemic preparedness plans, considering regional specificities.Keywords: Non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), COVID-19, Seasonal influenza trends, Influenza surveillance

Introduction

The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic led to more than 72 million infections and 1.6 million deaths worldwide [1]. Viral infection-driven alterations of innate and adaptive immunity shape susceptibility, disease course, vaccine response, and clinical outcomes [2]. SARS-CoV-2 adds further complexity by deploying multiple immune-evasion tactics including spike camouflage, impaired MHC-I presentation, interferon suppression, altered mitophagy, cell-to-cell spread, nanotube trafficking, and syncytia-mediated lymphocyte loss [3].

Influenza, which typically infects 5–15% of the global population and causes up to 650 000 deaths annually [4], is generally mitigated by vaccination and basic hygiene measures such as cough etiquette and handwashing [5]. SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses are both transmitted via respiratory droplets, aerosols, direct contact, and indirectly through contaminated surfaces (fomites), which are generated during coughing, sneezing, talking, or breathing [6, 7].

Due to their overlapping modes of transmission, NPIs such as face masks, hand hygiene, and physical distancing can effectively curb both influenza and COVID-19. In addition, both infections impose considerable economic burdens [8]. In working-age adults, up to 88% of the total economic burden of influenza is attributable to indirect costs largely productivity losses and hospitalizations drive 70–75% of the direct medical costs [8]. Seasonal influenza alone imposes multi-billion-dollar losses: estimates range from US $4–87 billion annually across high-income countries [9], and flu-like illness results in substantial work absenteeism often 2–4 days per illness episode further amplifying productivity losses [10, 11]. In Saudi Arbia, the total economic burden for both early and delayed diagnosis was disease management costs: 49% (USD 1880 million) and 58% (USD 2730 million), respectively [12].

In early 2020, before specific antivirals or vaccines were available [13], governments relied on non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) such as mandatory masking, social distancing, travel bans, remote schooling, and the closure of workplaces, entertainment venues, and houses of worship [14]. Saudi Arabia adopted some of the world’s earliest and strictest measures: halting flights to China [15], suspending Umrah, closing the Holy Mosques for disinfection, shifting all education online, quarantining inbound travelers, and even more unprecedented temporarily banning congregational prayers nationwide [16].

Although international data point to NPI-driven declines in influenza, no study has quantified this effect in Saudi Arabia particularly in the Jazan region, which differs from the country’s central provinces in climate, healthcare access, and cross-border population movement.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the impact of COVID-19 related NPIs on the trends of seasonal influenza in the Jazan region of southern Saudi Arabia. Accordingly, our research question was: How did NPIs implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic influence the incidence and demographic distribution of seasonal influenza in the Jazan region? We hypothesized that these interventions would substantially reduce influenza transmission during the pandemic period, with a subsequent rebound following their relaxation.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective observational study on aggregated surveillance records provided by the Saudi Ministry of Health between January 2017 and December 2023. This allowed encompassing both the pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post pandemic phases to enable a temporal comparison of influenza activity to assess the influence of NPIs implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic on the trends of seasonal influenza in the Jazan region, Saudi Arabia.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study included data of all individuals with laboratory-confirmed cases of seasonal influenza and/or COVID-19 reported in the Jazan region during the study period. Data was collected in aggregate form. No exclusion criteria were applied with respect to age, gender, or nationality, ensuring comprehensive coverage of all reported cases.

Data sources and case definitions

Influenza data was obtained from the National Influenza Surveillance Program, which systematically records influenza-like illness (ILI) and laboratory-confirmed influenza cases from healthcare centers across the Kingdom. COVID-19 case data was retrieved from the official COVID-19 dashboard maintained by the Ministry of Health. Confirmed influenza cases were defined as individuals presenting with ILI symptoms and a positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test for influenza A or B. Confirmed COVID-19 cases were defined as individuals who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR).

Variables and outcome measures

The primary outcome of interest was the monthly count of laboratory-confirmed influenza cases. Independent variables included the monthly number of confirmed COVID-19 cases, the timing and stringency of NPIs, and demographic factors such as age, gender, and nationality. The study aimed to assess whether fluctuations in influenza incidence were associated with the implementation or relaxation of public health measures during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Bias and limitations

Bias was mitigated using of standardized national data sources and uniform case definitions based on laboratory confirmation. The reliance on aggregated data further reduced the risk of individual-level misclassification and selection bias. However, the study is subject to certain limitations, including the unavailability of data on vaccination status, comorbidities, and healthcare-seeking behaviors, all of which may have influenced case detection and reporting.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software, version 25. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize data in terms of frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Time-series analysis was utilized to evaluate longitudinal patterns and temporal associations between COVID-19 activity and influenza trends. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test to examine differences in age, gender, and nationality distributions between COVID-19 and influenza cases. Statistical significance was considered at p-values ≤ 0.05, with results deemed highly significant at p-values < 0.01. In addition to descriptive statistics and chi-square tests, we applied an Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model to assess influenza and COVID-19 case trends over time. ARIMA modeling was used to capture underlying temporal patterns and confirm the robustness of observed fluctuations in influenza incidence before, during, and after the implementation of NPIs. The model was fitted to monthly case data from 2017 to 2023 for influenza and from 2020 to 2023 for COVID-19.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a marked alteration in seasonal influenza dynamics in the Jazan region during the COVID-19 pandemic. Influenza cases increased unexpectedly in early 2020 (449 cases compared to 130 in 2019), declined to a historic low in 2021 (46 cases), and rebounded to 430 cases in 2022 and 1,001 cases in 2023. These fluctuations closely corresponded with the introduction, strict enforcement, and subsequent relaxation of NPIs. The sharp decline in influenza cases observed during 2021 aligns with global reports of unprecedented reductions in influenza circulation under stringent NPIs [25–27].

In contrast, COVID-19 showed three distinct peaks in August 2020, July 2021, and January–February 2022 followed by a significant decline in incidence, mirroring the global patterns in SARS-CoV-2 trends.

Saudi Arabia was among the first countries in the region to implement comprehensive NPIs, including travel bans, school closures, and mandatory face masking, beginning in March 2020 [15]. Our findings align with ecological studies from China, Korea, and other countries showing that vigorous public-health measures significantly reduced influenza transmission [28, 29]. The “seesaw effect” observed in our study rising influenza activity as COVID-19 waned in 2022–2023 echoes World Health Organization regional analyses and the work of Wang et al. (2023) who conducted correlation analyses across World Health Organization regions and reported predominantly negative correlations between COVID-19 and influenza activity particularly during the Omicron phase suggesting alternating dominance that aligns with the seesaw analogy [30]. At the cellular level, mechanistic studies provide compelling support. Cheemarla et al. (2024) demonstrated in human airway epithelial cultures that infection with influenza A induces a robust interferon response that suppresses subsequent SARS-CoV-2 replication, whereas SARS-CoV-2 infection does not equivalently suppress influenza A, illustrating asymmetric interference [31]. Similarly, Gilbert-Girard et al. (2024) observed that contemporary influenza A/H3N2 strains strongly inhibit both ancestral and Omicron SARS-CoV-2 in nasal epithelium models through interferon-mediated pathways [32]. These findings reinforce the notion that influenza can outcompete COVID-19 in certain contexts, shaping the seesaw pattern observed at the population level.

Reduced influenza immunity after two suppressed seasons, along with gradual NPI relaxation and widespread COVID-19 vaccination [30, 33, 34], likely facilitated the 2022–2023 influenza resurgence.

Prior to the pandemic, influenza primarily affected children aged 5–14 and adults aged 25–64. Following the relaxation of NPIs the distribution shifted toward the 25–64 age group, comprising between 56.8% and 71.7% of cases annually. A slight male predominance in influenza cases persisted until 2023, when the gender distribution became nearly equal. Meanwhile, COVID-19 exhibited a shift from male predominance in 2020 (60.0%) to female predominance in 2022 (59.1%). These demographic trends may reflect differences in exposure patterns, compliance with public health measures, and potential biological susceptibility [35, 36].

Age-specific variations in influenza incidence may reflect immunological phenomena such as immune imprinting or original antigenic sin. Early life exposure to a specific influenza A subtype can confer long-lasting protection against that subtype later in life, altering susceptibility patterns across age groups over time. In one modeling study, children under 4 years had about a three-fold higher risk of medically attended influenza A infection compared to adults aged 20–29 years, even after adjusting for confounders [37, 38].

Socio-educational NPIs especially consistent use of facemasks and hand hygiene can substantially interrupt influenza transmission. In one randomized controlled study among young adults, combined face mask use and hand hygiene reduced influenza-like illness (ILI) rates by up to 75% during the study period, and decreased laboratory-confirmed influenza incidence by 43%, compared to control participants [39]. Additional evidence indicates that early and diligent implementation of masks and intensified hand hygiene in household settings significantly reduces secondary transmission of influenza [40].

The effectiveness of NPIs in reducing influenza transmission during the COVID-19 pandemic prompts consideration of their sustainable application in non-pandemic periods. While stringent measures like widespread lockdowns and border closures are effective, they carry substantial socioeconomic costs and may not be practically sustainable in the long term [41].

Nevertheless, selectively applied and contextually tailored NPIs such as enhanced mask usage during peak flu season, improved hand hygiene, and targeted class-level school closures could be adapted sustainably. For example, school-based micro-interventions, such as closing individual classrooms when cases are detected, have been shown to significantly reduce attack rates at a much lower cost than entire school closures [42].

Furthermore, evidence from New Zealand indicates that careful assessment of NPI components can identify measures that minimize societal disruption while effectively curbing seasonal and epidemic respiratory illnesses [41]. Importantly, risk communication and social distancing have emerged as highly effective yet less economically disruptive strategies when deployed strategically [43].

Finally, historical analyses of the 1918 influenza pandemic demonstrate that prompt and well-implemented NPIs significantly reduced mortality without worsening medium-term economic outcomes [44]. Taken together, this evidence supports the notion that a sustainable NPI strategy for influenza could involve a blend of targeted, low-cost measures such as enhanced hygiene practices, seasonal mask use in high-risk settings, improved risk messaging, and selective closures strategically timed and aligned with healthcare capacity and seasonal peaks.

The influenza surge observed in January–February 2020 may be explained by several factors: (i) delayed implementation of NPIs and initial public resistance to imposed restrictions [45], (ii) variations in circulating influenza subtypes [23]; (iii) intensified surveillance and diagnostic efforts prompted by early pandemic preparedness measures; and (iv) potential alterations in host immunity resulting from SARS-CoV-2 infection or uncertainties surrounding treatment strategies during the initial phase of the pandemic [46].

The dramatic suppression and subsequent rebound of influenza underscore the effectiveness and transient nature of broad NPIs. Policymakers should weigh dual benefits when considering future pandemic measures and reinforce routine influenza vaccination to mitigate post-NPI resurgence. Continuous surveillance remains essential to detect shifts in seasonality and guide targeted interventions.

The demographic shifts in influenza cases observed in our study may also be interpreted through sociocultural and occupational lenses. The post-NPI predominance of cases among adults aged 25–64 years likely reflects occupational exposure, as this group represents the bulk of the workforce that resumed in-person activity earlier than children fully returned to schools. Similarly, the balanced or female predominance observed in recent years could be influenced by changing gender roles in Saudi society, with increasing female participation in workplaces and universities leading to higher exposure opportunities. In contrast, the persistently lower burden among older adults (≥ 65 years) may reflect heightened caution, stronger adherence to NPIs, and prioritization for vaccination in this group. These sociocultural and occupational considerations highlight the importance of contextual factors when interpreting epidemiological shifts.

Strengths of the study include population-level surveillance data spanning seven years and direct comparison of two major respiratory pathogens within the same region. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Notably, it lacked data on vaccination status for both influenza and COVID-19, which limits the ability to assess how immunization trends may have influenced annual case numbers. Additionally, the absence of information on individual comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and cardiopulmonary conditions, which are known to impact susceptibility and disease severity further restricts the depth of analysis. The study also relied on aggregated case data without stratification by influenza virus types or subtypes (e.g., A/H1N1, A/H3N2, B lineages), which could have offered more detailed insights into transmission patterns. Moreover, potential underreporting or delays in surveillance reporting during pandemic peaks may have compromised temporal accuracy. Finally, these findings may not be generalizable to other regions in Saudi Arabia with differing population demographics or healthcare accessibility [47, 48]. These limitations highlight the need for future multi-regional studies with individual-level data to confirm and extend our observations.

Future research should incorporate individual-level data, including vaccination history, comorbid conditions, and behavioral factors such as adherence to NPIs. Integrating molecular surveillance of circulating influenza strains and assessing co-infection rates with SARS-CoV-2 would further elucidate interaction dynamics. Comparative studies across multiple regions of Saudi Arabia would improve national representativeness and allow for regional public health planning. Additionally, maintaining robust surveillance systems post-pandemic is critical to detect shifts in seasonality, guide vaccine strain selection, and support timely public health responses. Lastly, policymakers should consider dual-virus strategies that simultaneously address influenza and COVID-19, especially during overlapping respiratory seasons.

Conclusion

The implementation of NPIs during the COVID-19 pandemic significantly suppressed seasonal influenza activity in the Jazan region. Once these measures were relaxed, influenza rebounded and shifted toward more year-round transmission. These findings demonstrate an association rather than direct causation, highlighting the collateral benefits of NPIs but also their transient effect. Caution is warranted in extrapolating these results beyond the study setting. Continued surveillance, strengthened vaccination efforts, and targeted public health strategies remain essential to prevent future influenza resurgence.
