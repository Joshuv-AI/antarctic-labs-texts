Assessing the structural performance of additively manufactured carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based adherends bonded with graphene-enhanced adhesive using experimental and ANN analysis
Authors: Thulasidhas Dhilipkumar, N. Karthikeyan, Arun Prasad Murali, Karthik V. Shankar, P. Karuppusamy, Amanullah Fatehmulla, Rakesh Kumar, Karuppaiah Selvakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Additive manufacturing (AM) enables the fabrication of complex geometries; however, the mechanical performance of 3D-printed thermoplastic assemblies remains limited by the inherent weakness of 3D-printed polymers and by constraints in joining. The present research addresses these problems by examining the mechanical behaviour of adhesively bonded single-lap joints (SLJs) prepared from carbon-fibre-reinforced polylactic acid (PLA-CF) adherends and epoxy reinforced with graphene nano filler. Dog-bone specimens were fabricated to determine the optimal printing parameters, with a 0° raster orientation showing the highest tensile strength. SLJs with 0.5–1.5 wt.% graphene nano powder (GNP) enhanced epoxy were tested under shear, flexural, and free-vibration loading conditions. The results show that 1.5 wt.% GNP inclusion has the highest improvement, increasing shear and flexural strength by 107.71% and 67.15%, respectively, compared to unmodified joints. Failure analysis indicated that the failure mode shifted from mixed mode to cohesive failure with the addition of 1.5 wt.% GNP, due to microcrack bridging and interfacial debonding. Furthermore, a backpropagation Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model was developed to predict the nonlinear behaviour of the SLJs. The ANN demonstrated excellent agreement with experimental data, with an R2 value of 0.99345. Thereby, the combined experimental and ANN-based approach proves that incorporating short carbon fibres into the adherends and GNP reinforcement into the adhesive can significantly enhance the structural performance of SLJs. The present results will be useful for designing high-strength, lightweight AM-based bonded structures suitable for advanced engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42780-w.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) techniques are widely used in many industries, including automotive, aerospace, construction, and medical, to produce components with complex geometries. The AM process is usually known as three-dimensional (3D) printing technology. To make complex parts, both metallic and non-metallic materials are fabricated using 3D printing techniques1. Among the various 3D printing methods, fused deposition modelling (FDM) is a popular and cost-effective method for printing thermoplastic polymers such as polycarbonate (PC), polylactic acid (PLA), Polyethene terephthalate glycol (PETG), and acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS). In the FDM process, structural parts are formed by layer-by-layer deposition of melted thermoplastics2. However, the strength of 3D-printed components prepared from pure thermoplastics is insufficient for potential structural applications. To enhance the strength of thermoplastics, they have been reinforced with short fibres, nanofibres, and continuous fibres for load-bearing structural applications3–5.

The advantages of 3D printing techniques include the ability to produce complex materials and designs. However, a drawback of this technique is its restriction to the bed size of the 3D printing machine, making it unsuitable for large-scale structures6. Large-scale structures are assembled from smaller 3D-printed components. During this assembly process, the joining stage is unavoidable7. There are two main methods for joining polymer-based materials: traditional and modern techniques. The conventional methods utilise bolts, nuts, and rivet fasteners. This method has led to problems such as localised stress, increased weight, debonding during drilling for rivet and bolt insertion, and corrosion on fasteners8–15. However, modern methods have addressed these issues.

Among various thermoplastics, PLA-based composites are bioactive and biodegradable, with improved mechanical properties, making them easy to manufacture. Since they are made from renewable materials, they are suitable for lightweight structural uses. Reinforcing the PLA with CF powder further improved its mechanical strength for these applications. The adhesive joining technique was employed to assemble large-scale structures with 3D-printed adherends. To enhance the strength of the bonded joints, the epoxy was reinforced with various nanofillers, including carbon nanotubes (CNT), multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT), carbon blacks, single-walled carbon nanohorns, graphene oxide nano-platelets, graphene nanoplatelets, Nano clay, Al2O3, SiO2, and TiO216. Furthermore, the strength of adhesive-bonded joints was increased through geometric modifications of SLJs, such as scarf joints, joggle joints17–19, wavy interface geometries20, and stepped joints21.

Kovan et al.6 reported the effects of layer thickness and print orientation of PLA adherends on the strength of 3D-printed adhesively bonded SLJs. Khosravani et al.22 reported the optimal printing parameters for PLA to improve the mechanical behaviours of the 3D printed joints. Khosravani et al.23 also optimised printing parameters, including raster angle, raster width, and layer thickness, for 3D-printed PETG adhesive-bonded SLJs to achieve the maximum structural performance. Meanwhile, Kumar et al.24 investigated the shear performance of 3D-printed SLJs, in which a compliance-tailored adhesive improved their structural integrity. Khosravani et al.21 examined the influence of stepped geometry on the load-bearing capacity and failure mechanism of 3D printed PLA adherend-based SLJs. Richard Nash and Yaning Li.20 utilised the wavy geometry of the suture interface in 3D-printed adhesive-bonded SLJs to enhance mechanical interlock, thereby improving mechanical properties. Mohammad Reza Bagheri and Mojtaba Haghighi-Yazdi fabricated and conducted experiments on 3D-printed adherend-based SLJs made from functionally graded materials, including PLA and ABS, using the FDM method. The experimental results show that the joint strength was 41% higher than that of the neat adherends25. T. F. R. Ribeiro et al. investigated the tensile strength of a 3D-printed adherend-based adhesively bonded joint, in which the adherends were made of PLA, PETG-modified, and ABS, and joined with the adhesives Araldite 2015 and Sikaforce 7752. The experimental results show that this PLA adherend and adhesive yield a good strength26. Özkan Öz and Fatih Huzeyfe Öztürk27 reported statistical results showing that joint strength is influenced by adherend type to the extent of 5.28% and by adhesive type to the extent of 91.60%. The influence of joint strength on the adherend type is less than that on the adhesive type. Among the numerous types of polymers, PLA exhibits the highest joint strength in 3D printed joints3. The shear strength of PETG is 102% greater than that of TPU, where PETG shows the best performance in structural applications28.

Nidhal Naat et al. reported that the 3D printed PLA adherend-based epoxy incorporated adhesive bonded joint has enhanced joint strength through adherend geometry modifications, such as the creation of interlocks and printing orientation29.

Manoj et al.30 analysed the mechanical performance and failure assessments of 3D printed PLA-based SLJs with a tooth-shaped interface. Cetin and Tagni Fossi31 reported no significant effects of modified bonding surface pattern on the mechanical strength of the 3D printed PLA adhesive bonded joints under hygrothermal conditions. Moreover, the substantial effects of hygrothermal ageing on PLA 3D-printed joints were considered. Rajesh et al.32 reported that the joggle and wavy-shaped design has enhanced the structural integrity of the 3D printed joints, which improved the shear and vibration behaviour. Öztürk et al.33 investigated the effects of printing angle and overlap length on the tensile behaviour of PC adherend-based 3D printed SLJs. Present research addresses the limitations of developing large-scale component size in 3D-printed parts and enhancing their structural performance by incorporating nanofillers into adhesive-bonded joints.

Furthermore, Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) offer a reliable deep learning method for predicting the behaviour of adhesive joints under shear loading, particularly when addressing nonlinear issues in structural engineering and materials science. Although ANNs have not been widely used to predict adhesive joint performance, previous studies have shown their potential for modelling the performance of laminated and woven composites. Liu et al.34 used an ANN framework to analyse the complex relationship between shear stress and strain, achieving an average error of 5% compared to finite element method (FEM) calculations. Likewise, Kim et al.35 developed a deep neural network that accurately predicts the stress–strain responses of woven carbon composites under various loading conditions, yarn configurations, and material properties. Vinayak et al.36 investigated the influence of graphene content on the shear properties of PLA-based SLJ produced by FDM. They found that incorporating graphene nanoparticles into the epoxy adhesive resulted in improvements of 20.94%, 12.34%, 38.98% and 31.11%, ranging from 0.25 to 1 wt.%. The ANN method accurately predicted the failure loads, with a correlation coefficient of 0.8471, indicating a strong agreement. Vivek et al.3 studied the shear behaviour of 3D-printed ABS and PLA, focusing on lap, scarf, and stepped joints with different adhesives and surface treatments. Results highlighted the importance of material type, joint design, adhesive, and surface pre-treatment. An R2 of 95.6% indicated reliable results for large-scale, high-strength component production with FDM 3D printers. Faith et al.37 studied how the adherend material (PLA and PETG) and joint geometry (SLJ, notched SLJ) influence failure loads and fracture behaviour. A cohesive zone model (CZM) was developed to simulate load–displacement responses and failure progression. CZM simulations closely matched experiments, with discrepancies of 1.03% to 9.77%.

Despite significant progress in the adhesive bonding of 3D-printed structures, the combined use of graphene-enhanced adhesives with CF-reinforced 3D-printed PLA adherends has not yet been explored. The effect of adding GNP on the performance of 3D-printed PLA-CF-based SLJs under shear, flexural, and vibrational loads remains to be investigated for real-time structural applications. Therefore, this research addresses this gap by combining experimental testing with predictive ANN modelling to develop a dependable framework for optimising adhesive joint performance in additively manufactured structures. The study also aims to enhance the performance of adhesive joints on CF-reinforced PLA substrates through graphene-enhanced epoxy adhesives. Bulk specimens made from 3D printed PLA were tested, followed by evaluation of shear, flexural, and vibration behaviours of the bonded joints. Failure modes of samples subjected to shear and flexural loads were analysed according to ASTM D5573-99 by visual inspection, and morphological analysis was performed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). The adherends were produced via FDM.

3D-printing, incorporating short CF within PLA, and bonded with epoxy adhesive containing various weight fractions of GNP (0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0%). A backpropagation-based ANN model was employed to predict shear, flexural, and vibration responses, with its predictions validated against experimental data results.

GNP modified adhesive preparation

The GNP-modified epoxy adhesive was prepared by ultrasonication (Fig. 1). The adhesive mixture contained various weight percentages of GNP (0%, 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0%), which enhanced the strength of the 3D-printed PLA-adherend-based SLJs. The initial step involved sonication of GNPs with 100 g of acetone at 40 kHz for 1 h, during which 50% of the acetone evaporated, as confirmed by weighing the solution. After adding the epoxy to the mixture, it was sonicated for 1 h. The excess acetone was then removed by stirring the solution with a magnetic stirrer at 750 rpm and 60 °C25,26.Fig. 1Preparation of graphene-added epoxy adhesive through the ultrasonication process.

Preparation of graphene-added epoxy adhesive through the ultrasonication process.

Testing methods

Bulk specimen test

Bulk 3D-printed specimens were tested on a Tinius Olsen universal testing machine equipped with 10 kN load cells. A crosshead speed of 1 mm/min was used for testing the samples. Results were reported in accordance with ASTM D 638. The average of three replicate samples was used for all tests performed in the present study.

Shear test

At room temperature, the Tinius Olsen universal testing apparatus was used to perform the shear test. The shear test was performed on all three specimens with different GNP weight percentages at a crosshead speed of 13 mm/min (Fig. 4). Testing was conducted in accordance with ASTM D5868, using the specified procedures and sample sizes. The optimised printing orientation angle is 0°, as confirmed by the bulk specimen test results. The length of each adherend is 101.6 mm. The overlap length is 25.4 mm, the adherend thickness is 3 mm, and the adhesive thickness is 0.1 mm.Fig. 4Experimental analysis of carbon fibre reinforced dog bone specimen and lap joints.

Experimental analysis of carbon fibre reinforced dog bone specimen and lap joints.

Results and discussion

Bulk specimen test

The bulk specimen test was conducted to investigate the effect of raster angle on the tensile performance of 3D-printed PLA samples reinforced with CF, focusing on ultimate tensile strength (UTS), load-carrying capacity, and fracture strain. Figure 7a-c displays the tensile results of specimens printed at three raster angles (0°, 45°, and 90°). With a tensile strength of 38.3 MPa and a tensile load of 387 N, the sample oriented at 0° showed excellent mechanical properties. Fiber alignment with the load, which enables effective stress transfer and reduces interlayer failure, causes this improvement. Conversely, samples oriented at 45° and 90° exhibited lower load capacities of 341 N and 351 N, respectively, along with reduced tensile strengths of 33.7 MPa and 34.7 MPa. Because of off-axis fibre alignment, which led to shear failure and reduced load transfer, the 45° raster specimen’s tensile strength was 13.65% lower than that of the 0° specimens. This is due to off-axis fibre alignment, resulting in shear failure and reduced load transfer. The samples with 90° orientation, where filament paths are perpendicular to the load, depend more on interlayer bonding, making them more prone to premature failure under tension.Fig. 7Tensile properties of short CF reinforced PLA with different printing orientations, (a) Load vs displacement, (b) Stress vs strain, (c) Tensile strength vs raster orientation.

Tensile properties of short CF reinforced PLA with different printing orientations, (a) Load vs displacement, (b) Stress vs strain, (c) Tensile strength vs raster orientation.

Moreover, the 0° specimen also demonstrated greater strength, with a fracture strain of 14.5%. The ± 45° and 90° samples recorded lower strain values of 10% and 10.7%, respectively, indicating more brittle behaviour due to less optimal fibre alignment.

Influence of graphene nanofiller on shear behaviour and failure modes of PLA-CF-based SLJs

The shear response of PLA-CF-based SLJs with varying GNP content is illustrated in Fig. 8a-c. The addition of GNP improved the energy absorption capacity of the SLJs, as shown by the larger area under the load–displacement curves in Fig. 8a. Figure 8a shows that SLJ reinforced with GNP had wider curves with higher load and displacement values, which indicates higher energy absorption during shear loading. This improvement was evident at 1.5 wt.% GNP, reflecting enhanced stress transfer and delayed crack initiation. The 2.0 wt.% joints absorbed more energy than the neat adhesive; however, the improvement was less at higher filler contents due to microstructural irregularities.Fig. 8Shear properties of short carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based lap joints, (a) Load vs displacement, (b) Stress vs strain, (c) Strength vs graphene wt.%.

Shear properties of short carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based lap joints, (a) Load vs displacement, (b) Stress vs strain, (c) Strength vs graphene wt.%.

The variation in shear strength of SLJ with increasing GNP content is presented in Fig. 8b. Compared to the unreinforced (PLACF0), the inclusion of 0.5 wt.% GNP (PLACF0.5) improved the shear strength by 26.56%. Further increment in GNP content from 1.0 wt.% to 1.5 wt.% enhanced joint strength by 58.75% and 107.71%, respectively. Thus, it indicates that incorporating GNP up to 1.5 wt.% effectively strengthened the SLJ, due to improved load transfer and interfacial reinforcement. However, at 2.0 wt.% GNP content (PLACF2.0), the shear strength decreased (Fig. 8c), though remaining above the neat SLJ.

The failure surfaces of the joints are shown in Fig. 9a-e. The unreinforced joint (PLACF0) exhibited mixed-mode failure, with adhesive failure as the predominant mode, indicating weak adhesion at the joining area. At 0.5 wt.% GNP content (PLACF0.5), the failure was primarily adhesive, indicating that the reinforcement was insufficient to alter the failure mode. Mixed-mode failure was noticed at 1.0 wt.% GNP content (PLACF1.0), indicating partial improvement in interfacial strength.Fig. 9Shear failure modes of carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based lap joints.

Shear failure modes of carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based lap joints.

A change in failure mode was observed at 1.5 wt.% GNP inclusion (PLACF1.5), where the failure transitioned to cohesive mode, confirming enhanced interfacial bonding and matrix integrity. In contrast, the 2.0 wt.% GNP-reinforced SLJ (PLACF2.0) exhibited adhesive failure, suggesting that further addition of GNP beyond the optimum level reduced interfacial bonding, thereby affecting overall joint performance.

Microstructural analysis

The failure surfaces of single-lap PLA–CF 3D printed adherends bonded with neat and GNP-modified adhesives were examined using FE-SEM to understand the effect of GNP incorporation on the failure behaviour, as shown in Fig. 12a-d. The failure surface of the neat adhesive joint (PLACF0) exhibited a relatively smooth, flat morphology (Fig. 12a), indicating limited crack resistance and low energy absorption. A smooth surface led to a predominantly brittle failure mechanism, reducing bonding efficiency between the adherend and the adhesive. At 0.5 wt.% GNP inclusion (PLACF0.5) and irregular features on the failure surface (Fig. 12b) were observed, but no significant toughening mechanisms were evident. The limited GNP content and its partial dispersion led to weak regions within the adhesive layer, resulting in premature failure. As a result, the SLJs with 0.5 wt.% GNP showed poorer resistance to shear loading than both the neat adhesive and higher GNP-loaded joints.Fig. 12Fractography images of single-lap PLA–CF 3D printed adherends bonded with neat and graphene-modified adhesives (a) Neat, (b) 0.5 wt.% GNP, (c) 1.0 wt.% GNP (d) 1.5 wt.% GNP.

Fractography images of single-lap PLA–CF 3D printed adherends bonded with neat and graphene-modified adhesives (a) Neat, (b) 0.5 wt.% GNP, (c) 1.0 wt.% GNP (d) 1.5 wt.% GNP.

For the 1.0 wt.% GNP sample (PLACF 1.0), the failure surface was rough and irregular (Fig. 12c). The rough surface improved the bonding strength between the adhesive and adherend; meanwhile, at 1.5 wt.% GNP content (PLACF 1.5), multiple mechanisms, such as microcrack bridging, interfacial debonding cracks (Fig. 12d), and shear bands, were observed (Fig. 13a). The effective crack-arresting mechanism, in which GNP hinders crack propagation, forcing cracks to deviate along elongated paths. The formation of shear bands in the bond line further delayed catastrophic failure, thus enhancing the joint strength.Fig. 13Fractography images of single-lap PLA–CF 3D printed adherends bonded with graphene-modified adhesives (a) 1.5 wt.% GNP and (b) 2.0 wt.% GNP.

Fractography images of single-lap PLA–CF 3D printed adherends bonded with graphene-modified adhesives (a) 1.5 wt.% GNP and (b) 2.0 wt.% GNP.

However, when the GNP content increased to 2.0 wt.% (PLACF 2.0), agglomerates of GNP (Fig. 13b) appeared in the adhesive layer. These agglomerates acted as stress concentrators, creating weak spots in the bond line. Although GNP inherently provides reinforcement, its poor dispersion at higher concentrations weakens crack-bridging effectiveness and increases the risk of premature adhesive failure. Thereby, the fractography analysis revealed that incorporating GNP into the adhesive significantly altered the failure morphology of the SLJs. An optimal concentration (1.0 to 1.5 wt.%) formed rough surfaces and multiple toughening mechanisms, thereby enhancing load-bearing capacity and resistance to shear failure. Beyond this concentration, GNP presence reduced the overall strength of the SLJs.

Free vibrational analysis

PLA–CF adherends were bonded with a GNP-modified adhesive at different weight fractions to prepare SLJs, and their free-vibration response was measured through experimental modal analysis (Fig. 14a–d). The results demonstrate that natural frequency and damping values are influenced by the GNP content in the adhesive layer, with an optimum near 1.5 wt.% GNP content across all three bending modes, reflecting the microstructural changes observed in the FE-SEM analysis.Fig. 14Vibrational behaviour of short carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based lap joints, (a) Mode I, (b) Mode II, (c) Mode III (d) Modes of vibration vs Damping factor.

Vibrational behaviour of short carbon fibre reinforced PLA-based lap joints, (a) Mode I, (b) Mode II, (c) Mode III (d) Modes of vibration vs Damping factor.

For Mode I, the natural frequency increased from 21.202 Hz (neat adhesive, PLACF0) to 31.30 Hz at 0.5 wt.% GNP content (+ 47.6%), 36.985 Hz at 1.0 wt.% (+ 74.4%), and reached a maximum of 40.832 Hz at 1.5 wt.% GNP content (+ 92.6%), before decreasing to 32.130 Hz at 2.0 wt.% GNP content (+ 51.5% compared to baseline). The damping factor decreased steadily from 0.1702 (PLACF0) to 0.1068, 0.0987, 0.0422, and 0.03147. The increase in frequency and reduction in damping at 1.5 wt.% GNP content indicates a stiffer, less energy-dissipating material, which can be attributed to enhanced crack bridging, interfacial bonding, and shear band formation observed in the FE-SEM images. The slight decrease in frequency at 2.0 wt.% GNP content is due to GNP agglomeration and local microstructural non-uniformity, which also lowered shear and flexural properties of SLJ.

For Mode II, the frequency increased from 138.99 Hz (PLACF0) to 141.76 Hz at 0.5 wt.% GNP content (+ 2.0%), 147.52 Hz at 1.0 wt.% GNP content (+ 6.14%), reaching a maximum of 182.80 Hz at 1.5 wt.% GNP content (+ 31.5%), then decreased to 167.20 Hz at 2.0 wt.% GNP content (+ 20.3%). Damping decreased from 0.00856 (PLACF0) to 0.00860, 0.00542, 0.00286, and 0.00421. The significant increase in frequency and the reduction in damping at 1.5 wt.% GNP content indicates a more uniform stress transfer and lower energy dissipation, which correlates with improvements in the performance of SLJs during shear and flexural loading.

For Mode III, the baseline frequency of 451.51 Hz initially reduced to 418.20 Hz at 0.5 wt.% GNP content (–7.38%) due to local heterogeneities from partial GNP clustering, then recovered to 480.29 Hz at 1.0 wt.% GNP content (+ 6.37%) and reached 503.22 Hz at 1.5 wt.% GNP content (+ 11.45%). At 2.0 wt.% GNP content, the frequency slightly decreased to 459.31 Hz (+ 1.73%). Damping showed strong mode-dependent variation: 0.04084 (PLACF0) increased at 0.5 wt.% GNP content to 0.0615, then reduced to 0.00446 at 1.0 wt.% GNP content and remained low at 0.00486 at 1.5 wt.% GNP content, before slightly increasing to 0.02300 at 2.0 wt.% GNP content. The higher damping at 0.5 wt.% GNP content correlates with energy dissipation from local microstructural clusters, whereas homogenization at 1.0–1.5 wt.% GNP content improves shear strength and lowers damping, consistent with FE-SEM observations of rough, irregular fracture surfaces and enhanced crack deflection.

ANN analysis

ANNs are a category of machine learning algorithms designed to replicate cognitive functions by mimicking how the human brain processes information. ANNs consist of layers of interconnected nodes, also known as neurons. They provide a robust and adaptable method for forecasting the shear and vibrational properties of composite joints. These networks are trained to learn and perform tasks such as regression and classification by adjusting the weights and biases of individual neurons. Conclusion

This research examined the effect of adding GNP on the shear, flexural, and vibrational behaviour of PLA-CF-based SLJs. Furthermore, an ANN was used to predict SLJ failure modes, and the results were compared with experimental data. PLA-CF adherends for SLJs were fabricated using FDM, and, based on the bulk-specimen test, shear specimens were printed with a 0º raster orientation. GNP-modified adhesives with various weight percentages were prepared using ultrasound sonication. The results exhibited that GNP presence improved shear resistance and joint strength. Epoxy adhesive with 1.5 wt.% GNP content provided the maximum enhancement across all tests. Flexural testing showed a similar pattern, with the 1.5 wt.% GNP-reinforced SLJ exhibiting the highest flexural strength because of better interfacial bonding and enhanced cohesion between the adhesive and the PLA adherends. Failure surface analysis indicated that incorporating 1.5 wt.% GNP shifted the failure mode from adhesive to cohesive. Microstructural examination confirmed that graphene acted as a crack-arresting agent, altering crack paths and promoting the formation of shear bands, thereby delaying catastrophic failure and increasing the strength of the SLJs. Vibrational analysis demonstrated that increasing GNP content in the adhesive improved joint stiffness, particularly at 1.5 wt.% GNP content, where natural frequencies increased significantly in all three modes. Filler agglomeration at higher graphene levels limits wetting and reduces stress transfer across the bond line. The trained ANN model demonstrated high predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.99345), indicating excellent agreement between the experimental and predicted values. Overall, the findings show that adding GNP improved joint performance, and the ANN framework was reliable for predicting failure loads.

This study focused only on PLA-CF adherends with SLJ geometry and static loading. Future research can determine whether the optimal GNP content and raster orientation are also effective for other 3D-printed materials and different joint designs, such as scarf, wavy, joggle, and stepped configurations. The long-term durability under cyclic, hygrothermal, and environmental ageing conditions also requires assessment.
