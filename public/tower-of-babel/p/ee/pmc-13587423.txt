Risk prediction of sepsis-associated acute kidney injury: development, validation of a machine learning model with multicenter data
Authors: Renzhi Tan, Yongchen Li, Diman Mai, Jingwen Liu, Yushuang Wei, Chao Wang, Zengnan Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a common and severe complication in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, with high incidence and mortality. Conventional diagnostic indicators, such as serum creatinine and urine output, have limitations in early identification. Existing machine learning (ML)-based prediction models are mostly single-center and lack interpretability, highlighting the clinical need for reliable and applicable early prediction tools.MethodsData were collected from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV), eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), and Bobai County People’s Hospital ICU (BB-ICU). Eligible patients were those meeting the Sepsis-3.0 criteria, with SA-AKI as the primary outcome. Ten core variables were selected from 83 indicators measured within 24 h of ICU admission through a four-step screening process. Six ML algorithms were optimized using grid search and five-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The optimal model was interpreted via SHAP, externally validated, and translated into a web-based calculator using the Shiny framework.ResultsThe incidence of SA-AKI was 45.1% in MIMIC-IV, 28.1% in eICU-CRD, and 34.1% in BB-ICU. The XGBoost model performed best, with an internal area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72 and external validation AUCs of 0.82 (eICU-CRD) and 0.84 (BB-ICU). The XGBoost model also showed balanced classification metrics (sensitivity 0.73, specificity 0.60), a calibration curve close to the diagonal, and the highest net benefit in DCA. SHAP analysis identified partial thromboplastin time (PTT), creatinine, 24-hour urine output, and SAPSII as the key predictive factors.ConclusionsThe ML model developed in this study demonstrates good discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical applicability. SHAP improves the model’s interpretability, and the web-based tool supports early SA-AKI risk stratification. This study provides a technical basis for addressing the clinical challenge of early SA-AKI prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03620-z.

Introduction

Sepsis is a severe critical illness in the intensive care unit (ICU), defined as a systemic inflammatory response to infection that leads to organ dysfunction [1, 2]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most common organ failures in patients with sepsis [3]. Sepsis accounts for 45–70% of AKI cases in critically ill patients [4, 5]. AKI resulting from sepsis is referred to as sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) [6]. SA-AKI affects 46.6% of patients with sepsis and accounts for 15.8% of all ICU admissions [7]. The mortality rate of SA-AKI is approximately 25%, which is significantly higher than sepsis alone (11.2%) or AKI alone (13.5%) [8, 9]. Patients with SA-AKI often require prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer ICU stays, and have an increased risk of chronic kidney disease or long-term dialysis dependence [10, 11].

Current diagnosis of AKI mainly relies on serum creatinine and urine output, but both have limitations. In sepsis, reduced muscle perfusion may delay the increase in creatinine. Urine output can be affected by urinary obstruction and fluid status, making it an unreliable indicator for early renal impairment [8, 12]. These limitations often lead to delayed recognition of SA-AKI [13].

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has been increasingly applied in critical care research [14, 15], including early prediction of SA-AKI [16–19]. However, most existing models are limited by single-center design, insufficient external validation, and poor interpretability. The “black-box” nature of ML models remains a major barrier to clinical translation [20]. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) has been used to improve the interpretability of ML models [21].

To address the above gaps, this study aimed to: (1) identify key predictive features for SA-AKI; (2) develop and validate an ML-based risk prediction model for SA-AKI using multi-center data; (3) construct an interpretable and clinically usable tool for SA-AKI risk assessment.

Methods

Data source

Our study used data from three sources: the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1) database, the eICU Collaborative Research database (eICU-CRD, version 2.0), and Bobai County People’s Hospital. MIMIC-IV is a publicly accessible critical care repository containing de-identified clinical records of over 400,000 hospital admissions from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, spanning 2008 to 2022 [22]. Conversely, the eICU-CRD is a multicentric critical care database that includes detailed information on more than 200,000 patients scattered across 335 ICUs in 208 hospitals in the United States during 2014–2015 [23]. Both databases are freely available for critical care research. Renzhi Tan, the primary author, completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program and obtained formal authorization for access to both public databases through PhysioNet credentials (Record ID 66330355). Bobai County People’s Hospital is a tertiary hospital located in Bobai, Guangxi, China. We collected data from the 40-bed comprehensive ICU of Bobai County People’s Hospital (hereafter referred to as BB-ICU) between August 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bobai County People’s Hospital (Approval ID: 2024-KY-023). For machine learning analysis, the MIMIC-IV dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set at a 7:3 ratio. In contrast, the eICU-CRD and BB-ICU datasets were used as external validation sets to evaluate model generalizability. This study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement (Supplementary TRIPOD checklist) [24].

Study population and outcome

The study population was defined as patients meeting the Sepsis-3.0 criteria: confirmed or suspected infection plus a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 2 points [25].

Exclusion criteria were applied sequentially: (1) patients with a history of multiple hospitalizations or ICU admissions; (2) patients with an ICU stay duration of less than 48 h; (3) patients who were younger than 18 years old; (4) patients with malignant cancer; (5) patients with chronic kidney disease stage 4 or 5, or end-stage renal disease. The study outcome was the occurrence of SA-AKI (appearrence of AKI within 7 days of sepsis onset) [26]. Patients diagnosed with AKI using KDIGO criteria, which include either a serum creatinine (SCr) rise of ≥ 0.3 mg/dl (≥ 26.5 µmol/L) within 48 h, a ≥ 1.5 times increase in SCr from baseline (known or presumed to have occurred within the prior 7 days), or urine output < 0.5 ml/kg/h for 6 h [27].

Data extractions

Data extraction was performed using Navicat Premium (version 17) with Structured Query Language (SQL) queries. We focused exclusively on metrics obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission to ensure temporal consistency. Collected variables included: (1) demographics (age, gender, race); (2) laboratory examinations (creatinine, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], glucose, anion gap, electrolytes, coagulation markers, white blood cell count [WBC], platelet, red blood cell count [RBC], lactate); (3) vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure/diastolic blood pressure [SBP/DBP], 24‑hour urine output); (4) scoring systems (glasgow coma scale [GCS], sequential organ failure assessment [SOFA], charlson comorbidity index [CCI], simplified acute physiology score II [SAPSII]); (5) clinical therapies (vasopressors [epinephrine/norepinephrine], antibiotics [penicillins, cephalosporins, vancomycin, meropenem]; (6) comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure). Details regarding comorbidity definitions are shown in Table S1. In total, 83 variables were collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission.

To ensure data integrity, missing values were handled using two approaches: (1) continuous variables with a missing rate > 20% were excluded (Table S2), and (2) variables with a missing rate ≤ 20% were imputed using multiple imputation with the ‘mice’ package in R, a widely validated method for robust missing data management [28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v26.0, IBM Corp.) and R software (v4.3.3). Baseline characteristics were summarized as follows: continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and categorical variables as numbers and percentages (n, %). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. All predictive model performance indices were calculated together with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size calculation

We calculated the sample size based on the events per variable (EPV) principle [29, 30], a widely accepted standard for developing clinical prediction models. The incidence of SA-AKI in the training set was 45% (0.45). With 10 predictive variables and a recommended EPV value of 10 to avoid overfitting, the minimum required sample size was calculated as follows:

Model development and evaluation

The MIMIC‑IV dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set at a 7:3 ratio. The training set was used to construct six machine learning models, and the test set was used for model evaluation. The models included logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), radial support vector machine (RSVM), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM). Hyperparameter optimization was performed using grid search with five‑fold cross‑validation (Supplementary Hyperparameter Tuning Details).

Model performance was assessed in three domains: discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit. Discrimination metrics included accuracy, balanced accuracy, detection prevalence, sensitivity (SENS, this is also called Recall) and specificity (SPEC), the F1-Score (F meas), the positive predictive value (PPV, this is also called Precision), the negative predictive value (NPV), the Youden’s J Index (J index), the Cohen’s kappa (Kappa), the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and the area under the receiver-operating curve (ROC-AUC). Calibration was evaluated using calibration curves. Clinical net benefit was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). The optimal model was interpreted using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method to show the relative contribution of each feature to the predicted risk of SA‑AKI, and variable importance rankings were reported. To facilitate clinical use, a web‑based calculator was developed using the Shiny framework for SA‑AKI risk assessment. The overall analytical workflow is shown in Fig. 2.

Model development and comparison

We established six prediction models based on six machine learning algorithms, namely LR, DT, RF, XGBoost, RSVM and LightGBM. Variable importance results of other models are displayed in Figures S4–S7. All performance indicators with corresponding 95%CI in the test set are summarized in Table 2, and the overall comparison is visualized in Fig. 4. The XGBoost model yielded the highest AUC-ROC of 0.72 (95%CI: 0.68–0.76). It achieved balanced predictive performance with sensitivity of 0.72 (95%CI: 0.69–0.76) and specificity of 0.60 (95%CI: 0.56–0.63). Its PPV, NPV, Cohen’s kappa and MCC were 0.69 (95%CI: 0.65–0.72), 0.64 (95%CI: 0.61–0.67), 0.32 (95%CI: 0.28–0.36) and 0.32 (95%CI: 0.28–0.36), respectively. The ROC curve (Fig. 4A) for XGBoost was closest to the top‑left corner, indicating better discriminative ability. The calibration curve (Fig. 4B) showed that predicted probabilities from XGBoost agreed well with observed outcomes. In decision curve analysis (Fig. 4C), XGBoost provided a higher net benefit than other models across most threshold probabilities. Although LightGBM and RF also showed favorable performance, XGBoost performed best overall in discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility.

Optimal model interpretability

To interpret the XGBoost model, SHAP values were used to illustrate the contribution of each feature to the predicted risk of SA-AKI. Variable importance rankings and the SHAP summary plot are presented in Fig. 5. The four most influential variables were PTT, creatinine, 24-hour urine output, and SAPSII (Fig. 5A). The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 5B) shows the direction and magnitude of associations between each feature and predicted SA-AKI probability. Higher values of PTT, creatinine, SAPSII, magnesium, phosphate, BUN, sodium, platelet count, and lactate were associated with higher predicted risk. In contrast, higher 24-hour urine output was associated with lower predicted risk. Partial dependence plots (PDPs, Fig. 6) for the four key variables further illustrate these association trends. Increasing creatinine, increasing SAPSII, and decreasing 24-hour urine output were related to a higher predicted probability of SA-AKI. These plots depict the statistical relationships between features and model output, without implying causal effects or intervention thresholds.

Discussion

Key findings

This study developed and validated an XGBoost model for SA-AKI risk prediction using multicenter data (MIMIC‑IV, eICU, BB‑ICU), identifying 10 core predictors and demonstrating its stable performance across internal and external validation; SHAP analysis highlighted PTT, creatinine, 24‑hour urine output, and SAPSII as the most influential features, and a Shiny-based web calculator was constructed to support clinical translation with individualized interpretability.

Potential mechanisms

The key variables associated with SA‑AKI in this study—prolonged PTT, elevated creatinine, lower 24‑hour urine output, and higher SAPSII score—have been widely reported as clinically relevant indicators in patients with sepsis and acute kidney injury. PTT is a marker of coagulation function, and prolonged PTT has been associated with an increased risk of poor outcomes in critical illness and sepsis-related organ dysfunction [32, 33]. Abnormalities in coagulation pathways are commonly observed in sepsis [32], and previous studies have reported associations between prolonged clotting times and the development of acute kidney dysfunction [34]. As creatinine and 24‑hour urine output are components of the KDIGO criteria for AKI diagnosis, their strong association with SA‑AKI is consistent with clinical definitions and previous studies [26, 35]. These variables reflect changes in renal function and are routinely used to identify patients with evolving kidney impairment during sepsis [26]. SAPSII is a well‑established scoring system for assessing illness severity in critically ill patients [36, 37]. Higher SAPSII scores reflect more severe physiological disturbance and have been consistently linked to elevated risks of organ dysfunction and mortality in patients with sepsis [38, 39]. In our study, the association between SAPSII and SA‑AKI likely reflects the greater overall disease severity in patients who develop kidney injury. The inclusion of creatinine and 24‑hour urine output requires special clarification, as both are core components of the KDIGO diagnostic criteria for AKI and may raise concerns regarding circularity. In this study, these two variables were not incorporated as diagnostic markers for identifying established AKI, but as routine, readily available baseline indicators that are highly sensitive for anticipating subsequent renal deterioration during sepsis. By integrating creatinine and urine output with other physiological and laboratory variables, the model uses baseline values to predict future AKI risk, rather than merely confirming a current diagnosis. This design helps overcome the major limitation of delayed recognition inherent to conventional AKI diagnosis. For instance, clinicians often identify AKI only after creatinine has already risen, which reflects injury that occurred earlier. In contrast, our model leverages these baseline measurements to forecast impending AKI, enabling earlier risk stratification before overt diagnostic criteria are met. Thus, the model’s performance does not rely on diagnosing current AKI, but on predicting its future occurrence using early, routinely available data.

Implications of the study findings

This study offers three notable improvements for SA-AKI prediction: (1) In contrast to single-database studies such as Shi et al., which used only MIMIC-IV for SA-AKI prediction [40], the present study integrated three distinct datasets covering different ethnicities (Western and Asian populations), hospital types, and health care systems. MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD were consistent with the multicenter validation design of Li et al. [41], and we further included BB-ICU data from a county hospital in China, filling a gap in previous research that rarely included primary care settings in developing regions. (2) Variable selection strategies vary across published studies: Fan et al. used recursive feature elimination (RFE) to select 40 variables, while Gao et al. used ensemble stepwise selection to identify 11 variables [42, 43]. Our study used a more systematic “multi-step + multi-method integration” framework, including missing value filtering, univariate testing, multicollinearity assessment (VIF < 5), and seven feature selection methods (Forward, Backward, Stepwise regression, LASSO, Boruta, RF-MDA, and RF-MDG), followed by clinical expert consensus to determine the final 10 predictors. All variables were routine ICU indicators, avoiding non-standard biomarkers such as CCL14 or excessive feature numbers, thereby reducing barriers to clinical application [42, 44]. (3) For clinical translation, many previous models lacked accessible tools or separated prediction from interpretability. Yu et al. obtained a high-performance XGBoost model (AUC = 0.9357) with SHAP interpretation but did not develop a clinical application tool [45]. Wang et al. constructed a nomogram and web tool for mortality prediction but did not include SHAP-based explanations, limiting transparency for clinicians [46]. Our Shiny-based web calculator (http://zed99.shinyapps.io/shiny_shap_AKI/) supports real-time SA-AKI risk calculation and simultaneous SHAP visualization.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, as follows: (1) this study focused solely on predicting the early occurrence risk of SA-AKI, without investigating prognostic indicators related to SA-AKI, such as the degree of renal function recovery during hospitalization, long-term mortality after discharge, and the incidence of dialysis dependence. The lack of such prognostic analysis means the model cannot fully support comprehensive clinical decision-making from the acute phase to long-term follow-up. (2) the BB-ICU (a general ICU with 40 beds) included in this study had a relatively short data collection period (August 2024 to June 2025, approximately 11 months), resulting in a limited sample size (only 185 enrolled patients, including 63 SA-AKI cases). In future studies, it is necessary to extend the data collection period, expand the sample size, and include ICU data from more regions and different levels of hospitals, to further verify the model’s generalizability and improve its clinical credibility. (3) the inconsistent model performance across different cohorts. The XGBoost model only achieved a moderate AUC value of 0.716 in the test set from MIMIC-IV, while its discriminative performance was markedly improved in external validation datasets. Such performance discrepancy is largely attributable to inherent heterogeneity among included databases, including differences in patient demographic composition, overall disease severity distribution, varied SA-AKI prevalence, distinct admission inclusion criteria, regional clinical practice patterns and data collection time spans. These inter-cohort differences suggest that the generalizable predictive efficacy of the model still needs to be further verified in more diverse and well-balanced prospective cohorts. (4) the radial basis function support vector machine (RSVM) algorithm used in this study has inherent limitations in terms of interpretability. Due to the characteristics of the RBF kernel function, the model cannot directly quantify the contribution of each feature to the prediction result, which is different from tree-based models (such as RF and XGBoost) that can clearly reflect the importance of variables. This limits the further exploration of the internal relationship between each clinical indicator and SA-AKI.

Conclusions

This study used data from three distinct clinical datasets to screen out 10 core predictors and establish multiple machine learning models for SA-AKI risk assessment. The XGBoost model achieved favorable and stable discriminative performance across internal and external validation cohorts. Combined with SHAP analysis, this study clarified the contribution of each predictive variable, and a web-based prediction tool was further constructed. The above findings improve model interpretability and generalizability, and can facilitate early risk stratification of SA-AKI in clinical practice.
