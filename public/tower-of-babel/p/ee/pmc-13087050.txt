Data-driven explainable chronic kidney disease detection using RF based data imputation and meta-ensemble learning
Authors: Rupal Gupta, Shalini Gambhir, Ondrej Krejcar, Achin Jain, Arvind Panwar, Shakir Khan, Hamidreza Namazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive medical condition with significant public health impact, where early detection is critical for effective intervention. This work presents a clearly defined and structured data-driven framework designed to improve CKD prediction through robust preprocessing and optimized ensemble learning. This study proposes a novel hybrid framework that combines Random Forest (RF)-based imputation for missing value handling, categorical feature encoding, and synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) for addressing class imbalance, integrated with a Grey wolf optimizer (GWO)-based weighted ensemble of top-performing classifiers (Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, and Gaussian Naïve Bayes). The ensemble weights are optimized using the Grey Wolf optimizer (GWO) to enhance predictive accuracy. We evaluate the proposed framework on the UCI CKD dataset, demonstrating that it outperforms individual classifiers and conventional ensemble methods, achieving an accuracy of 98.75%, precision of 98.8%, recall of 98.6%, and F1-score of 98.7%. Additionally, explainable AI (XAI) techniques including SHAP and LIME are employed to analyze feature contributions, providing interpretable insights and confirming the clinical relevance of the predictions. Overall, the proposed framework offers a transparent, reliable, and computationally efficient clinical decision support model that bridges the gap between data-driven AI and nephrology practice.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Class imbalance, SMOTE, Random forest imputation, Ensemble learning, Grey wolf optimizer, Clinical decision support, UCI CKD datasetSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research, Nephrology

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a prevalent and progressively deteriorating medical condition that affects millions of people worldwide, significantly contributing to morbidity and mortality, particularly in developing countries1. Early and accurate diagnosis of CKD is critical for reducing complications, improving patient outcomes, and optimizing healthcare resources. However, challenges such as incomplete clinical data, imbalanced datasets, and limited generalization of traditional machine learning models often hinder the deployment of effective diagnostic systems. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning (ML), has emerged as a promising solution to improve the diagnostic accuracy of CKD through data-driven predictive modeling. Traditional ML models such as Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Trees (DT), and Support Vector Machines (SVM) have been applied to CKD datasets with varying degrees of success2. These models typically rely on handcrafted features and deterministic logic, which can limit their ability to generalize across patient populations and handle real-world clinical data anomalies such as missing or skewed values.

This study introduces a GWO-weighted ensemble framework that synergistically integrates multiple base machine learning classifiers with robust preprocessing techniques for CKD classification. Missing values in the data set are managed through random forest (RF) based imputation, while class imbalance is mitigated using the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE). The core ensemble model is formed by aggregating the predictions of the top performing classifiers through a weighted soft-voting scheme. These weights are not fixed but are dynamically optimized using the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), a bioinspired metaheuristic algorithm modeled on the leadership hierarchy and collaborative hunting behavior of grey wolves. GWO provides an effective strategy for continuous optimization, particularly in high-dimensional search spaces3. This hybrid pipeline offers several advantages. Firstly, RF-based imputation captures nonlinear feature dependencies better than statistical imputation methods, leading to more accurate data restoration. Secondly, SMOTE synthesizes new instances of the minority class to improve model training on imbalanced datasets. Lastly, the use of GWO for ensemble weight tuning enables optimal model fusion, enhancing the overall classification robustness and performance. This study makes the following major contributions:

Literature survey

Machine learning advances have substantially improved the predictive modeling of chronic conditions such as Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD). However, practical challenges such as missing data, class imbalance, and limited optimization of ensemble models still hinder real-world deployment. This section discusses previous work in the prediction of CKD using machine learning techniques, highlighting the gaps that the proposed GWO-based ensemble seeks to address. Traditional machine learning algorithms such as logistic regression, decision trees, support vector machines (SVM), and nave Bayes have been widely applied for CKD classification. Chhabra et al.4 developed an SMOTE-balanced stacking ensemble on the UCI CKD dataset, achieving 99.5% accuracy. However, their approach relied on internal cross-validation without external test validation and did not explore advanced imputation techniques.

Du et al.5 developed and externally validated a stacking ensemble model using six routine laboratory features of Chinese patients with CKD, achieving an AUC of 0.896 (internal) and 0.771 (external). However, their framework did not explore advanced imputation strategies. Vasanth and Balamurugan6 utilized Support Vector Machines combined with feature selection and kNN imputation, reporting accuracy 92. 8%. Despite improved feature selection, their study did not incorporate data balancing or model optimization techniques. More recent approaches have explored metaheuristics. Jamshed et al.7 proposed a Grey Wolf–optimized CNN for medical image analysis using pattern mining, showing its viability in biomedical classification tasks. However, this work focused on image frames rather than structured medical records and did not address missing data imputation. Arumuga Devi & Hepzibai8 employed a GWO-optimized CNN for hyperspectral imaging of diabetic foot ulcers, achieving 98.5% accuracy. However, their work focused purely on imaging data and did not tackle structured clinical records or missing value handling.

Khalid et al.9 proposed a hybrid ensemble model combining Gaussian Naïve Bayes, Gradient Boosting, Decision Tree, and Random Forest for CKD prediction on the UCI dataset, achieving up to 100% accuracy internally. However, their evaluation was limited to cross-validation and lacked external validation. Haque et al.10 compared CatBoost, RF, MLP, and Logistic Regression with optimized feature selection using nature-inspired algorithms, achieving 98.75% accuracy. However, their evaluation was restricted to internal test sets without external validation. Moreno-Sánchez11 proposed a voting-based ensemble tree model for the prediction of CKD, achieving 99.2% accuracy in cross-validation and 97.5% on external test data, although the study relied on only three characteristics and did not examine missing value techniques. Ganie et al.12 employed five boosting algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, AdaBoost, etc.) on the UCI CKD dataset, achieving up to 98.5% test accuracy with AdaBoost. Their work included pre-processing and hyperparameter tuning, but used only cross-validation without external validation.

Selvaraj et al.13 integrated multiswarm binary PSO for feature selection in kidney disease datasets and achieved 95% accuracy using SVM classifiers. However, the study focused solely on feature selection without addressing missing value handling or imbalance. Deep learning has emerged as a transformative approach in healthcare informatics, enabling high-accuracy predictions from complex medical data. Ravi et al.14 provided a comprehensive overview of deep learning applications in health diagnostics, highlighting its superiority in feature extraction and classification tasks in various clinical datasets. Their work highlights the potential of neural networks to uncover latent patterns in structured records such as those found in chronic disease datasets, offering an alternative to handcrafted feature engineering. Despite these advances, challenges such as class imbalance, missing values, and optimization of ensemble predictions remain insufficiently addressed in CKD-specific contexts.

Zhou et al.15. conducted an ensemble deep learning study with hybrid feature selection combining Boruta and recursive feature elimination followed by a DL classifier. This model improved accuracy by 2% over simple methods. Saif et al.16 proposed an ensemble deep learning framework that combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) and Bidirectional LSTM (BLSTM) architectures to predict CKD at 6-month and 12-month intervals. Their approach incorporated multiple optimizers such as Adamax and RMSprop to fine-tune performance and achieved a prediction accuracy of 98%. The model was evaluated on both the UCI CKD dataset and an optimized Kaggle dataset. In particular, the study addressed class imbalance and overfitting using dropout and early stopping mechanisms. This deep ensemble architecture demonstrated the potential for temporal and longitudinal prediction in CKD, extending the scope beyond static classification methods used in most previous studies. Finally, Sharma et al.17. carried out an extensive performance comparison of 12 ML classifiers, finding that the decision tree achieved a accuracy of almost 98.6%, providing a strong baseline for the ensemble models. Recent research has leveraged explainable machine learning models for the early prediction of chronic kidney disease (CKD), demonstrating that XGBoost and ensemble methods can achieve high accuracy while providing interpretability through SHAP and LIME analyses for individualized risk assessment18. Further, tri-phase ensemble and multi-layer stacking approaches have been shown to improve predictive performance on merged CKD datasets, with accuracies up to 99.5%19. Additionally, interpretable models for diabetic kidney disease (DKD) prediction highlight key clinical biomarkers influencing disease progression, enabling personalized patient care20. Numerous studies have explored chronic kidney disease (CKD) prediction using classical machine learning and deep learning approaches, often employing models such as SVM, Decision Trees, or Neural Networks. Although ensemble learning22 strategies such as stacking and boosting have demonstrated improved predictive capabilities, most existing works lack robust handling of missing clinical data and often overlook external validation. Moreover, although metaheuristic optimization algorithms like PSO, ACO, and ABC have been applied in general health analytics, their use to optimize the weights of the group for the classification of CKD remains relatively limited. In particular, very few studies integrate both imputation and imbalance handling along with optimization-based ensemble learning. Therefore, this study proposes a novel soft voting ensemble framework that incorporates the Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, and Gaussian Naïve Bayes classifiers, with ensemble weights optimized using the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO). To further improve the robustness of the classification, the model integrates random forest-based imputation and SMOTE, ensuring high reliability in the diagnosis of CKD from incomplete and imbalanced data sets.

Proposed methodology

The proposed framework, illustrated in Fig. 2, consists of data preprocessing, classifier training, and ensemble modeling. As described in Algorithm 1, the raw data set initially undergoes missing value identification and label encoding, followed by missing value imputation using a random forest regressor. This method retains feature correlations better than traditional techniques. The imputed data are then scaled and categorical variables are encoded. To mitigate class imbalance, SMOTE is applied within each cross-validation fold, resulting in a balanced training set. Multiple classifiers are trained including SVM, DT, LR, KNN, and GNB, and the top-3 performers are selected based on the validation accuracy. These top classifiers are then combined using a weighted voting ensemble37, where the optimal weights are determined by Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO), as shown in Algorithm 2. GWO emulates the hunting behavior and leadership hierarchy of grey wolves to search for the best weights that maximize validation accuracy. For fairness, the baseline ensemble weights are also derived using other optimizers, FPA, ABC,ACO, SLSQP and BO, with weights restricted to sum to 1. In all strategies, GWO consistently achieved superior performance, confirming its effectiveness. The integration of optimization-based ensemble model enhances the diversity and robustness of the models, leading to an improved classification of chronic kidney diseases. Results and discussion

This section presents the experimental evaluation of the proposed GWO-based weighted ensemble model for CKD classification. Performance is assessed through five-fold stratified cross-validation using standard classification metrics. All experiments were conducted in a Kaggle environment with Intel Xeon 2.2 GHz CPU, 16 GB RAM, CPU. The implementation was carried out using Python 3.10 with the following libraries: scikit-learn 1.3.2, imbalanced-learn 0.11.0, numpy 1.26, pandas 2.0, seaborn 0.12, matplotlib 3.8, shap 0.44, and lime 0.2.0. Random Forest-based imputation was implemented using RandomForestRegressor from scikit-learn. For handling class imbalance, SMOTE from the imbalanced-learn library was applied with default parameters. Model weights for the weighted-ensemble were optimized using the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO).. The code and environment specifications are available in the supplementary repository to ensure full reproducibility.The results of individual classifiers are benchmarked, followed by a detailed analysis of the optimized ensemble model.

The top three classifiers selected after evaluating the baseline ML classifiers were DT, LR, and GNB, chosen for their strong individual performance (Table 3). These classifiers were combined into a weighted ensemble, where the optimal contribution of each model was determined using the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO). The optimization process yielded final weights of 0.3267 (DT), 0.4574 (LR), and 0.2158 (GNB), resulting in a metaheuristic-driven ensemble that outperformed all individual classifiers. As per Table 4, the proposed GWO-based ensemble achieved the best performance across all metrics, with an accuracy of 0.9875, precision of 0.9900, recall of 0.9800, and F1-score of 0.9850, surpassing the top-performing base models (DT: accuracy 0.9675, F1-score 0.9579; LR: accuracy 0.9625, F1-score 0.9508). To validate the effectiveness of GWO in ensemble optimization, additional metaheuristic-based ensembles were constructed using SLSQP, Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), Flower Pollination Algorithm (FPA), Artificial Bee Colony (ABC), and Bayesian Optimization (BO). Although these approaches improved over individual classifiers (e.g. SLSQP and ACO ensembles achieved accuracy of 0.9825 and F1-score of 0.9800), none matched the superior balance of precision and recall attained by GWO. The GWO optimization required only 0.45 seconds of computation time on a standard CPU with negligible memory usage, and the ensemble inference latency was below 1 ms per sample. Given this low computational overhead, GWO’s complexity is well justified by the consistent and measurable improvement in classification performance. Ablation study

To evaluate the contribution of different components (Random Forest-based imputation and SMOTE-based class balancing), and to address potential concerns regarding data leakage, we conducted an ablation study using various combinations of missing value imputation and class balancing. In particular, imputation was applied in a controlled manner: missing values were imputed only within the training folds during 5-fold cross-validation, ensuring that no information from the test folds influenced the training process. This prevents data leakage and allows a fair assessment of model generalization. The ablation study also highlights the effect of class balancing using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) and different imputation strategies on classification performance. As shown in Table 7, scenarios without SMOTE show slightly higher overall accuracy due to preserving the original class distribution. However, SMOTE improves the model’s ability to correctly identify minority class instances, reflected in improved precision and recall for those classes. Notably, the proposed combination of full imputation and SMOTE achieves the best balanced performance across all metrics, indicating that proper handling of missing values and minority oversampling together enhances robustness and reliability. These results justify the use of full imputation and SMOTE in the proposed workflow. Overall, the ablation study demonstrates the incremental benefit of each preprocessing and balancing step, validating the design choices of the proposed ensemble framework.

Limitations

Although the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance, several limitations should be noted. The data set used in this study was relatively small (400 patient samples with 24 features), which limits the statistical power of the model and may not fully capture the broader clinical variability of CKD between diverse populations. The study focused only on binary classification (CKD vs. non-CKD) rather than multiclass staging of CKD progression, which is clinically relevant for the planning of treatment and monitoring disease severity. Additionally, the model training used default hyperparameters for the base classifiers and ensemble components, which may not be fully optimized for this data set; fine-tuning hyperparameters could potentially improve predictive performance. Finally, the study relied entirely on retrospective publicly available data without real-time clinical validation, limiting insights into deployment feasibility and system-level performance in real-world healthcare settings. Furthermore, the SHAP and LIME explainability analyses were not yet validated by nephrologists or clinical experts. Although these XAI visualizations provide interpretable insights into feature importance, thresholds such as “appet<= -0.53” arise from encoded and scaled representations. Mapping these back to human-readable categories requires domain expert validation to ensure correct and clinically meaningful interpretation. Future work will involve collaboration with nephrologists to verify and refine these interpretability outputs. Future research should address these limitations by using larger and more diverse datasets, extending the approach to multiclass CKD staging, performing hyperparameter optimization, and performing prospective clinical evaluations to enhance robustness and translational applicability.

XAI analysis

To ensure transparency and trust in medical predictions, Explainable AI (XAI) techniques are crucial in identifying the reasoning behind model decisions. In the context of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), XAI helps bridge the gap between complex machine learning models and clinical interpretability by revealing how various clinical features contribute to each prediction. This allows clinicians to understand why a patient is predicted as CKD positive or negative, supporting informed diagnostic and treatment decisions.

SHAP explanation for CKD

The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis in Fig. 9 provides comprehensive information on the importance of the features and the explanations of individual predictions. The feature importance plot (Fig. 9a) ranks predictors based on their impact on the model: albumin (al) has the highest influence, followed by red blood cell count (rbc) and red blood cell count in urine (rbcc). Clinically, this means that patients with abnormal albumin or RBC levels are more likely to be classified as CKD positive. Hypertension (htn), diabetes mellitus (dm), and serum creatinine (sc) also contribute significantly, while hemoglobin (hemo), specific gravity (sg), and appetite (appet) show moderate effects. The SHAP scatter plot (Fig. 9b) further illustrates patient-specific effects: red dots indicate high feature values pushing predictions toward CKD, while blue dots indicate low values favoring a negative prediction. For example, elevated albumin or abnormal RBC counts consistently increase the likelihood of CKD classification. This visualization allows clinicians to see both population-level trends and individual patient risk factors.

LIME explanation for CKD prediction

To complement global model interpretation, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) provide insights into how individual features influence specific patient predictions. This helps clinicians understand why the model made a specific diagnosis for a patient. Figure 10 demonstrates two contrasting cases: Sample 0 (Not CKD – borderline case): The model predicted “Not CKD” because key healthy indicators (good appetite, normal albumin, no pedal edema) outweighed several pathological features such as low specific gravity, hypertension, and anemia. Appetite was the most influential factor for this decision. Sample 50 (CKD – definitive case): The model predicted CKD with strong support from multiple pathological features, including hypertension, anemia, abnormal RBC count, elevated serum creatinine, and poor appetite. Hypertension had the largest contribution, indicating a high-risk patient profile. For categorical features, LIME thresholds (for example, “appet<= -0.53”) result from preprocessing steps such as label encoding and scaling. Clinicians can interpret these thresholds in terms of original categories, like “appetite = poor” or “appetite = good,” ensuring the explanations remain clinically meaningful.

Conclusion and future work

In this paper, a weighted ensemble learning framework based on GWO is proposed for the binary classification of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) using clinical characteristics. The model integrates the decision tree, logistic regression, and Gaussian naive Bayes classifiers into a soft vote ensemble, with weights dynamically optimized using the Grey Wolf Optimizer. The proposed model achieved outstanding performance with accuracy of 98.75%, F1 score of 98.5%, and AUC of 0.994 in 5-fold cross-validation. The results confirm that the integration of top-performing base classifiers with intelligent weight tuning significantly outperforms individual models and traditional ensembles. The framework also incorporates Random Forest-based imputation to manage missing clinical values and SMOTE to handle class imbalance, ensuring practical applicability in real-world clinical datasets. Furthermore, the low false negative rate and high recall underscore the diagnostic reliability of the set in critical healthcare settings. Future directions of this work include extending the framework with Explainable AI (XAI) techniques such as SHAP to improve the clinical interpretability of model decisions. Although the proposed framework demonstrates strong internal validation performance, the current study is limited to the publicly available UCI CKD dataset. External validation across multi-center datasets and diverse patient populations is essential to establish clinical reliability and generalizability. Future work will focus on testing the model with real-world clinical data obtained from multiple healthcare institutions to evaluate robustness under varying demographic and environmental conditions. Integration with real-time clinical decision support systems and testing on larger, multi-institutional CKD datasets could further validate its generalizability. In addition, applying this ensemble methodology to multiclass disease diagnosis or combined with deep learning-based feature extraction remains a promising avenue for future exploration.
