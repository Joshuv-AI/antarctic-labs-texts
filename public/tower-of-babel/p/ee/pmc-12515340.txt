Could the New Generation Mobile Base Stations Reduce the Nutritional Content of Primary Production Sector Products?
Authors: Sotirios Maipas, Nikolaos Kavantzas
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Mobile base station antennas emit radiation mainly in one direction, with the major portion of the radiation contained within a lobe. The determining factor for exposure is whether a living organism is inside the lobe or not. This means that the distance from the antenna is not the primary determining factor. However, the situation becomes more complex when multiple antennas are present, making it more challenging to identify radiation-free areas.

Readers should refer to the article by Nilsson and Hardell, 1 which presents a case study of a family experiencing the so-called “radiation syndrome” due to the presence of two 5G base stations directed toward their apartment. In addition to human exposure, numerous cases in the relevant literature describe negative effects on flora and fauna. Beyond the significant bioethical aspects, another important question arises: could such impacts on flora and fauna used in the primary sector reduce the quality of products such as vegetables, honey, and milk, thereby constituting an additional risk factor for human nutrition?

There is a large body of studies revealing negative impacts on plants (eg, growth, color), cows, bees, and other animals.2,3 It is worth noting that the recorded impacts also include milk production, whose quality is known to be affected by biological responses to environmental stressors. 4

It is well known that the primary sector is heavily affected by both climate change and the extensive use of agrochemicals. Another aggravating factor could potentially worsen the problem further and, given the continuous expansion of cellular networks, more research is required in this direction. If it is proven that there are areas of land in the primary sector where either a direct or indirect risk exists due to the presence of antenna base stations, several concerns arise regarding potential impacts on plant, animal, and human health.

Obviously, more relevant interdisciplinary research is needed, as every aspect of human nutrition is closely linked to the goals of achieving sustainability. With this letter, we aim to draw attention in this direction, considering both preventive and precautionary approaches, as it is likely that we are facing another emerging environmental health risk factor. Further research is required to clarify whether primary sector activities should be developed within the main lobes of radiation emitted by antenna base stations.
