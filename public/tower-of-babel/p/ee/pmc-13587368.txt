Establishment of a protoplast isolation and transient transformation system for tung tree (Vernicia fordii)
Authors: Jie Cao, Lichuan Xiao, Wenying Li, Zhangqi He, Mengyu Jia, Chengbo Yang, Junjie Chen, Yanli Li, Meilan Liu, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tung tree (Vernicia fordii) serves as an economically important woody oilseed plant. High-quality protoplasts are essential for applications such as somatic hybridization, studying cell signal transduction and rapid gene function analysis. A reliable protocol for protoplast isolation and transient gene expression was developed using petals, anther-derived callus, and root tips as source materials. Through systematic optimization of key parameters such as enzyme combinations, osmotic pressure, digestion duration, and centrifugation speed, we successfully established tissue-specific isolation protocols. To ensure the availability of widely applicable experimental materials, protoplasts isolated from anther-derived callus were used to optimize transient transformation conditions. Under the optimized parameters (20 µg plasmid, 40% PEG4000, and 30-min incubation), the transformation efficiency reached 44.91%. Using this system, we conducted multiple experimental validations, including subcellular localization of VfWOX1.2 protein, detection of its protein expression, analysis of its transcriptional regulation of phytohormone biosynthetic genes, and assessment of stress-responsive gene expression under various treatment conditions. Collectively, these results confirm the utility of this platform for functional gene studies in tung tree. This study establishes a robust technical foundation, which is helpful for overcoming the limitation posed by the absence of a stable genetic transformation system and will facilitate future research on gene function in woody species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13007-026-01553-2.

Introduction

The tung tree (Vernicia fordii), a perennial species of the Euphorbiaceae family that seasonally sheds its foliage, constitutes a valuable woody oil crop domesticated in China over thousands of years [1]. As one of the three principal species in the Vernicia genus, it serves as an important source of tung oil, a high-quality drying oil which is widely used for developing eco-friendly coatings and biodiesel [2–5]. With the progress in genome sequencing, numerous putative functional genes have been identified in tung tree. However, characterizing their function still primarily relies on heterologous expression systems, such as tobacco and Arabidopsis. Such systems may yield unstable or non-physiological results due to differences in genetic background and regulatory contexts, thereby limiting their predictive value. Although Agrobacterium-mediated transformation systems have been developed using seedling and callus explants [6, 7], these methods are time-consuming and low-throughput, constraining high-resolution functional studies and impeding molecular breeding progress. Consequently, developing a straightforward, rapid, and efficient experimental platform for gene functional analysis in tung tree is urgently needed.

Plant protoplasts are defined as living cells from which the cell wall has been removed while preserving the plasma membrane and intracellular organization [8]. This unique property makes them a versatile and physiologically relevant system for diverse applications in plant biology. These include tissue culture [9], cellular genetic engineering [10], and molecular biological studies [11]. Robust systems for protoplast isolation are well established across a range of species, including A. thaliana [12], rice [13], orchid [14], poplar [15], ginkgo [16], and citrus [17]. Among the various isolation methods, enzymatic digestion is the most widely adopted, with its efficiency critically dependent on factors including enzyme composition, osmotic pressure, digestion duration, and pre-treatment conditions [16, 18]. Notably, optimal isolation parameters often vary among tissues within the same species. For instance, in Camellia oleifera, distinct protocols have been developed for callus, leaves, and petals, underscoring the need for tissue-specific optimization in protoplast isolation [19–21].

Transient transformation of protoplasts enables rapid and efficient introduction of exogenous DNA, facilitating high-level expression of target genes within a short timeframe. Commonly used transformation methods include Agrobacterium-mediated delivery [22], polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transfection [21], electroporation [23], and biolistic transformation [24]. In comparison to other techniques, PEG-mediated transformation stands out for its advantages including simplicity, high efficiency, low cost, minimal cellular damage, and scalability, all of which can be achieved without the need for specialized equipment. This approach has been widely applied in studies of protein localization dynamics [25], binary protein interactions [26], transcriptional regulation [27], and multi-omics analyses [28]. While robust protoplast transient expression systems are well established in herbaceous and gramineous species such as Arabidopsis, rice, tomato, and sugarcane [12, 20, 29, 30], research on woody plant protoplasts has progressed more slowly.

The WUSCHEL-related homeobox (WOX) transcription factor belongs to a unique plant-specific class of key regulators governing a diverse array of developmental processes, most notably stem cell maintenance, organogenesis, and embryogenesis [31]. Beyond their well-established roles in development, emerging evidence suggests that WOX proteins are also intricately involved in mediating plant responses to various environmental stimuli, positioning them at the nexus of growth and environmental adaptation [32]. However, the molecular mechanisms underpinning these functions, particularly their roles in orchestrating phytohormone crosstalk and stress signaling, remain incompletely understood, especially in woody perennials. The WOX family is classified into three evolutionarily conserved lineages: the ancient, intermediate, and modern/WUS clades. Members of the modern clade, such as AtWOX6, have been implicated in regulating seed and fruit development, often through the intricate modulation of hormonal pathways [33]. Auxin and cytokinin, two central phytohormones, act as pivotal signaling molecules that coordinate cell division, differentiation, and patterning. Several WOX members have been demonstrated to interact with these hormonal pathways, yet whether they directly influence the transcription of biosynthetic genes to fine-tune hormone homeostasis is a subject of active investigation.

To overcome the technical limitations of stable transformation in woody plants, we developed a versatile transient gene expression platform in tung tree utilizing protoplasts. Using VfWOX1.2, a member of the WOX transcription factor family, we systematically validated the reliability and utility of this system through multiple approaches: subcellular localization analysis, examination of its regulatory roles in auxin and cytokinin biosynthesis, and characterization of its response to various stress conditions. This established platform provides a robust technical foundation for cutting-edge research in tung tree, including gene functional analysis, subcellular localization, single-cell omics, spatial multi-omics, and somatic genetic manipulation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Plant materials used for protoplast isolation included 8-year-old ‘Putao tung’ trees and 1-year-old ‘Putao tung’ seedlings. The mature trees were grown in the National Tung Tree Germplasm Conservation Repository, Yongshun County, Hunan Province. Petals and anther-derived callus were collected from these trees, while root tips were harvested from the seedlings. Protoplasts were isolated and purified from these three tissues: petals, anther-derived callus, and root tips (Fig. 1). For callus induction, anthers were inoculated on 1/2 MS containing 0.5 mg/L 6-BA, 0.1 mg/L NAA, and 1.0 mg/L 2,4-D. Induced callus was then proliferated on 1/2 MS medium containing 0.5 mg/L KT, 0.5 mg/L NAA, and 1.0 mg/L 2,4-D. All cultures were maintained in a light-controlled culture room at 26 ± 1 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod.

Protoplast isolation

Protoplast isolation was performed at room temperature using fresh petals, anther-derived callus, and root tips of tung tree. The materials were subjected to an initial rinse in sterile water to eliminate surface contaminants, then sectioned into approximately 1 mm filaments or 1 mm² fragments using a sterile double-sided blade in a petri dish. After washing, the tissues were incubated in a pre-plasmolysis solution consisting of CPW (Coolaber, China), 0.1% BSA, and D-mannitol, and placed on a shaker at 26 °C and 65 rpm in the dark for 10 min. Following pre-plasmolysis, the solution was discarded and substituted with 10 mL of enzyme solution. The samples were exposed to vacuum infiltration for 10 min before enzymatic digestion. The enzyme mixture contained Cellulase R-10 (Yakult, Japan), Macerozyme R-10 (Yakult, Japan), Snailase (Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), D-mannitol, 15 mM MES, 10 mM CaCl₂, 15 mM KCl, 2 mM NaH₂PO₄, 0.1% BSA, and 0.5% PVP K30. Digestion was performed in the dark at 26 °C with agitation at 65 rpm (Fig. 2). To optimize protoplast isolation, several parameters were systematically tested: enzyme concentrations (1.0%–3.0% Cellulase R-10, 0.5%–1.5% Macerozyme R-10, 0%–1.0% Snailase), osmotic pressure (0.3–0.8 M D-mannitol), digestion duration (1–8 h), and vacuum pressure levels (0, − 0.03, − 0.05, − 0.07, − 0.09 MPa).

Protoplast purification

Following enzymatic digestion, protoplasts were harvested and purified through a series of steps including filtration, centrifugation, and buffer exchange. Prior to purification, a 40 μm cell sieve was pre-rinsed with 1 mL of W5 solution (2 mM MES, 125 mM CaCl₂, 5 mM KCl, 154 mM NaCl, 400 mM D-mannitol), and 10 mL tubes were pre-cooled on ice. The enzymatic hydrolysate was passed through the sieve with a wide-orifice pipette tip. The filtered protoplast suspension was transferred into the pre-chilled tube and centrifuged directly via horizontal centrifugation at 100×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was then carefully aspirated. The protoplasts were resuspended and washed twice with 2 mL of W5 solution, followed by incubation on ice for 20 min. To optimize the purification process, the effect of centrifugation speed (300, 500, 700, and 900 rpm) on protoplast yield and integrity was evaluated.

Protoplast yield and vitality calculation

Protoplast quantification was performed using a hemocytometer under a light microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan). Fluorescein diacetate (FDA) staining was employed to assess protoplast viability. Briefly, the protoplast suspension was mixed with 0.01% FDA and cultured in darkness for 8 min. Protoplasts were examined using a Leica DMi8 inverted fluorescent microscope (Leica, Germany). Viable protoplasts were identified by the presence of green fluorescence under fluorescent illumination. Transient transformation of protoplasts

PEG-mediated transformation was employed for protoplasts with modifications to the calcium protocol. The PEG-calcium solution was freshly prepared on the day of transformation. Purified protoplasts were resuspended in MMG solution (4 mM MES, 15 mM MgCl₂, 400 mM D-mannitol) at a density of 5–10 × 10⁵ cells/mL. A mixture containing 100 µL of protoplast, 20 µg of plasmid DNA, and 120 µL of PEG-calcium solution (40% PEG4000, 200 mM CaCl₂, 200 mM D-mannitol) was gently pipetted using a wide-bore tip in a 2 mL sterile conical tube, then dark-incubated at 26 °C for 30 min. Thereafter, 800 µL of W5 solution was added to terminate the transformation, and the mixture was then centrifuged at 500 rpm. After removing the supernatant, the protoplasts were gently resuspended in 450 µL of W5 solution and then dark-cultured at 26 °C for 10–16 h to enable subsequent analysis of transformation efficiency or subcellular localization.

To optimize transformation conditions, several parameters were evaluated: plasmid amount (5, 10, 15, 20 µg), PEG4000 concentration (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%), and transformation duration (10, 20, 30, 40 min). After 16 h of incubation, protoplasts were collected, and a 50 µL aliquot was transferred to a confocal dish for confocal imaging using a Leica TCS SP8 microscope (Leica, Germany). Successfully transformed protoplasts were identified based on green fluorescence observed in the corresponding channel (Fig. 2). Subcellular localization

To construct the VfWOX1.2-GFP fusion, the VfWOX1.2 CDS was PCR-amplified from cDNA and assembled into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector using a commercial DNA assembly system (Yugong Biotech, China). The VfWOX1.2 sequence was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (accession: PP883543.1, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/PP883543.1). The primers for VfWOX1.2 are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The resulting recombinant plasmid, 35 S::GFP-VfWOX1.2, was transiently expressed in tung tree protoplasts, and the subcellular localization of GFP fluorescence was visualized by confocal microscopy (Leica TCS SP8, Germany). For comparative analysis, the same construct was transiently expressed in tobacco leaves, and GFP signals were similarly examined to confirm localization patterns.

Abiotic stress treatments

To investigate the stress response, protoplasts expressing 35 S::GFP-VfWOX1.2 were exposed to temperature stresses: cold stress at 0 °C and heat stress at 37 °C for 2 h. Control protoplasts were kept at 25 °C. After treatment, protoplasts were immediately harvested for total RNA extraction and subsequent qRT-PCR.

Analysis of gene expression using qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from transfected protoplasts with a Plant RNA Extraction Kit and digested with DNase I to remove contaminating gDNA. First-strand cDNA was reverse-transcribed using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit. qRT-PCR was performed using a Bio-Rad CFX96 system and SYBR Green Premix. The tung tree Actin gene served as the reference gene for data normalization. Gene-specific primers were designed to amplify VfWOX1.2, along with genes involved in phytohormone biosynthesis and stress responses. For cytokinin biosynthesis, the genes included VfCYP735A1.1 (Cytochrome P450 family 735 subfamily A polypeptide 1.1), VfIPT2 (Isopentenyltransferase 2), VfIPT9, and VfLOG3 (Lonely Guy 3). For auxin biosynthesis, the genes included VfTAA1 (Tryptophan Aminotransferase Related 1), VfTAA4, VfYUC3 (YUCCA 3), VfYUC4, VfYUC6, and VfCYP79B2 (Cytochrome P450 family 79 subfamily B polypeptide 2). For stress-responsive genes, the genes included VfCOLD1 (Chilling-Sensitive in Light and Dark 1), VfCBF2 (C-Repeat Binding Factor 2), VfSOD (Superoxide Dismutase), VfAPX1 (Ascorbate Peroxidase 1), VfHSFA1 (Heat Stress Transcription Factor A-1), and VfHSP101 (Heat Shock Protein 101). The gene sequences were derived from transcriptomic data generated by our research group. The raw transcriptomic data have been deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database of NCBI under the accession numbers SRX3843588, SRS3089151, SRS3089154, SRS3089148, SRS3089147, and SRS3089150. The genes used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Relative expression levels were determined using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with three technical replicates for each reaction.

Data analysis

Values are shown as the mean of three independent replicates. Statistical significance was assessed using SPSS 26.0. Differences among treatments were evaluated by one-way ANOVA coupled with Duncan’s multiple range test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Evaluation of enzyme combinations for protoplast isolation across three tung tree tissues

Petals, anther-derived callus, and root tips, which are composed of thin-walled and actively dividing cells, were used as experimental materials. Through systematic optimization of key parameters, isolation protocols adapted to the specific characteristics of different tissues were established. The composition and concentration of enzyme solutions critically influenced protoplast isolation efficiency. Using an orthogonal experimental design with nine enzyme combinations, we found that all treatments yielded viable protoplasts from petal, anther-derived callus, and root tip tissues, though significant variations in yield and viability were observed among treatments. Range analysis revealed that Cellulase R-10 exerted the strongest effect on both yield and viability in petal and root tip protoplasts, whereas Macerozyme R-10 and Snailase showed minimal influence in these tissues. For callus-derived protoplasts, Macerozyme R-10 exerted the strongest influence on yield, while Cellulase R-10 exerted the strongest influence on viability, respectively; Snailase showed the least effect. Overall, Cellulase R-10 was identified as the key factor affecting protoplast isolation across all three tissue types.

By comparing isolation outcomes across enzyme combinations, the optimal formulations for each tissue were determined. For petals, maximal yield (7.51 × 10⁶ protoplasts/g FW) and viability (84.59%) were achieved using 2.0% Cellulase R-10, 1.0% Macerozyme R-10, and 0.5% Snailase (Table 1). For anther-derived callus, the optimal combination was 1.0% Cellulase R-10, 0.75% Macerozyme R-10, and 0.4% Snailase, yielding 6.29 × 10⁶ protoplasts/g FW with 78.21% viability (Table 2). For root tips, the best results (2.95 × 10⁶ protoplasts/g FW; 77.42% viability) were obtained with 3.0% Cellulase R-10, 0.5% Macerozyme R-10, and 0.5% Snailase (Table 3). In all cases, these optimal combinations performed significantly better than other treatments.

Optimization of osmotic pressure for protoplast isolation across three tung tree tissues

Appropriate osmotic pressure is essential for maintaining protoplast integrity and physiological activity. As shown in Fig. 3, the most effective osmotic condition for the isolation of petal protoplasts was 0.5 M, resulting in a yield of 11.99 × 10⁶ protoplasts per gram fresh weight (g·FW) and 84.65% viability. Root tips also exhibited the highest protoplast yield and viability at 0.5 M (Fig. 3a–b), reaching 3.6 × 10⁶ protoplasts/g·FW and 83.46% viability. In contrast, anther-derived callus protoplasts achieved optimal results at 0.6 M, producing 9.76 × 10⁶ protoplasts/g·FW and 86.65% viability (Fig. 3c). Across all three tissue types, protoplast yield and viability displayed a bell-shaped response to increasing osmotic pressure, rising to a maximum before declining. Both excessively low and high osmotic pressures adversely affected protoplast survival, with the best structural and functional preservation observed under isotonic or slightly hypertonic conditions (Fig. 3d–f). It is worth noting that root tip-derived protoplasts exhibited considerable heterogeneity in size (Fig. 3e). This phenomenon is closely related to the cellular composition of root tip tissues: the root tip region comprises the meristematic zone, elongation zone, and root cap, with cells at different developmental stages that vary significantly in volume, degree of vacuolization, and cell wall thickness. Consequently, following enzymatic digestion, the population of root tip-derived protoplasts displays a broad distribution in diameter, reflecting the intrinsic cellular heterogeneity of this tissue.

Optimization of enzymolysis time for efficient protoplast isolation across tung tree tissues

To determine the optimal enzymatic duration for protoplast isolation from three distinct tissues of the tung tree, including petals, anther-derived callus, and root tips, we continuously monitored the samples over an 8-h period. The results demonstrated that digestion time was a critical determinant of both protoplast yield and viability. Protoplast yield increased progressively with extended digestion time. The highest yield from petals was achieved after 5 h of digestion, whereas yields from anther-derived callus and root tips peaked at 6 h. Beyond these time points, further digestion resulted in a substantial decline in both protoplast yield and viability. Accordingly, optimal enzymatic digestion time was established as 5 h for petals (Fig. 4a) and 6 h for anther-derived callus and root tips (Fig. 4b–c).

Vacuum infiltration enhances protoplast isolation in three tung tree tissues

Based on the established optimal conditions for enzyme composition, osmotic pressure, and digestion time, we further evaluated the effect of vacuum infiltration on protoplast isolation efficiency. Results demonstrated that vacuum treatment significantly improved protoplast release compared with untreated samples. Under the same treatment duration, protoplast yield increased with the degree of negative pressure. A vacuum of − 0.03 MPa effectively enhanced isolation efficiency in petals and anther-derived callus, yielding maximum protoplast densities of 14.43 × 10⁶/g FW and 14.23 × 10⁶/g FW, respectively (Fig. 4d–e). In contrast, root tip protoplasts reached a maximum yield of 6.5 × 10⁶/g FW at − 0.05 MPa (Fig. 4f). Beyond these optimal pressures, further increases in negative pressure reduced isolation efficiency and resulted in a higher proportion of ruptured and deformed protoplasts. Thus, the optimal vacuum conditions were determined to be − 0.03 MPa for petals and anther-derived callus, and − 0.05 MPa for root tips. During enzymatic digestion, notable differences in protoplast size were observed among the three tissue types. Statistical analysis revealed mean diameters of 43.58 ± 8.10 μm for petal protoplasts, 29.01 ± 6.95 μm for anther-derived callus protoplasts, and 13.47 ± 4.25 μm for root tip protoplasts (Fig. 4g–j).

Centrifugation speed shapes the purification efficiency of protoplasts in a tissue-specific manner in tung tree

Centrifugation speed significantly influenced protoplast purification outcomes. Both insufficient and excessive speeds adversely affected yield and purity. At low speeds, protoplast recovery was inefficient, whereas high speeds co-pelleted excessive cellular debris and induced protoplast deformation and rupture. Optimal purification was achieved at 500 rpm for protoplasts from petals and anther-derived callus (Fig. 5a, b), and at 700 rpm for those from root tips (Fig. 5c). The corresponding viabilities were 87.43% for petals, 87.48% for callus, and 85.92% for root tips (Fig. 5d-i).

Establishment of a robust and rapid transformation system for tung tree protoplasts

In this study, protoplast isolation protocols were successfully established for petals, anther-derived callus, and root tips. Further investigation revealed that the methods for transient gene transformation of the three types of protoplasts were largely consistent, with only minor differences in the centrifugation speed used to collect protoplasts, and this centrifugation speed was the same as that used in the respective protoplast isolation methods. Considering that anther-derived callus can be continuously maintained through subculture, providing a stable and sufficient material supply under sterile conditions, which makes it more suitable for transient expression experiments requiring multiple replicates and strictly controlled conditions, we selected anther-derived callus as the representative material. The pGreenII 62-SK-GFP vector was used as the plasmid for transient transformation of protoplasts derived from anther-derived callus of tung tree. Transformation efficiency was evaluated as a function of plasmid amount, PEG4000 concentration, and incubation time. Increasing plasmid dosage from 5 to 20 µg resulted in a corresponding rise in transformation efficiency, which peaked at 12.44% with 20 µg of plasmid (Fig. 6a). This value was thus selected as the optimal plasmid amount. We next examined the effect of PEG4000 concentration, a critical reagent in protoplast transfection. Efficiency was positively correlated with PEG4000 level up to 40%, reaching a maximum of 21.61%, beyond which it declined noticeably. Concentrations ≥ 60% led to marked protoplast deformation and rupture (Fig. 6b). Consequently, 40% PEG4000 was established as optimal. Transformation duration also significantly influenced outcomes. Short incubation times likely limited plasmid uptake, whereas prolonged exposure to PEG4000 caused protoplast damage. Efficiency increased with time up to 30 min, peaking at 44.91%, but fell sharply to 6.31% after 40 min (Fig. 6c). The comprehensive optimization results show that a plasmid amount of 20 µg, PEG4000 concentration of 40%, and incubation time of 30 min jointly achieved a peak transformation efficiency of 44.91%, thus confirming 30 min as the optimal incubation duration (Fig. 6d). Fluorescence microscopy confirmed successful transformation: untransformed protoplasts showed no signal upon 488 nm excitation (Fig. 6e), while transformed protoplasts exhibited clear green fluorescence (Fig. 6f).

Determination of the subcellular localization of VfWOX1.2 protein in tung tree

VfWOX1.2 is a member of the WOX transcription factor family. WOX genes are known to be involved in plant responses to environmental stress in aerial parts and to regulate the biosynthesis of auxin and cytokinin, thereby influencing the morphogenesis of metabolically active meristems [31–33]. Therefore, VfWOX1.2 was selected as a representative gene for overexpression in anther callus-derived protoplasts to validate the applicability of the established protoplast transient transformation system for gene function studies in tung tree. The VfWOX1.2-GFP protein was transiently expressed in tung tree anther-derived callus protoplasts under the established optimal conditions. Confocal microscopy revealed strong GFP fluorescence exclusively in the nucleus (Fig. 7a1-a3). This nuclear localization was consistent with the pattern observed in a heterologous system of Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Fig. 7b1-b3), thereby confirming the reliability of the anther-derived callus protoplast transformation platform and supporting the predicted nuclear function of VfWOX1.2 protein.

Overexpression of VfWOX1.2 regulates genes involved in the biosynthesis of auxin and cytokinin

To examine the involvement of VfWOX1.2 in the regulation of auxin and cytokinin biosynthesis, we leveraged its overexpression in tung tree callus protoplasts. It was found that the transcriptional level of VfWOX1.2 was markedly up-regulated by 196.25-fold in the overexpression group compared to the WT (Fig. 8a, b). Detection of the VfWOX1.2 protein verified the efficacy of the overexpression system. Among the genes related to cytokinin biosynthesis, the expression of VfCYP735A1.1 showed no significant difference between the overexpression group and WT (Fig. 8c). In contrast, the expression levels of VfIPT2 and VfIPT9 were upregulated by 2.98-fold (Fig. 8d) and 1.35-fold (Fig. 8e), respectively, and VfLOG3 expression was increased by 12.22-fold (Fig. 8f). In the auxin biosynthesis pathway, the expression levels of VfTAA1 and VfTAA4 were downregulated by 1.32-fold (Fig. 8g) and 11.93-fold (Fig. 8h), respectively. On the other hand, VfYUC3, VfYUC4, and VfYUC6 were upregulated by 1.25-fold (Fig. 8i), 3.34-fold (Fig. 8j), and 4.28-fold (Fig. 8k), respectively. A dramatic 35.87-fold reduction was observed in the expression level of VfCYP79B2 compared to the WT (Fig. 8l).

Effects of cold and heat stress on the expression of stress-resistant genes in VfWOX1.2-overexpressing protoplasts from anther-derived callus of tung tree

To examine the expression profiling of stress-resistant genes, we overexpressed VfWOX1.2 in protoplasts from anther-derived callus of tung tree and subjected them to cold (0 °C) and heat (37 °C) stress. Compared to the untreated controls, VfWOX1.2 expression in the overexpressing protoplasts decreased by 3.89-fold (Fig. 9a) under cold stress but increased by 2.50-fold under heat stress. Under cold stress conditions, the expression levels of VfCOLD1, VfCBF2, and VfSOD were reduced by 1.71-fold (Fig. 9b), 5.90-fold (Fig. 9c), and 3.94-fold (Fig. 9e), respectively, while VfAPX1 expression was upregulated by 1.43-fold (Fig. 9d). Following heat stress, the expression of VfHSFA1 and VfSOD decreased by 6.28-fold (Fig. 9f) and 7.25-fold (Fig. 9i), respectively. In contrast, the expression of VfHSP101 and VfAPX1 increased markedly by 4.76-fold (Fig. 9g) and 16.15-fold (Fig. 9h).

Discussion

Tung tree, a valuable woody species renowned for its industrial oil production, is widely cultivated across southern China [34]. With the growing global emphasis on forestry-based bioenergy and the continual expansion of tung tree applications, its potential for comprehensive utilization appears vast. However, progress in functional genomics and molecular breeding of tung tree has been significantly hampered due to a lack of streamlined genetic manipulation tools. To address this bottleneck, we turned to protoplasts, a versatile and widely adopted cell-based research platform known for their utility in convenient transient transformation assays. Here, we report the establishment of an efficient platform for isolating and transforming tung tree protoplasts. This system serves as a key platform for functional genomics studies in woody plants and paves the way for rapid analysis of gene function.

Our systematic optimization revealed that the efficiency of protoplast isolation is a complex trait influenced by a delicate balance of enzymatic composition, osmotic pressure, digestion time, and physical facilitation methods. The orthogonal experimental design effectively identified tissue-specific optimal conditions. Cellulase R-10 exhibits a predominant influence in the isolation of protoplasts from all three tissue types, namely petals, callus, and root tips, indicating that the hydrolysis of cellulose microfibrils plays a critical role in releasing protoplasts from the plant cell wall. During the enzymatic digestion of three tung tree tissues, the protoplast viability of anther callus and petal-derived materials showed a continuous decline with increasing digestion time, whereas the protoplast viability of root tip-derived materials exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Cellulase R-10 is a key enzyme for degrading cellulose in plant cell walls [35, 36]. Under the same digestion time, root tissues required a significantly higher concentration of Cellulase R-10 than anther callus and petals, indicating that the cell walls of roots are thicker than those of the latter two. Furthermore, during root elongation and nutrient uptake in soil, roots must effectively prevent cell damage, and therefore their tissue cells possess thicker cell walls and higher metabolic activity [37]. Based on these structural characteristics, during the initial stage of enzymatic digestion, root tip tissues undergo slow cell wall degradation, with protoplasts being gradually released and viability increasing accordingly. However, as digestion time prolongs, the cell wall becomes completely degraded, causing the cell membrane to lose its physical support. Coupled with prolonged exposure to a hypertonic environment, this leads to membrane damage and osmotic imbalance, resulting in a subsequent decline in viability. In contrast, anther callus and petals have thinner cell walls and more fragile cellular structures. Their cell walls are rapidly degraded at the early stage of digestion, exposing protoplasts prematurely to adverse conditions, which leads to a continuous decline in viability from the very beginning. Therefore, differences in cell wall structure, physiological status, and stress tolerance among tissues are the fundamental reasons for the distinct patterns of protoplast viability change. The distinct optimal enzyme cocktails and osmotic pressures for each tissue type likely reflect fundamental differences in their cell wall architecture and composition, a well-documented phenomenon across plant tissues and species [14, 38, 39]. Furthermore, the efficacy of mild vacuum infiltration in significantly enhancing protoplast yield highlights the importance of enzyme accessibility [40], a factor particularly beneficial for tissues with more complex intercellular structures.

The development of an efficient transformation protocol is equally critical. The optimized parameters of 20 µg plasmid, 40% PEG4000, and a 30-min incubation time culminated in a remarkably high transformation efficiency of 44.91% for anther-derived callus protoplasts. This efficiency is notably superior to many reported systems in other wood plants [41, 42], positioning our protocol as a valuable asset for dissecting gene function. The robustness of this platform was definitively validated through the subcellular localization of VfWOX1.2. The consistent nuclear localization of VfWOX1.2-GFP in both tung tree protoplasts and N. benthamiana leaves confirms the fidelity of protein targeting within the homologous system. This result, aligning with the nuclear localization of its Arabidopsis homolog AtWOX6 [16], robustly validates the anther-derived callus protoplast system as a trustworthy platform for studying protein localization and, by extension, other transient assays.

Leveraging this validated system, we elucidated the multifaceted role of VfWOX1.2 in coordinating phytohormone biosynthesis and abiotic stress responses. Following VfWOX1.2 overexpression in protoplasts, the VfWOX1.2-GFP fusion protein was successfully detected. Further experiments revealed that VfWOX1.2 overexpression led to marked upregulation of key cytokinin biosynthetic genes, suggesting a possible role for this transcription factor in positively modulating cytokinin production. This finding is consistent with the known function of WOX transcription factors in maintaining meristem activity, a process that heavily relies on cytokinin signaling [43]. In contrast, VfWOX1.2 exhibited a more complex regulatory role in auxin biosynthesis: it strongly suppressed the TAA pathway genes (VfTAA1, VfTAA4) and the VfCYP79B2-dependent branch, while concurrently upregulating several YUC genes. This suggests that VfWOX1.2 may fine-tune auxin homeostasis by redirecting metabolic flux between distinct biosynthetic pathways. Such precise regulation is critical for establishing specific auxin maxima or gradients, a recognized function of WOX genes in developmental patterning [44, 45]. Moreover, the coordinated downregulation of multiple auxin biosynthetic genes implies that VfWOX1.2 may suppress overall auxin levels to maintain a specific cytokinin-auxin balance, thereby influencing cell fate decisions. Beyond protein expression and hormone-related gene analyses, we also examined stress-responsive gene expression in VfWOX1.2-overexpressing protoplasts under cold and heat stress. VfWOX1.2 itself was downregulated under cold stress but upregulated under heat stress, indicating opposing expression patterns and suggesting distinct upstream regulatory mechanisms. Correspondingly, VfWOX1.2 overexpression suppressed the expression of core cold-responsive genes (VfCOLD1, VfCBF2), supporting its role as a potential negative regulator of cold tolerance. Under heat stress, this factor acts as a positive regulator, significantly increasing the expression of the heat shock gene VfHSP101 and, particularly, enhancing the expression of the key reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging enzyme VfAPX1. These results suggest that VfWOX1.2 may bolster cellular antioxidant capacity to mitigate heat-induced oxidative damage.

In conclusion, this study has successfully developed and systematically validated a high-efficiency transient protoplast transformation system for tung tree. This system has not only enabled the deciphering of the complex biological functions of the VfWOX1.2 transcription factor but has also demonstrated its broad applicability across multiple experimental scenarios, including subcellular localization, protein detection, stress treatments, and gene overexpression analyses. The establishment of this platform significantly advances functional genomics research in tung tree, accelerating gene functional characterization and providing key technical support for the development of efficient molecular breeding frameworks.

Conclusion

In summary, this study has developed a streamlined protocol to isolate high-quality protoplasts from three distinct tissues of tung tree, including petals, root tips, and anther-derived callus. Under optimized conditions, protoplast yields reached 1.44 × 10⁷/g FW (87.43% viability) from petals, 6.5 × 10⁶/g FW (85.92% viability) from root tips, and 1.42 × 10⁷/g FW (87.48% viability) from anther-derived callus. To ensure widely applicable experimental materials, we developed a highly efficient transient transformation system using protoplasts derived from anther-derived callus as recipients, achieving a transformation efficiency of 44.91%. Functional characterization of VfWOX1.2 through this system revealed its central role in cell fate determination via coordinated regulation of cytokinin and auxin biosynthetic pathways. Furthermore, VfWOX1.2 exhibited a dual regulatory role in temperature stress responses, functioning to suppress cold tolerance while acting as a positive regulator of heat stress adaptation. This study provides a versatile platform for functional genomics in tung tree and lays a foundation for physiological and molecular investigations, thereby opening up promising avenues for its breeding.
