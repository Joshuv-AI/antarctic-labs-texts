The relationship between compulsive shopping behavior, family functioning, and fashion orientation among female students: a descriptive cross-sectional correlational study
Authors: Fatemehsadat Baniashraf, Maryam Madineshat, Erfan Ayubi, Efat Sadeghian, Mona Arji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompulsive shopping, a dysfunctional behavior with psychological, financial, and social consequences, is a growing concern, especially among students. Given the potential influence of family functioning and fashion orientation on this behavior, the present study aims to examine the association between compulsive shopping behavior, family functioning, and fashion orientation among female students.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional correlational study, conducted in 2024, involved 314 female students from Hamadan University of Medical Sciences, selected through multi-stage sampling after meeting the inclusion criteria. Data were collected using a General Characteristics questionnaire, the Faber and O’Guinn Compulsive Buying Scale, the Family Assessment Device (FAD), and the Weber Fashion Interest Scale. Data analysis was performed with SPSS (version 24), including Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, Pearson correlation, independent t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and linear regression.ResultsThe mean scores for compulsive shopping behavior, fashion orientation, and family functioning were 12.12 (SD = 6.52), 33.54 (SD = 17.29), and 23.23 (SD = 20.36), respectively. A significant positive correlation was found between compulsive shopping and fashion orientation (r = 0.510, p < 0.001), explaining 10.4% of the variance. A significant negative correlation was observed between compulsive shopping and family functioning (r = -0.323, p < 0.001), accounting for 22.4% of the variance. Fashion orientation also showed a negative correlation with family functioning (r = -0.207, p < 0.001), predicting 8.2% of the variance.ConclusionsThis study shows a significant association between compulsive shopping behavior, fashion orientation, and family functioning. Strengthening family support and implementing educational programs on consumption management and the consequences of these behaviors are recommended.

Background

Shopping is a common and essential behavior in daily life, playing a crucial role in fulfilling material and psychological needs. However, for some individuals, this behavior becomes a response to negative emotions rather than fulfilling actual needs, evolving into a persistent and it is perceived as uncontrollable. This phenomenon, known as “compulsive shopping,” is regarded as a form of behavioral addiction [1].

Compulsive shopping (CS) is defined by an excessive preoccupation with shopping and spending, resulting in significant psychological distress and functional impairment in daily life [2]. This condition is marked by a diminished ability to resist shopping-related impulses, accompanied by intrusive thoughts and persistent urges to purchase. Such behavior leads to wide-ranging negative consequences, adversely affecting individuals’ psychological well-being, social functioning, and financial stability, while also imposing broader economic costs on society [3]. This behavior often stems from psychological needs, such as anxiety reduction, self-confidence enhancement, or emotional escape. Generally, perceived environmental threats, lack of resources, uncertainty about the future, and social pressures are considered contributing factors [4]. Evidence suggests that compulsive shopping is often used as a coping mechanism for life’s stresses [5]. Although it is not officially classified as a distinct disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5 TR), many experts recognize it as a form of behavioral addiction [6]. This phenomenon not only adversely affects individuals’ economic status but also leads to psychological consequences, including anxiety, depression, and reduced life satisfaction [7]. A large meta-analysis involving 32,000 participants from 16 countries estimated the global prevalence of compulsive buying to be approximately 4.9%. In some countries, such as Italy, this prevalence has been reported to be as high as 11.3% [8]. Available data indicate a notable increase in compulsive shopping behaviors among young people, particularly female students [9]. A study by Roberts and Roberts found that adolescents engage in compulsive shopping as a coping mechanism for stress, with gender having no significant effect on this relationship [10]. However, other studies suggest that women tend to engage in more emotionally-driven shopping compared to men [11]. Women are more inclined to engage in online shopping than men, constituting a large portion of online consumers [12]. A study by Thomas et al. found that compulsive shopping among female Emirati students was significantly higher than in other groups [13]. Furthermore, this behavior has been linked to disorders such as depression, anxiety, hoarding, gambling, and substance use, particularly among women [14].

Another contributing factor to compulsive shopping is fashionism. The desire for variety, identification with others, and the need to attract attention are central to fashionism [15]. Although fashionism has existed for centuries, it has gained considerable prominence in recent decades, particularly among young people [16]. Extreme fashionism can lead to adverse outcomes, such as compulsive shopping, hoarding, negative mood, and life dissatisfaction [17]. Additionally, fashionism, as a social and cultural factor, influences compulsive shopping tendencies by promoting inappropriate consumption patterns and pressure to conform to societal standards. Female students, due to their heightened sensitivity to beauty and fashion norms, are susceptible to these influences compared to other groups [12, 18].

The family, as the first and most influential social institution, may plays a significant role in shaping and reinforcing individual behaviors [19, 20]. Previous studies have indicated that unhealthy family environments, authoritarian or permissive parenting styles, and lack of emotional support can contribute to the development of compulsive shopping behavior in young people [11, 19, 21]. Family functioning plays an important role in the development of compulsive shopping behavior [22]. This concept encompasses how families communicate, interact, maintain relationships, and make decisions [23]. Adolescents raised in tense and conflicted family environments are more likely to engage in materialistic behaviors and compulsive shopping [19]. Additionally, evidence indicates that compulsive shopping is associated with negative childhood experiences and excessive parental control [21]. A research supports the significant influence of family dynamics and peer relationships on the development of compulsive shopping [18].

Given the pivotal role of women in society and the widespread impact of compulsive shopping on their mental, financial, and social well-being, it is crucial to examine this phenomenon. Women have become a central force in the consumer market due to their increased participation in social and economic activities [20]. However, in Iran, few studies have investigated some of the variables examined in the present study. For instance, Rajaipoor Meybodi et al. (2022) found that stress induced by the family environment and socio-oriented family communication has a significant positive influence on compulsive buying behaviour. Conversely, peer communication regarding consumption was found to have no statistically significant effect on compulsive buying [5]. Similarly, Zarei (2021) demonstrated that family functioning has a direct and significantly positive effect on compulsive buying behavior [24]. Additionally, Mosavi et al. (2020) revealed a positive and significant relationship between fashionism and compulsive shopping [15].

Despite the importance of this issue, no previous study has simultaneously examined the relationship between compulsive shopping behavior, family functioning, and fashionism among medical students in Iran. While some studies have addressed the effect of family functioning on compulsive behaviors or the role of fashionism in this regard, comprehensive investigations into these factors —and the complex relationships between them within the context of Iranian society and culture— have received limited attention. This gap in the literature underscores the need for more detailed studies in this area.

Accordingly, the present study aims to explore the relationship between compulsive shopping behavior, family functioning, and fashionism among female students at Hamadan University of Medical Sciences. The findings of this study could contribute to improving students’ mental health by providing reliable, local data, supporting the development of more effective interventions. Ultimately, the results may inform strategies to strengthen family functioning and implement targeted interventions for students.

Methods

Study type and design

This is a descriptive, cross-sectional, correlational study conducted at Hamadan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, during 2024. This university houses various faculties, with about 7,500 male and female students studying there. This study was designed according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross-sectional studies.

Participants

Participant selection for the study was performed using a multistage sampling method from female students. Initially, study participants were chosen in proportion to the number of female student studying in each faculty through stratified random sampling. Subsequently, the female student every faculty were selected using a sampling random method, based on the list of female students provided by the education department. Participants who met the inclusion criteria participated voluntarily in the study. The inclusion criteria included being female, enrolled at Hamadan University of Medical Sciences in 2024, having no history of specific or chronic physical and mental diseases as self-reported, and providing informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included being a guest student or failing to fully complete (100% completion) the questionnaires. Each participant was given 30 to 45 min to complete the questionnaires. Upon completion, the questionnaires were collected, and participants were thanked appropriately.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated based on a similar study [24], which reported a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.21 between family functioning and compulsive shopping in female students, and considering a power of 80% and a significance level of 0.05. Accounting for 10 variables (main and confounding variables) and adding 20 participants per variable, a final sample size of 314 was determined to allow for potential dropout. The university’s total female student population (N = 3,588). The final sample comprised 314 participants, distributed as follows: Rehabilitation Sciences (22), Dentistry (20), Health (37), Pharmacy (22), Medicine (116), New Technologies (3), Paramedics (43), and Nursing and Midwifery (51).

Measures and instruments

General characteristics

General characteristics included assessments of age, field and level of education, the year of entrance the university, income level, marital status, employment status, parental education and employment status, which were collected using a self-report questionnaire.

Compulsive Buying Scale (CBS)

CBS [25] was developed by Faber and O’Guinn in 1992. The scale consists of 7 items and assesses compulsive buying behavior using a five-point Likert scale. The first item (Question 1: “If I have money left at the end of my payment period, I should spend it”) is scored as follows: Strongly Disagree (5), Disagree (4), Neutral (3), Agree (2), and Strongly Agree (1). The remaining six items (Questions 2 to 7) are scored as: Very Often (1), Often (2), Sometimes (3), Rarely (4), and Never (5). Higher scores on this scale indicate a higher level of compulsive buying behavior [26]. The possible total score range is 7 to 35. A score of 7 to 11 indicates a low level of compulsive buying, 12 to 23 represents a moderate level, and a score above 23 indicates a high level of compulsive buying. The validity and reliability of the CBS were originally confirmed by Faber and O’Guinn (1992), with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.95 [27]. In the study by Rastegar and Siahsarani on the effect of personality factors on shopping addiction, Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.84, and its validity was also confirmed [27]. In the present study, test-retest reliability and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were 0.94 and 0.97, respectively, while the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.842.

Family Assessment Device (FAD)

FAD [28] consists of 60 items and was developed based on the McMaster Family Functioning Model by Epstein, Baldwin, and Bishop in 1983. This instrument includes six subscales: Problem Solving (items 2, 12, 24, 38, 50, 60), Relationships (items 3, 14, 18, 29, 43, 52, 59), Roles (items 4, 10, 15, 23, 30, 34, 40, 45, 53), Emotional Responsiveness (items 9, 19, 28, 35, 39, 49, 57), Emotional Participation (items 5, 13, 22, 25, 33, 37, 42, 54), and Behavioral Control (items 7, 17, 20, 27, 32, 44, 47, 48, 55, 58). It also provides an overall score measuring family functioning. The FAD uses a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 4 = strongly disagree). Scores range from 60 to 240, with higher scores indicating poorer family functioning [28]. Epstein et al. established the concurrent and predictive validity of the FAD through its correlation with the Locke-Wallace Marital Satisfaction Scale [29]. In a 2011 study by Yousefi, which examined the psychometric properties of the McMaster Family Assessment Method among 357 individuals in counseling centers in Iran, the internal consistency reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s alpha and classification) for the 60 items and seven factors were found to be satisfactory, with values of 0.83 and 0.82, respectively [30]. In the current study, the test-retest and ICC were 0.92 and 0.99, respectively, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.935.

Weber’s fashion involvement scale

The Weber Fashion Involvement Scale, developed by Sabine Weber in Canada in 2015, consists of 16 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (strongly agree = 5, agree = 4, neither agree or disagree = 3 disagree = 2, strongly disagree = 1). Scores range from 5 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater fashion involvement. A score of 80 represents a high degree of fashion involvement. Weber initially used this scale in her 2015 study titled “How Do Consumers Manage Textile Waste?” The convergence of this questionnaire with the Unwanted Clothing Management Probability Questionnaire was found to be significant (P < 0.001). In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.973, indicating acceptable reliability [31]. Additionally, in their 2022 study titled “How Fashionable Are We? Psychometrics of the Fashionability Scale with Multivariate Statistics,” Weber and Weber confirmed the validity and reliability of the scale among students, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.97 [32]. As this scale has not been previously used in domestic research, the current study involved obtaining permission from the original author. The scale was translated into Persian and then retranslated into English to ensure accuracy. To assess content and face validity, the Persian version of the questionnaire was reviewed by ten faculty members and ten students, who confirmed its validity. Furthermore, the questionnaire was administered to 30 students, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated. In the present study, the test-retest and ICC results were 0.99 and 0.99, respectively, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.965.

Data analysis

In this study, descriptive statistics such as mean (standard deviation) and frequency (percentage) were used to summarize quantitative data. Given the non-significant results of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the assumption of normality was met for the variables under study (Compulsive Shopping Behavior, p = 0.08; Family Functioning, p = 0.56; and Fashion Orientation, p = 0.05). Therefore, independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA were employed to compare quantitative variables across two and multiple categorical groups, respectively. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the pairwise relationships between quantitative variables. Univariate linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the strength of associations between independent and confounding variables and the dependent variable (compulsive buying behavior). Variables with a significance level of less than 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. The results were presented as regression coefficients along with 95% confidence intervals.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the relationship between compulsive shopping behavior, family functioning, and fashion orientation among female students at Hamadan University of Medical Sciences.

The results revealed a significant negative correlation between compulsive shopping behavior and family functioning. These findings align with previous research in this field. For instance, a study conducted by Topino et al. demonstrated that secure attachment, mediated by family cohesion and functionality, is negatively associated with compulsive online shopping [33]. Similarly, Zarei et al. identified a negative relationship between family functioning and compulsive shopping among adolescent girls [24]. Rachubińska et al. identified compulsive shopping as a behavioral addiction associated with ineffective coping mechanisms and dissatisfaction with both social and family life [34]. Similarly, Uzarska et al. reported that shopping addiction among Polish students is linked to low family satisfaction [35]. Rajabipour Meybodi and Farzinfar found that adverse family events significantly contribute to compulsive shopping behavior among young consumers in Yazd [36]. Likewise Moghadam Jezeh et al. identified poor family functioning as significant predictors of compulsive shopping behavior [37]. Additionally, Roberts et al. found that family conflict indirectly contributes to compulsive buying behavior [38]. In explaining similar results of studies, it may be said that positive and supportive family relationships serve as protective factors against compensatory behaviors such as compulsive shopping. Individuals who experience strong family bonds and mutual reliance are less likely to engage in maladaptive behaviors, whereas weak familial relationships may contribute to feelings of insecurity and an increased tendency toward excessive consumption [39].

Additionally, the study’s findings indicate a significant positive correlation between compulsive shopping and fashion orientation among students. International research has similarly confirmed the positive association between fashion orientation, and compulsive buying tendencies [27, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. One of the primary explanations for this positive correlation is the influence of social pressure and comparative behaviors among students. University environments foster continuous social comparisons in aspects such as appearance and lifestyle, thereby increasing the inclination to follow fashion trends and purchase related items [44].

The findings of this study further revealed a significant negative correlation between fashion orientation and family functioning, indicating that higher levels of family functioning are associated with lower tendencies toward fashion-oriented behaviors among female students. Previous research has also emphasized the importance of family influence in shaping consumer and fashion-related behaviors. For instance, Afrasiabi and Mirabzadeh found that family-related factors, such as parental fashionism and consumerist attitudes, significantly impact young girls’ fashion-related behaviors [45]. Similarly, koohestani bahr aseman identified a negative correlation between family identity and fashion orientation [46]. Empirical evidence suggests that families can prevent extreme fashion-related behaviors by fostering stable values and strong emotional bonds [47]. In this regard, Mamollahi and Elmi identified reference groups, including family, as well as the extent of media exposure, as key predictors of students’ fashion orientation [48]. Families that provide emotional support and maintain strong connections are less likely to have children who seek social validation through excessive engagement with fashion trends [49, 50, 51, 52]. Conversely, in families with poor functioning, social pressures and media influences may intensify fashion-oriented behaviors. Eastman et al. reported that luxury consumerism among university students is shaped by the combined impact of social media, celebrity culture, and family influences [53]. In Iranian society, rapid cultural transformations and the growing impact of social media have contributed to an increased prevalence of fashionism among young individuals [54].

Study limitations

Despite yielding valuable findings, the present study has several limitations. First, data collection was conducted through self-report questionnaires, which may have been influenced by the psychological and emotional state of the participants at the time of response. Additionally, the study’s sample was limited to female medical students, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Finally, the clear association between the studied variables can be elucidate from mediation analysis. Although mediation analysis can be performed in cross sectional studies, its interpretation regarding causal pathways is limited because the variables would be measured at a single point in time. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate simple associations to avoid overinterpreting causal relationships that the study design does not support. This exploratory approach allows for a more cautious and clears presentation of the results. Future prospective studies are recommended to investigate the nature of interrelationship among the studied variables.

Nonetheless, this study possesses notable strengths. It is among the first investigations conducted specifically among students at medical universities. Additionally, the present research represents the first instance in Iran where the translation, validation, and reliability assessment of the fashion interest instrument have been undertaken.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study identified significant associations between family functioning, fashion orientation, and compulsive shopping behavior among female university students. While the design precludes causal inferences, these findings suggest several important considerations: Family-focused interventions (e.g., communication workshops) may help mitigate compulsive shopping tendencies by improving familial support systems. Media literacy programs could reduce fashion-related social pressures that contribute to maladaptive consumption patterns. Early screening tools (e.g., CBS, FAD) may facilitate targeted interventions like cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) for at-risk individuals. Policy measures regulating exploitative marketing practices warrant further exploration. Future longitudinal or experimental research is needed to establish causal relationships.
