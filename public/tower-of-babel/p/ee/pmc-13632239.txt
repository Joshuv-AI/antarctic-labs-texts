Tetrasulfide-bridged virus-like particles enable host-sparing cascade membrane-metabolic disruption for drug-resistant bacterial keratitis
Authors: Wenlong Li, Qingdong Bao, Qinghua Li, Longfei Wang, Bingxin Ren, Hui Yan, Sihan Zhao, Fan Wu, Shunyao Jin, Hua Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-resistant bacterial keratitis remains difficult to treat because anatomical barriers and biofilm-associated tolerance restrict antibacterial access and efficacy. Intensifying bactericidal activity can enhance bacterial clearance, but may also cause nonspecific host injury and disrupt stromal homeostasis. Thus, effective therapy requires a strategy that redirects antibacterial action from bacterial surface killing to intracellular intervention without perturbing the corneal stromal microenvironment. Herein, we developed AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS, a virus-mimetic spiky nanoplatform that couples bacterial barrier breaching with intracellular redox-metabolic disruption to achieve bacteria-selective eradication. Its spiky nanotopography enhances biofilm contact and disrupts membrane barriers, driving antibacterial intervention from the bacterial exterior into the intracellular space. Within the reductive bacterial intracellular milieu, the tetrasulfide-bridged framework is cleaved, glutathione is depleted, and sulfur overload is induced, thereby undermining intracellular bacterial defenses. Mild near-infrared irradiation provides spatially controllable photothermal amplification, further enhancing interfacial disruption, deep penetration, and intracellular damage. AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS achieved > 99% bacterial clearance in vitro and reduced corneal bacterial survival to 3.1% in a multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis mouse model, while restoring stromal thickness from 280 μm to 120 μm. Introduction

Bacterial keratitis is a progressive and potentially blinding corneal infection driven by bacterial invasion and secondary inflammation [1]. It is estimated to cause 1.5–2.0 million new cases of monocular blindness worldwide each year [1, 2]. Within this spectrum, drug-resistant bacterial infections are particularly difficult to treat [2–4]. Such lesions are often embedded deep within the corneal stroma, where tear clearance, anatomical barriers, and limited stromal diffusion restrict topical drug delivery and prevent effective drug accumulation at the infectious core [4, 5]. Increasing the dose or administration frequency may transiently improve local exposure, but nonspecific antibacterial action can perturb stromal redox and metabolic balance, aggravating microenvironmental dysfunction [6, 7]. Drug-resistant pathogens can persist within infected corneal tissues as microcolonies or biofilm-like communities, reducing the efficacy of exogenous antibacterial agents and contributing to recurrent infection and irreversible visual impairment [2, 3, 8]. Therefore, the central therapeutic challenge in bacterial keratitis is not simply to enhance drug-mediated bactericidal potency, but to advance antibacterial efficacy from the bacterial exterior into the intracellular space while minimizing perturbation of the corneal stromal microenvironment.

In recent years, non-antibiotic antibacterial strategies based on physical interactions have emerged as promising alternatives for treating drug-resistant bacterial infections [9, 10]. Among them, physically disruptive nanomaterials with spike-like topologies have attracted considerable interest because of their strong interfacial contact, membrane perturbation, and biofilm penetration capabilities [11]. Inspired by viral morphologies, these materials enhance adhesion and localized stress at bacterial membranes and biofilms, improving pathogen capture, membrane destabilization, and deep biofilm penetration [12, 13]. Wu et al. developed a photothermally responsive nanozyme with a virus-like surface, which synergistically enhanced biofilm penetration and pathogen capture and enabled ICG@hMnOx to kill more than 90% of planktonic bacteria [14]. Zhang et al. further engineered nanomotors integrating viral topology with self-propulsion to mechanically disrupt deep biofilms, achieving nearly 100% antibacterial efficiency and almost complete keratitis resolution [15].

Nevertheless, current studies have mainly focused on disrupting bacterial external barriers or enhancing exogenous killing, with limited attention to intracellular redox defense as a deeper driver of bacterial resistance [16, 17]. Intracellular glutathione (GSH), a key component of bacterial redox buffering systems, helps bacteria withstand oxidative stress and is increasingly recognized as a metabolic determinant of damage repair, persistence, and antibiotic tolerance [16–18]. GSH is also present in the corneal stroma, where it contributes to local redox homeostasis and microenvironmental balance. Thus, nonspecific attenuation of redox buffering capacity may weaken bacterial self-repair, but it may also disrupt stromal redox homeostasis and further aggravate the compromised corneal microenvironment [19]. These considerations highlight the need for a host-sparing intracellular resistance-disarming strategy that breaches bacterial external barriers and selectively undermines intracellular defenses.

Herein, we developed AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS, an antibacterial platform integrating topological puncture, targeted intracellular GSH depletion, and near-infrared (NIR)-assisted enhancement, to enable a cascade intervention from external interfacial contact to intracellular defense disarming (Scheme 1). This system leverages a spiky topological architecture to strengthen contact with bacterial interfaces and deep biofilm regions, advancing antibacterial intervention into the bacterial interior. Subsequently, the tetrasulfide backbone is cleaved within the reductive bacterial intracellular milieu, triggering component release, targeted bacterial GSH depletion, and sulfur overload. This process disrupts bacterial redox homeostasis and reshapes metabolic networks associated with energy metabolism, cell wall synthesis, and resistance maintenance. Mild NIR irradiation provides a controllable exogenous energy input for spatially confined stromal lesions, synergistically enhancing interfacial disruption, deep penetration, and intracellular damage. This strategy achieved over 99% bacterial clearance in vitro. In a multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA) keratitis mouse model, it reduced corneal bacterial survival to 3.1%, restored stromal thickness from approximately 280 μm to the normal level of 120 μm, and enabled nearly complete resolution of keratitis. This study establishes a host-sparing intracellular resistance-disarming strategy that advances antibacterial action from bacterial external barriers to intracellular defense systems, providing a new design paradigm for precision treatment of drug-resistant infections.

Materials and methods

Materials, cell lines, and characterization instruments

Details of the materials, cell lines, and characterization instruments are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

AuNR synthesis

AuNR core were synthesized using a seed-mediated growth method. First, an Au seed solution was prepared by mixing a 120 mL ice-cooled NaBH4 solution with a 2 mL aqueous solution containing HAuCl4 and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB). Subsequently, 2.0 mL of the Au seed solution was added to the Au growth solution (2.4 mL HAuCl4, 16 mL AgNO3, 12 mL HCl, and 1.2 mL ascorbic acid). The mixture was then stirred vigorously for 12 h. Finally, the synthesized AuNR core were washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol to remove impurities.

Synthesis of AuNR@vTSMS core-shell nanoparticles (NPs)

The core-shell NPs of AuNR@vTSMS were fabricated via an epitaxial growth method on AuNR cores, with CTAB serving as a templating agent in an oil-water reaction system. Briefly, a chemical mixture (1.5 g AuNR, 0.2 g CTAB, and 100 µL 0.1 M NaOH) was first added to 12 mL deionized water. A separate mixture (3.2 mL tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), 3.2 mL bis[3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl] tetrasulfide (BTES), and 40 mL cyclohexane) was then introduced into the earlier-prepared dispersion and stirred vigorously at 65 °C for 72 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. After removing the supernatant, the precipitate was washed three times with deionized water and ethanol to remove impurities. On the other hand, the AuNR@sphere-like tetra-sulfide-bridged mesoporous silica (AuNR@sTSMS) core-shell NPs were prepared through a previously described protocol, but with ammonia substituted for NaOH.

Synthesis of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS

First, 1 mg AuNR@vTSMS was added alongside DLYS (1 mg) into a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (pH 7.4, 10 mL) and then stirred overnight. Following that, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm. After removing the supernatant, the precipitate was washed to remove impurities, yielding AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS.

Preparation of virus-like nanoparticles

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MS) were synthesized using a reported method. Briefly, hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 0.5 mg) was dissolved in deionized water (100 mL) and heated to 60 °C under stirring until a clear solution was obtained. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 0.1–0.3 mM) was added to adjust the solution to pH 10–11, followed by the slow addition of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 1 mL). The mixture was stirred for 4 h at 60 °C. The resulting nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min), washed alternately with deionized water and ethanol (5 times), dried at 60 °C overnight, and calcined at 550 °C for 4 h to remove the CTAB template. This yielded spherical MS for subsequent modifications. Virus-like mesoporous silica nanoparticles (vMS) were synthesized following the identical protocol used for MS, with the sole modification of substituting NaOH for ammonia. Tetrasulfide-bridged mesoporous silica nanoparticles (TSMS) were prepared through the same protocol regarding preparation of MS except that TEOS (1 mL) was replaced by TEOS (1 mL) and BTES (0.5 mL). Virus-like tetrasulfide-bridged mesoporous silica nanoparticles (vTSMS) were prepared through the same protocol regarding preparation of vMS except that ammonium hydroxide (1 mL) was replaced by NaOH (0.1 mM).

Loading and releasing abilities of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS

Photothermal abilities of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS

First, an 808 nm NIR (0.5 W cm− 2) was used to irradiate vTSMS, AuNR, and AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS for 10 min, with the sample temperature recorded every 30 s using an infrared thermal imager. Infrared thermal images were also captured every minute with the same device. Subsequently, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS was irradiated for 10 min using an 808 nm NIR (0.5 W cm− 2) and then cooled to RT. The sample’s temperature cycles were recorded during heating and cooling for ten cycles using an infrared thermal imaging device.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Preprocessing

The computational task involved analyzing the transmembrane process of silica via molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Gromacs 2022 was selected as the MD simulation software. The universal force field (UFF) was applied to silica, while the CHARMM36 force field was used for extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC) phospholipi d bilayers.

Simulation parameters

The Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) method was employed to handle electrostatic interactions. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm with a maximum of 50,000 steps. The TIP3P water model was used. Both the Coulomb cutoff distance and van der Waals cutoff distance were set to 1 nm. The system was equilibrated under the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble, followed by production MD simulations at ambient temperature and pressure. The integration time step was set to 20 fs. The v-rescale thermostat was used to maintain the simulation temperature at 300 K, and the C-rescale method was applied to control the pressure at 1 bar. Data analysis of the simulation trajectories was performed using the built-in analysis modules of Gromacs.

Antibacterial abilities of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS

First, PBS, the antibiotics penicillin (PEN) and ciprofloxacin (CIP), TSMS, vMS, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS and AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR were selected as the test samples. Their concentrations were then adjusted uniformly to 0, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 µg mL− 1 for measurement. Based on the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) results obtained from this concentration range, 20 µg mL− 1 was selected as the working concentration of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS. Unless otherwise specified, this concentration was used in subsequent antibacterial and mechanistic experiments. Subsequently, the samples were irradiated using an 808 nm NIR light for 10 min at an intensity of 0.5 W cm− 2. During the tests, CIP and AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS were considered representative samples for studying the antibacterial mechanisms.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) determination

First, the bacterial suspension was spread on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates using the streak plate method and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Single colonies were then picked, transferred to an LB liquid medium, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 6 h. Following that, the bacteria were washed 2–3 times with sterile PBS and resuspended in LB liquid medium to an absorbance of 0.15 at 600 nm, resulting in a bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1 × 108 colony-forming unit (CFU) mL− 1. This suspension was further diluted in an LB liquid medium to a 2 × 106 CFU mL− 1 concentration to obtain the working bacterial solution. For MIC determination, the drug was first diluted in a two-fold gradient using an LB liquid medium. Subsequently, in sterile, enzyme-free centrifuge tubes, different concentrations of the drug were mixed with a bacterial suspension at a volume ratio of 1: 1 (2 × 106 CFU mL− 1). An LB liquid medium mixed with the bacterial suspension at the same volume ratio served as the control group (with three parallel groups set for each concentration). Following that, 200 µL of the mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate, which was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before measuring the Optical Density (OD). The concentration that inhibited bacterial growth was defined as the MIC. For MBC determination, 10 µL of the supernatant from each well of the 96-well plate was spread onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The plates with a final colony count in the single digits were considered to have reached the MBC.

Hemolytic concentration determination

First, the test sample was mixed with 4% fresh mouse Red Blood Cells (RBCs). After standing for 1.5 h, the mixture was centrifuged and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was characterized using a microplate reader for UV-Vis spectroscopy, and the light intensity at 545 nm was compared to that of the fully hemolyzed group. The concentration at which the light intensity reached 50% was considered the half value of hemolytic concentration (HC50).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sample preparation and observation

For ultrathin section sample preparation, bacterial suspensions with drugs were first prepared using a sterile saline solution. The bacterial suspensions were then mixed with the drugs and cultured at 37 °C. The saline solution mixed with bacterial suspensions, which served as the control group, was also cultured at 37 °C. Bacterial pellets, ranging in size from sesame seeds to green peas, were then collected via centrifugation (5000 rpm, 5 min). Subsequently, the pellets were washed with a saline solution 2–3 times, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at RT for 2 h, and then stored at 4 °C, awaiting further tests. After dehydration, infiltration, embedding, and ultrathin sectioning, the morphological damage to the bacteria compared to the control group (cell wall damage and cytoplasm leakage, among others) and the enrichment of the drugs on the bacterial bodies were then observed.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) sample preparation and observation

First, suspensions with drugs were prepared using the sterile saline solution. Briefly, bacterial suspensions were mixed with the drugs and cultured at 37 °C. The saline solution mixed with bacterial suspensions, which served as the control group, was also cultured at 37 °C. Bacterial pellets, ranging in size from sesame seeds to green peas were then collected through centrifugation (5000 rpm, 5 min). Subsequently, the pellets were washed with the saline solution 2–3 times, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at RT for 1.5 h, and then stored. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed and then the bacterial samples were obtained and washed with deionized water 2–3 times. Following that, the samples were soaked in ethanol solutions of 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% concentrations for 15 min each to dehydrate the bacteria, and were finally resuspended in 100% ethanol. The bacterial samples were then spotted onto silicon wafers, which were fixed onto SEM sample holders (with conductive adhesive and sputter-coated with gold) after the ethanol had evaporated naturally. The morphological damage to the bacteria compared to the control group and the enrichment of the drugs on the bacterial bodies were then observed using SEM (Hitachi, Japan).

Bacterial live/dead staining

First, bacterial suspensions with drugs were prepared using a sterile saline solution. Briefly, the bacterial suspensions were mixed with the drugs and co-cultivated at 37 °C. The saline solution mixed with bacterial suspensions, which served as the control group, was also co-cultivated at 37 °C. After centrifugation (5000 rpm, 5 min) and washing, the live bacteria/dead bacteria were stained using a double staining kit. Subsequently, a 5 µL drop was applied to a slide, and the fluorescence was observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Characterization of bacterial cell membrane depolarization

The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 106 CFU mL− 1 to obtain the working bacterial solution labeled with DiSC3(5). An aliquot of 200 µL of the bacterial solution was mixed with each test sample. For the negative control, an equal volume of sterile physiological saline was mixed with an equal volume of the pre-labeled working bacterial solution. All mixtures were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 16 h. After incubation, 10 µL of each mixture was placed onto a clean microscope slide, covered with a coverslip, and the fluorescence signal was detected using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM-900) confocal laser scanning microscope (excitation: 622 nm; emission: 670 nm).

Biofilm construction and removal

For biofilm construction, the bacterial suspension was first streaked onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Single colonies were then picked and transferred to tryptic soy broth (TSB). The mixture was then cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 6 h. The obtained bacteria were then washed with a sterile saline solution 2–3 times. Following that, the bacteria were resuspended in tryptic soy broth and inoculated into a 24-well plate, ensuring that the bottom of the wells was covered. After > 24 h of static cultivation at 37 °C until the biofilm matured, the liquid culture medium in the wells was discarded, and the biofilms were washed with saline solution 2–3 times. For biofilm removal, 1 mL fresh TSB containing the drugs was first added to the wells and incubated at 37 °C. After aspirating the liquid, the resultant was washed with saline solution three times. Following that, 1 mL 0.1% crystal violet was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After aspiration and washing with saline solution 2–3 times, a 1 mL 95% ethanol solution was added, and absorbance at 595 nm was measured. For 3D Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (3D-CLSM) characterization of biofilm removal, the biofilms treated with the drugs were stained with a live bacterial stain and observed and photographed using a 3D-CLSM (Zeiss, Germany).

Bacterial metabolomics and antimicrobial mechanism studies

Sample preparation

For tissue preparation, the samples were first lyophilized and then ground with a tungsten bead in a 2 mL Eppendorf tube. Subsequently, the metabolites were extracted using a mixture of methanol, acetonitrile, and water, after which the resultant was sonicated in an ice bath, frozen, and then centrifuged. Following that, the supernatant was collected and dried.

For the preparation of the serum/plasma/culture solution, the samples were first mixed with cold methanol and acetonitrile, after which the resultant was sonicated, frozen, and centrifuged. The supernatant was then collected and dried for LC-MS analysis.

For cell preparation, the metabolites were first extracted using a mixture of solvents and processed in a manner similar to that of the tissue samples, followed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.

In preparing Quality Control (QC) samples, the samples were first pooled for normalization and analyzed using a procedure similar to that of the experimental samples. The dried extracts were then reconstituted in acetonitrile, filtered, and then stored at -80 °C, awaiting further tests.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

Metabolomics profiling was performed using a UPLC-ESI-Q-Orbitrap-MS system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column. The mobile phase comprised water with 0.1% Formic Acid (FA) and acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was applied and HESI source conditions were optimized for MS analysis. Finally, QC samples were injected periodically for data normalization.

Data preprocessing and filtering

Raw MS data were processed using MS-DIAL for peak alignment, retention time correction, and area extraction. Metabolites were identified by matching the data with a metabolite standard library. Variables with > 50% nonzero measurements in ≥ 1 group were retained.

Multivariate statistical analysis

Multivariate data analyses were performed using R packages including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Model overfitting was evaluated using permutation tests. The models’ performance was assessed using R2X, R2Y, and Q2 values, with Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores indicating the variables’ significance.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis

Herein, differential metabolite data were subjected to KEGG pathway analysis to identify perturbed biological pathways. The analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact test, with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction also applied for multiple testing. Significance was set at *p < 0.05.

Establishment of a multidrug-resistant bacterial keratitis model in C57BL/6 mice

6-8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. All animals were required to be kept in an specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment for one week for subsequent experiments. All experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Eye Institute and complied with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement (Number: W2024022700018). First, C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized using a ready-to-use tribromoethanol solution via intraperitoneal injection and then placed under a dissecting microscope. Three parallel incisions of ⁓1 mm in length were then made in the central cornea of the left eye using a 25-gauge needle to disrupt the corneal epithelial barrier. Subsequently, a 5 µL multidrug-resistant PA suspension (1 × 105 CFU mL− 1) was dropped onto the ocular surface. 24 h after infection, mice with successfully induced keratitis were randomly divided into five group. All treatments were initiated immediately after the 24 h post-infection period and lasted five days.

Clinical scoring of bacterial keratitis

Ophthalmic evaluations were conducted at 1, 3, and 5 days post-treatment on the infected eyes of mice in each group using a slit lamp microscope to assess the therapeutic effects. Clinical scoring and grading of bacterial keratitis were performed as outlined in previous research, with scores ranging from 0 to 12. Total scores of ≤ 5, 6–9, and 9–12 indicated mild keratitis, moderate keratitis, and severe keratitis, respectively.

Assessment of ocular retention time of the drugs

First, keratitis mice were divided into two groups and anesthetized. The animals were then administered 10 µL of free fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), FITC-labeled TSMS, and FITC-labeled vTSMS, on the ocular surface for imaging. An in vivo imaging system (IVIS) Lumina imaging system (PerkinElmer) equipped with filter sets (excitation/emission = 535/600 nm) was used to image the head region. Imaging was performed at 0, 5, 15, 25, 35, and 45 min post-treatment.

Corneal transparency examination

After treatment, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation, and their left eyes were extracted and gently separated using ophthalmic micro-scissors. The corneas were then placed on an “E-shaped” object and photographed using a dissecting microscope (Leica, Germany).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain

After five days of treatment, the left eyes were removed, dissected, and then fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution overnight. Following that, the eyes were washed with PBS and embedded in paraffin. Histological Sect. (4 μm) were stained with the H&E high-definition histological staining kit (Servicebio, China). Histological images were taken using a Leica DMi8 (Germany).

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

After five days of treatment, the mice were euthanized and then their left eyes were removed, dissected, embedded in paraffin, and frozen-sectioned (4 μm). The sections were then blocked in PBS containing 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 1 h, and then incubated with tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, 1: 400, CST) and interleukin-6 (IL-6, 1: 200, Proteintech) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Following that, cell nuclei were stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, ThermoFisher) before mounting the sections using an anti-fade fluorescence mounting medium. Finally, the sections were observed under a confocal microscopy (ZEISS LSM900) and the average relative fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software.

Western blot (WB) analysis

On the fifth day of treatment, corneal samples from each mice group (n = 6 per group) were placed in 200 µL lysis buffer (198 µL RIPA lysis buffer + 2 µL 1 Mm PMSF) and sonicated on ice. The proteins were then extracted after which protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). The proteins were then mixed with loading buffer, denatured via heating at 100 °C for 10 min, separated through Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to Polyvinylidene Difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against β-actin (1: 2000, Elabscience), TNF-α (1: 1000, Proteintech), and IL-6 (1: 1000, Proteintech) overnight, washed three times with TBST, and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h. Finally, the protein bands were detected and analyzed with Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL; Vazyme) and ImageJ software, respectively.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

After treatment, mice were euthanized, and their left corneal tissues were removed (n = 6 per group, with three biological replicates), with each group having three biological replicates. Total RNA was then extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara). Following that, RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences used included F: GATTACTGCTCTGGCTCCTAGC; R: GACTCATCGTACTCCTGCTTGC; NM_007393.5 for β-actin (mouse); F: ACCCTCACACTCAGATCATCTT; R: GGTTGTCTTTGAGATCCATGC; NM_013693.3 for TNF-α (mouse); and F: CACMGTCCGGAGAGGAGAC; R: CAGAATTGCCATTGCACAAC; NM_031168.1 for IL-6 (mouse).

RNA-sequencing

The corneas in the MDR-PA bacterial keratitis mice were treatment by PBS (n = 9) or AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR (n = 9). Total RNA was extracted from the corneas using TRIzol® Reagent according the manufacturer’s instructions (Magen). The mRNA was purified from 1 µg total RNA using oligo (dT) magnetic beads followed by fragmentation carried out using divalent cations at elevated temperatures in ABclonal First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer. Using the mRNA fragments as templates, double-stranded cDNA fragments were synthesized and connected with adapter sequences for PCR amplification. Finally, sequencing was performed with an Illumina Novaseq 6000 / MGISEQ-T7 instrument. The data generated from Illumina/BGI platform were used for bioinformatics analysis. All of the analyses were performed using an in-house pipeline from Shanghai Applied Protein Technology.

Biosafety assessment

To evaluate the safety of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR, six mice were randomly divided into two groups. One group received PBS treatment while the other group received AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR. On the fifth day, the corneas of mice in each group were examined using a slit lamp microscope, with fluorescein sodium staining performed to determine the integrity of the corneal epithelium. Fundus photography is used to observe the retina. Following that, the mice eyes were dissected for H&E staining. H&E staining of mouse tissue sections from the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney and blood routine tests were conducted to assess the structure and function of major organs. Furthermore, human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVECs) were cultured overnight in a 96-well plate at a seeding density of 1.5 × 104 cells/well with 90 µL of basal medium. Subsequently, sterile PBS and varying concentrations of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS were added to each well and incubated. Cell viability was then assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. To evaluate the thermal tolerance of corneal epithelial cells within the photothermal temperature range, HCECs were exposed to different temperatures, including 37, 42, 45, 48, 50, and 55 °C, for the same duration as the NIR irradiation treatment. After thermal exposure, the cells were returned to standard culture conditions. Cell viability was then assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay by adding CCK-8 working solution to each well, incubating the cells at 37 °C, and measuring the absorbance at 450 nm with a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 and shown in the form of mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. p < 0.05 was considered statistically different. Each treatment contained at least three biological replicates.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of virus-mimetic spiky nanoparticles

AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS was synthesized as a virus-mimetic nanostructure consisting of AuNRs coated with a tetrasulfide-bridged mesoporous silica shell and functionalized with DLYS ligands (Fig. 1A). AuNRs were first prepared using a seed-mediated growth method. Subsequently, the vTSMS shell was grown epitaxially on the AuNR cores in a biphasic cyclohexane-water phase, with CTAB as the micellar template and NaOH as the base catalyst in the aqueous phase. Meanwhile, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and Bis-(3-triethoxysilylpropyl)-tetrasulfide (BTES) were introduced into the cyclohexane phase and then added slowly into the aqueous phase (Fig. S1A). Finally, the surface of AuNR@vTSMS was functionalized with DLYS, integrating a virus-mimetic spiky silica shell. TEM imaging revealed pristine AuNRs as smooth rods, while the vTSMS shell generated uniform, dense nanoscale spikes 15–25 nm in height (Fig. 1B). High-contrast TEM confirmed complete encapsulation of the AuNR core (Fig. S1B).

Elemental mapping analysis by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) revealed that Au concentrated within the core, whereas Si, O, S, and C homogeneously distributed throughout the shell (Fig. 1C). Notably, both AuNR@vTSMS and AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS exhibited characteristic NIR absorption peaks due to the AuNR core (Fig. 1D), thus endowing them with a photothermal conversion effect. The hydrodynamic particle size of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS was 80.1 nm, with zeta potentials of 18.5 ± 3.2 mV (Fig. 1E-F). N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms revealed the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 240.2 ± 3.6 m2 g-1 and its average pore diameter of 8.6 ± 2.3 nm (Fig. S1C-D). These nanoparticles also maintained physiological stability in water, saline, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Fig. S1E). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed the presence of Si, O, S and C in the shell (Fig. 1G and S1F), revealing that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS exhibited a biomimetic virus-like surface topology and mesoporous structure, which is conducive to bacterial interfacial interaction and membrane-barrier disruption.

Glutathione-responsive degradation and releasing ability

Tetrasulfide bonds incorporated into the spiky silica framework enable responsive degradation of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS under reductive conditions. TEM imaging revealed that upon exposure to 5 mM GSH, approximating the intracellular GSH level in bacteria, partial disassembly of the spiky shell occurred within 4 h, and nearly complete disassembly was observed at 8 h, leaving behind the bare AuNR core (Fig. 1H). Consistent with this structural evolution, the absorbance intensity gradually decreased (Fig. 1I) and the suspension color faded (Fig. 1J), reflecting shell degradation and associated optical modulation.

Quantitative degradation analysis showed that the response depended on the amount of GSH present. Higher GSH concentrations accelerated shell degradation (Fig. 1K), indicating concentration-dependent responsiveness. Importantly, ligand release assays showed that GSH markedly accelerated DLYS release, with cumulative release reaching approximately 80% within 32 h, whereas PBS exhibited a slower and more gradual release profile, particularly during the early stage (Fig. 1L). Rather than representing an absolute redox-gated “on/off” release mode, this reduction-accelerated release behavior may help reduce premature ligand loss before bacterial internalization and promote more efficient intracellular delivery under reductive conditions. In this design, the virus-like spikes promote bacterial adhesion and membrane penetration, whereas tetrasulfide-mediated degradation enables timely intracellular ligand release and sulfur overload. This sequential synergy—mechanical piercing followed by redox-triggered release—differentiates AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS from either purely spiky topologies or simple GSH-sensitive spheres, establishing a cascade mechanism for enhanced antimicrobial efficacy.

Photothermal ability and stability of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS

Under 808 nm NIR irradiation, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS also showed photothermal conversion. Pristine AuNRs heated rapidly to about 60 °C, a temperature range that may induce irreversible protein denaturation in ocular tissues. By contrast, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS increased the temperature to approximately 45 °C within 10 min (Fig. 1M-N), a mild photothermal window sufficient to enhance bacterial membrane fluidity, accelerate tetrasulfide cleavage, and intensify intracellular oxidative stress while reducing the risk of thermal damage to ocular tissues. To further evaluate the cellular tolerance of this temperature range, we performed a temperature-dependent CCK-8 assay using HCECs. The results showed that HCECs maintained high viability at 45 °C, whereas obvious viability loss occurred when the temperature exceeded 50 °C (Fig. S10), supporting that the NIR-induced 45 °C photothermal window is well tolerated by corneal epithelial cells under the present treatment conditions. Repeated cycles of heating and cooling further demonstrated excellent photothermal stability, with only a small drop in temperature responsiveness after several rounds of irradiation (Fig. 1O). This durability is essential for clinical applications that require repeated topical administration and exposure to light. Moreover, compared with flat silica-coated AuNRs or non-spiky controls, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS achieved improved photothermal homogeneity and reproducibility, attributable to the uniform spiky surface that enhances light absorption and local field effects.

The spiky topology showed progressive penetration as early as 2000 ps, with individual spikes breaking through the membrane layer. By 8000 ps, substantial membrane disruption was observed, and lipid displacement around the spike regions was very noticeable (Fig. 2C). Spiky configurations consistently exhibited enhanced binding, with the spiky–DPPC interaction energy reaching 3000 kJ mol-1 by 8000 ps, in contrast to plate–DPPC (1000 kJ mol-1), spiky–EPS (2000 kJ mol-1), or plate–EPS (500 kJ mol-1). This indicates a higher affinity and greater disruptive potential associated with the spiky interface.

The spiky structure reached an insertion depth of about 0.8 nm by 8000 ps, while the flat plate stopped at about 0.2 nm. To confirm that this advantage was not merely a result of variations between planar and protruding geometries, simulations of smooth spherical nanoparticles and their spiky spherical counterparts were conducted (Fig. 2E-F). During the 8000 ps simulation window, the smooth particle only stuck to the surface, and the membrane bilayer stayed mostly intact. In contrast, the spiky sphere showed quick insertion and gradual rearrangement of lipids, with pore formation visible in density cross-sections. Interaction energy analyses favored the spiky morphology (Fig. S2A), with spiky–DPPC interactions nearing 3500 kJ mol-1, in contrast to 500 kJ mol-1 for smooth–DPPC and roughly 200 kJ mol-1 for smooth–EPS. Penetration depth curves (Fig. S2B) showed that spiky particles could penetrate to a depth of approximately 3.5–4.0 nm, while smooth particles reached only approximately 1.0–1.5 nm. These simulations indicate that nanoscale spiky features, rather than the overall particle geometry alone, dominate membrane-piercing efficiency. The spikes act as local stress concentrators, which helps them go deeper and interact more strongly with bacterial lipid bilayers. This interpretation is in line with our structural design strategy, which calls for virus-mimetic spikes to be added before any antibacterial events can happen. SEM images (Fig. 2G) demonstrated that while bacteria in the control groups retained their intact shape, exposure to vMS (spiky particles without tetrasulfide) resulted in partial deformation of the bacterial envelope. Conversely, bacteria treated with AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS suffered severe structural collapse, characterized by multiple spikes piercing the membrane and obvious cytoplasmic leakage. This confirms that the virus-like spikes are essential for penetrating the bacteria effectively.

The antibacterial efficacy of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS was evaluated against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA). Antibacterial evaluations showed extensive colony growth in the blank and antibiotic-control groups, with penicillin (PEN) used for MRSA and ciprofloxacin (CIP) used for MDR-PA (Fig. 2H). Treatments with TSMS and vMS resulted in only modest reductions in bacterial colonies. Nevertheless, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS significantly suppressed bacterial proliferation, achieving nearly total elimination when coupled with NIR irradiation. The robust bactericidal effect was corroborated by live/dead staining, which revealed predominantly red fluorescence in the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS-treated groups, particularly after NIR irradiation, indicating substantial bacterial death. Membrane depolarization assays further showed increased DiSC3(5) fluorescence after AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR treatment, supporting pronounced membrane potential disruption. It should be noted that DiSC3(5) mainly reports changes in membrane potential rather than bacterial viability itself. Therefore, the relatively low DiSC3(5) fluorescence observed in some AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS-treated samples should not be interpreted as weak membrane damage. Instead, severe membrane rupture and cytoplasmic leakage may impair probe retention or interfere with fluorescence readout, which is consistent with the SEM/TEM evidence of extensive membrane disruption and intracellular content leakage. Biofilm analysis by crystal violet staining further showed reduced biofilm formation after treatment, with the lowest residual signal in the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR group.

Quantitative analyses for both MRSA and MDR-PA (Fig. 2I-P) confirmed comparable viability in antibiotic-treated groups to blanks, minor reductions with TSMS, moderate improvements with vMS, whereas AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS resulted in substantially lower CFU counts and biofilm levels, together with higher levels of membrane damage and depolarization. Under NIR irradiation, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS achieved more than 99% bacterial clearance approximately 4 h earlier than the non-irradiated counterpart (Fig. S2C).

From a mechanistic perspective, the spiky interface operates as a nanoscale stress concentrator, facilitating membrane indentation and transient rupture. Flat shells, by contrast, primarily adsorbed at the EPS–cell boundary. While the virus-mimetic geometry increases both residence time and translocation probability, establishing a physical prerequisite for downstream intracellular processes. DLYS functionalization selectively promotes particle adhesion to bacterial surfaces, maintaining spike integrity and reducing nonspecific attachment to corneal cells. The tetrasulfide component facilitates redox-gated activation upon internalization. Engagement with intracellular GSH prompts localized bond dissociation and sulfur movement, altering the redox equilibrium towards oxidative stress and sulfur excess. This system, in contrast to those reliant on transient, diffusible reactive oxygen species within the biofilm, combines membrane accessibility with intracellular redox transformation to extend the disruptive period and target pathways crucial to bacterial resilience, such as glutathione buffering, TCA-associated redox equilibrium, and cysteine biosynthesis. Consequently, membrane penetration functions not as the therapeutic objective, but as the catalyst for definitive metabolic destabilization. Photothermal stimulation adds another layer of amplification. Upon NIR irradiation, localized mild heating increases membrane fluidity and accelerates tetrasulfide activation without inducing excessive thermal injury to surrounding tissues. This makes membranes more fluid and speeds up the activation of tetrasulfide without hurting nearby tissue at high temperatures. This controlled temperature input raises redox-driven stress, thereby improving compatibility with the ocular microenvironment.

Mechanistic analysis of membrane disruption and nanoparticle internalization

Changes in bacterial membrane integrity together with nanoparticle internalization were examined (Fig. 3A). SEM observations of MRSA (Fig. 3B) showed that bacteria in the blank and PEN groups retained smooth, spherical morphologies, whereas the vMS group displayed mild surface roughness with limited particle attachment. In contrast, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS treatment induced marked bacterial deformation, enhanced nanoparticle adhesion, and evident membrane rupture. These effects were further intensified by NIR irradiation, resulting in pronounced cell shrinkage, greater morphological deformation, and increased nanoparticle accumulation. A comparable morphological pattern was noted for MDR-PA (Fig. 3C). Bacteria in the blank and CIP control groups retained normal rod-like morphologies with smooth surfaces. The bacteria in the vMS group, on the other hand, had slightly wrinkled surfaces and only a few particles stuck to them. On the other hand, the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS group showed cells that were stretched out, membranes that were broken, and nanoparticles that were tightly grouped together. NIR irradiation further aggravated these structural defects, leading to increased cell lysis, complete cellular fragmentation, and increased particle adhesion.

To clarify the internal cellular damage, the ultrastructural morphology of the bacteria was analyzed. Untreated cells had thick cytoplasm that was tightly held in place by intact membranes. In contrast, the vMS group only had small, localized wrinkles in their membranes. AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS treatment caused extensive membrane disruption and deep nanoparticle internalization (Fig. 3D-E). Under NIR irradiation, the damage became more evident, with many cells displaying cytoplasmic void regions. Quantitative analysis indicated that the number of nanoparticles associated with bacterial surfaces after AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS treatment was approximately five times higher than with vMS, and NIR irradiation further increased surface attachment by roughly two-fold (Fig. 3F). Consistent with this trend, the fraction of residual cytoplasmic content decreased from nearly 100% in control bacteria to around 50% after AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS treatment, and further declined to approximately 20% with NIR irradiation (Fig. 3G).

Elemental mapping analysis in both MRSA and MDR-PA samples (Fig. 3H-I), showed uniform nitrogen signals with almost no detectable sulfur or silicon in untreated and antibiotic groups. The vMS treatment produced limited surface deposition, whereas AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS treatment resulted in clear intracellular accumulation of sulfur- and silicon-containing signals, together with a decrease in nitrogen intensity. NIR irradiation further increased sulfur and silicon signals by approximately 5–6 fold and nitrogen loss by more than 70% (Fig. 3J).

Comparative analyses against non-spiky or non-responsive controls clarify modality contributions. Flat, GSH-responsive spheres fail to overcome the biofilm–cell interface, yielding limited intracellular conversion. Spiky but non-responsive variants achieve partial disruption yet permit metabolic recovery. Only the integrated construct combines deep access with intracellular gating to transition bacteria from transient stress to irreversible collapse, consistent with the observed dismantling of biofilm architecture and the suppression of regrowth markers. These trends reaffirm that geometry and gating must act in series to outperform single-modality designs.

Considering that spike-mediated local stress concentration may theoretically interact with eukaryotic cell membranes under certain exposure conditions, we further examined the direct interaction between AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS and HCECs. FITC-labeled AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS showed only weak fluorescence signals in HCECs (Fig. S3A-B), indicating limited cellular uptake. Consistently, CCK-8 assays showed that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS did not cause an obvious decrease in HCEC viability across the tested concentration range, including the working concentration of 20 µg mL− 1 (Fig. S3C). After 24 h of co-incubation, confocal imaging of nuclei and F-actin cytoskeleton showed that HCECs retained normal cell outlines, spreading morphology, and cytoskeletal organization, without evident shrinkage or collapse (Fig. S3D). These results indicate that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS was well tolerated by HCECs under the present experimental conditions, which may be attributed to the absence of eukaryotic cell-targeting ligands and the differences in membrane composition, surface charge, and repair capacity between mammalian cells and bacteria.

Metabolomic analysis of photothermal-amplified metabolic collapse

Metabolomics analysis was performed on MDR-PA co-cultured with AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS. Initially, the partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) models were constructed for each group as shown in Fig. S4, and model evaluation parameters are presented in Table S1. The closer R2 and Q2 values are to 1, the more stable and reliable the model is; a Q2 value exceeding 0.5 indicates good predictive power of the model.

Univariate analysis identified significantly altered metabolites between the two groups. Volcano plot analysis revealed 403 metabolites with increased abundance and 523 metabolites with decreased abundance in the treatment group compared with the control group (Fig. 4A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed perturbations in GSH metabolism, cAMP/cGMP signaling, and protein digestion and absorption pathways, together with activation of ferroptosis and lysine degradation pathways (Fig. 4B). These metabolic changes point to a broad collapse of cellular metabolic balance, exceeding the relatively limited oxidative stress typically produced by conventional photothermal treatment or GSH depletion alone. Network analysis of metabolites and pathways revealed extensive connectivity and multiple shared nodes (Fig. S5). Subsequent analyses identified changes in several representative metabolites (Fig. 4C-D). In addition, bacteria obtained from the preceding experiments were further profiled, confirming that metabolite variations followed the same trends identified earlier, including changes in GSH [20], Acetyl-CoA [21] (energy metabolism), 3’,5’-cyclic GMP [22] (bacterial resistance and biofilm formation), Lysine [23] (cell wall synthesis), GlcNAc [24] (cell wall synthesis), cGMP [25] (thermogenesis), and thiosulfuric acid [26] (sulfur overload) (Fig. 4E).

Drug resistance was induced in normal P. aeruginosa by exposing bacteria to antibiotics (PEN, CIP, CTZ, and IPM) at 1/2 MIC concentrations, generating MDR-PA strains within 16 generations with resistance levels reaching eight times the MIC (Fig. 4F and Table S2). To examine whether AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS could weaken the drug-resistant phenotype of MDR-PA, we performed an in vitro antibiotic-resensitization assay. In this assay, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS was separately loaded with PEN, CIP, CTZ, or IPM through negative-pressure adsorption (Fig. S6-7). After treatment with the corresponding antibiotic-loaded formulations, MDR-PA showed restored susceptibility to antibiotic treatment at 1 MIC levels, which was further enhanced by NIR irradiation (Fig. S8 and Table S2). Together, these results demonstrate that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS can reverse the drug-resistant phenotype of MDR-PA by disarming bacterial resistance and restoring susceptibility to multiple antibiotics. Overall, this cascade antibacterial mechanism is summarized in Fig. 4G: virus-mimetic spikes first penetrate bacterial barriers, followed by tetrasulfide-mediated GSH depletion and sulfur metabolic overload, while photothermal stimulation further amplifies oxidative stress.

Elemental mapping and metabolomic profiling together showed how much the treatment induced substantial intracellular disruption. Treated bacteria showed a significant buildup of sulfur species and a simultaneous loss of important metabolites needed for energy production and envelope stability. In particular, lower levels of precursors for both cell-wall synthesis and osmotic regulation made cellular turgor and barrier integrity even worse. At the same time, genetic and metabolic signals that cause multidrug resistance and biofilm formation were strongly blocked. These biological responses are a perfect match for a “pierce-then-perturb” strategy that happens in order. The nanoplatform first penetrates the physical membrane, which allows for local intracellular chemical changes that flow through redox-sensitive networks without any problems. This ultimately stops the pathogens’ basic survival mechanisms.

Therapeutic effects of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS against MDR-PA keratitis

A murine keratitis model was established using MDR-PA and animals were randomly divided into five treatment groups: CIP (ciprofloxacin control), vMS, TSMS, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS, and AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS combined with NIR irradiation (Fig. 5A). Each regimen was applied three times per day over a period of five days. Ocular retention of free FITC, FITC-labeled TSMS, and FITC-labeled vTSMS was compared using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system. Fluorescence from free FITC decreased rapidly, with approximately half lost within 25 min and nearly disappearing by 45 min (Fig. 5B-C). FITC-TSMS showed improved retention relative to free FITC, maintaining roughly 27% of the fluorescence signal at 45 min. FITC-vTSMS had the best retention of the studied formulations, keeping about 60% of its fluorescence even after 45 min. These results indicate that the virus-mimetic spiky surface of vTSMS enhances interaction with the corneal epithelium and prolongs ocular retention. The progression of MDR-PA keratitis was monitored on days 1, 3, and 5 to evaluate therapeutic efficacy. Figure 5D-E show that all groups had similar mild corneal opacity on day 1, which matched clinical scores of about 5 and confirmed that the infection had the same severity at baseline. By day 3, the groups that had been treated with CIP-, vMS-, or TSMS showed that the infection was still becoming worse. The opacity was very high, and the clinical scores were close to 8. On the other hand, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS therapy improved corneal opacity, bringing clinical scores down to about 3.5. The strongest therapeutic response was observed in the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR group. The corneas showed markedly improved transparency after AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR treatment, with the lowest clinical score of approximately 2. Interestingly, by day 5, the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS group alone had findings that were similar to those of the NIR irradiation group. Consistent gross corneal examinations showed that eyes treated with AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS, with or without NIR, showed clearer visualization of the background “E” pattern than those in the CIP, vMS, and TSMS groups. This means that the corneal transparency was better restored (Fig. 5F-G). The biosafety of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR was further evaluated in vitro and in vivo. CCK-8 assays showed that HCECs and HUVECs maintained high viability over the tested concentration range of 10–320 µg mL− 1, suggesting that the effective working concentration of 20 µg mL− 1 was within a cytocompatible concentration window (Fig. S9E). In addition, no obvious ocular surface damage, corneal epithelial defect, retinal abnormality, corneal histological injury, major-organ damage, or hematological and biochemical abnormality was observed after AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR treatment (Fig. S9A-H).Corneal sections were stained with H&E five days after infection. Corneas from CIP-, vMS-, and TSMS-treated groups showed pronounced edema and disorganized tissue structures, with thickness reaching approximately 280 μm. Treatment with AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS, either alone or combined with NIR, restored normal corneal architecture, with thickness reduced to about 120 μm (Fig. 5H-I). Finally, residual bacterial loads within corneal tissues were quantified using agar plate culture methods (Fig. 5J). Compared with the CIP, vMS, and TSMS groups, corneas treated with AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS (with or without NIR) showed markedly fewer bacterial colonies and a survival rate of only 3.1%, indicating the pronounced therapeutic efficacy of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS against MDR-PA keratitis (Fig. 5K). These results indicate that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR integrates virus-mimetic spiky nanostructures with tetrasulfide-driven GSH depletion to achieve photothermal-amplified cascade membrane–metabolic disruption, thereby markedly reducing MDR-PA burden and alleviating inflammatory damage in the infected cornea.

Biocompatibility was evaluated in healthy mice treated with AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR. Slit-lamp microscopy and fluorescein staining revealed no corneal damage after 5 days of treatment compared to PBS controls (Fig. S9A). Fundus photography and corneal H&E staining analyses confirmed no significant structural differences in either corneas or retinas between AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR and PBS groups (Fig. S9B-D). CCK-8 assays on HCECs and HUVECs showed no cytotoxicity at all tested concentrations (Fig. S9E). Anti-inflammatory mechanism of AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS in MDR-PA keratitis

Immunofluorescence staining was used to assess macrophage and neutrophil infiltration in mouse corneas. Corneas from the CIP, vMS, and TSMS groups exhibited increased fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6A). AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS treatment (with or without NIR) significantly suppressed corneal macrophage recruitment on day 5 post-infection. Similarly, AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS (with or without NIR) also inhibited neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence was used to assess TNF-α and IL-6 expression in corneal tissues on the fifth day post-treatment. High levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were detected in the CIP, vMS, and TSMS groups, whereas their expression was reduced in AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS groups (with or without NIR) (Fig. 6C-D). Quantitative analysis of the mean relative fluorescence intensity showed reductions in the fluorescence signals for macrophages, neutrophils, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the cornea by 3.5-fold, 3-fold, 4.5-fold, and 2-fold in the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR group compared to the CIP group (Fig. 6E-H). In addition, RT-qPCR and Western blot quantified the mRNA and protein levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in corneal tissues. Similarly, TNF-α and IL-6 levels were markedly decreased in the AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS and AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS + NIR groups, whereas they remaining high in the CIP, vMS, and TSMS groups. These results demonstrated that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS (with or without NIR) exhibited excellent anti-inflammatory effects in a mouse model of MDR-PA keratitis (Fig. 6I-M).

Conclusion

In summary, we developed AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS as a virus-mimetic spiky nanoplatform for host-sparing intracellular resistance-disarming therapy against drug-resistant bacterial keratitis. By integrating topological puncture, targeted intracellular GSH depletion, and mild NIR-assisted amplification, this platform advanced antibacterial action from bacterial external barriers to intracellular defense systems while minimizing perturbation of the corneal stromal microenvironment. The spiky nanotopography enhanced bacterial interfacial contact, membrane disruption, and penetration into biofilm foci, facilitating antibacterial intervention into the pathogen interior. The tetrasulfide-bridged framework then underwent GSH-triggered cleavage within the reductive bacterial intracellular milieu, inducing bacterial GSH depletion, sulfur overload, and broad redox–metabolic dysregulation associated with energy metabolism, cell wall synthesis, and resistance maintenance. Mild NIR irradiation further amplified this cascade in a spatially controllable manner without detectable ocular or systemic toxicity. In vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that AuNR@vTSMS-DLYS effectively eliminated MDR bacteria and promoted corneal recovery in MDR P. aeruginosa keratitis. Topical administration reduced corneal bacterial survival to 3.1%, restored stromal thickness toward the normal level, suppressed inflammatory damage, and enabled near-complete keratitis resolution. Beyond the current nanomaterial-mediated antibacterial strategy, further optimization may broaden the therapeutic potential of this platform. Peptide- and peptide-based therapeutics have recently shown promise in tissue protection, mucosal repair, wound healing, and regenerative medicine [27–33]. Therefore, future studies may explore the integration of antimicrobial, barrier-protective, or pro-regenerative peptides with virus-like nanoplatforms to achieve coordinated bacterial clearance, epithelial protection, and stromal microenvironment restoration in keratitis [32, 33]. In parallel, broader validation with clinically isolated pathogens, longer-term evaluation of therapeutic durability and biosafety, and optimization of NIR irradiation parameters, dosing frequency, and formulation stability are still needed to better assess the translational potential of this intracellular resistance-disarming strategy. Overall, this study supports a host-sparing intracellular resistance-disarming strategy that advances antibacterial intervention from bacterial external barriers to intracellular defense systems while minimizing detectable perturbation of the corneal stromal microenvironment, offering a potential design principle for precision treatment of refractory drug-resistant infections in sensitive tissues.
