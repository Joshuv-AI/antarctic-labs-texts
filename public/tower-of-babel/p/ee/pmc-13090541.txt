Probable Loa loa-associated encephalopathy following ivermectin administration in a patient with malaria infection: A case report from the Democratic Republic of the Congo
Authors: Charles Malisaba Posite, Joseph Kasongo Findama, Moïse Kambale Vwiravwahali, Isaac Muhindo Molo, Gabriel Kambale Bunduki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIvermectin is widely used in mass drug administration programs for the control of onchocerciasis and lymphatic filariasis. In areas where Loa loa is co-endemic, ivermectin administration may precipitate severe neurological adverse events among individuals with high microfilarial densities. We report a case of probable Loa loa–associated encephalopathy following ivermectin administration in a patient with malaria infection.Case presentationA 37-year-old woman from Ituri Province, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, presented with altered mental status after receiving ivermectin for loiasis. Her illness began with colicky abdominal pain, mucoid-bloody diarrhea, and polyarthralgia, followed by unsuccessful self-medication. She was subsequently diagnosed with intestinal parasitosis and malaria at a peripheral health facility and treated accordingly, but her neurological condition worsened. On admission to a referral hospital, blood smear examination revealed Plasmodium trophozoites and Loa loa microfilariae. Within 24 h of ivermectin administration, she developed meningeal signs. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated the presence of Loa loa microfilariae, with no evidence of bacterial or fungal infection. Following intensified antimicrobial therapy, initiation of albendazole, and supportive care, her neurological status progressively improved. She was discharged after 19 days of hospitalization, and follow-up evaluation confirmed complete clinical recovery with clearance of microfilariae.ConclusionThis case fuls criteria for probable Loa loa–associated encephalopathy temporally related to ivermectin administration. Introduction

Loa loa filariasis (loiasis) is a vector-borne parasitic disease caused by the filarial nematode Loa loa, transmitted by Chrysops spp. biting flies across Central and West Africa including the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). Although loiasis is often considered benign, clinical manifestations can extend beyond Calabar swellings and eye migration to involve significant systemic pathology including renal and cardiac involvement, and in rare instances, central nervous system dysfunction. High microfilarial loads increase the risk of severe complications, particularly in the context of mass drug administration (MDA) programs targeting other filarial diseases such as onchocerciasis and lymphatic filariasis [1].

Ivermectin is widely used in MDA programs for the control of onchocerciasis, with more than 400 million treatments distributed annually in endemic regions. Nonetheless, rare but serious adverse events (SAEs) including encephalopathy have been observed after ivermectin administration in individuals with heavy L. loa microfilaremia. These neurological syndromes typically emerge within days of treatment and can range from altered consciousness to coma, sometimes resulting in persistent deficits or death. Risk estimates from retrospective surveillance indicate that a substantial proportion of SAEs temporally associated with ivermectin in co-endemic settings meet criteria for probable Loa loa–associated encephalopathy, with higher pre-treatment microfilarial densities correlating with more severe outcomes [2].

The co-endemicity of L. loa with onchocerciasis or lymphatic filariasis poses a major challenge for ivermectin-based MDA programs and necessitating careful risk assessment and pharmacovigilance. Mechanistically, encephalopathy has been linked to the massive release of microfilariae and subsequent inflammatory responses, with microfilariae detected in cerebrospinal fluid in some cases. The phenomenon is not only a concern in onchocerciasis programs but has been described in sporadic clinical settings, including patients co-infected with Plasmodium spp., underscoring the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities when severe malaria co-exists with loiasis [3].

Despite the rarity of these events, documenting and analysing individual cases of probable Loa loa–associated encephalopathy following ivermectin administration remain crucial. Such reports enhance understanding of risk factors, clinical progression, and management pathways, especially in regions like the DRC where filarial and malarial burdens overlap. Together with emerging strategies for microfilarial load assessment and Loa loa risk mapping to guide safe treatment policies, detailed case reports contribute to improved clinical vigilance and public health decision-making in endemic settings [4].

This report describes a patient with confirmed Loa loa microfilaremia and Plasmodium co-infection who developed encephalopathy following ivermectin administration.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old female farmer from Ituri Province, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, was admitted to the Centre Hospitalier Évangélique de Beni (CHE Beni) on January 15, 2022, with an altered level of consciousness. Two weeks prior to admission, she developed colicky abdominal pain associated with mucoid-bloody diarrhea and polyarthralgia. Initial self-medication with unspecified drugs provided no clinical improvement. She subsequently sought care at a local health facility. At the referring hospital, the patient was diagnosed with intestinal parasitosis and malaria initially considered severe, for which she received treatment with quinine, metronidazole, calcium, and thiamine. However, detailed documentation of the clinical criteria used to classify the malaria episode as severe was not available in the referral records. When reassessed at our facility, the parasite density observed on blood smear was relatively low and the criteria for severe malaria according to the World Health Organization definition could not be clearly confirmed. Despite six days of treatment, her clinical condition did not improve, and progressive alteration of consciousness prompted referral to our facility.

At admission, the patient’s general condition was altered (Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13/15). She was febrile with a temperature of 37.6 °C, tachycardic with a heart rate of 112 beats per minutes, and had borderline hypotension with a blood pressure of 90/70 mmHg. Neurological examination revealed confusion with spontaneous eye opening and motor responses appropriate to pain. Muscle tone and strength were globally reduced (Merle d’Aubigné score 3/5), while deep tendon reflexes were preserved. There were no signs of meningeal irritation at presentation. Eyes examination was normal. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Initial laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin of 10.2 g/dL, leukocyte count of 6300/mm³ , erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 80 mm/hour, serum creatinine of 32.2 μmol/L, and random blood glucose of 150 mg/dL. Thick smear examination revealed the presence of Plasmodium trophozoites and Loa loa microfilariae. Parasite densities were recorded using a semi-quantitative microscopy method based on the number of organisms observed per 100 microscopic fields, as routinely performed in the hospital laboratory. Thick blood smear microscopy revealed 11–100 Plasmodium trophozoites per 100 microscopic fields and 1–100 Loa loa microfilariae per 100 fields (Fig. 1A). Stool examination demonstrated the presence of Charcot-Leyden crystals. Serological tests for HIV, syphilis (VDRL), and Helicobacter pylori were negative. Laboratory investigations revealed urinary abnormalities including pyuria (40–60 leukocytes per high-power field) and hematuria (10–15 red blood cells per field), suggestive of urinary tract infection (Table 1).

Based on these findings, a working diagnosis of urosepsis and loiasis was made. Empirical treatment was initiated with intravenous ceftriaxone, gentamicin, supportive intravenous fluids, and symptomatic care. A vitamin B complex (Tribex Forte®) was also administered. On January 17, 2022, the patient received a single oral dose of ivermectin (9 mg).

Within 24 h of ivermectin administration, her neurological status worsened, with the development of neck stiffness and a positive Kernig sign. Lumbar puncture revealed clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with 4 white blood cells/mm³ , normal glucose concentration (71 mg/dL), and the presence of numerous Loa loa microfilariae (Fig. 1B). CSF Gram stain, India ink preparation, and Ziehl–Neelsen staining were negative. A diagnosis of probable Loa loa-associated encephalopathy was established.

In response, ceftriaxone was escalated to 2 g twice daily, and antiparasitic therapy with albendazole was initiated at a dose of 800 mg, followed by 400 mg one week later. Adjunctive therapy included piracetam (Nootropyl®) and vitamin C. The patient’s neurological status gradually improved over the subsequent two weeks, with progressive recovery of consciousness and motor function. Follow-up blood smears demonstrated a marked reduction in microfilarial density.

She was discharged after 19 days of hospitalization in stable clinical condition. At outpatient follow-up on March 3, 2022, the patient was asymptomatic and repeat parasitological examinations were negative.

Discussion

We present a case of probable Loa loa-associated encephalopathy occurring shortly after oral ivermectin administration in a patient with severe malaria and loiasis in the DRC. Although ivermectin has an excellent safety profile when used in appropriate settings, rare but serious neurological adverse events, including encephalopathy, have been documented in individuals with high Loa loa microfilarial loads, particularly in co-endemic regions targeted by mass drug administration (MDA) programs [5].

The diagnosis of probable Loa loa-related encephalopathy following ivermectin administration in this patient is consistent with previously published case definitions for post-ivermectin serious adverse events (SAEs) [2], [6]. Probable Loa loa-related encephalopathy is characterized by the onset of neurological symptoms within days following ivermectin treatment, the presence of Loa loa microfilariae in blood or cerebrospinal fluid, and the exclusion of alternative causes of encephalopathy. In the present case, the patient developed neurological signs shortly after ivermectin administration, and Loa loa microfilariae were detected both in peripheral blood and in cerebrospinal fluid. Other potential infectious causes of meningoencephalitis were excluded by cerebrospinal fluid analysis. Taken together, these findings support the classification of this event as a probable Loa loa-related post-ivermectin SAE.

Ivermectin’s microfilaricidal action causes rapid death of circulating larvae, which can trigger systemic inflammatory responses and microvascular obstruction. In individuals with heavy L. loa microfilaremia, this leads to capillary occlusion and inflammatory damage in vital organs including the central nervous system, manifesting clinically as encephalopathy, altered consciousness, seizures, or coma. Experimental and observational data suggest that the risk of encephalopathy increases substantially with microfilarial loads exceeding thresholds such as 8000–30,000 microfilariae/mL, though individual susceptibility varies [7].

Our patient’s neurological deterioration within 24 h of ivermectin administration, coupled with Loa loa microfilariae detected in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), strongly supports a causal relationship. Previous case reports have described similar temporal associations between ivermectin and encephalopathy in L. loa-infected patients, with neurological deterioration typically emerging within 48–72 h post-treatment [8], [9]. Although definitive proof of causality remains challenging due to ethical and logistical constraints, the temporal sequence and exclusion of alternative diagnoses in our patient strengthen the likelihood that ivermectin precipitated the encephalopathic syndrome. It is important to note that the patient already presented with neurological manifestations at admission, including altered mental status. Therefore, ivermectin administration cannot be considered the primary trigger of the neurological syndrome. However, a clear neurological deterioration was observed after ivermectin treatment, characterized by the development of meningeal signs and further clinical worsening. This temporal relationship suggests that ivermectin may have contributed to the progression of neurological involvement, possibly through inflammatory reactions associated with the rapid destruction of circulating Loa loa microfilariae.

The decision to initiate treatment for loiasis during the patient’s acute illness deserves consideration. In general, treatment of loiasis is not recommended in the setting of an acute systemic infection unless the parasitic disease itself is responsible for the presenting symptoms. Furthermore, ivermectin is not considered a definitive treatment for loiasis because it primarily reduces circulating microfilariae and has limited effect on adult worms. Importantly, ivermectin administration in individuals with high Loa loa microfilarial loads has been associated with severe neurological complications, including post-treatment encephalopathy. In the present case, ivermectin was administered after detection of microfilariae on blood smear during hospitalization. In retrospect, deferring antiparasitic therapy until resolution of the acute illness might have been a safer approach. This case therefore highlights the importance of careful clinical judgment when incidental Loa loa infection is detected during the evaluation of acute disease, particularly in endemic regions.

The patient had evidence of Plasmodium infection on blood smear; however, parasite density was relatively low. Although severe malaria had been diagnosed at the referring facility, this diagnosis remains uncertain, particularly given that severe malaria is uncommon in adults living in hyperendemic areas. Furthermore, the observed parasitaemia may have been influenced by prior quinine therapy initiated before referral. The neurological manifestations observed in our patient were therefore more consistent with ivermectin-associated Loa loa encephalopathy than with cerebral malaria. Nevertheless, severe malaria itself can cause neurological dysfunction (cerebral malaria) through sequestration of Plasmodium falciparum-infected erythrocytes in cerebral microvessels. However, cerebral malaria typically manifests earlier in the disease course and is characterized by profound parasitaemia, convulsions, and coma. In our patient, the onset of neurological symptoms after administration of ivermectin, rather than as a direct consequence of malaria, alongside the presence of Loa loa microfilariae in CSF, favors treatment-associated encephalopathy over cerebral malaria. To date, only one study has explored the potential association between Plasmodium infection and Loa loa-related post-ivermectin SAEs. However, that study did not report neurological SAEs, and therefore no conclusion could be drawn regarding the potential contribution of malaria infection to ivermectin-associated neurological complications. Moreover, the absence of evidence for an association in that study should not be interpreted as evidence that such an association does not exist [10].

Another hypothesis worth considering is the potential role of concurrent Plasmodium infection in facilitating the development of neurological complications following ivermectin administration. Severe malaria is known to be associated with endothelial activation and disruption of the blood-brain barrier, particularly in cases progressing toward cerebral malaria. Such alterations may increase permeability of the blood-brain barrier and allow greater penetration of circulating molecules or inflammatory mediators into the central nervous system. In this context, it is conceivable that malaria-related endothelial dysfunction could enhance the neurotoxic or inflammatory effects associated with the rapid clearance of Loa loa microfilariae following ivermectin treatment. Although the diagnosis of severe malaria in the present case remains uncertain, the coexistence of malaria parasitaemia raises the possibility that malaria-associated vascular changes may have contributed to the neurological deterioration observed after ivermectin administration. Further studies are needed to better understand the potential interactions between malaria infection and ivermectin-associated adverse neurological events in Loa loa-endemic regions.

There is no specific antidote for Loa loa-associated encephalopathy. Management focuses on supportive care and strategies to reduce microfilarial load safely. In our case, escalation of broad-spectrum antibiotics and introduction of albendazole were associated with gradual neurological improvement. Albendazole, though slower acting than ivermectin, is often used to reduce microfilaremia over several weeks before considering other treatments, especially in hypermicrofilaremic patients, to minimize the risk of rapid parasite lysis and associated inflammatory complications [7].

The initiation of albendazole after the onset of neurological symptoms also warrants consideration. Albendazole has occasionally been used in the treatment of loiasis because of its potential effect on adult worms and its relatively gradual reduction of microfilaremia. However, its role in the management of ivermectin-associated encephalopathy is not clearly established. In the context of suspected post-ivermectin encephalopathy, the priority of management is usually supportive care and the treatment of complications rather than additional antiparasitic therapy. Moreover, further antiparasitic treatment may theoretically exacerbate neurological manifestations through inflammatory reactions associated with parasite death. In the present case, albendazole was introduced after neurological deterioration had already occurred. While the patient ultimately recovered, the benefit of this intervention remains uncertain. This case therefore underscores the need for cautious therapeutic decision-making when managing loiasis in patients presenting with neurological complications following ivermectin administration.

The preventive role of pre-treatment microfilaremia assessment cannot be overemphasized. Tools such as rapid assessment of Loa loa (RAPLOA) and emerging point-of-care diagnostics (e.g., LoaScope) can help identify individuals with high microfilarial densities who are at elevated risk for severe adverse reactions, thus guiding treatment decisions and risk mitigation strategies in endemic settings [11].

Our case highlights a key dilemma in the control of filarial diseases in Central Africa: the coexistence of loiasis with onchocerciasis and lymphatic filariasis undermines blanket ivermectin MDA strategies due to the risk of serious neurological sequelae in a subset of the population. These risks have led to tailored approaches such as “Test and Not Treat” (TaNT) protocols to exclude high-risk individuals from MDA, balancing individual safety with population-level benefits [12].

We acknowledge limitations inherent to case reports, including the lack of precise quantitative pre- and post-treatment microfilarial counts and the challenge of excluding all alternative aetiologies. In endemic settings, detection of Loa loa microfilariae should ideally prompt quantitative evaluation before initiating ivermectin therapy, given the well-established relationship between high microfilarial loads and the risk of severe post-treatment neurological complications. In addition, the patient already presented with neurological manifestations at admission, which may have represented a relative contraindication to immediate antiparasitic treatment. In retrospect, delaying ivermectin administration until further parasitological assessment and stabilization of the patient’s condition may have been a more cautious approach. Despite these limitations, this case provides a notable observation: the detection of Loa loa microfilariae in the cerebrospinal fluid. The possibility that Loa loa microfilariae may invade the central nervous system has been discussed in previous reports but remains incompletely understood. The presence of microfilariae in the cerebrospinal fluid in this patient provides additional evidence supporting this phenomenon and contributes to the understanding of the pathophysiology of neurological complications associated with loiasis. Future research should focus on improved point-of-care diagnostics for microfilarial burden, better understanding of host and parasite determinants of encephalopathy risk, and exploration of safer therapeutic pathways for loiasis in endemic regions.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but serious neurological complication following ivermectin administration in a patient with probable Loa loa infection and concurrent malaria infection in an endemic setting. The close temporal relationship between ivermectin exposure and neurological deterioration, together with the detection of Loa loa microfilariae in cerebrospinal fluid and subsequent clinical improvement under supportive care and albendazole, supports a diagnosis of probable Loa loa-associated encephalopathy.

Our findings underscore the critical importance of considering loiasis in patients living in or originating from Loa loa-endemic regions prior to ivermectin administration, even outside mass drug administration campaigns. Systematic assessment of microfilarial burden, where feasible, and heightened clinical vigilance are essential to minimize the risk of severe adverse neurological events. In co-endemic settings such as the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where malaria, loiasis, and other parasitic infections frequently overlap, integrated diagnostic approaches and individualized treatment strategies are crucial.

This case contributes to the limited clinical literature on ivermectin-associated encephalopathy and reinforces the need for strengthened pharmacovigilance, improved access to point-of-care diagnostics for loiasis, and context-specific treatment guidelines to ensure patient safety while advancing neglected tropical disease control efforts in Central Africa.
