Disruption of starch biosynthesis and triacylglycerol degradation impairs growth but improves photosynthesis in Arabidopsis
Authors: Jilian Fan, Dongling Xie, Shivasharanappa S. Patil, Bagyalakshmi Muthan, Sanju A. Sanjaya, Changcheng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Enhancing lipid accumulation by redirecting carbon from starch to triacylglycerol (TAG) in vegetative tissues is a promising strategy for developing high‐energy‐density crops for bioenergy production. However, our understanding of how starch and TAG metabolism interact and how this interaction affects growth and photosynthesis is incomplete.Here, we investigated the metabolic and physiological consequences of disrupting starch biosynthesis and TAG turnover in Arabidopsis thaliana by generating single, double, and triple mutants involving ADG1 (starch biosynthesis), TGD1 (lipid trafficking), and SDP1 (TAG lipase). Unexpectedly, elimination of starch biosynthesis lowered TAG levels in the high‐TAG‐accumulating tgd1 mutant, primarily through enhanced TAG breakdown. This decline was completely reversed by sucrose supplementation, suggesting that TAG degradation was induced by carbon limitation.Genetic suppression of TAG turnover via SDP1 disruption in the starchless tgd1 adg1‐1 background led to a nine‐fold rise in leaf TAG accumulation in the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant, together with enhanced photosynthetic performance. However, this metabolic reprogramming incurred growth penalties.Our results highlight the role of dynamic TAG turnover in maintaining metabolic balance and photosynthesis in starch‐deficient backgrounds. These findings underscore the need for refined metabolic engineering strategies that coordinate TAG biosynthesis and degradation to optimize lipid accumulation in bioenergy crops.

Introduction

In photosynthetically active tissues such as leaves, plants assimilate atmospheric CO2 into organic molecules through the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle. A substantial portion of this fixed carbon is directed toward the biosynthesis of starch and lipids, two major carbon and energy storage forms with distinct, but occasionally overlapping, physiological functions. Starch is synthesized in the plastids via ADP‐glucose intermediates, whereas lipids, including triacylglycerols (TAGs), are assembled in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) from fatty acids synthesized in the plastids (Streb & Zeeman, 2012; Xu & Shanklin, 2016). A comprehensive understanding of how photosynthetically fixed carbon is partitioned between these pathways through shared metabolic intermediates, and how this partitioning is regulated under diverse developmental and environmental conditions, is essential for advancing plant biotechnology. Such information would also support efforts to engineer crops with improved carbon use efficiency, enhanced TAG accumulation, or greater environmental resilience for applications in food, feed, and bioenergy.

Starch serves as a transient carbohydrate reserve, accumulating during the day and mobilized at night to support growth and metabolism (Grennan, 2006; Geigenberger, 2011; Graf & Smith, 2011; Stitt & Zeeman, 2012). Starch biosynthesis involves the sequential conversion of triose phosphates into glucose‐1‐phosphate, ADP‐glucose, and finally starch polymers, catalyzed by enzymes such as plastidic phosphoglucomutase (PGM1) and ADP‐glucose pyrophosphorylase (ADG1) (Geigenberger, 2011; Pfister & Zeeman, 2016; Smith & Zeeman, 2020). Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) adg1‐1 and pgm1 are starchless mutants that lack the catalytic subunit of ADG1 (Lin et al., 1988) or plastidic PGM1 (Caspar et al., 1985), respectively. In the adg1‐1 mutant, photosynthesis is impaired by the loss of a major daytime carbon sink. As a result, photosynthetically fixed carbon accumulates as soluble sugars, leading to feedback inhibition of the CBB cycle and lower carbon assimilation (Kunz et al., 2010). In addition, the lack of starch reserves causes carbon starvation at night, preventing respiration and growth (Smith & Stitt, 2007; Geigenberger, 2011; Graf & Smith, 2011; Smith & Zeeman, 2020). Together, these effects diminish photosynthetic efficiency and highlight the transient starch storage in balancing energy and carbon fluxes in leaves (Preiss et al., 1991; MacNeill et al., 2017; Smith & Zeeman, 2020). Although starch serves as the primary daytime carbon sink, lipids such as TAG can also function as alternative sinks under specific physiological contexts, including genetic perturbation (Yu et al., 2018). Therefore, understanding how these pathways compensate for one another is crucial.

In contrast to starch, fatty acids are incorporated into membrane lipids or stored in the form of TAG, a dense energy‐storage molecule, and serve as valuable feedstock for the production of a wide range of products, including nutraceuticals, food, pharmaceuticals, and biofuels (Durrett et al., 2008; Xu & Shanklin, 2016). TAG is typically present at low levels in vegetative tissues under normal growth conditions (Chapman et al., 2013; Xu & Shanklin, 2016; Guzha et al., 2023), largely because of its rapid degradation (Kelly et al., 2013; Fan et al., 2014) and the limited flux of fatty acids toward TAG biosynthesis (Bates et al., 2007). While starch accumulation is a well‐established light‐dependent pathway that reaches a peak at the end of the day, recent studies have shown that lipid droplet (LD) abundance in the leaves of Arabidopsis plants follows an inverse diurnal pattern, reaching its maximum at the end of the night (Pyc et al., 2017). In Arabidopsis, TAG turnover involves lipolysis by the enzyme SUGAR‐DEPENDENT 1 (SDP1) (Eastmond, 2006), which releases fatty acids that are subsequently oxidized in the peroxisome via β‐oxidation, producing acetyl‐CoA for use in mitochondrial respiration and sugar production (Graham, 2008). Additionally, TAG plays a dual role as an energy reserve and a stress buffer; in fact, recent metabolic engineering strategies have focused on enhancing TAG accumulation in vegetative tissues by manipulating both biosynthetic flux and catabolic repression (Vanhercke et al., 2019; Park et al., 2025). Metabolic engineering and isotopic labeling studies in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) revealed a bidirectional relationship between starch and TAG metabolism, whereby leaf starch provides carbon backbones for TAG accumulation, while TAG turnover through the glyoxylate cycle regenerates sugars that contribute to starch biosynthesis, forming a starch–TAG cycle (Chu et al., 2022; Johnson et al., 2025).

This study was designed to test the hypothesis that raising TAG accumulation in starchless plants would mitigate carbon feedback inhibition and sustain photosynthesis. This approach has the potential to improve carbon use efficiency in crops that have been metabolically engineered. To explore this possibility, we introduced the adg1 mutant, which disrupts starch biosynthesis, into the trigalactosyldiacylglycerol 1 (tgd1) background. The tgd1 mutant, which is impaired in intracellular fatty acid transport from the ER to the plastids, exhibits increased fatty acid biosynthesis and greater TAG accumulation in leaves (Xu et al., 2003; Fan et al., 2013, 2014, 2024). The tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant exhibits enhanced TAG accumulation. Unexpectedly, disruption of starch biosynthesis in the double mutant resulted in a pronounced drop in TAG levels in soil‐grown plants. Biochemical and isotopic labeling analyses indicated that accelerated TAG turnover, rather than impaired TAG biosynthesis, was responsible for this change, likely reflecting greater reliance on TAG as an alternative carbon source under starch‐deficient conditions. To test this hypothesis, we generated the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant carrying an additional mutation of SDP1 encoding a key TAG lipase. Suppression of TAG degradation restored and substantially enhanced TAG accumulation in the triple mutant and improved photosynthetic energy conversion efficiency, albeit at the cost of slower growth, confirming that elevated TAG turnover is the primary cause of the lower TAG levels observed in the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant. These findings highlight the critical compensatory role of TAG catabolism in maintaining energy homeostasis and metabolic function in the absence of starch reserves. They also suggest that existing TAG‐engineering approaches may require refinement to better optimize their effectiveness in bioenergy crops.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) plants used in this study were in the Columbia (Col‐2) accession background. The tgd1 mutant was previously characterized by Xu et al. (2003), the adg1‐1 mutant by (Lin et al., 1988), the pgm1 mutant by (Caspar et al., 1985), and the sdp1 mutant by (Fan et al., 2014). The adg1‐2 mutant (SALK_134724C) was ordered from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center. The double and triple mutants (tgd1 adg1‐1, tgd1 adg1‐2, tgd1 pgm1, and tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1) were generated and confirmed as follows. Plants carrying tgd1 were identified based on trigalactosyldiacylglycerol accumulation in leaves; plants harboring adg1 or pgm1 were identified based on the absence of transient starch accumulation, as visualized by iodine staining; and plants carrying sdp1 were confirmed by genotyping PCR with the primers described in Fan et al. (2014).

Arabidopsis plants were cultivated using a two‐stage growth protocol to ensure uniform germination and optimal seedling development. In the first stage, seeds were surface sterilized and sown on ½‐strength Murashige & Skoog (½MS) medium (Murashige & Skoog, 1962) solidified with 0.6% (w/v) agar and supplemented with 1% (w/v) sucrose to support early growth. The plates were incubated vertically in a controlled‐environment chamber under a 16 h : 8 h light : dark photoperiod, day : night temperatures of 22°C : 18°C, and a light intensity of 80–120 μmol m−2 s−1. Seedlings were grown under these conditions for 10 d, ensuring the development of healthy root systems and cotyledons. In the second stage, well‐developed seedlings were carefully transferred to soil and grown in pots under similar photoperiod and temperature conditions. However, to support robust vegetative growth, the light intensity was raised to 150–200 μmol m−2 s−1. Plants were watered regularly and monitored to ensure consistent growth conditions throughout the experimental period.

Analysis of lipids and fatty acids

Total lipids were extracted from Arabidopsis leaf tissue using a modified protocol based on Dörmann et al. (1995). In brief, fresh or frozen leaves from 4‐wk‐old plants were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in a solution of chloroform : methanol : formic acid (1 : 1 : 0.1, v/v/v). To induce phase separation, 1 M KCl and 0.2 M H3PO4 were added, followed by centrifugation at 12 000 g for 10 min at room temperature (RT). The lower organic phase, containing the total lipids, was carefully collected, evaporated under a stream of nitrogen gas, and dissolved in chloroform for analysis.

Lipid classes were separated by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) on ammonium sulfate‐treated silica plates (Si250 with pre‐adsorbent zone; Mallinckrodt Baker). Neutral and polar lipids were resolved using specific solvent systems: hexane/diethyl ether/acetic acid (70 : 30 : 1, v/v/v) for neutral lipids and acetone/toluene/water (91 : 30 : 7, v/v/v) for polar lipids. Plates were briefly exposed to iodine vapor for visualization, and lipid species were identified based on their migration patterns against known standards. Individual lipid spots were scraped from the TLC plates and transesterified to generate fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) by incubation in 2.5% (v/v) sulfuric acid in methanol at 80°C for 1 h. After cooling, 1 ml each of hexane and 0.9% (w/v) NaCl were added, followed by vortexing and centrifugation at RT. The upper organic layer was collected, dried under nitrogen, and reconstituted in hexane for analysis. FAMEs were analyzed using a gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) system (Agilent 5975C; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a DB‐23 capillary column (60 m × 250 μm; J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). Quantification was performed using heptadecanoic acid as an internal standard as described by Li et al. (2006).

In vivo acetate labeling

Leaf tissue was radiolabeled with 14C‐acetate using a protocol adapted from Fan et al. (2013). Fully expanded, actively growing leaves were collected from 3‐wk‐old Arabidopsis plants and cut into uniform strips. These strips were incubated on a shaking water bath at 22°C and illuminated at a light intensity of 60 μmol m−2 s−1. The incubation medium (10 ml) consisted of 1 mM unlabeled sodium acetate, 20 mM MES buffer (pH 5.5), one‐tenth strength MS salts, and 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20 to facilitate wetting.

Radiolabeling was initiated by adding 0.1 mCi of [1–14C] sodium acetate (specific activity: 106 mCi mmol−1; American Radiolabeled Chemicals, St. Louis, MO, USA) to the medium. After incubation for 1 h, leaf strips were rinsed thoroughly three times with deionized water to eliminate unincorporated radioactivity and gently blotted dry using filter paper. For pulse‐chase experiments, the labeled tissue was transferred to fresh medium that lacked radioactive acetate but was otherwise identical in composition and incubated under the same temperature and light conditions. Lipids were extracted and separated as described above. The incorporation of the radiolabel into total lipids and individual lipid classes was quantified by liquid scintillation counting. Pulse‐chase labeling experiments were conducted under constant light to enable comparison of intrinsic TAG turnover rates across genotypes. Radiolabel was quantified in total lipid extracts and resolved lipid classes; however, radioactivity in the aqueous fraction was not systematically measured; total label recovery across all fractions was therefore not determined.

Assessment of plant growth

To evaluate plant growth, seeds from the wild‐type (WT) and mutant lines (single, double, and triple mutants) were germinated on ½MS medium as described above. After 10 d of growth, seedlings were transferred to pots (12 plants per pot) containing the same soil mixture used in prior experiments. The pots were randomly arranged in a walk‐in growth chamber maintained under the environmental conditions described above. Plants were grown for an additional 18 or 30 d, after which shoot fresh weight was recorded. Measurements were taken from 20 to 30 individual plants per genotype. Each experiment included three randomly selected pots per genotype, which were treated as independent biological replicates. Growth assessments were performed in at least five independent trials, all performed under comparable conditions and yielding consistent results.

Gas exchange measurements

Arabidopsis WT plants and single, double, and triple mutants were grown in 3‐oz cups under a 16 h : 8 h light : dark photoperiod at 22°C and a light intensity of 180 μmol m−2 s−1. Twenty‐day‐old plants with a green leaf area > 5 cm2 were used for whole‐plant gas exchange measurements using a LI‐6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI‐COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) equipped with an Arabidopsis small plant chamber (6800‐17) and a large light source (6800‐03). To ensure that only the rosette was exposed for gas exchange, the soil was covered with a plastic sheet before the plant was placed inside the chamber, thus avoiding the influence of soil respiration. Chamber conditions were set to 25°C leaf temperature, 50–55% relative humidity, 800 μmol s−1 airflow rate, 0.1 kPa chamber pressure, and a mixing fan at optimum speed. Plants were acclimated for 10 min before initiation of the response curve programs. For light response curves, net CO2 assimilation (A) was measured at an optimum CO2 level of 400 ppm across decreasing intensities of actinic light (R90B10): 1500, 1200, 1000, 800, 600, 300, 150, 50, and 0 μmol m−2 s−1 with minimum and maximum wait times of 60 s and 180 s, respectively. CO2 response curves (A/C i curves) were obtained at a saturating light intensity of 1000 μmol m−2 s−1 with stepwise adjustments of chamber CO2 concentration to 400, 300, 200, 100, 25, 400, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1300, 1600, and 1800 μmol mol−1 with minimum and maximum wait times of 60 s and 120 s, respectively. Measurements were performed independently on 5–6 biological replicates within the first 5 h of the light period. To estimate photosynthetic parameters (Vcmax, J max, and R d), A/C i curves were analyzed using the Farquhar, von Caemmerer, and Berry (1980) model, and model fitting was performed in R (v.4.4.2) using the plantecophys package (Duursma, 2015).

Measurement of Chl fluorescence

Twenty‐day‐old plants from the WT and single, double, and triple mutants were grown as described above and dark‐adapted for 30 min before fluorescence measurements. Chl fluorescence parameters, namely minimum fluorescence (F o), maximum fluorescence (F m), and nonphotochemical quenching (NPQ), were measured using a FluorCam instrument (Photon Systems Instruments, Czechia, Czech Republic) following standard protocols (Baker, 2008). In brief, F o was recorded for 5 s in darkness, followed by a saturating pulse (800 ms) to determine the maximum fluorescence yield (F m). During actinic illumination, steady‐state fluorescence (F t′) and maximum fluorescence under light‐adapted condition (F m′) were recorded using saturating pulses at each light intensity. NPQ was calculated as the difference between the maximum and steady‐state fluorescence yields under actinic light (Bilger & Bjorkman, 1990). All fluorescence measurements were performed on three to four biological replicates.

Measurement of Chl and soluble sugars

Total Chl was extracted from the rosette leaves of 21‐d‐old soil‐grown WT and mutant plants using 80% (v/v) acetone and quantified spectrophotometrically according to Wellburn (1994). For analysis of soluble sugar contents, tissues from WT and mutant plants grown either in soil or on Petri plates with or without 1% (w/v) sucrose were harvested 6–8 h into the light period. Soluble sugars were extracted and quantified using a commercial assay kit (ab65334; Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, glucose content was measured enzymatically using glucose oxidase, which generates hydrogen peroxide that reacts with a glucose probe to produce resorufin, detected colorimetrically at 570 nm. For determination of sucrose content, extracts were incubated with invertase to hydrolyze sucrose into glucose, and the released glucose was quantified using the same assay as above; sucrose content was calculated by subtracting free glucose from total glucose.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM, leaf tissues were fixed in 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h, followed by postfixation in 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide in the same buffer for 2 h at room temperature. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in EPON812 resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA), and sectioned. Ultrathin sections were stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined under a JEOL JEM‐1400 LaB6 transmission electron microscope operated at 120 kV (JEOL Inc., Peabody, MA, USA). Chloroplast ultrastructure was examined from multiple independent biological replicates. For each genotype, 5–8 chloroplasts per plant and at least three independent plants were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the sample mean, with error bars representing the SD. Statistical significance of differences among genotypes was determined using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) assuming data with equal variances, followed by appropriate post hoc multiple‐comparison tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 and are indicated by different letters. All statistical analyses were performed using graphpad prism (v.10.6.1).

Results

Disruption of starch biosynthesis results in significantly lower TAG accumulation in leaves

Under standard growth conditions, photosynthetic tissues of WT plants typically accumulate only trace amounts of TAG, making it challenging to investigate the metabolic fate and physiological role of this lipid class. To circumvent this limitation, we used the tgd1 mutant, which accumulates TAG in its leaves due to impaired lipid trafficking from the ER to chloroplasts (Xu et al., 2005). To examine the possible relationship between starch biosynthesis and TAG metabolism, we generated double mutants by crossing tgd1 with two well‐characterized starch‐deficient lines: adg1 and pgm1. For adg1, we included the two independent alleles adg1‐1 and adg1‐2 to improve reproducibility and rule out effects from a possible second‐site mutation in either mutant background. We grew the WT, single mutants (tgd1, adg1‐1, adg1‐2, and pgm1), and double mutants (tgd1 adg1‐1, tgd1 adg1‐2, and tgd1 pgm1) under a long‐day photoperiod (16 : 8 h light : dark) and collected leaf tissues 8 h into the light period at the mature rosette stage. Initial TLC screening revealed a marked drop in TAG levels in all double mutants compared to tgd1 (Fig. 1a). Similarly, TAG content was slightly lower in the adg1‐1, adg1‐2, and pgm1 single mutants than in the WT. We confirmed this qualitative assessment by quantitative GC‐MS analysis, which revealed significantly lower TAG content in all starch‐deficient backgrounds, regardless of the loss of TGD1 function (Fig. 1b).

Enhanced TAG turnover in starch‐deficient mutants

TAG can function as a respiratory carbon source in the absence of starch, particularly at night (Yu et al., 2018). We therefore hypothesized that the lower TAG levels in the tgd1 adg1 and tgd1 pgm1 double mutants may have resulted from greater nocturnal TAG degradation. To test this possibility, we quantified TAG levels at two key diurnal time points: dusk and subsequent dawn. TAG levels were indeed significantly lower in the tgd1 adg1‐1, tgd1 adg1‐2, and tgd1 pgm1 double mutants than in the tgd1 single mutant when samples were collected at dusk (Fig. 2a), consistent with our midday measurements (Fig. 1a,b). By the next dawn, TAG levels had declined to very low levels in all genotypes, and there were no statistically significant differences among them (Fig. 2b).

(a) TAG levels at the end of the light period. (b) TAG levels at the onset of the light period. (c) Changes in TAG levels during the dark period following the light phase. (d) Calculated rates of TAG breakdown derived from the time‐course data shown in (c). Data represent means ± SD of three to five independent biological replicates. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences among genotypes (one‐way ANOVA, P < 0.05).

To examine the biochemical basis underlying the reduced TAG accumulation observed in starch‐deficient tgd1 backgrounds compared with tgd1 (Fig. 2a), we monitored TAG levels over time following the transition from light to darkness (Fig. 2c). In the tgd1 single mutant, TAG levels declined gradually during the dark period. By contrast, the starch‐deficient tgd1 adg1 and tgd1 pgm1 double mutants exhibited a markedly accelerated drop in TAG levels, with a steeper decline observed during the first 2 h into darkness than that seen for tgd1. TAG levels in these genotypes approached substantially low earlier than in tgd1, after which little further change was detected. When we calculated TAG degradation rates based on this time course of TAG levels, we obtained significantly faster TAG degradation rates in the starch‐deficient tgd1 adg1 and tgd1 pgm1 double mutants than in tgd1 (Fig. 2d). Together, these results suggest that loss of starch enhances the rate of TAG turnover in the tgd1 background, consistent with a greater reliance on TAG as a respiratory carbon source when transitory starch is unavailable.

Because TAG levels did not recover by dawn, we hypothesized that the lower TAG accumulation might reflect greater TAG turnover, impaired TAG biosynthesis, or both during the light period. To dissect TAG dynamics in more detail, we performed [14C]‐acetate pulse‐chase labeling experiments using detached rosette leaves from plants grown in soil under long‐day conditions. Leaves were pulse‐labeled for 1 h, and radiolabeled TAG was monitored over a 3‐d chase period. The rates of label incorporation into TAG were constant during the pulse (Supporting Information Fig. S1) and were significantly higher in the adg1 and pgm1 single mutants than in the WT (Fig. 3a). Likewise, the incorporation rate of the radiolabel was higher in the tgd1 adg1 and tgd1 pgm1 double mutants than in tgd1 when grown in soil (Fig. 3a). However, the decline in radiolabeled TAG after the chase period was more pronounced in all starch‐deficient mutants (i.e. adg1‐1, adg1‐2, pgm1, tgd1 adg1‐1, tgd1 adg1‐2, and tgd1 pgm1) than in their respective controls (Fig. 3b). This pattern suggests that enhanced TAG degradation is a general feature of starch‐deficient mutants, regardless of the specific defect in the starch biosynthetic pathway. There were no differences in label incorporation into phosphatidylcholine (PC) (Fig. S2a), monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) (Fig. S2b) or the remaining membrane lipids (Fig. S2c) in the double mutants compared to tgd1. It should be noted that although radiolabel redistribution into nonlipid fractions and metabolic recycling cannot be fully excluded over the extended chase period, such effects are expected to occur across all genotypes. Therefore, the consistently accelerated decline in labeled TAG observed in starch‐deficient mutants relative to controls provides strong evidence for enhanced TAG turnover in these backgrounds. Together, these results suggest that higher TAG turnover represents a consistent metabolic response to starch deficiency rather than a consequence of a specific enzymatic defect in the starch biosynthetic pathway. The lower TAG content observed in starch‐deficient mutants is therefore most consistent with enhanced TAG degradation, occurring during the day and night, rather than a primary limitation in TAG biosynthesis.

Sucrose application modulates TAG levels in starch‐deficient mutants

The higher TAG turnover observed in starch‐deficient mutants is likely a consequence of carbon starvation at night. To test this hypothesis, we grew seedlings of WT, adg1‐1, adg1‐2, pgm1, tgd1, and the double mutants tgd1 adg1‐1, tgd1 adg1‐2, and tgd1 pgm1 on ½MS medium alone or with 1% (w/v) sucrose supplementation under a long‐day photoperiod. Lipid profiling of these seedlings revealed that TAG levels are significantly lower in the double mutants relative to the tgd1 single mutant and in pgm1 relative to the WT in the absence of sucrose treatment (Fig. 3c). Supplementation with 1% sucrose led to a marked rise in TAG levels across all double mutants compared to their single‐mutant counterparts on sucrose‐free medium (Fig. 3d). These findings suggest that sucrose supplementation can partially alleviate the carbon deficit suffered by starch‐deficient genotypes, thereby suppressing the catabolic breakdown of TAG.

To directly test whether sucrose supplementation affects TAG turnover, we performed [14C]‐acetate pulse‐chase labeling experiments to quantify TAG biosynthesis and degradation rates. Under sucrose‐free conditions, the starchless mutants exhibited significantly higher rates of TAG biosynthesis than the WT (Fig. S3a), suggesting a compensatory increase in lipid biosynthesis under endogenous carbon limitation. However, when sucrose was supplied, TAG biosynthesis rates were similar across all genotypes. Likewise, TAG degradation was significantly accelerated in starchless single and double mutants under sucrose‐free conditions, but this faster turnover was suppressed in the presence of 1% sucrose (Fig. S3b). Taken together, these results indicate that the lower TAG accumulation of starch‐deficient mutants under sucrose‐free conditions is associated with greater TAG turnover. While sucrose supplementation suppresses TAG degradation and partially restores TAG levels to WT levels, these findings, in combination with our measurements at different times along a diurnal cycle and pulse‐chase labeling analysis, support the conclusion that enhanced TAG turnover is a major contributor to the diminished TAG accumulation observed in these mutants.

To assess whether the above sucrose‐dependent effects on TAG metabolism are linked to changes in soluble carbohydrate pools, we quantified glucose and sucrose levels in WT, single, double, and triple mutants grown on ½MS medium alone or with 1% (w/v) sucrose supplementation. In the absence of sucrose treatment, glucose content remained broadly comparable among WT, sdp1, tgd1, and the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant. However, glucose content increased in adg1‐1 and tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant compared to WT (Fig. S4a). Importantly, starchless single and double mutants did not show a marked depletion of their soluble sugars relative to WT (Fig. S4b).

Supplementation with 1% sucrose led to significantly lower endogenous glucose levels across all genotypes compared to WT as well as lower sucrose levels in sdp1 and adg1‐1 and higher sucrose content in other genotypes compared to WT (Fig. S4a,b). There was no clear genotype‐specific accumulation of glucose or sucrose in starch‐deficient mutants following sucrose supplementation, suggesting that sucrose treatment did not preferentially raise free sugar pools in these mutants. This consistent elevation in soluble sugar contrasts with the pronounced, genotype‐dependent restoration of TAG levels to WT levels observed under the same conditions (Fig. 3c). Together, these results suggest that while sucrose supplementation elevates the levels of soluble carbohydrates in a largely genotype‐independent manner, it selectively restores TAG accumulation to WT levels in starch‐deficient mutants. This dissociation suggests that altered TAG homeostasis in starch‐deficient backgrounds is not simply explained by changes in steady‐state glucose or sucrose levels but is more consistent with carbon status–dependent modulation of TAG turnover.

Under constant light conditions, carbon starvation during subjective night is not expected. Accordingly, if the accelerated TAG degradation observed in starchless mutants results from carbon starvation that drives TAG utilization as an alternative carbon source, no differences in TAG levels between starchless mutants and the WT would be anticipated. Consistent with this idea, TAG levels did not differ among the WT and the starchless single mutants or among tgd1 and the double mutants when grown under constant light conditions (Fig. S5).

Inhibition of TAG turnover enhances TAG accumulation but impairs plant growth in starch‐deficient backgrounds

The lipase SDP1 is a key enzyme responsible for initiating cytosolic TAG hydrolysis in Arabidopsis (Eastmond, 2006; Kelly et al., 2013). To genetically assess the role of SDP1 in TAG degradation in starch‐deficient backgrounds and to test the hypothesis that these mutants experience accelerated TAG turnover, we generated a tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant by crossing the sdp1‐4 null allele (Fan et al., 2014) into the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant, followed by genotyping of the progeny. Strikingly, disruption of SDP1 function in the starchless tgd1 adg1‐1 background dramatically raised foliar TAG accumulation over ninefold relative to that in the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant (Fig. 4a). The substantially higher TAG content in the triple mutant strongly supports the notion that the lower TAG levels observed in tgd1 adg1‐1 plants were largely the result of SDP1‐dependent TAG degradation. This genetic evidence suggests that SDP1‐mediated TAG degradation serves as a compensatory pathway to meet the respiratory and metabolic energy demands under starch‐limited conditions. However, this metabolic adjustment incurs a physiological cost. Compared to the single and double mutants, the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant exhibited severely stunted growth and lower biomass (Fig. 4b,c), likely due to the inability to mobilize either transient starch or TAG reserves, thus imposing limitations on energy availability. Similar trade‐offs between growth and metabolism have been reported previously, where altered TAG turnover is associated with impaired photosynthetic performance (Johnson et al., 2025).

Effect of TAG accumulation on net CO2 assimilation in starchless mutants

To further assess the physiological consequences of disrupted TAG breakdown in starch‐deficient plants, we measured the photosynthetic activity of the WT, the tgd1 and adg1‐1 single mutants, the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant, and the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant. Because TAG degradation appeared to compensate for lower carbohydrate availability in starchless backgrounds, we hypothesized that blocking TAG breakdown in starchless mutants might preserve internal carbon reserves and alter photosynthetic efficiency by alleviating the feedback inhibition caused by sugar accumulation. To test this idea, we obtained a comprehensive set of plant gas exchange measurements at various light intensities and CO2 levels (Fig. 5a,b). The sdp1 and tgd1 single mutants had significantly lower light‐saturated net CO2 assimilation rates, reaching saturation at 600–800 μmol photons m−2 s−1 (photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD)) compared to 1000 μmol photons m−2 s−1 for adg1‐1 and the WT (Fig. 5a). Net CO2 assimilation was comparable among the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant and the adg1‐1 and tgd1 single mutants under low or moderate irradiance and at ambient CO2 concentrations, while the triple mutant showed improved assimilation under CO2‐saturated conditions (Fig. 5b). These results suggest that the biochemical reorganization in the triple mutant preserves photosynthetic capacity more effectively than in the adg1 and tgd1 single mutants.

To quantify photosynthetic capacity, we fitted A/C i response curves to the Farquhar‐von Caemmerer‐Berry (FvCB) model using the R package plantecophys, estimating the maximum Rubisco carboxylation rate (Vcmax), maximum electron transport rate (J max), and day respiration (R d) (Fig. 5c–e). The starchless mutant adg1‐1 exhibited significantly lower Vcmax (53.8 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1) and J max (92.0 μmol electrons m−2 s−1) than the WT (68.3 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 and 196.2 μmol electrons m−2 s−1, respectively). These lower values result from feedback inhibition of photosynthesis by sugar accumulation or from impaired triose phosphate turnover rather than an effect of lower TAG content itself. The tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant showed photosynthetic performance broadly similar to that of adg1‐1, with a comparable Vcmax and higher J max, but higher R d (9.74 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1), indicating elevated respiratory carbon loss that likely lowers net CO2 assimilation despite maintaining biochemical capacity of carboxylation and RuBP regeneration, respectively. The tgd1 mutant also demonstrated substantially lower Vcmax (42.9 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1) and J max (101.6 μmol electrons m−2 s−1) values, consistent with impaired ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) activity and ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate (RuBP) regeneration associated with disrupted lipid trafficking. The lower R d (R d (5.43 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1) in tgd1 compared to WT (6.79 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1)) highlights an altered respiratory carbon flux but does not necessarily indicate limited carbon assimilation due to impaired lipid trafficking alone. These results suggest that disrupting TAG breakdown in the starchless adg1‐1 background appears to mitigate feedback inhibition caused by sugar accumulation by redirecting carbon into TAG storage. In agreement with this idea, the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant remained photosynthetically impaired, with a Vcmax of 53.2 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 and a higher R d of 9.74 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1, indicative of greater respiratory demand compared to the adg1‐1 and tgd1 single mutants. Strikingly, the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant had the highest J max of all mutants (171.4 μmol electrons m−2 s−1), as well as a robust Vcmax of 61.0 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1. Simultaneous disruption of ER‐to‐chloroplast lipid transport (tgd1) and TAG breakdown (sdp1) in the starchless adg1‐1 background enhanced photosynthetic capacity, but only under CO2‐saturating conditions, as reflected by the higher Vcmax and J max values of the triple mutant. Under suboptimal or ambient CO2, however, the triple mutant showed significantly lower net assimilation, likely due to elevated respiratory CO2 release (R d = 12.30 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1).

The elevated respiratory CO2 release observed in the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant further suggested a shift in the nature of respiratory substrates under starch‐limited conditions. In the absence of transitory starch, mitochondrial ATP production must increasingly depend on alternative carbon sources that likely include enhanced sucrose turnover and, potentially, amino‐acid catabolism, both of which can sustain respiration under conditions of carbon imbalance. Consistent with this view, our data demonstrate that both the double mutants and the triple mutant accumulate significantly higher sucrose levels than the WT under both sucrose‐supplemented and sucrose‐free conditions (Fig. S4a,b). This accumulation indicates that sucrose serves as a readily available respiratory substrate in these genotypes. We therefore propose that the elevated daytime respiration observed in the triple mutant is driven, at least in part, by greater sucrose catabolism to meet cellular ATP demands in the absence of starch reserves. This metabolic shift provides a mechanistic explanation for the higher respiratory flux in the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant despite enhanced TAG accumulation and highlights the importance of soluble carbohydrate pools in maintaining energy homeostasis when canonical carbon storage pathways are disrupted.

Photoprotective mechanism in starchless mutants

To evaluate photosystem II (PSII) function, we analyzed Chl fluorescence by measuring NPQ in the different genotypes (Fig. 6a,b). The tgd1 single mutant exhibited higher NPQ values (1.13) than all mutants and WT, consistent with its lower Chl content (Fig. 6c) and visibly pale‐green leaf phenotype (Fig. 4c), suggesting impaired thylakoid membrane integrity and altered PSII organization (Xu et al., 2005). These defects likely contribute to higher NPQ as a photoprotective response. The tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant showed slightly higher NPQ than adg1‐1, indicating partial compensation for excess excitation energy in the starchless background with disrupted lipid transport. By contrast, NPQ in the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant reached WT levels, indicating that PSII photoprotection was largely restored despite metabolic constraints limiting net assimilation. The sdp1 single mutant also exhibited normal NPQ values, consistent with minimal effects on PSII photoprotection. Overall, these results suggest that blocking TAG breakdown in a starchless mutant background brings about normal PSII energy dissipation and maintains photoprotective function.

Disruption of TAG turnover alters chloroplast ultrastructure and Chl content in starch‐deficient backgrounds

To examine whether altered TAG metabolism influences chloroplast structure, we analyzed chloroplast ultrastructure by TEM (Fig. 7a). Chloroplasts in the leaf mesophyll cells of WT and sdp1 plants exhibited well‐organized thylakoid membranes with clearly defined grana stacks and stromal lamellae. By contrast, chloroplasts in the mesophyll cells of the starch‐deficient adg1‐1 mutant displayed a disorganized thylakoid architecture and diminished membrane stacking. Similarly, the tgd1 mutant showed aberrant thylakoid organization, likely reflecting disrupted lipid trafficking required for proper thylakoid membrane biogenesis. The tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant exhibited more severe alterations in chloroplast ultrastructure, including pronounced distortion of thylakoid membranes and lighter internal membrane density. Plastoglobule abundance was reduced in starchless mutant background compared with the corresponding sdp1 and tgd1 single mutants and WT (Fig. S6). Strikingly, simultaneous disruption of TAG breakdown in the starch‐deficient and lipid trafficking–impaired tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant partially restored thylakoid organization, with improved membrane continuity and stacking relative to the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant and WT levels (Fig. 7b–d).

Consistent with the ultrastructural observations above, quantification of total Chl content revealed significant genotype‐specific differences (Fig. 6c). Total Chl content was markedly lower in adg1‐1, tgd1, and tgd1 adg1‐1 compared to WT, indicating that starch deficiency and impaired lipid trafficking both negatively affect Chl accumulation. By contrast, sdp1 maintained Chl levels comparable to WT. Notably, Chl content returned to WT levels in the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant and was higher than in the starch‐deficient single and double mutants, suggesting that inhibition of TAG hydrolysis is associated with alleviation of Chl loss in this genetic background. Together, these data demonstrate that impaired TAG turnover in a starch‐deficient background partially restores chloroplast structural integrity and total Chl content. This recovery is consistent with the enhanced biochemical photosynthetic capacity (higher Vcmax and J max) observed in the tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant under CO2‐saturating conditions (Fig. 5b), supporting the idea that redirecting carbon into TAG storage can mitigate carbon stress–associated defects in chloroplast function, even though net CO2 assimilation remains constrained under growth‐relevant conditions by elevated carbon loss through respiration.

Discussion

In photosynthetic organisms, starch and lipid biosynthesis are antagonistic biochemical programs that compete for shared carbon precursors such as glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate and acetyl‐CoA. In recent years, this intrinsic metabolic trade‐off has been leveraged to develop a widely used approach for engineering TAG accumulation in vegetative tissues (Vanhercke et al., 2019; Park et al., 2025). One common strategy involves redirecting carbon flux from starch biosynthesis to TAG production. This strategy has proven effective in raising TAG levels in vegetative tissues and is being actively used to develop crops with higher energy density for bioenergy applications (Sanjaya et al., 2011; Chapman et al., 2013; Vanhercke et al., 2014). However, the effect of starch elimination extends beyond a straightforward shift in carbon partitioning. Emerging evidence indicates that, in the absence of starch, plants increasingly depend upon alternative carbon and energy reserves, such as lipids and proteins, to sustain respiration and growth (Smith & Stitt, 2007; Sulpice et al., 2009; Geigenberger, 2011; Stitt & Zeeman, 2012; Yu et al., 2018). In this metabolic context, TAGs do not function merely as inert storage compounds but rather as a dynamic energy reserve that is actively mobilized to meet metabolic demands. Notably, our previous work indicated that inhibition of starch biosynthesis leads to substantially higher lipid turnover, likely as a compensatory mechanism to make up for the loss of starch‐derived carbon and energy (Yu et al., 2018). This concurrent acceleration of both TAG synthesis and degradation reveals an enhanced flux through an active futile cycle in the starchless mutant. This enhanced lipid turnover may counterbalance the benefits of redirecting carbon flux toward TAG biosynthesis, thereby constraining net TAG accumulation in vegetative tissues such as leaves.

In this study, we disrupted starch biosynthesis in the tgd1 mutant background with high oil content to examine the metabolic relationship between starch and TAG in vegetative tissues. Unexpectedly, soil‐grown plants exhibited significantly lower TAG levels, which we attributed to greater TAG turnover rather than to impaired biosynthesis. These results contrast with established metabolic engineering strategies that aim to enhance TAG accumulation by redirecting carbon flux away from starch biosynthesis (Sanjaya et al., 2011). Instead, our findings are consistent with metabolic engineering studies demonstrating that leaf starch serves as a key precursor for TAG accumulation in the leaves of transgenic tobacco plants, concomitant with a drop in foliar starch levels (Chu et al., 2022). Our observations also suggest that in the absence of starch, plants use TAG as a source of metabolic energy instead of storing it as a carbon sink, challenging the assumption that starch inhibition intrinsically promotes TAG biosynthesis and storage. Supporting this view is our observation that TAG, like starch, functions as a dynamic carbon reserve, especially under carbon‐limiting conditions such as darkness or physiological stress. That TAG levels rose but did not return to WT levels after sucrose supplementation in starch‐deficient double mutants (tgd1 adg1‐1 and tgd1 pgm1) emphasizes the conditional stability of TAG and highlights the importance of metabolic context in regulating lipid turnover. While the present study focuses on biochemical and metabolic analyses, future work integrating transcriptomic profiling, particularly across diurnal cycles, will be important to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying the observed changes in carbon partitioning and lipid metabolism.

Although TAG is predominantly stored in cytosolic LDs, this study did not directly distinguish between TAG pools associated with LDs and those associated with electron‐dense plastoglobuli. Since plastoglobuli primarily function in thylakoid lipid remodeling and typically contain relatively minor TAG amounts (van Wijk & Kessler, 2017), the observed changes are most likely attributable to TAG metabolism in cytosolic LDs. Future studies that integrate ultrastructural imaging with subcellular lipid profiling, such as fluorescent lipid probes or other techniques, will be necessary to clarify the relative contributions of these compartments.

The diurnal dynamics of LD abundance introduce additional complexity to starch metabolism. The present results indicate that this diurnal coordination between starch and lipid metabolism is fundamentally disrupted in starch‐deficient mutants (Fig. 2). In these mutants, TAG turnover is significantly enhanced, suggesting a greater dependence on lipid reserves as alternative carbon and energy sources than in WT (Fan et al., 2019). This metabolic shift may disrupt the typical diurnal accumulation of LDs and promote sustained lipid mobilization rather than transient storage (Yu et al., 2018). Although LD abundance was not directly measured in this study, the observed acceleration of TAG degradation and its strong dependence on carbon availability are consistent with altered LD dynamics under starch‐deficient conditions (Fan et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings support a model in which LDs and TAG serve as adaptable components of the plant carbon buffering system, particularly when starch‐based storage is impaired. Future studies combining live‐cell LD imaging along with metabolic flux analysis will be necessary to explain the temporal coordination between LD dynamics, TAG turnover, and carbon allocation throughout the diurnal cycle.

The apparent discrepancy between the rapid breakdown of bulk TAG we observed (Figs 2 and 3) and the slower decay of 14C‐labeled TAG suggests that TAG serves as a highly active metabolic node rather than a terminal storage sink. Following a 1‐h pulse, most radiolabel was associated with PC and MGDG (Xu et al., 2003), both of which have been shown to undergo extensive acyl editing (Bates et al., 2007; Hurlock et al., 2018), thus creating a sustained source of 14C‐labeled fatty acids that continuously enter and exit the TAG pool throughout the chase period. Therefore, even as the bulk TAG pool is turned over, these membrane‐derived fatty acids are re‐esterified into TAG, effectively ‘masking’ the true rate of degradation when measured based on radioactivity incorporation alone. In the absence of starch, the nighttime degradation of LDs is likely exacerbated as the plant undergoes more intensive membrane remodeling to compensate for the lack of transient carbohydrate stores. The ‘masking’ effect we observed in our 14C‐labeling data supports the model that these nighttime LDs are not stagnant but are active participants in an acyl‐recycling loop with the membrane.

The adg1‐1 mutant accumulates excess cytosolic sugars, which impose feedback inhibition on photosynthetic electron transport (Kunz et al., 2010; Schmitz et al., 2012). Our gas exchange measurements corroborated these observations, showing lower CO2 assimilation, Vcmax, and J max values. In starch‐deficient mutant backgrounds, starch deficiency causes inorganic phosphate limitation within chloroplasts, diminishing ATP and NADPH production and further compromising CBB cycle activity and photosynthetic efficiency (Hausler et al., 2009). This energetic imbalance causes over‐reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain, triggering enhanced NPQ and raising the likelihood of photoinhibition and oxidative stress. These effects are compounded by structural changes in the chloroplasts, including thylakoid hyperstacking and downsizing of chloroplasts, as well as alterations in membrane lipid composition (Walters et al., 2004; Hausler et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2018). Our results demonstrate that inhibition of TAG turnover in a starchless mutant background alleviates these limitations. This finding is reflected in enhanced photosynthetic capacity, as evidenced by higher Vcmax and J max, together with lower NPQ.

In the WT, elevated Vcmax and J max values indicate a highly efficient photosynthetic apparatus in which carbon assimilation and electron transport are tightly coordinated. By contrast, each of the single mutants, adg1‐1 (starch‐deficient), tgd1 (defective in lipid trafficking), and sdp1 (TAG lipase–deficient), showed lower values for these photosynthetic parameters. The adg1‐1 mutant, which lacks functional ADP‐glucose pyrophosphorylase, exhibited lower Vcmax and J max, likely caused by feedback inhibition from accumulated soluble sugars and triose phosphates that were not directed into starch biosynthesis (Caspar et al., 1985; Lin et al., 1988). Similarly, the tgd1 mutant, with disrupted lipid trafficking from the ER to plastids (Xu et al., 2003, 2005), had significantly lower Vcmax due to impaired thylakoid membrane formation (Fig. 7) and associated effects on chloroplast function (Xu et al., 2005). This result suggests that TAG turnover is not as important in maintaining photosynthetic function in vegetative tissues under nonstress conditions (Kelly et al., 2011).

The sdp1 mutant exhibited significantly lower R d than other genotypes, suggesting less pronounced respiratory carbon loss due to impaired TAG turnover. This impaired turnover is likely to limit the recycling of fatty acids into sugars, thus limiting the accumulation of cytosolic carbohydrates that can trigger feedback inhibition of photosynthesis. This interpretation is consistent with findings by Johnson et al. (2025), wherein enhanced TAG turnover in the leaves of tobacco plants drives fatty acid catabolism and glyoxylate‐mediated sugar production, resulting in a lower photosynthetic rate. Consistent with this hypothesis, the sdp1 single and tgd1 sdp1 adg1‐1 triple mutant had lower NPQ and higher J max values than tgd1‐1 and the tgd1 adg1‐1 double mutant.

Fig. 8 presents a working model in which starch and TAG represent interconnected and dynamically regulated carbon storage pools in leaves. In WT plants (Fig. 8a), photosynthetically fixed carbon is predominantly partitioned into transient starch synthesis in the chloroplast, while a smaller fraction is directed toward ER‐associated lipid metabolism, with TAG undergoing rapid turnover through balanced synthesis and SDP1‐mediated lipolysis. Starch, produced via ADG1‐dependent ADP‐glucose formation, serves as the major daytime carbon reserve and is remobilized at night to support respiration and growth, whereas TAG remains a minor, highly dynamic pool with limited net accumulation. In the tgd1 adg1 sdp1 triple mutant (Fig. 8b), carbon partitioning is profoundly reprogrammed: loss of ADG1 eliminates starch biosynthesis and its role as a transient carbon buffer, while disruption of TGD1 alters ER‐plastid lipid trafficking, collectively favoring enhanced flux of assimilated carbon toward fatty acid and TAG biosynthesis via the Kennedy pathway. In parallel, inactivation of SDP1 suppresses TAG mobilization, leading to sustained LD accumulation throughout the diurnal cycle. Consequently, carbon storage shifts from a flexible starch‐based system to a stable lipid‐based reservoir in which TAG becomes the dominant energy sink. This reconfiguration of carbon allocation and remobilization disrupts normal diurnal buffering, resulting in elevated TAG accumulation but compromised growth despite improved photosynthetic performance. Collectively, our findings underscore the dual role of TAG as both a transient carbon buffer and an energy reserve, and highlight that effective enhancement of vegetative oil accumulation requires coordinated regulation of carbon partitioning, storage stability, and remobilization rather than isolated manipulation of starch or lipid pathways.

From a biotechnological perspective, this study reveals key limitations in current TAG‐engineering paradigms. Simply enhancing lipid biosynthesis or repressing degradation in vegetative tissues may not lead to sustainable gains in energy density if carbon flux is not balanced with plant energy needs. Our findings argue for a more nuanced engineering strategy that coordinates both storage biosynthesis and mobilization, possibly through inducible systems or environmental cue–responsive promoter constructs. Future investigations should examine combinatorial strategies that fine‐tune carbon allocation between carbon storage and metabolic function. For example, partial suppression of starch biosynthesis, combined with controlled activation of TAG biosynthesis or conditional control of TAG turnover, may offer more physiologically balanced outcomes. Further exploration of the signaling networks that detect and respond to carbohydrate status, particularly under starch‐deficient conditions, is warranted. Collectively, our findings advance the current understanding of metabolic interactions between starch and lipid pathways and suggest a framework for designing metabolically balanced, energy‐dense crops.
