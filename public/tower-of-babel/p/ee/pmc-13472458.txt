Evaluation of the relationship between the severity of periodontal disease and carotid artery calcification on panoramic radiographs: A case–control study
Authors: Yasamin Barakian, Elham Keykha, Hamed Rahimi, Mohammad Aghaali, Zahra-Sadat Mortazavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:According to the World Health Organization, periodontitis is the sixth most common disease in humans. Some evidence has pointed out that a relationship exists between periodontal disease and some systemic diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases. Given that cardiovascular events are predicted to be one of the leading causes of death by 2030, we decided to investigate the relationship between the severity of periodontitis and carotid artery calcification (CAC) on panoramic radiographs.Materials and Methods:A total of 132 individuals (66 CAC + and 66 CAC−) were included in this case–control study. Panoramic radiographs of the patients were evaluated. Patients aged 30 years or older, who were dentate, who were suffering from periodontitis, and who did not have any systemic diseases or potential risk factors affecting CAC were included in the study. Clinical and radiographic assessments were used to determine the severity and activity of periodontitis (bleeding on probing, probing depth, clinical attachment loss, tooth loss, and alveolar bone loss).Results:Among CAC + individuals, 20 people had unilateral lesions and 46 had bilateral lesions; comparison of the severity of periodontitis showed no significance between them. The severity of periodontal disease was significantly higher in the CAC + individuals compared to the CAC − individuals (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the body mass index values were considerably higher in CAC − individuals (P = 0.032). There was no significant difference in the distribution of age, sex, smoking, alveolar bone loss, and number of lost teeth between the two groups.Conclusion:The results of the present study showed a significant correlation between the severity of periodontitis and CAC.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal disease is a common multifactorial disease in the mouth that is caused by bacteria, genetics, host immune and inflammatory responses, and environmental factors.[1] Among them, bacteria in biofilm are recognized as the most effective factor.[2] Gingivitis and periodontitis are considered the most common periodontal diseases.[3] Today, the impact of environmental factors, such as diabetes and smoking, on the development and severity of periodontitis is well accepted.[45] There is ample evidence of a link between periodontal disease and systemic diseases, including diabetes, renal, liver, and cardiovascular diseases, and even cognitive diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease.[36] Although periodontal disease can occur in almost anyone with teeth at any age, it is more common in adults over the age of 30 years.[7] Since tooth loss has decreased in the elderly, a higher prevalence of periodontitis is expected in the near future.[8]

Atherosclerosis is the most common cardiovascular disease and the primary cause of death worldwide, which can lead to stroke or myocardial infarction.[9] The activated endothelium, followed by a cascade of events including lipid accumulation, fibrosis elements, and calcification, causes narrowing of the blood vessels and activation of inflammatory pathways.[10] The injury to the endothelium by bacteria is possible directly as well as indirectly. Periodontal pathogens, their products, and inflammatory cells have the ability to move throughout the human body via the bloodstream or gastrointestinal system.[11] Chronic periodontal infection can intensify systemic inflammation through increased levels of interleukin 6, fibrinogen, and C-reactive protein and thus result in impaired endothelial function.[12] Plaque at the carotid bifurcation is responsible for the majority of strokes, and carotid artery atherosclerosis can be considered a vital systemic condition.[1314] Calcified carotid artery atheroma and periodontitis are both associated with an increased risk of heart disease.[15] Obesity, diabetes mellitus, male gender, and increasing age are believed to be the common risk factors for both heart disease and periodontitis.[1617] Whether the relationship between periodontitis and carotid artery calcification (CAC) is causal or not still requires further studies.[1718]

Angiography is the most accurate method for examining CAC and is an invasive procedure.[19] Other modalities are also used for its diagnosis, among which panoramic imaging is fast and economical and is commonly used in dentistry. Extracranial CACs that are seen as irregular and nodular radiopacities or vertical radiopaque lines on panoramic radiographs can be detected at the carotid bifurcation at the level of the C3–C4 vertebrae and approximately 1.5–2.5 cm inferior and posterior to the mandibular angle.[2021] A recent study by Möst et al. showed that panoramic modality with an accuracy of 90.6%, sensitivity of 67.5%, and specificity of 99.5% can be used as a reliable method for identifying CAC compared to cone beam computed tomography (CBCT).[22] The aim of this study was to investigate the association between the severity of periodontitis and CAC, both clinically and paracilinically using panoramic radiography.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study has a case–control design. After getting ethical approval (IR.MUQ.REC.1402.070), 132 patients, including 66 cases with calcification and 66 controls without calcification, were recruited for the study. The sample size was calculated based on comparing the number of lost teeth in the two groups with and without carotid calcification, and considering an alpha of 5% and a beta of 20%, equal to 66 people in each group. The inclusion criteria included having a panoramic radiograph, being 30 years of age or older, having teeth, and suffering from periodontitis. Individuals with the following exclusion criteria were removed from the study: any systemic disease, use of bone-affecting drugs such as calcium and bisphosphonates, cardiovascular disease, and history of stroke, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia.

Panoramic radiographs were taken using a Planmeca ProMax 2D unit manufactured in Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Uusimaa, Finland, with exposure settings of MA = 8 and kVp = 66. Under standard conditions, these radiographs were examined to identify calcification of the carotid artery, which appears as radiopaque lines or nodular masses in the vicinity or below the C3–C4 intervertebral region. To differentiate these calcifications from the hyoid bone, the triticeal cartilage, the styloid process, the thyroid cartilage, and the epiglottis, careful evaluation was performed. The radiographs were assessed for the presence or absence of CAC and whether it was unilateral or bilateral. For these assessments, the SCANORA® software was used to adjust brightness and contrast for optimal visualization of CAC and bone level. The radiographs were also visually assessed for the amount of alveolar bone loss and tooth loss (excluding third molars and with a maximum of 28 teeth). To assess the amount of alveolar bone loss, the remaining bone from the lowest point of the crestal margin to the apex of the tooth was measured and expressed as a fraction of the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the apex. The total amount of remaining alveolar bone for each tooth was calculated by averaging the measurements for the mesial and distal aspects of the tooth. Finally, individuals were categorized into three levels: healthy (remaining bone ≥80%), mild to moderate (66%–79%), and severe (<66%). If <80% of the bone remained, the individual was considered to have periodontitis. Teeth with unclear CEJ levels on the radiograph were excluded from the analysis of this variable.[23]

In the clinical evaluation, to determine the presence and severity of active periodontal disease in all study subjects, the following indices were used:

Probing depth (PD): Measured using a four-point system (mesial, mid-buccal, distal, and lingual aspects of the tooth)Clinical attachment loss (CAL): Measured using a four-point systemBleeding on probing (BOP): Measured using a six-point system. A Michigan “O” periodontal probe with Williams marking was used. Third molars, supernumerary teeth, and implants were excluded from the study.

In this study, all the patients’ teeth were initially considered for the above conditions to confirm periodontitis, and their information was recorded. However, during statistical analysis, only the teeth suggested by Ramfjord et al. were used, which included teeth #3 (right maxillary first molar), #9 (right maxillary central incisor), #12 (left maxillary first premolar), #19 (left mandibular first molar), #25 (right mandibular central incisor), and #28 (right mandibular first premolar).[24]

The independent samples t-test, Mann–Whitney U-test, Pearson Chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test were used. It should be mentioned that in this study, the above calculations were made by SPSS 26 (IBM Company, Armonk, New York, United States), and the significance level for all tests was considered to be 0.05.

The present study examined the relationship between BMI and CAC, finding a significant statistical difference in average BMI between individuals with and without CAC. Given that BMI could be a common risk factor for periodontitis and carotid plaque formation, further analysis aimed to account for this potential confounder. To do so, a backward logistic regression analysis was employed, incorporating variables like BMI. In the final model, however, BMI was excluded as it did not significantly contribute to the prediction of carotid plaque classification. This suggests that while BMI is an important variable related to both periodontal disease and carotid plaque, its impact on carotid plaque status can be explained by other factors when controlled for appropriately.

The results of the logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, after controlling for variables such as age, sex, BMI, and smoking, the PD index was significantly associated with the presence of CAC, with an odds ratio of 5.18 [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

The term atherosclerosis is derived from the Greek words “athero” meaning gruel and “sclerosis” meaning hardening.[25] The rupture of atherosclerotic plaque leads to end-stage events such as coronary thrombosis, acute myocardial infarction, and stroke.[26] The mechanism by which periodontal disease influences the formation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques has been investigated in recent decades. Several articles emphasize the importance of inflammation caused by periodontal disease and the role of cytokines, while other studies point to the direct role of periodontal bacteria.[11272829]

Friedlander and Lande first mentioned the presence of CAC in panoramic images in 1981.[30] A study by Gustafsson et al. in 2019 showed that a general dentist, with only 2 h of training, could achieve a diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of a radiology specialist in this regard.[19] In this study, we tried to match patients as much as possible in terms of age, gender, and smoking status. Therefore, there was no significant difference between the control and case groups. After the investigations, we observed a statistically significant correlation between clinical indicators of periodontal disease, including BOP (P = 0.010), clinical attachment level (P = 0.029), and periodontal pocket depth (P = <0.001), and CAC. Only a few studies have used clinical assessments to investigate this relationship, among which our study is consistent with the study of AlSakr et al. and inconsistent with the study of Çetin.[1231] AlSakr et al. evaluated BOP, CAL, PD, and plaque index as measures for periodontitis and used CBCT for evaluation of internal CAC (ICACs). They found a significant relationship between the status of chronic periodontitis, age, and gender and ICACs.[12] Çetin et al. used plaque index, gingival index, BOP, CAL, and PD to investigate periodontitis. They also evaluated other parameters such as gender, age, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia and concluded that only age was considerably related to CAC.[31]

We also used the collected data to have a comparison among the case group, and we concluded that there is no significant difference in the clinical variables (BOP, CAL, and PD) between the patients with unilateral and bilateral calcifications; this result maybe a consequence of low sample size in each group and studies with high number of unilateral and bilateral paitents may reach to a more meaningful comparison. To approach a comprehensive study, we also examined radiographic variables associated with the severity of periodontitis. As a result, we found no correlation between CAC and the amount of alveolar bone loss on panoramic radiographs. This lack of correlation was previously seen in Gustafson’s study.[15] This may be justified by considering the fact that in our study, teeth with an indistinguishable CEJ were excluded from the study. Even if a relationship is found between bone loss and CAC, bone loss does not always indicate a high degree of ongoing inflammation and may indicate inactive disease.[3233] On the subject of the relationship between tooth loss and cardiovascular events, different results have been obtained in different studies.[343536] In the present study, no correlation was found between tooth loss and CAC. Considering our hypothesis regarding the effect of periodontal infection and its direct and indirect pathways of influence on CAC, and considering that several studies have shown that Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis disappear with the extraction of all teeth and do not even reappear in the presence of a hard surface in the mouth such as a denture,[3738] we decided to exclude edentulous patients from our study, which may be a reason for the absence of this relationship. It should also be noted that increasing age is a common risk factor for periodontal disease,[39] CAC,[40] and tooth loss.[41] In our study and several other studies, the number of women in the case group was higher, although there was no significant difference compared to affected men.[3142] To remove the effect of obesity on the formation of vascular calcification as a confounding factor, we collected our data from patients with a BMI below 30. BMI was statistically higher in the control group compared to the case group. Although BMI has been used as a measure of obesity for years, BMI does not always indicate obesity. Rather, patients with more muscle mass may be mistakenly considered obese.[43] In addition, abdominal fat is more closely related to the prevalence of atherosclerotic diseases,[44] so other criteria such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, or visceral adipose tissue/subcutaneous adipose tissue ratio may provide more accurate results.[45]

CONCLUSION

There is a statistically significant correlation between the depth of periodontal pockets, BOP, and the loss of clinical attachment with CAC in panoramic images. Improving periodontal conditions may help prevent or reduce the severity of CAC. Further research with a larger sample size and the use of radiographs such as bitewings for accurate assessment of bone loss is needed.

Limitations

In this study, we were unable to differentiate the causes of tooth loss, as many patients did not know or could not recall the primary reason for their tooth loss. Knowing the cause of tooth loss could lead to a more accurate conclusion about the existence of a relationship between the number of lost teeth and CAC. To assess the amount of alveolar bone loss, we used visual examination of panoramic images. The use of vertical bitewing or parallel periapical images and measurement software could more accurately express the research results.
