Glycosylation modification: a trigger for obesity and its complications?
Authors: Changzan Wang, Wenying Yi, Xianghui Li, Xinyu Li, Meiying Wu, Wen Zhou, Maierhaba maimaitiaili, Dilare tuerhong, Nuerbiye Nuermaimaiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity and its related metabolic diseases have increasingly become a significant public health challenge threatening the health of populations worldwide. The pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to the progression of obesity are intricate and diverse, highlighting the need for the creation of novel and effective intervention strategies aimed at the treatment of obesity and its associated metabolic diseases. An increasing number of studies indicate that glycosylation modifications play a crucial role in the onset and progression of obesity and its complications. Studies on glycosylation not only reveal its involvement in regulating the mechanisms of obesity and its complications but also provide new avenues for targeted therapies. Although significant advancements have been made in understanding the fundamental molecular mechanisms by which glycosylation modifications affect obesity and its related complications, many questions remain unanswered. For instance, through which specific sites does glycosylation regulate the occurrence of obesity and its complications? Furthermore, do glycosylation modifications interact with other post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins in the regulation of obesity and its complications? What are the specific pathways of O-linked β-N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) signaling in obesity and its complications? This article summarizes recent research on the role of glycosylation in obesity and its complications, lists relevant specific glycosylation sites, and discusses the interactions between glycosylation modifications and other PTMs of proteins, providing new perspectives and insights for clinical treatment of obesity and its complications.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

In recent years, shifts in global dietary habits and lifestyle patterns have normalized the consumption of high-energy foods, directly contributing to a sustained increase in the global prevalence of overweight and obesity (Nicze et al. 2024). Evidence indicates that obesity is not merely an accumulation of body fat, but a chronic and complex metabolic disease characterized by pathological remodeling of adipose tissue and disruption of energy metabolic homeostasis (Yang et al. 2024; Weijie et al. 2024). This condition can induce multi-system metabolic dysfunction and is currently one of the most prevalent chronic non-communicable diseases worldwide (Nicze et al. 2024; Noubiap et al. 2000). It imposes the heaviest societal disease burden and severely hinders the high-quality development of public health prevention and control systems (Islam et al. 2024).

Pathological obesity triggers persistent low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulation of the endocrine network, thereby promoting and exacerbating a range of metabolic complications (Weijie et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2026). Among these, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and cardiovascular disease pose the most significant clinical threats (Lecoutre et al. 2025; Tsakiridis and Steinberg 2026). Excessive visceral fat accumulation markedly impairs insulin signaling, suppresses glucose uptake and utilization in peripheral tissues, and progressively induces insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, driving the onset and progression of type 2 diabetes (Yang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2026). Concurrently, obesity-induced ectopic lipid deposition leads to hepatocyte lipotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the activation of inflammatory and fibrotic cascades, facilitating the malignant transition from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis, liver fibrosis, and even cirrhosis (Tsakiridis and Steinberg 2026). Furthermore, obesity-mediated dyslipidemia and chronic endothelial injury accelerate atherosclerosis, significantly increasing the risk of hypertension, coronary artery disease, and ischemic cerebrovascular events, thereby serving as a major independent risk factor for elevated all-cause mortality (Pakhare and Anjankar 2024).

Current clinical interventions for obesity and its associated metabolic complications remain largely centered on lifestyle modification and symptomatic treatment, lacking fundamental, targeted molecular strategies (Nicze et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024). The core challenge lies in the fact that the upstream regulatory network through which obesity orchestrates multi-organ metabolic dysfunction has not been systematically elucidated, impeding the identification of novel therapeutic targets and their clinical translation (Weijie et al. 2024; Lecoutre et al. 2025).

The development of obesity is closely related to the regulation of various transcription factors and signaling pathways. At the epigenetic level (Peng et al. 2024), demethylation occurs in the CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β (C/EBPβ) promoter region, increasing the transcriptional activity of the C/EBPβ gene and promoting adipocyte differentiation. At the transcriptional level (Rosen et al. 2000), adipocyte differentiation is regulated by various transcription factors, including CCAAT/enhancer-binding proteins (C/EBPs) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ). At the post-transcriptional level (Son et al. 2014), multiple microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) regulate the expression of obesity-related genes. At the PTM level, the functions of obesity-related proteins are altered through modifications such as methylation, glycosylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination (Wang et al. 2026). Glycosylation is one of the most abundant and diverse PTMs of proteins (Tian et al. 2025). Protein glycosylation refers to the process where amino acid residues in proteins form glycosidic bonds with sugar chains through the action of glycosyltransferases or glycosidases in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus (He 2024). This process involves a series of transport, trimming of sugar chain ends, modifications, and fucosylation or sialylation, ultimately resulting in the assembly of glycosylated proteins (Tian et al. 2025). There are various types of glycosylation modifications in the human body, which can be classified into N-glycosylation, O-glycosylation, and C-mannosylation, based on the glycosylation sites (Schjoldager et al. 2020; Reily et al. 2019). Among these, N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation are the most common and play significant roles in various biological functions and the development of diseases (Yue et al. 2023).

Recent research has shown that various types of modified proteins generated via glycosylation pathways are implicated in the onset and progression of obesity and its associated complications (Wu et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2025). These proteins are involved in enzyme regulation and interact with other proteins to facilitate cellular signaling processes that contribute to obesity. These pathways include the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) signaling pathway (Freund et al. 2017; Gewinner et al 2004) and the Notch signaling pathway (Rampal et al. 2007; Takeuchi and Haltiwanger 2010). Additionally, multiple studies have found that glycosylation modifications play an important role in the diagnosis and treatment of obesity and its complications. For example, the induction of O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase (OGT) removal reduces the O-GlcNAcylation levels of lipid droplet-associated perilipin 1 (PLIN1), leading to enhanced visceral fat breakdown (Yang et al. 2020). These studies demonstrate that glycosylated proteins can serve as potential biomarkers and targets for the prevention and treatment of obesity, although the underlying mechanisms linking them to obesity and its complications have yet to be fully clarified.

This review systematically summarizes the concept of glycosylation and its regulatory role in the development of obesity, diabetes, and MASLD. Glycosylation

Glycosylation is a major PTM in which glycans are covalently attached to proteins or lipids under the control of glycosyltransferases, glycosidases, glycosyl donors, and organelle-specific processing pathways. By regulating protein folding, stability, trafficking, receptor activation, and cell–cell communication, glycosylation contributes to both physiological homeostasis and disease progression (Kuehnel et al. 2018; Oliveira-Ferrer et al. 2017). According to glycan linkage and biosynthetic mechanism, protein glycosylation can be broadly divided into N-glycosylation, O-glycosylation, C-glycosylation, and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring (Hirabayashi et al. 2001; Wiederschain et al. 2013) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Glycosylation modifications can be categorized into N-glycosylation, O-glycosylation, C-glycosylation; GPI anchoring, and their specific assembly steps

N-Glycosylation

N-glycosylation refers to the process in which the side-chain amide group of asparagine in the ER is linked to an oligosaccharide N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) under the catalytic action of glycosyltransferases, resulting in the formation of glycoproteins (Schwarz and Aebi 2011). Not all asparagine residues in proteins can undergo N-glycosylation. Proteins that can be N-glycosylated possess certain structural characteristics. Since N-glycosylation occurs at the side chain amino group of asparagine (Asn), the second position can be any amino acid except proline, while the third position can be serine (Ser), threonine (Thr), or cysteine (Cys), with cysteine being relatively rare (Guay et al. 2025). Therefore, the motif for N-glycosylation has a unique sequence characteristic, represented as “Asn-X-Ser/Thr.” First, there must be an asparagine residue (Asparagine, Asn), and “X” can be any amino acid except proline, with the amino acid residue connected to “X” being either threonine or serine. All N-glycans share a common core oligosaccharide structure (Asn-GlcNAc2Man3) (Medzihradszky et al. 2015), which can be understood as the basic sugar chain unit attached to the asparagine (Asn) of the protein. Its structural composition is as follows: “Asn” represents the asparagine residue that undergoes glycosylation, “GlcNAc₂” indicates two N-acetylglucosamine units, and “Man₃” indicates three mannose units. The overall structure is the core pentasaccharide structure of the N-glycan (composed of two GlcNAc and three mannose), which serves as the common foundation for all mature N-glycans. This structure is retained as the core part after modifications of the initial oligosaccharide chain in the ER and Golgi apparatus, and subsequently, additional sugars (such as more GlcNAc, galactose, sialic acid, etc.) are added to form different types of N-glycan chains, including complex and hybrid types (Kelleher et al. 2007). As the "skeleton" of the N-glycan, its stability is crucial for the subsequent extension and function of the sugar chain, directly affecting biological processes such as glycoprotein folding, transport, and cell recognition (Reily et al. 2019; Bangarh et al. 2023; Pradeep et al. 2023).

This structure is further extended by terminal sugar residues. These glycan residues mainly include GlcNAc, mannose, galactose, fucose, and sialic acid. Depending on the sugar residues used and the types of linkages, these additions can influence the structure of N-glycans.

The main site for N-g lycan formation is the ER. Its synthesis mainly includes two stages: The first stage mainly occurs in the lumen of the ER, where two GlcNAc molecules are linked to dolichol, and then five mannose residues (Man5) are connected to GlcNAc, generating Man5-GlcNAc2-dolichol. Subsequently, Man5-GlcNAc2-dolichol is transported into the lumen of the ER. On the luminal side of the ER, mannosyltransferases and glucosyltransferases respectively add four mannose residues and three glucose residues, resulting in the Glc3-Man9-GlcNAc2-dolichol structure. Immediately after, in the lumen of the ER, specific oligosaccharyl transferases (OTase) transfer the mature glycan to the amino group of asparagine in the protein, forming a glycoprotein (Gao et al. 2001). The Man8GlcNAc2 structure of the modified glycoprotein can be recognized by LMAN1 (Lectin mannose-binding 1) (Duellman et al. 2015). LMAN1 loads the glycosylated protein into the Coat Protein complex II (COPII), which is then transported to the Golgi apparatus through the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) (Zeyen et al. 2020; Aebi et al. 2013). In the Golgi apparatus, most N-glycans are processed into complex and/or hybrid N-glycans by a set of different glycosidases and a series of glycosyltransferases (N-acetylglucosaminyltransferases (MGAT1–5) to perform their functions (Schjoldager et al. 2020; Mendoza et al. 1998).

O-Glycosylation

O-glycosylation is a complex PTM of proteins that primarily occurs in the nucleus and cytoplasm. It interacts with protein phosphorylation modifications and collectively participates in cellular signal transduction and gene expression regulation (Gonzalez-Rellan et al. 2022). O-glycosylation refers to the process in which the hydroxyl (-OH) groups of serine, threonine, residues are covalently linked to sugar chain molecules under the catalysis of glycosyltransferases, forming glycoproteins. Depending on the type of monosaccharide residue linked to serine, threonine, O-glycosylation modifications can be classified into mucin-type and non-mucin-type Li et al. (2022).In mucin-type O-glycosylation, N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) is connected to the protein, while in non-mucin-type O-glycosylation, various monosaccharide residues such as GlcNAc, fucose (Fuc), mannose (Man), glucose (Glu), xylose (Xyl), and galactose (Gal) can be linked to the protein (Chang et al. 2020; Joshi et al. 2018). Unlike N-glycosylation, which occurs in the ER and Golgi apparatus, O-glycosylation primarily takes place in the nucleus and cytoplasm, lacking fixed characteristic amino acid sequences and core oligosaccharide structures. These factors contribute to the complexity of the O-glycosylated protein structure.

Under the catalysis of OGT, the GlcNAc group from the sugar donor UDP-GlcNAc is transferred to the hydroxyl group of target protein serine (Ser) or threonine (Thr) residues. This process mainly occurs in the cytoplasm through the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway. After glucose enters the cell, it is first phosphorylated to form glucose-6-phosphate. Glucose-6-phosphate then reacts with glutamine under the catalysis of glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase to produce glucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcNAc-6-P). This compound is isomerized to N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphate (GlcNAc-1-P) under the action of phosphoglucosamine mutase. GlcNAc-1-P subsequently reacts with UTP in the presence of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine pyrophosphorylase (UAP1) to yield the final sugar donor UDP-GlcNAc (Wells et al. 2024).

OGT is the key enzyme that catalyzes the addition of GlcNAc to target proteins. After synthesis in the cytoplasm, a portion of UDP-GlcNAc is directly utilized for O-glycosylation modifications of proteins in the cytoplasm and nucleus. The catalytic enzyme OGT recognizes and binds to specific sequences on target proteins, which are typically rich in proline, serine, and threonine, referred to as PEST sequences or SP/TP sites. OGT catalyzes the transfer of the GlcNAc group from UDP-GlcNAc to the hydroxyl (-OH) group of serine (Ser) or threonine (Thr) residues on the target protein, forming an O-β-N-acetylglucosamine glycosidic bond, with the release of UDP (Morales et al. 2024).

C-Glycosylation

C-glycosylation is a relatively rare type of protein glycosylation modification, characterized by the connection of sugar chains to specific amino acids in proteins via carbon–carbon bonds, distinguishing it from N-glycosylation (which forms N-glycosidic linkages) and O-glycosylation (which forms O-glycosidic linkages). Its core feature is that it typically occurs on tryptophan (Trp) residues in proteins, with a common sequence being Trp-X-X-Trp (where X represents any amino acid). The most common sugar chain structure involves a single fucose (L-fucose) linked to the indole ring of tryptophan via a C–C bond, forming the core structure (Li et al. 2009). Research on its function is limited, but it is known to exist in certain signaling molecules (such as cytokines and chemokines) and may participate in processes such as protein structural stability, cell recognition, or signal transduction. The biosynthetic mechanism of C-glycosylation is not yet fully understood, and its modification process may be related to the ER, although specific enzymes and regulatory details are still under investigation.

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring is a highly conserved covalent lipid glycosylation modification in eukaryotes. This modification covalently links water-soluble proteins, which lack transmembrane domains, to the outer leaflet of the cell membrane. These modified proteins are referred to as GPI-anchored proteins (GPI-APs) and represent an important family of functional proteins on the cell membrane surface. The GPI anchor is a complex structure composed of phosphoethanolamine arms, a core glycan backbone, and phosphatidylinositol (PI), covalently linked from the protein-binding end to the cell membrane end. Among these components, phosphatidylinositol (PI) serves as the core hydrophobic region that can embed into the phospholipid bilayer of the cell membrane, while the glycan and phosphoethanolamine arms constitute the hydrophilic region that covalently binds to the C-terminus of the target protein, facilitating membrane localization. GPI anchoring is a post-translational covalent modification of proteins, completed in the ER, where the anchoring structure forms an irreversible covalent bond with the protein, providing a stable form of protein membrane localization. Recent studies have confirmed that abnormalities in GPI anchoring modifications and imbalances in GPI-anchored protein expression are closely related to abnormal adipocyte differentiation, lipid metabolism disorders, insulin resistance, and inflammation in adipose tissue, making them important molecular regulatory targets in the development of obesity. Targeted regulation of the GPI anchoring modification pathway holds promise as a novel intervention strategy to improve lipid metabolism imbalance and alleviate obesity and related complications, demonstrating significant value for basic research and clinical translation (Jing et al. 2022).

The role of glycosylation in obesity

In individuals with obesity, triglycerides (TG) accumulate within adipocytes, causing their abnormal enlargement and leading to dysregulation of adipocyte differentiation and metabolic function (Nagai et al. 2015; Luca et al. 2013). Targets of glycosylation for obesity

Glycosylation regulates adipocyte differentiation

Abnormal differentiation of adipocytes plays a crucial role in obesity and its associated metabolic diseases (Nakamoto et al. 2023). In recent years, the role of PTMs in the regulation of adipocyte differentiation has attracted attention. In the 3T3-L1 adipocyte model, a significant increase in O-GlcNAcylation levels has been observed following the induction of adipocyte differentiation. As adipocytes differentiate, the size of intracellular lipid droplets gradually increases, accompanied by a notable rise in the expression levels of adipocyte differentiation marker genes CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein α (C/EBPα) and adiponectin (Ishihara et al. 2010). Furthermore, O-GlcNAc modification of C/EBPβ is also present in nuclear proteins, with identified glycosylation sites at Ser180 and Ser181. Glycosylation at these two sites can impede the phosphorylation of Thr188, Ser184, and Thr179, thereby influencing the adipocyte differentiation process (Tang et al. 2005; Li et al. 2009). Studies have shown that the levels of O-GlcNAcylation of fatty acyl-CoA synthetase and pyruvate carboxylase also increase during adipocyte differentiation (Mendoza et al. 1998). Moreover, PPARγ also exhibits O-GlcNAc PTMs. In the N-terminal activation function-1 (AF-1) domain of PPARγ, Thr54 is considered the primary O-GlcNAc site. The addition and removal of O-GlcNAc are mediated by OGT and O-GlcNAcase (OGA) (Butkinaree et al. 2010). When OGA is inhibited and O-GlcNAc modification increases, the transcriptional activity of PPARγ and the extent of adipocyte differentiation are reduced. This suggests that glycosylation modifications regulate adipocyte differentiation (Ji et al. 2012).

Autotaxin (ATX) is an enzyme with lysophospholipase D activity, playing a crucial role in the synthesis of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA). ATX is primarily secreted by adipocytes and is believed to be closely associated with the process of adipogenesis (Ferry et al. 2003; Gesta et al. 2002). LPA significantly influences the proliferation and differentiation of preadipocytes by activating the LPA1 receptor (Valet et al. 1998; Pages et al. 2001; Simon et al. 2005). Furthermore, the expression levels of ATX exhibit upregulation during adipogenesis, and in db/db mice, which are models of obesity-related diabetes, indicating that ATX may play a role in adipocyte differentiation (Boucher et al. 2005). In studies involving 3T3-F442A mouse adipocytes, researchers utilized tunicamycin to inhibit N-glycosylation or employed specific double-point mutations to abolish N-glycosylation at asparagine residues N53 and N410 (e.g., N53Q and N410Q), thereby further reducing ATX secretion. Additionally, treatment with N-glycosidase or point mutations at amino acid N410 effectively inhibited the lysophospholipase D activity of ATX, thereby attenuating the inhibitory effects of LPA on the proliferation and differentiation of preadipocytes (Pradère et al. 2007).

Follistatin-like protein 1 (FSTL1) is recognized as a "fat factor" and demonstrates high expression levels in preadipocytes. The deficiency of FSTL1 inhibits differentiation of preadipocytes in vitro and prevents the development of obesity in vivo (Wu 2010). Studies have shown that glycosylated and non-glycosylated forms of FSTL1 possess different biological functions, highlighting the critical role of glycosylation modifications in FSTL1 protein functionality (Hambrock et al. 2004; Magadum et al. 2018). To explore this further, researchers constructed FSTL1 mutant plasmids with three N-glycosylation sites (N142Q, N173Q, N178Q) and transfected them into 3T3-L1 preadipocytes to induce differentiation into mature adipocytes. Results showed that N-glycosylation modifications are essential for FSTL1's function in lipid droplet formation, with the N142 mutation significantly reducing the expression of adipogenesis-related genes and the ratio of PPARγ to p-PPARγ. Additionally, FSTL1 inhibits the phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) by interfering with the integrin-β1 and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling pathways, and reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation further diminishes the conversion of PPARγ to p-PPARγ. Thus, FSTL1 promotes adipogenesis (lipid generation) by inhibiting the conversion of PPARγ to p-PPARγ. However, how FSTL1 inhibits the binding between collagen and integrin-β1 remains unanswered in this study (Fang et al. 2022).

Glycosylation modifications of C/EBPα, PPARγ, WAVE protein, long-chain fatty acyl-CoA, pyruvate carboxylase, ATX, and FSTL1 play significant roles in the differentiation of adipocytes. However, the specific effects of mutations at different glycosylation modification sites on the same protein during adipocyte differentiation remain unclear.

Glycosylation regulates lipid degradation in adipocytes

Certain proteins within the nucleus, cytoplasm undergo PTMs mediated by OGT and OGA, which catalyze the addition and removal of O-GlcNAc. A recent study has shown that the absence of adipose OGT can specifically promote the lipolysis of visceral fat, leading to a reduction in visceral fat mass (Yang et al. 2020). In this process, the PTM of the lipid droplet coating protein perilipin 1 (PLIN1) plays a crucial role. Unphosphorylated perilipin tightly associates with the surface of lipid droplets in adipocytes, preventing key lipases such as hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) from accessing the lipid droplets through steric hindrance, while simultaneously inhibiting lipase activity, thereby keeping TG degradation in a "turned off" state. When the body requires energy (during fasting or exercise), hormones such as adrenaline activate intracellular signaling pathways that ultimately lead to the activation of protein kinase A (PKA). The activated PKA specifically phosphorylates multiple serine residues on perilipin, resulting in a conformational change that releases its inhibition on lipases.

Phosphorylated perilipin actively recruits lipases, including HSL, from the cytoplasm to the surface of lipid droplets and enhances lipase activity. It may also help loosen the structure of lipid droplets, making TG more accessible to lipases for gradual hydrolysis into free fatty acids (FFA) and glycerol, which can then be utilized by the body. The phosphorylation of perilipin primarily promotes the degradation of TG within adipocytes by alleviating its inhibitory effect on lipases and facilitating lipase recruitment to the lipid droplet surface. The serine residues S492 and S517 of PLIN1 serve as both phosphorylation and O-glycosylation sites, and these sites can influence each other through competitive modification between O-GlcNAc and phosphate. When OGT is overexpressed, the O-GlcNAcylation of PLIN1 increases, while the phosphorylation level of PLIN1 decreases, thereby inhibiting TG degradation and promoting the development of obesity and lipid accumulation. Therefore, inhibiting O-GlcNAc signaling or preventing O-glycosylation modifications at the S492 and S517 sites of PLIN1 may have potential therapeutic value in the treatment of obesity and other metabolic diseases (Yang et al. 2020).

Glycosylation regulates oxidative stress and inflammation

Oxidative stress plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of obesity and other metabolic diseases (Schieber et al. 2014; Forman 2021). Prolonged exposure to hyperglycemia leads to periodic increases in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, particularly in the white adipose tissue (WAT) of obese individuals, where elevated release of FFA further stimulates ROS production (Yu 2006; Chattopadhyay et al. 2015). The excessive generation of ROS due to hyperglycemia, combined with the increased ROS production in obese WAT, results in metabolic dysregulation of adipocytes, triggering inflammatory responses (Chattopadhyay et al. 2015; Furukawa et al. 2017). Furthermore, inflammation can induce alterations in the glycosylation profiles of cells and tissues, and these glycosylation changes may contribute to the suppression of inflammatory mechanisms (Reily et al. 2019). However, oxidative stress in obesity is not solely a consequence of hyperglycemia. Adipocyte hypertrophy, chronic nutrient excess, elevated FFA, mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, local hypoxia in expanding WAT, NADPH oxidase activation, immune-cell infiltration, and reduced antioxidant defenses can all promote excessive ROS/RNS production. These events interact with inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 β (IL-1β) to create a feed-forward loop linking adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance, ectopic lipid deposition, and metabolic complications. In this context, changes in glycosylation, particularly O-GlcNAc signaling, may function as a nutrient-sensitive regulatory layer connecting metabolic stress with inflammatory responses (Naomi et al. 2023; Choi et al. 2025).The development of metabolic diseases such as obesity is accompanied by chronic low-grade inflammation in various organs. In metabolic tissues, macrophages, as the primary immune response cell type, play a key role in this inflammatory state. Increased energy intake is one of the high-risk factors for obesity. Studies have shown that (Choi et al. 2020) nutritional excess leads to an increase in O-GlcNAc signaling in macrophages, which in turn inhibits pro-inflammatory activation of these cells. OGT catalyzes the O-GlcNAcylation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase β-1 (S6K1), thereby inhibiting the phosphorylation of S6K1 and the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway, reducing macrophage pro-inflammatory responses. This further clarifies the potential role of the O-GlcNAc signaling pathway as a regulator of inflammation and metabolic homeostasis, suggesting it may serve as a therapeutic target for obesity, diabetes, and other immune-related diseases. Additionally, O-GlcNAcylation is critical for renal lipolysis during prolonged fasting, providing protection against diabetic lipotoxicity in proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTEC).

The role of glycosylation modifications in diabetes

T2DM, one of the major metabolic complications of obesity, is characterized by chronic hyperglycemia caused by impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance, or both. Obesity-associated nutrient excess, lipotoxicity, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction can impair pancreatic beta-cell function and peripheral insulin sensitivity. Glycosylation participates in these processes by regulating beta-cell survival, insulin synthesis and secretion, glucose transporter stability and membrane localization, and immune/metabolic signaling. Type 1 diabetes-related immune glycosylation mechanisms are discussed separately where FUT2, MGAT5, and CD25 are described (Garcia et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2019).

O-glycosylation (primarily referring to O-GlcNAc modification, with some involvement of mucin-type O-glycan modifications) plays a central regulatory role in maintaining β-cell survival, insulin synthesis and secretion, and responding to metabolic stress by dynamically modulating the activity and localization of key proteins within β-cells. An imbalance in this modification is closely associated with β-cell functional failure and the development of diabetes. The regulation of O-glycosylation in pancreatic β-cells is centered around O-GlcNAc modification (dependent on the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP)/O-GlcNAc pathway), which maintains a dynamic balance through the action of OGT and OGA, thereby regulating insulin synthesis (Soesanto et al. 2011).

Modification of the transcription factor pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 (PDX-1) enhances its nuclear localization and DNA-binding ability, promoting the transcription of the insulin gene (INS) and β-cell-specific genes (such as glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) and glucokinase (GCK)), thereby directly upregulating insulin synthesis. Modification of ribosomal proteins or translation initiation factors (such as eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E)) optimizes the translation efficiency of the insulin precursor (preproinsulin), ensuring stable production of proinsulin (Ampofo et al. 2024).O-GlcNAc modification regulates insulin secretion by influencing the β-cell "glucose sensing-calcium signaling-granule release" pathway (Durning et al. 2016): O-GlcNAc modification of glucose metabolic enzymes (such as GCK and phosphofructokinase (PFK)) enhances enzyme activity, promotes glucose oxidation and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation, accelerates closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels (KATP), and initiates cell membrane depolarization (Zhuang et al. 2009; Ruan et al. 2014). Additionally, O-GlcNAc modification of voltage-dependent calcium channels (VDCC) and secretory proteins (such as synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP-25) and synaptotagmin) facilitates calcium ion influx, enhances fusion of insulin granules with the cell membrane, and ultimately promotes insulin release (Yu et al. 2020).

When β-cells are exposed to metabolic stressors such as high glucose and high fat for an extended period, O-GlcNAc modification exerts a "protective effect": it modifies stress-related proteins (such as p53 and NF-κB), inhibiting the p53-mediated apoptotic pathway (de Queiroz et al. 2016) and downregulating NF-κB-driven inflammatory responses, thereby reducing β-cell apoptosis and inflammatory damage (Meyerovich et al. 2016). It also modifies ER stress-related proteins (such as inositol-requiring enzyme 1 alpha (IRE1α) and protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK)), alleviating high glucose-induced ER stress (ERS), reducing the accumulation of unfolded insulin precursors, avoiding "ERS-related apoptosis," and maintaining β-cell survival and stress resistance (Alejandro et al. 2015).

The glycosylation of glucose transporters (GLUTs) is a key PTM that regulates their membrane localization, transport activity, and protein stability, primarily through N-linked glycosylation (with a few instances of O-linked glycosylation). By altering the spatial conformation and intracellular sorting pathways of GLUTs, glycosylation influences the efficiency of glucose transmembrane transport, thereby participating in the regulation of blood glucose homeostasis and the occurrence of related diseases. Among the GLUT family (comprising 14 members), most have glycosylation modification sites, predominantly for N-linked glycosylation, with modification sites typically located in the extracellular domains of GLUTs (such as the N-terminus or loop regions), conforming to the glycosylation consensus sequence "Asn-X-Ser/Thr" (where X ≠ Pro). Glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1; widely distributed in red blood cells and brain endothelial cells) primarily undergoes N-glycosylation at Asn45 and Asn151 (Tan et al. 2024; Saiyasit et al. 2022); glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4; mainly found in adipocytes and skeletal muscle cells) is modified at Asn5 and Asn397, with glycosylation at Asn5 being crucial for its membrane transport (Haga et al. 2011); GLUT2 (distributed in pancreatic β-cells, liver, and kidneys) has N-glycosylation sites concentrated at the N-terminus, affecting its stable expression on the cell membrane (Ohtsubo et al. 2013). In addition to N-glycosylation, a few members such as glucose transporter 9 (GLUT9; a key protein for renal glucose reabsorption) exhibit O-linked glycosylation, primarily regulating their polarized distribution in renal tubular epithelial cells.

Glycosylation modifications influence the function of GLUTs, ultimately regulating glucose transport (Tan et al. 2024). Unglycosylated GLUTs are easily recognized by the intracellular quality control system as "misfolded proteins," leading to their retention in the ER and subsequent degradation. In contrast, glycosylation modifications (especially the initial addition of glycan chains within the ER) can assist GLUTs in folding into the correct conformation, enabling them to pass through the Golgi apparatus for further processing and ultimately sorting to the cell membrane to perform transport functions. For example, defects in N-glycosylation of GLUT4 can lead to its accumulation in intracellular vesicles, preventing its recruitment to the cell membrane under insulin stimulation and reducing glucose uptake by muscle and adipose cells (Hirayama et al. 2016). GLUTs on the cell membrane are susceptible to proteolytic hydrolysis or oxidative stress damage; glycosylation modifications form a "glycan protective layer" on their surface, reducing the likelihood of GLUTs being degraded by extracellular proteases.

Imbalances in GLUT glycosylation play a significant role in metabolic diseases such as diabetes and obesity. High glucose and high-fat environments can induce abnormal glycosylation of GLUTs: on one hand, defects in the N-glycosylation of GLUT4 impair its insulin-mediated recruitment to the cell membrane, thereby reducing glucose uptake by muscle and adipose cells. On the other hand, excessive glycosylation of GLUT2 in pancreatic β-cells decreases its sensitivity to blood glucose, resulting in delayed or insufficient insulin secretion and creating a vicious cycle of "elevated blood glucose-abnormal glycosylation-transport impairment" (Czuba et al. 2018).Defects in O-glycosylation of GLUT9, which is responsible for glucose reabsorption in the proximal renal tubules, can result in reduced membrane expression and decreased glucose reabsorption capacity, causing "renal glycosuria". In contrast, excessive glycosylation of GLUT1 in patients with diabetic nephropathy can enhance glucose uptake by mesangial cells, accelerating extracellular matrix deposition and exacerbating renal damage.

Targeting the regulation of GLUT glycosylation has emerged as a potential therapeutic direction for metabolic diseases and tumors. Regulating the activity of glycosyltransferases and glycosidases can improve high glucose-induced GLUT4 glycosylation defects and restore insulin sensitivity by inhibiting abnormal N-glycosyltransferases (such as MGAT1) (Zhou et al. 2024). Additionally, targeting glycan processing pathways using glycosyltransferase inhibitors like tunicamycin can moderately inhibit excessive glycosylation of GLUT1, thereby reducing glucose uptake by tumor cells and suppressing tumor proliferation.Monitoring the glycosylation levels of GLUTs in blood or tissues, for instance (abnormal glycosylation of GLUT2 may indicate impaired pancreatic β-cell function) can provide a basis for the early diagnosis of diabetes and tumors (Pohl et al. 1995; Goff et al. 2010).

The MGAT4A gene encodes the GlcNAcT-IV glycosyltransferase (GnT-4a), one of the two isoenzymes responsible for synthesizing the β-1,4-N-acetylglucosamine linkages on α-1,3-linked mannose. GnT-4a facilitates the branching of the glycan structures of glucose transport proteins, resulting in a multi-antenna complex type, which is the most common form of mature N-glycans. Golgi-mediated glycosylation by GnT-4a selectively maintains the surface expression of GLUT2 glycosylation on β cells. Genetic defects in GnT-4a do not impair GLUT2 glycoprotein maturation but reduce its cell surface half-life, triggering intracellular endocytosis and redistribution to endosomes and lysosomes (Ohtsubo et al. 2005).Consequently, this impairs glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, resulting in metabolic dysfunction associated with T2DM. Notably, chronic high-fat diet-induced diabetes is linked to reduced expression of GnT-4a and weakened glycosylation of GLUT2, consistent with the endocytosis of GLUT2 (Ohtsubo et al. 2005). Thus, glycosylation of β cell glucose transport proteins mediates the connection between diet and insulin production, typically suppressing T2DM pathogenesis. Additionally, elevated concentrations of FFA due to diet and obesity lead to nuclear exclusion in β cells, reducing the expression of transcription factors FOXA2 and HNF1A, which in turn leads to GnT-4a glycosyltransferase and glucose transport protein expression defects (Ohtsubo et al. 2011). Resulting β cell dysfunction can lead to impaired glucose tolerance and reduced insulin secretion, further triggering symptoms such as hyperglycemia and systemic insulin resistance.Protecting β cell GnT-4a glycosylation and glucose transport protein expression may disrupt this pathogenic pathway. HNF1A also trans-activates mouse Slc2a2 gene expression, with MGAT4A gene expression maintained by HNF1A. Multiple HNF1A binding sites exist in the promoters of the MGAT4A and Slc2a2 genes, and enforced specific expression of MGAT4A and Slc2a2 in β cells significantly impacts metabolic abnormalities, suggesting HNF1A influences diabetes pathogenesis through interactions with MGAT4A and Slc2a2 (Ohtsubo et al. 2011). The onset of type 1 diabetes (T1D) is primarily associated with T cell-mediated destruction of pancreatic β cells. Research indicates that over 50 gene loci are closely related to the development of this disease (Ilonen et al. 2019). Among these, the fucosyltransferase 2 gene (FUT2) is considered an important pathogenic candidate gene. As a glycosyltransferase, FUT2 is responsible for adding α1,2-linked fucose to the terminal galactose of various glycans, thereby forming H antigens in body fluids and intestinal mucosa. The absence of FUT2 expression is significantly associated with susceptibility to type 1 diabetes (Smyth et al. 2011). Furthermore, the α-1,6-mannosyl-β-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase (GnT-V), encoded by the MGAT5 gene, prevents the onset of type 1 diabetes to some extent by enhancing N-glycan branching in T cells, which reduces T cell activation (Rudman et al. 2023). Non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice serve as an experimental model for type 1 diabetes, and studies have found that IL-2Rα (CD25), a protein involved in T cell survival and proliferation, is modified by N-glycosylation. This modification and downstream signaling are inhibited by supplemented glucosamine, further highlighting the complex role of N-glycosylation in T cell differentiation (Chien et al. 2015). FUT2, MGAT5, and CD25 all influence T cell differentiation through glycosylation modification pathways, thereby affecting the onset of type 1 diabetes. The role of glycosylation in MASLD

MASLD (formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, NAFLD) includes simple steatosis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH; formerly NASH), with potential progression to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. To align with the current multisociety consensus, MASLD and MASH are used throughout this review, while NAFLD/NASH are retained only when referring to older nomenclature in cited studies (Rinella et al. 2023; Kanwal et al. 2024). It has become a leading chronic liver disease worldwide, yet effective treatment strategies remain elusive (Younossi et al. 2024; Rosso et al. 2019). A defining feature of MASLD is the excessive accumulation of TG within hepatocytes. Peripheral adipose tissue (such as subcutaneous and visceral fat) undergoes increased lipolysis due to insulin resistance, releasing large amounts of FFA into the liver, exceeding the metabolic capacity of hepatocytes (Portincasa et al. 2024). Insulin resistance activates hepatic lipid synthesis pathways (such as sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC)), promoting the excessive conversion of glucose into TG, which further contributes to the accumulation of TG in the liver (Loomba et al. 2021; Stefan et al. 2022). Impaired synthesis or secretion of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), which is responsible for transporting hepatic TG to peripheral tissues, results in the ineffective export of TG from the liver, ultimately leading to lipid deposition within hepatocytes. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of MASLD (Vily-Petit et al. 2020; Pal et al. 2023). Glycosylation plays a crucial role in lipid metabolism, and apolipoproteins have been identified as key high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-related proteins. Among them, apolipoprotein E (ApoE) contains seven mucin-type O-glycosylation sites, which are associated with the function of HDL in lipid transport (Zhu et al. 2019).

Research has shown that GnT-III (β-1,4-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase III) is a key enzyme involved in N-glycosylation, encoded by the MGAT3 gene. Its primary function is to catalyze the formation of bisecting N-glycans, which influence protein localization, activity, and signaling by modulating glycan chain structures. GnT-III plays a crucial regulatory role in MASLD (Lee et al. 2004).Research has shown that GnT-III can regulate insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism by modifying metabolic-related proteins in the liver, such as the insulin receptor and lipogenic enzymes. The absence of GnT-III may exacerbate hepatic insulin resistance and promote TG accumulation, thereby worsening the pathological progression of MASLD. The glycan modifications mediated by GnT-III may represent a new regulatory target for lipid metabolic disorders in MASLD. Additionally, GnT-III directly regulates the glycan structure of apolipoproteins (Apo proteins) through hybrid-type N-glycosylation modifications, which in turn affect the folding, assembly efficiency, secretion stability, and functional activity of Apo proteins, with a core focus on key apolipoproteins involved in lipoprotein metabolism, such as apolipoprotein B (ApoB) and ApoE. Apo proteins, particularly ApoB and ApoE, are essential structural proteins synthesized in the liver for lipoproteins (such as VLDL, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and HDL) and are all N-glycosylated proteins (containing potential N-glycosylation sites, such as Asn-2512 in ApoB and Asn-184 in ApoE). In hepatic lipid metabolism, ApoB is the sole structural protein of VLDL, responsible for transporting hepatic TG to peripheral tissues. Impaired assembly and secretion of ApoB are one of the core reasons for the "reduced hepatic lipid output" observed in MASLD, with GnT-III playing a crucial regulatory role in this process. When GnT-III expression is normal, its hybrid-type modification of ApoB promotes efficient binding of ApoB to TG in the ER, leading to the formation of mature VLDL particles and reducing premature degradation of ApoB. In MASLD model mice with downregulated GnT-III expression, a decrease in hybrid-type glycans and an increase in branched glycans on ApoB were observed, resulting in increased misfolding of ApoB, which was subsequently cleared by the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) system. This reduction in mature VLDL production leads to ineffective hepatic TG output, further exacerbating intracellular fat accumulation and promoting the progression of MASLD (Lee et al. 2004).

GALNT2 is a protein encoded by the GALNT2 gene in humans, officially known as polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 2 (GalNAc-T2). GALNT2 is a member of the glycosyltransferase 2 protein family and initiates mucin-type O-glycosylation reactions in the Golgi apparatus. It catalyzes the initial reaction in the biosynthesis of O-linked oligosaccharides by transferring N-acetyl-D-galactosamine residues to serine or threonine residues on protein receptors. GALNT2 has a wide range of peptide substrates, including EA2, Muc5AC, Muc1a, and Muc1b, and is generally considered a regulator of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) metabolism (Schjoldager et al. 2012).Deficiency of GALNT2 reduces the levels of HDL-C and phospholipid transfer protein (PLTP). As a glycosyltransferase, GALNT2 can indirectly influence hepatic lipid accumulation by regulating the glycosylation of angiopoietin-like protein 3 (ANGPTL3). ANGPTL3 is a key molecule in hepatic lipid metabolism, and abnormal levels can lead to impaired clearance of TG from the bloodstream, resulting in excess lipid deposition in the liver and promoting the occurrence and progression of MASLD. GALNT2 also affects the O-glycosylation level of apolipoprotein C-III (ApoC-III) at Thr74, further influencing hepatic lipid metabolism and providing a pathological basis for the development of MASLD.

PLTP is O-glycosylated in the plasma and liver of wild-type (WT) mice, but is absent in knockout (KO) mice, with the identified O-glycosylation site located at the C-terminus of PLTP. PLTP transfers phospholipids from apoB-containing lipoproteins to circulating HDL and is a known regulatory factor in plasma HDL metabolism. Mice lacking PLTP activity exhibit reduced HDL-C levels, while GalNAc-T2-mediated O-glycosylation of PLTP enhances its activity and increases HDL-C levels, promoting the onset of MASLD. Additionally, there are other specific targets of GalNAc-T2 glycosylation, including the C-terminus of ApoE (Thr305), hepatic lipase (HL) (Thr38), and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) (Thr27 and Thr3937) (Khetarpal et al. 2016).

In recent years, studies have reported that the insulin receptor is a new target for O-glycosylation mediated by GalNAc-T2. Galnt2−/− mice exhibit reduced obesity and altered insulin signaling. These findings suggest that the relationship between GALNT2 and MASLD is primarily linked to the regulation of lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity (Verzijl et al. 2022).Cyclic adenosine monophosphate response element-binding protein H (CREBH) is a transcription factor primarily expressed in tissues such as the liver and intestines. Its core function is to regulate the expression of genes associated with lipid metabolism, glucose metabolism, and inflammatory responses, playing a crucial regulatory role in MASLD.

Research has reported (Zeng et al. 2022) that N-glycosylation modification of CREBH can enhance its transcriptional activity. Specific glycosylation sites, such as asparagine residues, exist within its protein sequence and require glycosyltransferases for modification. Proper glycosylation promotes the correct folding, ER export, and nuclear localization of CREBH, enabling it to enter the nucleus and bind to target genes (such as genes that promote lipid catabolism and inhibit lipid synthesis), thereby exerting regulatory functions on metabolism. Glycosylated CREBH can synergistically interact with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), activating PPARα target genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, promoting fatty acid β-oxidation, and reducing hepatic lipid accumulation. Additionally, CREBH can directly or indirectly inhibit the expression of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD-1) through transcriptional regulation. SCD-1 is a key enzyme in lipid synthesis in tissues such as the liver, responsible for converting saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids, providing substrates for TG synthesis. Excessive SCD-1 activity can promote hepatic lipid accumulation and contribute to the development of MASLD. Glycosylated CREBH can inhibit SCD-1, thereby reducing hepatic lipid synthesis. In the pathological state of MASLD, the glycosylation process of CREBH often becomes abnormal. A common mechanism is that ER stress interferes with glycosyltransferase activity, leading to insufficient glycosylation of CREBH, which prevents its proper folding and transport to the nucleus, resulting in its degradation within the ER and reduced CREBH activity. This diminishes its regulatory effects on metabolic target genes, such as decreased expression of genes promoting fatty acid β-oxidation and impaired lipid clearance capacity. Conversely, the inhibitory effect on lipid synthesis genes (such as SCD-1) is weakened, leading to increased lipid synthesis and ultimately promoting hepatic fat accumulation, thereby driving the onset of MASLD.

Sterol regulatory element-binding protein cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) is a widely expressed membrane glycoprotein that plays a crucial regulatory role in lipid metabolism and inflammatory stress. The N-glycosylation of SCAP is a key switch in regulating lipid metabolism, primarily functioning to stabilize SCAP and drive the transport of SCAP/sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) from the ER to the Golgi apparatus, ultimately activating the SREBP transcription factor and promoting the synthesis of cholesterol, fatty acids, and other lipids (Zhang et al. 2020).Research has reported that a high-sugar environment can enhance the N-glycosylation modification of SCAP in hepatocytes, which increases the protein stability of SCAP and subsequently elevates its total protein levels. The absence of N-glycosylation significantly reduces the protein stability of SCAP, leading to decreased lipid accumulation and inflammation in hepatocytes. Furthermore, SCAP N-glycosylation not only enhances the transcription of acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2) mediated by SREBP-1 but also increases the S659 phosphorylation of ACSS2 protein mediated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), resulting in elevated levels of ACSS2 in the nucleus. This, in turn, increases the acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27ac), a key epigenetic modification associated with MASH. Modulating the expression of ACSS2 or its localization in the nucleus can negate the effects of SCAP N-glycosylation on H3K27ac, lipid accumulation, and inflammation. Therefore, SCAP N-glycosylation plays a crucial role in regulating histone H3K27 acetylation, and targeting SCAP N-glycosylation may represent a novel strategy for the treatment of MASH (Li et al. Future perspectives and conclusion

Glycosylation sites can serve as potential targets for the treatment of metabolic diseases such as obesity; however, many issues remain to be addressed. Understanding the specific targets and molecular mechanisms through which glycosylation exerts its effects could elucidate new therapeutic interventions for obesity and related metabolic disorders (He et al. 2024; Hwang et al. 2023).The glycosylation modifications and other PTMs of key transcription factors regulating obesity, such as C/EBPβ and PPARγ, play a significant role in adipocyte differentiation. Diabetes, as one of the most common complications of obesity, is also a representative metabolic disease characterized by glucose metabolism disorders. It primarily arises from limitations in insulin secretion and glucose transport. OGA, a well-known enzyme that regulates O-glycosylation, can modulate insulin secretion by affecting the function of pancreatic β-cells by modulating OGA levels. This indicates that OGA may serve as an important therapeutic target for diabetes (Yang et al. 2020).MASLD is a lipid metabolism disorder characterized by the excessive accumulation of TG in hepatocytes. Among the factors involved, GalNAc-T2 is commonly regarded as a regulator of HDL-C metabolism. The O-glycosylation of GalNAc-T2 by phospholipid transfer protein (PLTP) enhances its activity and increases HDL-C levels. Recent studies have reported that GalNAc-T2-mediated O-glycosylation exerts a local effect through the O-glycosylation of the insulin receptor, thereby influencing glucose metabolism. This finding provides new insights into the relationship between glucose metabolism and lipid metabolism (Verzijl et al. 2022).O-GlcNAcylation can inhibit visceral fat lipolysis and accelerate fat accumulation by regulating its O-GlcNAcylation levels of the lipid droplet-associated protein Perilipin1. In the liver, OGT can bind to fatty acid synthase (FASN) and inhibit its K48-linked ubiquitin degradation, accelerating lipid synthesis and leading to MASLD. Additionally, O-GlcNAc signaling may serve as a connecting pathway for obesity, diabetes, and other immune-related diseases. Meanwhile, regulating the levels of OGA can indirectly affect insulin secretion, thus participating in the progression of diabetes. Research findings (Wang et al. 2024) reveal the potential role of O-GlcNAc signaling in obesity and its complications. By regulating O-GlcNAc signaling, new therapeutic avenues may be opened for the treatment of obesity and its associated conditions.

There may be a close relationship among various types of PTMs, which can influence each other and jointly regulate the occurrence and development of obesity and its complications through antagonistic or synergistic interactions. This interdependence or influence among multiple types is referred to as "PTM Crosstalk" (Zhao et al. 2025).PTMs typically involve two components: modifying enzymes and modified substrate proteins. Crosstalk between different PTMs can occur in either or both of these components. We found that glycosylation influences the occurrence and development of obesity and its complications through interactions with other PTMs. Therefore, further investigation into the specific interactions between glycosylation and other PTMs of proteins is of significant importance for exploring the progression of metabolic diseases.

In summary, systemic metabolic dysregulation induced by obesity serves as the initiating factor driving multi-organ and multi-system metabolic complications. The resulting chronic inflammation, lipotoxicity, and endocrine disruption continuously promote the malignant progression of T2DM, MASLD and cardiovascular diseases, collectively forming a major chronic disease spectrum that threatens human health (Reily et al. 2019; Lin et al. 2025). Traditional metabolic pathways have been insufficient to fully explain the heterogeneity of obesity onset and the diversity of its complications. In contrast, dysregulation of glycosylation has been established as a key molecular link bridging simple fat accumulation and systemic metabolic deterioration (Lin et al. 2025; Onigbinde et al. 2025).

Maintaining glycosylation homeostasis is essential for orderly glucose and lipid metabolism, normal adipose tissue remodeling, efficient insulin signaling, and stable vascular endothelial function (Chen et al. 2025; Qiang et al. 2021). Under obese conditions, energy surplus-induced aberrant remodeling of the glycosylation profile leads to disordered glycan modifications on multiple key metabolic proteins. Through coordinated regulation across multiple pathways, these alterations trigger and amplify systemic metabolic disturbances, inflammatory activation, and oxidative stress damage, thereby participating throughout the pathological progression of obesity and its related metabolic diseases (Onigbinde et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025). Current research has firmly established a causal coupling between obesity-induced metabolic imbalance and glycosylation dysregulation, with aberrant glycosylation acting as an important upstream driving mechanism in the development and progression of obesity-related metabolic diseases (Muniandy et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2026). This provides a novel theoretical perspective for dissecting the complex pathogenic network of obesity (Li et al. 2025).

With the continuous advancement of modification omics, metabolomics, and molecular signaling technologies, research on the regulatory mechanisms of glycosylation in metabolic diseases has progressed from phenomenological validation to mechanistic exploration and clinical translation (Chowdhury et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025). Focusing on the glycosylation–obesity metabolic regulatory axis, future basic research and clinical applications can be directed toward three major directions: mechanistic elucidation, biomarker development, and targeted therapeutic translation (Yang et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025).

First, it is essential to dissect the specific signaling regulatory networks through which glycosylation mediates obesity-induced metabolic disorders. Current understanding of the differential pathway mechanisms of various glycosylation subtypes—such as N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation—in adipocyte differentiation, insulin resistance, hepatic lipid deposition, and vascular injury remains incomplete (Qiang et al. 2021; Svecla et al. 2025). Many studies suffer from fragmented targets and isolated pathway analyses. Future efforts should leverage multi-omics integration strategies to systematically identify key glycosylation modification targets, glycosyltransferase/glycosidase regulatory modules, and their upstream and downstream signaling axes at different stages of obesity (Reily et al. 2019; Li et al. 2025). This will enable the construction of a dynamic regulatory network linking “glycosylation modifications–core pathways–metabolic phenotypes,” elucidate the spatiotemporally specific mechanisms by which glycosylation regulates obesity complications, and refine the molecular framework underlying obesity-related metabolic disorders (Lin et al. 2025; Chowdhury et al. 2025; Svecla et al. 2025).

Second, the clinical translation of glycosylation-related molecules should be advanced to develop novel disease biomarkers. Compared with traditional clinical indicators, glycosylation modifications offer advantages such as high disease specificity, strong pathological sensitivity, early dynamic changes, and detectability in body fluids (Muniandy et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025). Future studies can characterize glycosylation expression profiles across different obesity subtypes and metabolic complications, identify specific glycan structures and glycoprotein biomarkers, and establish novel non-invasive assessment systems for obesity risk prediction, metabolic severity stratification, and early complication screening (Chen et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Ananchuensook et al. 2025). Such systems would enable early identification, precise classification, and dynamic monitoring of obesity-related metabolic diseases, addressing the limitations of current clinical diagnostic frameworks in terms of timeliness and specificity (Farkas et al. 2025; Renault et al. 2026).

Finally, the development of precision therapies targeting glycosylation signaling pathways and their clinical translation should be promoted. Currently, there is a lack of curative targeted drugs for obesity and metabolic syndrome. The dynamic and reversible nature of glycosylation modifications makes them highly promising for pharmacological intervention (Qiang et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2026). Future research can focus on validated key glycosylation–metabolic signaling axes, targeting core glycosylation enzymes and critical protein modification sites (Onigbinde et al. 2025; Chowdhury et al. 2025). By correcting aberrant glycosylation homeostasis, it may be possible to reverse insulin resistance, inhibit pathological adipose remodeling, alleviate hepatic lipotoxicity, and attenuate vascular inflammatory lesions, thereby achieving mechanism-based intervention for obesity and its complications (Yang et al. 2025). Furthermore, leveraging individual differences in glycosylation profiles may enable the construction of a precision stratification system for obesity-related metabolic diseases, overcoming the limitations of conventional symptomatic treatments and offering novel clinical therapeutic targets and translational directions for refractory obesity and severe metabolic syndrome (Chen et al. 2025; Renault et al. 2026).

Overall, in-depth exploration of the central regulatory role of glycosylation in the obesity metabolic network will not only advance the theoretical understanding of obesity and metabolic disease pathogenesis but also hold substantial promise for clinical diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and targeted therapeutic translation (Reily et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025). This represents an important frontier for future basic research and precision medicine in metabolic diseases (Li et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025; Farkas et al. 2025).
