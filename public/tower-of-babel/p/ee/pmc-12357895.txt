An interpretable CT-based deep learning model for predicting overall survival in patients with bladder cancer: a multicenter study
Authors: Meng Zhang, Yizhong Zhao, Dapeng Hao, Yancheng Song, Xiaotong Lin, Feng Hou, Yonghua Huang, Shifeng Yang, Haitao Niu, Cheng Lu, Hexiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting the prognosis of bladder cancer remains challenging despite standard treatments. We developed an interpretable bladder cancer deep learning (BCDL) model using preoperative CT scans to predict overall survival. The model was trained on a cohort (n = 765) and validated in three independent cohorts (n = 438; n = 181; n = 72). The BCDL model outperformed other models in survival risk prediction, with the SHapley Additive exPlanation method identifying pixel-level features contributing to predictions. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups using deep learning score cutoff. Adjuvant therapy significantly improved overall survival in high-risk patients (p = 0.028) and women in the low-risk group (p = 0.046). RNA sequencing analysis revealed differential gene expression and pathway enrichment between risk groups, with high-risk patients exhibiting an immunosuppressive microenvironment and altered microbial composition. Our BCDL model accurately predicts survival risk and supports personalized treatment strategies for improved clinical decision-making.Subject terms: Prognostic markers, Cancer models, Bladder cancer

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) is the 9th most common cancer and the 13th leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide1, posing a significant economic and social burden. BCa is broadly classified into muscle-invasive BCa (MIBC) and non-muscle-invasive BCa (NMIBC) subtypes2. Despite standardized treatments, patients’ prognoses vary widely, making prognostic evaluation important for guiding postoperative treatment of BCa. Current BCa stratification standards3 remain complex because of clinical heterogeneity and the incomplete integration of emerging tumor markers. Immune-related gene signatures show promise for BCa prognosis4, but require substantial resources and specialized expertise for identification. Thus, there is a pressing need for innovative prognostic biomarkers that could be critical for therapy selection and monitoring in BCa.

The widespread adoption of advanced medical image processing techniques has been instrumental in revealing high-dimensional information critical for precision medicine5. Radiomics quantifies imaging features to generate valuable non-invasive metrics. Studies by Chen et al.6 and Woźnicki et al.7 highlight the potential of pathological and imaging features as prognostic biomarkers in BCa. Deep learning (DL) algorithms process raw images through multi-layer transformations to produce output signals8, aiding clinical decision-making. For instance, Wei et al.9 developed a DL model for predicting survival outcomes in patients with MIBC after cystectomy. Similarly, a DL signature based on contrast-enhanced CT scans has been shown for predicting BCa recurrence10. However, the application of contrast-enhanced CT-based DL models for survival prediction across both NMIBC and MIBC remains underexplored.

Although DL models demonstrate superior performance in medical image analysis, their “black box” nature limits clinical adoption. To address this challenge, the SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) method can provide pixel-level interpretability for model predictions11. In addition, emerging evidence indicates that radiomic features extracted from medical images correlate with distinct biological pathways across various tumor types12,13. This link between imaging phenotypes and underlying biology suggests that interpretable DL models may reveal clinically relevant biological insights. Therefore, integrating SHAP-based interpretability with biological pathway analysis could deepen our understanding of how DL models make predictions and confirm their clinical relevance.

In this work, we aimed to develop an interpretable DL model for BCa survival prediction using preoperative CT imaging. The model was validated across multiple independent cohorts including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. We used the SHAP method for model interpretability and integrated TCGA RNA sequencing data to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the imaging-based predictions, providing both clinical utility and biological insights for BCa management.

Interpretability analysis of BCDL

The DL score, representing the risk score generated by the BCDL model, ranged from 2.93 to 4.67 in the training cohort. Figure 3a illustrates the distribution of DL scores within representative CT image samples from the training cohort. As shown in Fig. 3b–e, DL scores differed significantly between patients with different survival statuses across all four cohorts.

DL score evaluation across cohorts and visualization.

a Range of DL scores in the training cohort, with representative CT images from patients showing different scores. b–e Comparison of survival status (alive or dead) between patient subgroups stratified by DL score in the training cohort, validation cohort 1, validation cohort 2, and TCGA cohorts. f Representative axial CT images with corresponding DL scores, overlaid with CAM and SHAP interpretation for model visualization.

To better interpret the BCDL model, we used class activation maps (CAMs) and SHAP plots14,15. Figure 3f presents representative CT images from patients in the training cohort with different survival outcomes, alongside their corresponding CAM heatmaps and SHAP plots. CAMs visualize the activation intensity of image pixels, highlighting regions prioritized by the model. SHAP visualizations provide more detailed image information about the impact of individual pixels on survival risk. In SHAP plots, blue pixels indicate low risk, red pixels indicate high risk, and white pixels represent areas where input features are disregarded. For high-risk patients identified by the BCDL model, CAMs and SHAP plots show concentrated activations in lesion areas, with a predominance of red regions. By contrast, for low-risk patients, activations in lesion areas are less intense, with blue regions significantly outnumbering red pixels.

Stratification of survival risk

In the training cohort, X-tile software was utilized to identify a DL score threshold of 4.02, classifying patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. Figure 5a–c shows that high-risk patients had significantly worse OS than low-risk patients in the training cohort (hazard ratio [HR], 1.921 [95% CI, 1.383–2.668], p < 0.001), validation cohort 1 (HR, 2.393 [95% CI, 1.459–3.925], p < 0.001), and validation cohort 2 (HR, 2.604 [95% CI, 1.372–4.943], p = 0.002). However, in the TCGA cohort, the median OS was 57 months for high-risk patients and 62 months for low-risk patients, with no statistically significant difference observed (HR, 2.646 [95% CI, 0.778–9.007], p = 0.105) (Fig. 5d).

Prognostic stratification by BCDL model in patients with BCa.

a–d Kaplan–Meier survival curves comparing high-risk and low-risk groups stratified by DL score in the training cohort, validation cohort 1, validation cohort 2, and TCGA cohort. The number of patients at risk at each time point is shown at the bottom of each plot. Shaded regions represent 95% confidence intervals.

Subgroup Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that the BCDL model effectively stratified OS risk across the entire population based on age, tumor size, perivesical fat invasion, tumor contour, and muscle invasion status, with significant OS differences observed between high- and low-risk groups (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The model also identified significant distinctions in OS risk among male subjects and those with a single tumor when grouped by sex or tumor number (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences in OS were observed between high-risk and low-risk groups among female patients (HR 1.304, 95% CI 0.716–2.375, p = 0.386) or those with multiple tumors (HR 1.594, 95% CI 0.913–2.783, p = 0.098). Moreover, we conducted additional subgroup analyses stratified by surgery type in the population with complete surgical data (including the training cohort and two validation cohorts). The results demonstrated that our model predictions effectively stratified patients into distinct risk groups, both in the overall analysis and within subgroups defined by surgery type, mitigating concerns about the model’s clinical applicability across diverse patient populations (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Associations between DL score and clinical characteristics

In the integrated validation cohort, the DL score demonstrated significant associations with key clinical prognostic factors (Table 3). The strongest correlation was observed with tumor size (R = 0.634 [95% CI, 0.578–0.690], p < 0.001), indicating that larger tumors were associated with higher risk scores. Additionally, the presence of muscle invasion status (R = 0.382 [95% CI, 0.303–0.454], p < 0.001) and cystic necrosis (R = 0.295 [95% CI, 0.210–0.377], p < 0.001) were also significantly correlated with elevated DL scores. These associations further validate the clinical relevance of our model.

Clinical benefit assessment

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed on a composite cohort of 828 patients who received adjuvant therapy and 227 who did not (Table 4). The results (Supplementary Fig. S4) showed no significant benefit of adjuvant therapy in the combined cohort (HR, 0.784 [95% CI, 0.540–1.138], p = 0.201). However, an OS benefit was observed exclusively in high-risk patients identified by the BCDL model (HR, 0.556 [95% CI, 0.329–0.939], p = 0.028), with no benefit in low-risk individuals (HR, 0.867 [95% CI, 0.485–1.553], p = 0.632). Subsequent stratification of low-risk patients by sex and muscle invasion status divided the cohort into four subgroups. Analysis revealed that only female patients derived a significant survival benefit from adjuvant therapy (HR, 0.190 [95% CI, 0.037–0.971], p = 0.046).

Biological function exploration

To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the risk stratification of the BCDL model, we performed comprehensive molecular analyses using matched TCGA data. Patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the BCDL model cutoff value of 4.02. Figure 6a presents the volcano plot of 346 differential gene expressions (DEGs) identified between the high-risk and low-risk groups, with 66 upregulated and 280 downregulated genes in the high-risk group (Supplementary Table S2). These DEGs predominantly involved genes related to immune regulation and cellular adhesion, suggesting distinct biological states between the risk groups. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant differences in key biological processes (Fig. 6b). Immune microenvironment analysis demonstrated significant differences in immune cell infiltration patterns between the risk groups (Fig. 6c). Notably, high-risk patients showed reduced infiltration of dendritic cells (DCs), interdigitating DCs, and macrophages, suggesting an immunosuppressive microenvironment that may contribute to a poor prognosis. Microbiome profiling revealed distinct taxonomic compositions between the risk groups across multiple levels, identifying 47 phyla, 56 classes, 127 orders, 303 families, and 1607 genera (Fig. 6d–h). Partial least squares discriminant analysis clearly separated the microbiological profiles of the high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 6i). There were three distinct microbial families and four genera identified as significantly differentiating between risk groups (Fig. 6j). Our study leveraged multiscale data from DL-based medical imaging and multi-omics analysis to predict survival risk in BCa, thereby enhancing the biological interpretability of the BCDL model (Fig. 6k).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the BCDL model excels in predicting survival risk for patients with BCa. Verified through a comprehensive literature review, our dataset represents the largest collection of CT images and clinical data for patients with BCa to date. The BCDL model outperformed other models across multiple evaluation metrics, including the C-index and time-dependent ROC. External validation across several cohorts from different medical institutions highlights its potential applicability in diverse clinical settings. The integration of SHAP enhances the model’s interpretability, providing valuable insights into its decision-making process. Subgroup analyses and assessments of adjuvant treatment benefits further confirm the adaptability of BCDL, while exploration of underlying biological mechanisms suggests strategies for clinical translation. Overall, BCDL demonstrates the ability to extract prognostic information from CT images, supporting the optimization of treatment and monitoring strategies for patients with BCa.

There is increasing evidence to support the use of DL for predicting cancer prognosis using imaging biomarkers9,16. While Sun et al.17 integrated clinical, radiomics, and DL features to assess survival post-cystectomy, their study was limited by a small sample size. Similarly, Wei et al.9 developed a DL survival model but excluded patients with NMIBC. To address these gaps, we developed a comprehensive survival prediction model, trained on preoperative CT images from both patients with MIBC and those with NMIBC. The model features a multi-branch architecture and self-normalization layers for improved multicenter compatibility18. Critically, it employs the Cox partial likelihood loss function19, which effectively handles censored data by incorporating information from patients with incomplete follow-up rather than excluding them, thereby maximizing statistical power. This approach adheres to the proportional hazards assumption by guiding the model to learn stable risk scores that consistently distinguish high-risk from low-risk patients over time. By integrating advanced imaging with rigorous survival modeling, our approach achieves superior BCa survival prediction compared with prior DL methods.

Moreover, CAM and SHAP visualizations enhance transparency and interpretability by providing insights into the model’s decision-making process. While CAM generates heatmaps that highlight relevant regions, SHAP offers superior attribution analysis by quantifying both the direction and magnitude of each pixel’s influence on predictions, ensuring a more equitable assessment of feature contributions. The integration of SHAP analysis enhances model interpretability, addressing the “black box” criticism commonly associated with DL approaches in healthcare. By visualizing which image regions contribute to risk predictions, this approach enables clinicians to better understand and validate model outputs, which may facilitate clinical adoption and trust.

Risk stratification of patients supports tailored treatment decisions and follow-up management, ultimately improving the prognosis and prolonging OS20. Administering postoperative adjuvant therapy to all patients may result in overtreatment for those who do not need it, while withholding such therapy from patients who could benefit may compromise their outcomes. In this study, the BCDL model successfully stratified patients with BCa across three cohorts, but did not perform well in the TCGA cohort, likely due to its limited sample size. For low-risk patients, moderate follow-up protocols may be sufficient, while high-risk patients require more intensive monitoring and assertive treatment strategies. For these high-risk patients, therapies such as neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatments, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy could be employed to improve OS2. Additionally, the BCDL model facilitates the definition of novel risk groups and identification of distinct risk categories, informing personalized therapeutic trial designs for patients with BCa.

The BCDL model was associated with several identified risk factors, including tumor size, cystic necrosis, and muscle invasion status, providing insights into how these factors influenced its predictive performance and illustrating the model’s learning process. Notably, the majority of cases with tumors larger than 3 cm (173 cases, 87.37%) and muscle invasion (131 cases, 66.16%) were classified as high-risk by the BCDL model, suggesting that these factors were integrated into the model’s learning phase. These findings demonstrates that the BCDL model effectively analyzes patient data to predict OS accurately, with its key variables aligning with established medical evidence20,21. This alignment underscores the model’s reliability and its potential for practical application in clinical settings.

The optimal treatment for BCa requires a multidisciplinary approach. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, endoscopic resection with risk-based intravesical adjuvant therapy is recommended for NMIBC, while neoadjuvant or adjuvant systemic chemotherapy combined with radical cystectomy or trimodal therapy is advised for MIBC22. For patients with BCa, adjuvant therapy beyond surgery can improve local control and reduce recurrence rates20. In this study, the overall cohort of patients with BCa did not demonstrate significant benefits from adjuvant therapy. However, when stratified using the BCDL model, high-risk patients demonstrated substantial benefits from adjuvant treatment, while low-risk patients did not. Subgroup analysis of the low-risk group further revealed that female patients benefited from adjuvant therapy, especially those without muscle invasion. These findings align with Dobruch et al.23 who reported that female patients with BCa are often diagnosed at more advanced stages and have poorer survival outcomes, supporting the need for prolonged therapy in female patients within the low-risk group. These results provide valuable evidence and insights to guide patient-specific therapeutic strategies and individualized treatment plans.

Our multi-omics analysis offers crucial biological validation for the BCDL model. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant immune dysfunction in high-risk BCa, particularly in antigen presentation and leukocyte migration pathways, which is consistent with the observed reductions in DC infiltration. This finding aligns with research by Liu et al.24 who demonstrated that a high tumor-stroma ratio in BCa is associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, fewer DCs, poorer survival rates, and lower pathological complete response rates to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Additionally, the downregulation of cell adhesion pathways impairs intercellular connections and tissue integrity, thereby promoting tumor invasion25. Moreover, we found significant differences in microbiota composition between high-risk and low-risk patients, suggesting that microbial changes may play an important role in BCa progression26. These findings not only support the biological foundation of the BCDL model but also advance translational medicine, highlighting the need for further exploration and the development of therapeutic agents targeting the microbiome to improve patient prognosis and treatment response.

Our study has several limitations. First, although the BCDL model demonstrated promising results, it requires prospective validation in larger and more diverse cohorts, as our current evaluation was limited to a Chinese population and the TCGA dataset. Second, our analysis focused on OS without attention to disease-free survival or early recurrence. Third, we did not specifically stratify cohorts by variant histology subtypes, which present unique diagnostic challenges for conventional CT due to a higher risk of occult metastasis27. Finally, using only nephrographic phase CT images may limit comprehensive tumor characterization compared with multi-phase protocols.

In summary, our findings highlight the potential of DL in identifying prognostic imaging biomarkers from large patient datasets. The biomarker developed in this study was validated across independent multicenter cohorts and compared with multiple models, demonstrating its potential utility in guiding personalized BCa treatment. SHAP analysis was employed to interpret the model’s predictions, and its biological mechanisms were explored using the TCGA cohort. These results emphasize the feasibility of integrating this biomarker into clinical practice, supporting to the advancement of personalized treatment strategies for BCa.

Methods

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University No. QYFY WZLL 28166, Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University No.2023-526, Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital No. KY-Q-2022-418-01, and the Puyang Oilfield General Hospital No. 2023-05-0019-E01. The methodology adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived for this retrospective study, which complied with the REMARK criteria28.

Patients

Patients were recruited from four medical centers with data collection periods spanning January 2010 to March 2024 for the training cohort, January 2016 to December 2023 for validation cohort 1, and January 2012 to December 2018 for validation cohort 2. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with a pathological diagnosis of BCa, (2) complete clinical data were available and (3) contrast-enhanced CT scans available and performed within 20 days preoperatively. The exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete clinical data or unavailability of CT images, (2) poor quality of CT images, and (3) presence of other concomitant malignancies. The TCGA cohort was acquired from a public database, and patients with unenhanced CT scans, no visible lesions, or incomplete imaging data were omitted. Supplementary Figure S5 presents further details regarding participant selection. OS was measured from the first postoperative day until death or last follow-up, with patients evaluated every 3 to 6 months during the first 2 years postoperatively and every 6 months thereafter. Clinical information collected included age, sex, muscle invasion status, survival status, and OS. Imaging data were obtained from CT scans, including focus location, shape, size, calcification, cystic necrosis, tumor contour, number, stalk, perivesical fat invasion, and CT value of the lesion in the nephrographic phase. All images were independently interpreted retrospectively by a radiologist with 5 years of experience and carefully reviewed by a senior radiologist with 10 years of expertise.

Image acquisition and lesion annotation

Supplementary Table S3 provides a comprehensive overview of the CT image acquisition settings across multiple centers. All scans were stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format, and nephrographic phase images, acquired 60–70 s post-contrast administration, were used for analysis. The nephrographic phase in contrast-enhanced CT corresponds to the peak perfusion phase of the contrast agent, enabling clearer visualization of tumor angiogenesis and heterogeneity29,30. Moreover, utilizing a single-phase protocol helps ensure standardization across the multi-center dataset. Tumor segmentation into regions of interest (ROIs) was manually performed using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0; http://www.itksnap.org) by a radiologist with 5 years of experience, and ROIs were carefully reviewed by an expert radiologist with over 10 years of expertise in BCa image interpretation.

Image pre-processing and DL model construction

We developed an end-to-end deep neural network called BCDL to predict patient survival outcomes from CT images, leveraging a 3D DenseNet121 backbone (https://monai.io/). Eligible cases from the training cohort were randomly divided into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. Model training used a custom Cox partial likelihood-based loss function to account for censored survival labels. To address multi-center heterogeneity, a standardized preprocessing pipeline was implemented, in which CT images were resampled to an isotropic 3.0 × 0.7 × 0.7 mm³ voxel size using cubic B-spline interpolation, harmonizing slice thickness while retaining tumor detail31. To reduce memory usage and minimize interference from non-target regions, tumor regions were extracted from the original CT volumes. The volume of ROI was 32 × 128 × 128 voxels in size. Hounsfield Unit values were clipped to a [−175, 250] window to optimize soft-tissue contrast and exclude irrelevant extreme attenuation values, then normalized to the [0, 1] range32.

To further address variations in data distribution caused by factors such as hospital differences, scanner manufacturers, imaging protocols, and patient populations, batch normalization layers in the DenseNet backbone were replaced with instance normalization, and the first two branches of the multi-branch structure were substituted with self-normalization layers. The network backbone utilized feature channels of 64, 128, 256, and 512. To accommodate tumor size variability, a multi-branch structure was employed, where feature maps with 128, 256, and 512 channels were each sequentially processed by a normalization layer, a ReLU activation function, and an average pooling layer. The resulting features from these branches were then concatenated and passed to a fully connected layer for individual risk score prediction. To address the limited sample size and reduce overfitting, data augmentation techniques were applied during training, including random rotations of 90°, 180°, and 270°; vertical and horizontal flipping; and intensity shifts.

This method accommodates nonlinear associations between predicted risk scores and outcomes, eliminating the need to binarize predictions at specific time points (e.g., 2 years). The network was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 1e−5. The optimal model was selected based on the highest C-index on the validation set, ensuring the best alignment between observed survival times and predicted risk scores while effectively providing early stopping to prevent overfitting16. The final model, corresponding to the 60th epoch, demonstrated favorable generalizability. The DL score refers to the predicted risk score output by the BCDL model. The BCDL model’s architecture is detailed in Supplementary Fig. S6. Model development utilized PyTorch 2.1.0 and CUDA 11.8.0 (Nvidia), with training and validation performed on an Intel W5-3435×32-core CPU and an RTX 4090 24GB GPU. To validate the robustness of our approach, we also compared several standard DL architectures, including DenseNet121, ResNet18, ResNet34, and the Swin Transformer.

Radiomics model, clinical model, and combined model development

To address variations in acquisition parameters from different CT machines, CT images and segmentation outcomes were adjusted for pixel spacing and slice thickness to ensure consistency of radiomics features. The CT images were standardized to the [0, 255] range. In total, 1595 radiomics features were initially extracted from 3D ROIs in nephrographic phase CT images using the PyRadiomics package (version 3.0.1) in Python. Significant features (p < 0.05) were first identified using the Wald test. Next, five-fold cross-validation combined with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression and the Akaike information criterion was applied to refine feature selection. Ultimately, 13 features were selected to construct the radiomics model (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Clinical characteristics were systematically evaluated for prognostic significance using a two-step approach. Univariate Cox regression analysis was first conducted for each clinical characteristic to identify those associated with prognosis. Variables with statistically significant results (p < 0.05) were then incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression analysis, with the forced entry method used to determine independent predictors. The best-performing predictive model described above was subsequently combined with these independent risk factors to develop the combined model.

Statistics and reproducibility

The prognostic performance of the proposed models was evaluated using the C-index and time-dependent ROC analyses across all cohorts. Paired t-tests were used to compare statistical differences between multiple models. To assess the accuracy and reproducibility of survival risk predictions, validation tests were conducted with 2000 bootstrap repetitions33. Calibration curves were generated using 1000 iterations of the bootstrap resampling method. The net clinical benefits of the models were analyzed using DCA.

Baseline characteristics were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables. Optimal DL score cutoff points were identified using X-tile software in the training cohort and applied to stratify all cohorts into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test was used to evaluate OS differences between these two groups across the four cohorts, as well as to assess survival differences in various subgroups stratified by different clinical characteristics. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to evaluate relationships between DL scores and clinical characteristics.

We also evaluated the stratified efficacy of BCDL in adjuvant therapies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiochemotherapy, for patients with BCa. To reduce treatment selection bias, participants who received adjuvant therapy were matched 1:1 with those who did not using the PSM34,35. PSM was conducted with a 1:1 ratio via the nearest neighbor matching. The matching criteria included age, sex, tumor location, shape, size, calcification, cystic necrosis, tumor contour, number, stalk, perivesical fat invasion, CT value in the nephrographic phase, and muscle invasion status. Baseline characteristic comparisons before and after matching were performed using descriptive statistical analysis implemented through the compareGroups package. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and R software (version 3.5.1). The analysis codes are available in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/zyzlw16/Bladder).

Exploring biologic functions

Using RNA sequencing data with matched imaging data from the TCGA-BLCA dataset, generated by Illumina HiSeq, we performed comprehensive bioinformatics analyses to explore the biological mechanisms underlying our BCDL model. High- and low-risk groups were stratified by applying the trained BCDL model to the corresponding TCGA CT images. DEG analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package with stringent criteria (|log2(fold change)| > 1.0 and adjusted p < 0.05)36, followed by functional annotation of DEGs through pathway enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler for GO and KEGG databases. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was further applied using MSigDB Hallmark gene sets to identify biologically meaningful pathways. To characterize the tumor microenvironment, immune cell infiltration was evaluated using single-sample GSEA to compare immune compositions between risk groups37. Additionally, microbiome analysis leveraged data from the Bacteria in Cancer database, examining taxonomic diversity and distribution across five levels (phylum, class, order, family, and genus)38. Partial least squares discriminant analysis was employed to discriminate microbial community dissimilarities between high-risk and low-risk groups. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size analysis was used to identify differentially abundant microbial taxa.
