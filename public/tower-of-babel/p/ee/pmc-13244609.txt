Mesiobuccal root morphology influences biomechanical stability after endodontic microsurgery in maxillary first molars: a finite element analysis study
Authors: Jiahui He, Yuehong Li, Wenjuan Yan, Ziting Zheng, Jin Sun, Tiehua Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndodontic microsurgery (EMS) is an established treatment for refractory periapical lesions; however, late failures related to dentin fatigue and root fracture remain clinically challenging. The mesiobuccal (MB) root of maxillary first molars exhibits pronounced cross-sectional variability that is readily identifiable on cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), yet its biomechanical relevance in apical surgery has not been adequately investigated. This in vitro study aimed to investigate the interaction between MB root morphology and EMS extent on postoperative stress distribution and root fracture risk.MethodsValidated three-dimensional finite element models of post-root canal-treated maxillary first molars were constructed from CBCT data. Two clinically representative MB root morphologies, circular and oval cross-sections, were simulated. For each morphology, root-end resections of 2 mm, 3 mm, or 4 mm, as well as complete MB root amputation, with mineral trioxide aggregate retrograde filling were modeled. An intact tooth model served as control. Oblique functional loading (200 N) was applied. Relative changes in dentin and periodontal ligament (PDL) stress were assessed, and morphology-dependent fracture susceptibility was quantified using Weibull analysis.ResultsIncreasing root-end resection length produced progressive elevations in dentin stress and leftward shifts in fracture probability curves, with complete MB root amputation showing the most unfavorable biomechanical response. Peak stresses were consistently localized to the mid-to-upper root region. Root morphology significantly modulated behavior: although oval roots exhibited slightly higher baseline stresses, they demonstrated attenuated stress amplification and smaller increases in fracture probability following resection compared with circular roots. PDL stress increased modestly with resection extent, particularly after root amputation, reflecting load redistribution to the remaining roots.ConclusionsIn this finite element analysis study, the biomechanical stability after EMS appears to depend on both the extent of root-end resection and MB root morphology. Circular MB roots showed greater vulnerability to stress increases and fracture probability with longer resection, suggesting benefit from more conservative approaches. Oval roots tolerated structural loss better under the modeled conditions. These biomechanical findings suggest a potential role for preoperative morphology assessment in guiding resection extent to optimize root strength.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08152-6.

Introduction

Endodontic microsurgery (EMS) is a recognized treatment option for post-treatment apical periodontitis when nonsurgical retreatment fails to resolve persistent intracanal infection or is deemed impractical [1]. Advances including magnification, ultrasonic retrograde preparation, and biocompatible calcium silicate-based cements, have substantially improved clinical outcomes in EMS [2, 3]. Reported success rates range from approximately 78.4% to 94%; however, considerable variability exists across studies, largely attributable to differences in lesion characteristics, operator experience, and follow-up duration [4–6]. Clinically, long-term tooth survival appears to depend not only on microbial control but also on the mechanical resilience of the treated tooth. Dentin fatigue, apical microcracks, and vertical root fractures are increasingly recognized as important contributors to late surgical failure, particularly in roots with complex cross-sectional geometry [7, 8].

Although anatomical complexity is common among molars, the mesiobuccal (MB) root of the maxillary first molar is particularly notable for its high prevalence of MB2 canals and isthmus formations. In addition, the MB root demonstrates substantial morphological diversity, often presenting flattened or oval cross-sectional configurations, as documented in numerous anatomical and imaging studies [9]. Micro-CT evidence indicates the presence of MB2 canals in up to 90% of specimens, with the root cross-section evolving from round in single-canal roots to oval or ribbon-shape when two canals are present [10, 11]. Such morphological variations have direct clinical implications, influencing debridement difficulty, stress transmission patterns, and susceptibility to fracture [12, 13].

Endodontic microsurgery is frequently considered when nonsurgical retreatment fails. A 3-mm root-end resection is commonly recommended in endodontic microsurgery to balance the removal of apical pathology with preservation of root integrity [14, 15]. However, such recommendations are largely morphology-agnostic and do not account for the pronounced cross-sectional variability of the MB root. In practice, however, resection lengths fluctuate between 2 and 4 mm, with some cases necessitating complete MB root amputation [16]. An anatomical investigation of the maxillary MB root has suggested a minimal resection of approximately 3.6 mm to eliminate most accessory canals and reveal isthmuses while maintaining adequate canal wall thickness [17]. Nevertheless, the combined biomechanical influence of root cross-sectional geometry and resection configuration has not yet been fully elucidated. Persistent localized stress concentrations could interfere with healing and contribute to ongoing inflammation or less favorable outcomes after EMS [18].

Finite element analysis (FEA) has emerged as a pivotal tool for elucidating these biomechanical linkages, enabling quantitative assessment of tooth and periradicular tissues loading under ex vivo conditions. Earlier investigations emphasized single parameters such as resection depth or bevel angle [15, 19]. Accumulating evidence indicates that stress concentration is influenced not only by resection depth but also by surface curvature, apical bone support, and the elastic modulus of retrograde filling materials [20, 21]. Yoo et al. [21] observed heightened hoop stresses in curved apices lacking bone support, while Ashi et al. [20] demonstrated that calcium-silicate-based cement stiffness and retrograde cavity diameter significantly affect stress distribution in EMS. Subtle differences in geometry and material properties can thus alter stress patterns. Despite extensive FEA into EMS parameters, the biomechanical consequences of the MB root cross-sectional variants in maxillary first molars have not been systematically modeled under EMS conditions. Furthermore, MB root amputation may be considered when pathology is confined to a single root, such as vertical root fracture, crown-root fracture, or severe localized periodontal destruction, thereby preserving the remaining roots and partial masticatory function [22]. This approach redistributes occlusal forces to palatal and distobuccal roots, markedly increasing their loading [23, 24]. Long-term implications of this load redistribution remain incompletely defined, particularly in authentic three-dimensional posterior models.

In this study, validated three-dimensional FEA models were developed to investigate the interaction between MB root cross-sectional morphology and root resection extent on postoperative biomechanical behavior of post-EMS maxillary first molars under functional loading, incorporating authentic MB root morphological variants. Models encompassed circular (near-circular cross-section) and oval (flattened cross-section) MB root configurations subjected to 2 mm, 3 mm, or 4 mm root-end resections, a retrograde preparation of 3 mm and a retrograde filling with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA). In addition, a complete MB root amputation model was included as a distinct surgical condition. The investigation integrated three-dimensional FEA with Weibull statistical fracture risk analysis to systematically evaluate impacts of varying apical surgical protocols on dentin stress distribution and periodontal ligament (PDL) stress levels. This study tested two null hypotheses: (1) No significant differences exist in dentin and periodontal tissue stress distribution or fracture probability across resection lengths. (2) No significant biomechanical response differences occur between MB root morphologies (circular vs. oval) post-EMS.

Materials and methods

An intact right maxillary first molar was selected as the base model. The MB root exhibited a pronounced oval cross-section in the mid-to-apical region, characterized by an aspect ratio of 2.3, with a long axis of approximately 1.47 mm and a short axis of 0.67 mm (Fig. 1A). This configuration is representative of clinically prevalent MB root morphology in maxillary first molars [11, 25]. A three-dimensional solid model was reconstructed from cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) data (NewTom GIANO; NewTom, Imola, Italy). Image datasets were converted to Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format and imported into Mimics 15.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for segmentation and surface reconstruction. The reconstructed structures included dentin, pulp chamber, PDL (uniform thickness of 0.2 mm), and alveolar bone, comprising both cortical and cancellous components. For standardization, the MB root canal system was modeled as a single canal without isthmus formation. The surrounding alveolar bone block was extended apically to provide sufficient support for the entire tooth model (approximately 30 mm in height), thereby maintaining geometric stability during the simulation.Fig. 1Morphological configuration, occlusal loading condition, and sectional analysis used for finite element modeling. Model refinement was performed in SolidWorks 2020 (Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France). To examine the influence of root morphology, the base model was altered to produce a circular variant: the MB root was reshaped to a near-circular profile, yielding an aspect ratio of 1.15 in the mid-to-apical segment, a long axis of 0.93 mm, and a short axis of 0.86 mm (Fig. 1A). The models were subsequently transferred to ANSYS 21.0 (Swanson Analysis Inc., Canonsburg, USA) for meshing and biomechanical simulation. All models reflected a post-endodontic condition, with gutta-percha substituting the pulp in the root canal and composite resin restoring the region coronal to the pulp chamber. No artificial canal enlargement was performed; the canal geometry was preserved according to the original anatomical configuration. In the software environment, the entities were segmented into meshes consisting of nodes and tetrahedral elements. A convergence test with a 10% mesh control criterion was employed to ascertain the number of elements and nodes necessary for generating all simulated models.

Material properties were sourced from established biomechanical references and assumed linear elastic, isotropic, and homogeneous behavior (Table 1). The alveolar bone base was fully constrained to replicate physiological fixation. Interfaces, including those between PDL and adjacent tissues, were modeled as frictionless and fully bonded. Occlusal loading replicated the closing phase of mastication (Fig. 1B): a 200 N force was applied through a simulated food bolus [26] at the central fossa, directed 45° buccally relative to the tooth long axis [27]. A mesh sensitivity convergence analysis was conducted in this study to ensure the reliability of the computational results. The results indicated that when the mesh size was refined to 0.5 mm and 1 mm, the variation in equivalent stress fell within 1%, demonstrating convergence. To improve computational efficiency, a mesh size of 1 mm was ultimately selected as the final global size for the finite element model (detailed in Supplementary Material S1). Simulations were executed in ANSYS 21.0, focusing on von Mises equivalent stress in dentin and PDL. In this study, von Mises equivalent stress was analyzed to elucidate the biomechanical effects of various endodontic microsurgery conditions. Long-term dentin fracture risk was quantified using a Weibull risk-of-rupture model integrated with finite element stress data (focusing on maximum principal tensile stress), following methodologies established in prior literature [31]. While von Mises stress was used to characterize overall stress distribution, Weibull fracture probability was calculated based on maximum principal tensile stress, which is more relevant to brittle dentin fracture. Principal tensile stresses within critical regions were integrated across the dentin volume to derive failure probability curves spanning 50–600 N. Leftward shifts in these curves indicated elevated susceptibility consequent to surgical modification, consistent with observations of increased vulnerability in resected root configurations.

Results

The results revealed that EMS significantly altered von Mises stress levels in dentin, with circular and oval root models exhibiting markedly different response patterns (Fig. 3). Stress concentrations were predominantly located on the buccal or palatal surfaces of the palatal root in all models. Root morphology exerted a pronounced influence on stress distribution. In the intact state, the oval root showed a higher peak stress of 69.65 MPa at level 6 compared with 62.66 MPa in the circular root. Under complete amputation, the oval root model exhibited a different response pattern compared to the circular root: the stress at level 6 rose from baseline of 69.65 MPa to 73.78 MPa in the OV, whereas in the C it increased from 62.66 MPa to 73.11 MPa. Across all EMS conditions, including root-end resections and complete amputation, the increase in stress was consistently smaller in oval root models than in circular root models. Moreover, stress variations in the apical region (levels 10–12) of circular root models substantially exceeded those in the coronal region (levels 1–9).

Weibull failure probability in dentin

The Weibull failure probabilities reported here are based on static loading simulations and represent the relative risk of dentin fracture under increasing load magnitude (Fig. 6 and Tables 2 and 3). In circular root models, the intact root exhibited a failure probability of 0.16% under a 50 N load. This probability increased steadily with loading: 1.57% at 100 N, 14.91% at 200 N, and 46.63% at 300 N. Further load escalation to 400 N, 500 N, and 600 N resulted in probabilities of 80.72%, 96.91%, and 99.83%, respectively. Following EMS, failure probabilities systematically rose across all load levels. At 300 N, for example, probabilities after 2 mm, 3 mm, 4 mm, and complete amputation were 50.51%, 55.05%, 60.55%, and 59.93%, respectively, all exceeding the intact state's 46.63%. In oval root models, the intact state showed a failure probability of 0.22% at 50 N. The progression mirrored that of circular root, reaching 58.16% at 300 N and 89.81% at 400 N. Von Mises stress in the PDL

Across all simulated surgical protocols, stress concentrations in the PDL were primarily observed at the alveolar crest fiber attachments in the cervical region and the apical zone (Fig. 7). Stress magnitudes increased steadily with root-end resection level. Overall, von Mises stresses in the PDL remained relatively low. In circular root models, the intact root registered 3.54 MPa. After EMS, values showed a steady rise: 3.49 MPa after 2 mm resection, 3.58 MPa after 3 mm, 3.62 MPa after 4 mm, and peaking at 4.00 MPa following complete amputation. In oval root models, the baseline was 3.67 MPa in the intact state. The EMS conditions triggered a steady increase: 3.90 MPa after 2 mm resection, 3.95 MPa after 3 mm, 4.03 MPa after 4 mm, and a maximum of 4.27 MPa after complete amputation. Discussion

The impact of root morphology and resection extent on postoperative biomechanical behavior in first maxillary molars remains poorly understood [18, 23]. This FEA and Weibull analysis study aimed to investigate the interaction between MB root cross-sectional morphology and EMS extent on postoperative stress distribution and root fracture risk in post-root canal-treated maxillary first molars under functional loading. This study tested two null hypotheses: (1) no significant differences exist in dentin and periodontal tissue stress distribution or fracture probability across resection lengths. (2) No significant biomechanical response differences occur between MB root morphologies (circular vs. oval) post-EMS. Our findings highlight the importance of controlled resection and morphology-aware surgical planning and confirm that resection length significantly modified stress distribution patterns and fracture probabilities in dentin. Oval roots demonstrated greater postoperative biomechanical stability, as reflected by attenuated stress increments and lower fracture probability shifts compared to circular roots. Hence, both null hypotheses are refuted.

Accordingly, two representative MB root cross-sectional models were simulated: a circular model representing a geometry theoretically more sensitive to structural loss following apical shortening (mid-root aspect ratio: 1.15) and an oval model representing prevalent clinical long-axis extensions (mid-root aspect ratio: 2.3). Although circular cross-sections are uncommon clinically, this morphology was included as a theoretical comparator. This allowed isolation of the effect of aspect ratio on postoperative biomechanical behavior. By establishing this contrast, we could more clearly demonstrate how root cross-sectional geometry modulates the magnitude of stress increase and fracture probability following different extents of resection. This design drew from extensive CBCT investigations revealing predominantly non-circular MB root cross-sections, such as oval or ribbon-like configurations [11, 32, 33]. To examine the extremes of structural loss, we also included RAM. It provides a stark contrast to incremental resections and highlights load redistribution to the remaining roots. This comparative approach may help clinicians recognize morphologies that benefit from conservative resection, thereby enhancing the practical relevance of the findings.

From a biomechanical perspective, the mid-to-upper root segment (levels 4–7) emerged as the stress peak zone due to its location at the transition between the cervical fulcrum and the region of maximum root curvature. Stress peaks predominated on the buccal and palatal aspects of the palatal root across all models. This pattern arises from the rotational mechanics under oblique occlusal loading, with the cervical margin serving as the primary fulcrum. These observations align with prior reports of cervical stress predominance [25, 34]. In a tri-rooted maxillary molar, such loading tends to concentrate bending moments and shear forces toward the palatal root. Owing to its central position and greater root volume, the palatal root functions as the main load-bearing pillar. This region coincides with the initiation of alveolar crest support and maximum root curvature, subjecting it to the greatest bending moments under masticatory loads and rendering it prone to stress concentration. The gradual decline from the peak zone coronally and apically indicates differential stress dissipation mechanisms: coronal reliance on overall tooth geometry and apical attenuation likely reflects the combined buffering effects of PDL compliance and the surrounding alveolar bone. Although the highest von Mises stresses were consistently observed in the palatal root in all simulated models, clinical observations indicate that vertical root fractures following endodontic microsurgery most often involve the surgically treated root rather than adjacent untreated roots. This discrepancy likely stems from factors not fully captured in the present FEA, such as thinner dentinal walls after retrograde cavity preparation. While the models did simulate a 3-mm retrograde preparation in a single canal, the presence of an MB2 canal or isthmus (common in oval MB roots) would necessitate a wider preparation cavity, further reducing dentinal wall thickness and potentially amplifying local stress concentrations beyond those observed here. Other contributing factors may include preoperative or postoperative alveolar bone loss and variable healing responses in the periapical region [4, 35].

Consistently, simulated EMS elevated von Mises stresses in dentin and markedly increased Weibull fracture probabilities, primarily attributable to compromised root integrity and reduced load-bearing volume [36]. In circular root models, each additional millimeter of resection augmented stresses by an average of 3–4%. Notably, complete MB root amputation produced the greatest stress elevations and shifts in fracture probability, particularly in the apical regions of the remaining roots owing to load redistribution. These findings indicate a substantial reduction in overall fracture resistance with full root removal, suggesting that MB root amputation should be reserved as a last-resort procedure and accompanied by careful occlusal adjustment to protect the remaining roots.

Intriguingly, Aslan et al. [24] reported dentin stresses of 73.06 MPa following root amputation in mandibular first molars, nearly double the 39.10 MPa after root-end resection and 100% higher than the intact 36.60 MPa. This finding corroborates the observed association between greater resection extent and higher stress levels. In our maxillary models, even complete MB root amputation yielded far milder increments than mandibular extremes [24], owing to tri-rooted architecture and robust palatal root support that redistributes loads after MB root amputation, unlike the two-rooted mandibular configuration where single-root loss overburdened the remainder.

Our findings confirm root morphology as a determinant of postoperative biomechanical response. Although oval root models exhibited slightly higher baseline stresses in the intact state, this elevation primarily stems from their flattened cross-section. Such geometry creates thinner dentinal walls along the minor axis, which leads to uneven stress distribution and localized concentrations in non-circular configurations [37]. A FEA study confirmed this pattern: circular sections displayed superior stress distribution compared to oval ones [34]. Despite higher baseline stresses in the intact state, oval root models showed better tolerance to root-end resection length. This relative advantage likely stems from the oval cross-section's larger surface area and greater capacity for load dissipation, which facilitates more effective force transfer to the PDL. As a result, oval roots appeared less sensitive to incremental resection in the 2–4 mm range tested. In particular, the 4 mm resection left a somewhat blunter and more symmetrically rounded apical interface compared with the 3 mm level, a subtle geometric change that may have contributed to modestly improved stress distribution and a slight reduction in peak von Mises stress on the palatal aspect of the palatal root.

These relative advantages in oval roots were further supported by clear regional differences in stress increments: in peak zones (levels 4–7), increases in circular roots following complete amputation exceeded 15%, whereas in oval roots, increments remained below 10%; apically, increments in circular roots reached 45.9% versus 18.5% in oval roots. The combined buccal-palatal concentration at level 5 suggests multidirectional loading and potential crack initiation. Quantitatively, oval root-end resection stress increments were one-third to one-half those of circular root models. Under complete amputation, for example, level 6 oval increments were 5.9%, far below the 16.7% in circular root models. Weibull analysis substantiates this probabilistically: at 300 N, circular failure escalated from 46.63% to 59.93%, whereas the oval model showed a comparatively smaller increase, indicating a lower probability of structural failure under equivalent loading conditions. The 300 N threshold signals a steep escalation in fracture risk, urging occlusal force modulation and lateral load avoidance.

The PDL plays a critical role in postoperative tooth mobility and prognosis following EMS, working as a viscoelastic buffer [38]. Although absolute stress levels remain low, PDL stress increased monotonically with greater resection extent, particularly after complete root amputation and more prominently in oval root models. These modest yet consistent changes likely reflect structural adaptation of the PDL, driven by redistribution of periodontal fiber tension and loss of apical support. Localized stress concentrations may indicate regions of increased mechanical demand within the PDL [39]. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of postoperative periodontal monitoring and occlusal load control. Moderate root-end resection appears to offer a more favorable mechanical balance between effective apical tissue removal and preservation of periodontal stability, thereby supporting more predictable functional outcomes.

Clinically, these biomechanical findings suggest useful considerations for surgical planning in EMS. Oval roots appear to tolerate apical resection better under the simulated conditions. Circular roots, by contrast, seem more prone to stress increases following resection and may benefit from a more conservative approach. Preoperative imaging to assess root morphology could therefore help guide resection decisions, with 3 mm or less generally preferable for circular roots, whereas oval roots in the present models tolerated greater apical resection, with the 4 mm condition showing the most favorable stress distribution among the tested scenarios. These observations should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation from clinical studies.

The MB root shows substantial morphological diversity, with varying cross-sectional shapes at the apical third and their strong correlation to internal root canal system complexity commonly observed [9, 11]. Owing to the focused scope and computational limitations of the present study, these additional configurations were not included. In addition, the models relied on linear elastic assumptions and therefore did not simulate dynamic mastication, long-term fatigue, or time-dependent tissue responses. Important anatomical confounders such as isthmuses, MB2 canals, and variations in apical preparation size or taper were also not incorporated. Furthermore, the models were constructed using a standardized root length; variations in original root length or crown-root ratio were not evaluated and may influence the biomechanical response to apical resection. Consequently, the present analysis reflects immediate mechanical stress in dentin and PDL rather than long-term biomechanical or periodontal changes following EMS. Future studies incorporating a broader spectrum of root morphologies, anatomical variables, and loading conditions would help provide a more comprehensive understanding of how root shape modulates biomechanical outcomes after EMS.

Conclusion

In summary, this FEA study offers preliminary biomechanical insights that may assist in EMS decision-making. These patterns were observed under controlled modeling conditions and require validation through clinical outcome studies.

Resection extent: Increasing root-end resection was associated with progressive increases in dentin stress and fracture probability across the simulated conditions, with complete root amputation producing the most pronounced mechanical response.

Morphology-related behavior: Root cross-sectional morphology influenced postoperative biomechanical behavior. Circular MB roots showed greater susceptibility to stress elevation following resection, whereas oval configurations demonstrated comparatively lower stress increases under the simulated conditions.
