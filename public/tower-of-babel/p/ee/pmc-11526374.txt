Customizable Three-Dimensional Printed Zerovalent Iron: An Efficient and Reusable Fenton-like Reagent for Florfenicol Degradation
Authors: Dan Li, Yibo Fu, Wentao Hong, Shunlu Li, Mulan Qiu, Hongdie Yu, Heli Wang, Junhong Wu, Qian Yang, Sen Yang, Jianhui Xu, Yunfei Zhang, Shenggui Chen, Yin Zhong, Ping’an Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Zerovalent iron (Fe0)-based Fenton-like technology has great potential for treating recalcitrant organic pollutants (ROPs) in wastewater. However, rapidly and precisely manufacturing Fe0-based materials with the desired geometries is challenging. Herein, novel three-dimensional printed Fe0 (3DP-Fe0) and bimetallic 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were customized by 3D printing for efficient Fenton-like degradation of florfenicol (FLO), a typical antibiotic in wastewater. 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) exhibited superior reactivity toward FLO than 3DP-Fe0, generating hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and atomic hydrogen to achieve >90% dehalogenation and >70% total organic carbon removal within 10 min. The resulting degradation intermediates possessed lower antibacterial activity than FLO and did not cause resistance gene proliferation in activated sludge. The Fenton-like activity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was similar across different shapes but increased with increasing porosity and size. Compared with powdered Ni/Fe0, 3DP-Ni/Fe0 exhibited faster electron transfer during Fe(II)/Fe(III) cycling, which increased the utilization efficiency of dissolved Fe2+ and H2O2 for ·OH production. Moreover, 3DP-Ni/Fe0 could be reused >150 times, 5-fold more than powdered Ni/Fe0, owing to its lower metal ion release and Fe0 depletion. 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with H2O2 can also efficiently remove chemical oxygen demand from real wastewater and other ROPs (e.g., acetaminophen, carbamazepine, thiamphenicol, and tetrabromobisphenol A).

Introduction

Florfenicol (FLO) is a chloramphenicol antibiotic widely utilized in veterinary medicine and aquaculture1 and extensively detected in aquatic environments worldwide (see Table S1 in the Supporting Information for some examples), including wastewater, surface water, and drinking water, at levels up to 11 mg/L.2−4 Its prevalence can disrupt metabolic activity and promote antibiotic resistance in organisms, threatening ecosystems and human health.1 Given the growing public concern over FLO pollution and the spread of its resistance in aquatic environments, effective technologies to treat FLO-contaminated wastewater are essential. However, the relatively hydrophilic nature and recalcitrance of FLO make it difficult to be removed from wastewater via conventional treatment technologies, such as adsorption and biodegradation.5 Reductive dehalogenation is efficient to eliminate halogen atoms of FLO under anoxic conditions,6−8 but the mineralization of FLO remains a challenge.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) can enhance FLO mineralization and dehalogenation using reactive oxygen species [e.g., hydroxyl radicals (·OH), singlet oxygen (1O2), or superoxide radical anions (O2·–)],9,10 including photo-Fenton, electro-Fenton, and metal-based Fenton-like processes (see Table S2 for some examples). Zerovalent iron (Fe0)-based Fenton-like technology has advantages over other Fenton/Fenton-like processes in terms of superior stability, ease of handling, lower sludge production, and cost-effectiveness.11,12 It effectively generates ·OH in a controlled manner, which can remove various recalcitrant organic pollutants (ROPs), including antibiotics, chemical oxygen demand (COD), and colorants, and thereby enhances the biodegradability of wastewater from industrial, pharmaceutical, and municipal sources.13−15 However, Fe2+ regeneration is the rate-determining step in Fenton-like processes, limiting the overall Fenton-like activity of Fe0 toward ROPs.16,17 The modification of Fe0 by transition metals [e.g., copper (Cu), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co)] is a proven efficient strategy to promote the Fenton-like activity of Fe0.12,18−20

In the context of large-scale practical applications, the utilization of Fe0-based materials in water treatment encounters several challenges.21,22 For example, Fe0-based materials are typically powders prone to rapid aggregation, and their surface passivation can impede porosity, resulting in decreased reactivity and permeability. Additionally, deactivated Fe0-based powders are not easily recycled or reprocessed after use, with residual powders potentially exerting adverse effects on microbial communities and ecosystem functioning.23 Fe0-based powders are not easily shaped, preventing their reliable use in different physical spaces (e.g., pipes and reactors) during wastewater treatment. Although assembling Fe0-based powders into mechanically stable macrostructures may be possible,24,25 the reactivity and properties of the resulting Fe0-based macrostructures highly depend on the assembly process. Moreover, the available macrostructure geometries are often constrained, and Fe0-based powders tend to exhibit uneven distribution and detachment from such macrostructures. These drawbacks considerably weaken their reactivity and application potentials.

3D printing technology has emerged as an innovative approach to rapidly manufacture macrostructures with excellent size flexibility and controllable geometry to meet specific application scenarios.26,27 It is characterized by simple operation, strong flexibility, and high automation, facilitating rapid manufacturing of large-scale macrostructures in a single-step process.26,27 Selective laser melting (SLM) 3D printing technology is widely employed in the fabrication of metallic macrostructures.28 SLM utilizes a high-power laser beam to selectively fuse and consolidate fine metallic powders layer-by-layer based on a predefined computer-aided design, creating metallic macrostructures with complex and precise geometries.29,30 The geometric design of 3D printed metals enables precise control over the specific surface area (SSA), porous structures, inner surfaces, channel volumes, and spatial arrangements.29−31 These features have been shown to improve the performance of 3D printed metals (alloy, bulk metallic glass, and stainless steel) in various applications, such as methyl-orange degradation,29 metal infiltration and hydrogen production in methanol steam reforming microreactors,30 and liquid fuel synthesis and byproduct distribution in self-catalytic reactors.31 However, the effect of the structural properties of 3DP-Fe0-based materials on their Fenton-like activity remains unknown.

Herein, novel 3D printed Fe0 (3DP-Fe0) and bimetallic Fe0 (e.g., 3DP-Ni/Fe0, 3DP-Cu/Fe0, and 3DP-Co/Fe0) were customized using SLM 3D printing technology via predesigned models, and their Fenton-like activity were compared. The reusability of 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 in mediating FLO degradation was measured and compared with that of powdered Fe0 (P-Fe0) and Ni/Fe0 (P-Ni/Fe0) under the same conditions. The impacts of the structural properties of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (e.g., geometric shapes, porosities, and sizes) on the Fenton-like degradation of FLO were investigated. The mechanisms responsible for Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were explored via the analysis of the associated reactive species. Moreover, the intermediates of Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were identified, and their toxicities were investigated to assess the practical application prospects of 3DP-Ni/Fe0. The efficiency of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 in removal of COD from wastewater obtained from wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) and various ROPs [e.g., acetaminophen (ACT), carbamazepine (CBZ), thiamphenicol (TAP), and tetrabromobisphenol A (TBBPA)] frequently detected in aquatic environments worldwide (Table S1) was also investigated. Finally, a preliminarily cost analysis of 3DP-Fe0-based materials was conducted. Our findings provide insights into the preparation of 3DP-Fe0-based materials and their Fenton-like activity toward ROPs, paving the way for their practical applications in wastewater treatment.

Materials and Methods

Chemical Reagents

Chemical reagent details are provided in Text S1.

Preparation and Characterization of 3DP-Fe0-Based Materials

The 3DP-Fe0-based materials were prepared using an SLM150 3D printing machine (ZRapid Technologies Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), and the overall scheme is displayed in Figure 1a. The preparation of 3DP-Fe0-based materials is described in Text S2. The prepared 3DP-Fe0-based materials were stable and could be stored in open air. The microstructure, chemical composition, and physicochemical properties of the P-Fe0- and 3DP-Fe0-based materials were characterized via Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis, scanning electron microscopy–energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and electrochemical analyses. The geometric structures of 3DP-Fe0-based materials are presented in Figure S1. The characterization methods are described in Text S3.

Schematic diagram of the fabrication of 3DP-Fe0-based materials via selective laser melting 3D printing technology (a). SEM images of P-Fe0 (b,f,j), P-Ni/Fe0 (c,g,k), 3DP-Fe0 (d,h,l), and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (e,i,m). The 3DP-Fe0-based materials were in a cubic shape with a porosity of 40% and a size of 1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm. The Ni content of P-Ni/Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was 1%.

Fenton-like Degradation Experiments

Prior to Fenton-like degradation experiments, the Fe0-based materials were pretreated with 0.1 M HCl in an ultrasonic cleaning bath for 5 min and rinsed with deionized water 3 times to remove the surface iron oxide layer, which was beneficial to expose more active sites.32 The Fenton-like degradation experiments were conducted in 500 mL reactors under oxic conditions, which were placed on a horizontal rotary shaker (80 rpm) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) in the dark. The reaction suspension contained 1 g/L Fe0-based material, 10 mg/L FLO, and 1 mM H2O2, and the reaction suspension was initially adjusted to pH 3.0 ± 0.2 with 0.1 M HCl. FLO degradation was initiated by adding H2O2 to the reaction suspension. At predetermined time intervals, 2 mL aliquots of the reaction suspension were sampled and filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE filters in 5 mL screw-capped glass bottles. Subsequently, 2 mL of methanol was injected into each reaction suspension to terminate the degradation of FLO and its intermediates. After the mixture was uniformly mixed on a vortex mixer, a 1 mL aliquot was sampled from each reaction suspension for analysis of FLO and its degradation intermediates. Controls without materials or H2O2 were carried out under the same experimental conditions.

The reactivity of Fe0 and Ni/Fe0 after repeated reuse under oxic conditions and their H2 evolution in water under anoxic conditions were determined. The reusability and H2 evolution tests are described in Text S4. ·OH production and H2O2 activation were quantified during FLO degradation by Fe0 and Ni/Fe0, as described in Text S5. To identify the contributions of ·OH and atomic hydrogen (*H) to FLO degradation, several scavengers were added to the reaction suspensions during Fenton-like degradation, including tert-butyl alcohol (TBA), ethanol (EtOH), and 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP). Additional experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of the material dosage (0.5–2 g/L), H2O2 concentration (0.2–20 mM), initial pH (3.0–7.0), anion type and concentration (Cl–, HCO3–, and NO3–; 1.0–10 mM), and water sample type on Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0. Moreover, the Fenton-like activity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 in removing COD from wastewater obtained from WWTPs and various ROPs (ACT, CBZ, TAP, and TBBPA) was further evaluated.

Analytical Methods

The concentrations of FLO, ACT, CBZ, TAP, and TBBPA were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an LC-16 (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a UV detector. FLO degradation intermediates were identified by liquid chromatography (LC) using a Nexera Prominence LC20D (Shimadzu) coupled with an X500R QTOF high-resolution hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS, AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). The HPLC-UV and LC-QTOF-MS analytical parameters are listed in Tables S3 and S4. The H2O2 and Fe species were determined by UV–vis spectrophotometry using a UV-1800 instrument (Mapada, Shanghai, China) according to modified N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine and 1,10-phenanthroline analytical methods, respectively.33 The Fe0 content was determined by measuring the amount of H2 in the headspace after acid digestion with 37% HCl solution.34 The amounts of H2 and CO2 in the headspace were quantified by gas chromatography–thermal conductivity detection (GC-TCD) using an Agilent 7820A instrument (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the method reported by Qin et al.35

Toxicity Evaluation

Quantitative structure–activity relationship analysis was performed to estimate the toxicity of intermediates from FLO degradation by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 to aquatic organisms (e.g., fish, daphnids, and green algae) using the Ecological Structure–Activity Relationship (ECOSAR) tool. The antibacterial activities of the FLO degradation intermediates were evaluated based on their toxicity to Escherichia coli (E. coli) DH5α in the blank (culture medium), control (culture medium + Fenton-like reaction solution with 3DP-Ni/Fe0), FLO (culture medium + FLO), and treated FLO (culture medium + Fenton-like reaction solution with 3DP-Ni/Fe0 and FLO) systems. The impacts of FLO and its degradation intermediates on COD and NH4–N removal, microbial community composition and function, and FLO antibiotic resistance gene (floR) expression in activated sludge were evaluated in the above-mentioned systems. The toxicity evaluation details are described in Texts S6–S10.

Results and Discussion

Surface Characterization, Electrochemical Properties, and Hydrogen Evolution Potential of 3DP-Fe0-Based Materials

As shown in Figure 1b–m, 3D printing significantly impacted the surface structure of P-Fe0. 3DP-Fe0 displayed a stacked network structure with an irregular surface and uniform pores, which was different from P-Fe0 having a spherical structure with a fairly uniform size. The SSA of 3DP-Fe0 was greatly reduced, which was about 6.7 times smaller than that of P-Fe0 (Figure S2). The addition of Ni did not impact the surface distribution of Fe species but induced the formation of cracks on the surfaces of P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0 (Figure 1 and Figure S3). Compared with 3DP-Fe0, 3DP-Ni/Fe0 exhibited a rougher surface with a greater abundance of small and heterogeneous particles (Figure 1). The SSA of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (0.323 m2/g) with a porosity of 40% was higher than that of 3DP-Fe0 (0.177 m2/g), and it increased from 0.00022 to 0.395 m2/g as the porosity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 increased from 0 to 65% (Figure S2).

3DP-Fe0 exhibited XRD patterns similar to that of P-Fe0, which corresponded with that of Fe0 (JCPDS file no. 65-4899) (Figure S4), suggesting that the 3D printing process did not alter the phase composition of Fe0. Nevertheless, the values of the lattice constant and spacing of the Fe0 body-centered cubic (BCC) structure of 3DP-Fe0 were larger than those of P-Fe0 (Figure S5). The expanded lattice of the Fe0 BCC structure tended to enhance electron transfer,36,37 which was supported by the electrochemical analysis results (Figure S6, Table S5, and Text S11). 3DP-Fe0 exhibited a lower charge-transfer resistance (Rct), a higher corrosion current (Icorr), and a more negative corrosion potential (Ecorr) than did P-Fe0. Moreover, a pair of oxidation and reduction peaks was observed in the cyclic voltammogram of 3DP-Fe0 but not in that of P-Fe0 (Figure S6), indicating better Fe(II)/Fe(III) cycling for 3DP-Fe0 than P-Fe0.38,39 The addition of Ni increased the electrochemical activity and electron transfer efficiency of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Figure S6, Table S5, and Text S11). Similar results were observed for the addition of Cu and Co (Figure S7, Table S5, and Text S11).

Additionally, XPS analysis revealed higher abundances of Fe(III)–O and lattice oxygen species in 3DP-Fe0 than in P-Fe0 (Figure S8, Table S6, and Text S12), suggesting that Fe0 oxidation occurred during the 3D printing process and potentially induced lower reactivity of 3DP-Fe0 toward water and protons than P-Fe0. Indeed, less hydrogen (H2) was released from 3DP-Fe0 under anoxic conditions than from P-Fe0 (Figure S9), indicating its superior stability and longevity in water.34 The addition of Ni slowed the oxidation of Fe0 (Figure S8, Table S6, and Text S12) and increased the reducing ability of 3DP-Fe0,40 resulting in a smaller absolute value of the H2 evolution potential and higher H2 evolution of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 compared to 3DP-Fe0 (Figures S6 and S9).

Fenton-like Activity of 3DP-Fe0-Based Materials

FLO removal was not observed in the H2O2 or 3DP-Fe0 system, ruling out the possibility that FLO removal occurred solely due to H2O2 or FLO adsorption on 3DP-Fe0 (Figure 2a). Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Fe0-based materials fitted the pseudo-first-order kinetic model well (Figure 2a and Figure S10), and the values of the pseudo-first-order FLO degradation rate constant (kFLO) and the surface area-normalized FLO degradation rate constant (kSA) were in the order 3DP-Fe0 < 3DP-Cu/Fe0 < 3DP-Co/Fe0 < 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Table S7). The higher FLO degradation efficiency of bimetallic 3DP-Fe0 compared to 3DP-Fe0 suggested that the addition of metals such as Cu, Co, and Ni could promote Fenton-like activity.18−20 Notably, 3DP-Ni/Fe0 possessed higher Fenton-like activity toward FLO degradation than did 3DP-Cu/Fe0 and 3DP-Co/Fe0, indicating that Ni was a more efficient additive than Cu and Co. This finding could be ascribed to a scenario in which Ni can lower charge-transfer resistance and increase electron transfer of 3DP-Fe0 to a larger extent than Cu and Co, which is more beneficial for the production of ·OH and degradation of FLO (Figure S7, Table S5, and Text S11). Moreover, the Fenton-like degradation efficiency of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was comparable to or higher than that by other AOPs (Table S2), illustrating its potential superiority in applications exploiting Fenton-like reactions.

Fenton-like activity and cumulative ·OH concentration (e), Fe2+dissolved and Fetotal concentrations (f), Fe2+dissolved utilization efficiency for ·OH production (g), H2O2 concentration (h), and H2O2 utilization efficiency for ·OH production (i) from P-Fe0, P-Ni/Fe0, 3DP-Fe0, and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 during Fenton-like degradation of FLO. Unless otherwise stated, the material dosage, H2O2 concentration, FLO concentration, transition metal content, and initial pH of the reaction solution were 1 g/L, 1 mM, 10 mg/L, 1%, and 3.0, respectively. The 3DP-Fe0-based materials were in a cubic shape with a porosity of 40% and a size of 1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm. For the effect of size on the Fenton-like activity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0, the material dosage increased from 0.25 to 1 g/L with its increasing size.

Subsequently, the patterns of Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with varying geometric shapes (i.e., cubic, cylindrical, and cuboid; Figure S1), porosities (i.e., 0, 40, and 65%), and sizes (i.e., 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.2 cm, 0.75 cm × 0.75 cm × 0.2 cm, and 1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm) were explored. 3DP-Ni/Fe0 possessing cubic, cylindrical, and cuboid shapes exhibited comparable Fenton-like activity in FLO degradation (Figure 2b), with kSA values of 0.798, 0.766, and 0.787 L/m2/min, respectively. This finding suggested that the shape did not substantially impact the inherent properties of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (e.g., the porosity and SSA) (Figures S1 and S2). By contrast, 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with different porosities exhibited varying Fenton-like activity in FLO degradation (Figure 2c). Specifically, the Fenton-like degradation efficiency of FLO increased with increasing porosity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 from 0 to 65%, which was likely due to a scenario where the higher porosity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 provided more surface active sites (Figure S2). These results aligned with those of previous studies exploring the effects of porosity on the reactivity of 3D printed materials.29−31 However, the kSA value of FLO degradation decreased with increasing porosity of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Table S7), suggesting that 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with a lower porosity had a higher density of surface active sites for Fenton-like degradation. Moreover, the Fenton-like degradation efficiency of FLO increased with increasing size of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Figure 2d), which was likely due to the larger size of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 possessing higher mass to offer more surface active sites.

Performance Comparison of P-Fe0- and 3DP-Fe0-Based Materials

P-Fe0 vs 3DP-Fe0

The kFLO value of FLO degradation by 3DP-Fe0 (0.102 min–1) was lower than that of P-Fe0 (0.445 min–1), likely due to the lower SSA of 3DP-Fe0 (0.177 m2/g) compared to P-Fe0 (1.188 m2/g) (Figure 2e, Figure S2, and Table S7). It seems that increasing the SSA of 3DP-Fe0 may be an efficient way to improve the kFLO value of the FLO degradation. Nevertheless, the kSA value of FLO degradation by 3DP-Fe0 (0.576 L/m2/min) was greater than that by P-Fe0 (0.374 L/m2/min) (Figure 2e and Table S7), implying that the 3D printing process can increase the density of surface active sites. The cumulative ·OH concentrations produced by P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0 within 10 min were 117.6 and 100.2 μM, respectively (Figure 2e). The FLO degradation efficiency increased in tandem with ·OH production as the reaction proceeded. The total Fe ion (Fetotal) and dissolved Fe2+ (Fe2+dissolved) concentrations released from 3DP-Fe0 were much lower than those released from P-Fe0 (Figure 2f), which could be ascribed to the lower reactivity of 3DP-Fe0 toward water and protons than P-Fe0 (Figure S9). Quenching tests with BPY (a chelator of Fe2+dissolved)41 confirmed the crucial role of Fe2+dissolved in activating H2O2 to produce ·OH for FLO degradation (Figure S11). In theory, 1 ·OH is produced from the reaction of 1 Fe2+dissolved with 1 H2O2 (Fe2+ + H+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + ·OH + H2O).17,18,20 The utilization efficiency of Fe2+dissolved for ·OH production [UE(Fe2+→·OH) = Ct,·OH/Ct,Fe2+] ranged from 0.16 to 0.57 for P-Fe0 and from 1.48 to 7.49 for 3DP-Fe0 (Figure 2g). The higher UE(Fe2+→·OH) for 3DP-Fe0 than for P-Fe0 suggested rapid electron transfer in Fe(II)/Fe(III) cycling for ·OH production from 3DP-Fe0, possibly due to its higher lattice constant and lower charge-transfer resistance compared to those of P-Fe0 (Figures S5 and S6). However, Fe2+dissolved from P-Fe0 might participate in side reactions with ·OH/O2 (Fe2+ + ·OH → Fe3+ + OH–, 2Fe2+ + O2 + H+ → 2Fe3+ + H2O2),17,18,20 reducing its UE(Fe2+→·OH), as evidenced by the increasing residual H2O2 concentration in later stages of the Fenton-like reaction (Figure 2h). Moreover, the utilization efficiency of H2O2 for ·OH production [UE(H2O2→·OH) = Ct,·OH/(C0,H2O2 – Ct,H2O2)] was higher for 3DP-Fe0 than for P-Fe0 at the Fenton-like reaction onset (Figure 2i). The increasing UE(H2O2→·OH) for P-Fe0 in later stages of the Fenton-like reaction could be ascribed to the increasing residual H2O2 concentration.

P-Ni/Fe0 vs 3DP-Ni/Fe0

The addition of Ni increased the SSA of P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0 to 1.713 and 0.323 m2/g, respectively, resulting in higher kFLO values of FLO degradation by P-Ni/Fe0 (0.826 min–1) and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (0.258 min–1) compared to P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0 (Figure 2e, Figure S2, and Table S7). Moreover, the addition of Ni also increased the density of surface active sites on P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0, leading to higher kSA values of FLO degradation by P-Ni/Fe0 (0.482 L/m2/min) and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (0.798 L/m2/min) (Figure 2e and Table S7). The cumulative ·OH concentrations produced by P-Ni/Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 after 10 min of reaction were 127.8 and 114.1 μM, respectively, which were higher than those produced by P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0 (Figure 2e). These results suggested that the addition of Ni enhanced ·OH production. Additionally, the Fetotal concentration released from P-Ni/Fe0 (984.7 μM) was higher than that released from P-Fe0 (930.9 μM) (Figure 2f), indicating that the addition of Ni enhanced Fe0 corrosion. However, 3DP-Ni/Fe0 released a lower Fetotal concentration (85.8 μM) than 3DP-Fe0 (104.0 μM) (Figure 2f), likely because Ni oxidation during the 3D printing process resulted in the formation of a passivation layer that impeded Fe ion release from 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Figure S12 and Text S12). Moreover, the Fe2+dissolved concentrations from P-Ni/Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were 740.2 and 74.8 μM, respectively, surpassing those from P-Fe0 (736.3 μM) and 3DP-Fe0 (67.7 μM) (Figure 2f). These results could be ascribed to rapid electron transfer for Fe3+ reduction (Figure S6), which in turn boosted UE(Fe2+→·OH) for P-Ni/Fe0 (0.17–0.65) and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (1.53–13.7) (Figure 2g). The UE(H2O2→·OH) patterns for P-Ni/Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were similar to those for P-Fe0 and 3DP-Fe0, respectively (Figures 2i). However, the higher UE(H2O2→·OH) values for P-Ni/Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 indicated that the addition of Ni improved the overall Fenton-like reaction efficiency.

The Reusability of P-Fe0, P-Ni/Fe0, 3DP-Fe0, and 3DP-Ni/Fe0

After 30 cycles of reuse, the Fenton-like activities of P-Fe0 and P-Ni/Fe0 toward FLO degradation were nearly exhausted (Figure 3a), whereas 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 retained >70 and 90% of their initial activities (Figure 3b), respectively, suggesting that 3D printing enhanced the reusability of Fe0 and Ni/Fe0. In each cycle, the Fetotal concentrations released from 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were approximately 10-fold lower than those from P-Fe0 and P-Ni/Fe0 (Figure 3c,d). Moreover, the Fe0 depletion of 3DP-Fe0 (18.7%) and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (13.2%) after 30 cycles of reuse was lower than that of P-Fe0 (99.8%) and P-Ni/Fe0 (99.7%) (Figure 3e,f). The lower Fetotal release and Fe0 depletion for 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 compared to P-Fe0 and P-Ni/Fe0 suggested their excellent stability and longevity, even in the presence of oxidants in open air. After 150 cycles of reuse, the FLO degradation efficiencies of 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 decreased to 10 and 50%, respectively (Figure 3b), indicating that the addition of Ni could improve the reusability of 3DP-Fe0. This increased reusability could be attributed to a scenario where the addition of Ni facilitates Fe(II)/Fe(III) cycling during Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0.42,43 In fact, the proportion of Fe2+dissolved in the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 system was higher than that in the 3DP-Fe0 system (Figure S13).

The Effects of Reaction Conditions on Fenton-like Degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0

Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was further evaluated under various conditions, including different Ni contents, H2O2 and material dosages, initial pH, anions, and water sample types (Figure S14 and Table S7), as described in Text S13. Briefly, the highest kSA of FLO degradation (0.807 L/m2/min) was achieved with a 5% Ni content, which was similar to that achieved with a 1% Ni content (0.798 L/m2/min) (Figure S14). The Ni content greatly impacted the charge-transfer resistance and electron transfer efficiency of 3DP-Ni/Fe0, leading to a discrepancy in Fenton-like activity (Figure S15 and Text S11). The kSA of FLO degradation by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 increased from 0.541 to 4.644 L/m2/min as the H2O2 dosage increased from 0.2 to 5 mM and then stabilized at 4.356 L/m2/min as the H2O2 dosage reached 20 mM (Figure S14). The optimal H2O2 dosage for Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was 1 mM, with its higher utilization efficiency for ·OH production (Figures S14 and S16). Additionally, as the H2O2 dosage increased from 1 to 20 mM, the removal efficiencies of TOC, Cl–, and F– from FLO increased from 32.6, 34.4, and 28.7% to 72.4, 93.1, and 90.5% after 10 min of reaction, respectively (Figure S17). Increasing the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 dosage enhanced the kSA of FLO degradation by increasing the abundance of available surface active sites (Figure S14).44 The optimal pH for Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was 3.0 (Figure S14), which was likely attributed to the increased Fe0 corrosion and reactive species production.45 The kSA of FLO degradation decreased in the presence of Cl– and HCO3– (1–10 mM), whereas it increased at high NO3– concentrations (10 mM) (Figure S14). Additionally, the kSA of FLO degradation decreased in different water samples (e.g., surface water and artificial wastewater) (Figure S14 and Table S8), which was possibly due to a scenario in which coexisting substances occupied surface active sites.

Pathways and Mechanisms of FLO Degradation by 3DP-Ni/Fe0

Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy revealed the characteristic spectrum of the DMPO-·OH adduct46 in the Fenton-like reaction with 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Figure 4a). To clarify the contribution of ·OH, the effects of TBA (a scavenger of free and surface-bound ·OH)47 and ethanol (a scavenger of free ·OH)48 on the Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were investigated (Figure 4b). The decrease in FLO degradation efficiency associated with the addition of TBA was similar to that of ethanol, suggesting that free ·OH was dominant in the Fenton-like reaction.47,48 Interestingly, the addition of 2,4-DCP (a scavenger of *H) inhibited Fenton-like degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (Figure 4b), indicating the involvement of *H in the process.49 Indeed, the reductive degradation of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 alone was achieved under anoxic conditions (Figure S18), confirming that *H from 3DP-Ni/Fe0 contributed to FLO degradation. The *H in the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 system may be produced via (i) the H2 dissociation induced by the Ni additive, where the H2 originated from the reaction between Fe0 and water/H+, and (ii) the reduction of H+ facilitated by the synergistic interaction between Fe0 and Ni, where electrons released from Fe0 oxidation were transferred through the Ni surface to reduce H+.18,20 The resulting *H was strongly adsorbed and activated on the Ni surface, making it highly reactive and capable of participating in subsequent hydrogenation reactions.18,20

The material dosage, H2O2 concentration, FLO concentration, Ni content, and initial pH of the reaction solution were 1 g/L, 1 mM, 10 mg/L, 1%, and 3.0, respectively. 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was in a cubic shape with a porosity of 40% and a size of 1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm.

A total of 15 intermediates from Fenton-like degradation of FLO in the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 system with 1 mM H2O2 were detected by LC-QTOF-MS (Figure 4c, Figure S19, and Table S9), namely, C12H15Cl2NO5S, C12H14Cl2FNO5S, C12H13Cl2NO4S, C12H12Cl2FNO4S, C12H11Cl2NO5S, C12H11Cl2NO4S, C11H12Cl2FNO2, C9H10O4, C8H10O3S, C8H8O5S, C8H8O4S, C8H8O3S, C7H8O3S, C7H8O2S, and C5H6Cl2FNO3. Based on identification of the reactive species and degradation intermediates, several degradation pathways of FLO were proposed (Figures 4c), including (i) C–S bond cleavage to form C11H12Cl2FNO2 followed by defluorination, C–N bond cleavage, and ·OH electrophilic oxidation of the benzene ring and carbon chain to form C9H10O4; (ii) ·OH electrophilic oxidation of the carbon chain to form C12H12Cl2FNO4S followed by C–C bond cleavage to form C8H8O3S; (iii) defluorination and hydroxylation to form C12H15Cl2NO5S followed by C–C bond cleavage to form C8H10O3S and ·OH electrophilic oxidation of the carbon chain to form C8H8O3S; (iv) benzene ring hydroxylation to form C12H14Cl2FNO5S followed by C–C bond cleavage and ·OH electrophilic oxidation of the carbon chain to form C8H8O4S and C8H8O5S or C–C bond cleavage to form C7H8O3S; (v) C–C bond cleavage to form C5H6Cl2FNO3 and C7H8O2S followed by benzene ring hydroxylation of C7H8O2S to form C7H8O3S; (vi) dehydrofluorination to form C12H13Cl2NO4S followed by ·OH electrophilic oxidation of the carbon chain to form C12H11Cl2NO4S, benzene ring hydroxylation to form C12H11Cl2NO5S, and C–C bond cleavage to form C7H8O3S.

Subsequently, the intermediates from the Fenton-like degradation of FLO in the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 + 2,4-DCP system with 1 mM H2O2 were further analyzed (Figure S20 and Text S14). Compared to the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 system, the FLO hydrodechlorination intermediate C12H15ClFNO4S was observed only in the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 + 2,4-DCP system, likely because the capture of *H by 2,4-DCP hindered its further degradation. This discrepancy further confirmed the involvement of *H in the dehalogenation of FLO in the Fenton-like reaction with 3DP-Ni/Fe0.

The relative abundances of the FLO degradation intermediates in the 3DP-Ni/Fe0 system with 1 mM H2O2 increased to peak values and then gradually decreased as the reaction proceeded (Figure S21). After 10 min of reaction, 100% of FLO was degraded with the removal of TOC (32.6%), Cl– (34.4%), and F– (28.7%), respectively (Figure S17). Moreover, 68.9% of removed TOC was converted to CO2 (Figures S22 and S23), suggesting that FLO was dehalogenated and mineralized by 3DP-Ni/Fe0. These results suggest that the identified intermediates may undergo further transformation to form undetected small molecules that can be ultimately mineralized to nontoxic CO2 by ·OH. Additionally, the degradation and mineralization efficiencies of FLO increased with an increasing H2O2 concentration (in the range of 1–20 mM; Figures S17, S22, and S23). The mineralization efficiency of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with 20 mM H2O2 was higher compared to those by other AOPs (Table S2).

Toxicity Evaluation of FLO Degradation Intermediates

ECOSAR is a standard tool widely employed by previous studies within environmental toxicology to provide quantitative and qualitative predictions of chemical toxicity (e.g., acute and chronic) of compounds of interest based on chemical structures and structure–activity relationships.50 According to the ECOSAR analysis of FLO degradation intermediates, C12H13Cl2NO4S, C12H12Cl2FNO4S, C12H11Cl2NO4S, C9H10O4, C8H8O4S, and C8H8O3S could be considered as not harmful to aquatic organisms. In contrast, C11H12Cl2FNO2, C8H10O3S, C7H8O2S, and C5H6Cl2FNO3 were harmful, C12H15Cl2NO5S, C12H14Cl2FNO5S, and C8H8O5S were toxic, and C12H11Cl2NO5S and C7H8O3S were very toxic to aquatic organisms (Figure S24 and Table S10). However, the accuracy of these predictions largely depends on model assumptions and data quality,50 which should be carefully considered when assessing the environmental risks of these FLO degradation intermediates.

The antibacterial activity test demonstrated that the number of viable E. coli DH5α cells was greatly decreased in the FLO system compared to those in the blank and control systems (Figure 5a). By contrast, the number of viable E. coli DH5α cells in the treated FLO system was comparable to those in the blank and control systems, suggesting that FLO degradation intermediates did not inhibit the growth of E. coli DH5α and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 treatment mitigated the antibacterial activities of FLO (Figure 5a).

Additionally, FLO decreased the microbial community diversity (Figures S25 and S26) and negatively affected COD and NH4–N removal in activated sludge (Figure 5b,c). However, the α- and β-diversities of the microbial community in the treated FLO system were comparable to those in the blank and control systems and much higher than those in the FLO system (Figures S25 and S26). At the phylum level, Proteobacteria, known to be resistant to FLO,51 was greatly enriched in the FLO system but not in the treated FLO system (Figure 5d). At the genus level, the relative abundances of Brevundimonas and Nitrospira in the treated FLO system were higher than those in the FLO system (Figure 5e). Notably, Nitrospira is a key genus of nitrifying organisms (e.g., complete ammonia oxidizers and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria),52 and Brevundimonas is a genus of aerobic denitrifying bacteria.53 These results indicated that Fenton-like treatment with 3DP-Ni/Fe0 efficiently mitigated the negative impact of FLO on the nitrogen cycle.54 Indeed, NH4–N removal was much lower in the FLO system than in the treated FLO, blank, and control systems (Figure 5c). Moreover, qPCR analysis of FLO resistance gene (floR) expression revealed that its relative abundance in the treated FLO system (0.14) was similar to that in the blank (0.08) and control (0.14) systems, which was almost 100-fold lower than that in the FLO system (13.2) (Figure 5f). These results suggested that Fenton-like treatment of FLO by 3DP-Ni/Fe0 could substantially suppress floR expression in downstream activated sludge treatment processes, thereby reducing the potential proliferation and transmission of floR.

The Application of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 in Fenton-like Degradation of Various Pollutants

3DP-Ni/Fe0 exhibited excellent Fenton-like activity in the removal of COD from wastewater obtained from WWTPs, demonstrating a removal efficiency of approximately 80% after 10 min of reaction (Figure S27). Moreover, highly efficient removal of ROPs (90% for ACT and 100% for CBZ, TAP, and TBBPA) was achieved after 10 min of reaction with 3DP-Ni/Fe0 with 1 mM H2O2. The decreased TOC indicated that the degradation intermediates of these ROPs were partially removed (Figure S27). These results suggested that 3DP-Ni/Fe0 not only completely removed these ROPs but also increased the degradation of the intermediates of these ROPs, simplifying downstream treatment and removing the ROP contamination. These findings highlight the application potential of 3DP-Ni/Fe0 in Fenton-like wastewater treatment.

Cost Analysis

Generally speaking, the total cost for producing a 3D material by SLM 3D technology comprises direct (raw materials) and indirect (machine, production labor, and other) costs.55 Herein, Fe and Ni powders were utilized as raw materials. The prices of the Fe and Ni powders were approximately 0.0525 and 0.0364 USD/g, respectively, falling within the historical price range of conventional Fe0 (0.00055–0.088 USD/g)32 and Ni (0.005–0.05 USD/g)56 powders. Machine costs comprise expenses related to machine purchase, maintenance, and administration.55 Production labor mainly entails technical staff salaries, and other costs include gas, energy, and power charges.55 The costs of producing 1 g of 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (with a Ni content of 1%) are detailed in Table S11. The prices of 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were approximately 0.4698 and 0.4697 USD/g, respectively, representing a 9-fold increase compared to the Fe0 (0.0525 USD/g) and Ni/Fe0 (0.0523 USD/g) powders. Notably, the reusability of 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (150 cycles) was 5 times higher than that of P-Fe0 and P-Ni/Fe0 (30 cycles) (Figure 3), suggesting a potential 80% reduction in material input under identical experimental conditions. Consequently, the costs of treating wastewater with 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 were about 1.8-fold higher than those of using P-Fe0 and P-Ni/Fe0. However, the release of Fetotal from 3DP-Fe0 and 3DP-Ni/Fe0 was approximately 10-fold lower than that from P-Fe0 and P-Ni/Fe0 (Figure 3c,d), indicating potential cost savings in subsequent iron sludge treatment. Thus, the decreases in material input and iron sludge production by using 3DP-Fe0-based materials can enhance both the cost-effectiveness and ecological benefits.

Environmental Implications

In this study, a series of Fe0 and bimetallic Fe0 were prepared by SLM 3D printing technology, and their performances in Fenton-like degradation of FLO were explored. Our results demonstrated that 3DP-Ni/Fe0 had the most outstanding Fenton-like activity. Compared to conventional P-Fe0-based materials, 3DP-Fe0-based materials offered the following advantages: (i) They had a higher density of surface active sites, lower charge-transfer resistance, and faster electron transfer in Fe(II)/Fe(III) cycling for ·OH production, leading to higher kSA values of FLO degradation. (ii) They had higher stability, longevity, and reusability due to their slower Fe ion release and Fe0 depletion. (iii) They could greatly reduce the negative impacts of FLO, simplifying subsequent treatment processes. (iv) Although Ni2+ has harmful impacts on immunity, neurology, reproduction, and development,57 the Ni2+ concentration released from 3DP-Ni/Fe0 (0.010–0.016 mg/L) in the reusability test (10 min of reaction for each cycle, 150 cycles) was below the limit (0.02 mg/L) set by the Chinese National Standard for Drinking Water (GB 5749-2006)58 and the European Union Drinking Water Directive (Council Directive 98/83/EC),59 whereas that released from P-Ni/Fe0 (0.028–0.054 mg/L, 30 cycles) exceeded the limit. (v) The 3DP-Fe0-based materials exhibited unparalleled superiority in geometric customization (including shape, porosity, and size), thereby better fulfilling requirements of experiments and engineering applications than P-Fe0-based materials. As illustrated in Figure S28, 3DP-Fe0-based materials tailored to match laboratory reactor assembly conditions achieved a high Fenton-like degradation efficiency of FLO in the reactors. The potential applications of 3DP-Fe0-based materials in wastewater treatment included water pipes, an up-flow reactor, a horizontal flow reactor, and a river remediation system (Figure 6).

However, practical application of 3DP-Fe0-based materials in wastewater treatment remains in the initial stage of development, necessitating further research to enhance their reliability and efficiency. Moreover, the objects produced by 3D printing need to be assembled to meet large-scale demands, which may increase labor and manufacturing costs. Nevertheless, the design of advanced Fe0-based materials, such as surface-sulfidized and lattice-doped Fe0,60−63 through 3D printing technology may improve material performance and reduce scaling-up cost. By adjusting printing parameters (e.g., the temperature, speed, resolution, and cooling rate), 3D printing technology may regulate the physicochemical properties of 3DP-Fe0-based materials (e.g., the SSA, morphology, composition, hydrophobicity, lattice constant, coordination number, bond length, elemental distribution, charge-transfer resistance, and geometric characteristics). This kind of regulation has the potential to facilitate the optimal synthesis of 3DP-Fe0-based materials and to enhance their practical applications in wastewater treatment, which is a key area for future research.
