Crystalglobulin-Induced Nephropathy and Cardiac Nonamyloidotic Immunoglobulin Deposition Disease: A Fatal Case Report
Authors: Yusuke Ushio, Shun Manabe, Momoko Seki, Ayano Tanaka, Mayuko Suyama, Anna Nakai, Shizuka Kobayashi, Shiho Makabe, Yuki Kawaguchi, Naoko Ito, Saeko Yoshizawa, Shintaro Haruki, Kentaro Yoshinaga, Sekiko Taneda, Kazuho Honda, Junichi Hoshino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrystalglobulin-induced nephropathy (CIN) is a rare renal disorder associated with multiple myeloma, characterized by crystal-induced damage in multiple organs, with diagnosis based on the identification of crystals within affected tissues. Conversely, cardiac nonamyloidotic immunoglobulin deposition disease (CIDD) involves the deposition of nonamyloid light chains in the myocardium. In this study, we report a case of CIN combined with CIDD in a 55-year-old woman. Renal biopsy revealed crystalline structures within the interlobular artery, which stained positive for immunoglobulin G1 and lambda chains on fluorescent antibody analysis. Immunoelectron microscopy confirmed the deposition of lambda light chains within the crystalline structures. Fluorescent antibody staining demonstrated lambda-chain deposition in the skin, duodenum, and cardiac tissue. The patient was diagnosed with CIN and chemotherapy was initiated. However, the patient died owing to acute renal failure and rapidly progressive cardiac dysfunction. Autopsy findings revealed no crystalline structures in the myocardium, suggesting an association between CIN and CIDD. Reports on CIN are scarce, and its pathogenesis remains poorly understood. CIDD is also rare, and to date, no previous reports have described concurrent CIN and CIDD with such an abrupt clinical course. Early diagnosis and prompt therapeutic intervention are necessary.Index Words: crystal-induced nephropathy, crystalglobulin-induced nephropathy, monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance, multiple myeloma, cardiac nonamyloidotic immunoglobulin deposition disease

Introduction

Crystalglobulin-induced nephropathy (CIN) may result from the formation of crystals owing to increased monoclonal immunoglobulin (Ig) produced by abnormal plasma cells, as seen in multiple myeloma (MM); however, the pathogenesis remains unclear.1 Renal dysfunction associated with MM varies widely. Nasr et al2 reported the following 3 common renal biopsy findings in MM: (1) cast nephropathy (33%), (2) monoclonal Ig deposition disease (22%), and (3) amyloidosis (21%). Other reported findings include malignant plasma cell infiltration into the stroma (1%), fibrillary nephropathy (1%), immunotactoid nephropathy (0.5%), and light-chain proximal tubulopathy (0.5%). These reports indicate that CIN is an extremely rare disease.

When Ig is deposited in the heart, differential diagnoses include amyloid light-chain amyloidosis, light-chain deposition disease, and light- and heavy-chain deposition disease. If Congo red staining is negative, electron microscopy shows amorphous granular electron-dense deposits, and immunofluorescence is positive for light chains and/or heavy chains, the diagnosis is consistent with cardiac nonamyloidotic Ig deposition disease (CIDD).3

Neither CIN nor CIDD has been previously reported, and their clinical course varies widely. No previous cases have described the concurrent occurrence of CIN and CIDD. This report presents a rapidly progressive case of CIN complicated by CIDD.

Case Report

This case involved a 55-year-old female patient with a history of stroke, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and no relevant family history. Two months before hospitalization, she contracted coronavirus disease and subsequently developed bilateral lower limb edema 2 weeks later. Evaluation by her local physician revealed renal dysfunction (creatinine 1.89 mg/dL) and abnormal urinary findings including proteinuria (3+) and hematuria (occult blood 2+). She had a suspected history of allergy to local anesthesia and was admitted for an open renal biopsy.

Upon admission, her blood pressure was 160/99 mm Hg, and other vital signs were within normal limits. Pitting edema of the lower extremities was observed. Laboratory findings revealed a white blood cell count of 9.07 × 103/μL, hemoglobin level of 8.0 g/dL, platelet count of 17.9 × 104/μL, total protein of 6.1 g/dL, albumin of 2.8 g/dL, blood urea nitrogen of 29.2 mg/dL, creatinine of 2.4 mg/dL, estimated glomerular filtration rate of 17.4 mL/min/1.73 m2, sodium of 137 mEq/L, potassium of 3.5 mEq/L, chloride of 104 mEq/L, calcium of 7.7 mg/dL, phosphorous of 4.4 mg/dL, IgG of 1,105 mg/dL, IgA of 97 mg/dL, IgM of 79 mg/dL, CH50 of >60.0 U/mL, C3 of 133.9 mg/dL, and C4 of 15.6 mg/dL. Tests for antinuclear antibody, myeloperoxidase-antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, proteinase 3-antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, and antiglomerular basement membrane antibody were negative. Serum M-protein was of the IgG-λ type, and the free light-chain ratio (κ/λ) was 16.4/77.4 (0.21). Serum β2 microglobulin was elevated (13.6 mg/L), and cryoglobulin was negative. Urine analysis revealed proteinuria (9.33 g/gCr), glycosuria (2+), urinary sediment with 1-4 red blood cells /high-power field, N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase of 64.9 U/L, and urinary β2 microglobulin of 809 μg/L. Bone marrow biopsy revealed cluster of differentiation 138--positive plasma cells comprising ∼10% of the hematopoietic cells.

A renal open biopsy was performed, and light microscopy revealed 39 glomeruli, of which 3 showed global sclerosis, increased mesangial matrix, endocapillary hypercellularity, and periodic acid--Schiff--negative thrombus-like material in the glomerular tufts (Fig 1). Periodic acid--Schiff--negative thrombus-like material was also observed in the glomerular capillary loops. A few plasma cells were noted in the tubular interstitium, and proliferative changes were observed in the interlobular arteries (Fig 1). Immunofluorescence staining revealed IgG(+), IgA(-), IgM(±), C1q(-), C3(±), and C4(-) (Fig S1), and IgG subclasses IgG1(+), IgG2(-), IgG3(-), and IgG4(-). Kappa light-chain staining was negative, whereas lambda light-chain staining was positive (Fig 2). Paraffin sections stained positive for IgG and lambda chains, consistent with thrombus-like structures within the glomerular tufts. Electron microscopy did not reveal electron-dense deposits, and the glomerular tufts were filled with organized structures (Fig S2). Immunoelectron microscopy confirmed the presence of lambda chains within the crystal structures (Fig 1). Similar lambda staining was observed in the skin, duodenum, and heart. Immunofluorescence staining of the cardiac specimen showed IgG1(+), IgG2(-), IgG3(-), and IgG4(-). Kappa light-chain staining was negative, whereas lambda light-chain staining was positive (Fig 3). Congo red staining was negative in all specimens.

The patient’s kidney function worsened after admission and she became anuric; therefore, hemodialysis was initiated. She also experienced chest pain and was found to have lactic acidosis. Accordingly, she was admitted to the Cardiac Care Unit due to suspected cardiogenic shock. Coronary angiography was performed, but ischemic heart disease was ruled out. CIN was diagnosed based on previous pathology results. Treatment with dexamethasone and daratumumab plus bortezomib was initiated; however, the patient’s kidney function did not improve, and she remained on dialysis. Her cardiac function declined gradually (left ventricular ejection fraction from 50%-19%), lactic acidosis worsened, blood pressure continued to drop, and she died 3 months after admission. Pathological autopsy revealed no visible crystalline structures in the heart. Crystalline structures were found only in the kidneys, with no such structures identified in other organs, including the heart. Therefore, the cardiac dysfunction was attributed to light-chain deposition, and the case was diagnosed as CIN combined with CIDD.

After admission, the patient’s kidney function deteriorated, leading to anuria, necessitating initiation of hemodialysis. Chest pain accompanied by lactic acidosis prompted admission to the Cardiac Care Unit due to suspected cardiogenic etiology. Coronary angiography was performed, which ruled out ischemic heart disease. CIN diagnosis was established based on prior pathological findings. Treatment with dexamethasone, daratumumab, and bortezomib was started. Despite therapy, kidney function did not recover, and dialysis was continued. Cardiac function progressively declined (left ventricular ejection fraction from 50%-19%), lactic acidosis worsened, hypotension persisted, and the patient died 3 months after admission. Postmortem examination revealed no detectable crystalline deposits in the myocardium. Crystalline deposits were confined to the kidneys, with none detected in other organs, including the heart. Cardiac dysfunction was attributed to nonamyloidotic light-chain deposition, and the patient was diagnosed with CIN combined with CIDD.

Discussion

In this case report, a 55-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital with nephrotic syndrome and acute kidney injury and was diagnosed with CIN with CIDD based on pathological findings. Crystals were observed in the vascular space of the kidney, and uneven light-chain deposition was identified in other organs. However, embolization in the vascular space could not be demonstrated. The patient exhibited rapid deterioration of kidney and cardiac function and subsequently died 3 months after admission. To the best of our knowledge, no reports have documented cases in which each of these events was attributed to different etiological factors.

Crystalglobulin is a monoclonal protein that crystallizes within microvessels throughout the body and causes vascular injury. As mentioned above, this has been reported mainly in cases of MM, although the exact mechanism by which the monoclonal protein crystallizes remains unknown. The condition is diagnosed based on histological evaluation. However, when crystals are identified in kidney tissues, a differential diagnosis is required, and the disease concept of paraprotein-induced crystalline nephropathy has been proposed. Paraprotein-induced crystalline nephropathy encompasses light-chain proximal tubulopathy, crystal-storing histiocytosis, crystalline cast nephropathy, and CIN. Light-chain proximal tubulopathy and crystal-storing histiocytosis are suspected when the crystals are intracellular, whereas crystalline cast nephropathy and CIN are suspected when the crystals are extracellular. Clinically, the former is characterized by a slow decline in kidney function, often accompanied by Fanconi syndrome, whereas the latter typically presents with a rapid decline in kidney function.

Cardiac involvement is rare, occurring in ∼20% of cases of light-chain deposition.4 The outcome of CIDD and its response to treatment are not well understood, with some reports suggesting reversibility5 and others describing progressive lesions unresponsive to therapy.3 Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic presentations to overt cardiac failure and rhythm disturbances. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are useful for diagnosis.6,7

Treatment is not yet well established and is often modeled after regimens used for MM. Limited clinical improvement has been observed with cyclophosphamide and melphalan.8 Some reports have shown that chemotherapy with thalidomide and related agents may produce a therapeutic response.9 Apheresis has also been employed in some cases.8,10 However, kidney function improvement remains limited, and maintenance dialysis is frequently required. This is thought to reflect an underlying ischemic state8; hence, early diagnosis and intervention are considered essential.

The precise mechanism of crystalglobulin formation in this case remains unclear. Previous studies have suggested that physicochemical properties and specific amino acid sequences of monoclonal Ig heavy or light chains may determine the propensity of these proteins to form crystalline deposits in tissues.1,8,9

In particular, abnormalities such as increased hydrophobicity, aberrant glycosylation, or resistance to proteolysis have been reported to promote crystal formation in cases of paraprotein-induced crystalline nephropathies, including light-chain proximal tubulopathy, crystal-storing histiocytosis, and CIN.1,8,9

In this case, although detailed molecular analysis of the monoclonal Ig, including amino acid sequencing, was not performed, the presence of organized crystalline structures in the renal vascular spaces and monoclonal restriction to IgG1 and lambda chains suggests that intrinsic properties of the monoclonal protein contributed to crystal formation. Subtle physicochemical alterations in the variable regions of the light chain may have favored crystallization.9

Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise pathogenic mechanisms of crystalglobulin formation, which may involve not only the Ig sequence but also interactions with local tissue factors or posttranslational modifications.1,9

However, CIN is rare, and additional case accumulation is required to clarify its pathophysiology. Paraprotein-induced crystalline nephropathy, including CIN, necessitates tissue diagnosis for accurate identification. Aggressive biopsy of not only the kidneys but also other affected organs may be useful and facilitate early treatment.
