Sex Differences in Heart Transplantation Outcomes
Authors: Bhavadharini Ramu, Maria Masotti, Jessica Schultz, Valmiki Maharaj, Tamas Alexy, Michael Mulvihill, K. Joshua Wong, Tessa M.F. Watt, Andrew Shaffer, Ranjit John, Rebecca Cogswell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe U.S. heart transplant (HT) allocation policy was revised in 2018 with known sex differences in short-term outcomes; however, whether these translate to longer-term outcomes are unknown.ObjectivesThe authors sought to investigate long-term sex-specific waitlist and post-HT outcomes following this policy change.MethodsWaitlist cohort: Adults (≥18 years) listed for first-time, single-organ HT were identified using the United Network for Organ Sharing Registry between October 18, 2018, and October 17, 2023. Multivariable Fine-Gray competing risk analyses assessed: 1) rates of HT and 2) delisting due to death or clinical deterioration by sex. Transplant cohort: over the same time period, survival of first-time single-organ, HT recipients was analyzed by sex using multivariable Cox regression.ResultsAmong 15,422 waitlist candidates since 2018 (26.3% women), women had a higher rate of HT than men (adjusted subdistribution HR (aSHR): 1.24; 95% CI: 1.18-1.31; P < 0.001) and lower rate of delisting for death or clinical deterioration (aSHR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.70-0.94; P = 0.005). Among 12,892 HT recipients (27.4% women), women had a higher adjusted post-HT mortality than men at 1-year and 5-years of follow-up (aHR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02-1.28; P = 0.019 and aHR:1.14; 95% CI: 1.02-1.27; P = 0.021, respectively).ConclusionsFive years after implementation of the 2018 allocation policy, women have higher rates of HT and lower delisting due to death or clinical deterioration as compared to men with lower long-term post-HT survival.

Women account for about 25% of heart transplant (HT) recipients in the United States, a trend that has persisted over time.1,2 They face poorer outcomes while on the transplant waitlist as compared to men, particularly at the highest urgency status.2, 3, 4 Among those who survive to HT, there have been conflicting results on sex-specific outcomes on post-HT survival. In an analysis from the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) registry, after adjusting for donor and recipient characteristics, post-HT survival was similar in both sexes, despite women receiving hearts from higher risk donors;5 however, a more contemporary analysis of the ISHLT registry has demonstrated worse post-HT survival in women.6

Since the 2018 implementation of the new HT allocation policy, an initial analysis of the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) registry that included data until October 2020, showed that women had comparable short-term post-transplant survival as men at 180 days of follow-up and were more likely to receive a transplant despite lower utilization of temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS).7 However, longer-term sex-specific outcomes following this policy change are unknown. Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate the long-term sex-specific waitlist and post-HT outcomes, out to 5-years following the implementation of the 2018 U.S. allocation policy.

Methods

To perform these analyses, Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network registry data supplied by the UNOS were utilized. This registry includes deidentified data on all HT waitlisted candidates, transplant recipients, and donors in the United States. As data are collected as a part of routine clinical care and are deidentified, Institutional Review Board approval was not required. The data set used in this study was released on October 2, 2025. For the waitlist cohort, the UNOS registry was reviewed to identify adult patients (age ≥18 years) listed for the first-time, single-organ HT from October 18, 2018, to October 17, 2023, to allow a minimum of 1 year of waitlist follow-up. For the transplant cohort, the time period for cohort selection was identical and patients were included if they were waitlisted during this time and underwent first-time, single-organ HT. We utilized a complete case analysis. All covariates were missing <1% of the time, except for pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), which was missing in approximately 11% of cases (Supplemental Table 1). This variable was not independently associated with the outcomes of interest and did not materially influence multivariable model estimates. Supplemental Figure 1 provides a CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) diagram of the study population. Time was modeled starting at initial listing, until death, delisting, transplant, or date of last follow-up. Follow-up data were available through June 30, 2025. The primary waitlist outcomes included the rates of: 1) heart transplantation and 2) death or delisting for clinical deterioration by sex. The primary outcomes for the transplant cohort were 1-year and 5-years survival by sex.

Baseline characteristics

Waitlist cohort

Sex differences in baseline characteristics of patients listed for HT including demographics, comorbidities, diagnosis, and the use of temporary and durable MCS were obtained at the time of listing.

HT cohort

Recipient, donor, and transplant characteristics were obtained at the time of transplant. We calculated predicted heart mass (PHM) using established equations that integrate donor and recipient height, weight, age, and sex. The formula for calculating PHM, PHM ratio, and donor-recipient PHM difference (%) as established by Reed et al8 and Kransdorf et al9 was used.6,10 (Supplemental Table 2).

For both the waitlist and transplant cohorts, continuous characteristics by sex were assessed for normality of distribution using histograms and Shapiro-Wilk test for normality. Normally distributed characteristics by sex were compared with Student’s t-tests and non-normally distributed variables were compared with Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Continuous variables are presented as means and SDs and medians with IQR where appropriate; and categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages.

Waitlist analysis

First, to visually assess the unadjusted impact of sex on waitlist outcomes, the cumulative incidence of HT and delisting for death or clinical deterioration were plotted by sex. These plots were then repeated by acuity of initial listing status (high acuity: statuses 1, 2, and 3; middle acuity: status: 4; low acuity: status 6). To assess the adjusted association between sex and the rate of HT, a multivariable Fine-Gray competing risk analysis was performed using delisting for death or clinical deterioration as the competing outcome with a priori defined covariates, including age at listing, sex, creatinine, use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD), PVR, calculated panel of reactive antibodies (cPRAs), and listing status. The Fine-Gray competing risk analysis was then repeated using delisting for death or clinical deterioration as the primary outcome and HT as the competing outcome.

Sensitivity analysis

To validate our findings, we explored temporal recipient related changes and the effect of recipient sex on waitlist outcomes and included an interaction term for recipient sex and year of listing.

Post-heart transplant analysis

First, Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to visually display the unadjusted post-transplant survival at 1-year and 5-years of follow-up by sex and survival estimates were compared with log-rank tests. To determine the adjusted association between sex and post-HT survival, multivariable Cox proportional hazard modeling was performed adjusting for a priori defined covariates, including recipient age modeled per 10-year increase (age per decade), sex, race/ethnicity, history of diabetes, ischemic cardiomyopathy, prior cardiac surgery, serum creatinine, cPRA, listing status, use of durable LVAD, ECMO, ischemic time, and PHM. The time periods analyzed were 1-year and 5-year survival. Other post-transplant outcomes evaluated included postoperative complications, hospital length of stay, 30-day mortality, causes of graft failure, and treated acute rejection within the 1st year.

Sensitivity analysis

To validate our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses on adjusted post-HT survival models: 1) to explore temporal changes in recipient and donor features and the effect of recipient sex on post-transplant survival, we included an interaction term for recipient sex and year of transplant; 2) to address potential informative censoring due to UNOS data reporting delays, we analyzed a subgroup listed after the allocation policy change and underwent transplant by December 31, 2019, with follow-up through June 30, 2025, comparing their 3-year survival to that of the entire transplant cohort; 3) to explore whether the measured effects were due to mediation through cPRA levels given the high degree of association between cPRA levels and sex, we performed additional mediation analysis using an ordinal logistic mediator model and Cox survival model, with bootstrap resampling to estimate direct, indirect, and total HRs for women vs men; 4) to identify sex-specific predictors of post-transplant mortality, we performed covariate-by-sex interaction testing and sex-stratified Cox models at both time horizons; 5) to contextualize the magnitude of the sex difference, adjusted survival probabilities at 1 and 5 years were derived from the primary Cox model at cohort mean covariate values; and 6) to evaluate residual confounding from donor-recipient sex mismatch, we tested the recipient sex × donor sex interaction, donor sex effects within each recipient sex stratum, and a four-group donor-recipient sex pairing analysis at both time points. Proportional hazard assumptions were checked to ensure no violation. All P values were based on 2-sided tests with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

The waitlist cohort included 15,422 patients, including 4,061 women (26.3%) and 11, 361 men (73.7%). Sensitivity analysis

We explored the effect of temporal recipient–related changes on waitlist outcomes. The interaction between recipient sex and calendar year of listing did not demonstrate significant variation over time for the rate of HT (Pinteraction = 0.850) or death/delisting (Pinteraction = 0.343), suggesting that the effect of recipient sex on waitlist outcomes was stable over the study period (Supplemental Figure 3).

Transplant cohort

The transplant cohort included 12,892 HT recipients including 3,526 (27.4%) women and 9,336 (72.6%) men. A total of 16 patients did not have follow-up data available and were excluded from the post-transplant survival analysis.

Donor characteristics

The donors for women HT recipients were more likely to have a lower body mass index and be females (both P < 0.001), with female to male donor mismatch occurring in 14.7% of male transplant recipients. The use of Hep C + NAT + donors was not significantly different between the sexes (women vs men, 6.8% vs 6.9%, P = 0.514).

Transplant characteristics

Women HT recipients received donor hearts with significantly greater PHM difference (10.6 [IQR: −1.1 to 25.5] vs −1.4 [IQR: −9.3 to 8.8]; P < 0.001), and were more likely to receive oversized donors (42.9% vs 16.3%; P < 0.001) and less likely to receive undersized donors (4.4% vs 13.6%; P < 0.001) as compared to men.

Post-transplant survival

Unadjusted post-HT survival did not differ significantly between women and men at both 1 year (91.2% vs 91.6%; log-rank P = 0.359) (Figure 4) and 5 years (84.4% vs 85.1%; log-rank P = 0.521) (Figure 5) of follow-up. In a multivariable Cox regression analysis, after adjustment for relevant recipient and donor characteristics at the time of transplant, women HT recipients had a 14% higher hazard of post-HT mortality as compared to men at both time points. At 1-year post-HT, women demonstrated lower adjusted survival (adjusted HR [aHR]: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02-1.28; P = 0.019). This difference persisted at 5 years, with women HT recipients continuing to exhibit lower survival relative to men (aHR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02-1.27; P = 0.021). Sensitivity analysis

1) To explore the effect of temporal sex-related changes on post-transplant survival, the interaction between recipient sex and calendar year of transplant did not demonstrate significant variation over time (Pinteraction = 0.670 for 1-year survival and Pinteraction = 0.587 for 5-year survival), suggesting that the effect of sex on post-transplant survival was stable over the study period (Supplemental Figure 4). 2) To account for informative censoring bias due to UNOS data reporting delays, we analyzed 3-year post-HT survival for a subgroup of 6,822 recipients listed after the allocation policy change and transplanted between October 18, 2018 and December 31, 2021. Women (27.9%) had lower survival as compared to men in this subgroup (aHR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.01-1.34; P = 0.031), and with similar trends as the entire cohort (aHR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02-1.28; P = 0.021) (Supplemental Figure 5). 3) Mediation analysis: We demonstrated that cPRA levels and sex are highly associated, justifying the needs for additional analyses (Supplemental Figure 6). Women experienced a higher risk of post-transplant mortality compared with men, at 1-year of follow-up (total aHR: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.08-1.37; P < 0.001) and approximately 27% of the effect mediated by cPRA. This association persisted at 5 years, with women experiencing higher post-transplant mortality (total aHR: 1.22; 95% CI 1.07-1.37), ∼28% of which was mediated through elevated cPRA levels (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4). 4) Sex-stratified Cox models revealed that Black race/ethnicity, prior cardiac surgery, and cPRA >90% were significant predictors of post-transplant mortality in both women and men at 1- and 5-year follow-up, whereas age, ischemic cardiomyopathy, and ischemic time reached significance only in men. Formal interaction testing identified creatinine at transplant as the sole covariate with a statistically significant sex-specific effect at both time horizons (interaction P= 0.006), with a meaningfully attenuated association in women relative to men (HR: 1.08 vs 1.23 per 1 mg/dL increase at 1 year; HR: 1.07 vs 1.22 at 5 years). (Supplemental Table 5). 5) Adjusted survival probabilities from the primary Cox model were 83.7% for women vs 85.6% for men at 1 year (absolute difference −1.9 percentage points) and 75.4% vs 78.1% at 5 years (absolute difference −2.7 percentage points), contextualizing the modest absolute magnitude of the observed sex disparity (Supplemental Figure 7). 6) Donor-recipient sex mismatch: The interaction between recipient sex and donor sex on post-transplant mortality was nonsignificant at 1 year (P = 0.469) and 5 years (P = 0.290). Female-to-male donor mismatch was not associated with increased mortality in male recipients (HR: 0.92 and 0.90 at 1 and 5 years; both P > 0.18), nor was receipt of a male donor heart associated with differential outcomes in female recipients (HR: 0.93 and 0.92; both P > 0.40). In the four-group sex pairing analysis, only female recipients of female donor hearts had a significantly higher adjusted hazard vs sex-concordant male pairs (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.00-1.29; P = 0.049); men receiving female donor hearts did not (HR: 0.90; P = 0.210).

Discussion

In this analysis, we sought to evaluate the impact of candidate sex on waitlist outcomes and long-term post-HT survival following the implementation of the 2018 U.S. HT allocation policy change. Our major findings are as follows: 1) women as compared to men had a higher rate of HT and a lower rate of delisting for death or clinical deterioration under the revised allocation policy; 2) the advantage was largely driven by the effects in the middle and lowest acuity listing tiers; and 3) women had slightly worse post-transplant survival as compared to men at 1 year and 5 years of follow-up (Central Illustration).Central IllustrationSex Differences in Heart Transplantation Outcomes: A 5-Year Review of the 2018 U.S. Allocation Policy ChangeCreated in BioRender. To our knowledge, we present the first study of long-term sex-specific outcomes of contemporary waitlisted candidates and post-transplant survival since the implementation of the 2018 U.S. HT allocation policy changes. Before the implementation of the current allocation policy, previous analyses have demonstrated that women experienced increased waitlist mortality as compared to men,2,3 with conflicting reports on post-transplant survival.2,3,5,11

In 2018, a new 6-tier allocation policy was implemented to replace the previous 3-tiers system, with a goal to improve waitlist times, better stratify candidates by waitlist mortality to prioritize the sickest candidates, add objective criteria for common cardiac diagnoses, and broaden sharing of donor hearts.12 In an initial analysis of the new allocation system exploring sex-specific outcomes, DeFilippis et al.7 noted that women were more likely to be transplanted than men, with no difference in death or delisting in competing risk analysis and similar short-term post-transplant survival. However, this analysis included candidates listed only in the first 2-years following the allocation policy change and notably reported on post-transplant survival until 180 days of follow-up. In the current study, which included candidates waitlisted for 5-years post allocation policy change with long-term follow-up, we confirmed that women have increased rates of HT as compared to men under the 2018 allocation system and lower rates of delisting for death or clinical deterioration.

In the new allocation system, a greater proportion of patients are listed at the highest priority status, with a 2-3-fold increase in temporary MCS use, and reduced reliance on bridge-to-transplant durable LVADs.12, 13, 14, 15 DeFilippis et al.7 noted that women were less likely than men to be listed as status 1 or 2 at the time of transplant (48% vs 60%), aligning with our findings (51.1% vs 65.1%). They also reported reduced use of IABP and durable LVADs in women, with no difference in ECMO use. In our study, despite receiving less IABP, ECMO, or durable LVADs at transplant, women experienced shorter waitlist times and slightly higher transplantation rate. This advantage may relate to using PHM as the optimal donor-recipient size metric, as undersizing (PHM ratio <0.86) is linked to increased post-transplant mortality.8,16,17 As our data suggest, donor hearts for women were less often undersized and more often oversized as compared to men, enabling women to receive donor hearts that were considered undersized for men and thus contributing to a larger donor pool for women HT recipients. In addition, reduced reliance on durable LVADs has been linked to higher transplantation rates,18 suggesting that women, who receive less durable LVADs, may benefit more than men from a direct transplant strategy.

In a more recent UNOS analysis conducted since the implementation of the 2018 allocation policy, women continued to suffer higher waitlist mortality and lower rates of transplantation when listed as status 1.4 These findings were present regardless of whether patients were on ECMO and were not fully mediated by increased sensitization among women. In our study, when stratifying patients by acuity of listing status in an adjusted analysis, the sex-specific advantage in transplantation rates for women persisted across listing acuity, with the smallest gains noted at the highest acuity listing status. Notably, in the previous analysis, time started when patients became status 1 vs our analysis which only analyzed the status at initial listing.

Finally, and with regard to post-transplant survival, an initial study of the new allocation system, demonstrated similar sex-specific survival at 180 days of follow-up.7 Our findings demonstrate a slightly lower longer-term post-HT survival in women as compared to men, with a 14% higher hazard of post-HT mortality at both 1-year and 5-year follow-up, which is consistent with the results from the more contemporary analysis of the large ISHLT thoracic transplant registry, where female sex was associated with a 19% increased hazard of 1-year post-HT mortality.6 In our study, elevated cPRA levels appeared to be a partial mediator of increased post-HT mortality, but did not fully explain the increased hazard observed in women. Creatinine at transplant was the only covariate with a significant sex-specific effect, with an attenuated prognostic impact of renal dysfunction in women compared with men across both time horizons, suggesting that the threshold at which pretransplant renal impairment confers mortality risk may differ by sex and warranting further mechanistic investigation. Comprehensive donor-recipient sex mismatch analyses were uniformly nonsignificant, with only women recipients of female donor hearts demonstrating modestly higher adjusted mortality vs sex-concordant male pairs (HR: 1.14, P = 0.049), consistent with previous studies.9 In absolute terms, the adjusted survival difference was 1.9 percentage points at 1-year and 2.7 percentage points at 5-years, underscoring that although statistically robust, the sex disparity in post-HT survival remains modest in magnitude within this contemporary cohort.

Study Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. We present a retrospective analysis of registry data which are susceptible to the accuracy of the data submitted. Data regarding complications from temporary MCS are not captured in UNOS registry, which limits our ability to understand the differential impact of these on women and men, as they could have implications for waitlist outcomes. As cPRA data in the UNOS database are self-reported and inconsistently recorded, the true impact of sensitization in women cannot be accurately determined. However, since the 2018 allocation policy change, there is <5% missingness in the cPRA variable allowing us to perform the mediation analyses. The UNOS data only include patients who are listed for transplant, and thus this study is not equipped to explore barriers that women face in other aspects of heart failure care before listing for transplant. However, the strengths of our analysis include the ability to systematically study sex-differences across all phases of HT for up to 5 years after the allocation policy change and demonstrate the sex differences in waitlist outcomes and long-term post-transplant survival.

Conclusions

In this 5-year analysis of sex differences in HT waitlist outcomes and post-transplant mortality following the 2018 U.S. heart allocation policy change, women had higher rates of HT, lower rates of delisting for death, or clinical deterioration and lower adjusted post-transplant survival as men. The impact of policy change on specific groups, such as sex, is crucial as we prepare for continuous distribution.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: Sex differences in HT waitlist and long-term post-transplant outcomes persist in the modern allocation era, with women experiencing higher transplant rates but lower adjusted post-transplant survival compared with men. Recognition of these differences may inform risk assessment, counseling and tailored post-transplant management strategies for contemporary HT recipients.TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The under-representation of women in heart failure research, limits insights into sex-specific differences in waitlist and transplant outcomes.
