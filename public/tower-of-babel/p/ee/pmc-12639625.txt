Predicting rehabilitation discharge after lumbar decompression: Impact of age and preoperative assistive device use
Authors: Michael Avery, Fernando Terry, Andrew Y Powers, Forough Yazdanian, Chloe Lee, Punit Pandya, Martina Stippler, Efstathios Papavassiliou, Ziev B Moses
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElective lumbar decompression, commonly performed via laminectomy or microdiscectomy, is a well-tolerated surgical procedure. Patients requiring discharge to inpatient rehabilitation facilities are infrequent but generate increased healthcare costs and can be burdensome to patients and families. This study aims to develop an accurate predictive model for rehabilitation discharge following elective lumbar decompression, as well as to identify associated factors.MethodsThis was a retrospective, single-center, cohort study. Current Procedural Terminology codes for laminectomy and microdiscectomy were used to identify patients from October 2012 to November 2024. Charts were reviewed to obtain additional demographic and clinical characteristics on which an initial univariate analysis was performed. Primary outcomes for this study included measurements of accuracy for predicting rehab discharge. Secondary outcomes included associations of variables with rehab discharge. Those with confounding diagnosis or admitted from a rehabilitation facility were excluded. Machine learning models were trained and evaluated on the data using cross-validation and classification metrics were utilized for model comparison.ResultsA total of 486 patients were considered for the analysis. After performing a Benjamini-Hochberg correction, the univariate analysis identified multiple statistically significant variables associated with rehabilitation discharge some of which include age, preoperative leg pain, lower extremity weakness, need for a walking assistive device, and frailty. Of the 5 machine learning models tested, the logistic regression model demonstrated superior performance, with the highest area under curve (0.81), Youden’s J statistic (0.55) and balanced accuracy (0.78). The logistic regression model demonstrated that age and the use of an ambulatory assistive device are consistently predictive of rehabilitation discharge (p<.001).ConclusionsWhen considered alongside frailty, lack of support, and other variables, age and the necessity for a walking assistive device can serve as a reliable predictors of rehabilitation discharge, thereby improving patient workflow, minimizing uncertainty and reducing healthcare costs.Keywords: Lumbar decompression, Laminectomy, Machine learning, Discharge disposition

Introduction

Elective lumbar decompression, commonly performed via laminectomy with or without microdiscectomy, is a well-tolerated surgical procedure [1]. Increasing pressure on healthcare systems has resulted in efforts to better streamline surgical services to improve patient flow and economic efficiency. Patients requiring discharge to inpatient rehabilitation facilities are infrequent but generate increased healthcare costs and can be burdensome to patients and families. Moreover, patients and families who do not expect to go to a rehab facility may be financially or logistically unprepared, leading to short and long-term distress. Overall, this leads to significant costs to the patient, surgeon, and hospital [2]. Thus, accurate prediction of rehab discharge can reduce waste at multiple levels by helping patients, providers, and medical systems plan more effectively for the postoperative period.

To the best of our knowledge, several machine learning models have been developed to forecast the need of lumbar decompression surgery in spinal canal stenosis patients [3,4], and postoperative prolonged inpatient stay [5] or opioid dependence after such procedure [[6], [7], [8]]. Nonetheless, no predictive models have been identified to predict rehabilitation discharge following elective lumbar decompression.

The current study seeks to develop an accurate and practical predictive model for rehab discharge following elective lumbar decompression as well as delineate the most important factors contributing to rehab discharge. Such a model may improve the patient’s experience and assist in predicting, and ultimately reducing, unnecessary healthcare expenditure. Characterization of both medical and non-medical variable associations with rehab discharge furthers our understanding of the processes underlying this outcome and provides guidance for future research and intervention.

Methods

Data generated or analyzed during the study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study design

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent elective lumbar decompression, accounting for either laminectomy or microdiscectomy, at a large academic center in New England. The study has been approved by our hospital’s institutional review board and has been reported according to the Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [9].

Data collection

Patients who underwent lumbar decompression between October 2012 and November 2024 were identified from our department’s billing database using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code for lumbar laminectomy (CPT: 63047) and microdiscectomy (CPT: 63030). Patients who had surgery prior to 2012 were excluded, as the coding for both procedures changed in 2011 [10]. Patients were excluded based on the following conditions: (1) if their procedure was marked as “urgent” or “emergent” in the billing database, (2) they had missing discharge documentation, (3) they were admitted from a rehab facility, (4) they had confounding diagnoses, which could have been primary cause for the admission during which surgery was performed or which made it unclear if their symptoms or neurologic examination findings were attributable to their lumbar disease.

Age, gender, presence of comorbidities (COPD, diabetes mellitus type II, hypertension, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, prior percutaneous coronary intervention, peripheral vessel disease, cerebrovascular accident, and impaired functional or sensitive status), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification score [11], (type of health insurance, primary language, and race listed in the electronic medical record at the time of surgery were found in the department’s billing database. Outpatient provider notes, preoperative and intraoperative anesthesia assessment records, and scanned patient questionnaires were carefully reviewed for other data, including back pain, leg pain, weakness on neurologic examination, presence of home support, preoperative use of an ambulatory assistive device (either partially or fully dependent on straight cane, wheelchair, rolling walker), prior lumbar surgery, performance of a concurrent surgical procedure, coming from a rehab facility, and number of decompressed levels.

Statistical analysis

The dependent variable in the analysis was discharging disposition, either to home or to a rehab facility. The type of preoperative ambulation assistive device, type of insurance, primary language (English, Spanish, Portuguese, Cantonese, Vietnamese), and race (White, Hispanic, African American, other race) were dichotomized due to having few samples across many categories. Wilcoxon signed rank and Pearson’s chi-square tests were used for univariate analysis of continuous and categorical independent variables, respectively. For multivariate analysis, 5 classification models were evaluated using a stratified, repeated 5-fold cross-validation. The data were first randomly split into 5 equal partitions, each with equal proportions of patients who were discharged to rehab. Four of these partitions were used to train the models while the fifth was used to test performance. This process was repeated 5 times, with each partition serving once as the test partition. To minimize variance, the entire process was repeated ten times for a total of fifty iterations. All p-values for univariate analyses were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [12] with a false discovery rate of 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using MatLab version R2024b.

Machine learning models were trained in the following manner. Weighted binary logistic regression was performed on all 14 predictors. Weights were chosen based on the fraction of rehab to non-rehab discharges in order to account for class imbalance. A k-nearest neighbors’ model was generated using standard techniques previously described by Cunningham et al. [13]. Hyperparameter optimization was performed by adjusting the number of neighbors and distance metrics [14,15]. Predicted rehab discharge probabilities in the validation data were calculated from this model as the proportion of the k-nearest neighbors in the training data that were discharged to rehab. Support vector machines (SVMs) were optimized in terms of kernel function and scale and box constraint level. Neural networks were optimized by adjusting the number of hidden layers, activation functions, and regularization parameters. Decision trees were optimized in terms of number of splits and split criterion.

For each iteration, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and Youden’s J statistics [16], a composite measure of sensitivity and specificity, were determined. Balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were determined using the optimal decision threshold at which the maximum J statistic occurred. ROC curves and all metrics were averaged across iterations to compare model performances.

In addition to the supervised learning techniques above, we used factor analysis, an unsupervised learning technique, to help support and interpret our findings. We assessed correlations within the data and the Kaiser-Maier-Olkin Measure to assess sampling adequacy and determine factorability of the data set. We determined the number of factors to be used based on the Kaiser criterion for eigenvalues > 1. The most significant factors were based on the factor loadings > 0.7.

Results

Of the 759 potential study subjects initially identified through retrospective screening of medical records, 273 were excluded and 486 (64.03%) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The mean age and body mass index were 63.6 and 30.6, respectively. Most patients were male (262/486, 53.91%), white (275/486, 56.58%), and native English speakers (338/486, 69.55%). A small minority of patients used public health insurance (21/486, 4.32%). During the initial assessment, patients presented with leg pain (479/486, 98.56%), lower extremity weakness (111/486, 22.84%), and required a walking assistive device (166/486, 34.16%). 48.4% and 15.02% of the population presented high ASA and high frailty, respectively.

Following correction for multiple hypothesis testing independent variable analysis using multivariate logistic regression identified significant associations between discharge to rehab and age (p=.004) and the need for a walking assistive device (p=.002).

In addition to the supervised learning approaches above we used factor analysis, which is an unsupervised learning technique, to support and help further understand our finding that age and use of an assistive device are important predictive variables for rehab discharge. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of the data was 0.66, suggesting the data is factorizable. We also set the number of factors to be 2 based on the Kaiser Criterion. Consistent with our finding in the supervised models, the variables with the highest correlation (>0.7) to the 2 factors were use of an assisted device and age based on factor loadings. In Fig. 3 we show a scatter plot of the 2 latent factors. As shown, patients with rehab discharge tend to be >70 years old. Use of an assistive device, however, is able to capture the patients that are younger that also need to go to a rehab facility.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to develop a machine learning model capable of predicting discharge to a rehabilitation facility following elective lumbar decompression. After comparing multiple machine-learning methods, we found that the logistic regression model performed the best and was able to predict rehab discharge with 80% accuracy, potentially on its causality – driven confounder controlling, in comparison to machine learning models. The most important predictor variables (p<.05) were age and use of a walking assistive device in predicting rehab discharge.

Model performance

Previous studies have developed machine learning models for the elective lumbar decompression population. Andre et al.; 2022 [17] trained and validated an artificial neural network model to predict postoperative clinical outcomes after lumbar decompression surgery. This model was particularly suitable for screening patients who react negatively to lumbar surgery; however, the study sample was too small and required a larger dataset to obtain a robust statistical output demonstrating its predictive power. Saravi et al. [18]; also used multiple machine learning and deep learning algorithms to predict length of stay following lumbar decompression. They, however, found operation time was most predictive of length of stay which limits the usability of the model for predicting outcomes prior to surgery. Ogink et al. [19]; developed 4 machine learning models, from which the neural network was the best model to predict non-home discharge after elective surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis using age, sex, BMI, diabetes, functional status, ASA class, level, fusion, preoperative hematocrit, and preoperative serum creatinine. Other spine centers have aimed to construct risk scores for predicting discharge placement, for instance, McGirt et al. [20]; created the Carolina-Semmes grading score for all degenerative lumbar spine surgery based on logistic regression models, ultimately achieving an AUC of 0.731 by including variables such as age, ASA class, fusion, Oswestry disability index score, ambulation, and non-private insurance. Kanaan et al. [21]; built a multivariate logistic regression model, identifying preoperative need of home assistance, marital status, non-independent ambulation as prior level of function and length of stay as significant predictors for predicting discharge towards an inpatient rehabilitation facility, achieving an AUC of 0.80.

Accurate identification of patients at high-risk for rehab discharge facilitates efficient resource allocation, as well as reducing patient and provider stress. Case managers and surgical coordinators can work with these patients throughout the preoperative assessment to reduce the time of inpatient hospital stay while waiting for an available bed at rehab facilities. By doing so, high risk patients can be prioritized in the surgical schedule, to increase the likelihood of transfer to rehab [22]. By forecasting a prolonged inpatient stay before rehab discharge, nursing and ancillary staff ratios can be optimized, while hiring temporary staff in order account for the national nursing shortage [23]. Insurance companies are able to estimate healthcare utilization to determine bundled, risk-based reimbursement relying on the accurate prediction of discharge to rehab.

Variable associations

Preoperative factors previously associated with rehab discharge following elective lumbar decompression include baseline and postoperative ODI [24], operation time, ASA class, ambulation, and private insurance. This study demonstrated that age and use of an assistive device were associated with rehab discharge. Interestingly, the modified frailty index, which is a measure of chronic disease burden, was not significantly associated with rehab discharge as has been shown in other studies. This suggests that there is a component associated with age but not chronic disease that may determine rehab discharge. One explanation for this is weakness secondary to sarcopenia as has been alluded to in other studies [25,26]. Supporting this, we found the use of a walking assistive device, which may be due to multiple factors including pain, imbalance and weakness, is associated with rehab discharge. Based on factor analysis, use of an assistive walking device was able to account for younger patients who were discharged to rehab, being consistent with prior literature that outlined delayed discharge depending on limited mobility as a negative factor for the rehabilitation course (19). Given that, future studies should explore preoperative interventions targeting sarcopenia and mobility deficits to optimize discharge planning and improve postoperative patient recovery.

Limitations and strengths

This study is limited by its non-randomized design, lack of control arm and retrospective direction. The observational nature of our approach does not account for numerous potential explanatory confounding factors and prohibits any causal inference. Large clinical databases are always affected by miscoding and other inaccuracies [27], such as patients being deliberately excluded due to missing discharge documentation, scenario in which were likely discharged home, potentially introducing selection bias. Additionally, the dichotomization of several variables, including race and primary language, due to the large number of categories for these variables and the relative underrepresentation of nonwhite, non-English-speaking patients in our patient population is a source of bias that may affect the generalizability of our findings to populations not adequately represented in the study. Finally, statistical analyses were performed in the overall population (n=486) and no subgroup analyses (laminectomy and microdiscectomy) were performed due to the reduced statistical power (number of solely microdiscectomy patients) during the machine learning modeling. In the future, it would be interesting to expand our microdiscectomy patient subgroup population to determine if these patients are more or less likely to go to rehab than laminectomy patients and if this is also age and assistive device dependent.

Despite this, the current study has several strengths including those related to exclusion criteria, predictors considered, and model choices. Specifically, we were strict with our inclusion criteria and excluded patients with concurrent surgery or coming from a rehab facility. We also included both medical (eg frailty index) and non-medical (eg home support) predictors that might dictate rehab discharge. The exclusion of data that would not be available prior to surgery, such as operative time, was critical as it allows for the development of a model that can reasonably be used prior to surgery. In terms of model development, the use of cross-validation to establish accurate estimates of model performance yields robust models with improved ability to generalize. Comprehensive data collection, including the review of hand-written patient questionnaires, supplied highly accurate and granular data on which to train and evaluate our models. Although correction for multiple hypothesis testing decreases statistical power, the use of such conservative approaches minimizes the likelihood of spurious positive findings [12].

Conclusions

Lumbar decompression surgery is one of the most common spine surgeries performed and can provide patients with significant improvements in quality of life compared with non-surgical options. The costs to both the hospital system and the patient are highly dependent upon whether or not the patient needs rehabilitation services after their procedure. Here we show that we can predict whether or not a patient will need rehab with high accuracy. In particular, we found that age and use of a walking assistive device were the most significant factors predicting rehab discharge. This suggests that pre-surgery rehabilitation may be a possible method for improving outcomes and decreasing the need for elderly individuals that use an assistive device. There are, however, practical limitations to current algorithmic predictions including the inability to obtain insurance authorization for pre-surgical rehab as well as inability to obtain pre-authorization for inpatient rehab given the CPT codes defined by the paper. Despite this, developing models that deliver accurate, patient-specific predictions regarding outcomes are essential for providing optimal and cost-effective treatment plans for individuals while minimizing uncertainty and distress and may help guide insurance authorization specifications in the future.
