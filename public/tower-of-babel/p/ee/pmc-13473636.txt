Critical slowing down of semiarid vegetation resilience is amplified by intensifying heatwaves
Authors: Li Fu, Jianping Huang, Changyu Li, Wenmin Wei, Ming Peng, Guolong Zhang, Yu Ren, Jianming Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Critical slowing down is commonly used as an early-warning signature of declining ecosystem resilience. While widespread greening has been documented, vegetation resilience in semiarid regions has received limited attention, and how climate extremes influence this association remains poorly understood. Here, we quantify vegetation resilience by estimating lag-1 temporal autocorrelation derived from complementary satellite vegetation metrics across global semiarid regions. We find that 56.6% of semiarid vegetated area shows increasing lag-1 temporal autocorrelation, and the spatial mean lag-1 temporal autocorrelation exhibits a sustained upward trend during 2001–2024, indicating declining resilience. Using a quadrant framework, 47.5% of vegetated area is classified as greening concurrent with declining resilience, exceeding the 33.8% showing greening with increasing resilience. Moreover, heatwaves intensified, and precipitation declined, with trend magnitudes and land-cover composition varying among greening–resilience quadrants. Spatial error models indicate that increases in maximum heatwave intensity are more strongly associated with declining resilience under drying trends, whereas this association weakens under wetting. Within-quadrant analyses further characterize conditional associations, consistent with greening–resilience decoupling under hot–dry intensification. Our findings suggest that greening can mask declining resilience, underscoring the need to integrate resilience diagnostics into routine greenness monitoring as climate extremes intensify.

Introduction

Satellite observations have documented widespread increases in remotely sensed vegetation indices, commonly referred to as greening1, that have continued through 20242. However, greenness trends do not unambiguously indicate ecosystem improvement. Extreme climatic events have markedly reduced vegetation greenness in many regions2,3, and evidence indicates that global greening has recently begun to level off4 and may reverse in certain areas5,6. These complexities suggest that assessments of vegetation dynamics focused solely on monotonic changes in canopy greenness and structure may overlook how vegetation systems withstand disturbance and recover thereafter. Apparent greening trends may therefore mask underlying vulnerability, highlighting the need for resilience-focused diagnostics that characterize recovery dynamics and ecosystem stability.

Critical slowing down (CSD) is widely regarded as an early-warning signature of declining ecosystem resilience, whereby recovery from small perturbations progressively slows as systems approach a critical transition7 (Supplementary Fig. 1). This slowdown makes anomalies persist longer, increasing similarity between consecutive observations (i.e., stronger temporal persistence) and thereby elevating lag-1 temporal autocorrelation (TAC)8. Accordingly, we use lag-1 TAC as a theoretically grounded indicator of CSD and a proxy for inferring changes in vegetation resilience9,10. Consistent with this framework, mounting evidence indicates that resilience is declining across multiple regions and biomes10–13, with particularly pronounced losses reported in climate-sensitive ecosystems such as the Amazon rainforest9,14,15. Such resilience decline manifests as slower recovery from climatic and environmental stresses, including droughts, wildfires, and temperature extremes16,17.

Although previous studies have investigated vegetation resilience in relation to mean climate conditions and variability in temperature and precipitation11,13,18, the influence of climate extremes, particularly heatwaves, remains insufficiently explored. Heatwaves impose acute thermal stress, often accompanied by elevated vapor pressure deficit (VPD), which can suppress photosynthesis and vegetation growth19, thereby slowing recovery dynamics and undermining ecosystem stability. With ongoing warming, heatwaves are becoming more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting, and these trends are projected to continue under future climate scenarios20. A series of extreme heatwave events across Europe21, Australia22, North America23, and China24 underscores the growing potential for widespread ecosystem disruption. Gaining mechanistic insight into how heatwave characteristics shape vegetation resilience is therefore essential for diagnosing ecosystem vulnerability, without conflating greening with improved ecosystem stability.

Semiarid regions (0.20 ≤ AI < 0.50) constitute a major component of global drylands and are especially vulnerable to climate change (Fig. 1h). These regions face escalating pressures from water scarcity, ecological fragility, and desertification risk25, and they have accounted for more than half of recent global dryland expansion26. Moreover, vegetation resilience is strongly constrained by water availability and interannual precipitation variability27,28. Heatwaves often coincide with drought and can be particularly severe in drylands compared with humid regions29, while heatwave risk and frequency are elevated in water-limited environments30. Importantly, greening in these regions can reflect structural or compositional shifts, such as shrub or woody encroachment, that elevate satellite-derived vegetation indices but may signal land degradation rather than improvement31. In this study, we propose a resilience-based perspective to examine semiarid vegetation responses to intensifying heatwaves and their relationship with greening trends, complementing conventional greening assessments. We then test whether heatwave characteristics are associated with indicators of critical slowing down in ways that depend on background hydroclimatic conditions. To achieve this, we quantify vegetation resilience using lag-1 TAC derived from multiple vegetation indicators, including solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF), leaf area index (LAI), and normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). We then construct a quadrant framework to classify greening–resilience regimes. Building on this, we estimate the effects of heatwave characteristics on vegetation resilience employing a spatial error model that accounts for residual spatial autocorrelation, while incorporating precipitation trends to represent background hydroclimatic conditions (see Methods). Doing so is particularly valuable for disentangling the relationship between greening and resilience, providing a nuanced understanding of semiarid ecosystem resilience under intensifying heatwaves.

Results

Declining vegetation resilience despite greening

Using lag-1 temporal autocorrelation (TAC) of solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) as an early-warning indicator, we detected widespread critical slowing down across global semiarid regions during 2001–2024. Spatially, 56.6% of semiarid vegetated areas showed increasing SIF lag-1 TAC (trend = 0.0016 yr–1), consistent with declining resilience (Fig. 1a, b). This signal is robust to the choice of sliding-window length: estimates from 3-year and 7-year windows yielded spatial patterns consistent with the 5-year baseline (Supplementary Figs. 3–4). Moreover, NDVI- and LAI-based analyses showed consistently positive trends in lag-1 TAC (Supplementary Figs. 5–6), in line with the SIF-based results. NDVI and LAI primarily characterize canopy greenness and structure, whereas SIF is more directly linked to photosynthetic physiology33 and is sensitive to plant physiological stress under water limitation and heat extremes34,35. Given that SIF, NDVI, and LAI capture complementary aspects of vegetation functioning, their agreement reinforces the inference of resilience decline. Furthermore, mean SIF lag-1 TAC increases over time (Fig. 1c, f), while mean SIF showed a sustained upward trend (Fig. 1f), highlighting a mismatch between greening and declining resilience. Over the same period, 78.4% of vegetated areas exhibited positive SIF trends (Supplementary Fig. 7a), with consistent greening patterns also observed in NDVI and LAI (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). The greening–resilience mismatch is also evident across land-cover types: both SIF and lag-1 TAC trends were generally positive, yet lag-1 TAC increased most strongly in deciduous forests, followed by savannas and closed shrublands, whereas grasslands exhibited comparatively weaker trends (Fig. 1e). Regionally, lag-1 TAC increases were more pronounced in SAM and SAS and weaker in AUS and SAF (Fig. 1d, h; Supplementary Fig. 8), whereas SIF trends remained positive in all nine subregions (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Against the backdrop of widespread greening, we quantified how greening and browning relate to concurrent resilience changes by applying a quadrant-based framework that jointly classifies trends in SIF and SIF lag-1 TAC (Fig. 2). This bivariate scheme partitions the semiarid domain into four quadrants defined by combinations of greening versus browning and resilience decline versus increase. Nearly half of vegetated areas (47.5%) were classified as greening with declining resilience (Quadrant 1), exceeding the fraction exhibiting greening with resilience increases (33.8%; Quadrant 4) (Fig. 2b). Quadrant 1 and Quadrant 4 were often spatially adjacent, reflecting the close juxtaposition of greening areas with divergent resilience trajectories (Fig. 2a). Additionally, we evaluated whether resilience decline is more likely under greening than under browning using a conditional-probability difference (Δ), where negative Δ indicates that resilience decline is more likely under greening than under browning. Resilience decline was more likely under greening in AUS, NAF, EU, and NAM, whereas it was more likely under browning in SAM (Fig. 2c). Similar patterns were evident across land-cover types, with open shrublands and grasslands exhibiting a stronger tendency toward resilience decline under greening (Supplementary Fig. 10). Consistently, quadrant composition varies markedly among subregions (Supplementary Fig. 11), with Quadrant 1 exceeding Quadrant 4 in SAS, NAF, and NAM, whereas Quadrant 2 was concentrated in SAM. Taken together, these results indicate that greening was accompanied by declining resilience in global semiarid regions.

Heatwave and precipitation contrasts across greening–resilience regimes

Heatwave counts increased from 2001 to 2024 across global semiarid regions, indicating progressively more frequent heat extremes (Fig. 3a1). Heatwave duration, cumulative intensity, and maximum intensity exhibited nonlinear upward trends (Fig. 3a2–a4), reflecting an increasing heat burden, with a pronounced peak in 2011 that coincided with severe regional heat extremes reported for that period36,37. Meanwhile, annual precipitation increased initially and then declined steadily, resulting in an overall drying tendency (Fig. 3a5). This co-occurrence aligns with evidence for increasing compound hot–dry extremes38,39. Similar nonlinear temporal evolutions were observed across the four greening–resilience quadrants (Supplementary Fig. 12). Quadrant-level comparisons further showed that heatwave metrics increased across all quadrants, but with differing trend magnitudes (Fig. 3b1–b4). Quadrant 2 exhibited the largest increases across heatwave metrics and the most pronounced precipitation decline (Fig. 3b5). Among the two greening quadrants, Quadrant 1 was characterized by stronger heatwave intensification and drying than Quadrant 4 (Fig. 3b1–b5). These climate contrasts persisted when stratifying by resilience-change classes alone (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Land-cover composition varied across greening–resilience quadrants, although all remained grassland-dominated (Fig. 3c). Given that land cover was held constant throughout 2001–2024 (see Methods), these differences represent cross-quadrant contrasts in land-cover composition. Quadrant 4 contained a higher grassland fraction than Quadrant 1 (91.4% vs. 81.1%), while Quadrant 1 had relatively more open shrublands, savannas, and woody savannas. Quadrants 2 and 3 had lower grassland fractions and were comparatively enriched in shrublands and savannas. Moreover, stratifying climate trends by land-cover type reveals clear heterogeneity, with drying strongest in closed shrublands, followed by deciduous forests and savannas (Supplementary Fig. 14), and heatwave intensification most pronounced in shrub-dominated types (Supplementary Fig. 15). A consistent pattern emerges across nine semiarid subregions (Supplementary Table 2), where shrubland- and savanna-enriched subregions (SAM, AUS, and SAF) experienced stronger heatwave intensification and drying than grassland-dominated regions (SAS, CAS, EAS) (Supplementary Fig. 16). Heatwave extremes amplify resilience decline under drying

To identify climatic drivers of resilience change, we quantified how precipitation trends and heatwave intensification related to trends in SIF lag-1 TAC using spatial error models that account for residual spatial autocorrelation (see Methods). Heatwave intensification was widespread across the semiarid domain, with maximum intensity increasing over 66.3% of the domain (Fig. 4a1). Heatwave frequency and duration also increased, covering 86.5% and 61.2% of the region, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 17a1, b1). Meanwhile, precipitation declined across 55.5% of the domain (Fig. 4b1). Spatial error models indicated lag-1 TAC trends were positively associated with maximum intensity trends (declining resilience), whereas they were negatively associated with precipitation trends (Fig. 4a2, b2). Importantly, maximum intensity interacted with precipitation: the marginal effect of maximum intensity on lag-1 TAC strengthened under drying ( − 1 SD precipitation trend) but weakened under wetting ( + 1 SD) (Fig. 4c). In contrast, the marginal effects of heatwave frequency and duration were weaker and less certain (Supplementary Fig. 17a2, b2). Adding an interaction between heatwave frequency and maximum intensity did not improve model fit based on AIC (Supplementary Fig. 18; Supplementary Table 1), supporting the robustness of the retained precipitation–maximum intensity interaction. Subsequently, we fitted partial least squares structural equation models (PLS-SEM) within each greening–resilience quadrant to examine multivariate associations among precipitation, heatwave maximum intensity, SIF, and lag-1 TAC trends (Fig. 5). Because quadrants are defined by the signs of SIF and lag-1 TAC trends, the path coefficients represent within-quadrant conditional co-variation rather than causal effects or comparisons of coefficient signs across quadrants. Across quadrants, precipitation trends were positively associated with SIF trends, whereas the associations between precipitation and lag-1 TAC varied by quadrant. The greening quadrants (Q1 and Q4) also differed in their within-quadrant association structures. In Q4, precipitation was negatively associated with lag-1 TAC, whereas maximum intensity was positively associated with lag-1 TAC. In Q1, already classified as greening with declining resilience, maximum intensity covaried positively with SIF but showed a small negative association with lag-1 TAC. Because Q1 is a sign-selected subset with restricted ranges, this coefficient should be interpreted as within-quadrant conditional co-variation, rather than as a comparison with the cross-quadrant spatial error model results. This quadrant is relatively enriched in shrublands and savannas (Fig. 3c), while precipitation showed only weak and non-significant links with lag-1 TAC. In the browning quadrants, maximum intensity was positively associated with lag-1 TAC, with the strongest association in Q3 (Fig. 5). Moreover, within each quadrant, partial correlations between SIF and lag-1 TAC remained weak after controlling for maximum intensity and precipitation, suggesting that their co-variation was largely attributable to climatic covariates rather than a strong direct linkage. Discussion

Despite widespread satellite-observed greening, our analysis reveals that resilience has declined across global semiarid regions, suggesting that greenness gains do not necessarily translate into improved ecosystem stability. We infer resilience changes from lag-1 temporal autocorrelation (TAC), a widely used indicator of critical slowing down (CSD). Rising TAC reflects stronger temporal persistence and slower recovery from perturbations7. This greening–resilience decoupling is widespread across semiarid regions and consistent among complementary satellite indicators, strengthening confidence in our inference.

The quadrant framework further clarifies that similar greening trajectories can correspond to distinct resilience changes, and that these differences align with structured gradients in hydroclimate and land-cover composition. In particular, greening accompanied by declining resilience (Quadrant 1) is more common under warmer and drier conditions and in regions relatively enriched in shrub and savanna components, whereas greening with increased resilience (Quadrant 4) is more characteristic of grassland-dominated systems. This contrast suggests that greening in Quadrant 1 may partly reflect structural or compositional shifts (e.g., shrub or woody encroachment) that elevate satellite greenness metrics without necessarily enhancing recovery dynamics31,40. This is compatible with evidence that dryland vegetation metrics differ in their sensitivities to herbaceous versus woody components, such that apparent greening can arise from compositional reorganization rather than uniform functional improvement41. Moreover, although woody plants are often drought-tolerant, woody expansion can alter ecosystem water balance and increase transpirational demand, which may constrain post-disturbance recovery under intensifying hot–dry stress42. Overall, these considerations reinforce that greening trends should be interpreted alongside community composition, especially in water-limited ecosystems43.

Importantly, our findings do not contradict established drivers of widespread greenness increases; rather, they refine what greening implies for ecosystem stability. Previous studies have attributed greening to CO₂ fertilization44 and large-scale land management interventions45. Consistent with our findings, decoupling between greening and resilience has been reported in the Amazon rainforest14, and on the Chinese Loess Plateau46, where resilience does not increase consistently with greening. Beyond drylands, broader analyses also point to emerging declines in forest resilience under climate change11. Our results extend these insights by showing that similar greening trends can mask markedly different resilience trajectories, suggesting that greenness-based assessments alone may overestimate ecosystem improvement. Collectively, these lines of evidence suggest that apparent greenness gains in water-limited ecosystems may mask growing vulnerability, motivating the integration of resilience indicators into early-warning frameworks as climate extremes intensify47.

A central insight from our analysis is that hydroclimatic background modulates how heatwaves relate to resilience change: the negative association between heatwave maximum intensity and resilience strengthens under drying but weakens under wetting. This pattern suggests that drying acts not only as an independent stressor but also as a key modulator of how heatwaves affect ecosystem resilience. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that water availability and variability are key constraints on vegetation resilience27, and that wetter conditions can buffer drought legacies and facilitate recovery48. Although heatwave frequency and duration have increased, their relationships with resilience are weaker after accounting for spatial structure and drying background, whereas maximum intensity remains the most consistent correlate. This aligns with the view that ecological consequences of climate extremes often hinge on event characteristics, particularly magnitude or peak severity49. In this context, higher maximum intensity can impose acute physiological constraints, reducing productivity and generating legacy effects50. By contrast, the impacts of less extreme heat stress may be partly buffered by physiological acclimation51, and repeated exposure can increase leaf thermal tolerance and dampen impacts during subsequent events52.

This state dependence is consistent with a plausible mechanism whereby precipitation-driven drying may reduce evaporative cooling, increasing temperature extremes53. Under such conditions, heat stress can impose stomatal and photosynthetic limitations54, thereby delaying post-disturbance recovery55. In some cases, experiments suggest that drought-stressed plants increase stomatal conductance during heatwaves to cool leaves, accelerating water loss and compounding drought-induced mortality risk56. Additionally, greening itself may heighten exposure to hot–dry stress by increasing transpiration demand and accelerating soil-water depletion57. Taken together, these patterns suggest that drying may amplify the resilience impacts of heatwaves.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, lag-1 TAC is an indirect proxy for resilience within the CSD framework and can be sensitive to non-stationarity, long-term trends, and changes in the underlying noise structure. To minimize these effects, we performed robustness checks across multiple vegetation indicators and window lengths. Direct and precise measurement of resilience remains challenging because it generally requires controlled disturbance–recovery experiments or waiting for sufficiently large natural perturbations12. Accordingly, TAC-based metrics remain widely used in resilience change studies6,13,36,46. Second, precipitation trends provide a simple proxy for hydroclimatic drying but cannot capture all dimensions of drought processes; future work could incorporate complementary drought metrics (e.g., SPI, SPEI, and soil moisture) and consider explicit driver–response time lags to better attribute underlying mechanisms.

Our results reveal widespread decoupling between greening and resilience across global semiarid regions, raising concerns about the sustainability of greening trajectories. We further show that the association between heatwave maximum intensity and resilience decline is strongest under drying. Overall, these findings caution against treating greening as a proxy for ecosystem stability and underscore the value of integrating resilience indicators into routine greenness monitoring, particularly because resilience loss can be detectable before declines in greenness become apparent58. Such integration offers a practical pathway toward more robust assessments of semiarid ecosystem vulnerability and may help inform targeted conservation and restoration strategies under intensifying compound hot–dry extremes.

Methods

Study domain

Semiarid regions were defined using the Aridity Index (AI), calculated as the ratio of total annual precipitation to potential evapotranspiration (AI = P/PET). We used the AI dataset at 0.5° × 0.5° resolution based on the 1961–1990 climatology59. Following the standard UNEP dryland classification, semiarid regions were defined as 0.20 ≤ AI < 0.5026. Analyses were further restricted to 60°S–60°N to minimize potential high-latitude influences related to snow/ice conditions (Fig. 1h). We used a time-invariant semiarid mask to ensure a consistent study domain across all analyses.

Vegetation indicators

We used three satellite-based vegetation indicators to characterize vegetation resilience, capturing complementary aspects of photosynthetic functioning, canopy greenness, and canopy structure. (i) Solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) was obtained from the monthly GOSIF v2 product at 0.05° spatial resolution60. This dataset provides a proxy for photosynthetic activity and is generated using a machine-learning algorithm trained on OCO-2 SIF observations, MODIS surface reflectance, and meteorological reanalysis data. (ii) Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) was derived from the MODIS/Terra Vegetation Indices Monthly L3 Global 0.05° CMG product (MOD13C2, Collection 6.1) at 0.05° spatial resolution61. Pixels with very low values (NDVI < 0.1) were removed using a vegetation mask based on the 2001–2024 long-term mean NDVI, excluding barren or perennial snow/ice. (iii) Leaf area index (LAI) was derived from the MODIS 6.1 Terra LAI/FPAR 8-day composite product (MOD15A2H) via Google Earth Engine (GEE)62. Because MOD15A2H is provided at 500 m and an 8-day interval, we aggregated it to monthly values by averaging within each month and then regridded it to the ~0.05° analysis grid using bilinear resampling.

Selection of stable, low-disturbance vegetation pixels

To minimize potential confounding from land-cover conversion, we used the annual MODIS MCD12C1 land-cover product (Majority_Land_Cover_Type_1)63 to identify pixels with stable natural vegetation (IGBP 1–10; including forests, shrublands, savannas, and grasslands, etc.). We retained only pixels whose IGBP class remained identical in every year from 2001 to 2024; pixels exhibiting any class change were treated as converted and excluded. In addition, to further reduce recurrent fire disturbance, we used the GFEDv5 burned-area product over 2002–202264 (the latest period available for our analysis) to compute the cumulative burned fraction. We then excluded pixels with cumulative burned fraction above the 95th percentile as high-fire pixels65. This filter removes only the most fire-affected pixels rather than restricting the analysis to fire-free conditions. The resulting stable, low-disturbance vegetation mask was applied to all vegetation indicators in subsequent analyses.

Lag-1 temporal autocorrelation

Before estimating lag-1 temporal autocorrelation (TAC) as a proxy for vegetation resilience, monthly vegetation indicators (SIF/NDVI/LAI) were detrended and deseasonalized using STL (Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess). STL decomposes a time series into long-term trend, seasonal, and residual components; the residual component was treated as the anomaly series for estimating lag-1 TAC (Supplementary Fig. 2). The decomposition was implemented using the stl() function in the stats package in R v4.3.366.

Greening–resilience quadrant framework

To compare temporal changes in vegetation greening and resilience, we calculated spatial mean trajectories of the 60-month right-aligned sliding mean SIF and the corresponding lag-1 TAC time series (Fig. 1f). For each time step, uncertainty in the spatial mean was quantified using a two-sided t-based 95% confidence interval. We estimated the linear trend in the mean SIF series, with a positive (negative) slope indicating greening (browning). By comparing the mean SIF trajectory with the corresponding mean lag-1 TAC over the same period, we assessed whether greening coincided with increasing or declining resilience.

To characterize the joint distribution of greening and resilience changes, we visualized the relationship between SIF trends and SIF lag-1 TAC trends using a two-dimensional kernel density plot (Fig. 2b). Pixels were classified into four greening–resilience quadrants based on the signs of the two trends; only pixels with statistically significant trends in both variables (P < 0.05) were included. Specifically, Quadrant 1 represents greening (SIF trend > 0) with declining resilience (lag-1 TAC trend > 0), Quadrant 2 represents browning (SIF trend < 0) with declining resilience, Quadrant 3 represents browning and increasing resilience (lag-1 TAC trend < 0), and Quadrant 4 represents greening with increasing resilience.

Identification and calculation of heatwaves

Heatwaves are prolonged periods of anomalously high temperatures and were defined as periods of at least three consecutive days during which the daily maximum temperature (Tmax) exceeds the 90th percentile threshold for that calendar day, according to the relative threshold method67. The 90th percentile threshold for each calendar day was estimated using a 15-day moving window ( ± 7 days) and daily Tmax data from the full study period (2001–2024). Daily Tmax was derived from the ERA5 hourly 2 m air temperature reanalysis dataset as the daily maximum of the hourly values68. After identifying heatwaves, we calculated four commonly used annual metrics: heatwave count, heatwave duration, cumulative intensity, and maximum intensity19. Among them, heatwave count refers to the number of heatwave events occurring within a given year. Heatwave duration is defined as the average number of consecutive days per heatwave event in a year. Cumulative intensity is defined as the total temperature exceedance above the 90th percentile threshold, summed across all heatwave days in a year. Maximum intensity is defined as the mean of the peak daily temperature exceedances above the threshold recorded in each heatwave event in a year.

Spatial error models

To quantify how trends in heatwave characteristics and precipitation are associated with changes in vegetation resilience, we fitted spatial error models using the errorsarlm function in the R package spatialreg, which implements a simultaneous autoregressive error specification. The trend in SIF lag-1 TAC was used as the response variable, and trends in heatwave count, heatwave duration, maximum intensity, and precipitation were included as explanatory variables. All explanatory variables were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) prior to model fitting. To reduce multicollinearity among heatwave metrics, we excluded cumulative intensity because it was strongly collinear with other heatwave metrics; variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the remaining predictors were < 5.

We first fitted an ordinary least squares (OLS) model and assessed spatial dependence in the residuals. A Moran’s I test on OLS residuals indicated significant positive spatial autocorrelation. To characterize the spatial dependence structure, we computed an empirical variogram of the standardized OLS residuals and fitted an exponential variogram model. The fitted correlation range ( ~ 93 km) was used to define a distance-based neighbor list (great-circle distances) and to construct a row-standardized spatial weights matrix W. Second, using the 93-km weight matrix W, we conducted Lagrange multiplier (LM) diagnostics to compare alternative spatial specifications, which favored the spatial error specification. In spatial error models, spatial autocorrelation is captured in the disturbance term rather than through a spatial lag of the response. Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM)

To characterize within-quadrant multivariate associations among heatwaves, greening, and resilience change, we fitted partial least squares structural equation models (PLS-SEM) separately for each greening–resilience quadrant using the plspm package in R v4.3.3. PLS-SEM is a component-based path modeling approach suitable for estimating standardized path coefficients in multivariate systems without strict distributional assumptions69. The model included observed variables of heatwave maximum intensity trend, precipitation trend, SIF trend (greening), and SIF lag-1 TAC trend (resilience change). We focused on maximum intensity because it provided the most robust predictor of lag-1 TAC in the spatial error models. All variables were standardized prior to model fitting to ensure comparability of effect sizes. We assessed the stability and significance of path coefficients using 1000 bootstrap resamples. The bidirectional dashed arrow denotes the partial correlation between the SIF trend and the SIF lag-1 TAC trend after controlling for precipitation and maximum intensity trends. Models were fitted independently within each quadrant to avoid cross-quadrant inference and to reflect conditional relationships under the quadrant definitions.
