High-quality intraprocedural echocardiography is fundamental to successful tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER), where accurate tricuspid valve (TV) leaflet visualization is critical for effective grasping. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) remains the cornerstone imaging modality for T-TEER.1 However, suboptimal TEE imaging may reduce procedural success and lead to patients being declined for T-TEER; up to 14% of T-TEER screening failures are attributable to inadequate imaging quality.2

Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) has emerged as a complementary imaging modality in structural heart interventions. By positioning a transducer within the right atrium (RA), ICE improves leaflet visualization and grasping through higher frame rates, closer proximity to the anteriorly positioned TV, and reduced susceptibility to acoustic shadowing from cardiac implantable electronic devices or intracardiac prostheses.3, 4, 5, 6 ICE also reduces TEE probe manipulation, which carries higher complication rates during structural heart procedures than during cardiac surgery.7 Accordingly, the 2025 Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions Position Statement on ICE to Guide Structural Heart Disease Interventions recognizes ICE as an important adjunct when TEE imaging quality is suboptimal.3

Previous reports demonstrating the feasibility of combined ICE-TEE guidance and advanced 3-dimensional (3D) ICE imaging for T-TEER predominantly used a transfemoral ICE approach.6,8 Comparatively, recent work from Mariani et al9 described echocardiographer-guided transjugular 3D ICE imaging for TV interventions, illustrating potential advantages of superior vena cava (SVC) access. Unlike transfemoral ICE, where simultaneous ICE navigation and device implantation increase technical demands for interventionalists, transjugular ICE allows the echocardiographer to control the entire intraprocedural imaging strategy, while the interventionalist focuses exclusively on device implantation. This separation of responsibilities streamlines ICE-TEE integration and improves ergonomic workflow.

Transjugular ICE may also confer anatomic advantages by offering a more favorable trajectory to the tricuspid annulus (TA) than the transfemoral approach. SVC access optimizes coaxial alignment with less catheter flexion. Similarly, computed tomography–based analyses demonstrate a straighter, more reproducible SVC-to-TA relationship compared with the more variable, offset inferior vena cava trajectory.10 Despite these advantages, published experience for combined transjugular ICE-TEE guidance for T-TEER is limited, and optimal workflow strategies remain poorly described.

After institutional ICE acquisition, our structural heart team implemented combined transjugular ICE-TEE in 65% (20 of 31) of T-TEER procedures. Early experience from this cohort demonstrated a mean imaging setup time of 12.5 minutes, a mean procedural duration of 107.1 minutes, a mean fluoroscopy time of 31.8 minutes, a mean of 1.6 clips implanted per case, a mean residual tricuspid regurgitation (TR) severity of 1.9+ (using a 5-point grading scale), and 1 periprocedural complication (1 single leaflet device attachment). We describe our institutional technique and advantages of echocardiographer-led combined transjugular ICE-TEE for T-TEER.

Case Summary

A 91-year-old woman with massive TR, NYHA functional class II-III dyspnea and recent hospitalization for right heart failure was referred for T-TEER. Screening TEE, reviewed by our multidisciplinary structural heart team, confirmed anatomic suitability; however, suboptimal mid- and deep-esophageal TEE grasping views necessitated a combined ICE-TEE approach (Figure 1). Echocardiography showed massive atrial functional TR with a predominantly central jet, 7-mm coaptation defect, 40-mm TA diameter, moderate right ventricular (RV) dilatation, and borderline reduced RV function. A preexisting single-chamber pacemaker did not impinge leaflet motion.

Potential Pitfalls

Access-site complications represent an important consideration in transjugular ICE-guided imaging. Although comparative data between transjugular and transfemoral ICE are lacking, the consequences of transjugular bleeding and neck hematoma are important considerations. Ultrasound-guided access, meticulous hemostasis using a hemostatic suture strategy, and smooth anesthetic emergence are essential to reducing hematoma risk.1 Where resources permit, RIJ cannula removal can be deferred to recovery. Sterility is another important consideration. No commercially available sheath/barrier permits ICE manipulation while maintaining sterility, necessitating institution-specific solutions and highlighting an unmet need for standardized sterile ICE barrier systems.

Regardless of the access approach, the risks of intracardiac injury, perforation, and arrhythmia should not be overlooked. Current ICE catheters provide limited tactile feedback, necessitating careful fluoroscopic and TEE surveillance during catheter manipulation. If catheter maneuvers do not correspond with expected fluoroscopic movement or ICE image changes, manipulation should cease and the catheter position should be reassessed before further handling.

In the absence of contraindications or unfavorable internal jugular anatomy, combined transjugular ICE imaging may be feasible in most cases at experienced centers. Because ICE image quality depends on transducer proximity to the TV, marked RA dilatation may result in far-field imaging and reduced spatial resolution from the SVC approach. In such cases, a “candy cane” maneuver (120° tip retroflexion, microadjusting lateral flexion, and omniplane; Figure 9) can recreate standard grasping views. Given the risk of intracardiac injury, this maneuver should be reserved for marked RA dilatation and performed under continuous TEE guidance. Transfemoral ICE may represent a useful alternative when this maneuver is deemed unsafe or provides inadequate imaging. Although transfemoral access may provide favorable coaxial device alignment in vertically oriented TAs, our early experience suggests that transjugular ICE still provides adequate imaging without meaningful limitations in such patients; multiplanar catheter tip manipulation permits optimization of insonation alignment despite this theoretical constraint. A future study is needed to define predictors of challenging transjugular ICE imaging.

Conclusions

Transjugular ICE is a valuable adjunct in T-TEER, enhancing ICE-TEE synergy and intraprocedural ergonomics; by integrating complementary imaging within a coordinated workflow, combined transjugular ICE-TEE streamlines execution and may help improve procedural ergonomics.
