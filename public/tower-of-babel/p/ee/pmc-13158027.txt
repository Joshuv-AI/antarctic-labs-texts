Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists and Ocular Outcomes: Metabolic Transition, Retinal Vulnerability, and Risk-Stratified Monitoring
Authors: Su Jeong Song, EunAh Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and dual incretin-based therapies provide marked reductions in glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), body weight, and cardiovascular risk. As global adoption expands and recognition of their broad metabolic benefits grows, clinical attention is shifting toward potential secondary complications, including ocular manifestations, during rapid metabolic improvement. This narrative review synthesizes evidence from randomized trials, meta-analyses, and observational studies up to 2026 to evaluate the effects of GLP-1–based therapies on various ocular outcomes. Recent meta-analyses demonstrate an overall neutral long-term risk for diabetic retinopathy (DR) and macular edema. Transient early worsening of DR occurs primarily in patients with advanced baseline disease and rapid HbA1c reductions, reflecting a metabolic transition phenomenon rather than intrinsic retinal toxicity. This interpretation is supported by 2024 cardiovascular outcome data in a non-diabetic population (e.g., Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People with Overweight or Obesity [SELECT] trial) that showed no increased ocular risk. Observational data suggest protective associations with glaucoma via intraocular pressure-independent neuroprotection and a reduced risk of incident age-related macular degeneration, but a potential safety signal for nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy has emerged in recent datasets. Although the absolute incidence remains low, risk with a delayed temporal pattern appears to be increased in specific cohorts. Emerging evidence also suggests potential benefits in ocular surface homeostasis and uveitis. Accordingly, following a 2025 multidisciplinary expert consensus, risk-stratified ophthalmic monitoring, rather than routine treatment avoidance, is recommended during the early metabolic transition in high-risk diabetic patients.Keywords: Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, Diabetic retinopathy, Obesity, Macular edema, Ischemic optic neuropathy, Metabolic syndrome

INTRODUCTION

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have substantially advanced the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity by producing significant reductions in glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), body weight, and cardiovascular risk.1,2 As their use expands across diabetic and non-diabetic populations, clinical attention has shifted beyond glycemic efficacy toward the broad systemic consequences of profound metabolic change.3

Ocular outcomes can be influenced not only by receptor-level signaling within retinal tissues but also by the magnitude and rate of systemic metabolic correction. Early worsening of diabetic retinopathy (DR) during intensive glycemic control, first established in the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT), indicates that rapid glycemic improvement can transiently destabilize vulnerable retinal microvasculature.4 With the advent of highly potent incretin-based therapies that can induce marked early HbA1c reduction and rapid weight loss, similar questions about retinal vulnerability and broad microvascular safety have re-emerged, along with the possibility of tissue-specific protective or adverse effects across distinct ocular diseases, including reported signals involving nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION).5,6

Advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy in a young male receiving semaglutide

A 32-year-old man presented with decreased vision in the right eye. Best-corrected visual acuity was hand motion and a fundus examination found high-risk proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) complicated by dense vitreous hemorrhage and macula-involving tractional retinal detachment in the right eye (Fig. 1A). The left eye demonstrated extensive retinal hemorrhages and neovascularization at the disc and elsewhere, consistent with PDR (Fig. 1B and C).

(B) Wide-field fundus photograph of the left eye showing extensive retinal hemorrhages and neovascularization at the disc and elsewhere, consistent with proliferative diabetic retinopathy. (C) Fluorescein angiography of the left eye revealing neovascularization of the disc and neovascularization elsewhere with active leakage (orange arrow).

The patient had been diagnosed with T2DM and obesity 12 years earlier. Ten months prior to presentation, he initiated treatment with semaglutide in combination with oral hypoglycemic agents at another institution. Due to episodes of hypoglycemia, the oral agents were discontinued, and semaglutide monotherapy was continued. At the time of referral, fasting blood glucose levels were in the 80 mg/dL range and HbA1c was 4.7%. A review of the available records revealed no documented rapid decline in HbA1c or abrupt weight reduction within the preceding 3 to 4 months before the onset of visual symptoms.

Notably, the patient had not undergone an ophthalmologic evaluation at the time of his initial T2DM diagnosis in 2014, nor was retinal screening performed at the time of semaglutide initiation. Pars plana vitrectomy was performed in the right eye to manage the vitreous hemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment, and panretinal photocoagulation was performed in the left eye. Without prior ophthalmologic consultation, the treating endocrinologist discontinued semaglutide therapy during the surgical course due to concern about possible PDR progression. The advanced proliferative stage at the first ophthalmologic assessment reflects the absence of structured retinal surveillance and coordinated interdisciplinary evaluations at key clinical transition points.

Macular edema following rapid weight reduction with tirzepatide

A 27-year-old woman presented with blurred vision and metamorphopsia that had developed approximately 1 month prior to the evaluation. She had been receiving tirzepatide for obesity and experienced a 14-kg weight reduction over 3 to 4 months of treatment. Best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in both eyes at presentation. Color fundus photographs showed no clinically apparent macular abnormalities (Fig. 2A). Swept-source optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated intraretinal cystic spaces consistent with subclinical macular edema in the right eye. Fluorescein angiography demonstrated very mild macular leakage in both eyes (Fig. 2B). Topical bromfenac was initiated bilaterally twice daily. Follow-up OCT showed resolution of the intraretinal cyst in the right eye, with corresponding symptomatic improvement.

Fundus and multimodal imaging findings in macular edema associated with rapid metabolic transition. (A) Color fundus photographs of both eyes demonstrating no clinically apparent macular abnormalities at presentation. (B) Fluorescein angiography showing very mild macular leakage (red arrows) in both eyes at presentation. (C) Swept-source optical coherence tomography images obtained at recurrence demonstrating bilateral intraretinal cystic changes (orange arrows).

Two months later, intraretinal cystic changes recurred in both eyes (Fig. 2C). Topical bromfenac was increased to three times daily in both eyes. One month after that, OCT demonstrated resolution of macular edema, and visual symptoms improved.

The development of macular edema temporally coincided with a period of marked weight reduction. Despite recurrence, the edema responded to topical anti-inflammatory therapy without the need for intravitreal intervention, and tirzepatide therapy was continued during follow-up. These cases illustrate distinct manifestations of retinal vulnerability in the context of metabolic transition, underscoring the need for careful clinical interpretation within the context of metabolic transition.

PHARMACOLOGIC MECHANISMS AND METABOLIC EFFECTS

GLP-1 RAs improve glycemic control through glucose-dependent insulin secretion and glucagon suppression, and they promote weight loss via delayed gastric emptying and central appetite regulation.7 Dual incretin agonists, such as tirzepatide, enhance those metabolic effects through combined glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) activation.8 Recently, the therapeutic landscape has expanded to include triple-hormone receptor agonists such as retatrutide, which integrate glucagon receptor activation to achieve substantial weight reductions of up to 24%.9 Clinically, GLP-1–based therapies reduce HbA1c by 1.0%–2.5%, with larger early declines in individuals with higher baseline HbA1c, and produce weight loss of 10%–20%, depending on the agent and population studied.1-3,10,11

Beyond glycemic efficacy, landmark evidence from the Effect of Semaglutide Versus Placebo on the Progression of Renal Impairment in People With Type 2 Diabetes and Chronic Kidney Disease (FLOW) trial12 has solidified the renal benefits of these agents, demonstrating a 24% reduction in major kidney disease events. Together with experimental evidence that anti-inflammatory and vascular-protective effects are mediated through GLP-1R signaling,13 these systemic vascular actions frame ocular outcomes within a broad physiologic balance between early metabolic transition and long-term microvascular stabilization.

Diabetic retinopathy and diabetic macular edema

DR remains the leading cause of preventable vision loss among working-age adults with diabetes17 and the ocular outcome most extensively evaluated in association with GLP-1 RAs. Randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses of cardiovascular outcome trials have not demonstrated a statistically significant increase in overall DR risk.10 However, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis identified an increased relative risk of DR (1.21; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.05 to 1.39) associated with semaglutide, particularly in individuals with long diabetes duration and high baseline HbA1c.18 At the class level, comparative real-world analyses demonstrate similar rates of incident DR between GLP-1 RA and sodium glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor users.19

Diabetic macular edema (DME), a vision-threatening manifestation of retinal microvascular dysfunction, can occur at any stage of retinopathy.20 Compared with DR, data specifically addressing DME in GLP-1 RA users remain sparse. The available evidence does not demonstrate a consistent increase in DME incidence at the class level.10,11 However, case-level observations and a 2025 multidisciplinary expert consensus21 suggest that transient macular edema can occur, particularly during early periods of substantial glycemic improvement and in treatment-naive patients with high baseline retinopathy severity.

Mechanistic framework: early vulnerability and later stabilization in diabetes-related retinal diseases

Transient early worsening of DR has been observed primarily in individuals with advanced baseline disease who are undergoing marked early HbA1c reduction, consistent with the phenomenon described in the DCCT.4 Clinical evidence for this vulnerable phase has been reinforced by a recent meta-analysis that identified an increased risk of DR complications during the initial stages of semaglutide therapy.18 At the molecular level, transient alterations in hypoxia-related and angiogenic signaling pathways could contribute to temporary disruption of the blood–retinal barrier, predisposing susceptible eyes to retinopathy progression or macular fluid accumulation.14,22

In experimental ischemic retinopathy models, GLP-1 RAs have been shown to modulate myeloid cell-mediated inflammatory signaling and retinal angiogenesis, further supporting the possibility of context-dependent vascular effects.23 Similar transient retinal changes have been described following intensive insulin therapy and bariatric surgery, supporting a mechanism related to systemic metabolic adjustment, rather than direct drug-related toxicity.24

In contrast, sustained GLP-1R activation appears to promote endothelial stabilization and barrier integrity. Experimental studies demonstrate enhanced tight junction preservation and the inhibition of pro-inflammatory pathways, including RhoA/Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase and stimulator of interferon genes-mediated signaling cascades, indicating long-term support for microvascular stability.14,22 Notably, Machida et al.25 recently demonstrated that GLP-1R stimulation inhibits intraocular inflammation and pathological neovascularization by suppressing inflammatory cytokine expression, further reinforcing its contribution to long-term microvascular stability.

Together, these findings support a temporal framework in which early retinal vulnerability during rapid metabolic transition might be followed by progressive endothelial stabilization with continued therapy (Fig. 3).

Glaucoma and intraocular pressure

Glaucoma is a chronic optic neuropathy characterized by progressive retinal ganglion cell loss, with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) as the primary modifiable risk factor.26,27 Recent large-scale observational studies have reported a lower incidence of primary open-angle glaucoma among GLP-1 RA users than in users of other glucose-lowering therapies.28 Preclinical models of experimental ocular hypertension suggest that GLP-1R activation attenuates neuroinflammatory signaling and promotes retinal ganglion cell survival.29

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Amaral et al.30 further supports a possible protective association, identifying a trend toward reduced glaucoma risk across multiple datasets. The mechanism underlying this potential protection is a subject of ongoing debate. Notably, Hallaj et al.31 found that IOP reductions associated with GLP-1 RA exposure are minimal (typically <1 mmHg) and unlikely to fully account for reported associations, supporting the possibility of IOP-independent neuroprotective mechanisms.

Overall, current evidence suggests a possible protective association between GLP-1 RA use and glaucoma risk, but definitive causality awaits confirmation from prospective clinical trials.

Nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy

NAION is an acute ischemic optic neuropathy characterized by sudden painless vision loss, and it is associated with systemic vascular risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, nocturnal hypotension, and obstructive sleep apnea, as well as structural optic nerve susceptibility, including a crowded optic disc.32,33 Observational studies have identified a safety signal linking GLP-1 RAs, particularly semaglutide, to NAION. A study published in 2024 reported adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) of 4.28 (95% CI, 1.62 to 11.29) in patients with T2DM (reference: those prescribed non-GLP-1 RA diabetic medications) and 7.64 (95% CI, 2.21 to 26.36) in those with obesity (reference: those prescribed non-GLP-1 RA anti-obesity medications).34 A recent meta-analysis also reported a pooled risk increase with an HR of 2.62.35 Other large-scale observational studies across diverse populations have reported more modest relative risk increases across studies.36,37 In a claims-based target trial emulation comparing GLP-1 RAs with SGLT2 inhibitors, the adjusted HR was 1.85 (95% CI, 1.51 to 2.27).6 Despite those relative increases, the absolute incidence remains low, on the order of a few cases per 10,000 person-years.36,37

The findings remain heterogeneous, with other large-scale analyses reporting no statistically significant association.38,39 The European Medicines Agency Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee40 recently categorized NAION as a ‘very rare’ potential adverse event, noting that current evidence is insufficient to establish definitive causality. The discrepancies across observational datasets could stem from surveillance bias or differential outcome ascertainment because NAION was not systematically adjudicated in most of the cardiovascular outcome trials.18,34,38

Importantly, the temporal profile of reported GLP-1 RA–associated NAION differs from that described with vasoactive agents such as phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil), which have been hypothesized to precipitate NAION through acute vasodilatory effects.41 In contrast, when GLP-1 RA-associated risk divergence has been observed, separation of the incidence curves typically emerges after several months of exposure, suggesting a mechanism that might be consistent with long-term metabolic adjustment or microvascular remodeling, rather than immediate hemodynamic compromise.34

Age-related macular degeneration

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a chronic, progressive degenerative disorder of the macula characterized by drusen accumulation and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction that leads to central vision loss.42 Clinically, AMD is categorized into nonexudative (dry) disease—ranging from early and intermediate stages to advanced geographic atrophy—and exudative (neovascular) AMD, defined by choroidal neovascularization and subretinal fluid or hemorrhage.42

AMD has not been a prespecified endpoint in major cardiovascular outcome trials of GLP-1 RAs, and prospective adjudicated data remain lacking. Early evidence about GLP-1 signaling in AMD was largely limited to experimental models demonstrating that GLP-1R activation within retinal tissue produced anti-inflammatory effects.22,25

Recent large-scale real-world evidence has expanded the clinical signal in this domain. In a propensity score-matched electronic health record cohort including more than 600,000 adults with T2DM, GLP-1 RA exposure was associated with a lower observed risk of incident nonexudative and exudative AMD during long-term follow-up.43 Notably, risk divergence was time-dependent, emerging after approximately 5 years of therapy and persisting through 10 years of follow-up.43 However, an up-to-date national cohort study observed this protective effect earlier, within 1 to 3 years of medication prescription.44

Among patients with baseline early or intermediate nonexudative AMD, GLP-1 RA use was further associated with a lower risk of progression to advanced nonexudative or exudative disease during extended follow-up.43 Subgroup analyses suggested potential heterogeneity by GLP-1 RA subtype; the associations were more consistent among those taking long-acting human GLP-1 analogues than in those taking short-acting exendin-based agents.43 Among patients with baseline non-neovascular AMD, GLP-1 RA use showed no significant effect on the risk of conversion to neovascular AMD at the 3-year follow-up, even in validation analyses limited to those with early or intermediate stage disease.44

Mechanistically, GLP-1R activation has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects in preclinical models, including the suppression of inflammatory cytokine production, modulation of macrophage infiltration, preservation of blood–retinal barrier integrity, and protection of RPE and photoreceptor cells from degeneration.22,25 These pathways overlap with key mechanisms implicated in AMD pathogenesis, including chronic retinal inflammation and oxidative injury.

It is important to note that much of the current evidence about NAION and AMD is derived from observational cohorts and claims-based analyses. These findings should thus be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causative because reliance on administrative coding and the absence of standardized ophthalmologic adjudication preclude definite causal inference. Furthermore, the possibility of surveillance bias cannot be excluded; patients who begin taking high-profile therapies such as semaglutide might undergo more frequent ophthalmic screening than the general population, potentially leading to a higher than usual detection rate of asymptomatic or mild conditions. Thus, although the current data suggest potential long-term associations, prospective validation to confirm these findings is warranted.

Dry eye syndrome, uveitis, and other ocular conditions

Ocular surface and inflammatory disorders are common in individuals with metabolic disease; however, their relationship with incretin-based therapies remains incompletely defined.

GLP-1R expression has been identified in the lacrimal gland, suggesting a potential role in ocular surface immune regulation and tear homeostasis.16 Recent clinical data by Ottonelli et al.45 demonstrated that GLP-1 RA therapy is associated with significant improvements in ocular surface parameters, including tear break-up time and Schirmer test scores, in patients with T2DM.

Prospective data about uveitis have long been lacking. However, Mohan et al.46 analyzed a large electronic health record database and found that GLP-1 RA exposure was associated with a 19% reduced risk of noninfectious uveitis (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.74 to 0.89). This association was consistent across various subtypes of noninfectious uveitis, suggesting a potential systemic anti-inflammatory effect that might extend to the intraocular environment. These findings are consistent with experimental evidence of GLP-1R–mediated suppression of inflammatory cytokines within the eye.22,25

Overall, a systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that the available data do not indicate a clinically meaningful safety signal in anterior segment or inflammatory ocular disorders associated with GLP-1 RA therapy.18 On the other hand, recent clinical findings suggest a potential therapeutic benefit in dry eye and uveitis, likely mediated through localized and systemic anti-inflammatory signaling, though those findings remain preliminary. These areas represent an active frontier of investigation and warrant prospective evaluation in dedicated ophthalmic clinical trials.

DRUG-SPECIFIC SIGNALS AND INTERPRETATION

Although safety signals for ocular adverse events have been reported across the GLP-1 RA class, the strength and consistency of those signals vary significantly by agent. As noted in the NAION and DR sections, semaglutide exhibits the most prominent safety signal in observational datasets.18,34,35 This disproportionate reporting might reflect longer market presence and reporting dynamics, rather than clearly indicating pharmacological differences between molecules. Specifically, high-profile agents often experience a notoriety effect, in which increased clinical and public visibility leads to a higher frequency of adverse event reporting.

The Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People with Overweight or Obesity (SELECT) trial, which evaluated semaglutide in more than 17,000 patients without diabetes, provides an important counterpoint; it did not demonstrate a significant increase in adjudicated ocular adverse events during a mean follow-up of 40 months.47 Thus, the early worsening observed in diabetic cohorts is likely a secondary hemodynamic and metabolic phenomenon related to rapid glucose lowering in vulnerable retinas, rather than direct drug-mediated ocular toxicity.

As a dual GIP/GLP-1 RA, tirzepatide exhibits even greater metabolic potency than semaglutide.8 However, the current evidence provides no clear NAION signal for tirzepatide. This discrepancy raises the question of whether, on the one hand, GIP receptor activation might provide synergistic microvascular stabilization or, on the other hand, the current lack of signal is simply due to shorter post-marketing surveillance. Recent preclinical evidence suggests that GIP might have independent anti-inflammatory effects on the vascular endothelium, potentially buffering the stress of rapid metabolic transition.48 Similarly, for the triple agonist retatrutide, phase 2 data remain ocularly neutral, but long-term cardiovascular outcome trials are required to confirm its profile.9

The route of administration and subsequent pharmacokinetic profile can also influence risk perception. Although both oral and subcutaneous semaglutide are effective, the rapid peak concentrations associated with injectable formulations might induce more abrupt physiologic shifts than the more gradual exposure of oral formulations. Population-based data across 180 countries suggest that such abruptness factors might contribute to differences in physiologic adaptation.36

Ultimately, these molecule-level observations reinforce the importance of contextual interpretation. Current evidence suggests that although individual signals vary, the long-term benefits of systemic metabolic control likely outweigh transient early risks when those risks are managed with appropriate clinical vigilance.

RISK STRATIFIED MONITORING

The expanding use of GLP-1–based therapies highlights the importance of considering ophthalmologic context alongside metabolic benefit. Clinical attention might reasonably focus on periods of potential retinal susceptibility—particularly during early glycemic transition—and on individuals with elevated baseline ocular risk, while preserving the established systemic advantages of these drugs. Although transient retinal worsening has been described in selected contexts, long-term observational data suggest largely neutral associations, with some studies indicating potential protective effects in conditions such as glaucoma and AMD.

High-risk situations include advanced baseline DR or DME, recent retinal intervention, anticipated substantial early HbA1c reduction, pronounced metabolic change, and prior NAION. A 2025 multidisciplinary expert consensus21 identified a rapid reduction in HbA1c (more than 1.5%–2.0% within a 3-month period) as a key trigger for intensified ophthalmic monitoring. Intermediate-risk scenarios include systemic vascular risk factors for NAION (e.g., hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, and nocturnal hypotension) or structural optic disc susceptibility, such as a crowded disc. The authors acknowledge that the classification system and specific follow-up intervals they propose are based on clinical judgment and expert consensus because direct prospective evidence is currently unavailable.

In high-risk settings, retinal and optic nerve assessment at treatment initiation is important, and close follow-up during the first 3–6 months is appropriate. Individuals with severe non-proliferative or proliferative DR might benefit from coordinated endocrinologic and ophthalmologic management and gradual dose titration, although evidence-based protocols for such staged reductions are currently lacking. For those with intermediate-risk factors, particularly regarding NAION susceptibility, heightened awareness of acute visual symptoms and a low threshold for scheduling an urgent neuro-ophthalmic evaluation should be maintained.

For low-risk individuals without significant baseline retinopathy and gradual metabolic change, routine screening according to standard guidelines49,50 appears sufficient, especially when it is accompanied by education about visual symptom awareness. New visual distortion, sudden vision loss, or acute field defects should prompt an urgent ophthalmologic evaluation. CONCLUSION

Most available safety data derive from observational cohorts and pharmacovigilance databases and therefore remain subject to residual confounding and differential outcome ascertainment. Prospective studies incorporating adjudicated ocular endpoints are needed to clarify causality and identify the subgroups most likely to benefit from intensified monitoring. In parallel, a recent multidisciplinary expert consensus has noted that the overall cardiometabolic benefits of GLP-1 RAs generally outweigh the potential ocular risks.21 Taken together, these considerations support the incorporation of risk-stratified ophthalmic monitoring into comprehensive metabolic care for selected high-risk populations, particularly during the early period of metabolic transition.
