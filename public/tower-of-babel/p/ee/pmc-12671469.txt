Pulse Field Ablation for Persistent Atrial Fibrillation: Targeting the Persistent Superior Vena Cava
Authors: Sara Vázquez‐Calvo, Vladimir Poletaev, Atul Verma, Vidal Essebag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPersistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) is a rare vascular anomaly that can act as a trigger for atrial fibrillation (AF). Thermal ablation in this region carries a notable risk. Pulse field ablation (PFA), a non‐thermal modality, offers a safer alternative. We report the first case of successful PLSVC isolation using the PulseSelect catheter in a patient with recurrent persistent AF. The procedure was performed without complications, and no arrhythmia recurrence was observed at 6 months. This case supports the feasibility and safety of PLSVC isolation using PFA, highlighting its role in managing arrhythmia in patients with this venous anomaly.

Introduction

Persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) is an uncommon vascular anomaly. Left superior vena cava (LSVC) is part of the normal embryological venous system, originating at the junction of the left subclavian and internal jugular veins, crossing the left side of the mediastinum to typically drain in the coronary sinus (CS). LSVC usually regresses during intrauterine life, but it can persist. The incidence of PLSVC ranges from 0.2% to 3% in the general population, but it can be as high as 1.3%–11% among individuals with congenital heart disease [1].

While atrial fibrillation (AF) is commonly triggered by focal points in the pulmonary veins (PVs), PLSVC can also act as a trigger for AF through its connections with the CS and the left atrium [2]. Various ablation techniques have been proposed to address PLSVC, primarily focusing on targeting focal triggers or isolating the PLSVC. However, thermal ablation in this area often carries a significant risk of complications, including injury to the left phrenic nerve and cardiac tamponade [3].

Pulse field ablation (PFA) is an innovative ablation technique that utilizes high‐voltage electrical fields delivered in millisecond to microsecond intervals to create pores selectively within myocardial cells. PFA has been shown to be at least non‐inferior to traditional ablation methods regarding ablation efficacy. In addition, this selective targeting enhances the safety of the procedure [4].

Case Report

A 68‐year‐old male was referred to our institution for persistent AF. His past medical history was positive for a previous slow pathway modification 15 years ago due to recurrent atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT) episodes.

A first ablation procedure was performed 4 years ago after evidence of tachycardia‐induced cardiomyopathy (impaired LVEF of 40%–45% improving after AF control) and failure of cardioversion and medical treatment. Before this first procedure, a cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) revealed a PLSVC with no other abnormalities. The procedure was performed with the CARTO system and PV and posterior wall were isolated. Additionally, a conservative ablation of the PLSVC was performed, and a cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) line was added to mitigate the risk of atrial flutter.

Four years after this initial ablation procedure, the patient presents with recurrent AF requiring diltiazem and amiodarone. Given the recurrence of persistent AF, the patient was referred for a redo ablation procedure. A second CMR was performed before the procedure, showing a normal ejection fraction.

The second procedure was conducted under general anesthesia. Transeptal puncture was performed guided by intracardiac echo. A voltage map of the left atria was generated using the CARTO 3 system, revealing isolation of PVs but reconnection of the posterior wall and PLSVC. Ablation was performed using PFA, specifically PulseSelect system (Medtronic), reinforcing ablation in the posterior wall and targeting the gaps in the posterior antral aspect of the PVs until achieving isolation of the posterior wall (Figure 1). Subsequently, PLSVC ablation was conducted near the bifurcation with the CS until successful isolation was achieved (Figure 2). Regarding catheter positioning, the loop‐shaped PFA catheter was easily advanced into the PLSVC without difficulty. A total of six PFA applications within the PLSVC (Figure 3). Each ablation was preceded by a test pacing pulse to exclude phrenic nerve capture. No nitroglycerine was administered. Although no coronary imaging was performed, there were no ECG changes or hemodynamic instability during or after the procedure. Far‐field signals from the left atrial appendage were noted in the distal PLSVC; however, pacing attempts from this area did not result in atrial capture. The procedure concluded without any complications. The patient was equipped with a Kardia device and performed routine monthly electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings, as well as additional ECGs whenever symptoms were reported. Throughout the 6‐month follow‐up period, no arrhythmia recurrence was detected. Importantly, the patient has remained asymptomatic, with no angina or symptoms suggestive of coronary ischemia, both immediately post‐procedure and during follow‐up.

Discussion

Recently, the first case of PFA for PLSVC isolation using the PulseSelect catheter (Medtronic) have been published [5, 6], demonstrating the feasibility of this approach beyond the previously reported experience with the FARAPULSE system (Boston Scientific). The present case adds further clinical evidence supporting the use of this novel PFA technology and catheter design in anatomically challenging sites such as the PLSVC, reinforcing its safety and efficacy profile in this context.

The PLSVC is an uncommon vascular anomaly that originates from the junction of the left subclavian and internal jugular veins, coursing along the left side of the mediastinum near the aortic arch. Typically, it drains into the right atrium via the CS. Although PLSVC is relatively rare among vascular anomalies, it is the most frequent thoracic venous malformation. In most cases, PLSVC remains asymptomatic and is often identified incidentally during diagnostic or therapeutic procedures for unrelated conditions [1]. However, the role of PLSVC as a potential trigger for atrial arrhythmias is well established, with studies identifying myocardial tracts connecting the PLSVC to the lateral left atrium, particularly near the left PVs, and through the CS [7] and, more important, demonstrating its ability to initiate ectopic beats leading to AF [2], being the trigger or driver of AF in near 70% of cases [8]. For these reasons, PLSVC isolation appears critical to be targeted to prevent AF recurrence. Nevertheless, a significant rate of major complications has been reported in various studies using thermal ablation sources (radiofrequency and cryoablation), including cardiac tamponade and permanent phrenic nerve paralysis [3]. Although cases of coronary artery spasm have been described with PFA when applied near coronary arteries [9]—such as the potential involvement of the circumflex artery in this case (Figure 4)—in contrast, no major complications have been reported to date in the limited cases of PLSVC isolation using PFA [5, 6, 10, 11], supporting its favorable safety profile in this anatomically challenging region (Table 1).

PFA is an innovative form of ablation that uses high‐amplitude pulsed electrical fields to induce irreversible electroporation in targeted tissue. This process creates nanopores in cell membranes, disrupting membrane integrity and leading to cell apoptosis. The primary advantage of electroporation is its tissue specificity and relatively non‐thermal mechanism, which has been shown to significantly reduce the risk of long‐term complications such as phrenic nerve paralysis or esophageal injury, thereby greatly enhancing the safety profile of AF ablation procedures [4]. Different technical parameters can be adjusted across various commercial catheters, resulting in subtle differences between them.

While most published reports on PFA for PLSVC isolation have involved the FARAPULSE system (Boston Scientific) [10, 11], recent cases [5, 6] have demonstrated the feasibility of using the PulseSelect catheter (Medtronic), which delivers energy via a “skipped bipolar” configuration between non‐contiguous electrodes [12]. By delivering energy across more distant electrode pairs, the skipped bipolar setup generates a wider and deeper electric field, potentially increasing lesion depth compared to conventional bipolar configurations. This feature may improve transmurality in areas of variable wall thickness, which is particularly relevant in the PLSVC, where tissue thickness and fiber orientation can be irregular. Moreover, compared to FARAPULSE, the PulseSelect catheter features a smaller and more flexible circular‐array design, which may enhance maneuverability and allow easier access to the distal PLSVC.

Conclusion

PLSVC can serve as a potential trigger for AF. Isolation of the PLSVC can be safely achieved using PFA, which may help prevent the recurrence of AF.
