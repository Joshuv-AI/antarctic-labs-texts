High-resolution 3D scan of the Devil’s trails palaeontological ichnosite (Southern Italy) for the digital legacy
Authors: Francesco Carotenuto, Adolfo Panarello, Alberto De Vito Piscicelli, Alessandro Mondanaro, Marina Melchionna, Domenico Oione, Pasquale Raia, Stefano Tavani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ichnofossils are sedimentological structures left by humans and other organisms in the environment of the past. They can represent different activities of organisms but rarely can provide information about their behaviour. From this point of view, the “Devil’s trails” fossil footprints are exceptionally important since they show some of the oldest human footprints and trails in Europe and give a glimpse into the locomotor control ability of Middle Pleistocene humans. Impressed on a volcanic rock in a remote locality of Southern Italy, their extreme scientific importance deserves to be shared with the scientific community for further studies and novel interpretations. By using complex photogrammetric methods, we digitised the entire area bearing the imprints and shared the 3D model to provide a digital heritage of the site. In addition, by means of virtual reality technologies, we provided a virtual experience for an inclusive dissemination of this unique palaeontological site.

Background & Summary

The Devil’s trails fossil footprints (“Le Ciampate del Diavolo” in Italian language), located on the slopes of the Roccamonfina volcano (Province of Caserta, Campania Region in Southern Italy) (Fig. 1), are among the most significant scientific evidence of Middle Pleistocene hominin activity. The site was discovered in 20011 and, with a total of 81 human footprints5,6, it is one of the most important pieces of evidence of human settlements in these territories since the Middle Pleistocene. The site is represented by pyroclastic tuff rock produced during eruptions of the nearby Roccamonfina volcano, a stratovolcano belonging to the Roman Magmatic province and once active from around 630 to 50 kya7. While previous studies have established the site’s chronological, geological and palaeontological significance, the inherent vulnerability of the volcanic tuff to weathering requires a high-fidelity digital preservation strategy.

The fossil footprints cover a surface of approximately 2000 m2, consisting of a narrow, sub-planar area at the top and a south-east facing surface with an average slope of ~30°, reaching a maximum incline of around 38.6° 8,9. Humans and other mammals there crossing impressed their imprints on the ashes after the pyroclastic flows laid down10, when the material was still plastic. After the passages, in a relatively short period11, the pyroclastic material lithified, thus preventing erosion of the rock and the ichnofossils before their definitive burial by the materials of subsequent eruptions. Experts have identified several sequences of human footprints at the Roccamonfina site, which are attributed to at least four different trackmakers12.

The uppermost fossil structure, known as P1-pathway, consists of a ~50-metre-long fossil path with long winding grooves and bi-directional footprints within its space. Other trackways on this inclined surface, such as Trackway A and Trackway B, provide more distinct evidence of complex movement (Fig. 2). Trackway A follows a zigzag path, revealing a unique behavioural pattern4,13, which researchers suggest was a deliberate strategy to reduce energy expenditure and maintain stability while descending the steep and unsafe slope14. The dataset presented here offers the first sharable high-resolution three-dimensional reconstruction of the entire surface, taken with an unprecedented millimetric level of detail of the tracks and footprints, captured using advanced photogrammetric techniques. Error-based filtering of raw information makes this digital record surpassing in quality any previous documentation effort and allows for nuanced analysis of morphometric variability among the individual footprints. Furthermore, the data enable researchers to investigate the biomechanical adaptations required for descending steep, unstable terrain, as well as patterns of substrate interaction and taphonomic degradation over time16,17.

In line with the principles of open science, these data have been made available to the wider scientific community to ensure the permanent preservation of the Roccamonfina tracks, and to provide a robust foundation for future comparative studies in palaeoanthropology and vertebrate ichnology17,18. The material includes the full 3D model and a virtual reality experience for further dissemination.

Methods

To construct the 3D model of the Devil’s trails site, we employed the Structure from Motion – Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS) photogrammetric technique, using Agisoft Metashape Professional 2.2.2 software. Image acquisition was carried out by means of two devices:a DJI Mini 4 K Uncrewed Aerial Vehicle (UAV), equipped with a CMOS 1/2.3” sensor, which provided 1270 images at a resolution of 4000 × 2250 pixels;a Canon EOS 450D digital single-lens reflex (DSLR) camera, equipped with a CMOS APS-C 22.2 mm × 14.8 mm sensor, which yielded 1219 images at a resolution of 4272 × 2848 pixels.

The UAV was operated manually, including manual control of its tilt angle, to ensure optimal coverage through multiple viewpoints of the complex morphology of the outcrop, which is also partially covered by vegetation. A multi-scale acquisition strategy was adopted to obtain a scaled and highly detailed 3D model.

A first set of 44 UAV images was acquired from an altitude of approximately 90 m above ground level, with a ground sampling distance (GSD) of ~2.5 cm/px, in zenithal view. These covered a large area (~200 × 80 m) (Fig. 3a), and we have used the GNSS data of only these images (latitude, longitude, and ellipsoidal height) to register the model. This approach applied to such a large area preserves the overall scale and orientation relative to the Up-North-East reference frame19. A second UAV dataset focused on a more restricted area (~60 × 35 m), including the rock slope and surrounding vegetation (Fig. 3b). This included 134 images acquired at elevations ranging from ~10 m (GSD ~5 mm/px) to ~20 m (GSD ~10 mm/px). The remaining UAV images were acquired at close range—down to less than 1 m from the ground—achieving a GSD as fine as ~1 mm/px. The DSLR images were primarily focused on the detailed acquisition of the track-bearing patches. These images were taken from distances ranging between 2 m and 20 cm, reaching a ground sampling distance of up to 0.1 mm/px. The resulting dense point cloud is composed of approximately 388 million points. Of these, 309 million points are located in the central sector (60 × 35 m), which features the hominin trails.

We carried out a manual classification of the points in this second point cloud (Fig. 3c), aimed at separating points belonging to vegetation and the metal walkway (a total of 234 million points) from points representing the bedrock hosting the trails (75 million points). Lastly, we discarded points with low confidence values in the bedrock point cloud. In Agisoft Metashape, confidence is an integer from 0 to 255 that expresses the reliability of each dense cloud point based on image coverage and depth consistency. We removed points with confidence values between 0 and 3 in areas of negligible interest, except for two small bushes, where the threshold was set to 3. Along the trails and within the footprints, a threshold of 12 was generally applied, with local exceptions in areas with small patches of vegetation (e.g., lichens or moss) or in deep shadow, where confidence values as low as 7 were accepted to avoid creating holes (Fig. 3d). This resulted in a point cloud of 62 million points, where a point density of 0.12 points/mm² is achieved.

From the 62 million point cloud, we generated a mesh consisting of 100 million triangles, with an associated texture map of 16384 × 16384 pixels. We produced a second DTM at a resolution of 0.2 m/pixel covering the entire area (~200 × 80 m), for technical validation purposes, following the method described in the corresponding section.

Data Records

The data record includes the textured mesh in an open Figshare repository21 (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31771204) and is shared under the CC BY 4.0 use license. It consists of an OBJ format file for scientific research, provided in WGS 84/UTM zone 33 N [EPSG:32633] coordinate system, with the elevation provided in ellipsoidal height, and a BLEND file format to be loaded in the Blender 3D software (www.blender.org), for high-resolution renderings and dissemination. Both formats are scaled. The virtual reality experience with a low-resolution 3D model can be freely accessed by browser on any digital device22.

Technical Validation

To assess the geometric accuracy of our final DTM of the slope featuring the trails, we compared the large DTM (~200 × 80 m area) at 0.2 m/px resolution to the regional 1-m resolution LiDAR DTM provided by the Campania Region and available at https://sim.mase.gov.it/portalediaccesso/mappe/#/viewer/new (File 1148407). The comparison was based on the identification of morphologies clearly recognizable in both models. The observed agreement suggests a scaling error comparable to the resolution of the reference DTM, implying a maximum overall scaling error of approximately 1%. The comparison also revealed a systematic shift of our DTM with respect to the reference DTM, estimated at approximately 5 ± 1 m to the north, 3 ± 1 m to the west, and 5.5 ± 1 m downward. DTM comparison also shows no significant rotation about the vertical axis, suggesting an angular discrepancy of less than 1 m over a distance of 100 m, i.e., approximately 0.5°. Following the methodology of Corradetti et al.23, we also estimated the reconstructed tilt angle of the UAV photos. Given that the presence of a gimbal stabilizer constrains this angle to zero, our result—a discrepancy of 0.5°— confirms the low magnitude of rotational errors also about the X and Y axes. These negligible scaling and rotational errors, along with translational offsets of a few metres, are consistent with the expected uncertainty associated with georeferencing based solely on UAV GNSS data. They do not affect the internal consistency of the model, which remains fully suitable for local-scale morphometric and geological analysis.
