Short tandem repeat (STR) typing of a deceased individual with an extensive blood transfusion history: A case report
Authors: Bea G. Gallardo, Minerva D. Sagum, Miriam Ruth M. Dalet, Nelvie Fatima Jane A. Soliven, Paul Ryan L. Sales, Jazelyn M. Salvador, Maria Corazon A. De Ungria, Gayvelline C. Calacal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Detecting donor DNA in blood samples after multiple allogeneic transfusions remains a persistent concern in forensic human identification, given the limited and variable data. Although some studies have reported no secondary DNA profiles, transfusion-associated microchimerism (TA-MC) has been observed in deceased individuals who received perimortem blood transfusions.In this case report, we present autosomal short tandem repeat (aSTR) findings for a deceased male who received four units of plasma-reduced RBCs within 24 hours and 25 units over a period of eight months before his death. Given this extensive blood transfusion history, there was concern that donor DNA might persist and interfere with obtaining a reliable aSTR profile for paternity testing. To address this, we analyzed buccal swabs, skin, and muscle tissue, in addition to blood samples. No secondary aSTR profile was detected in the blood, and all four sample types produced concordant DNA profiles.In this instance, results suggest that any donor DNA present was below the detection threshold for STR analysis using capillary electrophoresis (CE) but may be detectable with more sensitive genotyping assays such as next generation sequencing (NGS) or digital PCR. Detection is more likely after a recent massive pRBC transfusion, the use of non-leukoreduced blood, or transfusion in leukopenic recipients. Despite the complications associated with massive allogeneic blood transfusions, aSTR typing remains a robust and reliable method for forensic human identification, particularly when suitable sample types are selected and rigorous procedures are followed in cases involving transfused individuals.

Introduction

Blood is commonly used for genotyping because of its ease of collection and processing, abundance of nucleated cells, and high DNA yield [1]. However, blood transfusions can complicate DNA interpretation due to microchimerism, in which a small number of nucleated non-self-cells (<5%) coexist with the recipient cells [2,3]. Microchimerism can arise during pregnancy or transplantation, complicating DNA analysis when detected using PCR-based methods [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]].

Transfusion-associated microchimerism (TA-MC) complicates DNA analysis because donor cells from massive allogeneic blood transfusions may persist in the body temporarily or for extended periods [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. This persistence raises concerns regarding the reliability of blood as the sole DNA source for short tandem repeat (STR) typing in forensic human identification. While some studies have suggested that blood transfusions do not affect STR typing [7], donor DNA may be introduced via extracellular DNA [8] and subsequently detected in the recipient genotype [9,10]. The detection of donor DNA depends heavily on the analytical sensitivity of the genotyping method. For instance, STR analysis via capillary electrophoresis (CE) detects variants at 1–5% [5,6], next generation sequencing (NGS) at 0.01–1%, and digital PCR at 0.01–0.1% [6]. In a simulation study examining transfusions of 2, 5, and 16 units of partially leukoreduced blood, donor-derived DNA was detected in a leukopenic recipient at rates of 6.3%, 12.2%, and 27.8%, respectively; for non-leukoreduced blood, the detection rate was reported to be 76-96% [5]. These events can undermine the accuracy of STR typing of blood samples, potentially leading to misinterpretations during forensic investigations.

Case information

A DNA paternity test was requested for a recently deceased male who received 29 units of plasma-reduced RBC concentrates (pRBC; packed red blood cells or leukoreduced blood) before his death. Four units were transfused within 24 hours to address acute blood loss, while 25 units were administered over an eight-month period. Unfortunately, the deceased's complete medical records were inaccessible; therefore, the details of this case were derived from the verbal accounts of his children. Consequently, accurate time-dependent diagnostic outcomes could not be obtained for correlation and further evaluation.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Informed consent was obtained from the family for publication of the study, provided that the persons involved would remain anonymous. The manuscript was reviewed by the University of the Philippines Manila Research Ethics Board (UPMREB) in compliance with institutional guidelines. A full board ethics review was deemed unnecessary because the work was done following the request of the deceased's family rather than as part of a research protocol (RGAO-2025-0619).

Samples

Given the deceased's extensive history of blood transfusion, multiple sample types were collected two days postmortem before embalming to mitigate potential DNA inhibition from formalin treatment. Buccal swabs were collected by swabbing the inner cheeks with sterile cotton swabs. Skin and muscle tissues were excised from the inguinal region at the intended site of embalming fluid injection. Blood samples were collected from arteries accessible via the same excision site and blotted onto two separate QIAcard® FTA® microcards (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). We attempted to collect other biological fluids, such as urine, that may contain shed epithelial cells and leukocytes in cases of infection. However, urine and semen samples were unavailable, and the family did not permit the collection of vitreous fluid and bone samples.

Biological samples were processed according to standard laboratory protocols [11]. Samples were securely sealed in separate sterile packages bearing signatures, and sampling was documented with photographs and chain-of-custody forms to ensure integrity and traceability.

Sample preparation and DNA extraction

Before DNA extraction, skin and muscle samples were washed three times with sterile saline solution (0.9% NaCl) for 5 min each to reduce blood contamination. DNA was extracted using the QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions [12]. Blood discs from FTA® cards were purified with three washes of FTA reagent and two washes of TE−4 buffer (10 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EDTA).

DNA amplification and analysis

The concentration and quality of DNA extracts from buccal swabs, skin, and muscle tissues were determined using the PowerQuant® System (Promega, WI, USA) on an AB® 7500 Real-Time quantitative PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and analyzed using AB® HID Real-time PCR analysis software v.1.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions [13].

DNA was amplified using the PowerPlex® 21 System (Promega) in an AB® GeneAmp® PCR System 9700 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 0.5 ng DNA template in a 25 μL PCR reaction volume. For the blood on FTA® sample, a single purified 1.2 mm diameter disc was used for amplification. DNA fragment analysis was conducted on an AB® 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed using GeneMapper® ID-X software version 1.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer's instructions [14].

A genetic comparison between the deceased alleged father and the child was conducted after generating a concordant autosomal STR (aSTR) profile from the four genotyped samples. Paternity was evaluated by calculating the Cumulative Likelihood Ratio (CLR) in accordance with the recommendations of the International Society for Forensic Genetics (ISFG) [15], the Philippine aSTR allele frequency database [16,17] and the parentage testing equations in [18]. A 50% prior probability was used to calculate the Probability of Paternity.

Results and discussion

The Philippine Clinical Practice Guidelines [19] recommend administering pRBC or leukoreduced blood, which are nuclei-reduced RBC concentrates in 150-200 ml bags, to patients experiencing acute blood loss (40% blood volume loss). The pRBC units are prepared by gradient density, allowing blood components to separate by gravity overnight in a refrigerator (+2 to +6 °C) or by centrifugation at low temperatures, followed by plasma removal [19]. This process does not ensure the complete removal of nucleated cells from this blood component, which can still contain approximately 109 leukocytes per unit [20]. Similarly, leukoreduction does not completely eliminate leukocytes from the blood after filtration. The accepted leukoreduction standard in the USA and UK [20,21], also adopted by Australia and many Asian countries, is a residual leukocyte count of ≤5 × 106 per unit. In contrast, the European Union applies a stricter threshold of ≤1 × 106 [22]. However, actual practices may vary among local centers that process blood components.

In an earlier report for immunocompetent recipients [23], transient increases in circulating donor leukocytes may be observed within the first few days, particularly around days 3–5 post-transfusion, and generally diminish over the following week, with detection declining by days 7–14 as donor cells are cleared from circulation. In severe trauma or extensive transfusion, donor leukocyte subpopulations may persist as TA-MC for months or even years; however, this outcome is uncommon and highly case-dependent. In a recent report, 2.5 × 105 residual leukocytes were isolated within 42 days of storage, and up to 48 hematopoietic progenitor cells/unit were detected during transfusion [24], indicating that blood products are not completely devoid of functional leukocytes and that certain progenitor cells can persist during storage. Cellular breakdown of nucleated cells may also occur during blood donation, blood component fractionation, or storage before transfusion [8,10]. Additionally, donor-derived DNA in partially leukoreduced blood components may interfere with the genotyping of immunocompromised recipients [8]. Moreover, the detailed mechanism of extracellular DNA clearance following blood transfusion in the recipient's bloodstream remains unclear [8], and the number of viable progenitor cells leading to TA-MC has yet to be established [24,25]. Hence, the deceased in this case may have had extracellular donor DNA despite receiving nuclei-reduced blood cells, and precautionary steps are still needed for aSTR typing.

A legally required paternity test comparing the deceased's DNA profile with that of a biological child yielded a CLR exceeding 381,000 and a Probability of Paternity of 99.999%, confirming the deceased's paternity and further reinforcing the accuracy of the single DNA profile obtained.

Contrary to previous reports that detected mixed stochastic genotypes after multiple perimortem blood transfusions [9,10], we found no evidence of donor-derived extracellular DNA in the recipient genotype. Unlike these cases involving large-volume transfusions of 6 and 23 pRBC units within 24 hours perimortem, the deceased received multiple transfusions from different donors over eight months. However, it remains unclear whether the patient received pRBC, leukoreduced blood, or both, relying solely on the deceased children's verbal accounts.

In a simulated contamination study [7], standard forensic protocols require at least 5-10 white blood cells (WBC) per μL of RBC concentrates to detect donor alleles. Generally, donor leukocytes are more prevalent in non-leukoreduced units, recent massive pRBC transfusions with residual buffy coat, recipients with severe leukopenia, and single-donor transfusions, thereby increasing the donor fraction in the recipient's blood [23,26]. Thus, the timing of blood sample collection relative to transfusions depends on multiple factors, making an optimal collection time difficult to establish, such as transfusion-related variables (e.g., volume, frequency, leukoreduction status), recipient-related factors (e.g., leukocyte count, host clearance), and analytical sensitivity of the genotyping assay. This could impact routine STR typing using PCR-CE-based systems and more sensitive PCR assays, such as NGS and digital PCR. It is therefore important to consider a person's medical history when performing DNA testing. While blood is an excellent source of DNA due to its quantity and integrity, other biological samples from the deceased should be considered depending on the medical history and postmortem conditions. Moreover, it is crucial to implement stringent protocols for sample collection, preservation, and analysis to minimize contamination risks, reduce the need for additional purification procedures, and ensure accurate genotyping.

Conclusion

Despite repeated nuclei-reduced transfusions, genotyping of the deceased's blood showed no detectable secondary aSTR profile, yielding a single concordant male profile across 20 aSTR markers in the analyzed samples. The high CLR value, which confirms paternity, further supports the reliability of DNA typing results. As nuclei-reduced RBC concentrates contain minimal leukocytes, donor DNA, if present, was below the detection threshold of CE-based STR analysis, although exceptions may occur with recent massive pRBC transfusions, administration of non-leukoreduced blood, or in recipients with low leukocyte counts. More sensitive assays, such as NGS or digital PCR, may reveal donor alleles. Therefore, medical history should be reviewed before DNA testing, and additional samples, such as buccal swabs, skin, muscle, bone, or body fluids, should be collected, preferably before embalming or burial in deceased cases, to maximize genotyping success.
