Case Report: Multimodal imaging, custom 3D-printed reconstruction and surgical stabilization of severe congenital occipitoatlantoaxial malformation in an adult dog
Authors: Karina M. Pinal, Mattisen L. DiRubio, Natalia Zidan, Samantha Loeber, Starr Cameron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Occipitoatlantoaxial malformations can vary in severity and clinical signs. A 1.8-year-old male intact Goldendoodle was presented with an acute onset of tetraparesis and severe neck pain after a fall. Neurologic examination revealed non-ambulatory tetraparesis with notable pain upon palpation of the cervical spine. Advanced imaging showed right-sided spinal cord compression at the level of C1 with an abnormal angle of the cranial cervical spine, severe congenital malformations including right atlantooccipital fusion, C1 and C2 hemivertebrae with variable hypo- and aplasia, and atlantalization of the axis (C2). An occipitoatlantoaxial stabilization was performed by a combination of intra-operative imaging with a 3-dimensional C-arm and 3-dimensional printed reconstruction of the occiput to C4 of the patient. The patient improved neurologically following surgery. This is the first documented case of an occipitoatlantoaxial malformation that was surgically stabilized using intraoperative 3-D imaging and custom 3-dimensional printed approach.

Introduction

Occipitoatlantoaxial malformations (OAAM) have been previously documented in dogs and can vary in the degree of hypoplastic or aplastic structures (1). These malformations are characterized by a developmental defect involving the occipital bones, C1, and C2 and have been previously described most in horses, but can occur rarely in dogs, with structures varying in being present or absent (Table 1) (2–6). Most often, OAAMs present as occipitoatlantal fusion, cervical vertebrae fusion, hypoplasia or aplasia of the atlas and dens, malformation of the axis, and subsequent alteration of the atlantoaxial joint (4, 7).

Incomplete ossification and/or abnormal development of the atlas can predispose these patients to have atlantoaxial subluxation and varying neurologic deficits (8). Clinical signs associated with OAAMs are attributable to spinal cord and brainstem compression from the malformed vertebrae or atlantoaxial subluxation (4). Most dogs develop clinical signs within the first 2 years of life, and a variety of purebred dogs have been described (4, 7). A few case reports have described surgical decompression and stabilization of OAAMs in dogs using a similar technique to atlantoaxial stabilization, with one case using 2 cortical bone screws (3) and another using a 3D printed implant (5). This report describes a severe case of OAAM resulting in severe aplasia of the atlas, use of a 3D-printed anatomical model and 3D C-arm imaging to guide placement of hardware for occipitoatlantoaxial stabilization within the operating room, and a novel stabilization technique.

Case report

A 1.8-year-old, 19.5-kg, male intact, Goldendoodle presented to the emergency service at the University of Wisconsin-Madison Veterinary Care (UWVC) hospital for an acute onset of tetraparesis after falling while trying to get into a car. Initially, he was able to rise but began vocalizing at home. He then became hyporexic with a notable head turn to the right and progressed to becoming non-ambulatory over 24 h. After presenting to the emergency service, the patient was promptly transferred to the neurology service. On initial examination, the patient was non-ambulatory tetraparetic (Modified Frankel Score of 3) with a right head turn and pain upon palpation of the cervical spine. Proprioception was absent in all four limbs with reduced withdrawal reflexes in the thoracic limbs. All other components of the neurologic exam were unremarkable. Complete bloodwork was performed and within normal limits. Thoracic radiographs revealed T4-5 spinous fusion but were otherwise normal. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; 3-Tesla GE SIGNA Premier, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) of the cervical spine was performed under general anesthesia. MRI sequences included multiplanar T2-weighted, myelo HASTE, STIR, zero echo time (ZTE), pre- and post-contrast T1-weighted, and PD FLEX images. Contrast enhanced MRI sequences acquired after administration of 0.1 mmol/kg gadobenate dimeglumine 529 mg/mL [Multihance (R), Bracco Diagnostics, Monroe Twp., NJ, USA] IV. Computed tomography (GE Discovery RT 16 slice, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) of the cervical spine in medium and high frequency algorithms (120 kVp, 200 mAs) was performed immediately following MRI for further assessment of osseous structures. MRI and CT revealed that the right transverse process of C1 was small and fused to the occipital condyle. C1 was incomplete with a left transverse process and small rounded bone fragments representing an unfused dorsal and ventral arch, right pedicle/lamina and intercentrum. The odontoid process of C2 was severely blunted with several small round mineral fragments at this level and thickening of the apical ligament of the dens. C2 was misshapen and incompletely fused on midline with an absent right pedicle, elongated right transverse process, and large transverse foramen typical of C1. The right dorsal lamina of C2 articulated with the articular process of C3. There was abnormal alignment of C1-C2 with mild dorsal angulation of C2 relative to C1 (C1-C2 overlap of 5.4 mm, C1-C2 angle of 165°), moderate to severe spinal cord compression (2.9 mm diameter at its narrowest location, approximately 42% compression), and narrowing of the vertebral canal at this level (4.5 mm diameter at its narrowest location). The spinal cord at C1-C2 had intramedullary T2w hyperintensity and focal central canal dilation. Regional cervical musculature was strongly T2w and STIR hyperintense and contrast enhancing. MRI and CT diagnostic interpretation was a severe congenital OAA and C3 malformation characterized by right atlantooccipital fusion, C1 and C2 hemivertebrae with variable hypoplasia and aplasia, hypoplastic dens with fragmentation, atlantalization of the axis, and partial fusion of C2-C3. There was associated atlantoaxial subluxation with rotatory instability, focal scoliosis, C1-C2 vertebral canal stenosis, moderate to severe extradural C1-C2 spinal cord compression, and focal spinal cord edema, gliosis, or myelitis (Figures 1, 2). Cisternal and lumbar cerebrospinal fluid taps were performed, and only the lumbar revealed very mild albuminocytological dissociation (total protein: 51 mg/dL). Infectious disease testing (Blastomyces Urine Antigen, Neospora titers, Toxoplasma titers, Cryptococcus latex agglutination antigen) was negative. He was diagnosed with acute instability and right-sided spinal cord compression due to his OAAMs. The patient remained hospitalized and was treated with anti-inflammatory corticosteroids (prednisone, 1.02 mg/kg/day PO) and a multimodal analgesic regimen. Pain management included pregabalin (3.8 mg/kg PO, BID), a ketamine infusion (initially 4 mcg/kg/min and gradually tapered), and a fentanyl infusion (initially 4 mcg/kg/h and gradually tapered). Over the hospital course, the patient showed progressive neurologic improvement; by day 5 post-presentation, he was markedly improved in his tetraparesis and was nearly ambulatory without assistance on discharge day (Modified Frankel Score of 4). Additionally, thoracic limb withdrawal reflexes were normal, the right-sided head turn was mild and had improved, and patient was comfortable on cervical palpation. For several logistical reasons, surgery was postponed for several weeks.

Upon presentation for surgery, the patient was ambulatory tetraparetic with proprioceptive ataxia and continued to have a mild right head turn (Modified Frankel Score of 4). Proprioception was absent in the pelvic limbs, delayed in the right thoracic limb, and intact in the left thoracic limb. He was non-painful upon palpation, and the remainder of his neurologic exam was unremarkable. Prior to surgery, a 3D-printed anatomical model of the occiput to C4 was made to aid in the understanding of his complex anatomical malformations (Figure 3). The patient was routinely anesthetized with dexmedetomidine (10 mcg, 0.5 mcg/kg) and fentanyl (85 mcg, 4.5 mcg/kg) IV followed by 80 mg (4.3 mg/kg) of propofol IV and isoflurane pre-operatively. The patient was clipped and sterilely prepped for surgery in dorsal recumbency. Intraoperatively, the patient was maintained under anesthesia with sevoflurane and placed on fentanyl and ketamine infusions for pain management. Cefazolin (420 mg, 22.3 mg/kg) was given IV every 90 min. Decompression of the previously mentioned C1-C2 spinal cord compression was not performed due to improvement in his neurologic status with medical management and priority for stabilization under anesthesia. It was considered, if the patient had residual deficits after stabilization, then reimaging and decompression would have been considered. A standard ventral atlantoaxial stabilization approach was performed until approaching the occipitalatlantoaxial joint, where several layers of thick, fibrous connective tissue and fascia were encountered. Within this fibrous connective tissue were several small pieces of bone, which were suspected to be remnants of C1. One of these remnants is visible on the 3D printed anatomical model. These pieces were removed to prevent further compression. The 3D-printed anatomical model was placed in a sterile sleeve and used to confirm the joint spaces, anatomical bone prominences and specific locations intraoperatively. A multi-implant with polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement method was used to stabilize the occipitalatlantoaxial joint. The occipital condyles were freed of residual soft tissue, and a 2.0 mm pin was placed transarticularly from C2 into the right and left occipital condyles. A 2.7 × 16 mm screw was placed in the C2 ventral process, a 3.5 × 16 mm screw was placed in the left occipital condyle, a 2.7 × 14 mm screw was placed in the middle of the right occipital condyle/remnant of C1, and a 3.5 × 10 mm screw was placed in the lamina on the left side of C2. For all implants, approximately 3–4 mm was left exposed for incorporation into the PMMA. The anatomy of the patient was difficult when navigating placement of the pins and screws, as there was limited bone that provided adequate purchase. Reference to the 3D printed anatomical model was crucial to identify the small, unique structures such as bifurcated articular surface of C2. The right wing of C1 in this patient was severely reduced in size, limiting palpation and bone purchase. Additionally, due to the lack of a true alar notch on the left of C1, the left transarticular pin was difficult to place. There was a lack of initial purchase of the left condyle, which warranted the removal of the pin and subsequent placement of a 3.5 × 16 mm screw within the hole. A smaller pin was then placed transarticularly on the left, more lateral to the original pin placement. The awareness of underlying vasculature was also difficult to determine and mild hemorrhage was encountered when drilling a hole over the lamina of C2 on the left side. A 2.7 × 12 mm screw was placed and hemorrhage was controlled with this placement. No additional hemorrhage was noted following this. Three-dimensional (3D) fluoroscopy (GE OEC 3D, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) utilizing a cone beam CT (CBCT; 61 kVp, 1.3 mAs) system integrated into a mobile C-arm for multiplanar 3D imaging capabilities was performed intra-operatively for real time surgical planning and assessment of pin and screw placement (Figure 4). After fixation, C1-C2 overlap increased to 7.4 mm and C1-C2 angle increased to 179°, with more parallel orientation of dorsal lamina of C1-C2. The vertebral canal diameter at its narrowest point mildly increased to 5.5 mm. Then, PMMA with added cefazolin was used to cover the implants completely. The muscles, subcutaneous, and skin layers were closed routinely. Post-operative cervical radiograph confirmed final implant placement (Figure 5). The patient recovered uneventfully from anesthesia. After 24 h of IV pain medication (Fentanyl 3–5 mcg/kg/h and Ketamine 3–6 mcg/kg/min), the patient was transitioned to oral pain medications (pregabalin 75 mg PO BID and carprofen 37.5 mg PO BID) and was discharged 3 days after surgery. At time of discharge, he was ambulatory tetraparetic with a similar proprioceptive ataxia (Modified Frankel Score of 4). The paresis was worst in the left pelvic limb. Proprioception remained absent in the left pelvic limb but was present in the remaining limbs. One month after surgery, neurologic exam revealed that he was ambulatory tetraparetic with a moderate proprioceptive ataxia (Modified Frankel Score of 4). He was spastic in all four limbs and had a mild right head tilt. Proprioception was delayed in the left pelvic limb, but intact in the remaining limbs. The remainder of his neurologic exam was normal. At the 3-month post-operative re-examination, the patient’s neurologic exam was improved in that he remained only mildly tetraparetic with a proprioceptive ataxia. The patient had intact proprioception in the thoracic limbs, and a mild delay in proprioception in the pelvic limbs when assessed with paw replacement. He remained spastic in all four limbs when walking, and continued to have a mild right head tilt. He remained non-painful upon palpation (Modified Frankel Score of 4). Radiographs were performed at this visit, which showed stable implants and static cervical alignment. A recheck examination performed about 9 months post-op yielded that the patient remained ambulatory tetraparetic with a spastic gait (Modified Frankel Score of 4). Proprioception was intact in all four limbs. He continued to have a slight right head tilt. Radiographs performed yielded similarly stable implants and similar alignment to immediate post-op radiographs.

Discussion

This is the first report of an OAAM with stabilization utilizing pre-operative and intraoperative imaging techniques, including 3D C-arm imaging in the operating room and a 3D-printed anatomical modeling to aid in anatomical location and placement of hardware, as well as novel surgical technique. Although OAAM are rare in dogs, they have been documented in small and large breeds (4). To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in the veterinary literature involving a large mixed-breed dog. Previous reports have identified OAAM in a Saint Bernard, Cavalier King Charles spaniel, German shepherd dog, Jack Russell terrier, French bulldog, Newfoundland, and a Shiba Inu, Cocker Spaniel, and a Border Collie (4–6). These malformations are caused by abnormal embryologic development resulting in shifting the atlantoaxial junction to the atlantooccipital junction (7). OAAMs occur most commonly in horses in veterinary medicine, with Arabian horses being most frequently affected. In Arabian horses, there is a presumed autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance (9) and one gene has been identified (HOXD3) to be associated with consequential OAAM (10). There may be a similar genetic mode of inheritance for OAAM in dogs that has not yet been identified. Suspicion for OAAM should remain for a young dog with acute onset of painful tetraparesis, even as a mixed breed dog.

Moreover, this patient had severe cervical malformations, including a markedly hypoplastic C1 with only small, bony remnants of the neural arch present. Therefore, it was not possible to incorporate C1 into the construct for stabilization and the occipital condyles were subsequently used, which has not been described in the literature to the authors’ knowledge. The patient also had fusion of the C2–C3 vertebrae, and partial fusion at the C3–C4 vertebrae. Fusion of the occipitoatlantal joint has been documented previously in dogs with atlas hypoplasia (5); however, none to this severity.

Surgical intervention is recommended for dogs with OAAM showing neurologic signs, although such procedures are infrequently reported in the literature (4). Prior to publication of two reports of surgical stabilization of OAAMs (3, 11), it was unknown whether patients with these malformations could have a good prognosis or have success with surgical stabilization (4). Previous reports used a combination of imaging modalities prior to surgical stabilization; however, none have described the use intraoperatively (3, 5). Without these techniques, navigating this patient’s complex anatomy would have been much more challenging and could have increased the risk of complications. The 3D-printed anatomical model, placed in a sterile sleeve, was essential for accurately identifying the C1-C2 joint and other individual anatomical landmarks, guiding the precise placement of hardware. The use of the 3D-printed anatomical model has been used in other surgical procedures for assistance in localizing anatomy and surgical planning (12–14), but has not previously been documented for use in OAAM stabilization surgeries. The 3D models help increase patient dependent knowledge and confidence, as well as intraoperative accuracy. The most common uses of 3D models are to act as personalized anatomical models, integration with surgical instruments, custom-made implants, or tissue scaffolds (14). The 3D-printed anatomical model for this patient served as a 1:1 personalized anatomical model to aid in the complex stabilization. The model was studied by the surgeons prior to the surgery and was briefly used to educate the client in the procedure, which a previously documented benefit of 3D-printed anatomical models (12). The use of the intraoperative 3D C-arm was pivotal in guidance of placement of the hardware necessarily to build the construct. The utility of checking the placement of pins/screws and making adjustments within minutes is unprecedented, especially when faced with such unusual anatomy. The speed of image acquisition and performing this within the operating room is a time-saving measure, that also proved beneficial in surgery in providing efficiency. In this case, the combination of the pre-operative imaging, intraoperative imaging, and 3D-printed anatomical model, greatly increased the accuracy, efficiency, and confidence for placement of stable hardware.

The limitations of the case mostly represent the logistics behind the initial inability of surgical stabilization and decompression at first presentation. Expired PMMA bone cement was the main limiting factor that caused the patient to have to delay the surgery. The risk of using expired PMMA is that the bone cement may not be safe or will not be effective (15). Given these risks, it was elected to perform the surgery until new PMMA was in stock. During the weeks that the patient was receiving medical management leading up to the surgery, we acknowledge that that patient could have dramatically worsened without surgical decompression or stabilization. Thankfully, the patient continued to improve at home and no additional neurologic worsening was noted. Additionally, a limitation of the case is that repeat advanced imaging, such as MRI or CT, was not performed following the initial presentation. This presumptively limits documenting the resolution of the compression of the spinal cord and of the previously noted spinal cord edema, gliosis or myelitis. Given that the patient continued to improve with both medical management and surgical stabilization, we can assume the there was likely edema or myelitis that improved.

The patient experienced a favorable outcome and continues to demonstrate progressive neurologic improvement at the time of writing (10 months post-op). His neurologic signs continued to improve with continued management and have been described using the Modified Frankel Scoring (16, 17). This case serves as a model for future OAAM stabilization procedures utilizing modern 3D methods and novel surgical technique to help increase patient prognosis.
