Polygenic risk for Alzheimer’s disease in healthy aging: age-related and APOE-driven effects on brain structures and cognition
Authors: Hao-Jie Chen, Xinyi Dong, Yichen Wang, Kexin Wang, Guozheng Feng, Tianyu Bai, Mingkai Zhang, Kaiyu Gan, Jun-Jie Peng, Weijie Huang, Zhanjun Zhang, Ni Shu, Guolin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration and cognitive decline with age. The genetic architecture of AD involves multiple loci, including the apolipoprotein E gene (APOE). The polygenic risk scores for AD (AD-PRS) provide a comprehensive genome-wide assessment of AD risk, yet their age-related effects on brain structures and cognitive function in cognitively unimpaired individuals remain largely undefined.MethodsWe analyzed cognitively unimpaired, genetically unrelated Caucasians from the UK Biobank (N = 21,236, 64.5 ± 7.6 years). AD-PRS was derived using a Bayesian approach incorporating approximately 5 million genetic variants (UK Biobank’s standard PRS). Brain structures were measured with regional gray matter (GM) volumes and tract-wise microstructural white matter (WM) integrity. Cognitive performance was evaluated with executive function, visuospatial function, reasoning, and memory. Sliding window analyses were performed to investigate age-related polygenic effects on brain structures, and mediation analyses tested whether structural changes mediated the gene–cognition relationship across different age groups. Analyses were replicated using two custom PRSs—one including APOE and the other excluding APOE regions—calculated with the clumping-and-thresholding approach.ResultsHigh AD-PRS was associated with accelerated GM atrophy (particularly in the hippocampus, thalamus, and parahippocampus), increased cerebral ventricular volume, and reduced WM integrity (especially in the fornix, cingulum, and superior fronto-occipital fasciculus). These polygenic effects demonstrated significant age-related amplification (pBonf < 0.05), with the strongest effects in individuals aged ≥ 75. Elevated AD-PRS was linked to lower cognitive performance across aging, especially in executive function, reasoning, and memory, which were significantly mediated by structural brain changes in subcortical and posterior limbic regions and their WM connections, predominantly in late aging (p < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings, emphasizing the dominant contribution of APOE, while also identifying age-specific effects from non-APOE variants.ConclusionsHigh polygenic risk for AD may be associated with accelerated cognitive decline in healthy aging, mediated by structural changes within hippocampal-thalamic regions and their connecting WM tracts. We provide insights into the early pathogenesis of AD and support the potential for age-targeted screening and early intervention for individuals at high genetic risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-025-01548-z.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia [1], marked by accelerated declines in episodic memory and executive function in its early stage [2]. The progressive nature of AD involves various pathophysiological processes, including amyloid-beta (Aβ) accumulation, tau propagation, and neurodegeneration, which may occur decades before the onset of dementia symptoms [3]. Previous studies have suggested the neurodegenerative changes in the early stage of AD, such as cortical thinning and atrophy in the medial temporal lobe [4], and reduced microstructural integrity of white matter (WM) tracts in the fornix and cingulum [5]. The neurodegenerative etiology of AD is influenced by multiple genes [6], as identified with genome-wide association studies (GWASs), among which the apolipoprotein E gene ε4 allele (APOE ε4) demonstrates consistent prominence [7, 8].

Beyond single risk variants, AD-GWASs have enabled the assessment of genome-wide risk through the polygenic risk score for AD (AD-PRS), which aggregates genetic risks across millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [9]. The AD-PRS has been associated with neuropathological burden [10–13], accelerated neurodegeneration [14–16], and cognitive decline [12, 14, 15] along the continuum of AD. Investigating the polygenic effects in the preclinical stage—particularly among those without cognitive symptoms—is crucial for understanding early AD pathogenesis and developing targeted interventions to delay cognitive decline and potentially prevent the onset of dementia [17].

While the preclinical effects of AD-PRS on brain structures have been realized, the temporal and spatial progression of these effects across the aging process remains poorly understood. Large-scale imaging-genetic datasets, such as the UK Biobank (UKB) [18], have facilitated research aimed at understanding the polygenic nature of AD across the aging process. Recent investigations based on the UKB cohort revealed region-specific differences in gray matter (GM) volumes between individuals with high and low AD-PRS, with these distinctions becoming notable around age 50 (N = 45,616) [16]. However, the specific regions affected and the extent to which APOE contributes to these effects remain debated. For instance, while the hippocampus has been implicated as sensitive to genetic risk for AD [14–16, 19, 20], a recent study reported no significant between-group differences in hippocampal volumes across APOE genotypes (N = 28,494) [21]. Furthermore, tract-specific WM microstructure has received less attention, and it remains unclear whether structural changes in the preclinical stage mediate the association between AD-PRS and cognitive decline.

To address these gaps, we analyzed data from 21,236 cognitively intact and genetically unrelated aging individuals in the UKB. We integrated multiple polygenic risk scores, multimodal neuroimaging markers, and cognitive assessments to investigate the age-related interplay among the AD-PRS, neurodegeneration, and cognition. Specifically, we aimed to (i) quantify age-related effects of AD-PRS on regional brain structures, measured through regional GM volumes and tract-wise microstructural WM integrity; and (ii) examine whether brain structural changes mediate the relationship between AD-PRS and cognitive performance across age. Our findings provide evidence that individuals with high polygenic risk for AD exhibit accelerated neurodegeneration, which contributes to cognitive decline even in the absence of clinical symptoms.

Methods

Participants

We initially considered 56,013 participants who attended the UK Biobank’s imaging visit between August 2013 and July 2022, with written informed consent. The UK Biobank offers comprehensive data for 500,000 participants aged 40–69 recruited between 2006 and 2010, including lifestyle information, physical measures, neuropsychological tests, brain MRI scans, and biological samples [22]. Exclusions were made for missing genetic information, residence outside of England, diagnosis of organic brain syndrome or dementia, unavailable imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs), and incomplete demographic information. After exclusions, 29,645 cognitively intact aging participants were categorized into genetically unrelated Caucasian (UKB-UC, N = 21,236, for the main analysis), related Caucasian (UKB-RC, N = 4487), and non-Caucasian (UKB-MIX, N = 3922) subgroups based on the genetic relatedness (data field 22020) and the genetic ethnic background (data field 22006) (see Additional file 1: Fig. S1 for flow chart). Precise participant age was calculated from the brain imaging scan date and birth date. Further details regarding variables included are provided in Additional file 2: Table S1 and Additional file 3: Method S1.

Genotyping and AD-PRS

Genotyping was conducted using a custom Axiom array, with data available for 55,850 participants. Detailed procedures for genotyping, imputation, and quality control (QC) have been reported previously [22]. The APOE polymorphism was assessed with rs7412 and rs429358. We utilized three metrics of AD-PRS: (i) the standard PRS for AD provided by UKB in May 2022 (PRS-UKB), calculated through a Bayesian approach integrating approximately 5 million SNPs genome-wide including the APOE region [23]; (ii) a custom PRS including the APOE region (PRS-APin); and (iii) a custom PRS excluding the APOE region (PRS-APex). For descriptive analyses, UKB-UC participants were grouped into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk tertiles based on continuous PRS scores. PRS-UKB was used as the primary polygenic metric for the main analyses.

PRS-APin and PRS-APex were calculated using imputed genome-wide genotype data provided by UK Biobank. Beyond the standard UKB QC pipeline, we excluded individuals with sex mismatches, genotype missingness > 5%, heterozygosity outliers, or extensive relatedness (more than ten third-degree relatives), yielding a sample of 54,387. SNP-level QC with PLINK [24] involved filtering variants with genotyping rates < 95%, minor allele frequency < 0.1%, or deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p < 1 × 10−10), leaving 12,215,199 SNPs for PRS calculation. Next, PRS-APin and PRS-APex were derived using a conventional clumping-and-thresholding (C + T) method, selecting the most significant SNP per 250-kb LD block at r2 > 0.1. The GWAS summary statistics used for PRS-APin and PRS-APex were based on Wightman et al. [25]; to ensure independence between source and target datasets, we used the summary statistics that excluded UK Biobank and 23andMe samples (see Data availability). Individual PRSs were calculated by summing clumped SNPs weighted by their reported effect sizes. After testing eight p value thresholds [15] (p ≤ 5 × 10−8, 1 × 10−6, 5 × 10−6, 1 × 10−5, 5 × 10−5, 0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01), we selected p ≤ 1 × 10−5 for both PRS-APin and PRS-APex, as this threshold gave the strongest correlation between PRS-APin and PRS-UKB (Pearson’s r = 0.73, p < 1 × 10−20, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). For PRS-APex, SNPs within the APOE locus (chr19: 44.4–46.5 Mb [15, 26–28]) were excluded.

Brain MRI phenotypes

The multi-contrast brain imaging data utilized in this study were obtained with Siemens Skyra 3 T scanners employing the standard Siemens 32-channel head coil. The image acquisition process, automated processing pipeline, and IDP generation are described previously [29]. Briefly, GM, WM, and ventricular cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were segmented with FAST tissue-segmentation [30] based on the preprocessed T1WI (T1-weighted images, acquired through a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence with 1 × 1 × 1 mm resolution). A total of 96 regional GM IDPs were then extracted within the GM component mask based on the Harvard–Oxford atlases, while 14 subcortical GM IDPs were generated with the FIRST program [31]. The WM lesion volumes, calculated as white matter hyperintensity volumes (WMHVs), were identified utilizing the BIANCA program with T2-weighted images (fluid attenuated inversion recovery sequence with 1.05 × 1 × 1 mm resolution) [32]. The microstructural measures of the integrity of WM tracts were obtained from diffusion scans (echo-planar imaging sequence, 50 × b = 1000s/mm2 and 50 × b = 2000s/mm2). The data from the b = 1000 shell were analyzed using DTIFIT to derive tensor-based measures, encompassing fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), and mode of anisotropy (MO) [33]. Meanwhile, the data from the two-shell acquisition were used to generate measures of neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) [34], including intracellular volume fraction (ICVF), isotropic or free water volume fraction (ISOVF), and orientation dispersion index (OD). To obtain tract-wise WM IDPs, WM tract FA-skeleton analysis was performed utilizing the TBSS program [35] with parcellation defined by 48 standard-space tract masks [36]. We focused on IDPs related to regional GM volume, WMHV, and tract-wise measures of WM microstructure, which served as measures of brain structures. All volumetric IDPs were normalized via multiplication with the volumetric scaling factor from individual native T1 space to standard MNI152 space [37].

Neuropsychological testing and cognitive factors

We considered nine tests with robust test–retest reliability from the UKB cognitive test batteries [38], including the reaction time test (data field 20023), numeric memory test (data field 4282), fluid intelligence (data field 20016), two trail-making tests (TMT-A: data field 6348; TMT-B: data field 6350), pairs matching test (data field 521), matrix pattern completion test (data field 6373), symbol digit substitution test (data field 23324), and tower-rearrange test (data field 21004). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted with the cfa function from the lavaan package [39] to identify cognitive factors corresponding to the related domains (Additional file 2: Table S2). Executive function (EF), memory, reasoning function (RF), and visuospatial function (VS) factors were extracted with good fits to the data (root mean square error of approximation = 5.81 × 10−3, 90% CI [3.41 × 10−3, 8.31 × 10−3], Tucker–Lewis index = 0.999, χ2(11) = 30.44) (detailed in Additional file 3: Method S2).

Sliding window analyses

We employed model-free sliding window (SW) analyses [40–42] to characterize aging trajectories of phenotypic measures (qualitative-SW analysis) and to capture the continuous and age-dependent nature of the polygenic effects (quantitative-SW analysis). For qualitative-SW analysis, each window contained 10% of participants from each risk tertile and shifted incrementally by 0.5 years along the age distribution. We calculated the median age and mean phenotype value with standard error within each window, followed by smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (width = 10) to enhance visualization.

For quantitative-SW analysis, we used windows of fixed 3-year width, incrementally shifting by 0.1 years along the age distribution. This window size was selected after evaluating the feasibility of alternative widths: narrower windows often failed to meet the minimum sample size requirement of statistical power, whereas broader windows reduced the temporal precision of age-related effects and increased participant overlap between adjacent windows (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). A 3-year width thus provided an optimal balance between statistical power and age-specific resolution, yielding 370 windows across the examined age range (50.90–77.22 years) with an average overlap of 1663 participants between consecutive windows. Within each quantitative window, we computed standardized regression coefficients of AD-PRS (intra-age β, IA-β) predicting phenotypic measures, adjusting for age, sex, education, Townsend deprivation index, hypertension, diabetes, scan date, scanning center, and 40 Genetic principal components. Quantitative windows containing fewer than 921 participants were excluded based on a priori power analysis (power = 0.99; f2 = 0.02; α = 0.05), ensuring reliable detection of small polygenic effects while accounting for covariates [43].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 [44]. Differences in demographic characteristics among AD-PRS tertiles (low, intermediate, high) were assessed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables.

First, we identified IDPs that showed biologically plausible associations (p < 0.05, sensitive IDPs) with AD-PRS among participants aged ≥ 75 years (N = 1663), using multivariate Linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, education, Townsend deprivation index, hypertension, diabetes, scanning date, scanning center, and 40 Genetic principal components. Next, sliding window analyses were performed for the identified sensitive IDPs, including 3 whole-brain volumetric IDPs, 25 regional GM volumetric IDPs, and 81 WM tract-wise microstructural measures. Mann–Kendall trend tests were conducted to evaluate monotonic trends of polygenic effects by age.

To further quantify how polygenic effects varied across aging, generalized additive models (GAMs) with basis complexity = 4 and P-spline smoothing were applied to fit age-related effects [45]. The estimated age of onset (EOA) of polygenic effects was defined as the age at which both the fitted β value and its first derivative simultaneously exceeded zero (or simultaneously fell below zero when Mann–Kendall’s τ was negative). Bonferroni correction was applied across the 109 sensitive IDPs identified, with corrected p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted separately for the 25 regional GM volumetric IDPs and the 81 tract-wise microstructural IDPs [46]. The optimal number of principal components (PCs) was determined by parallel analysis with 10,000 iterations. PC scores were extracted using the principal function from the psych package with promax rotation for 7 GM PCs and 15 WM PCs (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Finally, mediation analyses were performed using the mediation package [47] to examine whether the associations between AD-PRS and cognitive factors were mediated by these PCs. Mediation analyses were conducted within defined age groups (N: < 55 = 2941; 55–60 = 3395; 60–65 = 4166; 65–70 = 4948; 70–75 = 4123; > 75 = 1663) to explore age-related mediating effects. Within each group, average causal mediation effects (ACME) and corresponding confidence intervals were estimated via nonparametric bootstrap with 10,000 simulations, and nominal significance was set at p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to verify the robustness of our findings. To address potential computational bias in subcortical volumes, we replicated analyses using normalized subcortical volumes derived from the Harvard–Oxford subcortical atlas through the FAST segmentation approach. We also repeated the analysis based on volumetric IDPs without normalization. Tract-wise WM analysis was replicated using weighted mean diffusion measures from 27 major white matter tracts obtained via probabilistic tractography [48].

To evaluate the impact of APOE and PRS construction methods, we repeated all analyses using PRS-APin and PRS-APex and tested for interactions between APOE ε4 carrier status and PRS-APex on previously identified sensitive IDPs. To further account for the effect of p value thresholds in the clumping-and-thresholding approach, we examined associations between IDPs and both PRS-APin and PRS-APex across all thresholds [15] (5 × 10−8, 1 × 10−6, 5 × 10−6, 1 × 10−5, 5 × 10−5, 0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01) and within defined age groups (< 55, 55–60, 60–65, 65–70, 70–75, and > 75).

Given that most APOE ε4 carriers fell within the high-risk tertile by PRS-UKB, we further quantified age-related structural differences between high- and low-risk groups using the intra-age relative difference (IA-RD). Specifically, we first calculated the intra-age response ratio (IA-RR) as the sample-size bias-corrected ratio [49] between mean IDP values of lower- and higher-risk groups within each qualitative window. Finally, to assess genetic background bias, we repeated association analyses between AD-PRS and sensitive IDPs separately within the UKB-UC, UKB-RC, and UKB-MIX ethnic subgroups and across the defined age groups, adjusting for the same covariates. We also examined potential pleiotropic effects by testing associations between these sensitive IDPs and PRSs for other traits [23] using multivariate linear models adjusted for the same covariates within age groups.

Age-related polygenic effects on gray matter and cerebral ventricular volumes

Among participants aged 75 years and older, we found significant whole-brain associations between AD-PRS and GM volume (total GM: β = −0.059, p = 0.011; cortical GM: β = −0.062, p = 8.92 × 10−3), and ventricular CSF volume (β = 0.09, p = 1.75 × 10−4). At the regional level, AD-PRS showed significant associations (p < 0.05, Additional file 2: Table S3) with the volume of 7 subcortical and 18 cortical regions, including bilateral occipital poles, parahippocampal gyri, inferior frontal gyri, right angular gyrus, left temporal pole, left paracentral lobule, and right orbitofrontal cortex (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A, B).

Sliding window analyses revealed accelerated GM volume declines in participants with high AD-PRS (Additional file 1: Figs. S6–S8), accompanied by increased ventricular CSF volume (Fig. 1C). These polygenic effects were strongly age-related across MTL and subcortical structures (pbonf < 0.05, Additional file 2: Table S4), typically emerging in the mid-to-late 50 s and becoming pronounced after age 70, with standardized effect sizes reaching approximately −0.1 (Fig. 1A; see Additional file 1: Figs. Additionally, similar aging patterns were observed in subcortical volumes derived from the FAST procedure (Additional file 1: Fig. S14). The relationships between age and IA-β remained evident with FAST subcortical segmentation (p < 10−20, Additional file 1: Fig. S15, Additional file 2: Table S10), as well as for the microstructural diffusion properties of the parahippocampal cingulum and thalamic radiation, calculated using probabilistic tractography (Additional file 2: Table S11).

Our analyses also revealed polygenic effects beyond APOE. PRS-APex showed significant associations with multiple structural measures, particularly in participants aged 70–75 years (Additional file 1: Fig. S18; Additional file 2: Tables S12 and S13). Although no direct associations were found between PRS-APex and cognitive performance (p > 0.05 at a clumping threshold of 1 × 10−5), significant interactions with APOE ε4 status were observed for memory (p = 0.012), visuospatial function (p = 0.024), and reasoning (p = 0.035). These effects varied by age group and PRS threshold, with the most consistent findings centered on the 70–75 year group and extending to diffusion measures at thresholds between 1 × 10−6 and 1 × 10−4 (Fig. 4G; Additional file 2: Table S13). When excluding the APOE region, effect sizes were generally attenuated, for example, in the left posterior parahippocampus (β = − 0.033, p = 0.031 for PRS-APex vs β = − 0.054, p = 4.36 × 10−4 for PRS-APin at a 1 × 10−4 threshold) and in the fornix (β = − 0.031, p = 0.043 for PRS-APex vs β = − 0.036, p = 0.021 for PRS-APin at a 1 × 10−2 threshold).

In evaluating the PRS with IA-RD, we found age-related differences between the low- and high-risk groups, with hippocampal volume showing an estimated IA-RD of 2.2% after age 75, 8% for ventricular CSF volume, and 3.8% for fornix-FA (Fig. 4D and Additional file 2: Table S14). Similar trends were observed between high- and medium-risk groups, but not between medium- and low-risk groups, indicating that polygenic effects were primarily driven by the high-risk tertile.

We found significant associations between the PRS of other phenotypes and IDPs sensitive to AD-PRS (pbonf < 0.05). Particularly, the PRS for ischemic stroke, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension consistently related to multiple tract-wise microstructural WM integrity (Additional file 2: Table S15). Besides, while the primary findings were based on UKB-UC, we observed poor replication of the associations among the UKB-RC (N = 4487; demographics in Additional file 2: Table S16) and the UKB-MIX (N = 3922; demographics in Additional file 2: Table S17) (Fig. 4F, Additional file 2: Table S18).

Discussion

This study examined how genome-wide polygenic risk for Alzheimer’s disease contributes to age-related neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in cognitively intact aging adults. We found that individuals with high AD-PRS exhibited accelerated decline in executive and memory functions, mediated by structural changes in the hippocampal-thalamic system and its associated white matter tracts. These findings shed light on the early pathophysiology of AD and underscore the utility of polygenic risk profiling for identifying individuals at heightened risk in the preclinical stage.

We found that higher AD-PRS was associated with reduced gray matter volume in the hippocampus and medial temporal regions, with degenerative changes emerging earlier in subcortical structures. These findings are consistent with the typical atrophic patterns observed in late-onset AD patients [3], as well as the impacts of AD-PRS on the pathology in hippocampus [11, 14], the entorhinal cortex and frontal lobes [12], and whole-brain [15, 16, 20]. The temporal pattern of genetic-related degeneration was corroborated by a recent UKB study [16], confirming the hypothesized spreading of pathological proteins [3, 50]. In contrast to the well-documented increase in WMHV in clinical AD [51, 52], we found no significant association between AD-PRS and WMHV, even among participants aged 75 + (N = 1663), which is consistent with previous findings [20] but differing from a smaller preclinical study from the CARDIA cohort (N = 331) [53] and a longitudinal study [54]. This suggests that WMHV in healthy aging may be strongly influenced by variants beyond APOE—as supported by findings from non-APOE variants in the CHARGE cohorts [55–57] and a recent cell-type-specific PRS study [58]. However, we did observe accelerated age-related declines in tract-wise WM integrity among individuals with high AD-PRS, with earliest effects emerging in the fornix and cingulum around the late 50s. These findings reinforce the vulnerability of these tracts in AD [5, 15, 21] and highlight the age-dependent nature of polygenic effects on WM integrity.

We uncovered the mediating roles of hippocampal regions, fornix, cingulum, and SFOF structures in the relationship between AD-PRS and cognitive function. Aging studies have found the correlations between AD-PRS and executive function, working memory [14, 19], reasoning function [20], and AD progression [59]. Our findings reinforce the role of fornix, a critical fiber bundle connecting the hippocampi with other brain regions, which has been associated with episodic memory [60] and executive function [61]. In addition, the heightened mediation effects in the middle to late stages of aging call for the consideration of AD-PRS in cognitive prediction models.

Our findings reinforce the prominent role of APOE ε4 in preclinical AD, while also underscoring the contributions of non-APOE genetic variants—particularly in white matter integrity [62, 63]. Risk stratification analyses revealed that age-related differences in brain structure were primarily driven by individuals in the high-risk group, which included the vast majority of APOE ε4 carriers (ε3ε4: 81.6%; ε4ε4: 98.7%). Moreover, most age-related polygenic effects were identified using PRS-UKB and PRS-APin, but not with PRS-APex, consistent with prior evidence that excluding the APOE region substantially reduces the explanatory power of AD-PRS on brain volume and WM microstructure [15, 16, 64]. However, given previous reports linking non-APOE variants to Aβ and tau pathology [10, 12, 65, 66], structural alterations [12, 21, 53], and cognitive function [20, 66, 67], our results also highlight the significant interactions between APOE genotype and PRS-APex. Lastly, we note potential genetic pleiotropy, as the WM tracts sensitive to AD-PRS also showed associations with PRSs for ischemic stroke, cerebrovascular diseases, and other diseases—consistent with emerging evidence of overlapping neuro-genetic architecture between AD, psychiatric conditions [68], and vascular pathology [69, 70].

Our analysis supports the benchmark performance of Bayesian-based PRS compared to traditional clumping-and-thresholding PRS in capturing disease-specific structural and cognitive traits [23]. Nevertheless, PRS approaches are built solely on single-nucleotide polymorphisms and therefore cannot account for genetic variation arising from other sources, such as copy number variations [71]. Unlike a previous study that reported almost no significant PRS-APex effects across the entire aging population [15], our results suggest that the effects-beyond-APOE may be age-dependent. We also observed some counterintuitive associations in which higher PRS-APex was linked to larger regional volumes. This aligns with prior findings suggesting that non-APOE variants may, in certain contexts, play a protective role and contribute to resilience in cognitively normal individuals [53, 72]. While excluding the APOE locus to construct an “APOE-independent” PRS has been a common strategy [15, 26–28], this approach may overlook potential biological interactions between APOE and other genes [67]. Together, these results highlight the importance of simultaneously considering both APOE and non-APOE contributions, as well as age, in future studies.

The sliding-window approach captures nuanced differences, circumventing the pitfalls of significance and statistical power in other models. However, the cross-sectional design limits the understanding of the longitudinal alterations associated with AD-PRS. Future research should employ finer brain delineations, advanced scanning sequences, and state-of-the-art computations to elucidate early pathogenesis [73, 74]. Although the present study benefits from a large sample size, polygenic effects in the earliest stages of aging appear to be very subtle and may require even larger cohorts for robust detection. Moreover, variations in sample size across genetic ancestry groups underscore the need to interpret ethnicity-specific findings with caution [75]. Finally, modifiable factors significantly influence disease development; hence, future studies should examine the interplay between genetics and environmental factors to develop precise intervention strategies for pre-clinical individuals [76].

Conclusions

Overall, this study demonstrates that individuals with high AD-PRS exhibit accelerated brain structural degeneration in healthy aging—particularly in hippocampal, thalamic, and associated WM regions—which contributes to cognitive decline in later life. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings and emphasized the prominent role of APOE, along with the accumulative polygenic influences beyond APOE. These results underscore the value of polygenic risk profiling in elucidating preclinical AD pathogenesis and advancing strategies for early identification and intervention.
