Hierarchical vision transformers for Epstein-Barr virus status and histological subtype prediction in Hodgkin lymphoma whole-slide images
Authors: Zsolt Bedőházi, Zsófia Sztupinszki, Ragnar P. Kristjánsson, Mikkel Werling, Stephen Hamilton-Dutoit, Kristina L. Lauridsen, Lisa Ottander, Trine L. Plesner, Peter Hollander, Ingrid Glimelius, Lene Sjö, Estrid Høgdall, Carsten Utoft Niemann, Klaus Rostgaard, Péter Pollner, Henrik Hjalgrim, István Csabai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate stratification of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) by immunologic/histological subtypes and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status is essential for epidemiological and translational research, yet large-scale testing is impractical and expensive. Digital pathology models that utilize routinely used hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) whole-slide images (WSIs) could close this gap. We developed and validated a hierarchical Vision Transformer pipeline that aggregates cell-, patch-, and region-level context to predict EBV status and the three most prevalent immunological/histological HL subtypes: nodular sclerosis (NS), mixed cellularity (MC), and nodular lymphocyte-predominant HL (NLPHL)—from H&E-stained WSIs, and additionally evaluated a standard attention-based multiple-instance learning (ABMIL) baseline for direct architectural comparison. The development pool comprised 1643 HL cases (1952 WSIs) from 18 Danish hospitals and was used for hospital-preserving 5-fold cross-validation; external validation was performed on an independent hold-out cohort of 458 cases (532 WSIs) from five hold-out hospitals. For subtype prediction, analyses were restricted to the 1560 cases belonging to NS, MC, or NLPHL. On the external EBV cohort (N=458) the hierarchical pipeline achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC–AUC) of 0.73 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.68–0.77), precision–recall (PR)–AUC 0.57 (95% CI 0.49–0.66) with recall (sensitivity) 0.74 (95% CI 0.67–0.80) and macro-F1 score 0.60 (95% CI 0.54, 0.65). For 3-class subtype prediction on 359 external cases, discrimination reached ROC–AUC 0.84 (95% CI 0.80–0.88) and PR–AUC 0.63 (95% CI 0.56–0.71) with a macro-F1 of 0.56 (95% CI 0.48, 0.64) and macro-recall 0.55 (95% CI 0.47, 0.63); residual errors were dominated by NS-MC confusions. An ABMIL baseline using the same patch embeddings achieved ROC–AUC 0.76 (95% CI 0.72–0.81) for EBV and 0.89 (95% CI 0.86–0.92) subtype prediction, outperforming the hierarchical model on both tasks. This multicenter study shows that both hierarchical and attention-based architectures can determine EBV status and major HL subtypes directly from routine H&E slides with externally validated performance across hospitals, whereas the finding that the simpler baseline outperformed the hierarchical model suggests that strong foundation-model embeddings combined with attention-based pooling may reduce the need for explicit multi-scale modelling in cohorts of this size.

Introduction

Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) is a malignancy of the lymphatic system, diagnosed in more than 80,000 individuals and responsible for over 20,000 deaths worldwide each year.1, 2 Although modern treatments can achieve survival rates exceeding 90%,3, 4 access to such care is not universal,5 and in addition, late effects among HL survivors remain a concern.6 At the population level, HL incidence and mortality show opposing associations with socio-economic development: incidence is generally higher and mortality lower in affluent countries compared to low-income settings.1 With ongoing population growth and ageing, global HL incidence and mortality are projected to increase by 30% and 50%, respectively, by 2040.7 Therefore, efforts to better understand the causes of HL and to prevent it remain highly relevant.

Unraveling HL etiology has been complicated by evidence suggesting that HL comprises biologically distinct disease entities.8 Current histopathological classifications recognize two immunologically distinct forms: nodular lymphocyte-predominant HL (NLPHL, ∼5%–10% of cases) and classical HL (cHL).4, 9, 10 The latter is further subdivided into four histological subtypes: nodular sclerosis (NS, 70% of cHL), mixed cellularity (MC, 20%–25% of cHL), lymphocyte depleted (LD), and lymphocyte rich HL (LR).11 Epidemiological differences between cHL subtypes, particularly between NS and MC, have been repeatedly observed,12 but histological categories alone do not fully capture the underlying etiological diversity.

In 30%–40% of cHL cases, malignant cells harbor the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV),13, 14, 15 which is believed to be causative in these instances. EBV-positivity occurs across both NS and MC subtypes. Interestingly, EBV-positivity itself has been shown to associate with socioeconomic factors.16 Because EBV status currently does not influence standard treatment decisions, it is not routinely assessed during diagnostic work-up.15, 17 Consequently, epidemiological knowledge of EBV-positive vs. EBV-negative cHL is largely based on retrospective studies re-examining archived biopsy samples, often limited by small sample sizes and other methodological constraints.14, 15, 18, 19

There is growing interest in whether the tumor microenvironment in HL varies according to EBV status,20 with differences potentially detectable through advanced image analysis. Such variations could be of biological relevance and may influence disease behavior and patient outcomes.17, 21, 22 However, unlike other malignant lymphomas, HL lesions are dominated by a heterogeneous mix of reactive immune cells, with malignant cells comprising only 1%–2% of the tumor mass.23 This atypical biology of HL, including its strong dependence on the tumor microenvironment, unusual sex- and age-specific incidence patterns, and autoimmune-like features, suggests that digital pathology models optimized for other cancers cannot be directly generalized to HL.7, 8, 24, 25, 26

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers, have shown promise in analyzing WSIs in lymphomas, including HL.26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31 These models can detect complex spatial patterns not apparent to human observers. For example, CNNs have been used to predict disease progression in large B-cell lymphomas,32, 33 and Vision Transformers have improved classification and outcome prediction in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.34, 35

A robust, scalable WSI analysis tool capable of classifying large numbers of HL by histological subtype and EBV status would address several critical limitations of current epidemiological practice. First, it would overcome the subjectivity and variability that are inherent in manual histopathological assessment,36, 37, 38 thereby ensuring greater objectivity and reproducibility. Second, automation would enable efficient processing and classification of thousands of digital slides, an impractical task through manual review alone. Third, uniform, large-scale classification of cases would facilitate more comprehensive epidemiological studies of HL subtypes and EBV status, yielding deeper insights into disease etiology and prognosis. Finally, application not only to newly collected samples but also to historical material would maximize the value of both existing biobanks and ongoing clinical collections.

The starting point for this study was a desire to investigate the potential for machine learning image analysis to enable high-throughput epidemiological etiological research. This aim determined the end points of primary interest (EBV status and histology). Importantly, however, if successful, this study could also inform the design of clinically relevant digital pathology tools to aid the distinction between cHL and NLPHL, for which treatment strategies already differ, as well as between EBV-positive and EBV-negative cHL variants, which may become relevant with more modern treatment modalities.

In this study, we evaluated a context-aware Hierarchical Image Pyramid Transformer (HIPT) architecture39 for predicting HL histology and tumor EBV status from WSIs in a uniquely large population-based cohort of 2101 HL patients collected from 23 Danish hospitals.40 Our interest in HIPT was motivated by two considerations. First, many weakly supervised WSI pipelines follow a multiple-instance learning (MIL) paradigm,41 in which patches are encoded independently and aggregated with permutation-invariant pooling; while effective, this bag-of-patches abstraction can overlook spatial relationships and larger-scale tissue context.42, 43 Second, informative signals in WSIs span multiple physical scales, from cellular morphology to broader tissue architecture. HIPT is explicitly designed to model this hierarchy through staged aggregation of local and regional information. We therefore considered HIPT a biologically and computationally well-motivated architecture for HL WSIs, supported by prior HIPT benchmarks showing strong performance across multiple slide-level cancer subtyping and survival prediction tasks.39 At the same time, recent benchmarking of WSI aggregation methods has shown that no single aggregation strategy is consistently optimal across tasks, and that spatially aware aggregation may provide less additional benefit when strong domain-specific pathology foundation-model embeddings are used.44 We therefore treated the added value of hierarchical modelling as an empirical question rather than an assumption. To benchmark this design choice directly, we additionally evaluated a standard attention-based multiple-instance learning (ABMIL) baseline41 using the same pretrained UNI patch embeddings, patient-level splits, and external hold-out cohort, enabling a direct comparison between hierarchical and flat aggregation strategies on the same data.

Our dataset, substantially larger than any previously utilized HL dataset, provides a unique opportunity to characterize HL in greater detail and to develop robust, generalizable prognostic models. The development and validation of this image analysis tool represent the first step toward standardized, high-throughput HL classification, with significant implications for digital pathology, epidemiological research, and ultimately for reducing global inequalities in diagnostic quality and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Data

Cohorts and labels

The present investigation is nested within a nationwide Danish HL project, which has been described in detail elsewhere.40 In brief, the project includes a collection of available primary tissue biopsies from incident HL cases in Denmark in the period 1990 through 2010, which have been EBV-typed retrospectively by means of in-situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNAs (EBERs; INFORM EBER Probe, Benchmark ULTRA platform from Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA) as part of the project. WSI for all patients were scanned at a central pathology lab. The project was initiated by a broad search in the Danish Cancer Registry and the Danish Pathology Registry for potential HL cases incident in 1990–2010. From this list, true HL cases with suitable material for tissue microarrays were harvested. Collected tissues (all from excision biopsies) were re-examined, and histology was revised by one of two Danish pathologists. EBV typing was carried out by viewing digitized pictures by three Danes and replicated by two Swedes. The consensus EBV typing used resulted from combining the results from the two teams, such that if any core was deemed EBER-positive, the case was EBV-positive; if all useable cores were EBER-negative, the case was EBV-negative. Inter-rater agreements (kappa coefficients) were 91.5% overall, 92.5% in MCHL, and 90.0% in NSHL cases. We used all EBV-typed cases from this project, representing 75% of all HL cases in Denmark.

Our study included N=2101 lymphoma cases from 23 Danish hospitals (Bis, Esb, Gen, Glo, Her, Hil, Hjø, Hol, Hols, Hvi, Nyk, Næs, Ode, Ran, Rig, Ros, Sla, Sve, Søn, Vej, Vib, Ålb, and Årh), corresponding to 2484 hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained whole-slide images (WSIs; range per patient: 1–8, median: 1). WSIs from the same patient were grouped to prevent any patient-level leakage. Each case had an institutional label (hospital), EBV status (0/1), and a histological subtype label. Two prediction tasks were investigated: 1) EBV status (binary): all 2101 cases were used. 2) Histological subtype: analyses were restricted to the three most prevalent subtypes—NS, MC, and NLPHL—yielding N=1560 cases (1832 WSIs). For the three-class subtype task, we excluded cases labelled as Unspecified (UNS) and the two rarest cHL subtypes (LR, LD) to avoid unstable estimates.

Hospital-preserving partitioning and cross-validation

To ensure and emulate robust external validation and avoid institutional data leakage, we implemented a hospital-preserving partitioning strategy that assigned entire hospitals to either the development pool or an independent hold-out test cohort.

The selection of hospitals for the external hold-out cohort was performed using a combinatorial optimization approach designed to identify hospital combinations that yield balanced distributions of key clinical variables between the development and hold-out cohorts. For each candidate combination, we computed the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence between the development and hold-out distributions for four balancing variables: EBV status, sex, histological subtype, and smoking status (a HL risk factor).

The total optimization score for each candidate split was computed as the sum of JS divergences across all balancing variables plus a sample-size balance penalty term (weighted by a factor of 2) to discourage extreme imbalances in cohort sizes:Score=∑v∈VJSPvdev∥Pvhold+2∣ρobs−ρexp∣,where V=EBVstatussexhistologicalsubtypesmoking status denotes the set of balancing variables, and Pvdev and Pvhold are the empirical distributions of variable v in the development and hold-out cohorts, respectively. The observed ratio ρobs was defined as the fraction of cases assigned to the hold-out cohort, and the expected ratio ρexp was defined from the proportion of hospitals allocated to the hold-out cohort.

The minimum-score split assigned Glo, Hol, Ode, Ran, and Ålb to the hold-out set (458 cases) and left 1643 cases from 18 hospitals for development, with JS≤3.7×10−3 for all variables, indicating good balance.

The 18 development hospitals were divided into 5 cross-validation (CV) folds through a two-stage optimization: k-means clustering first grouped hospitals according to EBV positivity rate, sex ratio, subtype distribution, and smoking-data completeness, and the resulting assignments were then refined with simulated annealing that minimized the weighted sum of JS divergences across folds.

This hospital-preserving design ensures that model performance estimates from CV and external validation reflect generalization to entirely new clinical sites rather than interpolation within sites already seen during training. The identical hospital partitions were re-used for both tasks; for the subtype task, only the cases belonging to the three retained subtypes were kept within each split.

Image segmentation and patching

To handle WSIs with gigapixel dimensions,43, 45, 46 we used a custom-developed method to: (i) isolate background at low resolution and (ii) extract fixed-size regions for downstream modelling. Tissue localization began by generating a coarse segmentation mask from a downsampled thumbnail (on the order of a few hundred pixels per side), leveraging RGB intensity contrast between tissue and background. The resulting mask was then resized to the target extraction scale for patch extraction.

We adopted a HIPT-style hierarchical representation to retain spatial context across scales (patch → region → slide) while remaining computationally feasible for gigapixel WSIs. To align with the HIPT39 encoder stack, we extracted non-overlapping 4096×4096 pixel regions at 20× (0.45μm/pixel), retaining only regions intersecting the tissue mask. Rather than pre-extracting isolated 256×256 pixel patches and re-grouping them, each 4096×4096 region was deterministically partitioned into a 16×16 grid of 256×256 sub-patches. For a slide with M regions, this yields an array of shape M2562563; after embedding the 256×256 tiles with the patch-level encoder, we obtain M2561024 features that feed directly into the region-level Transformer. Segmentation hyperparameters (thumbnail size, histogram binning, and intensity cutoffs) were tuned by visual inspection. The workflow was optimized for efficient processing of terabyte-scale WSIs by applying lightweight operations on low-resolution data parallelizing execution, making disk I/O the main performance constraint.

We did not apply stain normalization before feature extraction, motivated by recent evidence that sufficiently pretrained foundation models are robust to stain variability with minimal impact on downstream accuracy.47 For downstream weakly supervised learning, all WSIs from a patient were grouped into a patient-level “WSI bag,” preserving region coordinates and linked to the corresponding EBV and subtype labels used to train the slide-level aggregator.

Hierarchical Vision Transformers

Gigapixel histopathology slides exhibit a natural hierarchy of visual patterns across spatial scales. At a fixed magnification, the pixel spacing is constant (approximately 0.5μm per pixel at 20×), so cell morphology, local cell–cell interactions, and broader tissue architecture appear at predictable physical scales. The HIPT39 exploits this structure by decomposing each WSI into nested windows: 16×16 “cell” tiles, 256×256 “patch” windows, and 4096×4096 “region” tiles. The model then aggregates information bottom-up using Transformers at each level, enabling multi-scale reasoning while avoiding the mixing of unrelated magnifications. Notation

Let xWSI denote a WSI. After tissue segmentation and tiling, each slide is represented as a set of M non-overlapping regions rkk=1M of size 4096×4096 pixels. Each region rk is subdivided into a 16×16 grid of 256×256 patches pk,jj=1256. In the original HIPT formulation, each 256×256 patch can be further tokenized into 256 non-overlapping 16×16 tiles, yielding a sequence of 256 tokens that is summarized by a learnable class token (CLS). We adopt the same hierarchical aggregation scheme, but in our implementation the patch-level content is represented by a pretrained foundation model embedding (UNI; see Training section) rather than a RGB ViT256−16.

Original HIPT hierarchy (for context)

HIPT stacks three Transformer modules. At the patch level, ViT256−16 aggregates 16×16 tiles within a 256×256 patch and outputs a patch summary token CLS256j. At the region level, ViT4096−256 aggregates the 256 patch summaries within a 4096×4096 region to produce a region summary token CLS4096k. Finally, a slide-level module ViTWSI−4096 aggregates the M region-level tokens to form a single slide representation for classification. This hierarchy may be written as:CLS256j=ViT256−16x16ii=1256,CLS4096k=ViT4096−256CLS256jj=1256,y^=ViTWSI−4096CLS4096kk=1M,where x16i denotes embedded 16×16 tile tokens within a patch, j indexes patches within a region, and k indexes regions within the WSI (with M regions per slide). Here, y^ denotes slide-level logits. Notably, both the patch- and region-level Transformers operate on fixed-length sequences of 256 tokens, keeping attention complexity bounded at those stages.

Implementation in this study (UNI → region-level ViT → slide-level ViT)

In our pipeline, the patch-level encoder is replaced by UNI,48 which maps each 256×256 patch to a 1024-dimensional embedding ek,j∈R1024 (CLS token of UNI). These patch embeddings serve as the 256 input tokens for the region-level Transformer. UNI was used as-is and kept frozen (no additional pretraining or fine-tuning). For each region k, the set of embeddings ek,jj=1256 can be arranged into a 16×16×1024 feature grid. We denote this region encoder by gθ· (ViT4096−256), which was trained in our study to output a single region token zk∈R192 summarizing region context. The slide-level aggregator hψ· (ViTWSI−4096) was then trained on slide-level labels and consumes the variable-length sequence zkk=1M to output slide-level logits for EBV status or histological subtype:zk=gθek,jj=1256,zk∈R192,y^=hψzkk=1M.

Overall, this hierarchy enables the model to integrate local cellular context (encoded in patch embeddings), meso-scale regional patterns (via the region Transformer), and slide-level heterogeneity (via the slide aggregator) within a single, context-aware representation learned from WSIs.

Attention-based multiple-instance learning baseline

To assess whether the hierarchical design provided an empirical advantage on this dataset, we additionally implemented a standard ABMIL baseline.41 The comparison was designed to be as direct as possible: the ABMIL model used the same patient-level splits, the same external hold-out hospitals, and the same pretrained UNI patch embeddings as the HIPT pipeline.

Concretely, in the HIPT pipeline each slide was represented as a set of M regions, where each region contained 256 UNI embeddings ek,j∈R1024 extracted from 256×256 patches (k=1,…,M, j=1,…,256). For the ABMIL baseline, we flattened this hierarchical representation into a single bag of patch-level embeddings per slide:Bslide=enn=1N,N=M×256,where each en∈R1024 denotes one UNI patch embedding from the slide. Thus, unlike HIPT, ABMIL does not explicitly model intermediate region structure and instead treats the slide as an unordered bag of embedded patches.

Each embedding en was first transformed by a multilayer perceptron into an instance representation vn∈R256. A learned attention function then assigned a scalar score to each instance representation, and the corresponding attention weights were normalized across the bag:αn=expavn∑m=1Nexpavm,where a· denotes the attention branch of the model. The slide-level bag representation was then computed as a weighted sum of instance features:vbag=∑n=1Nαnvn.

This bag-level representation was passed to a final classifier head to predict either EBV status (2 classes) or histological subtype (3 classes).

To maintain comparability with the HIPT experiments, we re-used the same training/validation splits and external hold-out cohort, and kept the optimization setup closely aligned where possible. In particular, the ABMIL baseline used the same learning rate, weight decay, class-balancing strategy, and task-specific training schedule as the slide-level HIPT classifier.

Training

We used a three-stage HIPT pipeline comprising: (i) a frozen patch-level encoder, (ii) a self-supervised region-level encoder, and (iii) a supervised slide-level aggregator trained separately for each task. All representation learning, hyperparameter tuning, and model selection were restricted to the development hospitals; the five hold-out hospitals were not used during region pretraining, slide-level training, or tuning.

Patch-level encoder (frozen)

Patch embeddings were extracted with the UNI pathology foundation model,48 a self-supervised Vision Transformer pretrained on >100 million H&E patches from more than 100,000 WSIs across diverse tissue types. Rather than retraining or fine-tuning the patch-level encoder on our cohort, we used UNI as a fixed feature extractor because it had been pretrained on a substantially larger and more diverse histopathology dataset than was available in our study. We therefore considered freezing the patch encoder a pragmatic design choice that leveraged a strong general-purpose pathology representation while focusing task-specific learning on the region- and slide-level modules. Each 256×256 patch pk,j yielded a 1024-dimensional embedding ek,j∈R1024 (CLS token), which served as the patch token within the hierarchy (Hierarchical Vision Transformers section).

Region-level encoder (self-supervised DINO v1)

We trained the region-level Transformer gθ (ViT4096−256; n=6 layers, h=6 heads, d=192) on UNI feature grids using DINO v1-style student–teacher self-distillation.49 For each 4096×4096 region rk, the set of patch embeddings ek,jj=1256 was arranged into a 16×16×1024 grid and spatially cropped using a multicrop strategy: 2 “global” crops of size 14×14 (196 tokens) and 8 “local” crops of size 6×6 (36 tokens), each crop retaining the 1024 feature channels. Let G and L denote the sets of global and local crops, respectively. The student network produces a probability vector ps· and the teacher produces pt·; training minimized the cross-entropy between teacher predictions on global crops and student predictions across all other crops:LDINO=∑v∈G∑u∈G∪L,u≠vHptvpsu.

Here H·· denotes cross-entropy and v,u index multicrop views of the same region feature grid. Region-level pretraining used AdamW50 with per-GPU batch size 256 and data-parallel training. We trained for 100 epochs with a 10-epoch linear warm-up to a base learning rate of 5×10−4 followed by cosine decay to 10−6. Weight decay followed a cosine schedule from 0.04 to 0.4, teacher momentum was scheduled from 0.996 toward 1.0, and teacher temperature was 0.04. After pretraining, each region rk was mapped to a single 192-dimensional region token zk, yielding an M192 sequence per WSI.

Slide-level encoder (supervised) and model selection

For slide-level prediction, we trained a lightweight aggregator hψ (ViTWSI−4096) on sequences of region tokens zkk=1M (Hierarchical Vision Transformers section). Before final training, we optimized the slide-level configuration on the EBV task using 5-fold, hospital-disjoint CV with Optuna51 (TPE sampler with Hyperband pruning) for 1000 trials and up to 30 epochs per trial. The objective was the mean across folds of the maximum validation area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC–AUC) attained over training epochs. The search space included learning rate, weight decay, Transformer width, number of heads, number of layers, and dropout rates. The selected configuration was reused unchanged for the three-class subtype task. For visualizations of the hyperparameter optimization process, see Appendix Fig. E.1.

Using the selected hyperparameters, the final slide-level model applied a token-wise projection MLP to each region token, followed by a single-layer Transformer encoder with 2 attention heads and a feedforward dimension of 256 operating over the sequence of region tokens. A gated ABMIL pooling module then produced a slide representation as a learned weighted sum of region features, which was passed through a post-pooling projection MLP and a final linear classifier head (2 outputs for EBV; 3 for subtype). Dropout was applied at four points: in the token-wise projection MLP (rate 0.5), within the Transformer encoder layer (rate 0.0), in the gated-attention pooling module (rate 0.0), and in the post-pooling projection MLP (rate 0.15). We trained with AdamW,50 batch size 1, and class-weighted cross-entropy for 50 epochs per CV fold (hospital-disjoint folds). The learning rate was 1.0578×10−5 and weight decay was 3.0048×10−4. To address class imbalance, we applied square-root reweighting of class frequencies anchored to the minority class. Within each fold, the checkpoint with the best validation ROC–AUC (on the hold-out validation hospitals) was retained. For inference, the 5-fold-specific models were ensembled by averaging predicted probabilities.

For the ABMIL baseline, training was performed separately for the EBV and subtype tasks using the same hospital-preserving splits. The model operated directly on frozen UNI patch embeddings and was trained end-to-end at the slide level with AdamW,50 batch size 1, and class-weighted cross-entropy, using the same learning rate (1.0578×10−5), weight decay (3.0048×10−4), and square-root class reweighting strategy as in the HIPT slide-level experiments. The feature-processing block preceding attention consisted of three fully connected layers with hidden dimension 256, each followed by a LeakyReLU activation and dropout (rate 0.25). The attention branch consisted of two fully connected layers with attention hidden dimension 256, tanh activation, and dropout (rate 0.25), followed by a softmax operation over all instances in the bag. The resulting bag-level representation was passed to a classifier head producing either 2 output logits for EBV prediction or 3 output logits for subtype prediction. Models were trained for up to 50 epochs per fold, and the checkpoint with the best validation ROC–AUC was retained. As in the HIPT analysis, 5-fold-specific models were ensembled at inference time by averaging predicted probabilities.

For the binary NLPHL-versus-cHL task, both HIPT and ABMIL were trained using the same optimization settings, class-balancing strategy, and model selection protocol as described above for the three-class subtype task, with the sole difference being the binary label definition.

Model calibration, threshold optimization, and evaluation

Data partitions and external validation

Models were trained with 5-fold CV using patient-level splits that preserved hospital provenance. In each fold, hospitals assigned to validation were disjoint from those used for training. External evaluation was performed on an independent hold-out cohort comprising hospitals that were fully disjoint from the development pool (see the Hospital-preserving partitioning and CV section). All hyperparameter selection, post hoc calibration, and decision-threshold optimization used only CV training/validation data from the development hospitals to prevent leakage into the hold-out cohort.

Temperature scaling (probability calibration)

Because neural-network softmax outputs are typically miscalibrated, probability calibration was performed via temperature scaling. For each CV fold i, we used the validation-set logits zi∈RNi×C and corresponding labels yi, where Ni is the number of validation cases in fold i and yn∈1…C denotes the true class index for case n. A scalar temperature Ti>0 was learned by minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL):T^i=argminT>0LNLLT=−1Ni∑n=1Nilogexpzn,yni/T∑c=1Cexpzn,ci/T.

Calibrated class probabilities were then obtained as p^n,ci=softmaxzni/T^ic. Per-fold calibration quality was summariszed using the top-label Expected Calibration Error (ECE; 15 bins) and the multiclass Brier score.

Post hoc decision rule and per-class thresholds

To mitigate class imbalance at decision time, a per-class threshold vector τ=τ1…τC∈01C was learned and applied with a margin-based selection rule. For a calibrated probability vector p^∈01C, let the qualified set be Q=c∈1…C:p^c≥τc. Predicted class y^ was assigned as:y^=argmaxc∈Qp^c−τc,ifQ≠∅,argmaxcp^c,otherwise.

Thresholds were optimized to maximize macro-F1 on validation data under this same decision rule using a coordinate-descent search over a fixed grid τc∈0.010.02…0.99. Concretely, within each fold i, we first calibrated probabilities with T^i, then obtained a fold-specific threshold vector τ^i by maximizing macro-F1 on that fold's validation set.

Pooled operating point

To obtain a single fixed operating point, calibrated validation probabilities from all CV folds were concatenated p^1…p^5, and a pooled threshold vector τ^ was refit by maximizing macro-F1 under the margin rule on the union of CV validation data.

Ensembling and application to the hold-out cohort

On the hold-out test set, five independently trained models produced logits zm∈RNtest×C (m=1,…,5), where Ntest is the number of cases in the hold-out cohort. Each model's logits were temperature-scaled with its corresponding T^m and converted to probabilities: p^m=softmaxzm/T^m. Ensemble probabilities were computed as the arithmetic mean, p-=15∑m=15p^m, and the pooled threshold vector τ^ was applied with the margin-based rule above to produce top-1 predictions.

Importantly, the temperature parameters and pooled decision thresholds were estimated exclusively from CV predictions within the development cohort and were then applied unchanged to the independent hold-out cohort. Thus, calibration in this study was not performed separately on the external test cohort, but was treated as a fixed part of the prediction pipeline learned on development data. In what follows, we refer to this combination of temperature scaling and per-class margin-based thresholds as the calibrated, threshold-tuned model (ensemble). As a baseline, we also evaluated the uncalibrated five-model ensemble using simple argmax over class probabilities (fixed 0.5 threshold in the binary setting); we refer to this as the uncalibrated argmax baseline.

Evaluation metrics and uncertainty

Performance was summarized using accuracy, F1, precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity, one-vs-rest ROC–AUC, precision–recall AUC (PR–AUC), and Cohen's κ. For the multiclass (subtype) task, label-based metrics were macro-averaged over classes and class-wise top-1 precision/recall/F1 and specificity were additionally reported; for the binary (EBV) task, precision/recall/F1 refer to the positive class and specificity to the negative class. For CV experiments, metrics were computed on each fold's validation set and summarized as the mean across folds with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) computed from the fold-wise standard error using a t-distribution. For the external test set, uncertainty was quantified via nonparametric bootstrap with replacement (10,000 replicates), reporting percentile 95% CIs (2.5th–97.5th percentiles). Calibration on the external set was additionally reported using top-label ECE and the (multiclass) Brier score computed on the ensemble probabilities before and after temperature scaling.

Implementation details

Temperature scaling used a one-dimensional search minimizing NLL (convex in T); ECE used 15 equally spaced bins. Threshold search employed a fixed grid 0.010.99 per class with a small number of coordinate-descent passes, which was found sufficient for stable macro-F1 optimization. All calibration and threshold fitting were confined to CV validation data; the external test set was never used for model selection or tuning.

EBV prediction within major histological subtype strata

Because EBV prevalence differed across histological subtypes in our cohort (Table 2), we performed a post hoc stratified analysis on the external hold-out cohort to examine whether the EBV classifier retained discriminatory ability within major subtype strata. Using the same calibrated ensemble predictions and fixed threshold τEBV=0.330 as in the primary EBV analysis, we evaluated EBV prediction separately in NS and MC.

Within NS (N=264; EBV +/EBV−=76/188), the calibrated model achieved ROC–AUC =0.725 (95% CI [0.657, 0.792]) and PR–AUC =0.545 (95% CI [0.438, 0.659]). Using the pooled threshold of 0.330, sensitivity was 0.658 (95% CI [0.549, 0.762]) and specificity was 0.644 (95% CI [0.575, 0.712]). Within MC (N=73; EBV +/EBV−=43/30), the calibrated model achieved ROC–AUC =0.666 (95% CI [0.524, 0.798]) and PR–AUC =0.665 (95% CI [0.536, 0.836]). Using the same pooled threshold, sensitivity was 0.907 (95% CI [0.811, 0.978]) and specificity was 0.333 (95% CI [0.167, 0.514]).

These subgroup analyses indicate that EBV prediction was not solely driven by between-subtype differences in prevalence, as the classifier retained above-chance discriminatory ability even when evaluation was restricted to a single histological subtype. The differing operating characteristics across NS and MC suggest that subtype-associated morphology likely contributes to the learned EBV signal, but does not fully account for it.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated a hierarchical Vision Transformer pipeline for EBV status prediction and histological subtype classification in HL, and compared it against a simpler ABMIL41 baseline using the same UNI patch embeddings and hospital-preserving data partitions. We chose this hierarchical pipeline to better capture multiscale context and spatial dependencies in WSIs, while also evaluating a standard ABMIL baseline to benchmark the added value of hierarchical modelling. Both models demonstrated good performance with potential for clinical decision-support, though further improvements in accuracy and robustness are needed (Table 4, Table 5).

The choice of HIPT as the primary architecture was motivated by evidence that hierarchical and spatially aware aggregation methods have shown strong performance across multiple slide-level cancer subtyping and survival prediction tasks.39 Moreover, HL presents signals at multiple physical scales—from individual malignant cells to the broader composition of the reactive microenvironment—making a multiscale architecture a biologically principled starting point. The ABMIL comparison was feasible without additional feature extraction because the first-level UNI patch embeddings, already computed for the HIPT pipeline, could be directly aggregated by attention-based pooling, effectively bypassing the region- and slide-level Transformer stages.

An important finding of this comparison was that the simpler ABMIL baseline outperformed HIPT on the external hold-out cohort for both EBV prediction and subtype classification, suggesting that explicitly modelling the patch-to-region-to-slide hierarchy did not translate into superior predictive performance in this setting. Several factors may explain this result. First, the region-level encoder in HIPT compresses 256 patch embeddings (each 1024-dimensional) from a 4096×4096 region into a single 192-dimensional region token, representing a substantial dimensionality reduction that may discard fine-grained information relevant to the prediction tasks. Second, the hierarchical architecture introduces additional trainable stages compared with ABMIL, increasing the risk of overfitting when training data are limited. In the original HIPT study,39 the lower hierarchical encoders were pretrained at pan-cancer scale, using more than 10,000 WSIs and over 400,000 extracted 4096×4096 regions for region-level pretraining. By contrast, our development cohort comprised 1952 WSIs and 98,973 extracted 4096×4096 regions, corresponding to approximately one quarter of the original region-level pretraining scale. This data limitation likely prevented the hierarchical model from fully exploiting multiscale context. Third, particularly for EBV prediction, the most informative morphological signals may reside in relatively localized regions that can be captured effectively by strong UNI patch embeddings combined with attention-based pooling, without requiring explicit spatial modelling. The same pattern was observed for subtype prediction, suggesting that data scale and representation bottlenecks may have limited the benefit of hierarchical modelling across both tasks. These observations are consistent with recent benchmarking of WSI aggregation methods, which found that spatially aware aggregation provides less additional benefit when strong domain-specific foundation-model embeddings are used.44

We therefore interpret HIPT in this study as a biologically motivated multiscale modelling strategy whose feasibility was demonstrated, but not as an empirically superior alternative to MIL for the present dataset. These findings do not diminish the value of hierarchical architectures in general, but indicate that their advantages may only materialize with substantially larger training cohorts. In settings where cohort size is limited, strong foundation-model patch embeddings combined with attention-based aggregation can provide a competitive and computationally more efficient alternative.

It should be noted that there is known label noise in the outcome; trained human pathologists assessing HL tumors have been shown to vary significantly in their diagnostic assessments.36, 37, 38 Historical re-review studies show that ∼30% of HL cases are reclassified on expert panel review,36, 37 and EBV detection studies report interobserver κ values as low as 0.6, depending on subtype and staining method.38 The fact that the model showcases reasonable performance despite this limitation gives credence to the hypothesis that modern deep-learning architectures are capable of extracting biologically meaningful signals from routine H&E-stained lymphoma biopsies,52 and that the approach is not fundamentally limited by a lack of signal in the images.

A specific instance of label ambiguity concerns EBV-positive NLPHL cases. EBV-positivity in NLPHL is unusual, and distinguishing EBV-positivity in lymphocyte-predominant (LP) tumor cells from EBV-positive reactive cells in the background infiltrate can be difficult. In this study, these cases were retained according to the available pathological review and consensus EBV assignment. The relatively high number of apparently EBV-positive NLPHL cases in our cohort suggests that some cases may reflect ambiguity in attributing EBV-positivity to LP tumor cells rather than background cells, or uncertainty in the subtype assignment itself. As more precise reclassification was not achievable within the constraints of this study, these cases were retained according to the consensus assignment.

For EBV prediction, post hoc calibration and threshold selection shifted the operating point toward higher recall (sensitivity) on both CV and external evaluation. This led to fewer false negatives at the cost of decreased specificity, without changing discrimination. In a clinical context where missing true positive cases may be less acceptable, such a trade-off could be operationally beneficial. Nevertheless, even the calibrated models leave substantial room for improvement before they could be relied upon without human oversight. Histological subtype and EBV status are biologically related in HL, so some overlap in the morphological information relevant to each task is expected. To address the possibility that EBV prediction mainly reflected subtype-associated morphology, we evaluated the EBV classifier separately within NS and MC on the external hold-out cohort. The persistence of above-chance discrimination within both subtype strata suggests that the model is not relying exclusively on between-subtype differences, although some degree of shared morphological signal is likely.

Subtype prediction followed a similar pattern, although external performance gains were limited. Whereas calibration and threshold tuning improved balanced metrics during CV, the effect did not translate into measurable improvement on the external dataset. Persistent misclassification patterns, particularly between NS and MC, suggest that either the available images do not carry enough discriminative information or that the current modelling approach cannot fully extract it. These confusion patterns are consistent with known diagnostic difficulty in this setting36, 37, 38 and support the interpretation that the model is approaching the same decision boundary limitations that challenge human pathologists.

The dedicated binary NLPHL-versus-cHL analysis confirmed that both architectures achieve high overall discrimination for this clinically relevant distinction (ROC–AUC > 0.94), but sensitivity for the rare NLPHL class remained limited, consistent with the small number of NLPHL cases in both the training and hold-out cohorts. Reliable binary NLPHL detection will likely require substantially larger cohorts with greater NLPHL representation.

Three broad explanations may account for the performance plateau. First, current models may be capable but limited by sample size, image heterogeneity, or lack of optimization. Larger and more diverse datasets, combined with architectural or methodological advances, may yield improved results. Previous results have pointed to large performance differences when models are transferred between different health-care systems (notably between the USA and Europe), indicating that models are overfitting to the specific factors that vary between sites, including protocols and reagents.53, 54 Second, the diagnostic categories themselves may reflect historical classification rather than discrete biological entities of etiological significance or indeed of clinical relevance in the context of specific treatments.55 If the subtype labels are imperfect proxies for underlying disease mechanisms, then the task may not be fully solvable on morphology alone.56, 57 Further machine learning efforts could therefore serve not only as a mechanism for improved classification of WSIs but also as a tool to ascertain whether the currently defined diagnostic groups truly align with biological reality. Third, the models consider slide-derived information alone. Incorporating additional patient-level data such as age, sex, smoking history (a risk factor for EBV-positive HL), or genetic profiling may provide orthogonal context and help resolve ambiguous cases. We have deliberately abstained from doing so here. Our aim was to assess what a machine could learn from assessing the visible composition of the tumor microenvironment only, without clues from orthogonal context or artifacts of the slides. Such a classifier presumably would be biologically based, and hence more reliable and transportable than would be the case for a classifier using additional orthogonal information.

We also did not perform an ablation study on freezing vs retraining the patch-level encoder. Our design deliberately relied on a strong pretrained pathology foundation model and focused task-specific learning on the higher levels of the hierarchy. Although end-to-end training or retraining the patch encoder from scratch may be worth exploring in future work, this would require substantially greater computational cost and may not be advantageous in a cohort that is much smaller and less diverse than the data used for large-scale pathology foundation-model pretraining. A further limitation is that we did not perform a systematic explainability analysis of model predictions. Although standard post hoc attribution methods could in principle be applied to both hierarchical and MIL-based WSI models, robust interpretation would require evaluation across a sufficiently large number of cases together with structured expert pathological review. Moreover, the underlying slide data cannot be shared publicly, which limits the reproducibility of any such analysis. We therefore chose not to include qualitative heatmap-based interpretations in the present study in order to avoid over-interpreting a small number of examples. A more comprehensive assessment of model explainability remains an important direction for future work.

Future work should address these challenges by expanding training cohorts, incorporating multimodal inputs, and evaluating alternative prediction targets beyond conventional histological labels. Approaches that integrate morphological features with molecular and clinical information may offer more stable and biologically valid decision boundaries. Evaluation against prospective workflows will also be necessary to determine how such models perform in the hands of practicing pathologists and whether they can meaningfully enhance clinical efficiency, accuracy, or consistency. In practical deployment on a truly new source, the fixed calibration and thresholding scheme learned on development data may still be used, but local recalibration could be beneficial if well-annotated site-specific validation data are available and probability-based decisions are required.

A major strength of this study is the scale and comprehensiveness of the dataset. To our knowledge, this represents the largest collection of EBV-typed HL WSIs assembled to date, comprising 2101 cases from 23 Danish hospitals. The cohort is also population-based, reflecting nationwide case ascertainment rather than referral- or center-selected sampling. The inclusion of WSIs from multiple institutions enhances the real-world relevance of the analysis and reduces the risk of center-specific bias, whereas the use of patient-level grouping prevents leakage and ensures valid evaluation of model generalization.

Despite this, the dataset remains modest in size relative to the complexity of modern deep learning models. Larger and more diverse cohorts, particularly including international centers, a wider spectrum of histological subtypes, and greater variation in staining and scanning protocols, will likely be required to fully evaluate the potential of hierarchical Vision Transformers in this domain. Furthermore, although all cases underwent standard diagnostic review, only two pathologists contributed to the labeling. Given well-established inter-observer variability in HL classification, some diagnostic noise is likely, and this may partially limit the achievable performance ceiling for purely morphology-based models. Future studies incorporating larger training cohorts, adjudicated reference diagnoses, and additional multimodal clinical or molecular information may therefore provide a firmer basis for evaluating the true limits of automated prediction in this setting.

This study represents a proof of concept rather than a deployable diagnostic tool. The results demonstrate that both hierarchical and attention-based architectures can capture clinically meaningful patterns from routine histopathology with cross-institutional generalizability, even in HL where more than 90% of cells on a slide are non-malignant immune cells. The finding that a simpler ABMIL baseline outperformed the more complex HIPT model further underscores that model complexity must be matched to available training data, and that strong foundation-model representations can compensate for simpler aggregation strategies. Whereas performance remains insufficient for autonomous use, these findings highlight a promising direction in which computational models serve as companions to pathologist expertise, potentially advancing toward more precise and biologically grounded diagnostic approaches.
