Association of childhood maltreatment with DXA‑derived body composition in middle‑aged and older adults: mediating roles of unhealthy lifestyle and depression
Authors: Yuanpeng Zhu, Di Liu, Xiangjie Yin, Terry Jianguo Zhang, Nan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood maltreatment (CM) may shape health outcomes later in life, yet its association with body composition in mid-to-late adulthood remains incompletely characterized, particularly regarding sex differences and mechanistic pathways.MethodsWe analyzed UK Biobank participants who underwent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans and grip strength assessment at imaging visits (2014 onward) and retrospectively reported CM in 2016. CM domains included physical and emotional neglect, as well as sexual, physical, and emotional abuse, assessed using the Childhood Trauma Screener. We examined DXA-derived indices of central adiposity and fat distribution (e.g., visceral adipose tissue [VAT]), lean soft-tissue indices (e.g., lean mass index [LMI]), and relative body-composition ratios (e.g., muscle-to-fat ratios [MFRs]), alongside grip strength/BMI as a functional measure. Linear regression models estimated associations between CM and body composition, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic factors, maternal smoking, and histories of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. Mediation analyses examined the role of unhealthy lifestyle and depressive symptoms. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the false discovery rate method.ResultsThe study included 21,817 participants (mean age 64.5 ± 7.8 years; 52.7% women). After full adjustment, CM was associated with higher central adiposity and a less favorable muscle–adiposity profile, reflected by lower MFRs and lower grip strength/BMI. Physical abuse exhibited the strongest associations, including higher central adiposity (VAT β = 0.130; 95% CI, 0.097–0.162) and higher LMI (β = 0.152). Neglect domains showed smaller but generally significant associations. Muscle composition was consistently less favorable, characterized by lower MFRs (e.g., physical abuse β=−0.092; sexual abuse β=−0.060) and lower grip strength/BMI. Notably, sexual abuse showed stronger associations in women. A higher cumulative CM burden was associated with higher central adiposity and lower MFRs. In cross-sectional mediation analyses, unhealthy lifestyle and depressive symptoms were associated with attenuation of these associations by up to 31.6%.ConclusionsCM was associated with higher central adiposity, lower MFRs, and lower muscle-specific strength in mid-to-late adulthood. Physical abuse showed the strongest associations, while sexual abuse displayed stronger associations in women. Unhealthy lifestyle and depressive symptoms partially mediated these associations, highlighting potential prevention targets among individuals with CM exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07950-0.Keywords: Childhood maltreatment, Body composition, Obesity, Lifestyle, Depression

Introduction

Childhood maltreatment (CM)—including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction occurring before the age of 18—are increasingly recognized to influence health outcomes across the lifespan [1–3]. Life-course theory posits that early-life adversity exerts enduring effects via pathways involving dysregulated stress biology, behavioral adaptations, and constrained accumulation of social resources, ultimately resulting in elevated risks for physical and mental disorders in adulthood and later life [1, 4]. Consistent with this theoretical framework, extensive empirical evidence supports associations between CM and increased risks of cardiometabolic disease, stroke, frailty, and premature mortality [5].

Body composition represents a central, modifiable phenotype linking lifestyle factors with disease risk [6, 7]. Specific depots and compartments carry distinct prognostic value: excess visceral adiposity promotes cardiometabolic disease [8], whereas low appendicular lean mass underlies frailty, disability, and cognitive and functional decline [9, 10]. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) serves as a gold-standard method in clinical practice and large-scale epidemiological research for quantifying fat and lean compartments with minimal radiation exposure [11].

Although CM is associated with adult obesity, systematic evidence regarding their relationship with comprehensive body composition profiles in mid-to-late adulthood remains limited. Prior work has largely relied on crude anthropometry (e.g., body mass index [BMI]) [12]; only one small-sample study has reported associations between childhood maltreatment and increased visceral adiposity [13], and emerging data suggest CM may impair skeletal muscle mitochondrial function [14]. Whether CM is associated with detailed DXA-derived adiposity distribution and lean-mass indices at scale, and whether these associations differ by sex, remains largely unknown. In parallel, both body composition and CM-related health risks are strongly influenced by modifiable behaviors (smoking, alcohol use, physical activity, diet, and sleep) and depressive symptoms [15], yet their mediating roles in the CM–body composition relationship have not been delineated in population-based cohorts.

We used data from the UK Biobank (UKB), a population-based cohort of ~ 500,000 adults, to: (1) quantify associations of CM with a broad panel of DXA-derived body composition indices capturing visceral and regional adiposity, lean mass, muscle-to-fat ratios (MFR), and grip strength-normalized metrics; (2) test for sex differences in these associations; and (3) evaluate whether an unhealthy lifestyle score and depressive symptoms mediate the observed associations. We hypothesized that higher CM exposure would relate to more adverse adiposity distribution and lower muscularity, with partial mediation by unhealthy lifestyle and depression.

Methods

Study participants

The UKB is a large, prospective cohort comprising more than 500,000 UK residents enrolled between the ages of 37 and 73 years during 2006–2010 [16]. In 2014, a total of 75,961 participants attended the imaging visit and underwent imaging assessments. From the initial 75,961 participants, we excluded 35,940 individuals lacking the required DXA-derived body composition data and further excluded 14,853 participants missing the necessary CM scores, leaving 25,168 individuals. After further exclusion of 3,351 participants missing required covariate data, the final analytical sample consisted of 21,817 individuals (Supplementary eFigure 1A). Furthermore, the timeline of data collection and variable assessment is presented in Supplementary eFigure 1B. The UKB study was approved by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee as a Research Tissue Bank. Written informed consent was provided by each participant before the study, and researchers are allowed to use data from UKB without an additional ethical clearance. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Assessment of childhood maltreatment

CM was assessed through five items from the Childhood Trauma Screener (CTS) included in the 2016 online mental health questionnaire, representing physical neglect (no one to take to a doctor when needed), emotional neglect (did not felt loved as a child), sexual abuse (sexually molested as a child), physical abuse (physically abused by family), and emotional abuse (felt hated by a family member) [17].

The CTS, a brief version of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, is a cost-efficient, validated, and reliable tool for assessing CM in large-scale epidemiological research. All items were prefaced with, “When I was growing up (age < 16 years).” Participants responded to each item using a five-point scale: never true, rarely true, sometimes true, often true, or very often true. Responses were dichotomized as follows: physical neglect was coded as 1 if participants responded never true, rarely true, sometimes true, or often true; emotional neglect was coded as 1 if participants responded never true, rarely true, or sometimes true; sexual abuse, physical abuse, and emotional abuse were coded as 1 if participants responded rarely true, sometimes true, often true, or very often true [18]. The summary score, ranging from 0 to 5, indicated the cumulative exposure to childhood adversities, with higher scores representing greater adversity.

Body composition and muscle functional measures

In the UK Biobank, whole-body DXA scans were acquired using GE Lunar iDXA scanners [19]. We selected 21 DXA-derived body-composition features grouped into seven categories: central fat (e.g., visceral adipose tissue [VAT] index), lean mass (e.g., lean mass index [LMI]), regional fat proportion (e.g., trunk fat percentage), fat distribution (e.g., android/gynoid fat ratio), lower-body fat (e.g., gynoid fat index), muscle-to-fat ratio (MFR; defined as DXA-derived lean mass divided by fat mass, with higher values indicating a more favorable muscle relative to adiposity profile; e.g., leg MFR), and total adiposity (e.g., total-body mass index [TMI]). In addition, grip strength was included as a functional measure of muscle-specific strength assessed at the imaging visit [20]. To account for individual differences in height, all mass-based features were standardized by indexing them to height, and all features were further standardized into sex-specific z-scores. Comprehensive variable definitions and preprocessing steps are provided in Supplementary eTable 1.

Assessment of lifestyle and depression

Consistent with previous research, we constructed an unhealthy lifestyle score based on five key lifestyle factors: smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, sleep duration, and dietary habits [21, 22]. Lifestyle data were collected at baseline using structured questionnaires and a 24-hour dietary recall assessment. An unhealthy status was defined as current or former smoking, alcohol consumption more than once per week, absence of engagement in strenuous sports or heavy do-it-yourself activities in the preceding four weeks, or daily sleep duration of less than 7 or more than 9 h [22, 23]. Dietary status was classified as unhealthy if participants failed to meet recommended intake levels for more than half of the following dietary components: fruits, vegetables, fish and shellfish, dairy products, whole grains, vegetable oils, refined grains, sugar-sweetened beverages, and unprocessed meats [24]. Each lifestyle factor was scored as 1 if unhealthy and 0 if healthy. The unhealthy lifestyle score was calculated as the sum of scores for the five lifestyle factors, yielding a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 5. Higher scores indicated progressively unhealthier lifestyles.

Depression was assessed at baseline using the Recent Depressive Symptoms (RDS-4) scale. The RDS-4 consists of four self-reported items evaluating depressed mood, disinterest, restlessness, and tiredness. Each item inquired about symptom experiences over the recent two-week period. Response options were: (a) not at all, (b) several days, (c) more than half the days, and (d) nearly every day, coded as a = 1, b = 2, c = 3, d = 4 [25]. Therefore, the total score from these four items ranged from 4 to 16. Higher scores indicated more severe depressive symptoms.

Covariates

Covariates were assessed at the imaging visit (concurrent with DXA) and included age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, employment status, Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), imaging assessment center, maternal smoking, and histories of cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, and cancer [18, 26]. Race was categorized into four groups: White, Asian, Black, and Mixed and others. Educational level was categorized into “college degree or above” and “high school or below.” Employment status included “employed”, “retired”, and “other”. The TDI was derived from census data on employment, housing, and social class based on participants’ postal codes, with positive numbers indicating lower socioeconomic status. Maternal smoking status was categorized as “no”, “yes”, or “unknown” [18]. Additionally, histories of CVD, diabetes, and cancer were ascertained from linked hospital inpatient records using ICD-10 codes. For each condition, we identified the first recorded occurrence date in inpatient data. Disease history at the imaging assessment was defined as “yes” if the first occurrence date was before the imaging date, and “no” otherwise. ICD-10 code ranges were: diabetes (E10–E14), cancer (C00–C97), and CVD, defined as ischaemic heart disease (I20–I25), heart failure (I50), and cerebrovascular disease/stroke (I60–I69). Each condition was analysed as a binary covariate.

Statistical analysis

We summarized participant characteristics using mean (SD) for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Each outcome indicator was evaluated in a separate regression model. First, general linear regression models were employed to investigate associations between individual CM and body composition and muscle strength indicators [27]. Regression coefficients and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained from two models: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, race, TDI, employment status, and imaging assessment center; Model 2 further adjusted for histories of diabetes, CVD, cancer, and maternal smoking. Second, sex-stratified analyses were performed by fitting the same models separately in women and men (without including sex as a covariate). Sex interactions were tested in the full sample by including a multiplicative interaction term (CM × sex) together with the main effects of CM and sex, and the p-value for interaction (P interaction) was derived from the interaction term. Third, we evaluated associations between cumulative CM burden (summary score) and each indicator using the same covariate sets, and assessed sex differences using the interaction approach described above. Fourth, mediation analyses were conducted using the R package “mediation” [28] with 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples to assess indirect associations via unhealthy lifestyle score and depressive symptoms for indicators that were significantly associated with cumulative CM burden. Two-sided p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) refitting models after imputing missing covariates to address potential bias. Although covariate missingness was modest (≈ 0.04%–5.92%; e.g., 5.92% for unhealthy lifestyle score, 5.75% for depressive symptoms), we employed an iterative random-forest approach (missRanger; predictive mean matching k = 3, 500 trees, 3 iterations; fixed seed) to generate a complete dataset for re-analysis; (2) additionally adjusting for the unhealthy lifestyle score and depressive symptoms to assess their potential confounding or mediating roles; (3) evaluating age interactions to determine if the associations between cumulative childhood maltreatment and body composition varied by age; (4) examining associations with clinically defined outcomes, including obesity, low lean mass, low muscle strength, and sarcopenic obesity, using established criteria [29, 30]; and (5) quantifying the mediation effects of individual lifestyle components on the associations between childhood maltreatment and body composition. All analyses were performed using R statistical software [31] version 4.3.0.

Results

Characteristics of participants

This study included 21,817 participants with a mean age of 64.5 years at the imaging assessment; 52.7% were women, and 97.1% were White (Table 1). Of the participants, 54.3% reported no CM, while 8.9% reported experiencing three or more CM. Participant characteristics varied according to their levels of CM exposure; those reporting more CM were more likely to be female, non-White, educated to high school level or below, non-retired, have greater socioeconomic deprivation, higher prevalence of diabetes and CVD, higher rates of maternal smoking, and higher scores on unhealthy lifestyle and depression measures.

The associations between individual CM and body composition

After full adjustment for age at imaging, sex, race, educational level, employment status, TDI, imaging assessment center, maternal smoking, and histories of diabetes, CVD, and cancer, the overall pattern of associations was broadly consistent across CM domains, with physical abuse showing the strongest associations (largest absolute β estimates) for multiple adiposity and muscle-related outcomes (Fig. 1, Supplementary eTable 2). Physical abuse was positively associated with central adiposity and lean-mass quantity indices (VAT: β = 0.130; 95% CI, 0.097–0.162; LMI: β = 0.152; 95% CI, 0.119–0.185), whereas sexual abuse was also positively associated with VAT but with a smaller magnitude (β = 0.105; 95% CI, 0.059–0.151). Associations for neglect domains were smaller but remained statistically evident (physical neglect—VAT: β = 0.089; 95% CI, 0.052–0.125; emotional neglect—VAT: β = 0.055; 95% CI, 0.024–0.087). Lean-mass quantity indices were also positively associated with abuse subtypes (emotional abuse—LMI: β = 0.100; 95% CI, 0.063–0.138), which may reflect higher overall body size rather than better muscle quality. In contrast, muscle-related indicators showed inverse associations: MFR was lower (physical abuse: β=−0.092; 95% CI, − 0.125 to − 0.060; sexual abuse: β=−0.060; 95% CI, − 0.107 to − 0.014; physical neglect: β=−0.079; 95% CI, − 0.115 to − 0.042), and grip strength/BMI was consistently lower (physical neglect: β=−0.095; 95% CI, − 0.131 to − 0.059; emotional abuse: β=−0.072; 95% CI, − 0.108 to − 0.037). Overall, individual CM—particularly physical abuse—were associated with greater central adiposity together with less favorable muscle function indicators.

The associations between individual CM and body composition stratified by sex

Patterns were generally consistent with the overall analysis but differed in magnitude, and sexual abuse showed evidence of effect modification by sex (Fig. 2). Physical abuse was positively associated with central adiposity in both women and men (VAT: women β = 0.137; 95% CI, 0.091–0.183; men β = 0.125; 95% CI, 0.079–0.170), and was also associated with higher lean soft-tissue quantity indices, while showing inverse associations with MFR and grip strength/BMI. For sexual abuse, associations differed by sex, with a positive association with android fat index in women (β = 0.170; 95% CI, 0.115–0.225) but no statistically evident association in men (β=−0.027; 95% CI, − 0.104–0.050), consistent with evidence of effect modification by sex (P for interaction < 0.001). Physical neglect was positively associated with adiposity indices in both sexes (FMI: men β = 0.117; 95% CI, 0.061–0.174; women β = 0.080; 95% CI, 0.032–0.127), whereas emotional neglect was modestly associated with higher VAT in women (β = 0.081; 95% CI, 0.039–0.124) but not statistically evident in men.

The associations between overall CM and body composition

Higher cumulative CM burden was associated with a more adverse DXA-derived body-composition profile, primarily reflecting higher adiposity indices: VAT (β = 0.052; 95% CI, 0.040–0.065), trunk/android fat indices (β ≈ 0.049–0.051), FMI (β = 0.050; 95% CI, 0.038–0.062), TMI (β = 0.055; 95% CI, 0.043–0.067), and fat percentages (β ≈ 0.040–0.042) were higher, along with a shift toward central fat distribution (android/gynoid fat ratio: β = 0.034; 95% CI, 0.022–0.046) (Fig. 3A). Lean-mass indices were also modestly higher (LMI: β = 0.047; 95% CI, 0.036–0.059; FFMI: β = 0.048; 95% CI, 0.036–0.060; android lean index: β = 0.049; 95% CI, 0.037–0.061), whereas muscle-related indicators were lower (MFR: β=−0.034; 95% CI, − 0.045 to − 0.023; grip strength/BMI: β=−0.039; 95% CI, − 0.050 to − 0.028), with absolute grip strength showing little association (β=−0.010; 95% CI, − 0.021–0.001). Results were directionally consistent across sexes. Associations with central adiposity and fat distribution were slightly larger in women (e.g., VAT: β = 0.055 vs. β = 0.049; android/gynoid fat ratio: β = 0.042 vs. β = 0.020), whereas men showed somewhat larger associations with arm and total lean mass (arms LMI: β = 0.060 vs. β = 0.042; FFMI: β = 0.052 vs. β = 0.046). MFR and grip strength/BMI were lower in both sexes (women: β=−0.040 and β=−0.043; men: β=−0.026 and β=−0.034) (Fig. 3B and C). Formal interaction tests did not identify statistically significant sex interactions after multiple-testing correction; nominal evidence of interaction was observed for android/gynoid fat ratio (interaction β=−0.026; 95% CI, − 0.049 to − 0.003) and arm MFR (interaction β = 0.024; 95% CI, 0.001–0.048) (Fig. 3D).

Mediating roles of unhealthy lifestyle and depression

In mediation analyses, unhealthy lifestyle and depressive symptoms showed evidence of partial indirect associations with the relationships between cumulative CM burden and body-composition indicators (Fig. 4), under standard mediation assumptions. Across adiposity-related indices, the proportion mediated via unhealthy lifestyle was approximately 10%–15% (VAT: PM = 12.2%; 95% CI, 9.3%–17.6%; trunk fat percentage: PM = 15.1%; 95% CI, 11.4%–19.4%), whereas the proportion mediated via depressive symptoms was approximately 18%–22% (android fat index: PM = 19.5%; 95% CI, 14.4%–24.3%; FMI: PM = 19.7%; 95% CI, 14.9%–27.9%). Fat distribution also showed indirect associations (android/gynoid fat ratio: unhealthy lifestyle PM = 13.0%; 95% CI, 8.6%–22.8%; depressive symptoms PM = 17.6%; 95% CI, 11.7%–30.1%). The largest proportions mediated were observed for muscle-to-fat metrics (unhealthy lifestyle—MFR: PM = 16.4%; 95% CI, 10.7%–25.8%; depressive symptoms—leg MFR: PM = 31.6%; 95% CI, 22.1%–48.3%; grip strength/BMI: PM = 23.9%; 95% CI, 17.5%–36.1%). Indirect associations for lean-mass indices were smaller (unhealthy lifestyle ≈ 1%–3%; depressive symptoms ≈ 4%–6%).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation yielded results consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary eFigures 2–4), and these associations remained robust after additional adjustment for unhealthy lifestyle and depressive symptoms (Supplementary eFigure 5). Age interaction analyses revealed significant modification effects on central adiposity and lean mass indices, whereas associations with muscle strength and MFRs did not vary significantly by age (Supplementary eFigure 6). In terms of clinical outcomes, childhood maltreatment was associated with a higher risk of obesity—and to a lesser extent, low lean mass and strength—but showed no significant association with sarcopenic obesity (Supplementary eFigure 7). Finally, component-specific mediation analyses indicated that smoking exerted the strongest and most widespread effects, followed by sleep and physical activity, while diet showed negligible mediation (Supplementary eFigure 8).

Discussion

In this UK Biobank imaging sub-cohort, a higher burden of CM was associated with a less favorable DXA-derived body-composition profile in mid-to-late life, characterized by greater central and total adiposity alongside poorer muscle-related indicators. To our knowledge, this is among the largest DXA-based population studies to comprehensively characterize CM–body-composition associations and to quantify behavioral and psychological correlates that may contribute to these links, which may help motivate trauma-informed risk stratification and health promotion for adults with a history of CM.

Our study indicated that a higher CM burden was associated with greater central and total adiposity and a more central fat distribution, consistent with previous studies [13, 32]. Importantly, our findings align with emerging population-based evidence that CM is related not only to general adiposity but also to visceral/central obesity-related traits. For example, the recent SHIP study [33] reported sex-specific associations of CM with multiple obesity-related anthropometric and body-fat measures. While SHIP relied on CTQ and focused primarily on obesity-related traits assessed by anthropometry and MRI, our UK Biobank imaging analysis extends this literature by leveraging a substantially larger sample and a broad, DXA-derived panel, while additionally testing sex- and age-related effect modification and evaluating attenuation patterns consistent with behavioral and psychological correlates, thereby offering a more phenotype-resolved and life-course–informed characterization of CM–body-composition links in mid-to-late adulthood. Together, these results support the view that CM is consistently associated with a more adverse adiposity profile in adulthood across cohorts and measurement modalities, although differences in CM instruments (CTS/CTQ items), covariate sets, age structure, and body-composition platforms may contribute to variations in which sex-specific patterns emerge and their magnitude.

The biological and behavioral mechanisms linking CM to adverse body composition are likely multifactorial. Mechanistically, CM exposure may become biologically embedded through structural alterations in stress-regulatory frontolimbic circuits [34, 35]. These changes can lead to chronic dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and systemic low-grade inflammation, processes known to disrupt metabolic homeostasis and specifically promote visceral fat accumulation [36, 37]. In parallel, our mediation findings align with the “self-medication” hypothesis, where survivors adopt maladaptive behaviors to regulate emotional distress [38]. Notably, smoking—which emerged as the strongest mediator in our analysis—along with alcohol consumption and palatable food intake, may serve as “soothing” mechanisms despite their metabolic costs [39]. Furthermore, the substantial mediation by depressive symptoms supports a distinct psychobiological pathway, wherein trauma-induced psychopathology exacerbates sedentary behavior and metabolic dysregulation, creating a vicious cycle that favors adiposity [40]. Given the established links between central adiposity and cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality, these adiposity patterns may represent clinically relevant correlates through which CM is associated with later-life health risks.

We observed a seemingly paradoxical pattern: higher CM burden was associated with higher absolute lean mass indices, yet consistently lower MFR and lower grip strength normalized to BMI. This concurrent increase in absolute lean mass likely reflects “obesity-associated lean mass accretion”—a compensatory physiological adaptation where skeletal muscle and connective tissue hypertrophy to support excess body weight. However, the inverse association with MFR and relative strength suggests that this accrual is maladaptive: the gain in adipose tissue vastly outpaces muscle growth. Furthermore, because DXA-derived “lean mass” encompasses all non-fat soft tissue, it inadvertently captures intramuscular fat infiltration (myosteatosis) and extracellular water, which are often elevated in states of chronic inflammation [41]. Thus, the “higher” lean mass observed in CM survivors may partly reflect compromised muscle condition rather than functional contractile tissue, consistent with emerging mechanistic evidence linking early-life stress to mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation [14].

It is noteworthy that despite observing the continuous components of a sarcopenic phenotype, we did not find a significant association between CM and clinically defined sarcopenic obesity (SO). This null result likely stems from the structural limitations of current consensus definitions, which typically mandate low absolute lean mass as a prerequisite. As described above, CM survivors in our cohort tended to have preserved or elevated absolute lean mass due to significant body-size scaling, thereby “escaping” the low-mass threshold required for a binary SO diagnosis. This discrepancy highlights that standard geriatric definitions may be insufficiently sensitive for populations where muscle dysfunction manifests as “dynapenic obesity“ [42] or relative insufficiency rather than absolute muscle wasting. Consequently, for individuals with a history of adversity, clinical risk stratification should prioritize functional metrics and compositional ratios over absolute mass cut-offs to accurately capture this “hidden” burden.

Our findings underscore the pervasive impact of physical abuse, which exhibited the strongest associations with adverse body composition across both sexes. With global prevalence estimates for childhood physical abuse ranging between 18% and 23% [43], this form of “threat” exposure represents a critical, yet often overlooked, upstream driver of the adult obesity epidemic. Beyond general effects, we observed a striking sex-specific vulnerability: sexual abuse was uniquely and robustly associated with central adiposity in women, but not men. This sex-dimorphic pattern aligns with prior meta-analyses [12] and may be understood through distinct psychobiological frameworks. Biologically, females may exhibit heightened sensitivity to stress-induced HPA axis dysregulation, where elevated cortisol preferentially promotes visceral fat deposition [44]. Psychologically, the “Theory of Protective Obesity” suggests that for female survivors of sexual trauma, weight gain may subconsciously serve as a physical barrier or a means to “desexualize” the body to avoid future victimization [45]. Given that approximately 18% of women report childhood sexual abuse—a figure likely underestimated due to stigma [46]—these findings emphasize that obesity in mid-to-late life may, for many women, represent a somatic manifestation of unresolved developmental trauma. Consequently, obesity prevention and treatment strategies for women must move beyond lifestyle advice to incorporate trauma-informed care that addresses these deep-seated safety behaviors.

Beyond comparing individual domains, our results suggest heterogeneity by adversity type: associations appeared stronger for abuse than neglect, and for physical than emotional adversities. This pattern aligns with the “Dimensional Model of Adversity,” which posits that threat-related experiences (e.g., physical abuse) and deprivation-related experiences (e.g., neglect) influence development through distinct neurobiological pathways [47]. Threat exposure is hypothesized to chronically activate the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system, leading to sustained hypercortisolemia and systemic inflammation [48]. Since visceral adipose tissue is biologically active and uniquely sensitive to glucocorticoids, this “threat-induced” stress physiology may directly drive the preferential accumulation of central adiposity, consistent with the robust associations we observed between physical abuse and VAT [49]. In contrast, deprivation exposure is thought to primarily impact the development of cortical networks involved in executive function and reward processing [47]. Consequently, the impact of neglect on body composition may operate more predominantly through behavioral dysregulation rather than the direct neuroendocrine-metabolic forcing observed in physical abuse. These distinctions underscore that CM is not monolithic; distinguishing between the physiological toxicity of threat and the developmental deficits of deprivation provides a critical framework for contextualizing downstream metabolic risk.

Our analyses suggested that unhealthy lifestyle and depressive symptoms may be relevant correlates of the observed associations between CM burden and later-life body composition. In addition to chronic stress responses, individuals exposed to maltreatment may be more likely to adopt socially patterned health behaviors as coping strategies [50]. Smoking, in particular, may play a pivotal role; beyond functioning as a maladaptive regulator of emotional distress, nicotine exposure exerts direct catabolic effects on skeletal muscle and potentiates cortisol-driven visceral adiposity. Such behaviors could plausibly contribute to the observed less favorable body-composition profile, characterized by higher adiposity and lower MFRs. Prior evidence also links CM to later brain development and mental health vulnerability, including a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms [51, 52]. Consistent with this literature, incorporating depressive symptoms into our models was associated with an attenuation of the maltreatment–body-composition associations, with the estimated proportion attenuated reaching up to ~ 31.6% in some outcomes. The contemporaneous assessment of mediators precludes causal inference; thus, these results are hypothesis-generating and warrant longitudinal confirmation.

Strengthened by a large-scale imaging cohort and robust sensitivity analyses, our findings underscore the dual necessity of upstream prevention targeting childhood environments and downstream, trauma-informed care for adults. These results advocate for integrating mental health support and lifestyle interventions to mitigate the long-term somatic sequelae of adversity, highlighting the importance of multi-level strategies that span from early protection to late-life risk mitigation. Several limitations merit consideration. First, CM, health behaviors, and depressive symptoms were self-reported, introducing recall and common-method bias. To the extent that misclassification is predominantly non-differential, associations—and any attenuation patterns used to describe mediation—would be expected to bias toward the null; however, differential recall cannot be excluded and could bias estimates in less predictable directions. Second, mediators and DXA outcomes were assessed contemporaneously, which precludes causal inference about pathways; the mediation analyses should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, with alternative explanations such as reverse causation, residual confounding, and measurement error remaining plausible. Third, we lacked information on the timing, severity, and chronicity of CM, which likely reduces exposure resolution and may dilute associations and obscure heterogeneity across adversity profiles. Fourth, selection and survivorship bias are possible. The UK Biobank imaging cohort is not a simple random sample of the full UK Biobank and may over-represent healthier individuals who were able and willing to attend imaging visits. Our analytic sample further excluded participants with missing CM measures and/or missing DXA outcomes. Multiple imputation was applied to covariate missingness within the analytic sample, but it does not address missing exposure/outcome data or selection into imaging. Together, these factors may influence both the magnitude and external generalizability of the observed associations.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to utilize a large population-based sample to demonstrate associations between CM and greater central and total adiposity as well as lower muscle-specific strength in mid-to-late life, with physical abuse showing the strongest associations. Additionally, sexual abuse exhibited particularly pronounced negative effects on women. Furthermore, our findings highlight the mediating roles of lifestyle and mental health, potentially guiding targeted intervention strategies for adults who have experienced CM.
