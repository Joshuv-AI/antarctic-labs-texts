Dual-modality imaging of immunofluorescence and imaging mass cytometry for whole-slide imaging and accurate segmentation
Authors: Eun Na Kim, Phyllis Zixuan Chen, Dario Bressan, Monika Tripathi, Ahmad Miremadi, Massimiliano di Pietro, Lisa M Coussens, Gregory J Hannon, Rebecca C Fitzgerald, Lizhe Zhuang, Young Hwan Chang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryImaging mass cytometry (IMC) is a powerful technique capable of detecting over 30 markers on a single slide. It has been increasingly used for single-cell-based spatial phenotyping in a wide range of samples. However, it only acquires a rectangle field of view (FOV) with a relatively small size and low image resolution, which hinders downstream analysis. Here, we reported a highly practical dual-modality imaging method that combines high-resolution immunofluorescence (IF) and high-dimensional IMC on the same tissue slide. Our computational pipeline uses the whole-slide image (WSI) of IF as a spatial reference and integrates small-FOV IMC into a WSI of IMC. The high-resolution IF images enable accurate single-cell segmentation to extract robust high-dimensional IMC features for downstream analysis. We applied this method in esophageal adenocarcinoma of different stages, identified the single-cell pathology landscape via reconstruction of WSI IMC images, and demonstrated the advantage of the dual-modality imaging strategy.Keywords: imaging mass cytometry, IMC, immunofluorescence, IF, esophageal adenocarcinoma, multimodal image registration, Barrett’s esophagus, multiplex imaging

Introduction

Understanding the cellular composition and its spatial distribution in tissue sections, termed “spatial biology,” is becoming increasingly important in a wide range of biological research fields.1 Spatial biology utilizes highly multiplexed tissue imaging (MTI) techniques that allow visualization and quantification of the spatially resolved expression of various protein markers at single-cell resolution in tissues by acquiring multiple images of the same tissue that correspond to different markers, followed by computational deconvolution of the image data into single-cell-based marker quantification. MTI enables detailed spatial characterization of individual cells along with the complex interaction between target cells and their microenvironments, such as cancer cells and their neighboring immune and stromal cells. However, every MTI has platform-specific limitations that pose a substantial computational challenge to overcome; specifically, critical parameters in MTI are the number of multiplexed biomarkers and spatial resolution, which are mainly determined by the platform hardware.

Optical microscope-based platforms such as cyclic immunofluorescence (t-CyCIF),2 multiplex IF (MxIF),3 multiplexed immunohistochemistry (mIHC),4 and codetection by indexing (CODEX)5 usually have a high imaging resolution (with 20×, the resolution is 0.35∼0.4 μm/pixel). However, all these platforms achieve multiplex, i.e., detection of multiple markers, via repeating the cycles of “antibody/marker incubation,” “imaging,” and “antibody/marker removal” in a sequential iterative process that usually takes 6 to 8 h per cycle. Also, t-CyCIF and mIHC require repeated heating and cooling of the slide, which causes microshrinkage or expansion or loss of tissue. Repeated heating and cooling of the slide does not apply to CODEX, as it uses a DMSO-based chemical stripping buffer to remove the barcoded fluorophore that is carried out at room temperature.5 However, like other sequential multiplex imaging methods, it still has the issue of residual markers of previous cycles that create false positives.5 The process, therefore, is prone to artifacts including autofluorescence (if detection is fluorescence based), microshrinkage, expansion or loss of the tissue, and batch effect, which are exacerbated by the increasing number of cycles and markers. It becomes a critical issue especially when processing larger tissues (on the scale of cm2), as the microshrinkage and expansion are becoming non-negligible, especially in platforms that use heat-induced removal of the previous marker. It results in grave difficulties in downstream image registration and introduces significant errors. Moreover, the presence of autofluorescence and variations introduced by experimental batch effects can introduce biases into the data, potentially obscuring the true underlying biological signals. To address these challenges, the application of normalization techniques becomes crucial in enhancing consistency among images and mitigating the impact of technical variations.6,7,8,9,10 For instance, innovative methods have been devised to identify negative control cells within each marker’s sample, utilizing their intensity levels to estimate background signals and effectively reduce intra-image variability.6 Additionally, statistical approaches initially designed for batch correction and image normalization can be customized to the intricacies of this complex MTI data.9,11,12 By doing so, systematic disturbances are minimized. However, normalization intensity across tissues is still a challenging and ongoing problem in MTI.

In contrast to the optical microscope-based MTI methods, mass spectrometry-based platforms such as imaging mass cytometry (IMC)13 and multiplexed ion beam imaging (MIBI)14 use metal isotope-conjugated antibodies and detect all markers in one round and raw images of all markers are acquired simultaneously. It is therefore free of the aforementioned artifacts that are caused by the repeated sequential cycles. Furthermore, these platforms have a significant advantage of low background signal, no autofluorescence, and low batch effect, as the marker detection is not based on fluorescence intensity but rather the time of flight (TOF) of the evaporating metal isotopes when the tissue is being ablated.13 However, IMC has a much lower image resolution (1 μm2 per pixel) that hampers accurate cell segmentation, resulting in inaccurate single-cell feature extraction and hindering accurate cell phenotyping.15,16 Baars and colleagues published a method that used high-resolution IF as a segmentation called MATISSE (imaging mass cytometry microscopy single-cell segmentation).17 However, this method is still restricted by the confined IMC field of view (FOV), as the acquisition capacity of the IMC platform is at the scale of mm2, and an area of cm2 will take hundreds of continuous hours to acquire and poses a significant risk of malfunction of the image processing software and the platform hardware. For larger cm2-sized tissue, the acquisition needs to be broken into a number of smaller separate rectangular FOVs, which greatly limits its application in studying large whole-tissue cellular landscapes, such as an entire tumor or an intact mouse cerebrum. We demonstrate that our approach is able to generate whole-slide images of IMC while providing the flexibility to select specific regions of interest (ROIs) based on disease stages and downstream analysis.

Here, we developed a comprehensive solution that not only significantly improved the accuracy of cell segmentation but also was able to reconstruct multiple separately acquired IMC FOVs to a single whole-slide image (WSI) so that the tissue’s cellular composition and spatial landscape could be studied as a whole. We demonstrated the method using endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) specimens that contained esophageal adenocarcinoma and adjacent precancerous Barrett’s esophagus. The results faithfully recapitulated the pathological transition of the cellular landscape between Barrett’s esophagus, dysplasia, and adenocarcinoma that was only possible to study as a whole in WSIs.

Results

Our method aimed to combine the advantages of IF and IMC by dual-modality imaging of the same tissue and then to reconstruct the small rectangle IMC FOVs to WSIs based on the IF reference to analyze the whole tissue with both high-dimensional and high-resolution imaging data (Figure 1A). To demonstrate its applicability in different tissue types, we stained large EMR tissues of early esophageal adenocarcinoma with adjacent precancerous Barrett’s esophagus, which represents various histologic features, including squamous and glandular epithelia and stromal tissue, and pathological progress from normal tissues to metaplastic precancerous lesion, dysplasia, and cancer in a large area measuring approximately 0.5 × 2 cm2 (Figure S1).

Experimental benchwork workflow for integrating dual-modality imaging

(A) The method principle: the same tissue sample was imaged for both IF and IMC. IF images provided a high-resolution image for accurate cell segmentation and whole-tissue reference, but only for up to three markers; IMC images provided a high-dimensional image dataset for complex spatial cell phenotyping but were acquired as separated small rectangle FOVs that depend on the tissue shape and size. We combined the advantages of the two imaging modalities with accurate cell segmentation and reconstruction of high-dimensional image data for whole tissue.

(B) Experimental procedure: the tissue slide was stained first for IF E-cadherin and CD45, followed by staining of 36 metal-tagged IMC antibodies. Then, a temporary mounting was performed to scan the IF images. IF optical scanning was performed to obtain high-resolution (0.3 μm per pixel), whole-area IF images. After dismounting and air drying, IMC markers were acquired as separate small rectangle FOVs (1 μm per pixel). The two datasets were then processed for cell segmentation and FOV stitching.

Staining and dual-modality imaging of IF and IMC

Briefly, we stained the slide for two rounds: first with the primary IF antibodies (E-cadherin, CD45) and second with the secondary IF antibodies and a cocktail of metal isotope-conjugated IMC antibodies (36 markers; Table S1). The slides were stained with DAPI before temporarily being mounted for whole-slide fluorescent scanning for IF markers, followed by dismount and air drying and acquisition of multiple small rectangle FOVs of IMC (Figure 1B; STAR Methods). The hands-on time for the benchwork was approximately 2 h, and the full preparation of the slide could be finished in 1 day.

Computational integration pipeline of IF and IMC images

In IMC, it is practical for large tissues that exceed several cm2 to be broken into smaller separate FOV images and acquired in separate batches, allowing hardware maintenance and “tuning” between each run. Therefore, large tissues are commonly analyzed as several separate image sets instead of a whole. To our knowledge, digitally stitching these separately acquired FOV IMC images to reconstruct the original large tissue images has not been reported because, in IMC, imaged tissues are destroyed during acquisition; therefore, there are no overlapping areas that could be used as references for stitching. In addition, stitching also cannot rely on the spatial coordinates of the individual IMC FOV images, as the slides cannot be unloaded and reloaded back to the original position without at least micrometer deviations.

Here, we developed a computational approach to map mm2-sized smaller FOV IMC images onto cm2-sized larger WSI IF images and then to register and stitch them together (Figure 2A). Briefly, we first determined the global coordinates of small-FOV IMC images by identifying high pixel-level correlation with the reference IF WSI and then refined the registration locally between small-FOV IMC and IF. By using our registration method (see STAR Methods), we successfully registered high-dimensional IMC images with 36 markers (Figure S2) onto WSI IF with a high subcellular resolution (Figure 2B).

Generation of customizable ROI masks from WSI IMC images

Once the whole tissue was reconstructed for both IF and IMC, we further registered the image with an adjacent slide stained for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The registered H&E image is important for clinical samples from which pathologists, who rely on H&E images to make the final diagnosis, are able to define disease-specific regions for research interest, for example, different pathological stages with any shape (Figure 3A). These areas could then be extracted from the WSI IMC images and individually studied for their cellular composition (Figure 3B).

Generation of accurate cell masks using registered IF

In order to establish accurate cell segmentation for low-resolution IMC images, we generated cell masks using MESMER, a deep-learning-based algorithm for nuclear and whole-cell segmentation,18 from the registered high-resolution IF using IF DAPI for the nuclear marker and IF E-cadherin for the epithelial cell membrane marker. We registered IF-based cell segmentation masks with IMC images so that we could extract high-dimensional, autofluorescence-free IMC marker intensity features from accurate cell segmentation boundaries for downstream analysis (Figure 4).

We then compared segmentation masks generated from high-resolution IF versus low-resolution IMC and found that the IF mask resulted in highly accurate demarcation of the true boundaries for both cell and nucleus across all tissue types and disease stages (Figures 5A and S3). In addition, cytoplasmic areas were more clearly delineated in IF-based cell segmentation than in IMC-based segmentation (Figure 5A).

We further compared the accuracy of IF-based and IMC-based segmentation masks by evaluating the inverted nucleus-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio in different disease statuses. Histomorphologically, the inverted N/C ratio decreases along with disease progression from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia and adenocarcinoma,19 which was faithfully recapitulated by the IF-based cell segmentation but not by the IMC-based segmentation (Figures 5B, S4A, and S4B), indicating that IF-based segmentation is sufficiently sensitive for subcellular phenotyping, especially for clinical samples.

The robustness of cell segmentation was further evaluated by biaxial plots of mutually exclusive marker pairs, such as PanCK (pan cytokeratin; epithelial cells) and αSMA (alpha smooth muscle cctin; vascular, fibroblast, smooth muscle cells), whereby double positivity indicates marker spillage that is usually caused by imperfect segmentation. Interestingly, both IF-based and IMC-based cell segmentation showed very low double positivity on three mutually exclusive marker pairs, PanCK/αSMA, PanCK/CD4, and PanCK/CD3 (Figure S4C). However, IF-based segmentation was able to detect 199,425 cells from the tested region, while the IMC-based segmentation detected 120,543 cells, which generally suggested that IF-based segmentation was more sensitive to distinguish individual cells that were particularly important when studying tissues with densely packed cells, such as tumor.

Finally, we evaluated the batch effect in IF and IMC by assessing the signals of the same markers that were acquired on different experiment dates. While each experiment was strictly controlled while staining and imaging were performed with the same parameters, we found that the batch effect was inevitable for IF markers when varied intensities were observed in different batches (Figure 6A). On the other hand, the signal intensity of IMC markers was highly consistent and free of batch effect (Figure 6B) in our case, indicating that IMC image data, even acquired in different batches, could be analyzed together with little need for batch correction compared with the other multiplex platforms,6,7 which highlights the advantage of IMC for studies with larger sample cohorts for which image data need to be acquired in multiple batches.

Accurate cell phenotyping results based on IF-based cell mask

We compared IF-based versus IMC-based segmentation in downstream analysis. Briefly, single cells extracted from the same high-dimensional image dataset using either IF-based or IMC-based segmentation were pooled together and analyzed for cell phenotyping using the GPU-boosted implementation of PhenoGraph (unsupervised clustering approach).7 All the cells were grouped into 19 clusters. Interestingly, IF-based segmentation was able to detect a higher number of cluster 5 (highlighted in Figure 7A). We chose cluster 5 as an example to compare cell subsets clustered using IMC segmentation versus those using IF segmentation and to visualize the cell subset’s in situ localization. Cluster 5 showed high PanCK and moderate CD1a and CCR7 expression. To further confirm the cell identity, we visualized the in situ distribution of cluster 5 using the open-source visualization tool Napari (https://github.com/napari/napari) (Figure 7B) and found that the cells, which were likely to be the Langerhans cells or a Langerhans cell subset, are enriched in the suprabasal layers in the squamous epithelium of the normal esophagus.20 It was clear that the high-resolution IF-based segmentation provided a more accurate cellular basis for high-dimensional downstream phenotyping when the details of cell location and morphology were maintained. It is important to recognize that IMC’s key issue lies in cell omission, not misclassifications, which holds significance for subsequent analysis. Our findings underscore the commendable approach of leveraging IF resolution for specific segmentation aspects.

Discussion

Summary of this study

In this study, we present a highly practical approach that integrates mass spectrometry-based IMC with optical microscope-based IF that exploits the technical advantages of both imaging platforms while overcoming their own limitations. Our method is capable of analyzing tissues of varying sizes and shapes with highly accurate cell segmentation (based on IF images) and high-dimensional and large-scale phenotyping (>30 markers based on WSI IMC images) without introducing autofluorescence or batch effects. By stitching smaller FOVs to obtain a larger WSI, our method overcomes the main limitation of IMC, which only acquires small rectangular FOVs with low resolution.

A practical solution of integrating IF into the IMC imaging system

Our staining protocol enables dual staining of IMC and IF on the same slide, which was enabled by a relatively simple tweak of the mounting method; also, our computational integration tool was designed based on a widely used open-access computational platform, which is easy and convenient for IMC users to employ our tool in their analysis. In addition, compared with metal-conjugated IMC antibodies, IF antibodies are highly available and widely validated. In our system, key markers could be detected by IF antibodies if the IMC antibodies are not available. Also, user-conjugated IMC antibodies with a new metal tag can be validated using the corresponding IF staining on the same slide, which facilitates the development of new antibody panels in IMC.

Advantages of large-size reconstructed IMC images

The registered and stitched WSI IMC images with adjacent H&E enable pathologists to examine and understand the semantic, structural, and spatial context with regard to the sequential progression of diseases, especially the subtle and transitional changes that appear across large areas. WSI IMC images also allow the selection of representative and customizable areas of the researcher’s interest instead of using small and rectangle FOVs of IMC images. The shape of an area of interest, such as a concave shape, can significantly affect spatial analysis, including neighborhood enrichment analysis and the shortest average distance. It is therefore important to have customizable area masks that avoid this bias. In addition, the cell population composition or cell number can be accurately compared for a meticulously drawn area via pathologic semantic evaluation from large WSI-level IMC, thus enabling robust comparison between different tumor microenvironments.

IF combination improved the accuracy of cell segmentation and downstream analysis

Although IMC has many advantages, it has a relatively low resolution (1 μm/pixel13) compared with high-resolution optical-based images such as CyCIF or MxIF (0.325 μm/pixel). Low-resolution images hamper accurate cell segmentation, induce lateral spillage of cell surface expression from neighboring cells, and result in inaccurate single-cell feature extraction. As a result, mutually exclusive markers are often detected in one cell (i.e., false double-positive cells), which hinders accurate cell phenotyping16 and subsequent downstream analysis. To avoid this issue, a recent study (REDSEA) proposed a new method for correcting cell phenotyping by assigning the signal in the cell boundary to each cell. However, there are still many difficulties in detecting accurate cell boundaries with low-resolution IMC images.16 In this regard, our results showed that IF-based cell segmentation lowered the rate of false double-positive cells (Figure S6; markers of PanCK and CD3 should be exclusively expressed).

Nuclear imaging of IF DAPI most effectively revealed the nuclear structure at high resolution, delineating nuclear contour and nucleus texture even in densely packed cells, intermixed immune cells, and membrane imaging of IF E-cadherin delineated stratified normal squamous epithelium without a nucleus (Figure 5A). IF DAPI is considered the empirical ground truth as the most widely used marker in nuclear segmentation and image registration.9 We were able to generate accurate nuclei and cell masks using high-resolution IF DAPI and E-cadherin images and register them with IMC images, which enabled us to analyze high-dimensional IMC images using accurate IF-based cell masks. It is possible to combine other IMC markers for better training of the segmentation algorithm, but it somewhat defeats the original purpose of IMC, which is to include more markers for research interest of cellular heterogeneity rather than segmentation. In addition, the primary constraint for IMC segmentation is the low resolution of the images rather than the number of markers utilized. It is more practical to include segmentation markers in high-resolution IF, which usually could accommodate four fluorescent channels.

There is a possibility that the poor IMC segmentation performance arises from poor E-cadherin staining in the IMC data, which might be caused by the competition of the IF and IMC E-cadherin antibodies. We therefore conducted an IMC-only staining within our cohort (Figures S5A–S5D), which showed faint staining that is comparable to the tissue stained by both IF-IMC. Furthermore, it is worth noting that the antibodies employed for E-cadherin in both IF and IMC experiments are different clones.

To further compare the segmentation performance between IF and IMC, we conducted nuclei segmentation based on nuclear images, which consistently exhibit high staining quality in both IMC and IF staining methods. The results distinctly demonstrated superior nuclear segmentation in IF images with higher image resolution (Figure S5E, left; 0.325 μm/pixel) compared with IMC images with lower resolution (Figure S5E, right; 1 μm/pixel). In summary, our study places significant emphasis on the observation that cell/nuclear segmentation is less accurate in images with lower resolution, such as 1 μm/pixel in IMC. This issue may become more pronounced in tissues characterized by compact cellularity but could be improved by using high-resolution images for segmentation.

A necessity for alignment to tile IMC

When stitching multiple ROIs from IMC, it is necessary to register them against a WSI, which in our case is the IF WSI. This is because although IMC data acquisition provides coordinates for ROIs within the same run under the current Hyperion platform, these coordinates do not remain consistent if the slide is removed and reinserted into the machine. Good practice for IMC equipment involves shutting down the machine overnight once a week. Upon restarting, a tuning slide needs to be inserted for calibration. Consequently, when acquiring data for large tissue areas (e.g., a few cm2) that may require hundreds of hours, the area has to be divided into smaller ROIs across different runs. Therefore, stitching these IMC ROIs cannot rely solely on IMC coordinates due to the slide being taken out and reinserted. In our method, IMC ROIs are registered to the IF image of the whole tissue, which serves as the sole ground truth for registering ROIs acquired in different IMC runs. This approach enables the acquisition of large tissue areas spanning several cm2 and provides researchers with flexibility in acquiring IMC ROIs in different runs.

It is worth noting that the bright-field image of the tissue (called “panorama” in the Fluidigm software), the dried and unmounted tissue stained with IMC metal antibodies, does not exhibit visible cellular features under bright-field illumination (Figure S6) and thus cannot serve as the WSI or the ground truth for registering IMC ROIs.

Comparison with other multiplexed methods using integrated imaging modalities

Multiplex imaging is a rapidly developing field. A few new spatial multiplexed technologies have integrated multiple platforms to improve research outcomes. NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling21 uses sequencing instead of an imaging-based method to analyze RNA or protein (oligo-tagged antibodies). IF imaging is used to define specific ROIs, such as pan-cytokeratin-stained epithelial areas or CD45-stained immune cell areas, from which multiplexed tags and/or oligos could be retrieved and sequenced; therefore, spatial profiling is achieved. It is noteworthy that the spatial region GeoMx can analyze is 40–800 μm, which, while theoretically possible, it is not intended for single-cell-based analysis, and it is also not practical for the platform to detect a large volume of single-cell-based data. A recent study attempted to reach single-cell level using GeoMx21; however, the results demonstrated the detection of 1.3, 2, and 3.7 cells, but these were not truly at the single-cell level. Furthermore, detecting a single cell in GeoMx requires processing and sequencing the probes from an individual ROI representing one cell. Therefore, the number of single cells, which cannot practically exceed the level of thousands, that could be detected is a bottleneck for GeoMx, while in other image-based platforms, there is no technical limitation, and usually hundreds of thousands of cells were detected and analyzed.

MATISSE17 used a similar concept that integrated IF with IMC to increase image resolution for cell segmentation. However, it used only one nuclear marker, and the analysis was restricted to one single rectangle FOV in IMC. The segmentation was done by only the nuclear IF marker (DAPI) using a CellProfiler pipeline. Cells were identified by the identification of individual nuclei and secondly by a simple expansion of these nuclei to the full extent of the cells. Here, we used deep-learning-based segmentation (MESMER) that takes not only a nuclear marker but also a membrane marker and is designed for whole-cell segmentation of tissue images with human-level performance using large-scale data annotation including IMC data. We compared MATISSE and our approach to demonstrate segmentation performance (Figure S7), from which we can clearly observe that the cell boundary from MATISSE does not capture the accurate boundary of tumor cells (E-cadherin positive). Also, for other cell types (i.e., E-cadherin negative), due to the simple expansion (10 pixels), it captures the neighborhood cell’s membrane, which causes spillover issues for cell phenotyping. In addition, for larger tissue that requires registration, completing segmentation on the WSI improves the accuracy of the segmentation, especially when there are tiling artifacts from IMC images (i.e., cells or nuclei are on the edge of two FOVs).

In the current study, we used E-cadherin for epithelial cells; researchers could apply different markers depending on their research needs, such as lineage and/or membrane markers, which could greatly facilitate downstream cell segmentation, or specific markers of interest that are not available in IMC. It is also noteworthy that our method allows the reconstruction of large whole tissues of high-resolution IF and high-dimensional IMC images with a registered H&E image. This is a great advantage in studying large clinical tissues where detailed pathological review is needed.

Advantage of the integration of IF and IMC modality in terms of imaging processing time

Our new integration method is time efficient in acquiring image data for WSIs. In optical microscope-based platforms such as t-CyCIF,2 the maximal number of antibodies for one cycle is usually 3–5 markers, and one channel should be a nuclei staining such as DAPI, as it is required for image registration. Therefore, for staining a large tissue with 36 markers, 9 rounds with 4 antibodies each are needed. Moreover, as a stripping-out process is needed between scanning and new staining during the cycles, it takes multiple days for image acquisition.22,23

In this regard, IMC has a significant advantage over cyclic multiplexing imaging in terms of image acquisition time and automation. Unlike the cyclic imaging modality, IMC is an all-in-one staining and an all-in-one imaging method using a single master mix of metal-conjugated primary antibodies and does not require repeated sequential steps such as antibody staining, image acquisition, and antibody removal.24 A complete set of antibodies with 30–40 markers can be applied simultaneously, and there is no need for manual intervention during the acquisition. In addition, there is no tissue loss or microexpansion issue from repeated antibody removal steps. As the hands-on time for the benchwork is approximately 2 h, acquiring multiple FOVs for WSI imaging can be completed within one day (time for acquiring 1 × 1 mm area is 2 h in Hyperion and only 1 h in Hyperion Plus); in our case, about 1.5 days were needed for the entire process of IMC imaging for a whole slide with 36 antibody markers.

In addition, it has been known to researchers in the field that slides stained with IMC can be stored for a long time with little degradation. We have compared IMC signals acquired from a slide that was stored for 2 years after being stained with IMC metal antibodies. There was no obvious reduction in the intensity of IMC signals (Figure S6D).

Furthermore, by utilizing IF and/or the registered H&E image of the whole slide, researchers can potentially identify and define specific ROIs, such as precancerous areas and immune hot areas, even further saving the total amount of IMC imaging needed and reducing the data acquisition time.

Limitations of the study

There are limitations in this study that are also shared by many spatial multiplexed imaging technologies whereby cells, which are three-dimensional, round, and voluminous, are cut with a microtome into two-dimensional planes for imaging analysis. This inevitably leads to variations in the observed morphology, depending on which plane the cells are cut from. Therefore, there was a limitation in fully evaluating the cell morphology, such as the N/C ratio, even under high-resolution images. This may be resolved by further development of three-dimensional spatial imaging technologies.

It is possible the performance of the IMC E-cadherin antibody is affected by the IF E-cadherin antibody, which binds to the antigen first as described in the protocol, which subsequently affects the segmentation performance of using IMC images. Although the two antibody clones recognize different regions of E-cadherin, we are not absolutely sure because the exact target epitope sequence of one clone is proprietary. We have compared IMC E-cadherin images of the same region that were acquired from the IMC-only method, i.e., no IF antibodies were used, versus the IF-IMC method as described here, and there were no obvious differences. We suggest using IF and IMC antibodies that do not have overlapping target epitopes, which will affect the performance of the IMC antibodies that are applied after the IF antibodies. In addition, we employed only two channels in Mesmer-powered cell segmentation, i.e., E-cadherin and DNA; while we could include more IMC markers for cell segmentation that may have similar performance as IF image-based segmentation, this might stray from IMC’s primary purpose of exploring cellular complexity. This underscores the need for researchers to consider the intended purpose and scope of IMC when making decisions regarding the selection of input channels and markers for segmentation algorithms.

Experimental model and study participant details

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the East of England - Cambridge Central Research Ethics Committee. The patients/participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

We used endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) samples from a pilot cohort of early EAC with Barrett’s metaplasia (n = 3) and normal esophagus (n = 2) from tissue donations. The dataset has been anonymized to ensure patient privacy and confidentiality. Due to data anonymization, specific gender and age information cannot be provided. The EMR tissues contain sequential stages of tumorigenesis including normal esophageal squamous epithelium (NSQ), Barrett intestinal metaplasia (NDBE), low-and-high grade dysplasia (Dys), and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC, Figure S1). The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committees and all subjects provided informed consent for the use of their tissue samples for research purposes (REC 01/149).

Method details

Pathology diagnosis for Barrett’s esophagus

Surgical pathologists independently assessed the pathology as described in our previous study.26 The H&E slides were scanned and printed out. Two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists (M.T. and A.M.) independently assessed the H&E slides using the definitions and histological criteria for NSQ, NDBE, Dys, and EAC recommended by The Royal College of Pathologists27 and guidelines by the British Society of Gastroenterology on the diagnosis and management of Barrett’s esophagus.28 High- and low-grade dysplasia were grouped together as dysplasia.

Antibody and off-target binding issue

We used three IF antibodies (CD45, E-Cadherin, Pan-cytokeratin) and 36 IMC antibodies targeting lineage markers (Pan-cytokeratin, E-Cadherin, Alpha-SMA, Col1, CD31), cell status markers (P53, Ki67), and immune cell phenotyping markers (CD45RO, CD3, CD4, CCR3, CCR7, CD56, CD8a, Granzyme B, ICOS, TIM3, LAG-3, CTLA4, FoxP3, PD-1, CD20, CD38, CD11b, CD16, Mast cell tryptase, CD11c, CD14, HLA-DR, CD68, CD163, Mannose Receptor, CD1a, CSF-1-R, PD-L1, VISTA, Table S1). Regarding concerns about the off-target binding of the secondary antibody to the same isotype target present in the cocktail, we did not observe any instances of off-target binding by the IF secondary antibody. For example, the IF secondary antibody that detects E-Cadherin (goat anti-mouse IgG1) does not bind to any mouse IgG1 antibodies in the IMC cocktail, which includes: CD1a, PanKeratin, FoxP3, CD163, CD68, CD8a, Ki67. These markers have very different localization from E-Cadherin, it will be very obvious if off-target binding occurs, and we did not observe any.

Antibody validation

A full panel validation was performed.24 All antibodies underwent extensive validation prior to multiplexing. A pathologist visually evaluated and validated the staining pattern of all IMC and IF antibodies by comparing the alleged positive and negative controls from the human protein atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org). We assessed whether single-plex IHC using identical antibodies and 36-plex imaging mass cytometry analysis yielded the corresponding results13 between unlabeled and metal-labeled antibodies. Antibodies were individually validated to verify target specificity.

New mounting technique for dual-modality imaging

We developed a new staining/mounting technique that allows both IMC (36 markers) and IF (DAPI/CD45/E-Cadherin) in the same slide section without harming/destroying the tissue. The proposed approach consists of a benchwork for image data acquisition and a computational pipeline for image data integration. The benchwork (Figure 1A) is a practical tweak of the IF and IMC staining protocols in which the slide is stained for three rounds of antibodies in the order of primary cell surface marker antibodies (E-Cadherin in this study), secondary fluorescent antibodies (Alexa Fluor 647 in this study) and a cocktail of metal isotope-conjugated IMC antibodies (36 markers in this study). The slides are temporarily mounted for whole-slide fluorescent scanning, followed by dismounting and air drying; the dried slides can be stored for months at 4°C. In addition, depending on the slide scanner, two more markers can usually be added for other cell types, such as CD45 for immune cells or CD31 for endothelial cells. The hands-on time for the benchwork is approximately 2 h. For optimal results, we recommend incubating the primary antibodies at 4°C. However, the incubation may be reduced to 1 to 2 h per round so that full preparation of the slide could be finished in one day.

IF staining

Dewax and antigen retrieval were performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) EMR tissues section as described.26 The slides were blocked by 5% BSA in PBS for 45 min at room temperature, followed by incubation of IF primary antibodies of CD45 (#13917, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:100) and E-Cadherin (#14472, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:100) that diluted in PBS-TB (PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20 and 0.05% BSA) overnight at 4C. The slides were then washed in PBS for 3 × 5 min, followed by incubation of IF secondary antibodies for CD45 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21428, 1:200) and E-Cadherin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A21240, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature. It was advised to prepare all buffers and reagents in plastic containers instead of glass bottles to avoid contamination of metal traces.

IMC antibody staining with 36 markers

The slides were then washed for 3 × 5 min and stained for IMC antibody cocktail that was prepared in PBS-TB overnight at 4C. Note, the IMC antibodies were applied after the IF secondary antibodies.

Temporary mounting

The slides were washed for 3 × 5 min and incubated with Intercalator-Ir (Cell-ID, 201192B, Standard BioTools Inc, 1:400) and DAPI (final concentration of 0.5 μg/mL) in PBS-TB for 5 min at room temperature. The slides were briefly washed once in PBS and mounted in 5% BSA in PBS. The edge of the coverslips was sealed with rubber cement (29010017000, Marabu Fixogum) and then air dried for 20 min.

IF image acquisition

The slides were then scanned in Zeiss Axio Scan Z1 for IF images for CD45, E-Cadherin, and DAPI. In this study, we chose a 20X objective for an image resolution of 0.325 μm per pixel. The image acquisition took approximately 45 min for one EMR tissue with a 0.5 × 1 cm size. 40x objective for a resolution of 0.163 μm per pixel was also available. The IF images were exported as TIFF format using ZenLite software.

Dismount

The dried rubber cement was stripped using forceps and the slides were submerged in PBS-T (PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20) to dismount the coverslips. The slides were then washed in PBS-T for 3 min and then in Milli-Q water for 3 min. The slides were then air-dried for 15 min at room temperature and stored at 4C until IMC acquisition.

IMC data acquisition and processing (1 μm per pixel)

Images were acquired using the Hyperion Imaging System (Standard BioTools Inc, previously Fluidigm). Each field of view (FOV), which was an adjacent plane of the EMR section, was ablated with a laser (400 Hz).

Stitching & registration of IMC images to IF images

We developed a two-step approach for the stitching and registering of dual-modality imaging data. For each small FOV of IMC, the normalized cross-correlation of the IMC DNA image and IF DAPI is first calculated. The peak of the cross-correlation matrix identifies the location where the small FOV IMC image is best correlated with the subregion of WSI IF. Then, feature-based image registration is performed by identifying matched features from each imaging modality.29 Each FOV image can be mapped onto WSI IF and stitched together. High-resolution of the segmentation mask from IF image is also registered by matching the resolution of the IMC image.

Note that horizontal line artifacts between each FOV image are from the gaps between IMC FOV as shown in Figures S6B and S6C. When stitched back, these gaps between IMC FOVs are seen as thin black lines. We have also acquired ROIs that were overlapped and without gaps (Figure S6C). Because our registration algorithm solely relies on the IF whole slide image, any IMC FOVs regardless of gaps or overlaps, or their localizations could be registered back to the whole tissue, demonstrating the robustness of our method.

Visualization

The registered IF images (n = 3) and IMC images (n = 36) were simultaneously visualized by open-source visualization software, Napari (https://github.com/napari/napari, Figure S2).

Single-cell segmentation and downsampling

We used state-of-the-art deep learning-based segmentation (MESMER)18 that takes not only a nuclear marker but also a membrane marker and is designed for whole-cell segmentation of tissue images with human-level performance using large-scale data annotation including IMC data. In our specific implementation, we combined a nuclear marker, which is DAPI for immunofluorescence (IF) imaging and DNA for imaging mass cytometry (IMC). Alongside this, we use E-Cadherin as illustrated in Figure 4 for membrane marker. This strategic integration of nuclear and membrane markers allowed MESMER to achieve a holistic understanding of cell structures, enabling the accurate and comprehensive segmentation of cells.

Following the acquisition of nuclei and cell segmentation masks from high-resolution immunofluorescence (IF) imaging, our workflow progresses to downsampling through nearest-neighbor interpolation post-image registration. Using open-source Python libraries, we apply a scaling factor of 0.325 to the IF images (given IMC_scan = 1.0 μm/pixel and IF_scan = 0.325 μm/pixel) to align them with the resolution of the IMC images. Subsequent to this, the Pillow library’s resize function (PIL.Image.NEAREST interpolation) is employed to maintain pixel values through nearest-neighbor interpolation.

By integrating the downscaled IF images and masks with the IMC images, we facilitate seamless comparisons and analyses between the two distinct imaging modalities. This integration substantially enriches our understanding of cellular and molecular landscapes within our study. Comprehensive details, including code and supplementary materials, can be accessed via our GitHub repository (https://github.com/enkim/IMC-IF, 04_mask tile merge 0325.ipynb).

Feature extraction

We used a cell segmentation mask made from IF images to obtain the mean intensity of high-dimensional IMC image sets (Figure 4). By applying the newly selected region of interest corresponding to each disease status (Figures 3A and 3B), we extracted the mean intensity of 36 IMC antibodies using a single cell mask generated from high-resolution IF images (Figure 4E). Feature extraction was performed using the Skimage python library (https://scikit-image.org). More cells were segmented using IF masks (DAPI, E-Cadherin, total: 199,425 cells) than by using IMC masks (DNA, E-Cadherin, total: 120,643 cells).

Unsupervised clustering

We performed cell phenotyping from the single-cell data using a GPU-boosted implementation of PhenoGraph7 (Figure 7A).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Segmentation comparisons were performed by a pathologist, Eun Na Kim. The analysis involved evaluating the accuracy and consistency of cell segmentation results obtained from different imaging modalities. Eun Na Kim assessed the segmentation outcomes by visually inspecting the segmented cells and regions of interest on both immunofluorescence (IF) and imaging mass cytometry (IMC) images. The segmentation comparison process included the following steps.

Visual inspection

Eun Na Kim reviewed the segmented cells and boundaries generated by both IF and IMC methods. She examined individual cells and cell clusters to determine the accuracy of segmentation and whether the boundaries aligned with the actual cellular structures.

Boundary agreement

The pathologist assessed the agreement between the boundaries of segmented cells in IF and IMC images. Cells that were correctly segmented and exhibited consistent boundaries in both imaging modalities were considered to have a high degree of agreement.

Quantitative assessment

In addition to visual inspection, quantitative metrics such as the inverted nucleus-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio in different disease statuses were calculated to provide objective measures of segmentation performance. We also evaluated by biaxial plots of mutually exclusive marker pairs whereby double positivity indicates marker spillage that is usually caused by imperfect segmentation.

Comparative analysis

Eun Na Kim compared the segmentation results from IF and IMC across multiple samples and regions of interest. She identified patterns of agreement, differences, and potential sources of variation between the two methods.
