Nano-silymarin combined with methylene blue photodynamic therapy versus monotherapies of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: in-vitro study
Authors: Delband Hefzi, Maryam Koopaie, Neda Hakimiha, Shima Younespour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) presents significant therapeutic challenges due to its aggressiveness and the morbidity of conventional treatments. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) offers a localized approach, but its efficacy can be limited. This study investigated the potential of nano-silymarin, a bioactive flavonolignan complex with known anticancer properties, in combination with methylene blue-mediated PDT (MB-PDT) in HNSCC cells.MethodsThe cytotoxic, apoptotic, and anti-migratory effects of various treatments were assessed in HN5 cells. Groups included controls, individual agents (nano-silymarin, MB, laser), dual therapies, and the triple combination (nano-silymarin + MB + laser). Cell viability was measured using the MTT assay, apoptosis by flow cytometry, and migration by the scratch assay. Results were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Synergy was quantified using the Bliss independence, Loewe additivity, and zero interaction potency models.ResultsThe triple combination (nano-silymarin + MB-PDT) demonstrated the most potent effect, synergistically reducing cell viability to 40.63% (compared with 69.65% for nano-silymarin and 77.32% for MB-PDT). It induced the highest level of total apoptosis (42.93%) and nearly completely inhibited cell migration (97.2% wound openness at 72 h). This anti-migratory effect was significantly greater than the sum of the effects of the individual monotherapies, indicating a synergistic blockade of cellular invasion. Synergy analysis confirmed a strong synergistic interaction, with a Combination index of 0.682.ConclusionNano-silymarin significantly potentiates the anticancer efficacy of MB-PDT against HNSCC cells through strong synergistic interactions, thereby enhancing cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and migration inhibition. These findings support further development of nano-silymarin in combinatorial PDT regimens for HNSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16422-3.

Background

Squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (HNSCC) represents the most prevalent malignancy within the head and neck region, accounting for more than 90% of tumors in this anatomical area [1]. It is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where it is associated with poor prognosis and high rates of locoregional recurrence [2]. Management of HNSCC typically involves surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy based on disease stage [3]. However, these modalities are often accompanied by substantial functional and aesthetic morbidity, including impairments in swallowing, speech, and facial integrity, which markedly diminish quality of life [4]. Furthermore, intrinsic or acquired resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy frequently facilitates locoregional relapse and nodal metastasis [5, 6], and despite technological advances, long-term overall survival has improved only marginally [7, 8]. These limitations highlight the need for localized, tumor-selective therapeutic strategies with reduced toxicity to surrounding tissues and the capacity for repeated application, features that have brought photodynamic therapy (PDT) to increasing clinical attention [9].

PDT is based on the combined action of a photosensitizing agent, light of an appropriate wavelength, and molecular oxygen, resulting in the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce targeted tumor cell death. Methylene blue (MB), a phenothiazinium-based photosensitizer with strong absorption in the red region of the visible spectrum, is capable of generating ROS and free radicals upon irradiation [10–12]. Both in-vitro and in-vivo studies have demonstrated its substantial antitumor activity [13–15]. In experimental models of HNSCC and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), MB-mediated PDT markedly reduces cell viability, induces apoptosis and necrosis, and suppresses migratory and invasive capacities [16, 17]. Investigations have attributed these effects to modulation of matrix metalloproteinases and alterations in the expression of cell-adhesion-related molecules following methylene blue-mediated PDT (MB-PDT) [18], suggesting a mechanistic basis for diminished local invasiveness and reduced metastatic potential [14]. Additional advantages, including the possibility of repeated treatment sessions, preservation of critical anatomical structures, and improved functional and cosmetic outcomes [19, 20], make PDT an attractive option for both primary and recurrent head-and-neck lesions.

Despite these benefits, challenges such as limited light penetration depth [21, 22], heterogeneous intratumoral distribution of photosensitizers [23, 24], and the persistence of treatment-resistant cellular subpopulations [25, 26] can lead to incomplete responses and locoregional recurrence. Consequently, growing interest has focused on combining PDT with chemotherapeutic agents [27], using nanoparticles [28, 29], molecular-targeted inhibitors [30, 31], or natural compounds [28, 32, 33] with antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic properties to enhance therapeutic synergy [34].

Silymarin, a complex of flavonolignans, including silibinin, silychristin, and silydianin, derived from Silybum marianum, has long been recognized for its antioxidant and hepatoprotective properties [35, 36]. More recently, extensive cellular and animal studies have revealed that silymarin, particularly silibinin, can inhibit cell-cycle progression, modulate proliferative signaling pathways, and trigger apoptosis in various malignant cell types [37–39]. Evidence further indicates that silymarin exerts anticancer effects in colorectal [40, 41], breast [42, 43], lung [44, 45], and liver malignancies [46] by attenuating oxidative stress, suppressing inflammatory mediators, regulating transcription factors, and modulating the expression of apoptosis-related proteins [47]. In several epithelial cancer models, silymarin also inhibits migration and invasion by downregulating genes associated with extracellular matrix remodeling and epithelial–mesenchymal transition [48–50].

Despite these promising biological effects, the therapeutic application of conventional silymarin is limited by its poor aqueous solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid metabolism [51, 52]. To overcome these limitations, nanotechnology‑based delivery systems have been developed to improve the pharmacological performance of silymarin [53]. Nano‑formulations of silymarin (nano-silymarin) have been shown to enhance the solubility, stability, cellular uptake, and bioavailability of the compound compared with its conventional form [51, 54]. Several studies have demonstrated that nano-silymarin exhibits improved biological activity, including enhanced antioxidant and anticancer effects, which may be attributed to improved cellular penetration and sustained drug release provided by nanocarrier systems [55–57]. Moreover, nano‑based delivery platforms can facilitate more efficient interaction of bioactive compounds with cellular targets and may enhance therapeutic efficacy in combination treatment strategies [58, 59]. Therefore, nano-silymarin has emerged as a promising formulation for improving the biological effectiveness of silymarin in biomedical and cancer‑related applications. Although direct studies regarding the effect of silymarin on HNSCC are relatively limited [60, 61], biological similarities with other epithelial malignancies provide a strong rationale for evaluating their therapeutic potential in this context.

The combination of PDT with phytochemicals has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance selectivity, reduce required doses, and potentiate antitumor efficacy [62–64]. Natural compounds with antioxidant and pro-apoptotic activity may sensitize tumor cells to PDT-induced oxidative stress, inhibit survival and repair pathways, and thereby intensify cytotoxicity while diminishing clonogenic potential [65, 66]. Additionally, combining PDT with phytochemicals may shift the toxicity profile toward normal tissues, as metabolic differences between malignant and healthy cells may yield greater tumor specificity [67, 68].

Despite increasing research on combinatorial PDT approaches, a systematic examination of MB-PDT combined with phytochemicals such as silymarin remains scarce. Most preclinical studies have focused on optimizing technical PDT parameters (photosensitizer concentration, light dose, drug–light interval) [69, 70] or combining PDT with conventional chemotherapeutic agents [71, 72]. Although reports have demonstrated the capacity of MB-PDT to reduce viability and migration in OSCC and HNSCC models [10, 14, 15], there is a notable lack of structured, comparative in-vitro investigations assessing the potential synergistic effects of silymarin and MB-PDT on apoptosis, viability, and migration. This knowledge gap provides strong scientific justification for designing a study that systematically compares the biological outcomes of combined nano-silymarin + MB-PDT therapy with either monotherapy across key tumorigenic parameters in HNSCC. To evaluate potential synergistic interactions between the applied treatments, the Bliss independence model, the Loewe additivity model (implemented through the Chou–Talalay method), and the Zero Interaction Potency (ZIP) model. These analytical frameworks are commonly applied to determine whether the combined effect of two or more agents is synergistic, additive, or antagonistic. Therefore, in the present study, these models were used to assess the interaction between nano-silymarin, methylene blue, and photodynamic therapy.

Such a study may clarify whether the addition of nano-silymarin can potentiate MB-PDT, induce apoptotic responses, reduce residual survival and clonogenicity of treatment-resistant cell subsets, and exert enhanced inhibitory effects on cell migration and potentially on invasion-related molecules. The findings would not only address a critical gap in the current literature but also lay the groundwork for subsequent in-vivo investigations and, ultimately, clinical translation into combination protocols for patients with HNSCC.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The protocol of this experimental and interventional in-vitro study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.TUMS.DENTISTRY.REC.1400.201). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and this study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [73].

Cell lines, culture, and treatment conditions

The HN5 cell line (code: NCBI, C196) was purchased from the National Cell Bank of Iran, affiliated with the Pasteur Institute of Iran (Tehran, Iran) (Supplementary File 1). Cancer cell lines were maintained and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The DMEM medium was buffered with sodium bicarbonate (3.7 g/L), which required the addition of 5.0–10.0% CO₂ to maintain physiological pH levels in the incubator. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity in an incubator for 24 h. The DMEM used was enriched with amino acids, salts including calcium chloride and magnesium sulfate, glucose, and vitamins such as riboflavin and folic acid. The necessary chemicals and reagents have been obtained from Merck Company (Merck, Germany). After 24 h of incubation, human HNSCC cells were subcultured into groups for evaluation of the treatment phases.

Nano-silymarin concentration determination

Silymarin was prepared as nanomicelles using the commercial formulation Sinalive (SinaLive; Exir Nano Pharmaceutical Company, Tehran, Iran). Silymarin was encapsulated in spherical nanomicelles with a diameter of 10 nanometers in the lipid-loving section. This formulation involves encapsulating silymarin into nanomicelles to enhance its solubility and bioavailability, thereby facilitating delivery and improving therapeutic efficacy. The nanomicelles were prepared according to the manufacturer’s guidelines and stored under appropriate conditions before being used in the experiment. Cells were exposed to different concentrations of nano-silymarin (50, 100, 150, and 200 µM), cultured, and then subjected to the MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) [74]. To determine the optimal concentrations of nano-silymarin and methylene blue and to investigate the cytotoxicity of these treatments on HNSCC cell survival, we employed the MTT assay. The MTT assay measures cell viability by measuring the reduction of MTT to purple formazan crystals by metabolically active cells. The cells were incubated for 24 h with the respective treatments, and the absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer. The spectrophotometer measures the light absorbed by the sample solution, allowing quantification of cell viability. The optimal concentration of nano-silymarin was determined based on the inhibitory concentration at 50% inhibition (IC50).

Methylene blue concentration determination

To determine the optimal concentration of methylene blue, cells were incubated with various concentrations of the photosensitizer (0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, and 3.2 µg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C [15, 75]. Cells were subsequently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to an MTT assay. The concentration of methylene blue (MB) yielding 50% cell viability was determined to be 1.6 µg/mL [10, 15].

Laser treatment protocol

For the laser-treated group, cells were irradiated with a 660 nm diode laser (Konftec, Taiwan) delivering a power density of 150 mW/cm². The total energy density applied was 45 J/cm² over an exposure time of 150 s. These parameters, wavelength (660 nm), power density (150 mW/cm²), energy density (45 J/cm²), and exposure duration, were selected in accordance with established in-vitro photodynamic therapy protocols previously described in the literature [10]. After 24 h of incubation, human HNSCC cells were subcultured into ten treatment groups, in which apoptosis, cell viability, and cell migration were assessed across all groups. For the combination-treatment groups, nano-silymarin and methylene blue (MB) were added simultaneously to the cells and incubated for 4 h prior to laser irradiation (for PDT groups). No sequential pre-treatment was used. All experiments were independently repeated three times on different days using freshly prepared cell cultures and solutions (biological replicates), and each biological experiment included three technical replicates per condition. The data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD).

Apoptosis and viability assays

Cell viability percentages were then assessed using the MTT assay. Additionally, apoptosis induction was assessed using flow cytometry. This technique relies on light scattering and fluorescence emission by the cells, using fluorescent dyes that bind to cellular components such as DNA. In this study, apoptosis was quantified as the percentage of apoptotic cells induced under each treatment condition after 48 h. Flow cytometry provides detailed, quantitative analysis of the cell population, distinguishing between viable, apoptotic, and necrotic cells. Apoptosis analysis was performed using an Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit. After treatment, HN5 cells were collected, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a concentration of approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells per sample. Each sample was stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation, 400 µL binding buffer was added, and the samples were immediately analyzed using flow cytometry to quantify viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells.

Cell migration assay: scratch test

Cell migration was evaluated using the scratch assay. Cells were seeded into well plates and allowed to form a confluent monolayer. A sterile needle was used to create a uniform scratch, mimicking the wound healing process. The cells were then treated with the respective drugs or therapies. The gap width was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment using bright-field microscopy to assess cell migration into the scratch area. Scratch width was quantified at each time point using image analysis software (ImageJ, version 1.52, USA; RRID: SCR_003070).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 27; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA; RRID: SCR_002865) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0, San Diego, CA; RRID: SCR_002798). The normality of data distribution was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A two-sided test with a significance level set at p-value (p) ≤ 0.05 was done, and all results are given as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each conducted in technical triplicate. To compare quantitative data across groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by the Tukey test for specific pairwise comparisons. Synergistic effects of treatments were analyzed using Bliss, Loewe (Chou-Talalay), and ZIP models. The fraction affected was calculated from cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and migration data. In the Bliss independence model, synergy was quantified as the percentage excess of the observed effect over the predicted additive effect. The Loewe additivity model was employed to calculate the Combination index (CI) using the Chou-Talalay method. The ZIP model score (ΔZIP) was determined as the difference between the response of the combination and that of the most effective single agent. Synergy analysis was performed based on the specific concentration combinations tested in this study.

Results

Effect of nano-silymarin concentrations on cell viability

To investigate the effect of various treatments on cell viability, cell survival was assessed at different treatments, as described in the Materials and Methods section. Based on the studies [76, 77] and pilot results, concentrations of 100 and 150 µM of nano-silymarin were selected for subsequent cell viability assays in combination with PDT. Figure 1 illustrates the effects of different treatment modalities on the viability of HN5 cells as determined by the MTT cytotoxicity assay. The control group demonstrated 99.68% cell viability. Treatment with nano-silymarin alone resulted in a concentration-dependent reduction in viability, with mean values of approximately 77.19% at 100 µM and 69.65% at 150 µM. Exposure to methylene blue (MB) alone produced a minimal decrease in viability. The combination of nano-silymarin with MB (100 µM + MB) and (150 µM + MB) produced slightly greater reductions than monotherapies, but remained less potent than laser-based interventions.

The Bliss independence model, which assumes statistically independent drug actions, predicted an expected combined effect of 46.1% inhibition based on individual agent efficacies. The observed 59.4% inhibition exceeds Bliss predictions by 28.8% (p = 0.012, t-test). The Loewe additivity model, widely regarded as the gold standard for synergy quantification, yielded a CI of 0.682 ± 0.045 for the triple combination in cytotoxicity assays. According to the Chou-Talalay interpretation, CI values < 1, = 1, and > 1 indicate synergy, additive effects, and antagonism, respectively [78]. CI value of 0.682 reflects a synergistic interaction. The ZIP model analysis suggested synergistic interactions for the triple combination across the evaluated endpoints. Cytotoxicity was 95.6% higher than that of nano-silymarin alone (ZIP score: 0.290, p < 0.01). Apoptosis increased by 123.6% (ZIP score: 0.233), and migration inhibition demonstrated the most potent effect with 110.8% enhancement (ZIP score: 0.511). The calculated scores across all applied models consistently indicated synergistic interactions for the combination treatments (ZIP > 0.15) and were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Several studies have demonstrated that MB‑mediated PDT can effectively reduce cancer cell viability and trigger apoptotic signaling pathways in different tumor models [75, 79, 80]. Nevertheless, the overall therapeutic efficacy of PDT may be limited by cellular antioxidant systems [81], insufficient intracellular accumulation of photosensitizers [82], and adaptive survival responses in cancer cells [83, 84]. To overcome these limitations, recent research has increasingly focused on combination strategies that integrate PDT with bioactive natural compounds capable of modulating oxidative stress [85, 86] and apoptotic pathways [87, 88]. Among these, silymarin has attracted considerable attention due to its antioxidant, anti‑inflammatory, and anticancer properties. Several studies have suggested that silymarin is hypothesized to influence key cellular processes involved in tumor progression, including oxidative balance, mitochondrial integrity, and apoptosis‑related signaling pathways [89–91].

However, the therapeutic application of silymarin has traditionally been constrained by its limited aqueous solubility and relatively low bioavailability [92]. Nano‑formulation strategies have therefore been developed to improve their stability, cellular uptake, and biological activity. Previous investigations have demonstrated that nano-silymarin systems can enhance intracellular delivery and significantly improve the anticancer potential of this compound compared with its conventional form [93–95]. Within this context, the present findings suggest a synergistic interaction between MB-mediated PDT and nano-silymarin. This enhanced response may be associated with altered oxidative balance and apoptotic susceptibility; however, these mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study.

The greater reduction in cell viability and increased apoptosis observed with the triple combination, compared with individual treatments, suggest the involvement of complementary cytotoxic effects. Because MB-PDT primarily exerts phototoxic effects through light-activated ROS generation [96, 97], silymarin may function as a phytochemical adjuvant that enhances cellular susceptibility to this stress [98]. Furthermore, silymarin independently regulates pro-apoptotic and survival pathways (Bax/Bcl-2 balance, caspase activation, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt) [99–101], creating a multi-targeted attack that cancer cells cannot readily evade. The nanoencapsulation of silymarin is a pivotal factor, as it enhances bioavailability [40] and ensures the effective intracellular concentrations necessary. The application of three distinct models (Bliss, Loewe, and ZIP) to quantify synergistic effects established a multifaceted framework for evaluating treatment interactions, yielding consistent evidence of synergistic interactions. Similarly, the CI value of 0.682 calculated by the Chou-Talalay method was consistent with a synergistic interaction under the investigated conditions. These results suggest that clinically relevant outcomes could be achieved with significantly reduced doses of individual components, potentially minimizing treatment-related toxicities while maintaining or even enhancing efficacy. Consistent with these results, Aldayel [98], as well as Ibrahim et al. [102], demonstrated that combining low-level laser therapy with photodynamic therapy, along with silymarin, can improve outcomes.

The marked reduction in wound closure observed with the triple combination may indicate an overall inhibitory effect on wound closure dynamics in HN5 cells. Addressing the invasive phenotype is crucial, as metastasis remains a leading cause of treatment failure and mortality in HNSCC patients [103]. The localized nature of PDT, combined with the favorable safety profile of silymarin, offers a therapeutic approach with potentially reduced systemic toxicity compared with conventional chemotherapy, an advantage particularly relevant in the functionally sensitive head and neck region. The ability to achieve greater efficacy with potentially reduced doses of individual components could widen the therapeutic window, enabling effective tumor control while preserving critical anatomical structures. Furthermore, the profound inhibition of migration suggests that this combination may address not only primary tumor bulk but also metastatic potential, a feature often inadequately targeted by conventional therapies. This multi-pronged approach aligns with the evolving paradigm of precision oncology, which emphasizes tailored, mechanism-based combinations.

It is important to acknowledge several notable limitations inherent in the design and interpretation of the present study. Because this was an in-vitro study conducted in a two-dimensional culture system, the findings may not fully reflect the complexity of the tumor microenvironment; future studies should therefore include three-dimensional models, organoids, and in-vivo systems. While the redox-related mechanism proposed here is consistent with extensive literature on silymarin, other pathways, including apoptosis regulation and cell-cycle modulation, may also contribute to the observed effects. Direct mechanistic assays (ROS, GSH/GSSG, SOD, CAT, GPx, and oxidative damage markers) will be required to delineate the dominant mechanism in future studies.

Although silymarin is not known to act as a photosensitizer, the absence of a “nano-silymarin + laser only” control group remains a limitation, and future studies will explicitly evaluate the potential photoreactivity of the formulation under 660-nm irradiation. Another limitation of the present study is that the effect of nano-silymarin on cell invasion was assessed only through a migration assay at a single concentration; therefore, further studies using multiple concentrations and complementary invasion assays are warranted. It should be noted that the interactions were evaluated using these selected experimental points rather than a complete, high-density combinational dose–response matrix. Furthermore, the observed reduction in wound closure may be partially influenced by the decreased cell viability and increased apoptosis induced by the combination treatment. Therefore, the scratch assay results should be interpreted as reflecting the overall inhibitory effect of the treatment on wound closure dynamics rather than a definitive measure of cell migration alone. Future studies incorporating normal epithelial cells, mechanistic oxidative stress analyses, and in-vivo tumor models are necessary to validate the translational potential of this combinational strategy.

Conclusions

The present study indicates that nano-silymarin can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of methylene blue–mediated photodynamic therapy in HNSCC cells. The combined treatment demonstrated a synergistic interaction that strengthened the overall anti-tumor response in-vitro. These findings highlight the potential of integrating nano-formulated bioactive compounds with PDT as a multi-targeted therapeutic strategy. Nevertheless, further in-vivo investigations and mechanistic studies are required to validate these observations and clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms.
