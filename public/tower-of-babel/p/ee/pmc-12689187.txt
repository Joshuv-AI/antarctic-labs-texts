Synthesis and properties of a Cu2O decorated BiVO4 nanocomposite photocatalyst
Authors: Guo Jiahu, Chen Xiaoping, Yu Yanwu, Yang Yang, Lai Jianbo, Liang Xinyue, He Shuai, Ma Xiaojun, Fu Ziguo, Chen Guijing, Wang Zhigang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBiomass-derived 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) is a key platform molecule for producing high-value chemicals and clean fuels. This study presents a Cu2O-decorated BiVO4 nanocomposite photocatalyst synthesized via a sol-gel-hydrothermal method, enabling efficient visible-light-driven oxidation of HMF to 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF) with simultaneous H2 production. The p-n heterojunction formed between Cu2O and BiVO4 promotes charge separation, enhancing photocatalytic activity. Under optimized conditions, the system achieved 49% DFF yield and 947 μmol/g H2 in 8 h, maintaining high selectivity and stability over five cycles. The work offers a sustainable route for biomass valorization and clean energy generation.Subject areas: Catalysis, Nanoparticles, Energy sustainability

Introduction

Recent trends in converting biomass into value-added products have attracted much attention due to the high demand for nonfossil feedstocks.1,2,3 Biomass, an alternative feedstock to fossil fuels, has the potential to produce energy and chemicals via pyrolysis, thermal degradation, and catalytic and photo(electro)catalytic oxidation.4,5,6 Lignin-derived molecules with diverse functions have attracted much attention, leading to various value-added products, such as benzyl alcohol, p-methoxybenzyl alcohol, and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF). These are being valorized to produce energy chemicals beneficial to different energy sectors.7,8,9 HMF, a lignin-derived molecule, is recognized as a versatile biomass feedstock that produces many high-efficiency fuels, e.g., higher alkanes, 2,5-dimethylfuran (DMF), and value-added chemicals, e.g., 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF), 5-formyl-2-furan carboxylic acid (FFCA), 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furan carboxylic acid (HMFCA), and 2,5-furan-dicarboxylic acid (FDCA) under catalytic and photocatalytic conditions.10,11,12 These are fundamental biomass-based furanic compounds with widespread applications that nurture the energy and chemical industries in many ways.13,14

DFF is a lignocellulosic-derived biomass compound widely used in the production of ligands, pesticides, antifungals, fluorescent materials, furanic biopolymers and pharmaceutical materials, electroplating, and electro-optical devices.15,16 In addition, DFF can serve as an essential intermediate for synthesizing diamines and Schiff bases.17 Recently, DFF has been produced from many feedstocks, such as fructose, glucose, disaccharides, and polysaccharides.18 Although DFF has been produced from many biomass feedstocks on various catalyst materials, the high efficiency and selectivity of DFF are highly challenging to achieve economically. HMF oxidation to DFF is highly beneficial as it produces H2, one of the most well-known fuels that has gained prominence in the fuel and transportation industries because of no carbon emission.

The photocatalytic oxidation of HMF has great potential to produce H2 as an environmentally friendly and clean fuel under aqueous conditions. HMF oxidation is important and has recently been reported for various semiconductor-based nanocomposite photocatalysts. Although a high selectivity of DFF was achieved over the Nb2O5 semiconductor, the conversion and the yield of HMF and DFF were low.19 The highly stable nature of HMF is the primary concern, which makes it challenging to oxidize under mild conditions and in an inert atmosphere. However, some pioneering works have been reported with significant conversion and yield of HMF and DFF, respectively. Zhang et al. achieved high selectivity (90%) of DFF using (methylammonium)PbBr3 perovskite photocatalyst in an O2 atmosphere and visible light.20 Under in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier spectroscopy (DRIFT), they found the decreased intensity of hydroxyl (-OH) group peaks in HMF and increased intensity of C=O mode which indicated the effective conversion of HMF to DFF under photocatalytic conditions. Zhu et al. synthesized a MoS2/CdIn2S4 nanocomposite photocatalyst and oxidized HMF to DFF with 80.93% selectivity under visible light irradiation.21 In this regard, Qiu et al. synthesized ZnIn2S4 nanosheets via cellulose-derived carbon dots for the oxidation of HMF to DFF under photocatalytic condition.22 The literature mentioned showed HMF oxidation to DFF only; the proton generated during this process can be reduced to H2.

Photocatalytic processes are essential and relatively cheap compared to the catalytic process. Semiconductor photocatalysis works beyond the molecule’s thermodynamics, establishing the photocatalytic materials’ impact.3 In photocatalysis, the conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB) are crucial and facilitate the oxidation-reduction reactions under irradiation conditions.23 Photocatalysts can activate the biomolecules and convert them into various value-added chemicals. The photogenerated electrons excited to CB participate in the reduction reaction while the photogenerated holes at VB oxidize the organic molecules and complete the process cycle. Therefore, semiconductor photocatalysis can be a breakthrough for commodity chemicals from biomass-derived molecules.24

To achieve significantly high H2 yields from HMF oxidation, hydrogen precursors, such as H2O, isopropanol, glycerol, etc., have been used to achieve good to excellent H2 yields under visible light conditions.25 Among these, H2O is an inexpensive, accessible, readily available proton source, which can improve the yield of H2 under experimental conditions.26 Dhingra et al. fabricated Zn0.5Cd0.5S/MnO2 nanocomposite heterojunction photocatalyst that achieved 46% yield of DFF with 1322 μmol/g of H2 within a span of 24 h under photon.27 Further, the PL intensity of the photocatalyst at λ = 345 nm exhibited the maximum luminescence quenching signified the efficient charge transfer and resulting higher photocatalytic activity. Yang et al. obtained an excellent yield of H2 from HMF oxidation using Zn0.5Cd0.5S and C, N co-doped CoP heterojunction.28 Using density functional theory (DFT) and in situ XPS analysis, they found that the e− was further improved as a spontaneous transfer from Zn0.5Cd0.5S to CoP occurred due to the p-n heterojunction formed between Zn0.5Cd0.5S (n-type) and CoP (p-type) which dramatically enhanced the photocatalytic activity. Bao et al. demonstrated the oxidation of HMF to DFF with 95% selectivity and an H2 production rate of 92.0 μmol/g-h using ultrathin g-C3N4 nanosheets under photon.29 They investigated the mechanistic studies which revealed that ·C6H4O3 is the key radical intermediate during the HMF oxidation process by an in situ electron spin resonance (ESR) trapping test. The above literature has reported the oxidation of HMF and H2 production in an O2 atmosphere. Therefore, improving the selectivity of DFF and H2 yield is highly desirable.

Bi-based semiconductors, such as Bi2WO6, Bi2MoO6, BiVO4, BiOBr, BiOI, etc., have two-dimension (2D)-like structures resembling steep absorptions in the visible region, making them good candidates for photocatalytic reactions.30,31 Bi-based nanocomposite photocatalysts are effective in HMF oxidation in visible light.32 The VB potentials of Bi-based semiconductors are probably suitable for HMF oxidation. Hence, they would favor HMF oxidation reactions. Kumar et al. synthesized the rose-like Bi2WO6 nanocrystal, oxidized HMF to DFF, and gained 99.5% selectivity and 58.3% yield in 10 h under an O2 atmosphere.33 They additionally performed the reaction under natural sunlight and gained 92.4% selectivity of DFF in 6 h. Xue et al. described the oxidation of HMF on Fe-doped Bi2MoO6 nanoplates and achieved enhanced DFF yield under visible light conditions.34

Recently, Zhou et al. performed selective oxidation of HMF to DFF using 2D g-C3N4/BiOCl nanocomposite photocatalyst and achieved 96.4% conversion of HMF and 52.3% selectivity of DFF.35 Fu et al. achieved the efficient conversion of HMF up to 95% over in situ grown MnO2/BiMoO6/Kaolinite 3D-layered nanocomposite photocatalyst.36 Yang et al. demonstrated the photocatalytic oxidation of HMF to DFF selectively using engineered Bi2O2CO3/ZnIn2S4 nanocomposite.37 They investigated that the metal carbonate/oxide heterointerface enables the effective charge optimized the surface oxygen activation, and facilitates the synergy of ·OOH and holes to selectively convert HMF into DFF. Golubovskaya et al. examined the photocatalytic oxidation of HMF to DFF and the photocatalytic decomposition of Rhodamine B over β-Bi2O3/Bi12SiO20 nanocomposite.38

Copper-based semiconductors have highly negative reduction potential and are highly efficient for photocatalytic water splitting into H2 production.39 Cu2O nanoparticles showed excellent visible light harvesting between 400 and 700 nm. Surface plasmon resonance on copper promotes the photocatalytic reduction of protons generated in the water-splitting.40 BiVO4 and Cu2O are the n-type and p-type semiconductors, respectively; this creates a p-n heterojunction combination that improves charge separation between the two semiconductors and would be effective in achieving significant conversion and yield of HMF and DFF, respectively, in visible light. In addition, the p-n heterojunction can show Z-scheme mechanism for the electron-hole pairs movement where electrons possess for the reduction process while holes possess for the oxidation reactions at the photocatalyst surface.41,42

Here, by exploiting the massive visible-light absorption of BiVO4 and the highly negative reduction potential of Cu2O, we synthesized Cu2O-decorated BiVO4 heterojunction photocatalysts for HMF oxidation and simultaneous H2 production selectively under an inert atmosphere and visible light conditions. The nanocomposite photocatalysts were constructed using a sol-gel-hydrothermal process, resulting in around ∼7 nm of Cu2O nanoparticles, which enabled the high yield and selectivity of H2 and DFF, respectively (Scheme 1). The semiconductor nanocomposite-based photocatalytic HMF oxidation and H2 production would likely improve the biochemical economy by producing clean fuels and opening new avenues for simultaneous biomass feedstock conversion to value-added chemicals and fuels.

Results

Synthesis and characterizations

The nanomaterials were synthesized in a two-step method (Scheme 2). BiVO4 nanosheets were obtained via a sol-gel-hydrothermal process, followed by Cu2O nanoparticles decoration on BiVO4 nanosheets. Different concentrations of Cu2O on BiVO4 were investigated and characterized in the photocatalytic experiments.

Schematic representation of Cu2O@BiVO4 nanocomposites via sol-gel-hydrothermal process and post-deposition of Cu2O nanoparticles

Powder XRD determined synthesized nanoparticles and nanocomposites’ crystal structure. The most exposed crystal plane (013) at 28.9° is characteristic of the monoclinic BiVO4 crystal structure (Figure 1A). The other planes were auxiliary and supported different facets of the BiVO4 crystal, confirmed by JCPDS Card #14-0688.43 This represents the refined crystal structure of BiVO4 with the most exposed facets (013). The stoichiometry remained the same even after the deposition of the Cu2O nanoparticles on the BiVO4 nanosheets and resembled the synthesized ones. In the CuBVO nanocomposites, (111) and (110) planes found at 36.5° and 42.3°, respectively, ascribed the cubic Cu2O in CuBVO composites obtained from the JCPDS Card #78-2076.44 Increasing the Cu2O content on BiVO4 showed the enhanced intensity of (111) and (110) diffraction planes for CuBVO-5, CuBVO-10, and CuBVO-15 nanocomposites. Interestingly, no shift in the diffraction angle indicates that the crystal lattice of BiVO4 remains intact even after the decoration with Cu2O nanoparticles. FT-IR is an essential tool for investigating the surface functionality of synthesized nanomaterials (Figure 1B). FT-IR spectra were plotted by correcting baselines of all the nanomaterials. The characteristic Bi-O stretch mode was found at 634 cm−1 with a broad peak; the structural vibrational modes associated with VO43− were identified at 743 and 831 cm−1.45 The adsorbed water bending mode was observed at 1628 cm−1.46 Stretching vibration modes of intercalated water molecules were found between 3400 and 3500 cm−1 with a broad-low-intense peak.47,48 In the FT-IR spectra of Cu2O, Cu(I)-O stretching mode was found at 628 cm−1. In comparison to BiVO4, the adsorbed water molecules’ bending and stretching modes were identified at 1614 and 3432 cm−1. Decoration of Cu2O nanoparticles on BiVO4 revealed enhanced stretching modes of Cu(I)-O in the CuBVO nanocomposites. Furthermore, CuBVO-15 showed an intense stretching mode of Cu(I)-O, indicating efficient binding of Cu2O to BiVO4. However, no significant changes in the vibrational frequencies of the stretching and bending vibrational frequencies were detected.

XPS describes elements’ chemical states in the synthesized nanomaterials (Figure 2). Bi-4f exhibited the characteristic 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks at 158.5 and 163.8 eV, respectively, indicating Bi3+ existence in BiVO4 nanoparticles (Figure 2A). Furthermore, no additional XPS peak was found for Bi5+, confirming the existence of Bi3+ in BiVO4.49 The decoration of Cu2O nanoparticles shifted the 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 signals to 158.3 and 163.6 eV of Bi-4f in the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite, respectively (Figure 2A). The binding energy shifting suggests that the electron movement on the nanocomposite surface would be more feasible, which could help achieve efficient electron participation in the photocatalytic reaction. Like Bi-4f, V-2p showed 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 XPS signals shifting in the nanocomposite (Figure 2B). 2p3/2 signal shifted from 516.1 to 515.9 eV, while the 2p1/2 signal moved from 523.6 to 523.4 eV in CuBVO-10 nanocomposite.50 O-1s XPS signals did not change the binding energy significantly; they had similar binding energies in both BiVO4 and CuBVO-10 nanocomposite with characteristic XPS signals from lattice oxygen (529.1 eV), hydroxyl (531.4 eV) and adsorbed water (532.9 eV) XPS signals except an additional peak that might be due to the Cu-O bonding occurred at 533.8 eV (Figure 2C). The Cu2O nanoparticles exhibited a Cu+ chemical state with 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 signals at 932.8 and 952.6 eV, respectively51; a weak satellite signal confirmed the existence of Cu+ only in Cu2O nanoparticles (Figure 2D). In addition, the Cu LLM peak obtained at 567.8 eV confirmed the presence of Cu+ in Cu2O nanoparticles (Figure 2E). Nevertheless, the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite exhibited a Cu2+ state, observed in the synthetic steps due to excessive oxidation. Therefore, Cu2O nanoparticles exist in Cu+ and Cu2+ chemical states in the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite. In addition, the calculated ratio of Cu+ and Cu2+ was 6.14:1 which would help to predict the redox behavior of the photocatalyst. The survey scan of Cu2O, BiVO4, and CuBVO-10 nanocomposite, confirmed Cu, Bi, V, and O elements’ existence (Figure 2F).

The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrometer scanned the synthesized nanomaterials to examine the optical behavior (Figure 4A). BiVO4 absorbed below 400 nm; the absorption from 490 nm indicates active harvesting of UV and visible light.52 Cu2O showed multiple absorptions in the visible region with a sharp fall corresponding to 642 nm.53 The decoration of Cu2O nanoparticles on BiVO4 improved the visible light absorption of the CuBVO nanocomposites. CuBVO-10 nanocomposite exhibited relatively higher visible light absorption than CuBVO-5 nanocomposite. This indicates that incorporating Cu2O nanoparticles in the BiVO4 surface enhanced the visible light absorption of the nanocomposite materials, which would favor significant photocatalytic activity.

The reduced bandgap of the nanocomposites refers to the improved photocatalytic activity upon Cu2O decoration on the BiVO4 surface.

Mott-Schottky (M-S) was employed to estimate the CB and VB potentials based on flat band potentials (Efb) calculated for the individual semiconductors and the slope in the M-S plot reveals charge density (Figure 4B). BiVO4 semiconductor showed a positive slope with Efb of −0.61 V near CB, corresponding to the n-type semiconductor.55 Cu2O showed a negative slope with Efb of 0.27 V near VB, referring to the p-type semiconductor. It was found that the CB of an n-type semiconductor would be 0.1 V higher than Efb, while for p-type, VB would be −0.1 V higher than Efb. Thus, the CB of BiVO4 would be −0.51 V, and VB can be calculated as 1.72 V using Eg = EVB − ECB. Similarly, the VB of Cu2O would be 0.17 V, and CB was calculated as −1.69 V.

Additional insight into the optical illusion could be obtained using photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. PL spectroscopy described charge carriers’ trapping efficiency in the BiVO4 and CuBVO-10, which can define the photocatalytic activity (Figure 4C). The intense PL signal at 450 nm exhibited the rapid recombination of charge carriers at the BiVO4 surface. This corresponds to the minimal separation of photogenerated electrons and holes, referring to the relatively lower photocatalytic activity. In comparison, CuBVO-10 nanocomposite heterojunction exhibited diminished PL intensity, referring to efficient trapping of charge carriers, facilitating the improved separation of electrons and holes. This concludes that the reduced PL intensity of the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite heterojunction corresponds to the enhanced photocatalytic activity than the BiVO4 semiconductor.

EIS was performed for Cu2O, BiVO4, and CuBVO-10 nanocomposite photocatalysts to investigate the charge transfer kinetics (Figure 4D). Large arc radius refers to the extensive charge transfer resistance with minimum charge transfer efficiency. In contrast, the small arc radius depicts the least charge transfer resistance with higher charge transfer efficiency. BiVO4 exhibited the largest arc radius, while Cu2O had a relatively smaller one. This indicates that Cu2O has relatively higher charge transfer efficiency than BiVO4. The decoration of Cu2O nanoparticles on the BiVO4 surface in CuBVO-10 nanocomposite photocatalyst reduced the arc radius with excellent charge transfer efficiency, corresponding to the relatively higher photocatalytic activity than Cu2O and BiVO4 semiconductors.56 Thus, the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite has proven to be an excellent photocatalyst compared to others.

Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis of multilayer adsorption was performed to investigate bulk properties (Figure 5A). BiVO4 had a type-IV isotherm with an H3 hysteresis loop at the elevated relative pressure of 0.86 p/po. BET surface area was 13.6 m2/g with 15.2 nm and 0.026 cm3/g of the average pore diameter and mean pore volume, respectively. Cu2O nanoparticles had relatively similar isotherm of type-IV, and the surface area found was too low, i.e., 2.74 m2/g with 0.009 cm3/g and 32.4 nm of mean pore volume and average pore diameter, respectively. Combining Cu2O in 10 wt. % on BiVO4, i.e., CuBVO-10 nanocomposite exhibited type-IV isotherm with H3 hysteresis loop like BiVO4, but the surface area went low up to 10.9 m2/g. In addition, pore diameter and volume diminished to 12.7 nm and 0.021 cm3/g, indicating the decoration of Cu2O nanoparticles on BiVO4 covers the pores and is inserted into the pores, which reduces pore diameter and volume.

The weight loss per °C was investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) under an N2 atmosphere between 50°C and 800°C with a ramping of 10°C/min (Figure 5B). BiVO4 hardly showed weight loss up to 800°C, indicating its excellent stability at high temperatures. In contrast, a 22.4% weight loss occurred for Cu2O from 451°C to 494°C due to the decomposition of NO3 precursors remaining in the synthesis steps. At higher temperatures, no marginal loss of weight was observed. The weight loss started at 353°C in CuBVO-10 but was not very important. A minimal weight loss of 1.4% was observed from 547°C to 586°C, indicating the high stability of CuBVO-10 nanocomposite at high temperatures. Discussion

Investigation of photocatalytic activity

The photocatalytic HMF oxidation and H2 production experiments were performed in a two-neck RB (Figure 6). One millimole of HMF was initially charged into an RB without the photocatalyst in a light source under an N2 atmosphere (Table 2). We found no oxidized product and H2 in the reaction mixture at 8 h (Figure 6A). This indicates that HMF was stable enough to oxidize alone in visible light. In addition, we performed the reaction in the dark using 10 mg of BiVO4; unfortunately, no product yield was obtained, confirming that the oxidation of HMF could not be converted in the dark even if we used a photocatalyst. By switching the light turn on, we observed the oxidation of HMF, which might make possible 2,5-DFF. For this, we examined the reaction mixture using GC-FID and found DFF to be the product of the reaction. However, the DFF yield was very low, 5.2%, confirming that adding a catalytic amount of BiVO4 resulted in product yield. In addition, the vapor phase reaction mixture was analyzed and a maximum of 84 μmol/g of H2 was detected for 8 h. This indicated that BiVO4 oxidized HMF in the presence of water to DFF with H2 under an N2 gas atmosphere and could improve the yield of products by tuning the photocatalyst properties by decorating metal nanoparticles on the BiVO4 surface.

Cu2O semiconductor is an effective photocatalyst for H2 production from various organic substrates under visible light conditions.39 Therefore, combining Cu2O nanoparticles with BiVO4 would be a sustainable approach, and we can achieve the desired products’ efficient yields. The photocatalytic experiment performed with Cu2O nanoparticles as a semiconductor photocatalyst afforded a 3.0% yield of DFF with just 48 μmol/g of H2 under similar experimental conditions; however, the selectivity of DFF was 93.7%. We varied Cu2O concentrations on the BiVO4 surface and verified them for HMF oxidation with H2 production under visible light irradiation. We synthesized three different concentrations of Cu2O (5, 10, and 15 wt. %) on BiVO4 nanosheets and encoded these nanocomposites as CuBVO-5, CuBVO-10, and CuBVO-15. At first, we performed the photocatalytic experiment with CuBVO-5 photocatalyst (Figure 6B). We found a slightly improved DFF yield of up to 24.1% with an H2 yield of 391 μmol/g, but significantly, not optimal. This suggests that combining Cu2O with BiVO4 can improve the product yield. A higher concentration, i.e., CuBVO-10 photocatalyst, afforded higher yields of 49.3% and 947 μmol/g of DFF and H2, respectively, showing that the interface was formed between Cu2O and BiVO4 and played a crucial role in HMF oxidation. The calculated H2 production rate was about 118 μmol/g-h with CuBVO-10, the H2 generation level is much higher than other nanoparticles, and nanocomposites were synthesized for the current photocatalytic experiments. The heterojunction constructed here is a p-n heterojunction, effectively separating the charge carriers and improving the photo response in visible light. Increasing Cu2O content by 15 wt. % by weight on BiVO4, i.e., CuBVO-15 nanocomposite, did not improve yields of DFF and H2; instead, we see somewhat reduced yields of 47.9% and 892 μmol/g of DFF and H2, respectively, confirming higher Cu2O content on the active sites of BiVO4 that affected the products yields. However, DFF selectivity was >95% in all the photocatalysis experiments. Therefore, CuBVO-10 nanocomposite proved to be an excellent photocatalyst for HMF oxidation with H2 production in an inert atmosphere. Although, we achieved significant yield of HMF and H2 with CuBVO-10 photocatalyst under optimized condition, further investigation of yields of HMF and H2 with a physical mixture of Cu2O and BiVO4 semiconductors in same ratio might be useful. We synthesized physical mixture of 10 wt. % Cu2O with BiVO4 and performed photocatalytic experiment under identical condition. We observed low yields of HMF (7.6%) and H2 (107 μmol/g) after 8 h. This indicates that Cu2O and BiVO4 did create a metal-support interaction which is critical for the efficient photocatalytic activity.

In addition, we used solvents other than water to examine the solvent effect on DFF and H2 yields under identical conditions. We used CH3CN, 1,4-dioxane, toluene, and CH3OH as solvents under similar conditions (Figure 6C). Using 1,4-dioxane, the DFF and H2 yields were too low or negligible, indicating that the solvent selection was incorrect. Toluene did not produce DFF and H2, confirming its unreliability for HMF conversion. In case of toluene and 1,4-dioxane, both the solvents are aprotic which could not boost the production of H2 instead they were inert or stable under visible light photocatalysis. Both CH3OH and CH3CN are protic solvents that are more likely to donate H+, which can participate in the reaction to get higher yields of the desired products. Both CH3OH and CH3CN resulted in moderate yields of 35.4% and 36.7% of DFF but a much higher yield of H2 (758 and 781 μmol/g). However, the DFF and H2 efficiencies with water under identical conditions were slightly higher, 49.3% and 947 μmol/g, compared to all other solvents used in the photocatalytic experiments. In addition, we mixed the H2O polar solvent and CH3CN polar aprotic solvent to investigate the activity. However, the yield of DFF and H2 were 41.5% and 648 μmol/g, respectively.

To bring more insight into the reaction system, we examined the appropriate amount of HMF, which can lead to optimal DFF and H2 yields. We performed the photocatalytic experiments using 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mmol of HMF under identical conditions (Figure 6D). By screening all these mentioned concentrations of HMF with 50 mg of CuBVO-10 nanocomposite, we found moderate to higher yields of DFF and H2. With 0.2 and 0.5 mmol, DFF yields were 38.7% and 44.2%, while H2 yields were 684 and 767 μmol/g. The yields of DFF and H2 were higher up to 49.3% and 947 μmol/g, respectively, when 1.0 mmol HMF was used. However, a higher amount of HMF, i.e., 2.0 mmol, remained with the relative yield of DFF (51.8%) and H2 (952 μmol/g). This indicates that 1.0 mmol of HMF is more prominent than the others under identical conditions. In addition, the progress of the reaction was examined and quantified using GC-FID and GC-TCD. At the start of the reaction, there was an explosion in the yields of DFF and H2, as much of HMF can be converted to DFF and H2 at the photocatalyst surface within 3 h of the reaction progressed. After that, the reaction slowed, and DFF and H2 yields improved, perhaps slightly, until 8 h. This indicates that the HMF conversion is effective for up to 8 h, and the prolonged time leads to improved DFF and H2 yields.

After carefully evaluating the photocatalytic experimental conditions, we performed a time-on-stream experiment to examine the stability and deactivation of the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite (Figure 7). We found that DFF and H2 yields gradually increase, and DFF selectivity is almost high, i.e., 95%. However, there was no improvement in the yield of DFF and H2 at the prolonged time, confirming that the reaction resulted in maximum yields within 8 h of the experiment.

Scavenger study

The effect of the additional moieties, i.e., scavengers, is an interesting experiment that could inform us of the activity under inhibitor conditions. We used 5 mg of each silver nitrate and sodium persulfate as the electron scavengers under identical photocatalytic conditions. When silver nitrate was used, it trapped electrons generated in the reaction, and the proton generated from the HMF could not be reduced, which did not result in any H2 yield. However, the availability of the holes potentially oxidized the HMF into DFF with 47.5% yield. Sodium persulfate behaved similarly, and we could not detect H2 within the limit of GC.57 This indicates that the electron scavengers inhibited the photocatalytic oxidation of HMF significantly. In addition, the hole scavenger could describe the minimized efficiency of the oxidation photocatalyst in the photocatalytic reaction. We used 5 mg of Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a hole scavenger to study the oxidation efficiency of the photocatalyst under photocatalytic conditions. The photogenerated hole trapped by the EDTA scavenger efficiently separated the electron-pair recombination, and the photogenerated electrons are easily available for the reduction of generated protons (H+), significantly yielding 926 μmol/g of H2. However, there was less availability of holes for HMF oxidation which resulted only 2.9% yield of DFF under photocatalytic condition.

Stability experiments

After carefully optimizing the photocatalytic experimental conditions for HMF oxidation to DFF and H2, the stability of the photocatalyst used should be investigated (Figure 8). After the photocatalytic experiment, the photocatalyst was centrifuged, washed with ethanol, and dried at 60°C overnight. We repeated this process after each investigation and recovered the photocatalyst. At the end of the fifth cycle, DFF and H2 yields and DFF selectivity were relatively similar to the fresh CuBVO-10 photocatalyst (Figure 8A). There were no marginal changes in the conversion and production rates of HMF and H2. In addition, the recovered amount of the photocatalyst in each step was ∼10, 9, 9, 8, and 8 mg in the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth cycles, respectively. After five repeated experiments, the recovered CuBVO-10 photocatalyst was analyzed using powder XRD, FTIR, and XPS to examine the changes after five cycles. Fresh and used CuBVO-10 nanocomposite showed similar diffraction patterns with (111) and (110) planes of Cu2O (Figure 8B). FTIR showed a similar vibrational spectrum without change in the vibration peaks of VO43− and -OH stretching modes, indicating no change in CuBVO-10 nanocomposite even after five consecutive cycles (Figure 8C). In addition, XPS showed a slight change in the satellite peak in Cu-2p XPS. However, no shift of 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 signals confirmed that the photocatalyst remained intact after five consecutive experiments and exhibited high stability under experimental conditions (Figure 8D).

Product identification

The conversion and yields of HMF and DFF were quantified using authentic samples injected in GC-FID and GC-TCD. In addition, the product of HMF oxidation, i.e., DFF, was further confirmed using GC-MS and 13C NMR spectra. An authentic sample identified a molecular ion peak at an m/z value of 124 in GC-MS spectra for DFF (see also Figure S3. GC-MS spectra of DFF as the reaction product). The de-shielded aldehydic carbon was found at 179 ppm in 13C NMR spectra, confirming DFF as a reaction product (see also Figure S4. 13C NMT spectra of the reaction with DFF product). The reduced product, i.e., H2 yield, was calculated using a calibrated GC-FID.

Photocatalytic mechanism

The oxidation of HMF into DFF and simultaneous H2 production are challenging to achieve in an inert atmosphere and visible light conditions. However, we have proposed a plausible mechanism of HMF oxidation into DFF and H2 production over Cu2O/BiVO4 heterojunction photocatalyst (Scheme 3). In the combination of n-type and p-type semiconductors, it has been shown that Efb is close to CB in n-type semiconductors, while Efb is close to VB in p-type semiconductors.63 The CB value of BiVO4 can be converted against NHE using the following equation (ENHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.197) and is found at −0.41 V (vs. Ag/AgCl). In M-S, it was mentioned that Efb is 0.1 V lower than CB of n-type semiconductor while 0.1 V is higher than VB for p-type semiconductors,64 with the help of which the CB of BiVO4 and Cu2O were −0.51 V and −1.69 V (vs. NHE at pH 7), respectively, while VB was 1.72 V and 0.17 V (vs. NHE at pH 7), respectively. This suggests that feasible oxidation at VB of BiVO4 and the CB of Cu2O may be applicable for reduction.

Band structure of the semiconductor with excitation of the photogenerated electrons and movement of the photogenerated holes

The photocatalytic production of DFF from HMF and H2 over Cu2O/BiVO4 heterojunction photocatalyst under visible light condition.

Constructing a heterojunction of BiVO4 and Cu2O made a p-n-type heterojunction that enhanced charge carriers’ separation and inhibited the recombination of photogenerated electrons and holes at the heterojunction surface.65 The inhibition of the charge carriers was also confirmed in PL and EIS spectra, and CuBVO-10 showed the lowest emission peak and smallest arc radius compared to other semiconductors used. As discussed in the M-S analysis, a p-type semiconductor is electron deficient, and when it approaches an n-type semiconductor, Efb tends to be equilibrium so that the electrons and holes participate effectively in the photoredox reaction.66,67 Constructing heterojunction, both the semiconductors encountered each other, and the Efb of Cu2O moved in an upward direction while the Efb of BiVO4 moved downward to make equilibrium, and band bending occurred.68 Under visible light irradiation, photogenerated electrons and holes are produced; the electrons get excited and concentrated at the higher energy CB of BiVO4 and Cu2O, and photogenerated holes are populated at the semiconductors’ lower energy VB. The photogenerated holes at VB of BiVO4 have a robust oxidizing ability for organic compounds due to their highly positive potential (1.74 V > 1.23 V H2O/H+).49 Under the effect of the p-n junction, electrons concentrated at CB of BiVO4 are transferred to VB of Cu2O, where the populated photogenerated holes of Cu2O are combined with them and inhibit charge carriers recombination at Cu2O and BiVO4.69 Therefore, the excited electrons at CB of Cu2O are readily available with strong reduction ability, and the photogenerated holes at VB of BiVO4 become more efficient for oxidation, making it a Z-scheme heterojunction rather than type 2.70 In the photocatalytic reaction, HMF is adsorbed at the photocatalyst surface; the photogenerated holes at VB of BiVO4 facilitated the oxidation of HMF to DFF efficiently by producing protons in and from water. At CB of Cu2O, the protons generated from HMF oxidation and water allow a remarkably high yield of H2. The enhanced photocatalytic activity of the photocatalyst is mainly due to the separation of charge carriers at the heterojunction interface, which could be determined using PL and EIS spectroscopy.

Environmental implication

The study on the Cu2O decorated BiVO4 nanocomposite photocatalyst for 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) oxidation to 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF) and concurrent H2 generation has significant environmental implications. By efficiently utilizing a renewable biomass feedstock in a clean, energy-efficient process under visible light, this technology reduces reliance on fossil fuels and minimizes carbon emissions. The production of DFF from HMF not only yields valuable chemicals used in various applications but generates H2 as a clean fuel with zero carbon emissions, contributing to sustainable energy solutions. This research paves the way for more efficient and eco-friendly chemical synthesis pathways, promoting environmentally responsible industrial practices.

Conclusions

The current study presents a successful approach involving incorporating Cu2O nanoparticles into BiVO4 nanosheets through a sol-gel-hydrothermal process. The engineered nanocomposite photocatalysts exhibited remarkable suitability for achieving selective DFF generation from HMF oxidation, accompanied by a noteworthy H2 production yield under experimental conditions. Overcoming the considerable challenge of HMF oxidation within an inert atmosphere, we accomplished a substantial DFF yield, boasting an outstanding selectivity of up to 95.7%. This research underscores the remarkably efficient and environmentally friendly production of clean fuels by converting biomass-derived HMF into DFF within a concise time frame. The thoughtfully devised hybrid semiconductor nanocomposite holds promise for forthcoming photocatalytic investigations to advance both bioeconomic practices and the generation of clean fuels.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we synthesized Cu2O@BiVO4 nanocomposite photocatalyst using sol-gel hydrothermal approach. It is important to note that achieving complete purity of the remains a challenge with this method, which may affect the HMF oxidation, and DFF and H2 yields and hinder our understanding of the metal-support interaction between the two semiconductors in the nanocomposite photocatalyst. Additionally, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and in situ experiments, e.g., in situ IR and in situ XPS, are needed for the identification of the exact movement of the electron at the photocatalyst’s surface which we were unable to perform in this study.

Method details

Materials used

Bismuth nitrate pentahydrate, conc. HNO3, ammonium metavanadate, sodium hydroxide, copper sulfate pentahydrate, sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS), ammonium hydroxide, hydrazine monohydrate, and ethanol were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. All the synthesis experiments were performed in de-ionized water.

Materials synthesis

Synthesis of BiVO4

BiVO4 nanoparticles were obtained by a slight modification of the literature.71 In a typical synthesis, 20 ml of a solution of 5 g of Bi(NO3)3.5H2O with 2 mol/L HNO3 solution and another 20 ml of a solution of 1.2 g of NH4VO3 with 2 mol/L NaOH solution were prepared and stirred continuously. 40 mg of SDBS was poured into each solution with continuous stirring. After one hour, the two solutions were mixed at a stirring rate of 800 rpm until complete dissolution. This solution was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 170°C for 3 h for crystal growth. After cooling this solution, the precipitates of BiVO4 were collected, treated with water and ethanol, and dried at 40°C overnight. The dried BiVO4 was used for further processing or further modification.

Synthesis of Cu2O

In the typical synthesis,72 50 ml of an aqueous solution of 0.5 g of CuSO4.5H2O was stirred at a stirring rate of 800 rpm in a round-bottom flask at 100°C for 15 min and whole the synthesis process stirred at the same rpm rate. Adding 1 mL of 5 M NH4OH altered its colour from blue to brown, followed by adding of hydrazine monohydrate solution dropwise up to pH of 7 at 100°C for one hour. The obtained precipitates were then cold and separated. Washing wet content with DI water, followed by ethanol, removed most of the moisture, then dried at 60°C overnight in an oven.

Synthesis of Cu2O@BiVO4 nanocomposite

Cu2O nanoparticles with 5-15 wt.% were decorated on BiVO4 nanosheets to synthesize the nanocomposite following the similar method of synthesis of BiVO4. We used 0.174 g of CuSO4.5H2O for 5 wt.%, 0.349 g for 10 wt.%, and 0.524 g for 15 wt.% for 1 g of BiVO4 support in the photocatalyst. The Cu2O contents on BiVO4 were coded as CuBVO-5, CuBVO-10, and CuBVO-15 for 5, 10, and 15 wt.%, respectively, of Cu2O. Also, the molar ratio of Cu2O with BiVO4 was 1:8.84, 1:4.42, and 1:2.94 for CuBVO-5, CuBVO-10, and CuBVO-15, respectively. The fabricated nanocomposite photocatalysts were screened for photocatalytic HMF oxidation reactions under an N2 atmosphere and visible light irradiation.

Experiment of photocatalytic reaction

The experiments of HMF oxidation and simultaneous H2 production were performed at 25 ± 2°C using a Xenon lamp (300 W, filter ≥ 420 nm). In a 50 mL two-neck round-bottom (RB) flask, HMF (1.0 mmol) and the photocatalyst (10 mg) were charged into 10 ml of deionized water with sonication for 10 min. Unwanted dissolved gases were removed by purging with N2 for 30 min, eliminating the possibility of unnecessary substrate oxidation. This suspension was kept under the dark with 1.5 h stirring gaining an adsorption-desorption equilibrium. RB was kept 5 cm away from the Xenon lamp. This lamp illuminated the reaction mixture, started the reaction, and monitored it regularly. In the end, the gaseous mixture was collected from the RB headspace and injected into the GC-TCD to quantify reaction product H2. The photocatalyst was separated and recovered by centrifugation. The separated reaction mixture was analyzed on a GC-MS. In addition, NMR confirmed the formation of DFF, evidenced by the existence of the product.

Photoelectrochemical analysis

Following the reported printing method, FTO plates were selected for the semiconductors and nanocomposite coating.73 This could be achieved by grinding photocatalyst (50 mg) and ethyl cellulose (25 mg), then adding terpineol (0.1 ml), acetic acid (0.02 ml), and ethanol (1 ml) for homogeneity. This ground mixture was manually printed by rolling a glass rod on an FTO plate. The semiconducting plates were placed in an oven at 150°C under a vacuum.

Quantification and statistical analysis

ImageJ software was used to analyze images of the CuBVO-10 nanocomposite. Origin 2016 (64-bit) was used to analyze the data from XRD, XPS, FT-IR, PL, and EIS. Agilent GC 890 GC system was used to quantify the products. The results are presented in the results and discussion section of the nanomaterials.
