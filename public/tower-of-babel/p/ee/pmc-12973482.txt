Matching Patients With Cell Surface–Targeted Clinical Trials Using Large Language Models
Authors: S. Carson Callahan, Matthew R. Chrostek, Nicholas Rydzewski, Matthew Bootsma, Kyle T. Helzer, Hamza Bakhtiar, Zachary S. Morris, David Kosoff, Joshua M. Lang, Vincent T. Ma, Marina Sharifi, Shuang G. Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSECell surface–targeted therapies (CSTs) are a rapidly expanding class of cancer treatments with high specificity and reduced toxicity. Matching patients who express specific targets to CST clinical trials remains challenging because of complex eligibility criteria, diverse targets, and the absence of centralized, up-to-date trial databases. These gaps limit patient access and contribute to poor trial accrual.METHODSWe developed a large language model (LLM)–driven pipeline to identify and annotate CST clinical trials. Using a two-pronged approach, LLMs extracted target information from ClinicalTrials.gov and the National Cancer Institute Drug Database. Eight LLMs, including GPT-4o and several open-source models, were benchmarked against manually curated data sets of 814 CST trials and 814 non-CST trials. We evaluated model performance at target and trial levels and analyzed sources of error. We also provide an up-to-date database of open CST trials and their targets from the >100,000 total oncology clinical trials in ClinicalTrials.gov.RESULTSGPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy in identifying CST trials (96.5%) and their targets (89.5%). Combining data sources improved performance, and accuracy increased with later trial phases. Most errors stemmed from vague therapy descriptions or string-matching issues. The model matched 94% of US trials and >95% of trials globally, with exceptions in China and New Zealand. In predicting cell surface localization, Gemma 3:27b and MedLlama3 correctly labeled all known clinical cell surface targets although performance varied beyond the most well-known CSTs.CONCLUSIONOur LLM-based approach enables real-time, automated matching of patients to CST clinical trials, addressing major barriers to enrollment and expanding trial accessibility. Errors were uncommon, and performance is poised to improve as LLMs evolve. Optimizing patient-trial matching for CSTs can improve both patient benefit and trial success.

INTRODUCTION

Clinical trials form the backbone of clinical cancer research and are required to investigate new therapies and set new standards of care. Notably, early patient accrual is associated with overall accrual,1 emphasizing the importance of identifying the right patients for enrollment. Furthermore, it is estimated that only a small minority (on the order of 2%-4%) of patients ultimately participate in cancer-related clinical trials, despite a large majority of Americans having a favorable view of participating in cancer-related clinical trials.2-4 While there are numerous reasons leading to difficulty in enrolling patients onto clinical trials, two of the most significant and well-documented obstacles are (1) knowledge of trial availability and (2) geographic availability of trials.3,5-9 Data suggest that institutional trial unavailability accounts for 55%-72% of missed enrollment opportunities and that expanding the search radius to approximately 20 miles from a patient's location can increase the number of trials identified by 91%.5-7,9 Previous studies have found that 70% of US counties have no active cancer treatment trials and 86% of nonmetropolitan counties lack trials entirely, which leaves rural patients at a severe disadvantage.7 Investigations into clinical trial enrollment logistics also emphasize the cost and effort required to successfully accrue a patient, with screening a single patient for eligibility requiring 3.4-8.8 hours and $129-336, depending on patient-specific factors.10 Ultimately, these obstacles to trial enrollment result in approximately 40% of clinical trials failing to meet accrual goals.11 An analysis of National Clinical Trials Network–sponsored trials has further shown that 18% of these trials were either closed or were at <50% of target accrual 3 years or more after initiation.12 Difficulty in enrolling patients causes delays in the development of new therapies and deprives patients who would have qualified for beneficial therapies.

Cell surface–targeting therapies (CSTs) represent an emerging class of highly effective drugs for hematologic and solid tumor malignancies, with several US Food and Drug Administration–approved therapies in use clinically. Compared with conventional cytotoxic therapies, CSTs offer greater specificity and reduced toxicity. A unique eligibility requirement for CST trials is confirmation of target expression, often via imaging or pathology. In many cases, expression of the target cell surface marker correlates with response13-23 although this is not universal.24 Most clinical trials of novel CSTs require cell surface marker expression, either directly or indirectly (eg, by selecting a disease state where expression is ubiquitous). High-throughput methods exist for identifying and confirming potential targets expressed in a patient.25-37 However, once suitable targets are identified, locating relevant trials outside the treating institution remains difficult, which often limits therapies that are available to patients. This challenge is amplified by the large number of potential targets and multicancer trials based solely on target expression. Thus, even disease site experts are unlikely to be familiar with all available CST trials for every possible target. Given the growing number of CST clinical trials and persistent accrual challenges, it is critical to develop a tool that can match patients to trials more broadly.

The recent development and expansion of large language models (LLMs) has made the process of parsing and interpreting large amounts of text much more efficient. LLMs have been used to match patients to clinical trials in an effort to make patient-trial matching more efficient and less costly.38 However, the oncology-focused approaches, while including trials investigating molecularly targeted therapies, lack data on their performance specifically for CSTs.39,40 Existing approaches are largely centered on parsing text from a patient's electronic health record (EHR) and matching this to explicit clinical trial criteria, such as the presence or absence of a specific mutation and other clinical laboratory values.11,39,41,42 In addition, these approaches tend to focus on institutional clinical trials or public benchmarking data sets, rather than identifying active trials nation-wide.11,39-42 We developed a unique LLM-based approach which integrates disparate sources of CST and trial information and created a unified database that can be updated in real time as new trials open. We then validated and compared the performance of eight different LLMs in a human-curated set of 814 CST trials.25 Our tool is capable of accurately identifying suitable CST trials for specific targets and can be used to both increase enrollment on these trials and better match patients with trials from which they are most likely to benefit.

METHODS

Model Selection

We evaluated a number of open models that can all be downloaded and run locally. These include Llama 3.1:70b and Llama 3.3 from Meta,43 Gemma 3:27b from Google,44 Phi-4 from Microsoft,45 Granite 3.2:8b from IBM,46 and Qwen2.5:72b from Alibaba Cloud.47 Given its popularity and robust performance in many tasks, we have also included GPT-4o from OpenAI in our evaluations, despite it being a closed model with associated costs.48,49 We additionally included a model tuned specifically for medical use, MedLlama3v-20.50,51

Clinical CST Data Set

In our evaluations of target/trial gene target accuracy, we used our previous work on cell surface genes as a ground-truth list of clinical trials.25 For matching gene targets to clinical trials, we leveraged both the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Drug Dictionary and intervention labels as reported by the clinical trial directly, as well as the Therapeutic Target Database.52

Prompting and Workflow

For both target calling and cell surface assignment, prompting was performed using application programming interfaces (APIs). For the Llama 3.1:70b and Llama 3.3, Gemma 3:27b, Phi-4, Granite 3.2:8b, Qwen2.5:72b, and MedLlama3v-20 models, we used ollama53 to conduct our queries. For GPT-4o queries, we used the API provided by OpenAI.

Cell Surface Localization

Gene lists were compiled from published data sets,54-60 GO Term Cell Surface (GO0009986),61,62 and the Human Protein Atlas.63,64 We created a ground-truth cell surface gene data set by selecting all genes that met at least one of the following criteria: (1) identified as a cell surface gene in an experimental data set and (2) identified as a cell surface gene in at least four of the seven computational data sets.

Analysis and Visualization

Analyses were performed using R 4.0.4, R 4.4.3,65 and Python 3.12.9.66

All prompts and additional methodologic details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

RESULTS

LLM Generation of a CST Clinical Trial Database

As of our cutoff date of May 25, 2025, ClinicalTrials.gov listed over 100,000 trials and the associated intervention and the NCI Drug Dictionary contained approximately 8,700 drugs. However, neither of these consistently identify the class or specific target in an indexable format, if at all. There is also no other up-to-date database that identifies CST clinical trials and their targets to our knowledge. Leveraging the emergent ability of LLMs to read and interpret these text databases can greatly improve the efficiency of identifying CST trials/targets and provide an automated way of creating and maintaining a CST clinical trial database67,68 (Fig 1). To achieve this, we first used LLMs to identify which trial drugs were CSTs. Then, we used a two-pronged approach, prompting each LLM to identify each drug target based on either the drug name in ClinicalTrials.gov or the drug description in the NCI Drug Database (Fig 2A). To establish a ground truth for evaluating LLM performance, we manually curated a list of CST clinical trials and their gene targets up to October 2023.25 This entire pipeline is automated and can be reproduced on-demand, allowing clinicians access to the most up-to-date CST clinical trial information when considering their patient's trial options.

LLM Performance—Identifying CST Trials

The first step in matching patients with trials is to identify which trials use CSTs. In total, we investigated six different general open models (Llama 3.1:70b, Llama 3.3, Gemma 3:27b, Phi-4, Granite 3.2:8b, and Qwen2.5:72b), as well as an open medical LLM (MedLlama3-v20) and a closed model (GPT-4o). We selected a range of open models since they can be downloaded for free and run locally, along with the closed GPT-4o model, which is widely used with excellent performance across domains. We evaluated these LLMs in a human-curated set of 814 CST trials.25 We also randomly selected an equal number of trials outside of this list to serve as non-CST controls. Details of generating the known CST and non-CST trial data sets can be found in the Supplemental Methods. GPT-4o performed the best, with the combined approach achieving an overall accuracy of 96.5%. The precision, recall, and F1 score were 0.937, 0.993, and 0.964, respectively (Table 1). It accurately identified 99.3% of the human-curated CST trials and 93.4% of the non-CST trials as such (Fig 2B). We then generated two alternative prompts for this task (Supplemental Methods). Prompt format, length, and information all affected results, with longer, more detailed prompts generally showing better performance (Data Supplement, Fig S1).69-73 Precision, recall, and F1 scores for all prompt versions are given in Table 1.

LLM Performance—Identifying CST Trial Targets

Since LLMs can accurately identify CSTs from non-CST trials, we next examined whether LLMs could also identify the target of the CST trials. To evaluate performance, we looked at both the accuracy in identifying the correct target(s) from the therapies used in each clinical trial (the target-level accuracy) and if a trial was correctly selected when starting from each CST (the trial-level accuracy). Using the combined approach, the GPT-4o model again had the best performance of those tested, with an accuracy of approximately 89.5% at both the trial and target level (Fig 2C). We also found that Llama 3.1:70b and Llama 3.3 have similar performance to GPT-4o (Data Supplement, Table S1). These findings are consistent with other work that demonstrates GPT models generally outperform open LLMs in both zero-shot and few-shot clinical trial–related tasks.11,39,74 Alternative prompts demonstrated that larger models benefit from longer, more detailed prompts, whereas smaller models benefit from shorter, more directed prompts (Data Supplement, Fig S2).

We found that the combination of the drug name (ClinicalTrials.gov) and description (NCI Drug Dictionary) yielded better performance than each individually (Fig 2D). While there were a large number of common matches, LLMs using each data source also find a unique subset of trials (Figs 2E and 2F). Interestingly, while GPT-4o successfully matches a similar number of therapies using either data set, other models perform comparatively worse with drug name alone and derive a greater percentage of overall matches from the description (Fig 2D, Data Supplement, Table S1). This suggests that part of GPT-4o's superior performance lies in its general knowledgebase, whereas other models rely more heavily on direct text interpretation.

The class of the CST can also affect target match accuracy. We found that CAR-related therapies generally were the most difficult to correctly identify (Fig 2G). This is likely indicative of more complicated naming schemes for CAR-related therapies when compared with straightforward and standardized drug names for other CSTs. We also investigated the effect of clinical trial phase and found that accuracy was lowest for phase I trials and progressively increased with later phase trials (Fig 2H). This is most likely due to the more complete information available for therapies in late-stage trials.

Factors Contributing to Target/Trial Matching Errors

We next performed an analysis on the underlying causes of situations where the LLM approach was not able to match a target and clinical trial, focused on the GPT-4o model, since it had the best performance. We investigated the 87 target-trial pairs that were not correctly matched by the GPT-4o model using either the drug name alone from ClinicalTrials.gov or the description in the NCI database and grouped errors into seven categories (Fig 3A). Forty-three errors were caused by vagueness in the therapy description in the clinical trial data (eg, “Car T cells” without specifying more; deemed “Vague Therapy Name”). Ten errors were caused by similar vagueness in the NCI drug database description (deemed “Vague Therapy Description”). These errors are due to a lack of information and likely would require human updating of the databases to fix. However, other errors were due to the inherent limitations of LLMs, which may be overcome as the models continue to advance. Nineteen errors were due to the fact that the therapy in the clinical trial did not match any drug in the NCI drug database (deemed “No NCI Match”). Twenty-seven errors were caused by string matching challenges for a drug. Fourteen errors were caused by errors interpreting the correct target in a complex therapy name (deemed “On-Topic”). Thirty-eight times, the LLM was not able to identify the target (deemed “No Target Identified”) and 23 errors were hallucinations. Hallucinations are when an LLM generates incorrect but plausible-appearing answers and are a well-known problem both inside and outside of the health care space.75-78 We did not observe any particular association between the error category and the therapy type (Fig 3B). We repeated this analysis on the Llama 3.3 model and found similar results (Data Supplement, Figs S3A-S3C), with most differences being a change in error type from “hallucination” to “no target identified” or vice versa (Data Supplement, Figs S3D-S3G).

Geographic Distribution of LLM Performance

Trial availability is a major barrier to patient enrollment, driven by physician knowledge of available trials and patient proximity to open trials.3,5 Of the five states with the largest number of CST trials in our curated list (California, Texas, New York, Florida, Washington), four of these states are among the most populous.81 The highest rate of target-trial match errors by GPT-4o was 10.53% in South Dakota although this represented an absolute number of only two incorrect trials. Overall, our workflow was able to match a target to 94% of the 483 trials in the United States (Fig 4A). Next, we expanded our investigation of model accuracy globally. Notably, not every country will register trials on ClinicalTrials.gov. The United States had the most trials, followed by China, Spain, France, and Italy. China had the highest rate of trial match errors at 16.17%, with New Zealand as the second highest with a rate of 11.11% (Fig 4B). Other than these exceptions, trials in countries outside of the United States had a match rate exceeding 95%.

LLM-Based Cell Surface Annotation

Accurate annotation of the cell surface localization of potential targets is increasingly important, both for the identification of clinical trial targets and for drug development efforts. However, experimental evidence does not exist for every gene. Therefore, we sought to use LLMs to make predictions on cell surface localization based solely on their existing knowledge (Fig 5A). Most LLMs were able to accurately identify almost all CSTs in our human-curated clinical trial list as such (Fig 5B). Gemma 3:27b and MedLlama3 had the most accurate results, successfully labeling all the clinical cell surface targets as cell surface–localized. All models performed quite well in this task, with the exception of Granite 3.2:8b, potentially related to its small parameter size.

To investigate this question genome-wide, we chose to focus on Gemma 3:27b and the top 3 best models from the target-trial matching task and the medically tuned MedLlama3. Each model was prompted to label every protein coding gene as cell surface or not (Fig 5C). We found that calls across replicates were highly reproducible (Fig 5D). We then compared the performance against a benchmark set of CSTs derived from the literature. Gemma 3:27b, Llama 3.3, and Qwen2.5:72b tended to more liberally assign cell surface localization, with increased false-positive rates relative to our benchmark (Fig 5E). This was extremely pronounced in MedLlama3, where nearly every gene was labeled as cell surface. Interestingly, GPT-4o showed a more conservative bias, having a higher rate of false negatives than false positives. Alternative prompting strategies showed that responses were both prompt and model-specific (Data Supplement, Fig S4). The precision, recall, and F1 scores for this task are given in Table 1. Overall, the performance in this task varies significantly based on the model used and demonstrates that LLMs have more difficulty in identifying subcellular localization, reflecting the real-world difficulty of this task from both an experimental and computational standpoint.

DISCUSSION

Improved matching of patients to clinical trials is critical for the success of new cancer treatments.10 The targeted nature and pan-cancer coverage of trials make this particularly challenging for CSTs as a treating physician would need to know all trials for all targets across all cancers—an impossible task. Trial awareness and availability are thus major barriers to enrollment,3,5 and no centralized and up-to-date database exists for CST trials. In this study, we benchmarked LLMs against a human-curated list of CST trials. We show that LLMs can accurately identify CST trials. We then develop a novel LLM-driven approach for identifying the targets of these trials. This approach demonstrates high accuracy, especially for non–Asia-Pacific and later-phase trials. This allows for a fully automated workflow that generates an up-to-date database of CST clinical trials. When this approach is paired with high-throughput methods for identifying and confirming potential targets expressed in a patient,25-37 it can dramatically enable expanded clinical trial options for patients.

We provide this updated list of open phase I-III trials through May 25, 2025, and their targets in the Data Supplement (Table S2). For example, if a patient with an esophageal or gastroesophageal junction cancer was identified as expressing human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (aka ERBB2), then examining this list, we would find 112 potential ERBB2 trials across a variety of cancers, which can be further filtered down to 13 trials targeting ERBB2 in esophageal or gastroesophageal junction cancers.

While the performance of our LLM approach is excellent compared with a human-curated ground truth, it will likely never be perfect. When the LLM inadvertently assigns a clinical trial incorrectly to a target, this is often easily identified by the treating physician or clinical trial staff. An error of omission is more problematic as it is unlikely the physician or patient would be able to identify that this had occurred. However, consider a hypothetical case where five additional trials were correctly identified for a particular patient beyond what the treating physician was aware of and one potential additional trial was missed by the LLM. This is still five more trials than would have been available previously. Thus, the potential for benefit is high, with a low impact from errors. Therapies introduced after an LLM's knowledge cutoff date could create temporary gaps in recognition, particularly when trial postings precede updates to external resources. This lag is an inherent challenge for any static knowledge system. However, our pipeline is designed to overcome this limitation by being model-agnostic. Users can integrate any LLM of their choice, and as the NCI Drug Dictionary is updated, the pipeline can incorporate this new information without retraining, ensuring long-term adaptability and robustness as the clinical trial landscape evolves.

We note that larger models generally perform better than smaller models across tasks, which is consistent with the existing literature.69,82 We found that MedLlama3 performed worse than expected compared with similarly sized models, likely due in part to catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning.83,84

Matching patients with expression of CSTs with trials targeting those specific targets has potential benefits for both patients and the trials themselves. This is reflected in the eligibility criteria of many CST trials although even in trials without restricted eligibility, most patients and physicians would still choose a trial for a highly expressed target. Increasing the rate of enrollment to CST trials for patients with expression of the target can also benefit the trials themselves as the odds of success may be higher, and a negative trial is more easily interpreted as a failure of the therapy as opposed to simply suboptimal patient selection. Ultimately, this approach can vastly streamline and grow our ability to identify appropriate clinical trials for patients while simultaneously improving clinical trial enrollment and providing therapies to the patients most likely to derive benefit.
