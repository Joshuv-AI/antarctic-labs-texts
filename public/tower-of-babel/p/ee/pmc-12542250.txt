Migration of Hem-O-Lok clip into the bronchus after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery accompanied by Pseudomonas aeruginosa: a case report
Authors: Beibei Meng, Juan Ren, Zhenhong Hu, Haichao Liu, Lei Qu, Yanwen Wu, Huiyun Liao, Ying Wang, Xiaoyu Cai, Congzheng Mao, Yao Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Hem-O-Lok clip is frequently used as a surgical consumable for the occlusion of blood vessels and bronchi during Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS). However, there is limited available literature addressing the potential complications associated with its clinical application.Case presentationThis article reports a patient with bronchiectasis who underwent VATS for treatment of hemoptysis on August 12, 2021. During the postoperative follow-up, chest CT scan accidentally revealed a displaced Hem-O-Lok clip located within the tracheal lumem on April 12, 2024. ConclusionThis case suggests a potential risk of Hem-O-Lok clip detachment after VATS, underscoring the importance of maintaining clinical vigilance and controlling chronic infection in postoperative patient management.

Background

Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS) is a minimally invasive surgical technique widely utilized in pulmonary procedures. It enables partial or complete resection of lung lobes through the use of a miniature camera and specialized surgical instruments. This approach provides several advantages, such as reduced surgical trauma, faster postoperative recovery, and shorter hospitalization duration [1]. Hem-O-Lok clips are extensively used in biliary, urinary, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal surgeries owing to their low tissue reactivity, wide ligation range, consistent clamping force, and ability to secure structures [2–5]. In the context of VATS, Hem-O-Lok clips are frequently used for ligating blood vessels, lymphatic ducts, and occasionally certain bronchial structures. This case presented the gradual migration of a Hem-O-Lok clip into the bronchial lumen, rarely reported previously.

Case presentation

On August 2, 2021, a 55-year-old female patient was admitted to the hospital with a history of persistent hemoptysis lasting over one year. She had previously been diagnosed with bronchiectasis complicated by infection at a tertiary care facility in April 2021. Despite receiving conservative treatment, her symptoms recurred and were refractory to treatment.

Surgical course

On August 12, 2021, the patient underwent VATS on the right upper lobe of the lung at the same tertiary hospital due to hemoptysis. During the procedure, the surgeon carefully performed dissection and used a linear cutting stapler to securely seal the bronchus of the right upper lobe. However, subsequent lung inflation testing revealed incomplete closure of the residual bronchus, and minor air leakage was observed. Consequently, Hem-O-Lok clips were applied to effectively occlude the inadequately sealed segment. After the surgery, no further hemoptysis recurred.

Follow-up

The patient underwent a chest CT scan on April 12, 2024, which displayed a slightly increased-density shadow within the tracheal lumen. However, bronchoscopy revealed that the right upper lobe of the lung had healed satisfactorily. No foreign bodies or airway obstructions were identified, and no evidence of fistula formation. After thoroughly rinsing and removing purulent secretions, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was sent for bacterial culture and confirmed the positivity of mucoid pseudomonas aeruginosa (++++) (Fig. 1A). After anti-infection treatment, fourteen days later, a follow-up chest CT scan did not display abnormality (Fig. 1B). Nevertheless, on October 6 of the same year, the patient was readmitted due to “recurrent productive cough and worsening wheezing” . This time, the chest CT scan suggested a foreign body with slightly increased-density located within the trachea. Bronchoscopy confirmed the presence of a white foreign body lodged in the middle lobe and the dorsal segment of the lower lobe of the right lung (Fig. 1C). The white foreign body was safely removed with forceps and was subsequently identified as a Hem-O-Lok clip (Fig. 1D). The BALF sample was still indentified positive of mucoid pseudomonas aeruginosa (++++). After removal of the Hem-O-Lok clip and administration of antimicrobial therapy using cefoperazone/sulbactam and moxifloxacin, the patient’s respiratory condition improved significantly. Discussion and conclusions

The Hem-O-Lok clip is fabricated from an absorbable polymer and incorporates a secure locking mechanism, curved legs, anti-slip teeth, and elastic hinges (as Figure D shown). It effectively addresses the limitations of traditional metal clips, such as the risks of tissue trauma, clip migration, and electrical conductivity. Furthermore, it provides several advantages, including low tissue reactivity, a broad ligating range, even distribution of clamping force, and a non-tissue-cutting design [6]. It is widely favored for its enhanced safety and reliability across multiple clinical applications [7]. The Hem-O-Lok clip has become one of the most prevalent and extensively used surgical instruments in VATS.

To date, only one prior case report has documented the incidental identification of Hem-O-Lok clip displacement into the bronchus during routine postoperative follow-up. In this case, postoperative edema at the anastomosis site exacerbated the tension on the suture line, resulting in the formation of this fistula [8]. Our study reports a case of successful management of bronchiectasis with massive hemoptysis through VATS. Our findings indicate that the displacement of the Hem-O-Lok clip may be attributed to a combination of multiple factors. First, the continuous elastic contraction caused by long-term respiratory movements, along with its anatomical proximity, may have led to gradual erosion and penetration of the bronchial wall by the clip. Second, postoperative inflammatory or immune responses could potentially have contributed to this migration. Third, mucoid-type Pseudomonas aeruginosa, residing in bronchus, may cause localized tissue damage and indirectly increase the risk of device migration [9, 10]. Furthermore, the migration may also be promoted by tissue erosion, poor tissue integration, and the effects of surgical intervention.

This case highlights a potential displacement associated with Hem-O-Lok clip and underscores the importance of clinical vigilance after VATS. Postoperative dynamic imaging is recommended to monitor the migration trajectory in bronchus, especially for those patients with chronic respiratory tract infection.
