Research on hydraulic shock suppression of crawler crane slewing mechanism based on hydraulic simulation
Authors: Yulan Wei, Yuncong Gu, Yang Zhang, Tongxi Zhu, Boyang Zhang, Qingzhu Zhang, Qiang Zhang, Bing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the rapid startup and braking of the crawler crane’s slewing mechanism, excessive transient pressure peaks in the hydraulic motor’s inlet and return lines generate significant hydraulic shock. This shock induces intense vibration in the cab, compromising operational safety and reducing the equipment’s service life. First, baseline hydraulic data were analyzed to identify the underlying causes of the hydraulic shock. Subsequently, a hydraulic system optimization model was developed, and a co-simulation model of the crawler crane was established using AMESim and Adams to validate the effectiveness of the proposed optimization scheme. Finally, the simulation results were verified against field test data. The results demonstrate that the optimized hydraulic system reduces hydraulic shock by approximately 50% in simulations, with a validated simulation accuracy exceeding 90% based on real machine measurements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42887-0.Keywords: Hydraulic simulation, Hydraulic shock, Crawler crane, VibrationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the rapid development of global infrastructure construction, the market demand for crawler cranes has continued to grow, accompanied by increasingly stringent requirements for their operational performance1. In practical working conditions, the hydraulic shock within the slewing system is a key factor affecting the hoisting stability of crawler cranes. When the slewing hydraulic system executes rapid start or braking commands, the internal pressure surges instantaneously and forms peak pressure, inducing intense hydraulic shock2. This shock load, transmitted through the mechanical structure, leads to strong vibrations in the upper platform, the operator’s cab, and the boom system, which significantly compromises hoisting precision and construction safety.

As an indispensable core component of a crawler crane, the dynamic characteristics of the slewing mechanism directly dictate the overall operational efficiency and control performance3. In fact, high stability requirements for slewing systems are universal across various types of construction machinery, such as hydraulic rock drills, cantilever roadheaders, and overhead cranes4–7. While the slewing mechanism relies on complex hydraulic circuits to realize rotational motion, its enormous inertial load makes it a primary source of hydraulic shock. This frequent hydraulic shock leads to multi-dimensional adverse effects8,9: firstly, mechanical components are prone to abnormal vibration and fatigue wear under the action of shock waves, leading to premature failure of key parts; secondly, intense noise and vibration deteriorate the dynamic stability of the mechanical system; furthermore, pressure fluctuations diminish the response accuracy and reliability of the hydraulic control system; ultimately, accumulated hydraulic shock shortens equipment service life and significantly elevates maintenance costs.

Aiming at the optimization of slewing mechanisms, scholars worldwide have carried out extensive research. Jin10 and Gao11 utilized AMESim simulation technology to analyze the influence of load-sensing pumps and related parameters on the dynamic characteristics of the system. Zhou12 and Yang et al.13 verified the correctness of excavator trajectory control and hydraulic motor models through Adams and AMESim co-simulation, respectively. B. Jerman14 investigated the impact of extreme inputs on load sway through mathematical models. Additionally, researchers such as Wang15, Xie16, Ding17, and Fan18 proposed optimization strategies from the perspectives of finite element analysis, circuit improvement, and pressure suppression, which mitigated the pressure shock of the slewing system to a certain extent.

Despite advancements in hydraulic shock suppression, certain limitations persist: (1) Existing studies focus predominantly on the hydraulic shock of slewing systems in excavators or small-to-medium wheeled cranes, while research targeting large-tonnage, high-inertia crawler cranes remains scarce; (2) There are few studies utilizing mechanical-hydraulic co-simulation methods to optimize the slewing systems of large-tonnage crawler cranes. Therefore, conducting in-depth research on the suppression of hydraulic shock during the start-stop phases of crawler crane slewing mechanisms is of great significance for reducing overall machine vibrations.

To suppress the hydraulic shock in the slewing systems of large-tonnage crawler cranes, this study presents an optimized design for the hydraulic system and its key components. A mechanical-hydraulic co-simulation approach, integrating Adams and AMESim, is employed to analyze the dynamic performance of the slewing mechanism. Finally, the proposed optimization is validated through experimental analysis on a full-scale crawler crane.

Optimization of the hydraulic system of the rotary mechanism

Conventional hydraulic system for slewing mechanisms

The characteristics of the crawler crane’s slewing mechanism directly influence the overall operational performance of the machine. Ideally, the slewing mechanism should maintain a stable operating speed that is independent of the movements of other mechanisms. However, during transient processes, the magnitude of the driving torque fluctuates significantly, which has a substantial impact on both the components and the load19.

Due to the large rotational inertia of heavy-duty crawler cranes, rapid starting or braking generates intense hydraulic shock caused by inertial forces20,21. If the hydraulic system is not properly designed, this shock can result in severe vibrations and even compromise safety22. Therefore, optimizing the hydraulic system design is critical for improving machine efficiency, mitigating operational vibrations, and enhancing the overall dynamic characteristics of the hydraulic control system23–25. As indicated in Table 1, the simulated hydraulic shock in the conventional slewing mechanism generally exceeds 460 bar/s, reaching peaks of over 1000 bar/s. Such high-magnitude shock generates severe vibrations, which, over the long term, compromise the stability of the hydraulic system and the safety of the slewing mechanism. During climbing or heavy-load operations, the hydraulic system undergoes significant pressure fluctuations within the motor chambers to maintain equilibrium. Simultaneously, rapid startup and braking maneuvers induce abrupt speed changes, resulting in substantial flow impact. While these conditions appear as high hydraulic shock values in the simulation data, in practice, they manifest as violent vibrations within the crawler crane’s cab.

Optimization of the hydraulic system of the slewing mechanism

To mitigate the cab vibration caused by hydraulic shock resulting from significant flow surges and pressure fluctuations in the crawler crane slewing mechanism, an optimization scheme for the hydraulic system is proposed. The specific measures are as follows:

Optimization of the Slewing Valve Neutral Function (H-type to O-type) The neutral function of the slewing valve spool was changed from H-type (normally open) to O-type (normally closed). The characteristics of these two configurations are compared below:H-type (Open Center): Ports P, A, B, and T are interconnected. The hydraulic motor is in a floating state and can be moved by external forces. Since the pump unloads to the tank in the neutral position, re-establishing pressure during the transition from stop to start causes significant hydraulic shock.O-type (Closed Center): Ports P, A, B, and T are closed. The hydraulic pump does not unload, and the hydraulic motor is locked. The return chamber remains filled with oil, providing a damping effect.Conclusion: Switching from H-type to O-type effectively cushions the slewing action, thereby reducing system vibration and improving response speed during startup.

(2)Optimization of the Damping Orifice Diameter The diameter of the damping orifice in the slewing spool was reduced from Φ0.8 mm to Φ0.5 mm. Simulations indicated that hydraulic shock is highly sensitive to the orifice diameter. A larger orifice allows for a rapid surge in flow rate, which intensifies hydraulic shock. Conversely, reducing the orifice diameter increases the control precision of the valve and utilizes the damping effect to suppress hydraulic system vibration.

(3)Application of Counterbalance Valves The original overload relief valves were replaced with counterbalance valves.Limitation of Relief Valves: Overload relief valves primarily limit pressure for protection. They cannot actively regulate flow based on load conditions, often requiring complex porting to manage flow rates effectively.Advantage of Counterbalance Valves: Counterbalance valves regulate flow by dynamically adjusting the opening (gap) between the spool and the valve seat, thereby altering flow resistance. Conclusion: Compared to overload relief valves, the use of counterbalance valves results in more stable motion control, higher efficiency, and a more compact system design.

Limitation of Relief Valves: Overload relief valves primarily limit pressure for protection. They cannot actively regulate flow based on load conditions, often requiring complex porting to manage flow rates effectively.

Advantage of Counterbalance Valves: Counterbalance valves regulate flow by dynamically adjusting the opening (gap) between the spool and the valve seat, thereby altering flow resistance. Conclusion: Compared to overload relief valves, the use of counterbalance valves results in more stable motion control, higher efficiency, and a more compact system design.

(4)Integration of Make-up (Replenishment) Valves Two make-up valves were added to the system. During rapid startup and braking, drastic internal pressure changes can lead to oil depletion in the pipelines, causing transient negative pressure (cavitation). The make-up valve detects pressure drops in the motor chamber and automatically opens to replenish oil. This modification effectively prevents cavitation, significantly improving the reliability, flexibility, and stability of the hydraulic system.

Optimization analysis of hydraulic systems

Analysis of single strategy optimization effect

Verification of rotary spool inlet optimization.

Verification of rotary spool inlet optimization.

With all other hydraulic components remaining unchanged, a comparative simulation was conducted to evaluate the effect of switching the slewing spool’s neutral position from normally open to normally closed. The hydraulic motor inlet pressures during a rapid startup sequence for both the normally open and normally closed configurations are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Hydraulic motor inlet pressure at fast startup with rotary spool inlet port normally open and normally closed.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, with the slewing spool in the normally open configuration, the motor inlet pressure measures 13 bar at 0.5 s and peaks at a maximum of 240 bar at 0.7 s, resulting in a start-up hydraulic shock of 1135 bar/s.

Conversely, when the slewing spool is normally closed, the inlet pressure is 12 bar at 0.5 s and peaks at only 140 bar at 0.7 s, yielding a significantly reduced start-up shock of 640 bar/s. The substantial decrease in start-up shock following the transition from the normally open to the normally closed configuration demonstrates that the optimized system exhibits superior stability.

(2)Verification of rotary spool throttle orifice optimization.

Verification of rotary spool throttle orifice optimization.

While maintaining all other hydraulic components unchanged, simulations were conducted using throttle orifice diameters of 0.8 mm, 0.7 mm, 0.6 mm, 0.5 mm, and 0.4 mm. The hydraulic motor inlet pressures during rapid startup for these different orifice diameters are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Hydraulic motor inlet pressure diagram for different throttle orifice diameters.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the hydraulic motor exhibits varying inlet pressures and hydraulic shock magnitudes during rapid startup across different throttle orifice diameters. The data reveals that in all five cases, the pressure rise initiates at 0.7 s and reaches its maximum peak at 0.9 s. These results are summarized in Table 2. It is evident that the start-up hydraulic shock is minimized when the throttle orifice diameter is 0.5 mm. Consequently, 0.5 mm is determined to be the optimal diameter for the system.

Joint simulation analysis

Joint simulation modeling

Joint simulation modeling

When establishing a co-simulation model, it is critical to ensure synchronized and consistent data exchange between the software platforms. This requires configuring the interfaces of both software packages to enable collaborative operation and real-time data transfer. In this study, AMESim is designated as the primary simulation platform (Master), while Adams serves as the secondary software (Slave).

A 750-ton crawler crane was selected as the experimental subject, and its simulation model was constructed as shown in Fig. 8. The model primarily consists of the main boom, pendant bars, hook block, rear counterweight, mast, slewing mechanism, undercarriage (traveling mechanism), superstructure, and luffing mechanism. The main chords of the boom are constructed from high-strength steel tubing, with each 12 m boom section connected via pins. The hook block has a lifting capacity of 75 t and is equipped with 5 pulleys. The total mass of the rear counterweight is approximately 24 t, consisting of an 8 t base plate (pallet), two 3.9 t blocks, two 4.1 t blocks, and two 2 t detachable blocks.

Conclusion

In order to reduce the hydraulic shock when the crawler crane slewing mechanism starts and stops, and alleviate the vibration of the cab, a hydraulic system optimization scheme is proposed, and the effectiveness of the design method is verified by joint simulation. In this paper, AMESim is the main software and Adams is the auxiliary software to build the joint simulation model and verify the simulation accuracy of the main circuits. An improvement measure to the hydraulic simulation schematic is proposed and an optimized model is built for simulation, and the accuracy of the simulation results is verified by real machine measurement data. The optimized hydraulic system can reduce the hydraulic shock up to 58% under simulation. The accuracy of the simulation data compared with the experimental data is more than 90% as verified by the real machine. The experimental and simulation results show that the designed hydraulic simulation improvement measures can reduce the hydraulic shock of crawler crane slewing system to a certain extent, and the vibration problem caused by the high impact of hydraulic motor inlet pressure and return pressure in a short period of time during the rapid startup and braking of crawler cranes has been effectively alleviated.

However, this study has certain limitations that should be addressed in future work. The analysis of nonlinear effects in the hydraulic system and their underlying mechanisms remains insufficient. Additionally, the validation of long-term system stability requires further extended observation and testing. These aspects represent important directions for subsequent research to enhance the comprehensiveness and practical applicability of the findings.
