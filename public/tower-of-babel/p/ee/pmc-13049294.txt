Incriminating the inotrope: Dobutamine, eosinophilia, and myocarditis
Authors: Abigail M Chang, Rachel U Lee, Lillian E Grobman, Omonigho A Aisagbonhi, Bruce L Zuraw, Sandra C Christiansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report of dobutamine-associated eosinophilia, fever, and eosinophilic myocardial hypersensitivity illustrates the substantial risk of severe sequelae stemming from diagnostic delay. Recognition of this rare condition should catalyze exploration for feasible dobutamine alternatives, thereby minimizing morbidity and optimizing outcomes.Key words: Eosinophilia, dobutamine, myocarditis, hypersensitivity, EMH

Referrals for eosinophilia can pose a challenging undertaking for the consulting allergist. Identifying the precipitating cause necessitates a comprehensive and systematic evaluation: the culprits may include blood dyscrasias, neoplasms, infections, drug reactions, autoimmune diseases, vasculitic and/or inflammatory disorders, atopy, and idiopathic conditions.1 Owing to the underlying complexity, a vigilant approach to gathering the patient’s history, physical examination, laboratory review, and (often) tissue examination is required. In this case report we describe an unusual presentation of eosinophilia with myocardial involvement secondary to dobutamine.

Case description

A 52-year-old man with a history of dilated cardiomyopathy presented to the emergency department with cardiogenic shock and elevated liver enzyme levels. After 2 weeks of extensive clinical evaluation, aggressive diuresis, and dobutamine inotropic support, the patient was discharged with a dobutamine drip. Five days later, he was readmitted with fever and worsening cardiogenic shock, prompting evaluation for cardiac transplantation. Placement of a ventricular assist device was necessary on day 3. Trials of empiric antibiotic therapy did not result in any improvement. The patient’s liver function test results, which normalized by the time of readmission, increased progressively (Fig 1). A liver biopsy on day 12 revealed hepatic ballooning (swollen injured hepatocytes), Mallory hyaline and stage 1a to 2 fibrosis with neutrophilic satellitosis and occasional necrosis without eosinophilic infiltrate were noted. There was no evident dermatologic, renal, or pulmonary abnormality. Allergy was consulted because of intensifying peripheral eosinophilia (Fig 1). At the time of the referral, the patient’s absolute eosinophil count (AEC) had escalated to 1900/mm3. The patient’s ancillary laboratory test results included a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein level of 31.1 (reference range 0-4.9 mg/L). It was notable that eosinophilia (AEC > 500/mm3) had been initially recorded 12 days before the allergy evaluation, by which point the patient had been receiving dobutamine continuously for 34 days. Detailed correlation of drug exposure and timing of eosinophilia onset suggested dobutamine as the most likely culprit. Infectious disease and rheumatology evaluations excluded relevant parasitic, fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens, as well as autoimmune and inflammatory disorders. There was no suggestion of blood dyscrasias, atopy, or underlying neoplasm. Discontinuation of dobutamine was recommended, resulting in prompt AEC normalization and defervescence.

The patient underwent an orthotopic heart transplant 1 day after the allergy evaluation. Explanted cardiac tissue was obtained for examination. As displayed in Fig 2, there was a significant eosinophilic myocardial infiltrate. After the transplant, the patient was initially managed with immunosuppressants, including prednisone. An endomyocardial biopsy sample taken 13 days after the transplant revealed no sign of rejection or eosinophil infiltration. The patient’s eosinophil counts remained in the normal range in the wake of immunosuppression and prednisone discontinuation after the transplant (Fig 1).

Discussion

Here, we have reported a rare but recognized cause of acute eosinophilia, namely, dobutamine administration. Dobutamine has previously been linked to elevated level of peripheral AECs alone and in constellation with fever and myocarditis.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 In each instance, the AEC time course mirrored that of our patient, with delay of onset following exposure, escalation over days, and rapid resolution with dobutamine discontinuation. The features of our case (fever, myocardial organ involvement, and elevated liver function test results) could theoretically raise consideration of an unusual presentation of drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), meeting the threshold criteria for “possible” by Registry of Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions (RegiSCAR) scoring. Other manifestations commonly associated with DRESS, such as facial swelling, lymphadenopathy, mucosal lesions, and noncardiac visceral involvement, were notably absent. The clinical trajectory (which in DRESS is often protracted and severe following drug discontinuation), lack of hepatic eosinophils, and absence of cutaneous involvement (found in < 3% of cases) would be highly atypical for DRESS, weighing strongly against this categorization.8

On genetic screening (MYBPC3 c.1513_1515delAAG; p.K505del), the patient's underlying cardiomyopathy was determined to have a hereditary etiology. Cardiac function, which had improved at the time of initial discharge, decompensated dramatically despite continuous dobutamine administration. CRP level elevation, which is in keeping with the state of cardiogenic shock, is recognized as an independent risk factor for enhanced early mortality.9 In addition to the underlying dilated cardiomyopathy, development of myocarditis with eosinophilic cardiac infiltration provides a contributory pathomechanism for the progressive functional cardiac deterioration. The findings described in the explanted cardiac tissue are consistent with eosinophilic myocardial hypersensitivity (EMH), diagnosed by the presence of an interstitial predominant eosinophilic infiltrate within the myocardium. Patients with EMH typically develop heart failure and are significantly more likely to require an escalation of bridging therapy such as a ventricular assistance device, as in our patient. In a case series of individuals awaiting cardiac transplantation, dobutamine was associated with a 14% incidence of EMH.7 In this cohort, the baseline demographics, dose, and duration of dobutamine therapy failed to indicate which patients were at risk for the development of EMH. The duration of increasing peripheral eosinophil counts, however, was a significant predictor. This retrospective review identified cases of EMH solely associated with the use of intravenous dobutamine. In contrast, milrinone, which belongs to the phosphodiesterase inhibitor class of inotrope, was not correlated with a diagnosis of EMH.7 It has been hypothesized that dobutamine-induced hypersensitivity is a type IV hypersensitivity reaction to the sulfite excipient. However, observational studies demonstrate that only 25% to 57% of patients with EMH have previously reported allergies, and only a fraction of those allergies are sulfite specific.5 Additional exploration is needed to clarify the fundamental mechanism of dobutamine-associated eosinophilia and EMH.

Conclusion

Dobutamine continues to be widely utilized to stabilize patients in cardiogenic shock. The rarity of dobutamine as a causal factor for eosinophilia raises the substantial risk of diagnostic delay. This concern was exemplified in our case by the protracted elevation in AEC preceding a request for allergy evaluation. The ensuing cardiac decompensation in the setting of EMH underscores the severe potential sequelae stemming from a failure to identify the connection with dobutamine administration. We have reported our experience to highlight the association of dobutamine with onset of acute eosinophilia. It is reasonable to speculate that timely dobutamine discontinuation or substitution of an alternative inotrope such as milrinone may have reduced the likelihood of EMH progression or mitigated its severity. Early recognition of increased AEC due to dobutamine could thus catalyze the exploration for feasible alternatives, thereby minimizing morbidity and optimizing patient outcomes.
