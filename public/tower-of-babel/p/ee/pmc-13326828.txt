Preoperative Computed Tomography Colonography‐Angiography for Vascular Mapping to Guide and Standardize D3 Lymphadenectomy in Right‐Sided and Transverse Colon Cancer
Authors: Takeharu Kato, Yukihide Kanemitsu, Mototaka Miyake, Gen Tomizawa, Kohei Takura, Hiroshi Nagata, Yasuyuki Takamizawa, Konosuke Moritani, Shunsuke Tsukamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn right hemicolectomy (RHC) and left‐sided transverse colectomy (TC), D3 lymph node dissection is oncologically significant but technically demanding because of marked vascular variability. Standardization requires reliable preoperative anatomical assessment. We evaluated colic vascular anatomy and quantified D3 dissection distance using computed tomography colonography‐angiography (CTC‐A).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 591 patients with primary right or transverse colon cancer who underwent preoperative CTC‐A during 2014–2023. Arterial origins, venous drainage, and positional relationships around the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and vein (SMV) were classified. After excluding aberrant cases, branching patterns relevant to D3 dissection were categorized and D3 distances measured along the SMA centerline using curved planar reformation images.ResultsAn accessory middle colic artery (aMCA) was present in 38.1% of patients (SMA‐derived, 76.4%). The MCA originated as a common trunk in 77.8% and as independent branches in 19.0%. The right colic artery (RCA) was observed in 41.8%. Four reproducible vascular patterns each were identified for RHC and TC. In RHC (n = 490), independent‐origin MCA was consistently associated with shorter D3 distances (RCA‐present, 22.5 vs. 27.5 mm, p = 0.012; RCA‐absent, 16.5 vs. 20.5 mm, p < 0.001). In TC (n = 524), the aMCA originated dorsal to the pancreas in ~23%, and D3 distance was shorter in aMCAs with an independent‐origin MCA (18.0 vs. 25.0 mm, p < 0.001).ConclusionsHigh‐resolution CTC‐A yielded a single‐modality, operation‐oriented mapping integrating tumor location with mesenteric arteriovenous anatomy. Pattern‐based classification and objective D3 distance estimation may support preoperative planning and help establish standardized, safer D3 lymphadenectomy for right‐sided and transverse colon cancer.Keywords: colon cancer, computed tomography angiography, computed tomography colonography, lymphadenectomy, minimally invasive surgical procedures

Introduction

Complete mesocolic excision (CME) and central vascular ligation (CVL) for colon cancer have been reported to reduce local recurrence and improve survival, and are now widely adopted as the standard of care in Western countries [1]. In Japan, D3 lymphadenectomy, defined as ligation of the tumor‐feeding artery at its root with en bloc mesocolic resection, has long been a standard practice, and its philosophy is broadly aligned with CME/CVL [2]. A distinctive feature of D3 lymphadenectomy is that the extent of dissection is determined by the level of arterial ligation, highlighting the need for precise vascular assessment [1].

Because of the marked anatomical variability around the right‐sided and transverse mesocolon, preoperative simulation of the tumor location, feeding vessels, and mesenteric vascular anatomy is essential for setting an appropriate D3 field and resection margin. Computed tomography colonography (CTC) achieves highly accurate tumor localization [3], and with a triphasic contrast‐enhanced protocol, CTC‐angiography (CTC‐A) can visualize both the tumor and vascular anatomy in a single three‐dimensional dataset.

For central lymph node dissection during right hemicolectomy (RHC), the target field typically spans from the ileocolic artery (ICA) to the middle colic artery (MCA). In particular, prophylactic lymphadenectomy around the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) is considered for transverse colon cancer and for cecal/ascending colon cancers with suspected nodal involvement at the ICA or right colic artery (RCA) roots [4, 5]. For left‐sided transverse colon cancer, the extent of resection depends on the left branch of the MCA and the presence and course of an accessory MCA (aMCA). Standardization of these procedures is difficult owing to marked branching variability around the SMA and superior mesenteric vein (SMV) and the constraints imposed by adjacent organs, notably the pancreas and duodenum.

Accordingly, safe CME/CVL requires preoperative vascular mapping. In right hemicolectomy, surgeons must delineate the branching patterns of the ICA, RCA, and MCA from the SMA and their relationship to the SMV. When planning left‐sided transverse colectomy (TC), it is also important to identify the aMCA and its relationship to the inferior pancreatic border. Triphasic CTC‐A can depict tumor location and mesenteric arteriovenous anatomy in a single three‐dimensional dataset, which is useful for preoperative simulation in RHC and TC [6]. Although numerous anatomical studies have been carried out, most have focused on individual branches such as the aMCA or RCA [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12], and few comprehensive analyses have used CTC‐A with high resolution (0.5 mm).

Therefore, in this single‐center cohort study, we used high‐resolution (0.5‐mm) CTC‐A to systematically characterize mesenteric vascular branching around the SMA and SMV and to classify anatomical patterns relevant to RHC and TC. In addition, we quantified the D3 dissection distance using curved planar reformation along the SMA centerline. By simultaneously integrating tumor location, arterial branching, and venous drainage within a single modality, this study provides the first comprehensive classification directly linked to the extent of D3 lymphadenectomy, including objective quantification of the required dissection distance.

Methods

Data Sources and Study Design

This retrospective study used data from a prospectively maintained database at the National Cancer Center Hospital in Tokyo, Japan. We screened 619 consecutive patients who underwent preoperative CTC‐A and curative‐intent surgery with lymphadenectomy for a first primary colorectal cancer located in the appendix, cecum, ascending colon, or transverse colon between 2014 and 2023. Patients with a history of gastrectomy involving dissection around the gastrocolic venous trunk (n = 9) or synchronous colorectal cancers (n = 19) were excluded, leaving 591 patients for analysis. Note that cases of perforation and intestinal obstruction were not included in this study because CTC was not performed under those conditions.

Contrast‐Enhanced CT Colonography Protocol

Immediately after lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, patients proceeded to the CT suite for CTC without fecal tagging. Bowel preparation consisted of oral polyethylene glycol solution (MOVIPREP; EA Pharma Co., Tokyo, Japan) taken before endoscopy. Scans were obtained on an 80–detector‐row multidetector CT (MDCT) scanner (Aquilion PRIME, Canon Medical Systems Co., Tochigi, Japan), including non‐contrast, arterial, portal venous, and delayed phase acquisitions.

An enema tube was inserted into the rectum and carbon dioxide insufflation was performed (PROTOCO2L Insufflator, E‐Z‐EM Inc., New York, NY) just before CTC. To shift residual stool and fluid and minimize blind areas, imaging was performed in two positions: prone for the non‐contrast series and supine for the three contrast‐enhanced phases. For arterial‐phase triggering using bolus tracking, a region of interest was placed at the L2–L3 intervertebral level, and a non‐ionic iodinated contrast agent (600 mgI/kg) was injected over 30 s. The portal venous phase was acquired 30 s after the arterial phase, while the delayed phase was acquired 180 s after contrast injection. The slice thickness was set to 0.5 mm. This protocol was consistently applied throughout the study period.

CT Colonography‐Angiography Reconstruction

All CT data were transferred to a dedicated workstation (Ziostation 2, Ziosoft Inc., Tokyo, Japan). For CTC, the portal venous phase data were used to generate a virtual dissection view to depict the tumor location and any post‐endoscopic clips, as well as to create a virtual barium enema view. For CTA, the arterial phase was used to reconstruct the SMA and its branches, and the portal venous phase was used to reconstruct the SMV system and its tributaries.

Radiological Analysis

Analyses were performed retrospectively by a single gastrointestinal surgeon (Observer 1) using CTC‐A images, together with contrast‐enhanced coronal and sagittal images, according to the definitions below. The vascular classifications determined by Observer 1 constituted the final dataset for the main analysis.

To evaluate interobserver reproducibility, a stratified random subset of 100 cases was selected to include both common and uncommon/complex variants. A second independent board‐certified gastrointestinal surgeon (Observer 2), blinded to Observer 1's results, re‐evaluated the same CTC‐A dataset for the aMCA, MCA, and RCA classifications using the predefined criteria. Interobserver agreement was quantified using percent agreement and unweighted Cohen's kappa (κ) with 95% confidence intervals. Because the second reading was performed solely to assess reproducibility, discrepant interpretations were not adjudicated and did not alter the final dataset based on Observer 1's classifications. For discordant cases in the reproducibility subset, we retrospectively reviewed operative records and/or videos to qualitatively assess concordance when the target vessel was exposed and documented; intraoperative information was not used for adjudication.

Definitions

Arterial nomenclature (ICA, RCA, MCA, and aMCA) followed conventional colorectal surgical terminology, consistent with the Japanese Classification of Colorectal, Appendiceal, and Anal Carcinoma (JSCCR), in which these arteries constitute the anatomical basis for lymph node grouping and D3 lymphadenectomy [2].

Branching‐pattern classification was performed to facilitate reproducible anatomical interpretation for surgical simulation. For this purpose, branching patterns were categorized into commonly described configurations and other anatomically variant patterns based on vessel‐specific rationales derived from foundational anatomical studies [13] and contemporary anatomical and imaging‐based literature [14, 15].

For the ICA and RCA, a classical pattern was defined in a deliberately strict manner as independent origin from the SMA. Any non‐independent configuration—including ICA–RCA common trunks, secondary origins from another colic artery, or duplicated vessels—was categorized as an aberrant pattern (or classified as absence when not identified).

For the MCA, both (1) a single MCA trunk arising from the SMA and subsequently dividing into right and left branches and (2) a two‐stem configuration in which the right and left branches arise independently from the SMA have been widely described as conventional patterns in classical anatomical literature and contemporary imaging‐based studies. Accordingly, both configurations were included within the classical spectrum in the present study.

Accessory Middle Colic Artery

An aMCA was defined as an artery running along the inferior border of the pancreas within the transverse mesocolon and supplying the splenic flexure. This definition is consistent with previous anatomical and CT angiography studies reporting that, although the aMCA most commonly originates from the superior mesenteric artery, it may also arise from various other arterial sources, while sharing an identical downstream course beneath the pancreas [16]. The drainage site of the accompanying vein, as well as the relationship between the aMCA origin and the inferior border of the pancreas, was recorded. aMCAs arising directly from the SMA were classified as direct‐origin aMCAs, while those arising from other sources were classified as aberrant aMCAs.

Middle Colic Artery

The MCA was defined as the artery supplying the transverse colon running within the transverse mesocolon. The branch toward the right transverse colon was defined as the right branch (right MCA), and the branch toward the central–left transverse colon as the left branch (left MCA). When the right and left branches shared a common trunk from the SMA, it was classified as a common‐trunk MCA; when each branch arose independently from the SMA, it was classified as an independent‐origin MCA. MCAs arising from the ileocolic artery (ICA) or the celiac axis were classified as aberrant MCAs. Cases that did not align with predefined categories were assigned to the unclassifiable type.

Right Colic Artery

The RCA was defined as the artery running in the ascending mesocolon and supplying the mid‐to‐distal ascending colon. When branching directly from the SMA, it was classified as a direct‐origin RCA, and when branching from the ICA or MCA, it was classified as an aberrant RCA.

Ileocolic Artery

The ICA was defined as the artery running in the ascending mesocolon toward the terminal ileum, cecum, appendix, and proximal ascending colon. When the ICA did not share a common trunk with other colic arteries, it was classified as a direct‐origin ICA.

Venous Terminology and Definitions

Venous terminology was prespecified to ensure consistent interpretation of arterial–venous relationships. Colic veins were categorized according to their drainage territory and venous outflow. Veins draining the ileocecal, ascending colonic, and transverse colonic regions were recorded as ileocolic, right colic, and middle colic veins, respectively. Specifically, for the middle colic vein (MCV), tributaries accompanying the right and left branches of the MCA were defined as the right branch vein and left branch vein, respectively. These were classified based on their confluence pattern into a “common‐trunk veins” type (merging to form a single trunk) or a “separate veins” type (draining independently).

The gastrocolic trunk of Henle (GCT) was originally described as a venous confluence of right‐sided colic drainage and the right gastroepiploic vein draining into the superior mesenteric vein [17]. The accessory right colic vein (aRCV) was defined as a venous tributary draining the marginal veins of the right colic flexure into the GCT or SMV [18].

Venous Drainage Accompanying Each Colic Artery

Based on the above venous definitions, for each colic artery, the drainage sites of its accompanying vein were identified, including direct drainage to the SMV, inferior mesenteric vein (IMV), splenic vein, jejunal vein, GCT, or ileocolic vein (ICV).

Positional Relationships of ICA/RCA With the SMV, and Jejunal Vein Crossings of the SMA

We assessed the positional relationships between the ICA and SMV and between the RCA and SMV (ventral vs. dorsal courses), which are important in D3 lymph node dissection during right hemicolectomy. These were examined in cases where the ICA and RCA were classified as direct‐origin type.

Distance Measurements of the D3 Lymph Node Dissection Area

The distances analyzed in this study were defined as imaging‐based anatomical measurements derived from preoperative CTC‐A and were intended to represent the potential anatomical field relevant to D3 lymph node dissection, rather than the actual extent of lymphadenectomy performed intraoperatively.

As illustrated in Figure 1, the D3 dissection field was delineated geometrically as follows. For right hemicolectomy: from the ICA root to the MCA root; in independent‐origin MCA cases, from the ICA root to the right MCA root. For left‐sided TC: from the MCA root to the aMCA root; in independent‐origin MCA cases, from the left MCA root to the aMCA root. To measure the distance of the central lymph node dissection area with high precision, we generated curved planar reformation images from the SMA origin to the ICA origin in Ziostation 2 by placing a centerline path along the SMA, effectively straightening tortuous segments. Distances were measured at 0.5‐mm resolution along the SMA centerline on curved planar reformation images.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed with R (ver. 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are presented as the median [interquartile range, IQR] and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Interobserver agreement was assessed using percent agreement and unweighted Cohen's kappa (κ). Two‐sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Branching Pattern of the MCA and Accompanying Venous Drainage

An MCA was present in all patients, with approximately 80% exhibiting a common‐trunk MCA. An independent‐origin MCA was observed in 19.0%, and an aberrant MCA in 3.2% (Table 2). For the right branch, the accompanying vein drained into the GCT in 55.2% and directly into the SMV in 41.5%. For the left branch, venous drainage was to the SMV in 71.0% and to the IMV in 17.8% (Table 3).

Branching Pattern of the RCA and Accompanying Venous Drainage

An RCA was present in 247 patients (41.8%); among these, 160 (64.8%) originated directly from the SMA, while the remainder were aberrant, arising from the ICA or MCA (Table 2). The accompanying vein drained into the GCT in 80.6%, and directly into the SMV in 7.7% (Table 3).

Configuration of the Gastrocolic Trunk

The configuration of colic tributaries draining into the GCT was classified based on the number of inflowing vessels (Table 3). A single tributary pattern was the most common (82.7%). Multiple tributaries (two or more), such as the confluence of the right branch of the MCV and the aRCV, were observed in 16.9% of cases.

Branching Pattern of the ICA

An ICA was present in all cases and most commonly originated directly from the SMA. Cases in which the MCA or RCA originated from the ICA were uncommon (5.2%) (Table 2).

Anatomical Branching Patterns Relevant to Right Hemicolectomy

After excluding aberrant arterial types, 490 patients were classified into four patterns relevant to D3 dissection for right hemicolectomy (Figure 2): Type 1, direct‐origin RCA with common‐trunk MCA (n = 138, 28.2%); Type 2, direct‐origin RCA with independent‐origin MCA (n = 21, 4.3%); Type 3, no SMA‐derived RCA with common‐trunk MCA (n = 259, 52.9%); Type 4, no SMA‐derived RCA with independent‐origin MCA (n = 72, 14.7%).

Positional Relationships of the ICA/RCA with the SMV, and Jejunal Veins with the SMA

In Types 1 and 2, four positional patterns were observed based on whether the ICA and RCA coursed ventral or dorsal to the SMV. When the RCA coursed dorsal to the SMV, the ICA also tended to course dorsally (p = 0.001). In Types 3 and 4 (no SMA‐derived RCA), the ICA coursed ventral to the SMV in 52.6% and dorsal in 47.4% (Figure 3a).

Interobserver Reproducibility of Vascular Classification

Interobserver reproducibility was evaluated in the double‐read subset (n = 100) (Table S1). Percent agreement and Cohen's kappa (95% CI) were 87.0% and 0.75 (0.63–0.87) for aMCA classification, 86.0% and 0.74 (0.61–0.86) for MCA configuration, and 85.0% and 0.74 (0.62–0.86) for RCA classification, indicating substantial agreement [19].

Discussion

Using 0.5‐mm triphasic contrast–enhanced CTC‐A, we integrated preoperative visualization of arterial and venous anatomy from the right‐sided through the left transverse colon within a single modality. This approach allowed not only descriptive anatomical analysis but also operation‐linked classification of arterial branching patterns, objective quantification of the D3 lymph node dissection distance, and mapping of venous drainage. Such comprehensive, patient‐specific assessment is directly relevant to preoperative planning and the standardization of CME/CVL procedures.

Previous studies have consistently documented substantial diversity around the SMA, sometimes with misclassification when CTA is interpreted in isolation. For the MCA, combined cadaveric and CTA series describe a predominance of a common trunk (57.4%) with frequent independent (41.8%) and rare celiac (0.2%) origins [15]. For the RCA, a cadaveric systematic review indicated that a direct origin is a lower‐frequency pattern (36.8%), with many RCAs arising from the ICA (31.9%) or MCA (30.2%) [14]. A meta‐analysis indicated that the aMCA occurs in approximately 25% of patients, most frequently arising from the SMA [16]. In our cohort, the common‐trunk MCA anatomy predominated (77.8%), and RCAs were present in 41.8% of patients, approximately two‐thirds of which arose directly from the SMA. In comparison with certain other reports, fewer independent‐origin MCAs were observed, and relatively more direct‐origin RCAs were recorded. By fusing colonography with angiography and adjudicating vessels by mesenteric compartment (ascending vs. transverse mesocolon), using venous outflow as a tie‐breaker, we reduced ambiguity between true RCAs and right MCA branches. Because reported frequencies depend on naming conventions and imaging protocols [14, 20], methodological differences likely account for inter‐study discrepancies. Furthermore, regarding the classification of vascular variations, most previous studies have described the branching patterns of the MCA, RCA, and ICA independently.7,8,14–16 While some classifications for the aMCA10,16 or RCA14 exist, few have integrated these variations into a unified system specifically designed for D3 lymphadenectomy. The classification system proposed in this study differs from these conventional anatomical descriptions by combining the MCA configuration with the presence of the RCA or aMCA. This pattern‐based approach provides a more practical roadmap for surgeons, as it directly delineates the boundaries of the D3 dissection area (from the MCA/aMCA root to the ICA root) rather than merely listing individual vascular probabilities. Accordingly, the differences observed here are more plausibly attributed to protocol and adjudication criteria than to true biological variation. These features provide a more pragmatic map for surgeons compared with prior cadaveric or radiological reports.

From a clinical perspective, there are several implications. First, CTC‐A enables bleeding‐risk mapping around the GCT. The aMCA‐accompanying vein drained into the proximal IMV in 88.4% with smaller proportions to the SMV (8.0%) and splenic vein (3.6%), a feature that may assist in preoperative identification of aMCAs. For the MCA‐accompanying vein (MCV), a venous common trunk of right and left branches occurred in only 31.9% and drained mainly to the SMV (68.8%) and GCT (22.8%). When branches drained separately, the right branch favored the GCT (70.4%) over the SMV (28.6%), while the left branch favored the SMV (71.9%) over the GCT (23.1%). Regarding the venous arrangement, our study revealed that the veins accompanying the RCA predominantly drained into the GCT (80.6%). According to anatomical definitions which classify veins draining the right colic flexure into the GCT or SMV as aRCV, the vein running parallel to the RCA frequently exhibits the anatomical characteristics of the aRCV in our cohort. The configuration of colic tributaries draining into the GCT was also consistent with previous reports [18]. Previous reports have documented the overall proportions of MCV inflow (e.g., SMV 62.5%, GCT 29.3%, IMV 4.8%, splenic vein 4.7%, and jejunal vein 0.6%) [21]. However, these reports have not resolved right‐branch versus left‐branch MCV drainage with this level of granularity. This pattern suggests that the “MCV” described in prior studies often corresponds to the left MCV branch in our framework, whereas the right MCV branch may have been previously subsumed under the aRCV. Taken together, venous return from mid‐to‐left transverse colon preferentially reaches the SMV (and IMV), whereas venous return around the hepatic flexure preferentially enters the GCT via an aRCV pathway. Practically, after MCA ligation in RHC, surgeons can plan to control (i) the MCV right branch arising from the SMV if a venous common trunk exists, or (ii) the right‐branch vein entering the GCT when branches drain separately.

Second, pattern‐specific D3 measurement provides an objective estimate of the required dissection length. Across the four RHC patterns, an independent‐origin MCA consistently predicted a shorter D3 distance (RCA‐present, 27.5 vs. 22.5 mm, p = 0.012; RCA‐absent, 20.5 vs. 16.5 mm, p < 0.001). Although a meta‐analysis has reported an average distance of 16.0 mm between the RCA and ICA [22], we are not aware of prior studies that quantify the ICA–MCA length by the preoperative pattern. Shorter D3 distances are therefore predictive of technically less demanding central ligation, which can guide the D3 lymphadenectomy strategy. Beyond length, course predictions were actionable: a dorsal RCA relative to the SMV predicted a dorsal ICA (p = 0.001), supporting a strategy of maintaining dissection along the anterior surface of the SMV as the landmark during central dissection. Within the D3 field (MCA to ICA), two jejunal‐vein crossings of the SMA predominated (65.1%): “any‐anterior” crossings occurred in 28.5% while “both‐anterior” crossings were rare (7.8%). These anatomical details highlight critical steps when advancing from ICA clipping toward the MCA root, particularly for transverse colon cancer requiring dissection along the left margin of the SMA.

Third, for left‐sided TC, independent‐origin MCA was associated with shorter D3 distances, even in cases with an aMCA (25.0 mm vs. 18.0 mm, p < 0.001), again indicating technically less demanding central ligation. Approximately 23% of aMCA origins lay dorsal to the pancreas, with the majority situated caudal to the inferior pancreatic border. Given that ICG fluorescence has demonstrated lymphatic flow along the aMCA in splenic flexure cancers [23], preoperative identification of a dorsal aMCA origin should prompt anticipation of posterior pancreatic mobilization and inform CVL/D3 planning.

In the 100‐case reproducibility subset, discordance was observed in 25 cases (25%). Discordant classifications frequently showed interdependency: MCA type disagreement (14/25, 56%) often co‐occurred with shifts in RCA type or aMCA presence. Among MCA‐involved discordant cases (n = 14), paired shifts were most commonly observed with RCA type (6/14 cases, 42.9%) or aMCA presence (5/14 cases, 35.7%). These co‐occurring shifts clustered in borderline MCA‐region configurations that are intrinsically difficult to delineate and label on CTC‐A. Specifically, discordance tended to arise when a hepatic‐flexure–directed branch was ambiguous, leading to alternative labeling as either the RCA or a right MCA branch, and when transverse‐colon branching could be labeled as an aMCA‐associated configuration versus an independent MCA branch. We reviewed operative records and/or videos for discordant cases when exposure permitted assessment. In the 25 discordant cases, intraoperative assessment was feasible for the MCA in 22 cases and for the RCA in 21 cases, whereas the aMCA was not exposed and therefore not evaluable in 19 cases (76%). Within these exposure‐limited comparisons, Observer 1 was more often concordant with operative documentation for RCA classification than Observer 2 (16/21 vs. 8/21 evaluable cases), although intraoperative information was not used to adjudicate discrepancies and we did not perform formal statistical comparison between readers. This difference may, in part, reflect the practical difficulty of recognizing small‐caliber branches and overlapping courses on 3D reconstructions in borderline configurations.

This study has some limitations. It was a single‐center, retrospective analysis. Although the primary imaging interpretation was performed by a single surgeon, we addressed potential subjectivity by performing an interobserver reproducibility assessment in a stratified random subset of 100 cases. Agreement was substantial across key arterial components (aMCA, MCA, and RCA; κ = 0.74–0.75), supporting the consistency of the proposed classification. However, systematic intraoperative validation of the vascular classification was limited. Since the surgical field of view varied by procedure, and retrospective operative records often lacked detailed descriptions of branching patterns, comprehensive artery‐by‐artery verification across the entire cohort was not feasible. We did not establish causal links between patterns or D3 distances and clinical outcomes (blood loss, operative time, lymph node yield, R0 rate, or survival). Future work should prospectively and across centers validate the reproducibility of this classification across multiple readers, cross‐validate imaging with intraoperative and pathological findings, and test whether pattern‐guided algorithms (ligature level and approach) translate to improved perioperative and oncologic outcomes, thereby defining the role of CTC‐A within CME/CVL pathways.

In conclusion, CTC‐A integrates tumor localization with mesenteric arteriovenous anatomy to provide operation‐oriented vascular maps. Pattern‐based classification with objectively measured D3 distances supports standardized, safer D3 lymphadenectomy in right hemicolectomy and TC.
