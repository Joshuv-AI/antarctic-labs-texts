Development of a Clinical Dental Reference Data Set for Dubai Emirati Children and Adolescents Based on the Demirjian Method
Authors: Sharifa AlHaj, Manal Al Halabi, Mawlood Kowash, Anas Salami, Ammar H Khamis, Iyad Hussein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ability to predict the difference between chronological age (CA) and dental age (DA) is an important aid in the accurate diagnosis and treatment planning process in the pediatric population.AimTo establish a dental reference data set (DRDS) of Emirati children aged between 5 and 15 years.Materials and methodsDental panoramic tomography (DPT) of 139 Emirati children aged 5–15 years was obtained from a retrospective case-note cross-sectional study and used to establish a DRDS. Introduction

Age is a period of human life that is measured by the chronological passing of days, weeks, months, and years from birth. Biological age refers to how old a person seems; accordingly, biological age assessment can be achieved by various techniques, including physical examination, cervical vertebral maturation,1 hand-wrist radiographs, dental examination, and also by using dental panoramic tomography (DPT) images.2 In forensic medicine, age estimation is no longer only beneficial in the identification of deceased victims or human remains but additionally in age estimation of living individuals, which would possibly be required in such cases where chronological age plays a necessary role, for example, criminal liability, immigration, school attendance, and social benefits.3 In dental and medical practice, age estimation is regarded to be of outstanding importance.4,5 For example, pediatric dentists and orthodontists must be capable of understanding a child's growth and developmental status represented by both the chronological and dental age, as it is important to lead to a more accurate diagnosis and treatment planning.6 Dental age assessment is commonly based on observing the stage of tooth eruption and the degree of mineralization of the developing tooth buds' enamel from radiographs.7 The process of maturation is much more uniform, progressive, sequenced, and continuous compared to tooth eruption, and is much less affected by endocrine disease, dietary deficiency conditions, and environmental changes.8 These factors influence tooth development and may show differences in chronological age compared to dental age.3 For these reasons, a sequence of dental age estimation methods has been developed primarily based on the mineralization stage of tooth germs.9

Many authors have developed scoring methods to assess dental age using dental calcification stages of permanent teeth. Examples of these methods include Demirjian et al.,10 Nolla,11 Willems et al.,12 and Moorrees et al.13 methods. The most widely used dental maturity staging system is the method developed by Demirjian in 1973 on a sample of French-Canadian children.14,15 Demirjian method10 is based on eight stages of tooth mineralization, from calcification of the cusp to closure of the apex, on the seven left mandibular teeth, and each tooth is given a score based on its phase of calcification. Its use continues to be reported.16,17

Studies have been conducted internationally since,18,19 and in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries 6,8,20–22 to assess the dental ages of different populations. These studies have shown that there is a variation in the dental age of individuals belonging to different ethnic groups.23 This has led to the need to develop separate standards according to ethnicity and race across the globe. To the author's knowledge, there is a lack of such studies using Demirjian's method in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), especially those living in its most populous Emirate, namely Dubai, hence this study. The null hypothesis was that there was no difference in the chronological age (CA) and dental age (DA) in Emirati children, based on Demirjian's method. The aim was to create a dental reference data set (DRDS) for Emirati children and adolescents between 5 and 15 years using Demirjian's method from a subgroup of patients attending a postgraduate dental institute in Dubai, UAE. The specific aims were to (1) compare the CA of the above subjects with their DA as assessed from already obtained DPT radiographs; (2) determine differences in Demirjian's scores between UAE boys and girls of the same chronological age, and finally (3) compare the results with other regional and international data.

Materials and Methods

This was a retrospective case-note cross-sectional study of a group of Emirati child patients who attended Dubai Dental Hospital (DDH) in Dubai, UAE. Ethical approval was obtained in full conformance with principles of the “Declaration of Helsinki,” Good Clinical Practice (GCP), and within the laws and regulations of the UAE. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee in Hamdan Bin Mohammed College of Dental Medicine and the IRB of Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences (Ethical approval number: MBRU-IRB 2020-019).

The study patients were selected from those who had required a DPT for established disease diagnosis and treatment planning. Thus, no patients were exposed to radiation for the purpose of this study. The DPTs were obtained from a period from January 1, 2018, until January 25, 2021, from DDH's dental electronic records (D4W©). The chronological age of each subject was calculated using the date of birth at the time of taking the DPT. The inclusion criteria were: fit and healthy children with the absence of systemic diseases; children and young adults aged from 5–15 years who had DPTs; dated DPT radiographs of diagnostic quality (grade I/acceptable)24 that had a clear date of birth; and finally, Emirati children by identification card. The exclusion criteria were: individuals with medical conditions and dentofacial anomalies that could influence dental development; poor-quality DPTs or incomplete patient records; and patients with a history of orthodontic treatment, dental extractions, or dental trauma.

Using a power calculation formula and data from a previous study,19 the planned sample of this study consisted of at least 106 DPTs. The digital radiographs in DDH are all taken with Veraviwepocs© 3D R100© panoramic system, images processed using I-DIXEL 3DXD version 4.3.4 software©, with a resolution of 0.125 mm Voxel per side of the image. Therefore, there was one source for all the radiographs used in the study, and they were all viewed using the same computer monitor, a Lenovo© monitor with screen resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. The search for these DPTs was conducted by independent DDH information technology staff in the D4W© system for all patients aged 5–15 who were Emirati and had a DPT taken. Inter-examiner and intra-examiner reliability were assessed on the use of the Demirjian method. The principal investigator PI (SA) was calibrated by an experienced assessor (IH) in how to estimate the chronological and dental age from the records and DPT, using the Demirjian method on 10 random DPTs. Cohen's kappa coefficient scores were assessed. The inter-examiner kappa score for the tooth developmental stages was 0.71, demonstrating good agreement. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by using 10 randomly selected radiographs, which were scored more than 1 week after initial examination by the PI. Two assessments to calculate maturation scores and dental age were conducted a week apart. The intra-examiner kappa score for the tooth developmental stages was 0.82, demonstrating an almost perfect agreement.

The process of the study is summarized in Figure 1. The DPTs were extracted and viewed on the same monitor. The chronological age in years and months of each subject was calculated using the date of birth as per the radiograph. The dates of birth were blinded from the assessor at the time of assessment. If there were more than one DPT per child present, the image with better quality and resolution was chosen. If two were of good quality, the most recent one was chosen. The tooth development stages system (Table 1),10 was used to assess all seven left mandibular teeth. If the left side was not clear, then the right side was assessed. The data obtained was analyzed and used to establish Emirati self-weighted scores.

All seven left mandibular permanent teeth were rated in the following order: Central incisor (I1), lateral incisor (I2), canine (C), first bicuspid (P1), second bicuspid (P2), first molar (M1), second molar (M2). Teeth development stages (TDS) were rated on a scale of A to H according to a graphical and written anatomical description of TDS as per Demirjian's method (Table 1).

Each tooth was given a rating; this rate was then converted into a score using tables in the paper of Demirjian et al.10 using the original self-weighted scores (for boys or girls) as appropriate. The scores of all seven teeth were added together to give a final “maturity score.” The maturity score was plotted on the “centile charts” for boys or girls as appropriate,10 where the age of the child is known. The maturity score was converted directly into a “dental age” either by using the maturity score/dental age conversion chart10 or by reading off the coordinates on the horizontal scale, the age at which the 50th centile attains that maturity score value (Fig. 2).

A completed example of “maturity score” and “dental age” calculation using a DPT for a patient: In the above male example, each tooth was given a rating according to Demirjian's tooth stage development table. This rate was then converted into a score using Demirjian's standard conversion table assigned for boys. The scores of all seven teeth were added together to give a maturity score that was equal to 67.9. The maturity score was converted directly into the dental age as per Demirjian's standard conversion table (in this case, 7.8) either by reading off on the horizontal scale the age at which the 50th centile attains that maturity score value, or by using the centile charts. Chronological age was calculated by subtracting the date of birth from DPT date. This was not used in the study

Statistics

Data were entered into the computer using IBM-SPSS for Windows version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Categorical variables were described by using proportions, and continuous variables (CA, DA, and year score of maturity) were described by a measure of tendency and a measure of dispersion. Testing of normality was conducted for both Emirati children CA and their calculated DA. Continuous variables (maturity score, dental age, and chronological age) were described by a measure of tendency and a measure of dispersion. Shapiro–Wilk was used to test the normality of continuous variables. Student's t-test was used to compare variables. Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient was also performed. Dental age was calculated from reference tables,10 by normalization of the values, and compared to CA for boys and girls separately. Kolmogorov–Smirnov was used to test the normality of continuous variables. The Mann–Whitney test was used to compare the means between the two groups. The association between the two ages was performed by the Spearman correlation coefficient. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant in all statistical analyses.

Creation of Self-weighted Scores Based on the Emirati Population Dental Panoramic Tomographies

As Demirjian's standards were appropriate for the Emirati population, and to be able to achieve more specific and reliable age estimates from the Emirati DPTs used in this study, new self-weighted values were calculated. This calculation was based on a normalization process, where dental ages per tooth and per stage were calculated for the Emirati children. The calculations started off by using the lowest DA corresponding to the self-weighted score given for each stage, but this calculation relied on the used Emirati DPTs. Thus, a process of normalization of the values was conducted for each value [for each tooth (I1, I2, P1, P2 M1, M2 and for each formation stage A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H)]. Each stage had a number, or a self-weighted score. However, occasionally, a self-weighted stage was not listed in the self-weighted table of Demirjian's tables in order to match the Emirati population, so the process of normalization was required to create this sample's values through a simple process of calculation. In other words, normalization was conducted due to some missing measurements in the Canadian-French population tables. The corresponding base values were calculated, and the rest were compared.

To explain this further, (1) for each formation stage of the tooth, Demirjian's method gave a self-weighted score. (2) Emirati children's own self-weighted scores were calculated. This is seen in example below: Data obtained from Emirati DPTs were subdivided into groups of DAs that showed the same stage formation of a tooth. For example, from our study data, tooth I1 in males “stage F” was repeated in three of the Emirati DPTs with the lowest DA of 7.7, which led to a self-weighted score according to Demirjian's table of 4.1. The following age after 7.7 in the Emirati DPTs sample (that had I1 in males and a stage F self-weighted score of 4.1) was 7.9. Because the self-weighted score was the same for two different DAs, the process of normalization was conducted. Another example: Tooth I2 “stage E” in males had two different DAs in our sample that were 6.8 and 7.9 years according to the Demirjian table. When the lowest DA (i.e., 6.8) self-weighted score was extracted from Demirjian's table, it was found to be 5.2. For the following DA (i.e., 7.9), the normalization process took place as follows:

After obtaining the new self-weighted scores (Table 7), the mean value of self-weighted scores of each tooth and stage in the Emirati sample was calculated. For instance, the tooth I1 stage F, in males, had a self-weighted score of 4.1 and 4.21, and was repeated (frequency) in three DPTs in the sample. To create the mean value of the Emirati self-weighted scores, the sum of those numbers was divided by their frequency. For example: I1 stage F in males repeated three times: once 4.1 self-weight score and twice 4.21. They were added up and divided by three. Thus (4.1 + 4.21 + 4.21)/3 = 4.17. Therefore, 4.17 represented the new mean self-weighted score value of tooth I1 stage F in the Emirati sample (Table 8). This process was repeated for each tooth and for each stage according to gender. These new values (shown in Table 8) were calculated and presented in terms of the mean value, standard error, standard deviation, and total number of boys and girls and combined groups in ‘a tooth formation stage’ for each tooth. The summary of these values that represent the reference data set for the Dubai Emirati children sample is presented in Table 9 for both males and females. This represents the final DRDS for this sample.

Discussion

This study represented, as far as the authors know, the first attempt to assess chronological and dental ages specific to a subgroup of the Emirati child population using their DPTs and based on the Demirjian standards. The only other UAE study for age estimations in UAE children was reported in 2014;25 however, they used the “Willems model” and their study involved children in addition to sub-adults and adults categories, and involved third molar assessment (even for children).25 Many of our DPTs did not have third molars in them thus, the latter model was not used. In addition, the Willems models was recently deemed as inaccurate from some populations, compared to the Demirjian method.26 While Altalie et al. reported that Willems' model was very accurate in age estimation, our study reported an overestimation of the DA compared to the CA, as has been noted elsewhere when using the Demirjian method globally.27 This meant that in our sample Dubai children matured dentally earlier. This study's findings were similar to a recent study conducted in Qatar, an ethnically similar population.22

Demirjian's eight-stage method, used in this research, was and is still one of the principal methods used to quantify the degree of maturity.10,11,13,28 Recent studies have confirmed that Demirjian's classification is a widely used method for age estimation around the globe 9,29–32 and performs well in terms of both observer agreement and correlation between estimated and true age,9,33–35 although specific variations apply to different populations. The verification of this method on various population samples revealed a consistent over-estimation of the chronological age.25 Although other studies found that Demirjian's standards were not suitable for their specific population,36 it was found that those standards were applicable to this specific sample of Dubai Emirati children, and that Emirati children's dental maturity was similar to that of their French-Canadian counterparts. Based on the above and within the limitations mentioned below, this study successfully developed a self-weighted score DRDS specific to this population, but based on the Demirjian standards.

The developmental stages of permanent teeth were considered as a valuable indicator of dental age for this study's Emirati population, given the scarcity of other available age indicators. In dentistry and medicine, two methods are commonly used to assess dental age: assessment of tooth eruption by tooth counting and assessment of mineralization of permanent teeth by DPTs.37 The latter's use was considered the basis of this present study. Because relying on the date of eruption of permanent teeth varies widely among children of the same ethnic background, making it an unreliable method of estimating age.38–40 Other modified methods,41 are based on radiographic shape criteria and root length ratio, using relative value to crown height; therefore, shortened or lengthened projections of developing teeth will not affect the reliability of the assessment.10 Other advantages of the latter method include reliability and feasibility to estimate an individual's age because teeth can be preserved for a long time after all other tissues have disintegrated.42

While our sample size was relatively equal to some other studies19,43 and considered adequate and representative of the smaller UAE population, it was larger than that of Pratyusha et al.'s study in India,41 but may be small compared to other similar studies.6,44,45 These studies included a cross-sectional study that compared DAs with CAs in 162 Somali and white Caucasian children residing in Sheffield in the UK.46 Likewise, Prabhakar et al. tested the applicability of Demirjian's method among 151 Indian children living in Davangere.47 They found that the Davangere children were dentally more advanced, like our study, but they also found that Demirjian's method was not applicable to their study group due to an overestimation of CA compared to DA. Other studies48,49 with larger sample sizes than ours, including those that surveyed older age groups, recommended the creation of an adaptive tool to avoid the overestimation observed using Demirjian's method.

The difference between DA and CA in our research was found to be 0.79 years in males and 1.21 years in females. The majority of the children's DAs were clearly higher than the CA. This suggested that children in the UAE matured dentally quite early, as in the Omani20 and Qatari populations.22 Other research had found similar differences; 0.73 and 0.51 years for males/females44 and 0.27 and 0.23 years50 both studies in the UK; 0.5 and 0.5 years in Australia in 2013,45 between 1 and 1.3 and between 0.0071 and 1.25 years in China in 2010,51 0.71 and 0.67 years in Kuwait in 2009,6 0.66 and 0.059 years in Saudi Arabia in 2015,8 all for boys and girls, respectively. It should be noted, however, that when attempting to compare the results of this study with previous research on the accuracy of dental age determination methods, it became clear that such comparisons are complicated by differences in sample sizes, age structures, groupings, and statistical analyses used, which make direct comparisons difficult.9

Our results revealed a difference between the DA and the CA of 0.79 years in the male group; this was much higher than those results obtained using similar age groups of Serbian52 (0.45 years), Dutch53 (0.4 years), and French54 (0.47 years) males. In the female group, a mean difference of 1.21 years was calculated. This was again much higher than those reported (0.3 years) among females living in Norway.31 The reverse observations were reported (3.04 years in males and 2.82 years in females) for South Indians.30 This region has historic geographical links to the UAE and may explain the similar upward scores.55 Overall, however, the differences in age estimation between this study and those of other studies may be related to differences in the sample size, age groups, and studied populations. Other factors, such as socioeconomic status, nutrition, and dietary habits, may also affect the outcomes.56

In our study, the pattern of mean DA–CA against CA in females was different from that in males of similar age groups (i.e., mean DA–CA in age subgroup 6.5–7.4 in females is 1.5 and in males is 1.1). The mean difference between DA and CA in males decreased with age (except in the age group 12.5–13.4 of males), while it fluctuated in age groups of females. In this case, the mean DA–CA decreased. In a study of 521 children aged between 4 and 9 years of age, Liversidge et al.44 found that the mean DA–CA in boys decreased with age, while it peaked in the middle age group (6.00–6.99 years) in girls. Although the populations examined in these two studies were of different age groups, the patterns were similar. For most maturation events, the pace of maturation is faster in girls.57 This is consistent with the findings of this study, where girls were more dentally advanced than boys at most tooth formation stages.58 In another study of 509 children aged between 3 and 14 years of age,6 found that the standards of dental maturation described by Demirjian may not be suitable for Kuwaiti children, and creation of a completely separate reference data set using a nonlinear regression model was necessary, which disagreed with the current study. Because of the acceptability of Demirjian's norms, this present study created a reference data set based on the normalization process of Demirjian's norms, but applicable to the age range and maturity of Emirati children in this study's sample.

As stated in earlier studies,31,54,59 the difference in dental maturation between groups using a single score, such as Demirjian's, does not provide a clear picture of where and how these differences occur. Demirjian's scoring system relies on weighted scores of tooth formation stages. Differences in the timing of one or more of these stages relative to other stages or teeth are usually hidden.60 Due to this, self-weighted scores based on normalization were created for this Emirati population. This was different from that study of 160 children in Minia, Egypt,36 where the Demirjian scores were not deemed suitable, and they created a reference data set independent of the Demirjian standard, based on a log regression formula. This was also the case for others.6 Others have used simple average methods in the quest for creating reference data sets for their populations.22

When comparing the mean ages of Emirati children with those of children from Australia, Belgium, Canada, England, Finland, France, South Korea, and Sweden as presented in a meta-analysis study,61 it is worth mentioning that for most of the teeth, there seems to be a close similarity in children's mean ages in a given tooth formation stage. Appreciating the limitation of such a comparison due to the difference in the sample size of the current study (139 DPTs) and that of the previous study (that assessed 9002 DPTs), Liversidge et al. showed no clear difference in the timing of formation. The differences found were mostly related to the small sample size.

Finally, in the UAE, as in many other countries around the world, dental radiographs are performed as part of diagnosis and/or treatment planning. It would be difficult to assess how a larger and evenly distributed sample (from other UAE Emirates, for example) would have affected the results of this study. One drawback of this study was that, rather than being chosen at random, all subjects were patients who had been treated at the recently opened Dubai Dental Hospital at that time. Hence, they may not represent the general population of the UAE. However, Dubai is the most populous emirate in the UAE, and many UAE nationals from other emirates may seek dental treatment in Dubai. This meant that this study may have potentially included Emiratis from the remaining six emirates; however, this data was not included. Asking about the child's Emirate of origin was omitted from this retrospective study since it was not documented in the patient's file and may be considered a limitation.
