Noninvasive blood pressure measurements demonstrated a significant underestimation of blood pressure in a patient with severe preeclampsia: a case report
Authors: Yan Zhang, Juan Gu, Chao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Preeclampsia, an exclusively obstetrical condition, affects between 3 and 5 % of pregnant women [1]. Severe preeclampsia can threaten the lives of both the mother and the unborn child. Diagnosis and successful management of preeclampsia depend on accurate and current blood pressure (BP) measurement. In the guidelines for the management of women with severe pre-eclampsia suggest BP measurement, but the principle of the measurement method is not detailed [2]. Various BP measurement techniques differ from one another; non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) measurement is simple and is commonly used [3]. We will present a case of NIBP significantly under-estimated BP in a patient with chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia.

Case presentation

A consent from the patient has been acquired and this work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [4]. A 33-year-old gravida 1, 80Kg, Para 0 at 27 weeks of gestation presented to the emergency room with a 14-h history of blurred vision. The patient had chronic hypertension for 2 years and treated with hydrochlorothiazide and clonidine. Albuminuria was observed at 12 weeks of gestation, labetalol, methyldopa and aspirin was administrated. 2 days ago, the patient's self-measurement of BP was 210/100 mmHg, and accompanied by dizziness and discomfort. 14 h ago, the vision in her right eye was blurred, and a red area about the size of a coin was seen in the center of his field of vision. No chest tightness or abdominal pain was complained. When she arrived at our hospital, NIBP (intermittent oscillometric arm BP, Phlips, 42.0–54.0 cm) was 204/143 mmHg. Laboratory tests showed severe proteinuria, platelet count was 42*10^9/L. She was diagnosed with chronic hypertension with severe preeclampsia, incomplete HELLP (elevated liver enzymes and lower platelet count) syndrome and abnormal umbilical cord blood flow (cord blood flow spectrum is unimodal). Magnesium sulfate and dexamethasone was administrated. Oral labeolol, nitroglycerin and urapidil were continuously infused based to control NIBP at about 150/100 mmHg. Emergency cesarean section was planned.

When she arrived at the surgery room, left radial intra-arterial blood pressure (RIBP) measurement was performed, after the atmospheric pressure is adjusted to zero, the sensor was placed at the heart level and it showed 190/112 mmHg (Philips monitor and Yixinda arterial sensor). However, the NIBP of left arm was 155/105 mmHg. Intraoperative blood pressure was managed according to the RIBP. After general anesthesia was performed with propofol 150 mg, remifentanil 50μg and succinylcholine 100 mg, RIBP decreased to100/75–130/90 mmHg, and the difference between the NIBP and RIBP reduced to eight mmHg. Endotracheal intubation and transport to neonatal intensive care unit was performed after the baby delivery. The patient's RIBP progressively increased to 190/112 mmHg following recovery from anesthesia and sodium nitroprusside was continuously infused. When the patient was transferred from the transport bed to the bed of intensive care unit, the NIBP showed 120/70 mmHg, while the RIBP was 180/110 mmHg. Then treatments of spasmolysis, antihypertension and sedation were performed, and difference between the NIBP and RIBP progressively decreased to 5-10 mmHg. The patient was discharged on the 5th day after surgery.

Discussion

Appropriate BP control t is very important for patients with severe preeclampsia, and this depends on accurate and current BP [5]. Intra-arterial measurement is a gold standard in BP measurement, which giving the clinician a direct and near-instantaneous measurement of BP [6]. NIBP is simple, but compared to intra-arterial measurement devices, NIBP methods poorly reflect actual BP. NIBP suffers some of the disadvantages, including poor performance in larger patients and patients with calcified arteries, as well as in those with reduced blood flow [5]. Wax et al., who compared BP from automated oscillometric readings with arterial BP readings derived from intravascular catheters found that standard deviations of the difference between the 2 to be approximately ±12.5 mmHg at a mean arterial pressure of 75 mmHg. At the extremes (hypertension or hypotension), the standard deviation worsened substantially [7].

The significant difference between NIBP and RIBP in this patient is related to the pathophysiology of severe preeclampsia. Both peripheral arteriole spasm and sensitivity to catecholamine were glaringly obvious. Firstly, the measured value of BP relates to the site of measurement. In the process of pulse wave propagating outward, the systolic pressure and pulse pressure increase in the direction of the periphery [6]. Secondly, vascular resistance is inversely proportional to the radius of the vessel to the fourth power [ 8]. Peripheral arteriole spasm in patients with severe preeclampsia can significantly increase the difference of BP values between radial artery and brachial artery. Thirdly, autonomic nerves regulated peripheral arterioles. Because of the vascular endothelial injury, patients with severe preeclampsia are sensitive to catecholamine [9]. If general anesthesia performed, it is probably recommended to use remifentanil in a 0.5 mg/kg bolus to decreased arterial blood pressure after intubation [5]. Agitation such as transport in this patient can significantly activate the sympathetic nerves, increase blood catecholamine and increase BP. Effective sedation such as general anesthesia in this patient can significantly reduce blood catecholamine and decrease BP. Finally, compared to RIBP, NIBP falsely decreased in more hypertensive patients [3]. This phenomenon is also common in the elderly with poor blood pressure control. So, in this patient NIBP significantly under-estimated actual BP, especially when the BP sharp raised. And this may delay anti-hypertensive treatment.

Intra-arterial measurement a gold standard in BP measurement, but it is invasive and has some complications [6]. This is a single case, whether invasive blood pressure monitoring is necessary for prenatal management of patients with severe preeclampsia needs to be studied and confirmed. The use of invasive vascular monitoring in the perinatal management of some women with severe preeclampsia can refer to the following indications: (1) poorly controlled maternal blood pressure; (2) need for frequent arterial blood gas measurements, especially in the context of pulmonary edema; (3) planned use of a rapid-acting vasodilator; (4) use of calculated systolic pressure variation to estimate intravascular volume status; and (5) need for continuous blood pressure monitoring [10]. Furthermore, the best site for monitoring invasive blood pressure also need confirm. Compared to brachial artery and femoral artery, small arteries such as radial artery and dorsalis pedis artery are more near tissue. Intra-arterial measurement of small arteries may be better to reflect arteriole spasm in patients with severe preeclampsia. In addition, BP control according to BP value of small arteries may acquire more effective vascular dilatation and improve tissue perfusion. In severe preeclampsia patients, 1 mmHg rise in systolic BP associates with an 3 % increase in stork risk and 1 mmHg rise in diastolic BP with an 8 % increase in stork risk [11].

In conclusion, NIBP may significantly under-estimated actual BP in severe preeclampsia patients with poorly controlled maternal BP. Invasive blood pressure monitoring may be useful for assessing rapid changes in cardiovascular status and may be benefit for severe preeclampsia with labile BP despite aggressive NIBP-targeted therapy. Weather this can improve severe preeclampsia mother and baby's health need further study.
