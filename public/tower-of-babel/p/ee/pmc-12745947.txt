Introduction

Ependymoma is a glial tumor of the central nervous system that usually develops in the brain or the spinal cord [1].

Extraspinal ependymomas are ependymomas occurring outside of the central nervous system. They are an extremely rare entity [2]; and their unusual locations can lead to diagnostic difficulties.

The curative treatment is complete surgical resection given the risk of metastatic extension [3].

We herein report the case of an extraspinal sacrococcygeal subcutaneous ependymoma in a young female adult.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [4].

Case presentation

A 20-year-old girl was referred to our general surgery department to evaluate a subcutaneous mass on the left buttock, appeared three months ago.

Physical examination revealed a 5 cm soft, mobile, non-tender subcutaneous mass, approximately 3 cm to the left of the midline and 2 cm above the tip of the coccyx, within the intergluteal cleft. The overlying skin was intact, with no inflammatory signs.

Ultrasonography showed a heterogeneous, non-encapsulated, subcutaneous nodule.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a well-defined, oval mass measuring approximately 5.2 × 3.8 cm in the subcutaneous plane of the sacrococcygeal. The lesion was located posterior to the lower sacrum and coccyx, slightly left-sided, without extension into the presacral space or infiltration of the sacral or coccygeal bones. It appeared closely related to the coccygeal tip but without bony erosion. Histological examination found a Grade 3 anaplastic ependymoma with clear surgical margins.

A cerebro-medullary MRI was performed, showing no cerebral or medullary localization of ependymoma; and the patient was referred for postoperative radiotherapy after multidisciplinary consultation meeting.

Discussion

Pathogenesis

Ependymomas develop from ependymal cells of the central nervous system. Therefore, they usually occur in the cerebral ventricles or the spinal cord [1]. Occurring outside of the central nervous system is very rare. The most common hypothesis is that they derive from the coccygeal medullary vestige in the caudal part of the neural tube. Some have suggested that they originate from ectopic ependymal cells of the filum terminale. Another hypothesis is that primordial germ cells with neuroectodermal differentiation can be the origin of extraspinal ependymomas [2,[5], [6], [7]].

Epidemiology

Extraspinal scroccoygeal ependymomas represent less than 5 % of all ependymomas [2,8].

Most of authors described a male predominance [2,3].

The age distribution is bimodal with two groups of patients: children under 10 years, and young adults between 30 and 40 years [2,3].

Clinical presentation

Extraspinal ependymomas mainly occur in the sacrococcygeal subcutaneous or presacral regions [1,2,5,6].

Only limited cases have been reported in the ovary, the posterior mediastinum, and the uterine ligaments [9,10].

Extraspinal scroccoygeal ependymomas are usually divided according to their location with the sacrum:They can develop posterior to the sacrum, as an asymptomatic or occasionally painful subcutaneous soft tissue mass that can be misdiagnosed with a pilonidal cyst, other benign tumors (teratoma, lipoma, chordoma…), or malign tumors (soft tissue sarcoma, metastatic carcinoma…) [[1], [2], [3],5,11].They can also develop anterior to the sacrum, as a large pelvic mass, that can be responsible of bowel and/or bladder dysfunction or neurological symptoms [[1], [2], [3]].

Imaging

CT scan of the pelvis and MRI show the characteristics of the lesion and its extension [3].

When the diagnosis of ependymoma is evoked, cerebro-medullary MRI should be performed in search of lesions in the spinal cord or the brain.

Histologic examination

World Health Organization classifies ependymomas into three histologic grades [3,6,11,12]:-Grade 1: including subependymoma, and myxopapillary ependymoma which represents the most common subtype [5,12,13];-Grade 2: classic or papillary ependymoma [12];-Grade 3: anaplastic ependymoma, a high grade tumor that grows fast and has a significant metastatic risk [3,11,12].

It is classified as anaplastic ependymoma when the tumor demonstrates two or more aggressive criteria: more than 2 mitosis/mm2, 10 % or higher Ki-67 index, microvascular proliferation, and necrosis [14].

Treatment

Complete surgical resection of the lesion is recommended to avoid local recurrence and metastatic extension [3,11,13]. It can be combined with coccygectomy in the case of coccygeal invasion [5,6].

For presacral lesions, anterior approach allows a better access to the pelvic mass, particularly in the absence of bone invasion, in which case a combined approach can be considered [2].

There are no standardized guidelines for the adjuvant treatment in ependymomas, but postoperative radiotherapy is useful in incomplete resections, high grade tumors, or in the presence of metastases [3,5,6,11].

Prognosis

Extraspinal ependymomas show metastatic potential to distant organs, especially to lymph nodes, lungs, bones and liver [1,2,15,16].

The risk of local recurrences and distant metastases of extraspinal ependymoms seems to be higher than intraspinal tumors [2,3,5,15,16]. These recurrences metastases can develop after a 10-to-20-year-delay [1,2].

Therefore, follow-up should be careful and prolonged [1,2].

Conclusions

Extraspinal ependymoma is a rare and potentially malignant tumor that can atypically present as a soft subcutaneous mass, often mimicking benign conditions such as a pilonidal cyst or sinus.

This atypical clinical presentation may delay accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. Therefore, clinicians and especially general surgeons should consider this rare entity in the differential diagnosis.

Preoperative MRI is essential to assess the full extent of the lesion, guide surgical planning, and ensure complete resection with negative margins, which remains the cornerstone of curative treatment.

Long-term follow-up is crucial given the tumor's potential for late recurrence and distant metastases, particularly in high-grade forms such as anaplastic ependymoma.
