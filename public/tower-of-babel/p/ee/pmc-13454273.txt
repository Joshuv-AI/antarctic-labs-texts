Apple production under changing climate conditions in major producing countries
Authors: Robert Becker Pickson, Chenchen Zhao, Yaodan Hu, Elliot Boateng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApples are a vital dietary resource supporting nutrition across diverse populations; however, global climate change increasingly disrupts apple production, posing growing risks to food security. This study examines the climate-related impacts on apple production in 13 major producing countries using advanced panel econometrics on 3120 observations from 1961Q1 to 2020Q4. The results indicate that elevated temperatures adversely affect apple production, whereas prolonged periods of heavy rainfall also reduce output in the major producing countries. In contrast, expansion of cultivated area and increased fertilizer use enhance apple production. Country-specific results show that, except for Guatemala, higher temperatures are associated with increased apple production, whereas rainfall effects vary across countries. Based on these findings, this study recommends strengthening government investment in research and extension services to support climate-adaptive apple farming. This policy intervention should prioritize improving farmers’ access to climate-resilient technologies, promoting sustainable agricultural practices, and enhancing training to manage the effects of temperature variability and extreme rainfall.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54941-y.Keywords: Apple production, Apple-cultivated area, Fertiliser consumption, Temperature, RainfallSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Apples are a staple dietary resource with far-reaching economic significance1–3. In 2020, China led global production with 40.5 million tonnes, accounting for 63.63% of global output. The top five producers, namely China, the United States, Turkey, India, and the Russian Federation, accounted for 83.48% of the total production (63.6 million tonnes) in 20204. Apple trees are highly sensitive to climatic conditions, particularly during the flowering period5,6. Temperature fluctuations, changing precipitation, and extreme weather events affect the yield, quality, and sustainability of apples7–10. These changes alter the geographic distribution of apple production, influence trade dynamics, and create challenges in soil suitability, infrastructure, and market access11.

Climate change significantly alters temperature and precipitation patterns12, which impact flowering, pollination, fruit development, and harvest13,14. Warmer temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns exacerbate water stress, disrupt bloom timing and fruit maturation, and alter the suitability of regions for apple cultivation15–17. During summer, apple trees form vegetative and reproductive buds that enter winter dormancy. Bud break requires the accumulation of sufficient chilling hours18–21. Insufficient chilling delays bud break, which reduces both yield and fruit quality22–24. Unseasonal warm spells followed by frost during flowering or atypically cold spring events can damage buds and blossoms, causing significant crop losses25,26.

Changes in precipitation, such as droughts and excessive rainfall, affect soil moisture, weaken tree health, and reduce fruit development, thereby lowering crop yields27. Frequent and intense extreme weather events, such as storms, hail, and heavy precipitation, physically damage trees and fruits23,28. Climate change also influences the prevalence of pests and diseases, with warmer temperatures and changing precipitation favouring pests, such as aphids and mites, and diseases, such as apple scab and fire blight29–32. These changes necessitate adjustments to pest management strategies, potentially increasing production costs.

While most debate on the impacts of climate change on food security has predominantly focused on cereal crops33–37, fruit crops such as apples, with their distinct growth patterns and climatic requirements, have received comparatively less attention. This study addresses this gap by examining the effects of changing climatic conditions on apple production across 13 major producing countries, namely, Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Guatemala, India, Iran, Japan, Mexico, New Zealand, South Africa, Turkey, and the United States, covering the period from 1961Q1 to 2020Q4. To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive and unified study has examined the impacts of climate change on apple production across such a diverse range of apple-producing nations.

Previous studies have employed crop simulation models to estimate the effects of climate change on apple production20,38–40. However, these models are limited by their reliance on highly specific datasets on farm management, crop growth stages, and soil composition, which complicates generalizing results to broader regional or national contexts41,42. In addition, simulating the effects of temperature is particularly complex, as varying temperature intervals interact with factors such as precipitation and irrigation conditions43. To overcome these limitations, we employ advanced econometric methods, including Driscoll-Kraay standard error regression, fully modified ordinary least squares, and panel quantile regression. These techniques provide a more robust and effective means of examining the climate response of apple production in the selected countries. Unlike crop simulation models, these methods do not require highly specific datasets, enabling more generalized and comprehensive results. This study enhances our understanding of the impacts of changing climatic conditions on apple production across diverse regions.

Pre-estimation panel-based techniques

This study employed a panel data analysis. However, panel data models often entail methodological challenges that, if not adequately addressed, may undermine the reliability and validity of statistical inferences. The presence of cross-sectional dependence (CD) in panel datasets can lead to inefficient and inaccurate test statistics. We used the Breusch-Pagan LM, Pesaran scaled LM, and Pesaran CD tests to determine whether cross-sectional dependence exists in the panel data. The Lagrange multiplier (LM) test proposed by48 is appropriate when the time dimension (T) exceeds the number of cross-sectional units (N). The CD test, developed by49, is suitable for panels with extensive time () and cross-sections (), while50 introduced a bias-corrected LM test to address cross-sectional dependence.

Moreover, multicollinearity can lead to estimation problems, including inconsistent parameter estimates, statistically insignificant coefficients, and erroneous conclusions. We used the variance inflation factor (VIF) to assess multicollinearity. According to the literature, the absence of multicollinearity can be inferred when the average VIF is below five36,51. Additionally, incorrect hypotheses can be formulated when ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates are biased by heteroscedasticity52. The modified Wald test is commonly used to assess the extent of heteroskedasticity in the residuals from fixed effect analyses53. Our study examined serial autocorrelation in panel data, which, if uncontrolled, can lead to biased standard errors and suboptimal estimates using the Wooldridge test54.

Panel stationarity and cointegration tests

Given the cross-sectional dependence among panel members, we employed the cross-sectionally augmented Dickey–Fuller (CADF) unit root test, which accounts for it55. We conducted the Westerlund panel test and the Kao and Pedroni cointegration tests to determine the long-run cointegrating relationships among the underlying variables in the leading apple-producing countries. The first-generation cointegration tests by56 and57 address sample size limitations and account for heterogeneity in slopes and intercepts across cross-sectional units within panels. However, these tests do not account for cross-sectional dependence in panel data. The cointegration test proposed by58 considers panel cross-sectional dependence, slope heterogeneity, and structural disruptions59,60. The Westerlund cointegration test includes an optional bootstrap procedure that improves robustness against cross-sectional dependence.

Panel quantile regression

We employed quantile regression (QR), introduced by66, to account for distributional heterogeneity. The QR is robust to outliers and skewness and enables the estimation of the effects of temperature and rainfall on apple production across low, medium, and high quantiles, thereby capturing potential asymmetric impacts without assuming constant effects across the distribution67. Thus, the QR allows us to compare the differing impacts of the factors affecting apple production across the entire distribution. This methodology is preferable because it addresses the limitations of linear regression while retaining its robustness. Panel fully modified OLS approach

We also used the fully modified ordinary least squares (FMOLS) approach to examine factors affecting apple production across individual countries. The FMOLS method, developed by68, is used to estimate the parameter values of the cointegrating vectors identified through panel cointegration tests. This approach allows for heterogeneity among individuals within a given cross-sectional group, with68 identifying two key sources of heterogeneity among panel members. The first factor stems from differences in the means of the variables across panel members, and this form of heterogeneity is addressed by introducing individual-specific intercepts. In addition, individual panel members exhibit diverse reactions to short-term deviations from the equilibrium cointegrating vectors induced by stochastic disturbances. Addressing this heterogeneity involves accommodating variations in the serial correlation properties of the error processes across individual panel members. The t-statistic from the group-mean panel FMOLS tests whether the cointegrating vector has a uniform value, as posited by the null hypothesis, or whether it is allowed to vary, as suggested by the alternative hypothesis. Calculating the FMOLS group mean estimator entails averaging the individual FMOLS estimates.

The results reported in Table 5 (Panel A) show that, after first differencing, apple production, fertilizer consumption, cultivated land, temperature, and rainfall became stationary. The cointegration test results in Table 5 (Panels B, C, and D), based on the approaches of56,57 and58, confirm a cointegrating relationship among the variables. Although they may fluctuate independently in the short run, their movements are linked so that they do not drift too far apart over time. This long-run equilibrium helps explain how changes in one variable, such as fertilizer use or temperature, are associated with long-term changes in apple production.

Table 6 presents the Driscoll–Kraay regression results on the climate implications for apple production. The model exhibits strong explanatory power, with an R-squared of 0.892, indicating that the independent variables explain about 89.2% of the variation in apple production across the 13 major producing countries. The F-statistic confirms the model’s overall statistical significance in explaining the apple production. The results show a significant positive relationship between the cultivated area and apple production, holding other factors constant. Thus, a 1% increase in the land allocated to apple cultivation increases apple production by 0.78% in major apple-producing countries. The market demand for apples drives the expansion of cultivated land. As demand increases, producers expand the area under cultivation to meet the market requirements. This finding is consistent with previous studies36,69,70, which found a significant positive relationship between cultivated land and agricultural production.

Fertilizer consumption has a significant positive effect on apple production in the selected countries. With a coefficient of 0.467, a 1% rise in fertilizer use is associated with a 0.47% increase in apple output. This result indicates that adequate fertilization enhances apple yields by supplying essential nutrients, particularly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, which support fruit development71. Fertiliser application is critical in orchards with nutrient-deficient soil72,73. Adequate fertilization enhances apple size, colour, and flavour74, thereby attracting consumers and potentially increasing market prices. This result is consistent with prior studies75–80, which established that fertilizer consumption improves food production.

Regarding climatic factors, warmer temperatures negatively impact apple production. Specifically, a 1% increase in temperature is associated with a 0.26% decline in apple output in the sampled apple-producing countries. A plausible explanation for this association between temperature and apple production is that prolonged exposure to high temperatures can stress apple trees, impairing their ability to produce and ripen fruit properly81,82. Warmer temperatures can also promote the proliferation of pests and diseases that can harm apple trees, potentially reducing their yields32. This result agrees with44, who suggested that temperature negatively affects apple production.

Like temperature, increased rainfall reduced apple production in the selected countries. We found that a 1% increase in precipitation lowers apple output by approximately 0.51%. The reason for this relationship is that excess rainfall waterlogs soils, limits nutrient uptake, and encourages fungal diseases such as apple scab, which damage fruit and decrease yields. Our study contrasts with44, who reported that rainfall positively affects apple cultivation. While some studies36,37 have highlighted the importance of consistent rainfall for sustaining crop growth and food self-sufficiency, others77,83,84 have demonstrated that excessive or continuous rainfall negatively impacts agricultural production.

Fertiliser use significantly increases apple production across all quantiles. Although the magnitudes of the coefficients vary, fertilization enhances output, with relatively stronger effects at the lower and upper quantiles. This finding highlights the critical role of adequate fertilization in sustaining productivity across different production levels. The existing literature demonstrates that poor soil fertility is a significant constraint on fruit productivity, as adequate nutrient conditions are essential for sustaining optimal growth and maximizing harvest yields88. The integration of organic and inorganic fertilizers in orchard systems has been shown to improve soil conditions, thereby enhancing fruit yields89,90. For example89, observed that the combined application of organic and inorganic fertilizers, together with effective irrigation systems, significantly increased apple productivity while maintaining soil fertility.

Climatic variables constrain apple production. Temperature negatively affects apple production at the lower and middle quantiles, while the effect is insignificant at the highest quantile. This result implies that high-producing countries can better adapt to temperature stress through resilient practices or improved varieties. Similarly, rainfall negatively and significantly affects apple production across all quantiles, though the impact weakens at higher quantiles. The diminishing magnitude at higher quantiles suggests that top-producing countries are relatively less vulnerable to rainfall shocks due to better drainage systems or orchard management. Our results indicate that climate change, through rising temperatures and erratic rainfall, significantly reduces apple production and its economic value91. The primary impact arises from shifts in climatic zones, which alter the suitability of regions for apple cultivation92,93. Further evidence suggests that climate change reduces apple production in low-altitude areas while creating more favourable conditions for cultivation at higher altitudes94. Additionally, rising temperatures disrupt vernalization in high-chill fruits, while increased rainfall hampers flowering and triggers premature fruit shedding, thereby reducing overall production95.

We conducted quantile slope equality tests to examine whether the coefficients of the explanatory variables are homogeneous across quantiles (see Table 7). The results reject the null hypothesis of equal slopes for all regressors, namely cultivated area, fertilizer usage, temperature, and rainfall. This finding indicates that the effects of these variables on apple production differ significantly across quantiles. Country-wise regression analysis

In this section, we examined how cultivated area, fertilizer use, temperature, and rainfall influence apple production at the country level. Table 8 presents the results of the country-wise FMOLS regression. The results indicate a significantly positive relationship between cultivated area and apple production in most countries, except China. In China, cultivated area has no significant effect, indicating that area expansion does not drive growth in apple production. The results are consistent with37 and96, who showed that expanding farmland increases crop production. Similarly97, reported that increasing total arable land enhances agricultural output in the Middle East and North African countries. At the same time98, found that the availability of arable land significantly promotes crop production in the East African Community.

Across the sampled countries, fertilizer use shows a positive association with apple production in Australia, Chile, China, India, Iran, New Zealand, Turkey, and the United States. This result is consistent with evidence that nutrient inputs enhance agricultural productivity80. However, this relationship is not statistically significant in Guatemala, Japan, and Mexico, and in Brazil and South Africa, increased fertilizer use reduces apple production. These contrasting effects emphasize the need for farmers to apply fertilizer judiciously to optimize yields, reduce environmental impacts, and promote sustainable apple production. The literature shows that excessive use of chemical fertilizers pollutes air, soil, and water, drives climate change, and intensifies other environmental impacts99,100. Fertilizers, especially nitrogen, release major pollutants in agriculture, harm plant health, contaminate products, and threaten human health100–102. Thus, the efficiency of chemical fertilizers in agriculture must be improved to reduce environmental impacts and enhance crop health.

Contrary to expectations, rising temperatures enhance apple production in Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Iran, Japan, Mexico, New Zealand, South Africa, and the United States, but have a negligible effect in Guatemala, India, and Turkey. The positive relationship between temperature and apple production in most of the selected countries is due to the widespread adoption of advanced irrigation systems, which help mitigate the negative effects of heat stress. Technologies such as drip irrigation and other water-efficient systems ensure that apple orchards receive adequate moisture during warmer periods, thereby facilitating crop growth despite elevated temperatures103,104. This finding contradicts the notion that higher temperatures reduce apple production44,92. Apple trees require specific temperature ranges for optimal growth and fruit development105–108. Temperatures exceeding these thresholds can damage the trees. Many apple varieties also require a certain number of winter chill hours to break dormancy and set fruit109,110. While apple trees can tolerate temperatures as low as −35 °C during dormancy, the most suitable regions for cultivation feature cold winters, moderately warm summers, and some humidity28. Insufficient chill hours during warmer winters can reduce fruit production23. Extreme temperatures, such as late-spring frosts or excessive heat during the growing season, can also damage crops and reduce yields, leading to poor fruit set or drop, sunburn, and lower fruit quality111,112.

Consistent with a priori expectations, we found that rainfall exerts varying effects on apple production across the selected countries. Specifically, rainfall significantly and positively influences apple production in India, Mexico, South Africa, and the United States. However, its impact is statistically non-significant, whether positive or negative, in other countries, including Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Guatemala, Iran, Japan, New Zealand, and Turkey. The study by113showed that climate shocks, particularly extreme precipitation before the growing season, significantly reduce apple yields. Consistent with this evidence114, found that most farmers in Lower Mustang, Nepal, identify rainfall as the most significant climatic hazard adversely affecting apple production. Extending this line of argument115, accentuated that even slight variations in climatic conditions can substantially reduce crop productivity because agriculture depends heavily on stable and predictable precipitation patterns. Nevertheless, other studies have shown that rainfall has no significant impact on crop production80,116,117.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Apples support nutrition across diverse populations. However, intensifying climate change alters global temperature and precipitation patterns and increasingly affects agricultural systems, including apple production. This study investigated the climate-related determinants of apple production using data from 13 major apple-producing countries. We employed Driscoll–Kraay regression, panel quantile regression, and fully modified ordinary least squares to assess the effects of climate variables on apple production at the aggregate and country levels. The results show that elevated temperatures and increased rainfall are associated with reduced apple production, while expanding harvested areas and fertilizer use positively influence apple production. Country-level results reveal substantial variation in climate impacts on apple production across the 13 major producing countries.

These findings have policy relevance for both apple producers and agricultural policymakers. The significant positive effect of cultivated area points out the central role of efficient land management in expanding apple production. Therefore, agricultural policymakers must promote advanced land-use practices such as precision agriculture and improved soil management to maximize productivity. Also, given the strong positive relationship between fertilizer use and apple output, we recommend precise, coordinated fertilizer application to substantially enhance production. Agricultural policies should promote integrated nutrient management strategies that align fertilizer use with soil conditions and crop nutrient requirements, as timely, well-calibrated applications can improve fruit quality and sustain higher apple production levels.

Moreover, the results accentuate the sensitivity of apple production to variations in temperature and rainfall. Given this vulnerability, policymakers must develop and implement adaptation strategies to strengthen the resilience of apple farming systems. Strengthening the resilience of apple farming systems requires adopting climate-smart agricultural practices, investing in irrigation and water management infrastructure, and developing and disseminating apple varieties with enhanced tolerance to climatic variability. To this end, governments must invest in strengthening research and extension services to support climate-adaptive apple farming. This policy intervention should include providing farmers with access to climate-resilient technologies, promoting sustainable agricultural practices, and enhancing training to manage the effects of temperature fluctuations and extreme rainfall.
