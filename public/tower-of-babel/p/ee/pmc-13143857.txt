Induction of Clinical Operational Tolerance Through Immunosuppression Minimization: Less Is More?
Authors: Emilia Knioła, Bartosz Foroncewicz, Jan Lerut, Leszek Pączek, Krzysztof Mucha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe goal of achieving clinical operational tolerance in liver transplantation has sparked significant interest, driven by the desire to mitigate the long‐term adverse effects of immunosuppression. Advances in understanding the immunological mechanisms underpinning operational tolerance in liver transplantation have highlighted the central role of regulatory T cells and tolerogenic dendritic cells. These cells actively suppress effector immune responses and aid in achieving operational tolerance. Immunosuppressive medication differentially influences the expansion, survival, and function of these cells, demonstrating the importance of understanding the potential of immunosuppressive regimens to optimize the possibility of achieving an immunosuppression‐free state. The potential of stepwise immunosuppressive reduction to achieve operational tolerance without compromising graft function has been demonstrated. The immunosuppressive withdrawal protocols underline the feasibility and safety of immunosuppressive withdrawal in selected liver transplant recipients, highlighting factors such as time since transplantation, recipient age, and gender as strong predictors of success. Furthermore, minimization of immunosuppressive, even early post transplantation, has been shown to be successful and sets the stage for successful immunosuppression withdrawal later. This review highlights the necessity to optimize immunosuppressive regimens by demonstrating its possibility to aid in achieving minimal immunosuppressive to maximize success rates of drug withdrawal and diminish the cumulative immunosuppressive related complications to increase the quality of life of liver transplant recipients.

Introduction

The father of liver transplantation (LT) Thomas E. Starlz dedicated his life to establishing the groundwork for organ transplantation [1]. One of his many achievements that opened a new field in transplantology was his research on drug‐free organ tolerance, a phenomenon now defined as clinical operational tolerance (COT). It is a clinical state where the graft maintains its function in the absence of all immunosuppression (IS) for at least 12 months, with no clinical and histopathological evidence of damage to the graft (described in Table 1) [4, 5, 6].

Importantly, minimization of IS has been proposed as a step leading up to achieving immune tolerance of the graft. Minimization is the state in which a low IS level is sufficient to maintain the proper graft function without pathologic damage of the graft [7]. Some patients might reach unexpected COT either by stopping the medication without medical supervision usually in the context of non‐compliance or when the transplant physician is obligated to stop IS because of severe complications such as sepsis or de novo tumour formation. Importantly, complete IS withdrawal is protocolized cessation of all maintenance IS drugs, including minimization, whereas COT describes the sustained outcomes as defined above.

The interest in achieving tolerance stems from the observation of the deleterious side effect of the long‐term administration of IS [8, 9]. The introduction of IS such as calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) significantly improved the short‐term post‐LT outcomes mainly by preventing acute rejection (AR) of the allograft [10]. However, the increasing number of complications attributed to drug side effects such as de novo tumour formation, infections, chronic kidney disease, metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, or cardiovascular disease significantly increased the long‐term mortality risk of the recipients [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Moreover, the burden of concomitant diseases further increases late morbidity and mortality [16].

In this context, the induction of COT has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance long‐term survival, particularly as the pool of LT recipient candidates continues to expand [17]. COT is observed more frequently in liver recipients compared to other solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients. This is due to the fact that (1) the liver is an immune privileged organ, (2) it has an increased functional reserve, (3) there is a lower prevalence of chronic rejection, (4) the requirement for initial and maintenance IS lower, and (5) the donor HLA constellation is less important [18]. As a result, LT recipients are the best candidates to lower IS load. However, it is important to recognize that not all LT recipients can achieve COT, and in these patients, the IS needs to be tailored to donor and recipient characteristics as well as the indication for LT. The Italian Liver Transplant Working Group agreed on a consensus to optimize the choice of IS regimens that aims to reduce the burden of side effects generated by maintenance IS [19, 20].

The Role of Regulatory T Cells in Operational Tolerance Induction in Liver Transplantation

The central role in the development of immune tolerance is played by T regulatory cells (Tregs) which dampen excessive immune responses and promote immune quiescence [21]. In a transplanted liver, effector T cells are responsible for mounting immune responses against specific non‐self‐antigens, leading to the allograft rejection [22]. Antigen‐activated Tregs, however, exert their function through both direct and indirect mechanisms, resulting in the inhibition of activation and proliferation of various effector T cell subsets (T helper (Th) 1, Th2 and Th 17) as well as mature dendritic cells (DCs) [23] depicted in Figure 1. Moreover, Tregs can induce the generation of tolerogenic dendritic cells (tol‐DCs) that are characterized by reduced expression of major‐histocompatibility complex (MHC) and costimulatory molecules, decreased secretion of IL‐12 and increased production of IL‐10 and TGF‐β. This subpopulation of DCs has a reduced ability to stimulate activation of T‐cells and promotes further increased proliferation of Treg [31].

With such potential to counteract effector immune response, the advances in cellular therapies have looked at the effectiveness of allo‐antigen specific Tregs at establishing operational tolerance (OT). Currently, there are 3 strategies to generate alloantigen specific Tregs (described by Kurt et al. [32]). When the allo‐antigen specific T reg is activated, they suppress priming and function of donor‐reactive T and B cells thought IL‐10/TGF‐beta release, CTLA‐4 mediated APC modulation, and other contact dependent mechanisms described above. This local, donor‐restricted immune control promotes a shift toward tolerogenic dendritic cells and clonal exhaustion/deletion of effectors, allowing in some recipients to enable OT [33, 34, 35].

Immunosuppressive Drugs and Immune Tolerance Development

It is clear that IS may have either a positive or negative impact on the of development of Treg cells (depicted in Figure 2) and tol‐DC subset. Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus (TAC) or cyclosporine A (CsA)) and mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus (SRL), everolimus (EVE)) influence the expansion and survival of Tregs [36, 37] [full review by Camirand et.al [37]].

Studies suggest that CNi might have a negative impact on Tregs and tol‐DCs [38]. In vitro studies on murine cells proved that the proliferation of Tregs stimulated by tol‐DCs treated with CsA drops from 78% to 48%, while direct treatment of Tregs with CsA further decreases their proliferation to 24% [39]. CsA alters the function of Tregs by inducing the synthesis of IL‐2 and INF‐γ, dysregulating their suppressive activity [38, 40]. Although the treatment of tol‐DCs with CsA cells increased their tolerogenic phenotype by decreasing their expression of MHC II molecules, their function was altered [39]. Interestingly, in vitro, the inhibitory effect of CsA on the suppressive function of Tregs was only observed in low doses of 20 ng/mL to 40 ng/mL. Additionally, a low dose of CsA switched the profile of cytokine production to a more proinflammatory one. Higher doses of 100 ng/mL and 400 ng/mL had no effect on the Treg function. The effect observed with a low dose of CsA is thought to be independent of the calcineurin pathway [40].

Although TAC shares a similar mechanism of action with CsA, studies assessing its impact on Tregs have reported no inhibitory effect on Treg proliferation. This was independent of the drug concentration [40]. In mice, TAC treated DCs could suppress the activation of CD4+ T cells and have increased production of IL‐10 [41]. However, TAC treatment of human derived immature DCs showed no effect on tol‐DC. In comparison to control tol‐DCs, there is no difference between the receptor expression phenotype as well as the profile of secreting cytokines, although the ratio between IL‐10/IL‐12 was of a pro‐tolerogenic profile [42]. It has been shown that LT recipients on monotherapy with TAC have a higher ratio of precursor DC2 to DC1 than those on other IS drugs [43]. The DC2 population of cells are regulatory in nature and regulate the activation of effector CD8+ T cells. In clinical setting, LT recipients receiving low dose TAC in a weaning protocol were found to have high number of precursors DC2 cells in comparison to those with those of high dose maintenance TAC dose [44].

On the other hand, it has been shown that mTOR inhibitors might stimulate Treg proliferation and function, possibly contributing to a more tolerogenic environment [45, 46, 47]. SRL and EVE have been found to promote the expansion of Tregs in comparison to both TAC treated as well as to control Treg cells. Additionally, mTOR increases the ability to maintain the expression of FOXP3. In a clinical setting, LT recipients on maintenance SRL monotherapy have a higher percentage of CD4 + CD25high + FOXP3+ T cells in comparison to those recipients on other monotherapy [43]. This finding should not be surprising as FOXP3+ expression requires IL‐2, which transcription is blocked by CNIs [37].

There have been attempts to upregulate the proliferation and suppressive function of Tregs by adding other IS drugs to CNIs. Although glucocorticosteroids have been found to increase Treg proliferation [48, 49, 50], they were unable to reverse the effects of CsA. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), in contrast, did counterbalance the ratio of Treg cells when combined with CsA [51].

In contemporary LT, induction therapy may facilitate delayed or lower‐dose initiation of maintenance immunosuppression and help reduce early immunologic risk, although current evidence shows only low‐certainty support for its impact on acute rejection or long‐term graft outcomes [52]. Induction IS depletes or blocks effector lymphocytes and also exerts profound effects on Tregs. In vitro and ex vivo studies demonstrate that rabbit anti‐thymocyte globulin (rATG, Thymoglobulin) and IL‐2 receptor (IL‐2R) blocking antibodies such as basiliximab and daclizumab have divergent effects on Treg biology.

Several groups have shown that rATG has reproducible Treg‐promoting effects in vitro/ex vivo: rATG converts conventional CD4+CD25− T cells into FOXP3+ Tregs and expands human Tregs through NFAT1‐ and STAT3‐dependent programs, with resultant suppressive function [53, 54]. Additional in vitro work shows rATG can increase FOXP3 expression and regulatory activity, supporting a tolerogenic shift in the T‐cell compartment [55]. At the same time, rATG is a lymphodepleting polyclonal antibody; its net effect in vivo is dose‐ and context‐dependent, with studies demonstrating broad T‐cell depletion (including naïve subsets) and variable Treg kinetics during immune reconstitution [56, 57, 58, 59].

By contrast, IL‐2 receptor (CD25) – blocking antibodies can transiently impair Treg biology. IL‐2 signalling is essential for Treg survival and function [60]. Basiliximab and daclizumab both reduce surface CD25 expression on T cells, including Tregs, via receptor blockade and modulation. In vitro, this results in reduced FOXP3 expression, diminished IL‐10 production, and impaired proliferative capacity of Tregs [61]. López‐Abente et al. showed that basiliximab exposure decreased circulating Treg counts and attenuated their suppressive function in paediatric transplant recipients, findings consistent with earlier in vitro work [62]. Taken together, available mechanistic and translational data indicate that induction choice can push Treg homeostasis in different directions with rATG tending toward Treg enrichment while IL‐2R antagonists toward transient Treg attenuation, but there is no definitive evidence that these cellular effects translate into higher rates of clinical operational tolerance after LT. Furthermore, with the emerging Treg cellular therapies, the maintenance IS surrounding infusion can either potentiate or erode the persistence and function of infused cells. The mTOR inhibitors support FOXP3 stability, proliferation, and lineage fidelity of human Tregs and can rescue CAR‐Tregs from tonic‐signal dysfunction, whereas CNIs depress IL‐2 transcription and can blunt Treg function. However, the CNI effects are partially reversible with physiologic low‐dose IL‐2 [63, 64]. Therefore, in practice Treg products should be paired with CNI‐minimizing, rapamycin‐based maintenance to promote in vivo engraftment, survival, and donor‐specific modulatory activity long enough to translate cellular effects into durable clinical benefit of OT.

Clinical Experience

The single centers reports describing their experience with purposeful withdrawal of IS in selected LT recipients have been previously summarized (refer to Lerut J et al. [65] for a detailed review) with the described success of around 20%. These reports were the bases that guided future trials. One of the first studies performed by the Kyoto group showed that in 38.1% of LT recipients it was feasible to achieve complete ISW while in 36.5% of LT recipients IS minimization was underway with the intent to wean off IS [66]. The possibility of this procedure was documented to be safe in an elective manner and not only in the clinical circumstance when ISW is necessary due to infection or de novo malignancy. Across prospective and observational adult cohorts, successful complete ISW is uncommon when attempted early after transplantation (< 5 years) with a rate of 13%, whereas late (> 5 years) withdrawal succeeds in around 30% overall (range 19%–53%) in carefully selected recipients and longer time since LT is a consistent positive predictor. In a prospective study conducted by Benitez C et al. [5], 102 LT recipients were enrolled to estimate the prevalence of COT in LT recipients. In this study, immunosuppression (IS) was tapered gradually, with the dose reduced by one‐quarter to one‐half every 3 weeks, guided by trough levels. Once subtherapeutic levels were reached, the dosing frequency was gradually reduced until IS was administered only once per week, with the goal of complete withdrawal within 6 to 9 months. Fourty‐one (41.84%) recipients reached the status of COT without detrimental progression of histological changes in the graft. None of them developed AR during the follow‐up period of 36 months. Additionally, based on the clinical parameters, high probability of successful ISW was associated with longer time from LTx, older age at transplantation as well as the male gender, with time since transplantation being the strongest predictor.

Feng et al. [6] reported higher early (< 3 years = 60%) and late (4–5 years = 37.5%) success rates in paediatric living donor LT recipients under strict protocolized conditions. The findings of the first prospective multicenter pilot trial in 20 paediatric recipients of parental living donor liver grafts (WISP‐R) reported that 12 LT recipients met the primary end‐point of the study: at least 1 year IS free with preservation of normal graft function. Biopsies obtained 2 years after ISW showed no significant changes compared to the baseline. In an additional study performed on a larger paediatric population (iWITH), COT, defined by both biochemical and histological criteria, was achieved in 33 (37.5%) of 88 children [67]. Sixteen were only biochemically tolerant while histological examination revealed features of interface hepatitis, with no clinical indication of rejection. The protocolization of ISW in paediatric patients is specifically important as opportunistic ISW success rates are reported as low as 4.8% [68].

The findings in the beforementioned studies demonstrate that carefully selected recipients both in adults and children can be IS free whilst maintaining the function of the graft. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the safety of ISW and the carried benefits of reducing the IS‐related comorbidities. Shaked A et al. [69] assessed the safety of ISW in LT recipients with hepatitis C or non‐immune liver disease initiated during the first 2 years post‐transplantation. In this study, 95 patients were randomly assigned to either withdrawal or maintenance of IS. Out of the 77 LT recipients who were in the ISW group, 10 patients (13.6%) were able to achieve COT in the intention‐to‐treat analysis. However, maybe one of the most important findings in understanding COT is that 71 LT recipients (92.2%) tolerated once‐a‐day dosing and 52 (67.5%) tolerated a reduction to ≤ 50% of the baseline dose of IS. This protocol of ISW included IS tapering in eight steps every 8 weeks and continued follow‐up for another 2 years after withdrawal. Longer follow‐up would be essential to demonstrate the other benefits of ISW [69]. An important but maybe indirect conclusion of this study is that broad‐based ISW trials conducted early after LTx without specific selection of LT recipients are unlikely to be successful, confirming the previously established finding in this field. Achieving minimal IS post‐LT has been shown to be feasible. The benefits of using minimal CNIs prove to be a favourable factor in preventing the development of malignancies, metabolic syndrome and chronic kidney disease post transplantation [14, 81, 82, 83, 84]. Many approaches have been proposed including monotherapy with CNIs, steroid free IS and conversion to mTOR inhibitors [85, 86, 87, 88, 89]. Lerut et al. demonstrated that TAC monotherapy can be achieved from the moment of transplantation [87], while ISW is rarely observed early post transplantation. A systemic meta‐analysis, which analysed data from 17 studies (691 patients), indicates that the likelihood of achieving COT increased with increased time from transplantation. It was calculated that in non‐Asian ISW candidates, the likelihood of COT increased by 7% per year after LT [90]. Additionally, the factors that have been associated with COT after multivariable logistic regression analysis were IS monotherapy, lack of rejection and time since LT [91]. This data suggests that if LT recipients are successfully maintained on minimal IS early post transplantation, ISW could be initiated later post transplantation when the likelihood of success increases, while simultaneously the cumulative dose of IS would be lower, thus decreasing the risk of developing IS side effects like malignancies [81]. The different trials conducted in relation to ISW have different protocols to achieve drug‐free state, making it difficult to draw clear conclusions. Most of them have identified specific recipient subgroups who can discontinue IS safely without graft injury, yet the criteria for patient selection and the tapering process remain poorly standardized.

A significant recent advancement toward standardizing IS withdrawal selection was presented by Vionnet et al. [92], who conducted the first multicenter randomized trial evaluating a liver tissue transcriptional biomarker as a predictive tool for safe IS withdrawal in adult LTx recipients. The study sought to operationalize tolerance assessment by using a five‐gene expression signature obtained from pre‐withdrawal biopsies to stratify candidates for tapering. Although conceptually promising, the biomarker failed to reliably distinguish tolerant from non‐tolerant recipients. It demonstrated that only 16% of participants achieved operational tolerance, while most developed rejection during tapering. Importantly, this study confirmed that late withdrawal and patient‐specific immune features, including exhausted CD8+ T‐cell phenotypes, correlated more closely with successful outcomes than molecular profiling alone. These findings underscore both the complexity of immune regulation within the liver allograft and the need for integrated predictive models combining clinical, immunologic, and transcriptional parameters to guide future IS minimization strategies.

Together, these observations highlight that while progress has been made in defining candidates for IS reduction, robust biomarkers capable of prospectively identifying tolerant recipients are still lacking. This gap reinforces the importance of mechanistic insight into the immunological basis of tolerance. In animal models, in the absence of IS, the default outcome of the allogenic liver graft tends to be spontaneous acceptance with infiltration of activated alloreactive T cells that can induce histopathological changes that resemble mild rejection. The exhaustion of activated alloreactive T cells with concurrent expansion and accumulation of Tregs in both the graft and periphery has been shown to establish donor‐specific tolerance in animal rodent models [93, 94, 95, 96]. Furthermore, the exhaustion of T cells develops in the presence of an abundance of antigens, and those T cells lose the ability to initiate rejection, promoting the clinical operational tolerance of the graft [97]. Striking the right balance between preventing graft rejection and maintaining the suppressive function of the immune system is crucial and must be understood as a continuum rather than separate stages [98]. Minimization of IS at the optimal time after transplantation may create conditions that favour the recovery of immunosuppressive Treg cells and tol‐DCs. This shift supports a cellular balance that helps prevent graft rejection and enables a more targeted, donor‐specific clonal exhaustion or deletion, which is hypothesized to contribute to the successful establishment of COT [99].

In both animals and humans, studies have shown that the liver possesses distinct immunological characteristics that contribute to its tolerogenic potential [100, 101]. These include the Kupffer cells, hepatic stellate cells, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells that all play pivotal roles in shaping the immune response. (reviewed by Dai H et al. [101] and Jiang Y et al. [100]). Additionally this unique subset of cells in the liver's microenvironment promotes the generation of Tregs and tol‐DCs which are designed to prevent excessive immune responses and maintain immune homeostasis [102, 103, 104]. Studies have shown that recipients, who achieve ISW have an enrichment of CD4 + CD25 + FOXP3+ Tregs in their liver graft [105] as well as in peripheral blood samples [106]. Additionally, when the population of these cells was assessed in LT recipients prospectively during ISW withdrawal, a 3.5—fold increase of FOXP3+ expression was observed in the group of LT recipients who achieve COT. However, those who experience rejection exhibited no change in numbers FOXP3+, even during successful minimization steps of IS before the episode of intolerance to the reduction [106]. This suggests that the ability to expand the population of CD4 + CD25 + FOXP3+ Tregs during minimization of IS might be an essential capacity of the recipient to achieve COT and potentially act as a biomarker of COT. The importance of Tregs expansion has been documented in LT recipients COT prospectively as the infiltration of liver portal tracts with these cells increases transiently 1 year post ISW. This phenomenon was also accompanied by later downregulation of proinflammatory, immune response and endothelial‐related genes at 3 years post transplantation [95].

Despite growing interest in complete immunosuppression withdrawal (ISW), robust long‐term outcome data are scarce. Most prospective studies define success at 12 months off therapy and follow patients for ≤ 3 years (e.g., Benítez et al.), with only a handful of cohorts extending beyond 5 years [5, 69, 107]. In adults, the largest long‐term single‐center series of COT (n = 23) reported a mean 7.3 years drug‐free, yet documented a late moderate rejection at 5.3 years, underscoring that tolerance may not be permanent [79]. Other reported long term follow up report 10% success rate after 17 years after ISW [108], while histopathological evaluation demonstrates minimal fibrosis 8 to 9 years after ISW [109]. Paediatric data from WISP‐R show stable histology and no fibrosis progression to 5 years off IS despite persistent class II DSA, and the multicenter iWITH program provides mid‐term (≈4‐year) trajectories under strict biopsy criteria [67, 110]. Collectively, these reports are insufficient to establish the net long‐term benefits and risks of ISW. There is a clear need for adequately powered, multicenter longitudinal studies with standardized histopathologic (including AMR phenotypes), serologic (DSA/C1q), and noninvasive fibrosis endpoints, with hard outcomes such as patient/graft survival, chronic rejection, renal/metabolic effects, infection, malignancy and quality‐of‐life and health‐economic analyses to define the true long‐term impact of this strategy.

Conclusion

The current knowledge and clinical experience that is available about the possibility of IS minimization and withdrawal in LT recipients is constantly growing. The immunological responses that are responsible for rejection as well as operational tolerance lie at the ends of the same continuum. The factor that strongly determines the direction the immunological system is going to take could be the IS regimen. It is important to recognize that the clinically used IS during minimization and/or withdrawal plays an important role in the induction as well as the maintenance of COT. As the scientific community dives deeper into this conundrum, it will become possible to identify the most tolerogenic profile of the IS regimen. However, till then we can only hypothesize the answer.
