Cross‐Cultural Adaptation and Psychometric Assessment of the CULIG's Questionnaire to Measure the Extent and Reasons for Medication Nonadherence in Iranian Patients With Type 2 Diabetes During (2017–2018): A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Susan Arghadeh, Hassan Mahmoodi, Seemin Dashti, Parvin Sarbakhsh, Abdolreza Shaghaghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThis study aimed to test adaptability of the CULIG's questionnaire (CULIG‐P) for assessment of medication regimen adherence among the Persian speaking patients with type 2 diabetes.MethodsA standard forward and backward translation procedure was utilized to adapt first draft of the CULIG‐P which was sent in the next stage to 15 experts for content and face validities' appraisal. The approved final draft of the CULIG‐P was completed by the 304 recruited patients with type 2 diabetes. Cronbach's alpha and Kappa with Linear weighting were also estimated to check internal consistency and reliability of the scale. The structural validity of the CULIG‐P was examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA).ResultsThe values of Cronbach's alpha (0.77) and weighted kappa coefficient for most of the scale's items were in acceptable range. Content Validity Index (CVI) and Content Validity Ratio (CVR) were also indicated reasonable construct of the CULIG‐P for assessment of the reasons of medication regimen nonadherence among the study participants. The implemented factor analysis with varimax rotation extracted five factors which explained 41.81% of the data's total variance.ConclusionsThe findings revealed usability of the CULIG‐P in studies and probably practice settings for assessment of medication regimen adherence/nonadherence in Persian‐speaking type 2 diabetes patients.Keywords: Cross‐cultural adaptation, CULIG, medication non‐adherence, Psychometric assessment, scale, Type 2 diabetes, Validation

Introduction

Medication regimen adherence is defined as degree of acquiescence with the recommended medication regimen by a patient such as taking medication regularly, having balanced diet and lifestyle changes to manage a disease or prevent its complications [1]. Generally in the case of chronic disease that need long term adherence to medication recommendation, the first 6 months of the therapy is pivotal in terms of success or failure to fully adhere to the recommended medication regimen [2]. Insufficient medication adherence is one of the main challenges for health systems and greater communities worldwide and in the case of type 2 diabetes it can lead to major life‐threatening complications or infuriate the disease state [1]. There are quantum of permissible evidence that reveal inadequacy of medication adherence in patients with type 2 diabetes [3, 4] which could raise the risk of myocardial infarction (MI), microvascular complications and the disease‐related mortality [5]. Empirical evidence also suggest that Iranian patients with type 2 diabetes do not fully adhere to the recommended medication recommendation [6, 7] therefore; incidence of type 2 diabetes related complications, comorbidities and even premature deaths were reported to be high among the patients which is a major burden on the patients themselves, their families and economy of the country [8, 9]. Such a pattern in resource‐limited countries may lead to overwhelming pressure on demand side of the health care equation where they are already facing significant challenges in responding to accumulating pressure of health care costs [8]. Therefore, identification and intervention on determinants of medication adherence, especially in case of chronic diseases is an urgent health priority in view of the seriousness of consequences if patients do not fully adhere to the recommended medication advices [10, 11]. An improved adherence to medication recommendations on the other hand, may lead to enhanced diseases management, prevention of diseases‐related complications and improved quality of life among the patients [12] that all are crucial for a healthy population.

Different factors such as degree of complexity and nature of a disease, patients' age and sex, their perceived social support, co‐existing morbidities for instance depression and anxiety, patients' self‐esteem and self‐efficacy and quality of a patient/care giver relationship were reported to be influential in extent of adherence to medication recommendations [1]. Health systems' functions relative to the available human, physical, and fiscal resources and socioeconomic status of the rearing environment are other prominent elements in this regard [1].

There are a number of data collection tools to study adherence/nonadherence to medication recommendations and based upon self‐reporting [13] i.e. Medication Adherence Questionnaire (MAQ), Beliefs about Medicines Questionnaire (BMQ) and Medication Adherence Rating Scale MARS) [14, 15]. The CULIG's Questionnaire [16] is another instrument that contain 16 items to examine reasons for medication nonadherence. We believe that culturally adapted, psychometrically sound instruments like the CULIG‐P are essential tools for advancing adherence research and informing context‐appropriate interventions in healthcare systems, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Given the current epidemiologic pattern of type 2 diabetes and its complications in Iran [17, 18], and the lack of a valid, culturally adapted instrument to identify reasons for medication nonadherence among Persian‐speaking patients, this study aimed to translate, adapt, and psychometrically assess the 16‐item CULIG questionnaire (CULIG‐P) for this population and this is the first research effort to culturally adapt and validate the CULIG questionnaire for Persian‐speaking patients with type 2 diabetes in Iran. We believe this adaptation provides a much‐needed tool for assessing reasons for medication nonadherence in a culturally relevant and linguistically appropriate manner, contributing to both local healthcare practice and global literature on chronic disease management.

Material and Methods

The CULIG's questionnaire was selected for this study because it comprehensively examines common reasons for medication nonadherence, aligning closely with the study's objective to identify determinants influencing medication adherence behavior among Iranian patients with type 2 diabetes. Additionally, the CULIG's instrument conciseness which offers a balanced number of items [16], is suitable for research‐hesitant respondents with varying literacy levels, due to minimizing respondent burden. Moreover, the items in the CULIG's questionnaire which predominantly focus on socio‐economic and health system related parameters seems to be pertinent in the Iranian context, making it a socio‐culturally relevant choice for application in similar studies. To the best of current knowledge, the CULIG's questionnaire had not been previously adapted or psychometrically validated in Persian‐speaking populations. The methodology gap highlights the importance of this cross‐cultural adaptation and validation study.

The Instrument's Translation and Validation Procedures

The standard forward/backward translation was performed [19] to prepare the Persian version of the CULIG's questionnaire. We followed internationally recognized forward–backward translation procedures to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence between the original and Persian versions of the CULIG questionnaire. This process was further strengthened by expert panel review and pilot testing to ensure cultural relevance and clarity for the target population. At the first stage, two academic and fluent speaker of Persian and English languages translated the original English version of the tool into Persian (CULIG‐P) independently. At the next step two other proficient translators back translated the Persian version into English. The back‐translators were unaware of the original version of the questionnaire. At the last stage, the prepared back‐translated English version of the CULG was compared with the original version word by word, minor modifications were made and the final draft was approved for use by the research team. This draft of the CULIG‐P were sent to 15 specialists in the field of epidemiology, health education and health promotion, social medicine, infectious disease and pharmacy for the content and face validities appraisal. The panelists were asked to give their opinion about clarity, simplicity, transparency and relevancy of the scale's items. The content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were calculated based on the panel members' feedbacks and the Lawshe's recommended cutoff points [20] were used for deciding on acceptable values of the indices. The drafted CULG‐P was also completed on 5 patients with type 2 diabetes to check understandability, ambiguity and lucidity of the items' wordings. Few amendments were made according to the patients’ comments and the final draft was used for its quantitative psychometric appraisal.

To assess internal consistency of the CULIG‐P Cronbach's alpha coefficient was estimated and to verify the test‐re‐test reliability of the instrument Kappa with linear weighting was calculated based on the given responses by 30 patients in 15 days interval as recommended in the COSMIN (Consensus‐based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments) reporting guideline [20]. Structural validity of the CULIG‐P was evaluated separately through conducting exploratory factor analysis (EFA).

Study Sample

Study participants were 304 patients with type 2 diabetes residing in the rural area of the city of Bonab, North West of Iran. Patients were randomly selected from the integrated electronic health information system (SIB) maintained by the Iranian National Health System (INHS). The SIB registry included all patients with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes in the study area. A list of eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria was generated, and each was assigned a unique identification number. Using a computerized random number generator, 320 patients were selected to be contacted, of whom 304 consented to participate and completed the questionnaire. The inclusion criteria were having 30–65 years of age, being diagnosed with type 2 diabetes at least 1 year ago, having a fixed phone line or cell phone (the patient or his/her close family member), being under treatment with oral or injectable drugs to control the illness. The exclusion criteria included not consenting to participate in the study or refusal to continue participation or having a severe disability that interfere with collection of reliable data.

The Data Collection Instrument

The CULIG questionnaire is a sixteen‐item tool to assess most frequent reasons for medication nonadherence. Each item has a four‐point Likert type answering option (never = 0, very rare or 1–2 yearly = 1, sometimes or 3–5 year = 2, often or more than 5 yearly = 3). Any item that receives the highest score is considered the most common reason for non‐adherence [21].

Ethical Considerations and Approval

This study was approved by the institutional level Medial Ethics Board of Trustees (MEBoT) within the Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR. TBZMED. REC.1396.629). Permission to contact with the study participants were obtained from the INHS's regional officials and a written/oral informed consent (oral consent in case of illiterate participants) was obtained from the respondents before initiation of the data collection and after provision of information about the study aims and objectives. Participants were also assured about confidentiality of the collected data and their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without obligation to explain reason(s).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

To assess the construct validity of the CULIG‐P scale, we conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) as a prespecified analysis. Sampling adequacy was first examined using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was used to test whether correlations between items were sufficient to justify factor analysis [22]. EFA was performed using Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) with varimax rotation to identify the underlying factor structure and reduce multicollinearity. Factor loadings ≥ 0.30 were considered acceptable for item retention [23, 24]. Items with loadings below this threshold were excluded due to weak associations with any extracted factor.

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a significance level of α = 0.05 was used. Where applicable, effect sizes, confidence intervals (CIs), and test statistics (e.g., χ², degrees of freedom) are reported alongside P values in accordance with best practice guidelines [24]. No subgroup or post‐hoc exploratory analyses were conducted beyond the prespecified EFA.

Content and Face Validity

Content validity was assessed using both the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and the Content Validity Index (CVI). All items, except items 1 (CVR = 0.06), 4 (CVR = 0.14), 6 (CVR = 0.20), 9, and 10 (CVR = 0.06), met the minimum CVR threshold of 0.49. CVI values were within acceptable limits for all items except items 1 (CVI = 0.46) and 10 (CVI = 0.60) [25]. Nevertheless, both of these items had an impact score above 1.5, indicating their relevance. Based on clinical judgment and the potential utility of these items in future studies, all 16 items were retained for further psychometric testing [26].

Structural Validity

The structural validity of the CULIG‐P was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) and varimax rotation. Findings of Bartlett test revealed the adequacy of the sample size for meaningful exploratory factor analysis (χ2 = 1191.28, df = 120, p < 0.001) and the estimated KMO (0.68) [22]. Internal Consistency and Reliability Measures

The estimated value of Cronbach's alpha for the CULIG‐P's first factor was 0.76 with three items [5, 8, 15], 0.62 for the second factor with four items [4, 7, 10, 11], 0.66 for the third identified factor with five items [1, 3, 6, 9, 13], 0.43 for the fourth extracted factor with 2 items [2, 16] and 0.28 for the fifth factor with two items [12, 14]. Test‐retest reliability was assessed using weighted kappa coefficients with linear weighting due to the ordinal nature of the questionnaire items. According to Landis and Koch (1977) [27], kappa values above 0.75 indicate excellent agreement, values between 0.40 and 0.75 indicate moderate agreement, and values below 0.40 suggest poor agreement. In our study, 10 items [1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16] demonstrated excellent reliability, 1 item showed moderate reliability, and 5 items [4, 6, 9, 12, 13] had fair to moderate reliability.

Discussion

The main aim of this study was to ascertain psychometric specifications and cultural adaptability of the CULIG's questionnaire for use in studies on main reasons of nonadherence with medication recommendations among Iranian type 2 diabetes patients. The original version of the CULIG's scale was tested on a sample of hypertensive patients by CULIG et al. [16] however, it was also suggested that this tool could be used for patients with chronic diseases [21, 28]. There are a number of self‐report scales for monitoring of medication adherence/nonadherence [13, 14, 15, 29] nevertheless, due to reasons such as differences in nature of the studied illnesses and socio‐cultural diversities of the recruited samples no gold‐standard instrument was identified nor introduced for application in studies on medication adherence [21]. In addition, except the CULIG's questionnaire, none of the other existent scales was precisely planned for use in studies on reasons of medication nonadherence [21]. Hence, the CULIG's questionnaire was selected for translation and psychometric appraisal in this study because of its relatively simple and understandable items, convincing number of items and inclusiveness of the stated reasons for nonadherence with medication recommendations that are consistent with the logics that are admissible in the Iranian socio‐economic and cultural context. This questionnaire was not adapted for application in the Iranian context before, and this is the first attempt to study its usability for Persian‐speaking patients with type 2 diabetes.

Comparison of the extracted factor structure of the CULIG‐P with findings of other research (including the original CULIG study) was not possible since, factorial structure of the scale was not analyzed to identify latent factors in studies that used 16‐item CULIG's questionnaire for data collection purposes [16, 21, 25, 26].

Based on our findings, the loaded items in Factor 1 reflect risk perceptions and concerns related to medication safety, including adverse effects, toxicity, and fear of dependence. Therefore, this factor was labeled as “Fear of Medication Harm and Dependence.”

The loaded items on Factor 2 represent situational or health‐related influences and the complexity of treatment, reflecting both the burden of illness and the burden of medication as contributors to nonadherence. Accordingly, this factor was labeled as “Health Conditions and Medication Burden”.

The extracted items in Factor 3 were related to unintentional nonadherence arising from memory lapses and contextual constraints. Therefore, the factor was labeled as “Forgetfulness and Daily life Inconveniences”.

Factor 4 consisted two conceptually coherent items describing issues related to medication access and affordability therefore, it was labeled as “Medication Access Barriers.”

Finally, the loaded two items in Factor 5 covering issues such as feeling recovered or experiencing difficulties with medication timing that could potentially cause discontinuation of the prescribed medications. Consistent with the items content this factor hence, was labeled as “False Sense of Recovery/Medications Timing”.

Factor loading for one of the CULIG‐P's items (0.138) in factor 2 was below the acceptable range (0.3). The value may suggest that the item i.e. “my doctor frequently changes my therapy” is not strongly contribute to the underlying factor structure. However, the decision to retain the item was based on its clinical relevance and acceptable value of the corresponding factors’ internal consistency measure (0.62). Thus, it was concluded that benefit of keeping this item could overweighs the benefit of its elimination. The estimated values of Cronbach's alpha for the factors four and five, each with two loaded items, were below the acceptable rage (0.6) that might be due to a low number of questions (two) in the extracted factors [30]. That being the case, further construct validity appraisal of the CULIG‐P in future large scale studies is recommended to see if any other model fit into the collected data.

The Study Limitations

This study results warrant further speculation concerning several methodological issues that could convey some limitations in generalizability and external validity of the findings. Reasons for medication nonadherence was examined in this study in a specific context and on generally those participants that had low level of education. Consequently, probability of the sample selection bias and self‐report bias might not be ruled out. The sample used in this study was predominantly female (78%) and had a low education level, with approximately 70% of participants being illiterate. While this reflects the demographic characteristics of the rural population studied, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to more diverse or urban populations. Future studies should aim to include more balanced samples in terms of gender, literacy, and socioeconomic background to improve the external validity and applicability of the CULIG‐P questionnaire across various subgroups of patients with type 2 diabetes.

One limitation of the current study is the absence of an evaluation of construct validity in the sense of examining the relationship between the CULIG‐P scores and other relevant measures, such as established medication adherence questionnaires or clinical biomarkers like HbA1c. According to COSMIN guidelines, such comparative analyses are important to confirm that the instrument under study accurately reflects the construct it intends to measure [31, 32]. Future studies are encouraged to assess convergent validity of the CULIG‐P by simultaneous application of other medication adherence tools and relevant clinical outcomes in the sub‐samples of Iranian population. The observed gender imbalance in the sample recruitment process might have high potential to cause bias in the collected data. Non‐diligent male participants and their low level of enthusiasm to engage in health studies could be a major challenge for researchers that needs to be investigated. Recall bias was another potential confounder in this study that could have tipping impact on the collected data. Regarding all above mentioned caveats a broader, socio‐culturally diverse and more representative sample is needed for collection of a normative and objective data in psychometric assessment of the CULIG‐P's questionnaire in future studies.

Although the sample size (n = 304) met general guidelines for exploratory factor analysis, the extraction of five factors from a 16‐item scale may limit the stability and generalizability of the resulting factor structure. The KMO value of 0.68 supported the adequacy of the sample, but future validation studies with larger and more diverse populations are warranted to confirm the factor solution and improve structural reliability. Factors 4 and 5 each contained only two items, which may compromise the stability and reliability of these factors. The decision to retain a five‐factor model was based on the eigenvalue criterion and the conceptual distinctiveness of the items within these factors. However, given the small number of items, we acknowledge that these factors may be less stable and recommend that future studies with larger samples consider revising or collapsing these factors to improve psychometric robustness. The absence of comparisons with other adherence instruments or clinical biomarkers (e.g., HbA1c) also limited assessment of the CULIG‐P's construct validity. Future studies should evaluate convergent and divergent validity using complementary tools and clinical indicators.

Conclusions

The study results support the feasibility of CULIG‐P and its relatively acceptable psychometric properties for application in examination of the reasons for medication nonadherence in the Iranian context. Further studies and conduction of invariance analyses are recommended in future to verify the CULIG‐P's questionnaire applicability as an equivalent tool across socio‐demographically and culturally diverse sub‐samples and to reassess some of the mentioned shortcomings. Albeit, the study findings revealed that with minor modification the Persian version of the CULIG's questionnaire can be considered as an economic and reliable assessment tool of medication nonadherence in Persian‐speaking type 2 diabetes patients.
