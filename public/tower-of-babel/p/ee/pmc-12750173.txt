Glutamine synthetase in astrocytes of the caudate and putamen is responsible for locomotor sensitization after nicotine exposure
Authors: Ju Hwan Yang, Sumin Sohn, Sunghyun Kim, Jieun Kim, Su Yeon Seo, Aqsa Kazmi, Hanwoong Woo, John Q. Wang, Eun Sang Choe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glutamine synthetase (GS) in astrocytes regulates glutamatergic neurotransmission by maintaining glutamate clearance in the brain. This study determined that GS in astrocytes of the caudate and putamen (CPu) regulates locomotor sensitization after repeated nicotine exposure. Nicotine increased phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase (pJNK) by stimulating α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in cultured glioma C6 cells and primary astrocytes in a Ca2+-dependent manner. Active JNK phosphorylated metabotropic glutamate receptor 1a (mGluR1a) at the carboxyl terminus of glutathione S-transferase-tagged mGluR1a in vitro. Interference with the pJNK–mGluR1a interaction using the inhibitory peptide, Tat-mGluR1a-i (10 μmol/L), decreased the nicotine-induced increase in GS activity in glioma C6 cells and primary astrocytes. Similar results were obtained by bilateral intra-CPu infusion of the inhibitory peptide (2 nmol/side). Inhibition of GS activity by bilateral intra-CPu infusion of methionine sulfoximine (50 nmol/side) decreased the repeated nicotine-induced increase in locomotor activity. These findings suggest that astrocytes in the CPu upregulate locomotor sensitization by activating GS via the pJNK–mGluR1a interaction, which is linked to α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in response to nicotine.

Introduction

Astrocytes are the most abundant type of neuroglia throughout the brain1,2. They produce a small amount of electrical excitability due to the lack of axons, but release glutamine, regulating glutamatergic neurotransmission in the forebrain3, 4, 5. A single astrocyte forms approximately 20 to 120,000 synapses in a rodent brain, thus expressing numerous receptors and transporters on its membrane6,7. Astrocytes express heteromeric β2 and homomeric α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs)8, 9, 10. It is known that α7 nAChRs are functional subtypes responsible for mediating the effects of nicotine after direct binding to the receptors in the astrocytes of rodents9,11.

Studies have shown that nicotine exposure leads to morphological and functional modulation in astrocytes by stimulating α7 nAChRs. Chronic exposure to nicotine increases the volume and extension of astrocyte processes in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and substantia nigra of mice13. Stimulation of α7 nAChRs by direct binding of nicotine in astrocytes increases intracellular Ca2+ concentration12, 13, 14, 15, 16. The elevation of Ca2+ concentration activates a series of protein kinases, including c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), which is activated by stressful stimuli, such as drug exposure17. Furthermore, activation of JNK upregulates locomotor sensitization by phosphorylating metabotropic glutamate receptor subtype 5 (mGluR5) via intracellular Ca2+ cascades in the caudate and putamen (CPu) of rats18. These findings suggest that nicotine alters behaviors by stimulating α7 nAChRs through activated JNK-mediated phosphorylation of mGluR5 in astrocytes, a process that is Ca2+-dependent.

Like neurons, astrocytes express group I mGluRs (mGluR1/5)19,20. These receptors in astrocytes maintain glutamate homeostasis by clearing extracellular glutamate. Studies have shown that stimulation of mGluR5 in astrocytes activates ion-mediated glutamate transporter-1 (GLT-1, also known as excitatory amino acid transporter 2) that uptakes excess glutamate from the synaptic clefts20. Activation of glutamine synthetase (GS) converts glutamate to glutamine, presenting it to glutamatergic neurons, which is necessary for maintaining glutamate homeostasis in the CPu21,22. A previous study has shown that JNK regulates GS activation in astrocytes23. Taken together, GS can be activated by JNK-induced mGluR1/5 stimulation in astrocytes, which is likely involved in glutamate clearance in the CPu, regulating glutamatergic neurotransmission in neurons.

The fact that chronic exposure to nicotine upregulates glutamatergic neurotransmission in neurons of the CPu may represent the disruption of glutamate homeostasis, leading to the development of dependence. Indeed, astrocytes play a crucial role in maintaining glutamatergic neurotransmission by precisely presenting the glutamate precursor, glutamine. In this study, we demonstrate that elevation of phosphorylated (p)JNK linked to α7 nAChRs upregulates GS activity by stimulating mGluR1a in astrocytes, thereby enhancing locomotor sensitization in response to nicotine. This finding implies the crucial role of astrocytes in behavioral sensitization by upregulating glutamatergic neurotransmission in neurons of the CPu.

Materials and methods

Animals

Sprague–Dawley male rats (200–250 g) (total number of rats = 96) and C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Hyo-Chang Science (Daegu, Korea). Animals were housed in pairs in a controlled environment during the experimental period. Food and water were provided ad libitum, and the rats were maintained in a 12 h light/dark cycle. Injections were given in a home cage in a quiet room to minimize stress on the animals. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Pusan National University (PNU-2023-3236) and Kangwon National University (KW-230907-3), and were performed following the provisions of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All experiments conducted in the present study were carefully designed as randomized double-blind studies to minimize potential bias and thus enhance the reliability of the results.

Reagents and peptides

Nicotine hydrogen tartrate salt was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and dissolved in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) for cell culture or in sterile 0.9% physiological saline for rats. The solution was then adjusted to a pH of 7.2–7.4 using sodium hydroxide. The nicotine concentrations applied in this study were 100 μmol/L for cell culture and 0.5 mg/kg/day for subcutaneous injections to rats24,25. To minimize stress in the animals caused by the systemic injections of saline or nicotine, dummy injections were performed for 3 days. After habituation, rats were randomly divided into two groups and administered saline or nicotine for 7 consecutive days. The inhibitory (Tat-mGluR1a-i) and scrambled (Tat-mGluR1a-s) peptides were synthesized to interfere with pJNK binding to mGluR1a (Peptron, Daejeon, Korea). The inhibitory peptide contained a D-motif that served as a pJNK binding site on the mGluR1-CT domain, and the scrambled peptide included random amino acid sequences. Both peptides contained the HIV-1 Tat N-terminus domain (YGRKKRRORRR) to enhance cell permeability. Drugs were purchased from Tocris Bioscience (Bristol, UK) or Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Solutions were prepared freshly before the beginning of each experiment. The α7 nAChRs antagonist, methyllycaconitine citrate (MLA), and glutamine synthetase inhibitor, methionine sulfoximine (MSO) were dissolved in DMEM. The JNK inhibitor, SP600125, was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide. They were dissolved by heating at 35 °C and applied to glioma C6 cells or primary astrocytes 1 h prior to nicotine treatment. The cell-permeable Ca2+ chelator, BAPTA-AM, or inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) receptor blocker, (−)-xestospongin C, was dissolved in DMEM by heating at 35 °C and applied to the primary astrocytes 1 h prior to nicotine treatment. Intra-CPu injected peptides or MSO were dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4) (Gibco). The concentrations of MLA (20 nmol/L), BAPTA-AM (20 μmol/L), (−)-xestospongin C (10 μmol/L), SP600125 (20 μmol/L), MSO (2 mmol/L for glioma C6 and primary astrocytes; 5 or 50 nmol/side for rats), and peptides (10 μmol/L for glioma C6 and primary astrocytes; 2 nmol for rats) were determined based on cell viability test and previous studies26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 (Supporting Information Fig. S1A–S1E).

Surgery and intra-CPu infusion of peptides and drugs

Rats were anesthetized by the subcutaneous injection of a mixture of Zoletil 50 (75 μL/kg) (Virbac Korea, Seoul, Korea) and Rompun (50 μL/kg) (Bayer Korea, Seoul, Korea). The animals were then fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus, and a 23-gauge stainless steel dual guide cannula (Plastics One, Roanoke, VA, USA) was implanted bilaterally into the CPu (1 mm anterior to the bregma, ±2.5 mm lateral to the bregma, and 4 mm below the surface of the skull). The guide cannula was sealed with a stainless steel wire (Plastics One) of the same length. Rats were allowed to recover from surgery for 5 days prior to starting saline or nicotine injections. On the day of the experiment, the inner steel wire was replaced with a 30-gauge stainless steel injection cannula that protruded 2 mm beyond the guide cannula. Peptides (2 nmol) or MSO (50 nmol) were infused 7 min prior to the final injection of saline or nicotine into both sides of the CPu, with a total volume of 1 μL/side delivered at a rate of 0.5 μL/min for 2 min, in freely moving rats. The injection progress was monitored by observing the movement of a small air bubble along the length of the pre-calibrated PE-10 tubing inserted between the injection cannula and a 2.5 μL Hamilton Microliter syringe (Hamilton Company, Reno, NA, USA). After completing the injection, the injector was left in place for an additional 5 min to reduce any possible backflow of the solution along the injection tract25. The possibility of gliosis caused by implantation of the guide cannula, intra-CPu infusions of reagents or peptides was assessed using Nissl staining and immunofluorescence (IF) staining (Supporting Information Fig. S2A and S2B).

Cell culture

Glioma C6 cells, derived from rat brain, are used as an astrocyte-like cell line in various research fields. The cells were purchased from Korea Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea) and cultured in DMEM containing 5% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (Gibco). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator with an atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. For all experiments, the cells were scraped out and washed twice with ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco). Primary glial cells were isolated from the brains of postnatal Day 1 C57BL/6J mice. The CPu was placed in ice-cold HBSS (Gibco). After washing twice in low-glucose DMEM, the brain was filtered through a 70-μm nylon mesh and cultured in low-glucose DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco) in a 5% CO2 incubator. On Day 14, the mixed glial cells were agitated at 200 rpm (SHO-2D digital orbital shaker, DAIHAN-Sci Co., Wonju, Korea) at room temperature (RT) for 12 h to remove primary microglial cells. Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) (Gibco) was subsequently used to detach primary astrocytes, which were centrifuged 3 times at 1500 rpm (T04B low-speed benchtop centrifuge, Hanil Scientific Inc., Yongin, Korea) for 10 min before use12.

Cell viability test

Since high concentrations of nicotine can cause cytotoxicity in vivo and in dissociated culture cells, we tried to identify the effective nicotine concentration without damaging glioma C6 cells. The viability of glioma C6 cells was determined using a water-soluble tetrazolium salt assay (BIOMAX, Guri, Korea). The cells were plated at a density of 5 × 103 per well on a 96-well cell culture plate and incubated for 24 h in the CO2 incubator. The cells were incubated for 24 h with various concentrations (1, 10, 100, 1000 and 10,000 μmol/L) of nicotine. After incubation, the water-soluble tetrazolium salt solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min before measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

Calcium imaging

Glioma C6 cells were cultured in a confocal dish (SPL, Pocheon, Korea) with a confocal region of 1.33 cm2 at a density of 8000 cells. The 5 μmol/L of fluo-4 AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was applied to glioma C6 cells 45 min prior to nicotine treatment. The dye was washed with DMEM and calcium imaging was performed using an LSM 700 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany). We used the following conditions: a 488 nm laser with a 40 × water-immersible objective, 500 ms interval with 2-frame averaging, and 8-bit depth over a period of 10 min. The Ca2+ dynamics were analyzed by measuring the fluorescence signals. The fluorescence intensity was analyzed by measuring the mean pixel intensities of the selected regions of interest in cells using ImageJ (version 1.54, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). The fluorescence changes were calculated as ΔF/Fbase relative to the fluorescence intensity baseline (Fbase) by using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). The Fbase was measured prior to nicotine treatment.

Synthesis of glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tagged proteins

GST-tagged proteins containing mGluR1a intracellular fragments were synthesized according to the method described previously33. The cDNA fragments that encode mGluR1a intracellular loop 1 (IL1) (L616-E629), -IL2 (R681-Q706), -IL3 (K773-K785), -intracellular C-terminus 1 (CT1) (K841-T1000), -CT1a (K841-N885), -CT1b(A886-K931), -CT1c(N925-T1000), -CT2 (P1001-L1199), -CT2a (P1001-R1121), -CT2b (R1121-V1166) and -CT2c (A1161-L1199) were generated by polymerase chain reaction amplification from full-length cDNA clones (Geneart, Regensburg, Germany). These cDNAs were subcloned into the BamHI–EcoRI site of a pGEX4T-3 plasmid (Amersham Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA). To confirm appropriate splice fusion, all constructs were sequenced. GST-tagged proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 cells (Amersham Biosciences) and purified from bacterial lysates.

Phosphorylation and dephosphorylation reactions in vitro

GST or synthesized GST-tagged proteins were incubated with active or inactive JNK1 (200 ng) (Sigma–Aldrich) for 30 min at 30 °C in a reaction buffer (25 μL) containing 10 mmol/L 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid pH 7.4, 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 1 mmol/L Na3VO4, 1 mmol/L dithiothreitol, 50 μmol/L adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) and 2.5 μCi/tube [γ-32P] ATP (3000 Ci/mmol; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The phosphorylation reaction was stopped by adding sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer and boiling at 95 °C for 5 min. Phosphorylated proteins were resolved by bisacrylamide gel electrophoresis and visualized by autoradiography or immunoblotting. For the dephosphorylation assay, GST-tagged proteins were incubated with active JNK1 (200 ng) in 25 μL of the reaction buffer. After incubating the samples for 30 min at 30 °C, GST-tagged proteins were precipitated, and the supernatant containing JNK1 was removed. Precipitates were washed twice, suspended in a solution containing 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl (pH 8.5), 1 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.1 mmol/L ZnCl2, and calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP; 100 units/mL; Signal Chem, Richmond, BC, Canada), and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The dephosphorylation reaction was stopped by adding SDS sample buffer. Samples were then subjected to 10% bisacrylamide gel electrophoresis and visualized by immunoblotting34.

In vitro binding assay

In vitro binding assay was performed using a binding buffer containing 200 mmol/L NaCl, 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin, and 50 mmol/L Tris (pH 7.5). Synthesized GST-tagged mGluR1a proteins were incubated with binding buffer for 3 h at 4 °C. Glutathione-sepharose (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA) was added to each sample and gently mixed for 1 h at RT. Precipitates were washed five times with binding buffer and eluted with SDS sample buffer. The bound proteins were resolved by 10% bisacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was incubated with primary antiserum (Phospho-SAPK/JNK or SAPK/JNK) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) (1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 for 10 min each, the membrane was incubated with the appropriate secondary antiserum for 1 h at RT. The immunoreactivity of protein bands was detected by enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (ratio of reagents A to B = 1:500; Ab Frontier, Seoul, Korea) and exposed to X-ray films34,35.

Western blot

The glioma C6 cells or primary astrocytes were lysed in a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged for 20 min at 13,000 rpm (Centrifuge 5424R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 4 °C. Concentrations of solubilized proteins in the supernatants were determined using the Bradford method with a Bio-Rad Protein Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). 10 μg of proteins were resolved by 10% SDS-bisacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and the separated proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were then probed with rabbit primary antiserum against phospho-SAPK/JNK (Cell Signaling Technology), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, α7 or β2 subtype of nAChRs (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) overnight at 4 °C on a shaker. The membranes were then washed three times and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antiserum (KPL, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) for 1 h at RT. Immunoreactive protein bands were detected using an iBright Western blot imaging systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the enhanced chemiluminescence solution (Ab Frontier). The samples were probed using a rabbit primary antiserum against total SAPK/JNK (Cell Signaling Technology) after stripping the same membrane, which had previously been confirmed to contain the phosphorylated protein. Immunoreactive protein bands on films were semi-quantified using ImageJ (version 1.54, NIH)36. All images are provided as Supporting Information (Supporting Information Figs. S3 and S4).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Glioma C6 cells were harvested at 10 min after nicotine (100 μmol/L) treatment and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. The cells were homogenized on ice in IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Solubilized proteins (500 μg) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit antiserum against mGluR1a/b (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). The complex was then precipitated with 50% protein G sepharose bead slurry (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences) for 4 h with gentle shaking at 4 °C. Beads were spun down and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, and then the supernatants were collected. The supernatants were kept at −80 °C until use. Proteins were separated by 10% bisacrylamide gels and probed with mouse primary antiserum against mGluR1 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) or rabbit primary antiserum against pJNK (Cell Signaling Technology)34. Western immunoblotting was performed as described above.

IP3 assay

The concentration of cells was diluted with PBS (pH 7.2–7.4) to 100 million/mL, and stored overnight at −20 °C. After two freeze-thaw cycles to break up the cell membranes, the cell lysates were centrifuged at 5000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was kept and assayed immediately. The level of IP3 production was determined using an IP3 ELISA kit (Cusabio Technology, Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After performing the assay, the microplates were read at absorbance of 450 nm using a GloMax Multimode Microplate Reader (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

GS activity assay

Glioma C6 cells, primary astrocytes, or the extract of the CPu were homogenized with ice-cold GS assay buffer and kept on ice for 10 min. The cell lysates were centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000×g at 4 °C. The supernatants were kept, and protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay. The GS activity in glioma C6 cells, primary astrocytes, or the rat CPu was determined using two different kinds of Glutamine Synthetase Activity Assay kit (PromoCell GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany, for glioma C6; Abcam for primary astrocytes and the rat brain) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After performing the assay, the microplates were read at an absorbance of 570 nm for 60 min using a VICTOR Nivo Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer).

IF staining of primary astrocytes

Cells were fixed for 10 min in 4% paraformaldehyde and washed three times with PBS. They were then incubated overnight with the following primary antiserum in glycine-detergent buffer: anti-mGluR1 (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology) or anti-GFAP (1:1000, Abcam). Anti-pJNK (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology) antiserum was conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 using the Flexible CoraLite kit (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). Following incubation, cells were washed with PBS and treated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antiserum (1:200, Invitrogen, MA, USA) for 2 h at RT. The cells were washed three times with PBS and then incubated in a DAPI solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min. After DAPI staining, the cells were washed, mounted onto slides using fluorescence mounting medium (Dako, Glostrup, DNK), and imaged with a THUNDER imager 3D assay microscopy (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

IF staining of brain sections

Rats were deeply anesthetized and perfused transcardially with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (pH 7.2) at 4 °C. The brain was removed and post-fixed in 10% sucrose in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h at 4 °C and then placed in 20% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C overnight. The brain was sectioned into 30 μm coronal sections using a vibratome. The sections were blocked with a blocking buffer containing 4% normal goat serum and 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS. The sections were incubated in mouse antiserum (1:200) against S100B (Proteintech) or rabbit antiserum (1:200) against pJNK (Cell Signaling Technology) for 48 h at 4 °C on a shaker. After rinsing three times using PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antiserum (1:500) conjugated to goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 or goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam) for 2 h at RT on a shaker. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C on a shaker with antiserum (1:100) against mGluR1a (Cell Signaling Technology) conjugated with CoraLite Plus 647 (Proteintech). The sections were mounted on glass slides, cover-slipped using a Vectashield mounting solution (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), and visualized with a fluorescence camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) or an LSM 700 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) equipped with specific filters for detecting specific wavelengths. Images of the sections were obtained using ZEN 2012 SP2 (blue edition) image processing software37. The photographed areas in each brain section were shown in red squares, and double-immunofluorescent-labeled pixels in red squares were counted. Quantitation of pJNK immunoreactivity was performed by calculating the ratio of S100B and pJNK double-stained cells to total S100B stained cells. The values of each group were then compared.

Behavioral assessments

Locomotor activity after bilateral intra-CPu infusion of the GS inhibitor, MSO, prior to the final injection of saline or nicotine was measured in an open field using an infrared photocell-based Opto-Varimex-4 Auto-Track (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH, USA) under sound-attenuated and illuminated conditions. Rats were placed in a standard transparent rectangular chamber (44.5 cm × 44.5 cm × 24 cm) and habituated for 30 min prior to injections to minimize environmental variations and stress. Three pairs of sensors were positioned on orthogonal axes within the cage to provide the coordinates of rat movements in the locomotor testing chamber. Each pair of sensors produced 16 infrared light beams intersecting the animal cage (beam scan rate = 10 Hz). The Auto-Track system detected the presence of animals based on interruptions in the beam. Locomotor activity was recorded in 5 min intervals for over an hour after the final injection of saline or nicotine. The measurements were transferred to a computer running Columbus Instruments version 4.99B software33. All rats were anesthetized with a mixture of Zoletil 50 and Rompun, and brains were removed to identify the placements of guide cannula.

Statistics

The significance of differences between groups was determined by unpaired t-test, or by one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test, using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM) for each group (n = 3–12). Statistical significance was determined by a P value < 0.05. The sample size for the behavioral study was determined through power analysis using GPower (version 3.1.9.7; Universität Kiel, Kiel, Germany), with a power level greater than 0.8 (P > 0.8), based on other studies38, 39, 40.

Results

Nicotine increases pJNK-mediated GS activity by stimulating α7 nAChRs in cultured cells

This study examined whether nicotine increases GS activity via JNK phosphorylation by stimulating α7 nAChRs in cultured glioma C6 cells or primary astrocytes. We first determined an appropriate nicotine concentration that did not induce cytotoxicity in the cultured cells. Cell viability decreased at 1000 μmol/L and decreased by more than 50% at 10,000 μmol/L (Supporting Information Fig. S5A) (treatment, F(5, 18) = 206.1, P < 0.05). Therefore, we selected 100 μmol/L as an optimal concentration of nicotine for the subsequent experiments. Nicotine treatment increased pJNK immunoreactivity at the 10 min time point, and then returned to the control level at 30 min after nicotine treatment in both glioma C6 cells and primary astrocytes (Fig. 1A and B) (A, treatment, F(3, 8) = 50.92, P < 0.05; B, treatment, F(3, 8) = 12.59, P < 0.05). There was no difference in the expression of α7 nAChRs after nicotine treatment (Fig. S5B and S5C) (S5B, treatment, F(3, 8) = 0.1610, P = 0.9196; S5C, treatment, F(3, 8) = 0.8069, P = 0.5245). Immunofluorescence staining showed that pJNK, mGluR1a, and GFAP were co-expressed in the primary astrocytes, and pJNK immunoreactivity was increased after nicotine treatment (Fig. S5D). We quantified Ca2+ concentration by measuring the intensity of fluo-4. Pre-treatment with the selective α7 nAChRs antagonist, MLA (20 nmol/L), decreased elevated fluorescence intensity at the time slot between 199 and 231 s after nicotine exposure in glioma C6 cells. The effects of interaction, nicotine and MLA showed F(1, 44) = 7.778, P < 0.05, F(1, 44) = 15.56, P < 0.05, and F(1, 44) = 5.577, P < 0.05, respectively when Ca2+ intensity reached a peak (Fig. 1C). Treatment with MLA (20 nmol/L) prior to nicotine treatment decreased the nicotine-induced pJNK immunoreactivity (Fig. 1D and E) (D, interaction, F(1, 8) = 25.31, P < 0.05; nicotine effect, F(1, 8) = 17.52, P < 0.05; MLA effect, F(1, 8) = 23.51, P < 0.05; E, interaction, F(1, 8) = 20.88, P < 0.05; nicotine effect, F(1, 8) = 0.02362, P = 0.8817; MLA effect, F(1, 8) = 3.188, P = 0.112; post hoc: vehicle + repeated saline group versus vehicle + repeated saline group, P < 0.05) in glioma C6 cells and primary astrocytes. Parallel to the results, blockade of the receptors decreased IP3 concentration in glioma C6 cells (Fig. S5E) (interaction, F(1, 8) = 17.56, P < 0.05; nicotine effect, F(1, 8) = 48.91, P < 0.05; MLA effect, F(1, 8) = 17.39, P < 0.05). Since pJNK immunoreactivity was increased, we determined the involvement of Ca2+ mobilization after nicotine treatment. Blockade of IP3 receptors with xestospongin C (10 μmol/L) (Fig. 1F) (interaction, F(1, 8) = 27.24, P < 0.05; nicotine effect, F(1, 8) = 11.90, P < 0.05; xestospongin C effect, F(1, 8) = 21.29, P < 0.05) and the Ca2+ chelator, BAPTA-AM (20 μmol/L) (Fig. 1G) (interaction, F(1, 8) = 80.02, P < 0.05; nicotine effect, F(1, 8) = 74.59, P < 0.05; BAPTA-AM effect, F(1, 8) = 109.7, P < 0.05) decreased the nicotine-induced increase in pJNK immunoreactivity in primary astrocytes. Discussion

The present study found that blocking α7 nAChRs in glioma C6 cells and primary astrocytes decreases the nicotine-induced increase in pJNK. Since α7 nAChRs have the highest Ca2+ permeability among the nAChRs, stimulation of these receptors in astrocytes increases intracellular Ca2+ influx, which facilitates JNK phosphorylation12,45. Evidence also shows that the elevation of intracellular Ca2+ concentration increases JNK phosphorylation in primary cultured cortical astrocytes46. These findings suggest that nicotine leads to JNK phosphorylation by increasing Ca2+ conductance via stimulation of α7 nAChRs in astrocytes. This prediction is further supported by the present finding that blockade of α7 nAChRs decreases the nicotine-induced increase in Ca2+ concentration and chelation of Ca2+ or blockade of the IP3 receptors in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) decreases the nicotine-induced increase in pJNK in primary astrocytes.

Blockade of α7 nAChRs also decreases the nicotine-induced increase in the concentration of IP3 in glioma C6 cells in the present study. A previous study has shown that stimulation of α7 nAChRs elevates IP3 concentration in astrocytes47. IP3 is coupled to mGluR1a in the cerebellar neurons of rats32. These findings suggest that nicotine upregulates JNK phosphorylation by stimulating α7 nAChRs, leading to mGluR1a stimulation in astrocytes. Taken together, JNK phosphorylation may enable to interact with mGluR1a, leading to IP3-mediated Ca2+ mobilization from the ER of astrocytes.

The mGluR1a has a splice variant with a unique long CT that is capable of interacting with various protein kinases, as demonstrated previously32,48,49. The CT of mGluR1a contains a D-motif for a binding site present in the substrate of mitogen-activated protein kinase that is composed of Arg/Lys1-2-X2-6-ØA-X-ØB43,44. Activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase phosphorylates serine residues in the CT of mGluR1/5 in vitro but binds to D-motif within the CT32,50. Given that the CT of mGluR1a contains the D-motif that binds to pJNK, we postulate that an increase in pJNK induced by nicotine exposure would interact with mGluR1a. In this study, nicotine exposure increases the binding of pJNK to mGluR1a in glioma C6 cells. In vitro, active JNK1 phosphorylates the CT region of mGluR1a, although it binds to different amino acid sequences, D-motifs arranged in a reverse direction in the mGluR1a-CT1a. Taken together, activation of JNK phosphorylates the CT region of mGluR1a by binding to the D-motif within CT2c, indicating the interaction between pJNK and mGluR1a in astrocytes in response to nicotine.

This interaction may, in turn, potentiate IP3 production, resulting in intracellular Ca2+ mobilization from the internal Ca2+ store, ER, by stimulating IP3 receptors in astrocytes. This prediction can be supported by the present finding that the nicotine-induced increase in IP3 production is decreased by interfering with the pJNK–mGluR1a interaction in glioma C6 cells. Consistently, stimulation of mGluR1a facilitates IP3 production by activating phospholipase C hydrolysis that is coupled to the receptors51.

Expression of GLT-1 in astrocytes, which is responsible for taking up glutamate, is decreased by chronic exposure or self-administration of nicotine in the nucleus accumbens of rats52, 53, 54. Reduced expression of GLT-1 can induce glutamate accumulation in the synaptic cleft of the striatum, leading to the nicotine-induced behavioral sensitization and nicotine-seeking behavior25,39,55, 56, 57. Unlike GLT-1, inhibition of GS induces glutamate accumulation in astrocytes and decreases glutamine synthesis, impairing glutamine export to neurons21,58. A previous study showed that decreased glutamate release is recovered after treatment with glutamine22. These findings suggest that decreased glutamate precursor generated by decreased GS activity in astrocytes leads to dysfunction in glutamatergic neurotransmission in neurons.

GS plays a significant role in glutamate homeostasis through the clearance and recycling of glutamate in astrocytes59. Our results show that mitogen-activated protein kinases can activate GS in astrocytes, as JNK inhibition attenuated the nicotine-induced increase in GS activity. A recent study showed that extracellular signal-regulated kinase1/2 and p38 are involved in the regulation of GS activity in cortical astrocytes after stimulation of CB1 and CB2 cannabinoid receptors60. A study has shown that inhibition of JNK attenuates the endocannabinoid-induced increase in GS activity in primary astrocytes23. In the present study, nicotine potentiates GS, which is decreased by interfering with the pJNK–mGluR1a interaction in glioma C6 cells, primary astrocytes, and the CPu of rats, suggesting that activation of GS is necessary for regulating glutamate clearance by stimulating the pJNK–mGluR1a interaction in astrocytes. Taken together, nicotine may activate GS by stimulating the pJNK–mGluR1a interaction. This interaction is coupled to α7 nAChRs in a Ca2+-dependent manner in astrocytes after nicotine exposure. The present study showed that inhibition of GS in astrocytes of the CPu decreases the repeated nicotine-induced increase in locomotor activity. Although the correlation between GS activity and locomotor behavior was not statistically significant, the observed trend supports a functional association between these two factors (Fig. S7). These findings suggest that the activation of GS plays a pivotal role in upregulating glutamatergic neurotransmission by facilitating glutamate clearance in the synapses of the CPu. Therefore, it is possible to postulate that repeated nicotine upregulates locomotor sensitization by activating GS through the pJNK and mGluR1a interaction that is linked to α7 nAChRs in astrocytes of the CPu.

Unfortunately, we were unable to specifically suppress the expression of JNK, mGluR1a, and GS in astrocytes in both intact brain and dissociated cultured cells. The Tat-mGluR1a-i peptide did not exhibit astrocyte-specific effects in the intact brain. However, the novelty of this study lies in the finding that the GS activity of astrocytes is responsible for developing nicotine dependence, as demonstrated by results obtained from comprehensive experiments at molecular, cellular, and behavioral levels. This innovative finding may help elucidate how nicotine dependence is regulated not only by neurons but also by astrocytes, thereby providing a potential target for the treatment of nicotine dependence.

Current pharmacotherapies for nicotine dependence primarily target nicotinic acetylcholine receptors that are expressed in the dopaminergic system. For instance, varenicline (Chantix) acts as a partial agonist for α4β2 nicotinic receptors, while bupropion inhibits the reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine, thereby mimicking some of nicotine's rewarding effects61,62. There is growing evidence that the glutamatergic system may represent a novel therapeutic target for nicotine dependence. Inhibition of glutamatergic neurotransmission has been shown to attenuate nicotine intake and seeking behaviors63. Furthermore, upregulation of astrocytic GLT-1, a key glutamate transporter, reduces the nicotine-induced conditioned place preference and reinstatement in rodent models64, 65, 66. In line with these findings, nicotine-dependent patients treated with N-acetylcysteine, a compound that enhances GLT-1 function, demonstrate the reduction of cigarette consumption in a randomized controlled trial67.

While GS plays a central role in glutamate homeostasis, its involvement in nicotine dependence has not been extensively studied. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that GS modulates the development of dependence in response to psychostimulants. For example, local administration of fluorocitrate, an astrocyte metabolism inhibitor, into the ventral tegmental area and the NAc decreases nicotine self-administration and relapse behaviors in rats68. Chronic exposure to e-cigarette vapor is associated with increased glutamine levels in the prefrontal cortex and the CPu of mice69. Furthermore, the conditional deletion of GS in oligodendrocytes results in the reduction of glutamate and glutamine levels and attenuates the cocaine-induced locomotor sensitization70. Importantly, GS has also been proposed as a potential biomarker for exposure to psychostimulants. Elevated blood GS activity has been observed in individuals with alcohol use disorder71. Taken together, these findings support the notion that GS may serve not only as a novel biomarker for the dependence on psychostimulants, including nicotine, but also as a promising therapeutic target. The present data may provide direct evidence implicating astrocytic GS activity in the development of nicotine dependence, opening new avenues for glia-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

As depicted in Fig. 7, nicotine binding to α7 nAChRs results in JNK phosphorylation by upregulating Ca2+ cascades. Activated JNK interacts with mGluR1a by phosphorylating the CT region of the receptor after binding to the D-motifs within the CT, leading to GS activation. Activation of GS in astrocytes upregulates glutamine synthesis, which leads to locomotor sensitization by facilitating glutamatergic neurotransmission in neurons of the CPu following nicotine exposure. Therefore, activation of GS linked to the JNK–mGluR1a interaction in astrocytes of the CPu may be necessary for regulating locomotor sensitization after repeated nicotine exposure in rats.
