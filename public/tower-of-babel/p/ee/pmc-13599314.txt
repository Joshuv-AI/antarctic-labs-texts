ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn December 2024 and early January 2025, China reported increased acute respiratory infections with increased detections of seasonal influenza, rhinovirus, RSV and hMPV during the Northern Hemisphere winter. We strengthened hMPV surveillance through the existing pan-India surveillance network for acute respiratory illness (ARI) and severe acute respiratory infection (SARI).MethodologyTo rapidly generate nationwide situational data without overburdening laboratories already performing routine respiratory virus surveillance, each site was asked to test up to five eligible SARI samples per week for hMPV from January to April 2025. Eligible samples were hospitalized SARI cases that were negative for influenza, SARS-CoV-2 and/or RSV, as applicable to age-based testing algorithms, across 70 laboratories.ResultsOf 8,246 enrolled SARI cases, 4,149 eligible samples were tested for hMPV under the capped weekly sampling strategy, and 59 were positive (1.42%; 95% CI: 1.10-1.83). Positivity ranged from 0.5% to 2.9% across age groups and was highest in children aged <2 years. Most patients (63%) were aged <2 years. Positivity was higher among males (1.7% vs 1.1% in females), and was highest in January (2.1%) before declining to 0.5% in April. The most common symptoms were cough (95%), fever (88%), and breathlessness (58%). Outcomes were available for 54/59 patients: 11 required ICU admission, 51 were discharged alive, and 3 died. Among seven patients with comorbidities, all were discharged alive. More than half of the sequenced viruses belonged to clade B1, followed by B2 and A2b2.ConclusionIn this limited sentinel surveillance of severe respiratory infections, hMPV was detected infrequently but was observed predominantly among young children. Background

Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) are a global public health problem, causing significant morbidity and mortality in extremes of age and in immunocompromised individuals [1–3]. Repeated outbreaks of respiratory viral infections adversely impact countries by burdening their health care systems, economy, and development [4]. Year-round surveillance of respiratory infections, is, therefore, crucial to detect early rise in cases, so that appropriate mitigation measures may be timely implemented. Many countries have some acute respiratory infection surveillance in place, especially for the pandemic-prone influenza viruses. However, testing for other common respiratory viruses is still limited in most parts of the world.

From late December 2024, China reported an increase in detections of acute respiratory pathogens, including influenza, rhinovirus, RSV, and hMPV. However, the World Health Organization considered this rise to be within the expected range for the Northern Hemisphere winter season [5]. In India, respiratory virus activity is influenced by the country’s wide geographic and climatic diversity, with influenza peaking during the monsoon in much of the country and during winter in northern temperate regions [6]. In the absence of adequate post-pandemic data on hMPV, close monitoring of hMPV infections in India became important. hMPV was first identified in India in Pune in 2003 among hospitalized children [7], and since then, several reports have emerged from different parts of the country in the pre-COVID-19 period [8–37]. Most of these were singlecentre studies or case reports describing both hospitalized and community-based infections. Nearly half of the studies focused on children under 5 years of age, and a few also documented circulating hMPV genotypes in India. However, there is still no systematic surveillance system in the country for routine monitoring of hMPV infection trends.

Since July 2021, the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) has established an integrated year-round sentinel surveillance of priority respiratory viruses – influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV across 70 sites in India [38].

Amid growing international concern about respiratory virus activity, and given the lack of baseline post-pandemic data on hMPV, we used the existing sentinel surveillance network to assess hMPV trends among patients hospitalized with severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) in India. This manuscript presents the pattern of hMPV infections observed from January to April 2025.

Nasopharyngeal and/or oropharyngeal swabs are collected in Virus Transport Medium (VTM), along with requisite epidemiological and clinical details, and transported to the VRDLs in cold chain. All samples from individuals aged 5–65 years of age are tested for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza A (H1N1 & H3N2) & B (Victoria & Yamagata). Samples from individuals < 5 years of age and >65 years of age are tested for RSV A/B.

From January 2025 onwards, the routine testing algorithm was expanded to include hMPV. To enable rapid, feasible, and uniform implementation across the 70 site network and continuing the laboratories perform the ongoing surveillance for influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV, each site was instructed to test up to five eligible SARI samples per week for hMPV using a validated in-house real-time PCR assay targeting the conserved N/F genes of the virus. Eligible samples included consecutive hospitalized SARI cases that had tested negative for influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and/or RSV according to the age-specific testing algorithm followed at each site. As a result, not all virologically eligible SARI samples were expected to undergo hMPV testing. The gap between the total eligible sample pool and the 4,149 samples actually tested reflects this protocol-defined weekly cap, rather than any unexplained exclusion. Testing was focused on hospitalized individuals to monitor trends in severe hMPV infection and to support health-system preparedness.

Discussion

To place these findings in the broader international context, the World Health Organization reported increased detections of acute respiratory pathogens, including hMPV, in China during late 2024 and early 2025, especially in the northern provinces. However, this rise was considered to be within the expected range for the Northern Hemisphere winter season, with no unusual outbreak pattern identified [5]. In India, respiratory virus seasonality varies considerably across regions, and previous studies have shown that the timing of influenza circulation differs substantially between Indian subregions [6]. Similarly, in the United States, CDC surveillance documented winter circulation of hMPV, with weekly positivity peaking at 6.7% in early January 2025 [39]. Taken together, these observations suggest that the hMPV activity seen in our Indian SARI cohort from January to April 2025 occurred as part of a broader period of seasonal international circulation, rather than as an isolated event. Our study contributes important multicentric post-pandemic clinical and molecular data from India, including the predominance of genotype B1 and the concentration of cases among children aged under 2 years.

Our study provides multicentric post-pandemic data on severe hMPV infection in India. hMPV positivity among the tested hospitalized SARI cases was low (59/4,149; 1.42%; 95% CI: 1.10–1.83), with almost three-fourths of positive cases aged ≤ 5 years. This finding is broadly consistent with previous Indian reports showing generally low hMPV positivity among SARI cases, including children, although most available data are from the pre-pandemic period [20–22, 24, 32]. Two pre-pandemic reports described higher positivity among hospitalized under-five children in Delhi and Jaipur [35–37]. Because children aged under 5 years made up a large proportion of the enrolled SARI cases in our surveillance, these findings should be interpreted to mean that most of the severe hMPV cases detected were in young children, rather than as evidence of greater age-specific severity in this group. Three deaths were recorded, all in children. About 15% of hMPV-positive cases developed hypoxia and 19% required ICU admission, although none required non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation. The relatively high proportion of ICU admissions despite low mortality may partly reflect precautionary admission practices for young children, as well as local tertiary-care protocols across participating sites. Most hMPV-positive SARI cases (88.1%) had no documented comorbidities. We also specifically reviewed neurological manifestations and identified one case of encephalitis with seizures in a 12-month-old child. As the dataset was not powered to support robust genotype-severity comparisons, we have avoided making strain-specific inferences.

hMPV positivity was higher during January and February, and then declined through March and early April over the four-month observation period. Previous studies from India have described varying temporal patterns across different regions [9, 16, 22, 29, 31]. These findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting short-term variation during the study period rather than true seasonality, as a meaningful assessment of seasonality would require surveillance over at least one full year, and ideally across.

Whole genome sequencing of 44 hMPV-positive samples showed predominance of clade B1, followed by B2 and A2b2. Previous reports from India have documented circulation of A2c, B1 and B2 strains, although these studies were site-specific and based on relatively small numbers [9, 15, 16, 33, 36]. We did not perform formal genotype-severity comparisons because of the limited number of sequenced cases and outcome events. The genomic data from this period nonetheless provide a useful baseline for future comparisons with subsequent Indian hMPV sequences.

Although there is no specific antiviral treatment for hMPV, diagnosis of this pathogen in severe respiratory infections remains important because some patients may deteriorate and require escalation of supportive care. Continuous baseline hMPV surveillance, including genomic analysis, would help identify changing transmission patterns and inform preparedness. hMPV is not widely tested in India, and this partly informed our decision to initiate hMPV testing first in severe hospitalized cases that were negative for other commonly tested respiratory viruses. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a sentinel surveillance study restricted to hospitalized SARI cases, and sites tested only up to five eligible samples per week; therefore, the findings are not intended to estimate the full community burden or nationally representative prevalence. Second, the sequential testing algorithm may have underestimated co-infections and favored detection of mono-infections, although one co-infection with influenza B was identified in the subset screened. Third, respiratory support practices, ICU admission thresholds and ancillary investigations such as liver function tests were not standardized across sites; therefore, extra-pulmonary involvement and inter-site severity comparisons could not be assessed comprehensively. Finally, the four-month duration limits inference regarding seasonality and longer-term temporal trends.

Conclusions

This surveillance activity helped provide early situational data on hMPV in India during a period of international concern about respiratory virus activity. India did not observe an hMPV-driven surge in cases during the surveillance period. The rapid adaptation of an existing respiratory virus surveillance network for hMPV testing demonstrates operational preparedness, while also underscoring the need for longer-duration and more comprehensive surveillance to define burden, seasonality and genomic trends.
