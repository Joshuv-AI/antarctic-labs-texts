Skeletal muscle oxygenation and fluid content in upper extremity postthrombotic syndrome: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Laura Avila, Scott Thomas, Athena Mancini, Gina Wong, Brenna Wong, Leonardo R Brandão, Angelika Stavrakoukas, Leigh Ward, Robert F Bentley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about the pathophysiology of upper extremity (UE) postthrombotic syndrome (PTS).ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate the effect of PTS severity on UE muscle oxygen saturation (SmO2) and fluid content during exercise and recovery in adolescents and young adults.MethodsEleven cases with unilateral or bilateral UE-PTS who sustained deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in childhood, and 11 age- and sex-matched controls from the general population were recruited for this case-control study. PTS was measured using CAPTSure (0-100-point score). SmO2 and segmental extracellular fluid content were measured in both UEs using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and bioimpedance spectroscopy (BIS), respectively. Incremental and constant load UE exercise tests were completed using an ergometer while monitoring NIRS and BIS parameters in both UEs. NIRS and BIS changes throughout exercise and recovery were analyzed using mixed models to account for nested data.ResultsMedian age of DVT diagnosis among cases was 5.6 years (quartile [Q]1-Q3, 0.2-16.1 years). At the time of the study, median CAPTSure score in the DVT-affected UE was 39 points (Q1-Q3, 27-55 points); 55% of DVTs were associated with a central venous catheter. Mixed models showed that increasing PTS severity was associated with lower SmO2 during both incremental and constant load exercise and exercise recovery. We found no evidence of fluid accumulation during exercise.ConclusionThe lower SmO2 suggests that UE-PTS severity is associated with muscle microvascular dysfunction and impaired blood flow during and following exercise.Keywords: adolescent, exercise test, near-infrared spectroscopy, venous thrombosis, young adult

Background

Postthrombotic syndrome (PTS) is the most common complication of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in adults and children. PTS is a chronic condition that can affect 25% to 50% of children diagnosed with DVT [1], which can negatively impact their functioning, movement ability, and mobility [2].

While compression garments are the cornerstone of PTS management [3], ∼30% of children report that compression garments are not helpful [4]. A critical first step in developing alternative management strategies to treat pediatric PTS is to better understand its pathophysiology.

We recently identified that adolescent patients with lower extremity (LE) PTS had impaired microvascular function during exercise and exercise recovery compared with age- and sex-matched controls [5]. Abnormal microvascular function may serve as a potential therapeutic target. However, whether this pathophysiological manifestation is also present in upper extremity (UE) PTS is presently unknown. The objective of the present study was to investigate changes in muscle oxygen saturation (SmO2) and extracellular fluid content during and following UE cycling exercise in adolescents and young adults with UE-PTS.

Methods

This case-control study involved 11 adolescents and young adults who sustained UE-DVT during childhood and were diagnosed with UE-PTS according to index for the Clinical Assessment of PTS in children (CAPTSure), and 11 sex- and age-matched controls from the general population. Sex was defined as a biological variable (assigned at birth). The study was approved by the Research Ethics Boards of The Hospital for Sick Children and the University of Toronto according to the terms of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. Study procedures were conducted during a single 2-hour study visit at the research laboratory at the Goldring Center for High Performance Sport, University of Toronto, or The Hospital for Sick Children Research Institute.

Outcomes

The main outcomes of the study were SmO2 and extracellular fluid content measured in both UEs in each participant during and immediately after incremental and constant load UE cycle ergometry tests. During these tests, SmO2 was measured using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), and extracellular fluid content was assessed using bioimpedance spectroscopy (BIS). The principles of NIRS and BIS were detailed previously [5]. In short, NIRS is a noninvasive technique that measures in vivo SmO2 based on light attenuation by the oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin content at specific infrared wavelengths [6]. NIRS was assessed bilaterally by placing 1 sensor (Moxy monitor; Fortiori Design) on the medial deltoid muscle of each UE. SmO2 was measured every 0.5 second throughout the tests. BIS is a noninvasive technique that allows estimating intra and extracellular fluid content based on the opposition of biological tissues to the flow of imperceptible electrical current [5]. Segmental BIS was determined in both UEs according to the principle of equipotentials using SFB7 (ImpediMed Ltd) devices modified to measure resistance and reactance every millisecond for 10 seconds [5]. Impedance was estimated at the 5 kHz frequency, as it reflects extracellular fluid content. Voltage-sensing electrodes were placed on the lateral aspect of the UE immediately below the NIRS sensors, 6 cm apart. Drive electrodes were placed on the ipsilateral intermalleolar and interstyloid line of each UE. Resistance values, measured in ohms, were recorded every 2 minutes during the incremental test and every minute during the constant load test.

Predictors

UE-PTS severity, the main predictor of the study, was measured using CAPTSure, a tool with tested reliability and validity that assesses PTS signs and symptoms [3]. Scores range between 0 and 100 points, with 0 to 10 points indicating no PTS, 11 to 30 points indicating mild PTS, and ≥31 points indicating moderate to severe PTS.

Handedness was determined with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory Short Form [7]. Other collected variables included body mass index, leisure-time moderate to strenuous intensity physical activity level, measured using the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire [8], and UE physical function and symptoms according to the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) instrument [9].

Exercise protocols

Cases and controls first participated in an incremental UE cycling ergometry test, followed by a constant load UE cycling ergometry test, described below. SmO2, extracellular fluid content, heart rate, ventilatory threshold, and rating of perceived exertion were monitored during both tests. Ergometry tests were completed using a PhysioTrainer PRO Electronically Controlled Upper Body Ergometer (HealthCare International Inc). Participants were seated with the ergometer positioned so that the right UE was lifted at 110 degrees from the participant’s torso when holding the hand grip at the highest point. Participants cycled starting at a minimal power output of 30 W (males) or 20 W (females); power output was increased by 20 W (males) or 10 W (females) every 2 minutes until any of the following stopping criteria was met: ventilatory threshold was reached; heart rate reached 75% of percent predicted maximum for age; participants could not maintain a crank rate of 50 to 60 revolutions min-1; or participants asked to stop. Exercise was followed by a 2- to 3-minute recovery phase with the UE resting by the participant’s sides. The ventilatory threshold, a noninvasive measure of exercise capacity [10], was identified using the Talk Test, a valid and reliable method [11]. Heart rate was measured during the test using a Polar heart rate monitor (Polar H10) to determine the percentage of the age-predicted maximal heart rate according to the Tanaka method [12]. Rating of perceived exertion was determined using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion scale, which ranges from 6 (no exertion) to 20 (maximum effort) [13].

After a 20-minute rest, participants completed a constant load UE cycling ergometry test consisting of a 1-minute warm-up period during which the power output was ramped up to 50% of the maximum reached at the ventilatory threshold. Participants then cycled at this constant power output for 5 minutes. Exercise was followed by a 2- to 3-minute recovery phase.

Statistical analysis

Measures of central tendency and dispersion or percentages/ratios were used to describe continuous and categorical variables, as appropriate. Handedness, body mass index, Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire scores, and QuickDASH scores were compared between cases and controls using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Exercise capacity markers (age-predicted maximum heart rate percentage, rating of perceived exertion, and power output) at the ventilatory threshold during the incremental test were compared between cases and controls using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Changes in the SmO2 values obtained from each UE throughout active exercise and during the first 10 seconds of recovery (reoxygenation slope, which is considered a marker of microvascular responsiveness [14]) were analyzed using linear mixed models to account for the bilateral UE assessment of each patient (ie, repeated measures). Covariates of the models included PTS severity (0 for controls, in line with the definition of PTS [15]) and handedness. The models included a random intercept for each UE (right/left) of each participant. Models with and without a random slope for time were compared, and the final models were selected using the Bayesian information criterion. Similar linear mixed models were fit to analyze changes in extracellular fluid content during exercise and recovery. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Core Team 2024). Alpha level was set at 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Participant characteristics are shown in Table 1. Median age of DVT diagnosis among cases was 5.6 years (quartile [Q]1-Q3, 0.2-16.1 years). Median time from DVT to study participation was 12 years (Q1-Q3, 8-16 years). DVTs were central venous catheter-related in 55% of cases (6/11). Nine cases had unilateral DVT (9/11, 82%), and 6 had right UE-DVT (6/11, 55%). The last duplex ultrasound, performed at a median of 20 months (Q1-Q3, 6-62 months) from the index DVT, showed complete DVT resolution in 5 of 11 cases (45%) and partial resolution in 6 of 11 cases (55%), with only residual irregular veins or small foci of thrombus in 5 of 6 of them [16,17]. Median CAPTSure score at the time of study visit was 39 points (Q1-Q3, 27-55 points); 7 of 11 cases (64%) had moderate to severe PTS (≥31 points).

There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics or exercise capacity markers between cases and controls (Table 1), except for higher QuickDASH scores and slightly higher rating of perceived exertion at the end of constant load among cases. Higher functional disability scores have been reported in adults with UE-PTS, with mean DASH score of 23 to 58 points [18,19]. Since QuickDASH scores tend to be slightly higher than those of DASH scores [20], the data suggest that long-term disability in adults with DVT might be more severe than that of patients who sustain a DVT in childhood. The deleterious effect of age at the time of DVT could potentially be explained by the multiple cellular and molecular processes associated with aging [21,22], which can result in reduced venous compliance and distensibility [21,23], and increased vein wall remodeling, thickening, and stiffening, all of which may predispose to PTS.

We observed a significant decrease in SmO2 with increasing UE-PTS severity (ie, increasing CAPTSure scores) during both exercise tests, adjusted for time and handedness (Table 2), and a significant decrease in SmO2 during the first 10 seconds of recovery in both tests, adjusted for handedness. The reduction in SmO2 with increasing UE-PTS severity during exercise (0.22 and 0.15 reduction for every point increase in CAPTSure score for the incremental and constant load test, respectively) suggests an attenuated exercise hyperemic response, while the blunted rise in SmO2 immediately postexercise (0.26 and 0.21 lower for every point increase in CAPTSure score for the incremental and constant load test, respectively) suggests the presence of a microvascular impairment. Therefore, increasing UE-PTS severity may impair the ability to perfuse active tissues, thus contributing to the observed impairment in functioning [2]. Results are in line with those of our previous study of adolescents with LE-PTS, which also showed worsening of microvascular function with increasing PTS severity [5].

The finding of microvascular impairment with increasing PTS severity in both LE and UE must be highlighted, in view of the vastly different local pressure environment in these territories. Due to the hydrostatic effects of gravity and position, the local perfusion pressure in the UE (positioned slightly above the atrium level) was reduced, whereas the pressure in the LE (with the participant sitting with a slight forward lean [5]) was enhanced. A rise in local pressure in the UE would be further attenuated by the absence of significant residual venous obstruction in all patients. The lower hydrostatic pressure in the UE may explain why we found no evidence of fluid accumulation (tissue resistance) with increasing PTS severity (Table 2), which suggests that intravascular pressures did not rise to such an extent to significantly increase the local extracellular fluid content.

Whereas fluid accumulation and edema have been classically thought to explain many of the symptoms of venous disease [24], it has now been proposed that microcirculatory changes may be central to symptom occurrence and likely the first evidence of chronic venous disease [25]. The fact that we found microcirculatory impairment without evidence of fluid accumulation in UE- and LE-PTS lends support to the importance of the microvascular changes that explain the clinical manifestations of PTS. Further, it is possible that while the lymphatic system can successfully maintain tissue fluid balance in younger patients, the age-related changes in the structure and function of the lymphatic system in adults [26,27] may affect tissue fluid balance, resulting in excess fluid accumulation. This could explain why teenagers report that their PTS symptoms occur at any time of the day (36%) or midday (26%) [28], whereas adults are more likely to experience venous symptoms at the end of the day [29].

Venous hypertension is a key factor in the pathophysiology of chronic venous disease, and its multiple deleterious effects on the microcirculation have been extensively described at the skin level [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. There is some evidence, however, indicating that venous hypertension may lead to ultrastructural changes in the muscle and intramuscular veins [34,35], increased precapillary resistance, capillary endothelial cell damage, and decreased distensibility [36]. These changes may further compromise the ability of the vascular systems to perfuse active muscles in the affected extremity.

Similar to aging, environmental stressors and detrimental lifestyle habits aggravate microvascular and lymphatic dysfunction [37]. Therefore, young patients with PTS are at risk of exacerbating venous disease progression secondary to these mechanisms, which emphasizes the relevance of counseling and education regarding lifestyle habits as they grow older.

There are limitations to the present study. Our sample size is likely underpowered to detect smaller changes in the main outcomes, and therefore, results should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept for future studies.

In conclusion, we describe findings compatible with microcirculation dysfunction and impaired blood flow in adolescents and young adults with UE-PTS, highlighting the importance of follow-up and counseling of affected patients, and the relevance of research on the management and prevention of PTS to support patients throughout their lifespan.
