Comprehensive analysis of dehydrin genes reveals ZmDHN3 contributes to drought resistance in maize (Zea Mays L.)
Authors: Yongfeng Xie, Yuyi Ma, Xin Sun, Hao Liu, Zhiquan Qiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDehydrin (DHN) proteins, belong to subfamily members of late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins, are widely recognized as key determinants in plant abiotic stress tolerance.ResultsIn this study, we identified eleven DHN genes in Zea mays and systematically analyzed their evolutionary relationships, structural features, cis-acting elements, expression patterns, protein interaction relation, and function validation in drought resistance. All ZmDHN proteins contained K-segment, and were classified into three subgroups, i.e., KnS-, SKn-, and YnSKn-type. Promoter analysis results showed abundant stress-responsive cis-elements were identified in ZmDHN promoter regions, especially MBS and ABRE elements. Consistently, the most ZmDHNs were induced by cold, heat, salt, and drought stresses, except ZmDHN7 to ZmDHN11. Protein interaction and transcriptome data analysis suggested that ZmDHN1 might interact with cell division protein, ZmDHN3 interacted with nucleic acid binding protein, ZmDHN4 interacted with alpha/beta-hydrolases, ZmDHN5 interacted with ATP synthase, ZmDHN6 interacted with glycine-rich RNA-binding protein, ZmDHN8 and ZmDHN9 interacted with late embryogenesis abundant protein Lea14-A under drought stress. Functional validation results demonstrated that ZmDHN3 was located in the cytoplasm, and overexpression of ZmDHN3 in maize enhanced drought tolerance, with higher relative water content and lower relative electrolyte leakage compared with wild-type maize plants.ConclusionsThis study increases our understanding of DHN proteins, demonstrates that ZmDHN3 improves drought tolerance in maize, and provides candidate genes for further molecular breeding to improve maize drought stress tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07223-0.Keywords: Dehydrin protein, Maize, Drought stress, ZmDHN3

Introduction

In the long-term evolutionary process, plants have developed intricate regulatory mechanisms to sense, respond to, avoid, or defend against environmental stress, including drought, salinity, cold and heat stresses. Among them, late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins perform crucial functions in protecting the integrity of biomolecules via preventing protein aggregation during adverse environmental stress [1].

Dehydrins (DHNs), subfamily members of LEA family, are one of the most important functional proteins in plant response to abiotic stress [2]. DHNs usually include commonly conserved K-, Y- and S-segments, the amphipathic K-segment (EKKGIME/DKIKEKLPG) is universally present in all DHN members, forming α-helix to inhibit the aggregation of phospholipid liposomes and proteins under adverse conditions [3, 4]. The Y-segment (V/TDE/QYGNP) is located at the N-terminal region of DHNs. The S-segment, a stretch of serine, contains potential phosphorylation site for kinases [5]. According to the combination of conserved K-, Y- or S-segments, DHNs can be classified into five subgroups, including YnKn-, YnSKn-, KnS-, SKn- and Kn-type dehydrins [6].

DHN was initially identified in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) cotyledons [7], and then found the accumulation during late embryo development stages or in vegetative tissues subjected to abiotic stress/ abscisic acid (ABA) treatment in Arabidopsis thaliana [3], rice (Oryza sativa) [8], barley (Hordeum vulgare) [9], wheat (Triticum aestivum) [10], and maize (Zea mays) [11], etc. DHNs have been reported as protecting agents that maintain cellular integrity under abiotic stress by regulating protein folding, and stabilizing enzymes, membrane or DNA [4, 12–14]. Barley DHN5 protein protects lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and β-glucosidase against stress-induced damage in vitro, and the K-segments play essential roles to prevent the aggregation of LDH and β-glucosidase [15]. In Medicago truncatula, dehydrin MTCAS31 contributes to the drought-induced autophagic degradation of MTPIP2;7, thereby reducing root water conductivity to enhance drought resistance [16]. Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) dehydrin CaDHN3 confers salt and drought resistance by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [17]. Overexpression of CaDHN5 in A. thaliana enhanced resistance to osmotic and salt stresses [18].

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the most widely cultivated and the highest-yielding cereal crops in the world. In order to improve the tolerance to abiotic stress of maize, the study of dehydrin is very important. Although DHN gene family has been investigated in maize [19, 20], a systematic genome-wide analysis of DHN family remains underexplored. In the present study, we identified eleven DHN genes on a genomic scale, and systematically analyzed the physicochemical properties, gene distribution on chromosomes, gene duplication, gene structures, cis-acting element composition on the promoters, gene expression profiles in different tissues and abiotic stress conditions, and interacting proteins of ZmDHNs. Overexpression of ZmDHN3 improved the resistance to drought stress in maize. These findings provide significant insights into the investigation of DHN genes and identify ZmDHN3 as a promising candidate gene for molecular breeding to enhance drought tolerance in maize.

Results

Identification of dehydrin genes in maize

A total of eleven dehydrin genes were identified in maize genome using hmmsearch with dehydrin domain (Pfam ID: PF00257) (Table 1). According to their chromosomal location, these dehydrin genes were named as ZmDHN1 to ZmDHN11. In addition, the CDS of ZmDHN7 was re-annotated by using Fgenesh software in maize genomic DNA database (http://www.softberry.com), and the original and re-annotated CDS sequence of ZmDHN7 were showed in Fig. S1. The identified ZmDHN genes encoded proteins varying from 100 (ZmDHN1) to 326 (ZmDHN11) amino acid residues in length, 11.31 (ZmDHN1) to 31.77 (ZmDHN10) kDa in putative molecular weight, 5.51 (ZmDHN3) to 8.85 (ZmDHN10) in theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and − 2.16 (ZmDHN4) to -0.74 (ZmDHN10) in calculated grand average of hydrophilic index (GRAVY), suggesting that ZmDHN proteins were highly hydrophilic proteins (GRAVY < 0).

Characteristics of dehydrin family genes in maize

Chromosomal localization, collinearity, and ka/ks analysis of ZmDHNs

The chromosomal localization of ZmDHNs were visualized based on the maize genome annotation information from Phytozome database (Fig. 1A; Table 1). These eleven ZmDHN genes were distributed on seven chromosomes, i.e., ZmDHN1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 11 were located on chromosome 1, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, and 9, respectively. ZmDHN7, 8, 9, and 10 were all located on chromosome 8, which undergone tandem duplicate events (Fig. 1).

Chromosomal localizations and collinearity analysis of ZmDHN genes. (A) Chromosomal localizations of ZmDHN genes. (B) Red lines indicate the syntenic ZmDHN gene pairs. The red triangle indicates tandem replication

The collinearity analysis of ZmDHN genes were performed in intraspecies and interspecies, with the comparison to (A) thaliana, (B) distachyon (Brachypodium distachyon), H. vulgare, O. sativa, and T. aestivum (Figs. 1B and 2 and Table S2). A total of six paralogous gene pairs were identified in maize genome, including ZmDHN1-ZmDHN4, ZmDHN3-ZmDHN5, ZmDHN7-ZmDHN11, ZmDHN8-ZmDHN11, ZmDHN9-ZmDHN11, and ZmDHN10-ZmDHN11. All Ka/Ks values of paralogous gene pairs were less than 1, suggesting these ZmDHN genes undergone positive/ purifying selection to keep the function of the dehydrin gene family after duplication events (Table S3).

Collinearity analysis of DHN genes from Z. mays, B. distachyon (A), H. vulgare (B), O. sativa (C), and T. aestivum (D). Red lines indicate orthologous gene pairs

The interspecies collinearity analysis showed 5, 6, 5, and 15 orthologous gene pairs were detected between ZmDHNs with other monocots, i.e., B. distachyon. H. vulgare, O. sativa, and T. aestivum genome, respectively (Fig. 2 and Table S2). Notably, no orthologous gene pairs were detected in A. thaliana (dicot). These results suggested the DHN diversification occurred after differentiation of the monocot and dicot.

Phylogenetic relationship, gene structure and conserved motif analysis of ZmDHNs

To elucidate the evolutionary relationship of dehydrin genes, a phylogenetic tree was constructed with dehydrin protein sequences from (A) thaliana, (B) distachyon, H. vulgar, O. sativa, and Z. mays (Fig. 3 and Table S4). The result suggested these 52 dehydrin genes were classified into five subgroups, including YnKn-, YnSKn-, KnS-, SKn- and Kn-type (Fig. 3). Specifically, ZmDHNs were only divided to KnS-, SKn- and YnSKn-type subgroups, and no ZmDHNs were found in YnKn- and Kn-type subgroups (Fig. 3). Among these, ZmDHN1 and 4 was KnS-type dehydrin, ZmDHN3 and 5 belonged to SKn-type dehydrin, and ZmDHN2, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11 were classified into YnSKn-type dehydrin.

The neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree of DHN proteins. The phylogenetic tree was constructed with DHN protein from (A) thaliana (At), (B) distachyon (Bd), H. vulgar (Hv), O. sativa (Os), and Z. mays (Zm) with 1000 bootstrap replicates

To clarify the structural characteristic of ZmDHN genes, the exon–intron structure, conserved motif, and secondary structure were analyzed (Fig. 4). The KnS-type subgroup members (ZmDHN1 and 4) lacked introns, and SKn-type subgroup members (ZmDHN3 and 5) included one intron. Among YnSKn-type subgroup members, ZmDHN7 contained two introns, and other six YnSKn-type members (ZmDHN2, 6, 8, 9, 10, and 11) only included one intron (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, five conserved motifs were identified among ZmDHN proteins using the MEME service (Fig. 4B). Motif 1 and 3 were universally presented in all ZmDHNs, which made up K-segment. Motif 4 was unique to YnSKn-type members, which was typical Y-segment. S-segment, part of Motif 2, was detected in KnS-, SKn- and YnSKn-type members. In addition, secondary structures analysis showed the K-segment formed amphiphilic α-helical structures (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrated conserved structural characteristic across ZmDHN subgroups with subgroup-specific variations reflecting functional diversification (Fig. 4).

The cis-acting elements composition in ZmDHN promoters

To characterize the regulatory mechanisms underlying ZmDHN genes expression, the 1.5 kb promoter sequences of ZmDHNs were retrieved from maize genome, and analyzed using PlantCARE database to identify and count the stress- and hormone-related elements (Fig. 5). Ten stress-related elements were identified, i.e., anaerobic induction-element (ARE), drought responsive-element (DRE), anoxic specific induction-element (GC-motif), low-temperature responsive element (LTRE), MYB binding site (MBS), MYC binding site, stress responsive-element (STRE), defense and stress responsive-element (TC-rich repeats), WRKY binding site (W-box), and WRE. Among these elements, MBS involved in drought-inducibility was the most abundant in ZmDHN promoters. Meanwhile, five plant hormone-related elements were also found in the promoter of ZmDHN genes, including ABA-responsive element (ABRE), auxin-responsive element (AuxRE), MeJA-responsive element (CGTCA-motif), gibberellin-responsive element (GARE), and salicylic acid-responsive element (SARE). Among these elements, ABRE were the most common in ZmDHN promoters, which respond to abscisic acid (ABA) and played central roles in drought stress response. These results suggested that ZmDHN genes might play significant roles in the response and defense of drought stress.

Expression patterns of ZmDHNs in different tissues and abiotic stress conditions

To detect the tissue-specific expression of ZmDHN genes in maize, the public RNA-seq data was used to analyze the gene expression levels of ZmDHN genes in different maize tissues (Fig. 6A). The results showed that YnSKn-type ZmDHNs were mainly expressed in pericarp/aleurone at 27 DAP (days after pollination) and embryo at 38 DAP, especially ZmDHN6 was abundantly expressed. ZmDHN1 and ZmDHN3 were expressed in the most tissues. ZmDHN4 was specifically expressed in mature pollen, which might be functionally specialized to regulate pollen development. ZmDHN5 was highly expressed in root-cortex and silks. These results suggested that ZmDHNs were functionally differentiated to perform different roles during the growth and development process of maize.

Dehydrins were commonly induced by abiotic stress [10]. Therefore, the expression profiles of ZmDHN genes under cold, heat, salt, and drought stresses were analyzed through the public transcriptome data (Fig. 6B and C). The results indicated that the expression level of ZmDHN1 was up-regulated under cold and salt stresses in maize seedlings, up-regulated under drought stress at V12 and V16 stage in maize leaves, and down-regulated under heat and drought stress at V14 and R1 stage in maize leaves. ZmDHN2 transcripts were accumulated under salt and drought stresses, and no obviously changed under cold and heat stresses. The expression level of ZmDHN4 was up-regulated under drought stress at V12 and V16 stage, down-regulated under drought stress at V14 and R1 stage, no changed under cold, heat and salt stresses. ZmDHN7, ZmDHN8, ZmDHN9, ZmDHN10 and ZmDHN11 transcripts were no obviously changed after these stress treatments. The expression levels of ZmDHN3, ZmDHN5 and ZmDHN6 were all up-regulated under cold, heat, salt and drought stresses compared to control. These results indicated that ZmDHNs play a functional role in mediating multiple abiotic stress responses.

Protein-protein interaction network of ZmDHNs

In order to investigate the regulatory mechanism of ZmDHNs, the interacting proteins were identified utilizing STRING and IntAct Molecular Interaction database (Fig. 7 and Table S5). The results showed that ZmDHN1 could interact with 22 proteins through Y2H assay, including aldo-keto reductase, ATP binding protein, cell division protein, gamma-tocopherol methyltransferase, hexosyltransferase, mitochondrial Rho GTPase, nucleotide/sugar transporter family protein, peptidase, phosphatidate cytidylyltransferase, protein kinase, E3 ubiquitin transferase, replication protein, secreted protein, secretory carrier-associated membrane protein, transcription factor, vacuolar protein, and zinc finger protein. ZmDHN3 was able to interact with ZmDHN1, ZmDHN4, ZmDHN6, phosphoribosylanthranilate transferase (Y2H), nucleic acid binding protein (Y2H), and beta-glucosidase (Y2H). ZmDHN4 interacted with alpha/beta-hydrolases superfamily protein (Y2H). ZmDHN5 interacted with ATP synthase subunit beta (Y2H). ZmDHN6 interacted with ZmDHN1, single-stranded nucleic acid binding protein, BTB/POZ domain-containing protein (Y2H), glycine-rich RNA-binding protein (Y2H). ZmDHN8 and ZmDHN9 could interact with late embryogenesis abundant protein Lea14-A, respectively (Fig. 7A and Table S5).

Besides, among interacting proteins of ZmDHNs, the most similar expression profiles were observed between ZmDHN1 and cell division protein (Zm00001d042044), ZmDHN3 and nucleic acid binding protein (Zm00001d032597), ZmDHN4 and alpha/beta-hydrolases (Zm00001d043454), ZmDHN5 and ATP synthase (Zm00001d043834), ZmDHN6 and glycine-rich RNA-binding protein (Zm00001d041750), ZmDHN8 and ZmDHN9 with late embryogenesis abundant protein Lea14-A (Zm00001d009382) under drought stress (Fig. 7B). These proteins that have similar expression profiles with ZmDHNs might be involved in response to or resistance to drought stress via interacting with ZmDHNs.

ZmDHN3 confers drought resistance in maize

To validate the function of dehydrin in maize, the overexpression maize lines of ZmDHN3 were generated, which driven by ubiquitin promoter (Fig. 8). Real-time PCR results showed that the expression of ZmDHN3 was initially up-regulated and then down-regulated with the progression of PEG-induced drought stress duration, peaking at 24 h with approximately 52.03-fold compared with the control (Fig. 8A). Compared to the wild-type B73 maize, the expression of ZmDHN3 was significantly higher in ZmDHN3-overexpressing maize lines (OE3 and OE4) (Fig. 8B). Under normal condition, the OE and wild-type lines were no obviously different. After drought treatment for 10 days, the drought tolerance of OE lines was significantly higher than WT lines, and OE lines had lower REC and higher RWC compared to WT lines (Fig. 8C-E). After re-watering for 4 days, OE lines had longer shoot tissues (Fig. 8C). The subcellular localization result showed ZmDHN3 was located in the cytoplasm (Fig. 9). These results suggested that overexpression of ZmDHN3 improved the drought resistance of maize, and increased the biomass of maize under drought stress.

Discussion

DHNs are crucial stress-responsive proteins that play essential roles in plant adaptation to adverse environmental conditions [5]. In order to improve the drought tolerance of maize, we systematically identified and analyzed dehydrin family genes in maize. In this study, a total of eleven DHN genes were identified in maize, which belonged to KnS-, SKn- and YnSKn-type DHNs, whereas YnKn- and Kn-type DHNs were absent (Table 1). The similar subgroup members of DHNs were detected among monocotyledonous species, as B. distachyon and O. sativa also lacked YnKn- and Kn-type DHNs (Table S4). And H. vulgare retained the Kn-type DHNs while lacking YnKn-type DHN members (Table S4). An obvious evolutionary divergence was observed in A. thaliana, which possessed all five subgroup members (Table S4). These results suggested that the differential loss and expansion of DHN gene subgroups might have occurred during the long-term evolutionary processes of plants.

Collinearity analysis revealed six paralogous ZmDHN pairs with Ka/Ks ratios < 1 (Fig. 1B and Table S3), indicating purifying selection that preserved main stress-responsive functions during maize evolution [21]. The absence of orthologs of ZmDHNs in Arabidopsis but their presence in monocots (e.g., B. distachyon, H. vulgare, O. sativa, and T. aestivum) suggested DHN diversification occurred after monocot-dicot divergence, driven by environmental adaptation pressures (Fig. 2).

The exon–intron structure analysis suggested that KnS-type ZmDHN genes lacked introns, while SKn- and YnSKn-type ZmDHNs included one or two introns (Fig. 4A), suggesting structural conservation linked to functional specialization during evolution. The conserved K-segment, forming the amphiphilic α-helical structure, was present in all ZmDHNs (Fig. 4), aligning with its proposed role in stabilizing membranes and preventing protein aggregation under dehydration [1, 5, 12]. The Y-segment, unique to YnSKn-type ZmDHNs (Fig. 4), may enhance nucleic acid binding capacity, as observed in Arabidopsis AtDHN5 [2, 13]. These structural features underscore the functional diversity of DHNs, which may be generated through gene duplication and subsequent neofunctionalization.

Promoter analysis of ZmDHNs identified abundant stress-responsive cis-acting elements, including MBS and ABRE elements, suggesting ZmDHNs may mediate drought and ABA-dependent transcriptional activation (Fig. 5). Similar cis-elements were also identified in the promoter regions of AnDHN and GmDHN9, and these two genes enhanced the drought resistance of plants [22, 23]. These results were consistent with the expression of ZmDHNs under abiotic stress. ZmDHN3, 5, and 6 acted as broad-spectrum abiotic stress regulators, while ZmDHN1, 2, and 4 displayed stress-specific responses, suggesting their involvement in stage-specific stress adaptation and ABA-mediated signaling pathways (Fig. 6). Previous study also reported that DHNs could been induced by ABA and stress treatments, such as CaDHN4 [24], VvDHN1 [25], and TAS14 [26]. Therefore, transgenic maize of the ZmDHN3 were generated, and revealed ZmDHN3 conferred drought resistance in maize (Fig. 8). Protein interaction networks revealed ZmDHN3 was the most likely to interact with nucleic acid binding protein under drought stress, suggesting ZmDHN3 may perform the function in DNA/ RNA stabilization to improve maize drought tolerance (Figs. 7 and 8). The similar results were also found in Vitis riparia that VrDHN1 was able to bind to DNA to protect DNA from ROS-induced damage, driving by lysine-rich that located in the conserved K-segments of dehydrins [13]. In conclusion, our study indicated ZmDHN3 is a key regulator of drought tolerance and provides the foundation for enhancing maize drought tolerance in molecular breeding.

Materials and methods

Identification of dehydrin gene family in maize

The maize genomic data (Z. mays RefGen_V4) was acquired from Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/pz/portal.html). The candidate dehydrin protein sequences were identified by using hmmsearch with dehydrin domain (Pfam ID: PF00257) [27] to search against maize protein database. In addition, dehydrin protein were also identified based on homology with 10 Arabidopsis dehydrin protein sequences (Table S4) via a BLAST search against the maize genomic data (Z. mays RefGen_V4) in Phytozome database. Then, the dehydrin proteins were validated by using InterPro [27] and SMART (Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool) [28] database. The physiological and biochemical parameters of the dehydrin proteins were predicted by using ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Chromosome localization, gene duplication, and collinearity analysis

The chromosome localization information of ZmDHNs were retrieved from Phytozome database and visualized by using TBtools software [29]. The paralogous and orthologous gene pairs of dehydrin genes between Z. mays and five plant species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Brachypodium distachyon, Hordeum vulgare, Oryza sativa, and Triticum aestivum) were identified via TGT (Triticeae-Gene Tribe) [30–35]. PLAZA 5.0 database was used to detect the gene duplication events [36]. The Ka, K, and Ka/Ks values of the paralogous gene pair were calculated by using TBtools software with the Nei-Gojobori (NG) method [29].

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction

Multiple sequence alignment of dehydrin protein sequences was conducted using ClustalW in MEGA 11 software, with the default parameters [37]. The phylogenetic tree was generated with dehydrin protein sequences through the neighbor-joining (NJ) method in MEGA 11 software with 1000 bootstrap replicates [37]. Then, the tree was drawn in Evolview v3 website service [38].

Gene structure and conserved motifs analysis

The exon-intron structures of ZmDHNs were identified by Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0) (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/index.php). The CDS of ZmDHN7 was re-annotated by using Fgenesh software (http://www.softberry.com) in maize genomic DNA database. The MEME program [39] and GOR IV (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?%20page=npsa_gor4.html) online service were used to analyze conserved motifs and secondary structures of ZmDHN, respectively.

The cis-acting elements analysis

The 1.5 kb promoter sequences of ZmDHNs were retrieved from Phytozome database. The cis-acting elements in ZmDHN promoters were identified and counted using PlantCARE website (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Transcriptome data analysis

The publicly available RNA-seq data (GEO No. GSE50191) [40] in qTeller website (http://qteller.maizegdb.org) was used to analyze the gene expression profiles of ZmDHN genes in 21 different maize tissues (cv. B73). The transcriptome data (SRA No. PRJNA244661) [41] in qTeller website (http://qteller.maizegdb.org) was used to detect the gene expression profiles under cold, heat, and salt stresses in maize shoot tissues at seedling stage (cv. B73). The transcriptome data (SRA No. PRJNA291919) [42] in Plant Public RNA-seq database (https://ipf.sustech.edu.cn/pub/plantrna) was used to detect the gene expression profiles under drought stress at vegetative stage 12 (V12), vegetative stage 14 (V14), vegetative stage 16 (V16), and reproductive stage 1 (R1) in maize leaves (cv. B73). Gene expression levels were indicated as TPM values. The heatmap of expression levels was visualized by TBtools [29].

Plant material and growth conditions

The maize cultivar B73 was obtained from our lab in Northwest A&F University. The seeds of uniform size and full grains were surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 1 min, and then washed with ddH2O. The seeds germinated on filter paper wetted with ddH2O in greenhouse condition (16-h light/25°C, 8-h dark/20°C). The seedlings were cultured to three-leaf stage in Hoagland’s nutrient solution (pH 6.0). For PEG-induced drought stress treatment, the three-leaf stage seedlings were treated with Hoagland’s nutrient solution containing 20% PEG6000 The leaves were collected at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48 and 60 h after PEG treatment, respectively. The collected leaves were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

The total RNA was isolated from frozen leaf tissues using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa), and cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using PrimeScript™ II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa). The real-time PCR was performed using gene-specific primers (Table S1) with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa), and ZmActin was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. All reactions were performed on three technical and biological replicates. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−△△CT method [43].

The interacting proteins prediction of ZmDHNs

The interacting proteins of ZmDHNs were predicted using STRING database (https://string-db.org/) with confidence value > 0.7 and IntAct Molecular Interaction database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/home) derived from yeast-two hybrid (Y2H) assay with the confidence value > 0.9 [44].

Subcellular localization assay

The Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 containing pCAMBIA1300-GFP-ZmDHN3 vector was culture at 200 rpm and 28℃ until the OD600 reaches approximately 1.2. The recombinant Agrobacterium was collected and resuspend with infection solution (10 mM MES, 200 µM AS, 10 mM MgCl2) to an OD600 of approximately 1.0. After incubating at 28℃ for 2 h in dark condition, the suspension was injected into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaves at the 6–8 leaves stage. The empty vector pCAMBIA1300-GFP was used as negative control. The injected tobacco plants were incubated in the dark at 25℃ for 16 h, then transferred to alternating cycles of 16-h light/ and 8-h dark at 25℃ for 2–4 days. The GFP fluorescence signal was observed with 30 ms exposure in tobacco epidermal cells by fluorescence microscopy (LSM 900).

Maize transformation and drought tolerance assay

The coding sequence (CDS) of ZmDHN3 was amplified from B73 cDNA and inserted into pCAMBIA1300 vector with transcription driven by ubiquitin promoter. The primers were showed in Table S1. The recombinant vector pCAMBIA1300-GFP-ZmDHN3 was transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105, and then transformed into immature embryos of B73 by using Agrobacterium-mediated maize transformation [45]. Ten three-leaf stage seedlings of transgenic (OE) and wild-type (WT) maize grown in soil were treated with water-deficit treatment for 10 days, followed by re-watering for 4 days. The relative water content (RWC) was calculated as (fresh weight - dry weight) / (rehydrated weight - dry weight) × 100% [46]. The relative electrolyte leakage (REC) was determined as previous study [47].
