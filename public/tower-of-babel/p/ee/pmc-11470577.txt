Gender differences in the association between inter-generational interaction and depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults
Authors: Dan Li, Jinjuan Yang, Liang Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInter-generational interaction is a carrier of Chinese traditional culture, and it can exert important influence on the depressive symptoms on Chinese older adults. The study aims to analyze gender differences in the association between inter-generational interaction and depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults and explore factors contributing to the differences.MethodData from China Family Panel Studies in 2020 were used. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. The latent class analysis was applied to identify patterns of inter-generational interaction of older adults (aged 60 and above). Before the comparison between older man and older woman, we used Coarsened Exact Matching to control confounding factors and improve causal inferences. Multiple linear regression was conducted to explore the association between inter-generational interaction and depression symptoms. Oaxaca-blinder decomposition method was used to analyze the gender difference and the sources.ResultOur study identified three types of inter-generational interaction: detached, nearby but discordant and two-way tight-knit. Analysis indicated that most of older man (54.39%) and older woman (49.78%) were in the type of nearby but discordant. Older man and older woman who in detached type had higher depression scores than other types, and the depression score of two-way tight-knit type accounted for 12.42 and 13.77 respectively. Our findings demonstrated that two-way tight-knit type (-11.89%) significantly decreased the gender differences in the depression symptoms. Other major contributors also included living without spouse (20.56%), primary school and junior middle school (15.95%), higher middle school and above (9.50%) and no illness for two weeks (47.70%).ConclusionOur study highlighted three patterns of inter-generational interaction, and most of older man and older woman were in the pattern of nearby but discordant. In addition, the two-way tight-knit pattern significantly can decrease gender differences in depression symptoms. The contributors should be taken into account in more targeted intervention strategies for narrowing gender differences in the depression symptoms, which could achieve a gender dividend in the era of China's aging population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05345-y.

Background

China has embarked on a journey of population aging, experiencing a scenario where the population ages before achieving economic prosperity [1]. However, the current social pension system in China falls short of meeting the demands of social and economic progress. Deeply rooted in Chinese culture, filial piety has long been regarded as the cornerstone of familial devotion, with families playing an irreplaceable role in supporting the elderly. However, intertwined with China's unique challenges such as low fertility rates, a rapidly aging population, and diverse family structures, inter-generational interaction assumes paramount significance in ensuring population security and fostering social development in the long run. Even in China's aging society, the ethical principles of Buddhism, particularly the virtue of filial piety, continue to hold significant relevance. Moreover, the imbalanced sex ratio at birth in China will eventually manifest in the older population, resulting in a gender disparity among older adults. Against the backdrop of an aging society and future population structures, the traditional family-based support system for the elderly remains relatively fragile, and these inherent contradictions become increasingly prominent in the face of population aging.

In Western countries, inter-generational relationships typically encompass various aspects such as child-rearing, financial support, inheritance, and reciprocal exchanges among different generations. Additionally, at the societal level, inter-generational relationships involve cooperation, interaction, and exchanges between different age groups, including kinship, geographical, and societal connections [2, 3]. For instance, inter-generational projects involving young individuals and older adults from non-adjacent generations have been explored since the 1860s, gradually maturing over time. In contrast, Chinese culture, deeply rooted in tradition and obligations, has established strong social norms regarding inter-generational relationships. As noted by Hsiao-Tung [4], differences in cultural traditions and policy systems between China and Western countries have led to distinct understandings of inter-generational relationships and interactions. In China, the focus of inter-generational dynamics lies in the exchange of economic support and caregiving between parents and children [5]. Feedback and relay, representing different inter-generational patterns, are observed in China and Western countries, respectively. Both Chinese and Western cultural concepts incorporate economic and emotional support, emphasizing a two-way mode to enhance exchange and communication between parents and children. While Western countries place the primary responsibility of upbringing on parents, it does not imply a general estrangement in their inter-generational relationships [6]. Thus, further studies should be conducted to explore inter-generational interactions with Chinese characteristics, thereby enriching research on the differences in inter-generational dynamics between China and other countries.

Several studies [5, 7] have explored similar concepts in China, such as inter-generational support, which emphasizes the reciprocal nature of support from both the giving and receiving sides, encompassing economic, temporal, emotional, and other forms of assistance. Evaluating inter-generational interaction poses inherent challenges due to its multifaceted nature. It involves not only the delineation of relationships between different groups and reference groups but also the recognition that various factors, such as economic and emotional aspects, influence inter-generational dynamics [8]. In contrast to the concept of inter-generational support, the definition of inter-generational interaction places greater emphasis on the role of feedback and interaction [7]. Inter-generational interaction encompasses the dynamic exchange, profound communication, and harmonious collaboration among diverse generations, which stands as a pivotal pillar within society, exerting significant influence on individuals, families, and communities alike [9]. It serves as a conduit for the transference of wisdom, experiential knowledge, and sagacity from venerable forebears to the burgeoning youth. This symbiotic exchange bridges the chasm between generations, fostering empathy, understanding, and social cohesion. Furthermore, inter-generational interaction bears the potential to amplify personal growth and well-being across all age cohorts. The younger generation stands to gain immeasurably from the sagacious guidance and mentorship of their elders, while the seasoned adults find purpose, social interconnectedness, and renewed vigor through their engagements with the younger populace [10]. Moreover, inter-generational interaction may serve as an adjunctive therapy in managing common mental health conditions in residential living older adults, and carries the transformative power to address societal quandaries and instigate positive outcomes, including a sense of belonging, purpose, and positive affective experiences [11].

The existing evidence in China regarding the association between inter-generational interaction and mental health is predominantly positive, albeit limited. For instance, Ku's study revealed that older adults living with their spouse were less likely to rely on paid help compared to those living with adult children [12]. Another study by Li found that the mental health of older adults could significantly improve when they were willing to provide financial support to their children within their means, highlighting the crucial role of emotional support in enhancing the mental well-being of older adults [13]. Furthermore, inter-generational emotional connection proved to be particularly beneficial for older adults in coping with the pain caused by the sudden loss of family members and reducing depressive symptoms [14]. Previous studies have provided valuable insights into inter-generational interaction, encompassing financial and emotional dimensions. To comprehensively understand inter-generational interaction, it is essential to conduct a detailed analysis from multiple perspectives, including economic factors, care-giving, and emotional aspects, while considering the direction, degree of dependence, and frequency of interaction. However, it is worth noting that the limited enumeration or aggregation of these dimensions may restrict assumptions regarding their independence from each other. Therefore, a more comprehensive examination of inter-generational interaction in China is necessary to fully comprehend its multidimensional nature.

Gender stratification has a distinct influence on mental health disparities [15]. Gender differences in China are influenced by complex mechanisms encompassing the social gender system, as well as prevailing cultural and ideological factors accepted by both men and women [16]. Gender differences have evolved from a demographic concern into a long-term socioeconomic issue that permeates the entire life cycle. However, many studies have treated men and women as homogeneous groups when investigating the relationship between inter-generational interaction and mental health among Chinese older adults, overlooking the heterogeneity within each group. Exploring how inter-generational interaction operates from a gender perspective is an important area for further research.

As the nuclear family structure becomes more prevalent in China, the traditional extended family network of "Three or Four Generations Under One Roof" is diminishing, leading to an increase in depressive symptoms among older adults. While several studies have emphasized the significance of inter-generational interaction quality in impacting the well-being of older adults [17, 18], few have addressed the issue of gender differences, which can further perpetuate gender inequality. It is crucial to recognize the pivotal role that gender differences play in inter-generational interaction. For instance, Huang et al. discovered that compared to older men, older women tend to receive more help from their children and provide more assistance to them [19]. The depressive symptom is a severe mental health condition among older adults in China, associated with a high incidence of mortality and morbidity. Many studies have utilized role theory (including role enhancement theory and role conflict theory), social exchange theory, and other frameworks to elucidate the impact of inter-generational interaction on the psychological well-being of older adults [20, 21]. In light of it, as depicted in Fig. 1, inter-generational dynamics between elderly parents and adult offspring are predominantly concentrated in the realms of economic reciprocity, practical care-giving, and emotional bonds. Employing the tenets of role theory and social exchange theory, this study elucidated the intricate interplay of inter-generational interactions and its profound impact on the overall life satisfaction of older adults. Also, numerous studies have explored gender differences in mental health [22, 23]. Inter-generational interaction serves as a carrier of Chinese traditional culture and can significantly influence the manifestation of depressive symptoms in older men and women. However, insufficient attention and research have been dedicated to gender differences in inter-generational interaction and its impact on depressive symptoms, particularly concerning the growing number of older women in China's aging society. Furthermore, the determinants of these gender differences remain largely unexplored. In light of the foregoing, our hypothesis can be expressed as follows: there are significant gender differences in the patterns of inter-generational interaction among Chinese older adults, and these differences are associated with varying levels of depressive symptoms, with different contributing factors of gender differences in depressive symptoms between older women than in older men.Fig. 1Inter-generational interaction diagram

Inter-generational interaction diagram

To test this hypothesis, the study used nationally representative datasets to identify the patterns of inter-generational interaction among Chinese older adults and examine the association between these interaction patterns and depressive symptoms, with a particular focus on understanding the gender differences in this association. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to enable meaningful comparisons and conduct a thorough analysis of gender differences in inter-generational interaction and mental health outcomes. The secondary objective was to dissect the factors that contribute to gender differences in depressive symptoms. Coarsened exact matching (CEM) was used to reduce bias and control selection bias, thereby enhancing comparability of older man and older woman. Our study may be referential for the illumination on gender differences in the inter-generational interaction, and also provided scientific basis for the development of more targeted intervention strategies to narrow gender differences in depressive symptoms and to capitalize on the potential gender dividends in the context of China's aging population.

Methods

Data

Data came from the survey data of the Chinese Family Panel Studies (CFPS) carried out in 2020. CFPS is a nationally representative survey conducted by the Institute of Social Science Survey of Peking University, China. This dataset is a rich repository, encompassing a comprehensive array of demographic, health, economic, and cognitive data, capturing a diverse spectrum of factors pertinent to the study population, and enjoys extensive application across various fields [24–26]. Access to the data is typically made available for inspection one year after the conclusion of data collection. The sampling method for the CFPS is a multi-stage, stratified, and probability-proportional-to-size sampling process, which aims to ensure the representativeness of the sample across different regions and demographic groups in China. Detailed description of the sampling method, quality assurance measures and the questionnaire has been previously published (http://www.isss.pku.edu.cn/cfps/). According to China’s “Elderly Rights Law” says that the starting standard of older adults age is 60 years old. In addition, our study focused Chinese older adults, so the samples aged 60 and above were screened. Despite a highly selected sample size meant that some estimates lacked precision, and affect the representativeness of demographics distribution, excluding missing related answer on depressive symptoms and inter-generational interaction is necessary to ensure the accuracy of the analysis. This exclusion of missing values was conducted on a list-wise basis, ensuring that only complete cases were included in the final sample. The study comprised a total of 4,909 respondents, including 2,544 older men and 2,305 older women. Measurement

In our study, depressive symptoms were assessed by eight-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-8), which exhibited a good internal consistent reliability in older adults, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7888. The scale has been validated in older adults, and it has been used in epidemiological studies of depression [27]. The questions of CESD-8 scale determined whether, in the past week, an older adult felt depressed, had restless sleep, felt everything was an effort, felt lonely, felt happy, felt hopeful about the future, could not get going, felt unusually bothered. The two positive emotions, “felt happy” and “hopeful about the future”, were re-coded to be similar to the negative emotions. CESD-8 score ranged from 0 to 24, with higher score indicative of higher degree of depressive symptoms of older adults. Several studies have investigated the psychometric properties of the CESD-8 in a large sample of Chinese older adults and also found good reliability and validity for CESD-8 in this population. The Cronbach’s alphas of the total scale and sub-scales ranged from 0.82 to 0.88. The test–retest reliability was sufficient and the smallest detectable change was 2.98 and the responsiveness was adequate, with no floor/ceiling effect [28].

Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM)

Causal inference poses challenges when analyzing observational data because member assignment is not randomized. As a result, outcomes reported from these designs may be biased or inefficient if the study groups are not comparable or balanced before analysis [31]. In our study, the distributions of covariates between the treatment groups (older men and older women) were different, making a simple regression model inappropriate for analyzing the association between the treatment and depressive symptoms among older adults. The assumption of no bias in the data would be violated [32, 33]. To address this issue, we employed Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM), a matching method that retains more members and achieves better balance between matched individuals compared to other matching methods [31]. The use of CEM enhanced the balance of covariates between the treatment groups, reducing model dependence for subsequent estimation of treatment effects [34]. By employing CEM, we aimed to mitigate the biases and confounding effects inherent in observational data, thereby strengthening the causal inference in our analysis. Typically, confounding factors were selected based on their potential association with both the treatment variable and the outcome variable. By reviewing relevant studies [31, 34], consulting experts, or considering established theories to confounding factors, our study included age, living arrangement, educational level, Hukou and health insurance for CEM, and matching weights were generated by CEM. To assess the imbalance and heterogeneity between older men and older women, we utilized the L1 metric. This metric measures the overall extent of imbalance, with a value close to zero indicating a high degree of balance and similarity between the two groups. On the other hand, a value of 1 indicates perfect dissimilarity between older men and older women. To implement the Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) method, we used the "cem" command code in Stata 16.0. It's important to note that the "cem" command is not an official Stata command but was developed by Blackwell [35]. This command facilitates the implementation of CEM and allows for the assessment of balance and matching quality between treatment groups.

Latent class analysis

Building upon previous studies [7, 8], our research aimed to empirically explore the patterns of inter-generational interaction and expand the understanding of this concept. In our study, inter-generational interaction encompassed various multidimensional aspects, including economic support, emotional comfort, life care, daily contact, and emotional exchanges. We identified seven explicit variables to capture these dimensions: the relationship with children; parents provide financial assistance to their children; parents do housework or look after their children; children provide financial assistance to their parents; children do housework or take care of food and daily life for their parents; frequency of meetings between both parties. To identify distinct patterns of inter-generational interaction among Chinese older adults, we employed latent class analysis (LCA). LCA utilizes a categorical latent variable, allowing for the identification of different classes without making assumptions of linearity or normal distribution [36–38]. A fundamental assumption of LCA is the local independence, suggesting that observed variables are not highly correlated within each identified subgroup. In our analysis, LCA was performed using the correlation coefficients of the seven explicit indicators, followed by an orthogonal varimax rotation to interpret the emerging factors [7, 39]. This approach enabled us to uncover the underlying patterns and dimensions of inter-generational interaction in our study population. In our analysis, we assumed that for each potential class identified through latent class analysis, the levels of potential variables were independent of each other. This assumption implies that the presence or absence of one particular pattern of inter-generational interaction does not influence the likelihood of other patterns occurring within the same class. Additionally, within each class, the sum of conditional probabilities of each explicit variable equaled 1. This means that the probabilities of observing different levels or categories of each explicit variable within a specific class add up to 1, reflecting the distribution of responses within that particular class.

To identify the best-fit model for our analysis, we followed a common approach. Initially, we calculated a model with only one potential category, assuming complete independence among the seven explicit variables measuring inter-generational interaction. Subsequently, we gradually increased the number of categories and evaluated several model fit indicators to select the best pattern that provided the optimal fit to the data [40]. The process involved stepwise addition of categories until the most suitable solution for the data was obtained. The optimal number of distinct classes was determined by considering five fit measures or indices. These included the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and entropy. These fit measures provided valuable insights into the goodness-of-fit of the models, allowing us to compare different solutions and identify the one that best captured the underlying patterns of inter-generational interaction within our dataset. The AIC is designed to select the model that accurately represents the true data process by balancing goodness of fit, measured by likelihood, and model complexity, measured by the number of parameters. Similarly, the BIC penalizes the number of parameters but with a stricter approach. The BIC originates from a Bayesian perspective, aiming to identify the model with the highest posterior probability given the data. However, in situations where the standard BIC may overly penalize and select overly simplistic models, the aBIC addresses this issue. The aBIC provides a valuable tool for researchers to make informed decisions in model selection, considering the trade-off between goodness of fit and model complexity. It allows researchers to choose models that strike the right balance and effectively capture the underlying data process. Lower values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC value indicated better model fitting. Entropy ranged from zero to one, with values closer to 1 indicating better differentiation. BLRT assessed the relative improvement in fit between models with different classes [38, 40].

Oaxaca-Blinder Decomposition

In order to measure the extent of differences between older man and older woman and understand factors contributing to gender differences, we employed the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method. This method allowed us to decompose the observed differences between the two groups, specifically the "endowment part" and the "discrimination part". The endowment part refers to the differences in the mean values of the independent variables within each group, while the discrimination part captures the contribution of the gender differences in depressive symptoms [41–43]. By utilizing the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method, we can identify and quantify the factors that contribute to the gender differences in depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults. LCA analysis which was conducted using the Mplus software, began by specifying the number of latent classes to be identified. The model then estimates the probabilities of observing each category within each variable for each latent class. Table S1 presented the results of the LCA analysis, showing the fit indices for different numbers of latent classes. Upon reviewing the fit indices, we ultimately selected the three-category model as it had the best balance of fit indices and practical interpretability. Based on the results presented in Table 2, as analyzed using the Stata software, the inter-generational interaction exhibited diverse characteristics, which could be divided into three distinct categories: detached, nearby but discordant, and two-way tight-knit. The detached pattern, the first category, had poor performance across multiple variables: weak relationship with children, little parental financial assistance, minimal involvement in housework or childcare, limited financial assistance from children, infrequent care provision by children, low meeting frequency, and infrequent contact. The nearby but discordant pattern, the second category, had a closer relationship with children compared to the detached pattern. However, it showed relatively low levels of economic assistance, involvement in housekeeping or childcare, meeting frequency, and contact frequency between the two parties. Discussion

Our study incorporated three patterns of inter-generational interaction: detached, nearby but discordant, and two-way tight-knit. Individuals in the detached pattern for both genders exhibited higher CESD-8 scores compared to other types. Significantly, the two-way tight-knit pattern was found to substantially decrease gender differences in depressive symptoms. Our study indicatively identified and analyzed distinct patterns of inter-generational interaction, while also examining the impact of gender on depressive symptoms. Our study offered a detailed and nuanced understanding of how gender influences the relationship between inter-generational interaction and mental health. The use of CEM and Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition methods allowed for a robust analysis that controlled for confounding variables, enhancing the credibility of the study's findings.

The primary findings from this study were that there were gender differences in the association between inter-generational interaction and CESD-8 scores. The difference in CESD-8 scores between older women with the pattern of nearby but discordant (14.82%) and those with the pattern of detached (14.73%) was negligible. In parallel with Wang [37], the inter-generational relay model has gradually emerged and developed, in which there is only nurturing but no back-feeding, that is children do not bear or less bear the obligation of support and care. In addition, the intensifying trend of the core structure of families and the dispersion of family members’ living styles lead to obvious reducing trends for family’s old-age security functions. To a certain degree, the pattern of nearby but discordant showed that Chinese older adults become more independent of their children, which is of progressive significance. In the context of depressive symptoms, there were conflicting views. One viewpoint suggested that providing filial care can improve the overall quality of life for older adults, thereby reducing their tendency towards depression [46]. Financial support from adult children can ensure that basic needs of older adults are met, thus reducing the risk of depression [47]. On the other hand, an opposing perspective argued that excessive inter-generational support may actually lead to depressive symptoms among older adults, causing a decline in self-confidence and fostering negative attitudes towards aging [21, 48]. The bestowal of financial support from offspring may potentially engender an undue reliance, thereby heightening the likelihood of depressive symptoms [49]. An ever-expanding body of literature has undertaken the task of dissecting inter-generational interaction, discerning its diverse styles through various lenses. For instance, when considering the contrast between urban and rural contexts, Guo et al. discovered that a predominant pattern among both urban and rural older adults was that of a tightly woven fabric, characterized by a unidirectional flow of support from children to parents [50]. Moreover, examining the interplay between economic support and inter-generational care, inter-generational interaction can be categorized into four distinct patterns: the symbiotic alliance, the supportive bond, the nurturing embrace, and the fostering haven [51]. Remarkably, our findings align harmoniously with other scholarly investigations which have consistently demonstrated that individuals with robust familial ties tend to experience enhanced mental well-being [52].

Our findings revealed that older men exhibit lower CESD-8 scores than older women, thereby fully reflecting the impact of old age on the gender paradox of depressive symptoms. Extensive research has explored the gender paradox of health [53, 54], widely recognized as the gender health-survival paradox, with women being more susceptible to the effects of stressors than men. The pathways that underlie gender disparities in physical health differ from those influencing mental health, and our study can provide valuable insights into the realm of depressive symptoms among older adults in China. We concurred with Bi [53] that the psychological well-being of older women exhibited a heightened sensitivity to variations in inter-generational support emanating from their progeny. This gender difference may stem from gender roles and gender-oriented segregation. In the context of the swift aging of the Chinese populace, inter-generational interaction has emerged as an essential aspect in creating a secure and comfortable retirement environment for older adults. As per the National Bureau of Statistics, by the end of 2021, men accounted for 51.2% of the population, while women accounted for 48.8%. Notably, the prevalent societal concern regarding gender differences raises significant scientific questions regarding depressive symptoms among older adults in China. In the context of an aging population, the sex ratio among older adults (aged 60 and above) is currently below 100. However, as the cohort affected by the gender imbalance at birth reaches old age, the sex ratio among older adults in China is projected to accelerate and peak at 102.4 in 2088 before gradually declining, remaining above 100 by the end of the century [54]. The elevated sex ratio at birth in China is influenced by the inertia law of population development, and the three-decade-long one-child policy has proven challenging to reverse. As individuals traverse the course of their lives, the imbalances in sex ratio at birth are eventually transferred to old age, resulting in a gender imbalance within the older adult population.

The finding that the two-way tight-knit pattern significantly reduced gender differences in depressive symptoms was particularly noteworthy. The rigorous process of CEM ensured that potential sources of bias are adequately addressed, leading to more accurate and reliable research findings. It suggested that mutual support and positive communication within families can buffer against the negative mental health impacts of aging, especially for women who may be more vulnerable due to sociocultural factors. China's "Elderly Rights Law" clearly outlines the filial obligations, emphasizing that adult children should attend to their aging parents' spiritual needs without neglect or disrespect. Additionally, adult children bear the financial responsibility for their elderly parents. However, despite these legal provisions, many older adults may still harbor feelings of burden on their loved ones. Therefore, it is imperative to focus on fostering a two-way tight-knit pattern and place significant emphasis on the merits of the "filial piety" paradigm within the Chinese context. Enriching the intellectual and cultural pursuits of older adults by establishing dedicated hubs for senior education and lifelong learning. Initiating community-driven events that foster inter-generational interactions, thereby fostering cultural assimilation and fostering a shared sense of identity within society. By means of community-level endeavors, it is imperative to furnish essential venues, amenities, and financial provisions to adequately cater to the social engagement requisites of the elderly populace. Simultaneously, fostering a sense of inter-generational inclusivity among community residents and organizing diverse forms of inter-generational integration activities.

The results of this study indicated that gender differences in depressive symptoms were influenced by multiple factors, including living without a spouse, educational attainment, and not experiencing any illness for two weeks were important contributing factors. Notably, the most significant factor contributing to gender differences was the absence of illness for two weeks, suggesting that our study effectively captured disparities in physical health. The existence of a health deterioration-education gradient among older adults [55, 56] aligned with our findings, indicating that higher education levels exacerbated gender differences in depressive symptoms. This may be attributed, in part, to highly-educated older adults' increased understanding of chronic disease prevention and health promotion, leading to engagement in regular check-ups. However, highly-educated older adults in rural areas may face challenges related to chronic health issues. Our findings can inform policymakers in identifying key determinants that can be targeted through health education programs to reduce gender differences in depressive symptoms. While previous studies have raised important questions regarding the association between living without a spouse and depressive symptoms in older women but not men in China [57], our results did not support the belief that living without a spouse increases gender differences. Therefore, targeted intervention strategies that account for these contributors are essential for narrowing gender differences in depressive symptoms. Creating a policy framework that caters to the specific needs of elderly Chinese women is crucial in order to tap into their full potential.

Our study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional analysis in our study did not allow for the establishment of causal relationships, leading for potential hidden biases. Given that inter-generational interaction is dynamic throughout the entire life cycle, a longer-term follow-up study is necessary for the evolving nature of inter-generational interaction and draw more robust conclusions regarding causal inferences. Secondly, our analysis of inter-generational interaction patterns and their relationships with depressive symptoms was based on the simplified assumption of two generations in a family: parents and children. However, inter-generational interactions encompass a more complex and multi-generational dynamic. Therefore, further exploration is warranted to investigate a more comprehensive pattern that encompasses interactions involving multiple generations. In addition, we can’t include the life expectancy between man and woman, anxiety, negative life events, which should be considered as they widely exist in the society. Nevertheless, for us, this is the most detailed study into the topic to date, allowing for gender differences. Third, many variables associated with depressive symptoms in older adults, for example, social networks, socialization with others, social capitals, neighborhood cohesion and carer’s role, etc., had not been mentioned in the study, caused by data limitations. There may be unknown confounding or crucial mediating factors between depressive symptoms and inter-generation relationships. Nonetheless, our findings offered a research perspective on the inter-generational interaction in gender differences which has rarely noticed.

Conclusion

Our study advanced the understanding of the role of inter-generational interaction and the gender difference in depressive symptoms of older adults in China. Our study identified three distinct patterns of inter-generational interaction: detached, nearby but discordant, and two-way tight-knit. Notably, the two-way tight-knit pattern was found to significantly reduce gender differences in depressive symptoms. Additionally, our study revealed that living without a spouse, educational attainment (ranging from primary school to higher middle school and above), and no illness for two weeks were significant contributors to gender differences in depressive symptoms. These findings have important implications for addressing gender disparities in depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults. They underscore the need for the government to develop gender-sensitive strategies that consider the role of inter-generational interaction when designing effective treatment approaches.
