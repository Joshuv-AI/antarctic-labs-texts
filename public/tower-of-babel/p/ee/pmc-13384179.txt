GLP-1 receptor agonists in systemic lupus erythematosus: a scoping review
Authors: Robin Sia, Ee Lynn Ting, Suet-Wan Choy, Shereen Oon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are increasingly recognised for benefits beyond glycaemic control, including anti-inflammatory, anti-atherogenic and potential immunomodulatory effects. Given the high burden of metabolic dysfunction, accelerated cardiovascular disease and chronic systemic inflammation in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), significant interest has emerged regarding their therapeutic role in this population.MethodsWe conducted a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-guided scoping review of PubMed, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library from database inception to April 2026, including peer-reviewed clinical studies, case series, case reports and relevant research evaluating GLP-1 RAs in SLE while excluding conference abstracts and unpublished material.ResultsFourteen studies were included, comprising narrative reviews, mechanistic analyses, editorials and case reports. Across these studies, GLP-1 RAs demonstrated consistent anti-inflammatory, metabolic and endothelial effects relevant to SLE, with mechanistic data suggesting reductions in cytokine signalling, oxidative stress and macrophage activation. Current evidence specific to SLE is largely derived from small observational cohorts, retrospective analyses and isolated case reports, while much of the supportive anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory data originate from preclinical studies or extrapolated from the broader rheumatic disease populations. GLP-1 RAs appear to have an acceptable safety profile in autoimmune populations, although drug-induced lupus was reported in two case studies.ConclusionCollectively, findings from the included studies suggest that GLP-1 RAs may provide metabolic and anti-inflammatory benefits in SLE with an acceptable safety profile; however, clinical data specific to SLE remain limited and large high-quality prospective clinical trials are required to refine therapeutic indications, optimal patient selection and immunological effects.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disorder characterised by immune-mediated inflammation and tissue injury across a wide range of organs, most commonly involving the mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, haematological and renal systems. Globally, SLE affects an estimated 3.4 million individuals, with a marked female predominance, accounting for approximately 88% of those affected.1 In addition to organ-specific manifestations, patients with SLE experience an increased prevalence of obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and cardiovascular disease, contributing significantly to excess mortality.2 This is particularly important given the diabetogenic effects of glucocorticoid therapy, which may exacerbate metabolic risks.3 In patients with lupus nephritis, steroid-induced diabetes has been associated with more severe renal disease and poorer outcomes, with these risks further amplified in Southeast Asian populations where lupus nephritis is more prevalent and associated with higher mortality.4 5

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are incretin-based therapies widely used in the management of diabetes and obesity. Beyond their metabolic effects, GLP-1 signalling has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory actions, including modulation of macrophage activity, cytokine production and T-cell responses.6 7 These properties have prompted increasing interest in the role of GLP-1 RAs in immune-mediated and rheumatic diseases.

However, concerns remain regarding the safety of use of these therapies in SLE, particularly the potential for disease flares or immune-mediated adverse events.8 9 To date, evidence regarding GLP-1 RA use in SLE remains relatively scarce and their role in this disease undefined. This scoping review aims to systematically map the available literature regarding GLP-1 RAs use in SLE, summarise reported outcomes and identify key knowledge gaps.

Methods

A comprehensive search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library was performed from inception to April 2026 using controlled vocabulary and free-text terms combining (“glucagon-like peptide 1” OR “GLP-1” OR semaglutide OR liraglutide OR dulaglutide OR exenatide OR lixisenatide OR tirzepatide) AND (“lupus*” OR “systemic lupus erythematosus”) (figure 1). Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and GLP-1 RA, was included due to its shared GLP-1 receptor activity and overlapping mechanistic pathways with GLP-1 RAs. Eligible articles included published full-text studies relevant to the review topic, including clinical trials, observational studies, case series, case reports, editorials and review articles that provided clinical or mechanistic insight into the role of GLP-1 RAs in SLE. Excluded were non-English publications, conference abstracts, posters, unpublished manuscripts and duplicate or overlapping publications without additional novel information. Two authors (RS and ELT) independently screened titles, abstracts and full texts, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Data abstraction was undertaken using a prespecified extraction tool developed in Microsoft Excel. Extracted variables included study characteristics, patient demographics, intervention details, outcomes (including renal, metabolic, cardiovascular, safety) and key findings. Results were synthesised descriptively without formal risk of bias assessment, consistent with scoping review methodology. For the purpose of synthesis, evidence was categorised a priori as either ‘direct clinical’ or ‘supportive’ evidence. ‘Direct clinical evidence’ referred to studies evaluating GLP-1 RAs specifically in SLE patient cohorts or assessing SLE-specific outcomes. ‘Supportive evidence’ referred to mechanistic, translational, animal, genetic or non-SLE rheumatic disease studies providing indirect biological plausibility for the role of GLP-1 RAs in SLE.

Results

A total of 14 studies were included. No randomised controlled trials or systematic reviews were identified. The evidence base was heterogeneous across study designs and populations (online supplemental file 1), with the majority of studies being narrative reviews or editorials (n=7), with additional evidence coming from cohort studies (n=3), Mendelian randomisation study (n=1), pragmatic target trials (n=1) and case reports (n=2). Studies originated from North America, Europe, Asia and the Middle East, reflecting global interest in this topic. Across the 14 included studies, the literature demonstrated a rapidly growing interest in the immunometabolic and anti-inflammatory properties of GLP-1 RAs within the broader landscape of autoimmune and rheumatic diseases, including SLE. Although several publications examined GLP-1 RAs in rheumatic diseases, most focused on broader autoimmune or rheumatic disease populations rather than SLE specifically. Direct SLE-specific evidence was limited to one observational study and one Mendelian randomisation study; however, all included publications provided indirect evidence relevant to SLE through mechanistic, preclinical, translational or clinical data from related autoimmune conditions.

Discussion

Principal findings

This scoping review identified 14 publications evaluating GLP-1 RAs in SLE, comprising observational clinical studies, narrative reviews, editorials, mechanistic and preclinical studies, a Mendelian randomisation analysis and case reports. Although direct lupus-specific evidence remains limited and no randomised controlled trials were identified, emerging observational data suggest potential cardiometabolic and renal benefits, while mechanistic studies provide biological plausibility for immunomodulatory effects. Conversely, genetic analyses and isolated case reports raise important concerns regarding disease susceptibility and safety, highlighting substantial remaining knowledge gaps and the need for additional data to resolve the current conflicting evidence.

Emerging clinical evidence in SLE

Lupus-focused analyses have recently emerged from large US-based electronic health record cohorts. Jorge et al conducted a pragmatic target-trial emulation using a multicentre electronic health record database to compare GLP-1 RAs with dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i) among adults with comorbid SLE and T2DM. GLP-1 RA use was associated with clinically meaningful reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events, kidney disease progression, venous thromboembolism and all-cause mortality compared with DPP-4i, with similar directional associations observed in the lupus nephritis subgroup. These findings represent some of the strongest comparative efficacy data currently available supporting potential cardiometabolic and renal benefits of GLP-1 RA therapy in SLE and lupus nephritis.10

Similar findings have been reported when GLP-1 RAs were compared with sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i). In a retrospective cohort study presented as an American College of Rheumatology conference abstract (and therefore not included in our formal analysis as it has not been published in full text), Grzybowski et al identified 1034 patients with lupus nephritis treated with GLP-1 RAs and 1375 treated with SGLT2i. Following propensity score matching, 694 patients remained in each group. Compared with SGLT2i, GLP-1 RA use was associated with reduced chronic kidney disease progression (rate ratio (RR) 1.53, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.98), reduced risk of acute myocardial infarction (RR 2.25, 95% CI 1.29 to 3.90) and decreased all-cause mortality (RR 2.00, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.23), providing further support for potential renal and cardiovascular benefits in lupus nephritis.11

Additional evidence regarding safety and disease activity is derived from smaller cohort analyses. In a retrospective evaluation from a single academic lupus centre, 18 patients with established SLE who received GLP-1 RAs, primarily for obesity or diabetes, were followed for 1–10 months. Treatment was associated with clinically meaningful and sustained reductions in body mass index, with no clear signals of increased disease activity observed; there was only one documented mild-to-moderate disease flare and no patients met additional SLE classification criteria. Of note, although all participants were managed by rheumatologists, no GLP-1 RAs were initiated by rheumatology clinicians. While the reasons for this were not stated, this may reflect limited integration of GLP-1 RAs into routine rheumatology practice, potentially related to prescribing familiarity, access or insurance barriers, cost or uncertainty regarding efficacy in rheumatological disease populations.12

Complementing these findings, a large TriNetX-based retrospective cohort using federated, de-identified electronic health record data from multiple healthcare organisations reported a modest but statistically significant reduction in incident SLE among individuals with diabetes or obesity exposed to GLP-1 RAs. Although suggestive of a potential protective effect, the absolute risk reduction was small and residual confounding cannot be excluded.13

Emerging clinical, mechanistic and supportive evidence for GLP-1 RAs in systemic autoimmune diseases

Population-level observational studies provided emerging real-world outcome data relevant to patients with systemic inflammatory disease. A large cohort study from British Columbia by Karacabeyli et al found that in 10 855 patients with coexisting systemic autoimmune disease (including SLE) and T2DM, GLP-1 RA use was associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes and reduced mortality compared with DPP-4i use. However, outcomes specific to patients with SLE were not reported.14 There are, however, limitations inherent to observational studies such as this and the others included in our review, and the findings may therefore be affected by residual confounding and prescribing bias between treatment groups. Furthermore, the observational cohort study populations largely comprised individuals with diabetes and/or obesity, potentially limiting generalisability to the wider SLE population without these comorbid conditions.

The experience of pain is an important clinical consideration in patients with SLE. Limited evidence suggests that GLP-1 RAs may exert analgesic effects across inflammatory, neuropathic and visceral pain pathways, although current data are largely derived from preclinical and observational studies and demonstrate reduced neuroinflammation and enhanced endogenous analgesia.15 This raises the possibility that GLP-1 RAs may provide symptomatic benefit in SLE, where pain represents a substantial contributor to disease burden and impaired quality of life. However, our scoping review did not identify any studies specifically evaluating the effects of GLP-1 RAs on pain outcomes in patients with SLE.

Several narrative reviews and editorials1621 22 highlighted mechanistic and preclinical data from autoimmune disease settings, including ex vivo human peripheral blood mononuclear cell studies, animal models and other translational research studies. Collectively, these demonstrated anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of GLP-1 receptor signalling, including reduced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, modulation of macrophage polarisation, suppression of T helper (Th)1/Th17 responses, enhancement of regulatory T-cell activity and inhibition of nuclear factor kappa B signalling pathways.23 2429 Within this broader mechanistic evidence base pertinent to autoimmune diseases, lupus-specific data remain limited. SLE-specific evidence included one Mendelian randomisation study suggesting a potential association between genetically proxied GLP-1 receptor signalling and increased SLE susceptibility30 31 . Together, these findings highlight a growing biological rationale for GLP-1 RA use in SLE but a relative paucity of lupus-specific evidence.

Reconciling therapeutic promise with potential risks

While several lines of evidence suggest potential benefits of GLP-1 RAs in SLE, as outlined above, a small number of studies have raised concerns regarding a possible association with lupus susceptibility. One such study was the drug-target Mendelian randomisation study mentioned previously, which evaluated genetically proxied GLP-1 RA pathways and autoimmune outcomes, identifying a potential association with increased SLE risk. In further detail, this was a two-sample Mendelian randomisation study using publicly available genome-wide association study summary statistics employing genetic variants associated with GLP-1 receptor expression as proxies for GLP-1 receptor signalling, to investigate potential associations with several autoimmune diseases, including SLE. This study methodology addresses distinct biological questions; however, Mendelian randomisation signals should be considered hypothesis-generating, reflective of a potential association rather than causation of GLP-1 RA exposure and SLE.32

In contrast to generally reassuring cohort data, rare immune-mediated adverse events have been described. Two case reports have documented lupus manifestations following exposure to semaglutide, including drug-induced lupus erythematosus with multiorgan involvement and biopsy-confirmed discoid lupus erythematosus. In both cases, symptoms resolved after discontinuation of the GLP-1 RAs and initiation of appropriate therapy, suggesting a possible causal relationship.8 9

Importantly, these events appear exceedingly uncommon when considered in the widespread and increasing global use of GLP-1 RAs. Nevertheless, they highlight the potential for immune-mediated cutaneous lupus reactions in susceptible individuals.

Implications for rheumatology practice and research

The increasing use of GLP-1 RAs for obesity and T2DM is highly relevant to SLE, a disease characterised by substantial cardiometabolic and renal comorbidity. Emerging observational data in SLE and lupus nephritis populations suggest potential cardiovascular, renal and metabolic benefits of GLP-1 RAs when used for non-SLE-specific indications, without a consistent signal for increased SLE disease activity within a limited SLE-specific evidence base. Given the scarce available lupus-specific evidence, prospective studies systematically evaluating SLE disease activity, flare rates, patient-reported outcomes and organ-specific outcomes, particularly in lupus nephritis, are needed. Randomised controlled trials would ideally be required to provide more robust evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of GLP-1 RAs in SLE.

Additionally, although a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed, consistent with scoping review methodology, it is noted that the included studies were heterogeneous and predominantly observational in design, which limits internal validity. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that GLP-1 RAs may provide favourable cardiometabolic and renal outcomes in patients with SLE without consistent evidence of disease exacerbation. However, no randomised controlled trials have been conducted in this population, and the existing literature is predominantly observational, with limited lupus-specific data, either specific to SLE populations or regarding SLE-specific outcomes like disease activity measures or flare rates. Rare associations of GLP-1 RA use with SLE onset have been reported, underscoring the need for well-designed prospective and ideally randomised studies to better define long-term safety and disease-specific effects.
