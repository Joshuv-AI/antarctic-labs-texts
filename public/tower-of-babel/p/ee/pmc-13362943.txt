Impact of chronic pain on the families of U.S. adults
Authors: Jennifer S. De La Rosa, Katherine E. Herder, Rita D. Romero, De'Sha S. Wolf, Tally M. Largent-Milnes, Mohab M. Ibrahim, Stacy S. Pigott, Greg T. Chism, Beth E. Meyerson, Julie G. Pilitsis, Benjamin R. Brady, Allison J. Huff, Alicia M. Allen, Maria Manriquez, Kristyn Piñeda, Kyle A. Suhr, Taylor Young, Freya Spielberg, Todd W. Vanderah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Examining prevalence and phenomenological characteristics of chronic pain with high impact on the families of U.S. adults. Situating HICP-family within a tiered cascade of functional impact.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain (CP) presents a significant adaptive challenge47,55,64,75,87,99,102,107 that can profoundly affect family systems.71,72,88,89 Family dysfunction is strongly predictive of pain and pain-attributed disability64,64,71,96; at the same time, CP may also strain previously functional family relationships and communication patterns.3,12 If the challenge presented by CP outmatches the family's resources and capacities, relational disruption is a likely consequence.4,35,39,89,103 Unhelpful pain responses by family members, whether overprotective/disempowering19,21,37 or invalidating/dismissive13,86—may frequently be associated with enmeshment or avoidance,85,95 marital dissatisfaction,58,70,111 and conflict.103

Poor or worsening mental health is regularly observed in families highly affected by CP.8,12,16,17,24,25,29,69 Such families may be marked by cycles of emotional reactivity,16,17 including dysregulated emotional experiences and behavioral expressions of anger, frustration, resentment, anxiety, despair, and hopelessness,1,5,7,32,105 perceived invalidation and stigma,31,114,115 and catastrophic thinking.46 Additional impacts of chronic pain on families may include disrupted functioning in social or family roles,2,106 eg, infantilization of adults, parentification of children,20,33,54,94,97,100,113 pain-attributed distress and disability,15 social isolation, rejection, neglect, or abuse.6,68,93

Despite the high risk of adverse family impacts, clinicians also routinely observe families that appear capable of effective adaptation. Family-level resilience to chronic pain may be linked to the quantity and quality of resources and capacities the family is able to bring to bear, including financial resources, social capital, access to care, individual and collective coping skills, and the flexibility with which expectations are held.28,38,39,50,59,61,89,108,112 Relationally, communications about pain that effectively resolve the tension between acceptance and change may reduce pain intensity, increase function,2,18,42 and enhance emotional security.57,118

The importance of CP's impact on families is clear, yet, to our knowledge, no studies have examined the population-level impact of CP on the families of U.S. adults, nor clarified its phenomenological relationship with CP and high-impact CP—chronic pain that limits individuals' lives most days or every day. This study used 2023 National Health Interview Survey data to advance understanding of the impact of chronic pain on the families of U.S. adults.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and analysis plan

The current study proceeded in 2 phases. In the first phase, we aimed to estimate the prevalence of chronic pain with high impact on families of U.S. adults, and to clarify the relationship of high-impact chronic pain (HICP)-Family with 2 well-established epidemiological constructs: chronic pain (CP) and HICP. Results generated in the first phase prompted a second phase of analysis, in which a set-theoretic approach, “cascade analysis” was adopted. Cascade analysis is an established approach in health services research40,44,45,49,120; typical applications include characterization of systems-level “flows” between inflection points to identify unmet needs and identify opportunities to improve system performance. Accordingly, in the second phase, we estimated the proportion of U.S. adults with CP and concomitant HICP, and the proportion of U.S. adults with HICP with concomitant HICP-Family. To characterize disparities in HICP-Family, we examined key sociodemographic characteristics among U.S. adults with HICP, comparing those who did and did not report concomitant HICP-Family.

Because the research literature closely links family impact of CP with psychological functioning,24,25,36,116,117,119 we estimated the prevalence of clinically significant anxiety/depression symptoms across the chronic pain functional impact cascade: among U.S. adults with CP, HICP, and HICP with concomitant HICP-Family. Subsequently, we reversed the contingency, estimating the prevalence of CP, HICP, and HICP with concomitant HICP-Family, among those who do and do not have clinically significant anxiety/depression symptoms.

2.2. Data source

We use data from the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), a nationally representative, publicly available, deidentified data set suitable for calculating prevalence statistics about chronic pain in the noninstitutionalized U.S. adult population. In 2023, there were 29,522 sample adult interviews; the sample adult response rate was 47.0%, with a household response rate of 48.8% (n = 30,670).83 Detailed information on the survey methodology has been published by the National Center for Health Statistics.83,84 The NHIS has been identified as the best data source for population-level surveillance of chronic pain.27

2.3. Measures

Chronic pain (CP) was measured using the survey item “In the past 3 months, how often did you have pain? Would you say never, some days, most days, or every day?” Those who answered “Most days” or “Every day” were considered to have chronic pain, whereas those endorsing “Never” or “Some days” were considered not to have chronic pain. This is consistent with the International Association for the Study of Pain's definition of chronic pain as implemented in the International Classification of Disease110,122 and is the standard operationalization used by the National Center for Health Statistics.74,122 High-impact chronic pain was measured using the survey item “Over the past 3 months, how often did your pain limit your life or work activities? Would you say never, some days, most days, or every day?” Those with chronic pain and who answered “Most days” or “Every Day” to this item were considered to have HICP; this is the standard operationalization used by the National Center for Health Statistics.74,122 Chronic pain with high impact on families (HICP-Family) was operationalized using the survey item “Over the past 3 months, how often did YOUR pain affect your family and significant others? Would you say never, some days, most days, or every day?” For analysis of national prevalence, we considered those who answered “Most days” or “Every day” to this item to have chronic pain with high family impact (HICP-Family).

Sex response options include “Male,” “Female,” or “Not ascertained.” The NHIS does not collect data on gender. Age in years was categorized to facilitate interpretation: 18 to 24, 25 to 34, 35 to 44, 45 to 54, 55 to 64, 65 to 74, 75 to 84, and 85+. Race/ethnicity response options include “Hispanic,” “Non-Hispanic White Only,” “(Non-Hispanic) Black/African American only,” “(Non-Hispanic) Asian only,” “(Non-Hispanic) AIAN (American Indian or Alaska Native) only,” “(Non-Hispanic) AIAN (American Indian or Alaska Native) and any other group,” and “Other single and multiple races.” Educational Attainment was aggregated into “No high school diploma or GED,” “High school diploma/GED,” “Some college,” “Bachelor's Degree or higher.” Employment status: respondents who worked for pay last week, who performed seasonal or contract work during the previous 12 months, or who were working at a job or business, but not for pay, were considered employed. Those who were not employed or on a temporary leave or doing contract work were then asked the reason for not working: response options include “Unemployed, laid off, looking for work,” “Retired,” “Unable to do work for health reasons/disabled,” “Taking care of house or family,” “Going to school,” “Working at a family-owned job or business not for pay,” or “Other.” To reduce complexity, the last 3 categories were aggregated into “Other.” The National Health Interview Survey records family income as a percentage of the Federal Poverty Line, using the weighted average thresholds published annually by the U.S. Census Bureau. We categorized family income to facilitate interpretation: “<100% FPL,” “100% FPL to <200% FPL,” “200 FPL to <400 FPL,” and “≥400% of FPL.” Family structure was measured using mutually exclusive and exhaustive combinations of marital/partnership status and presence/absence of children. Respondents reporting one adult and no children in the household were considered to Reside Alone.

Health-attributed functional limitations were measured across 3 social/occupational domains: limitations in work “Are you limited in the kind OR amount of work you can do because of a physical, mental or emotional problem?” with response options “Yes,” “No.” Limitations in activities of daily living was measured using the survey item: Because of a physical, mental, or emotional condition, do you have difficulty doing errands alone such as visiting a doctor's office or shopping?, with response options of “No Difficulty,” “Some difficulty,” “A lot of difficulty,” “Cannot do at all.” Limitations in social participation was measured using the survey item: Because of a physical, mental, or emotional condition, do you have difficulty participating in social activities such as visiting friends, attending clubs and meetings, or going to parties?, with response options of “No Difficulty,” “Some difficulty,” “A lot of difficulty,” “Cannot do at all.” Limitations in activities of daily had response options of “No Difficulty,” “Some difficulty,” “A lot of difficulty,” “Cannot do at all.”

Clinically significant depression symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2),62 a validated brief scale used to screen for probable depression. Respondents who scored 3 or higher on the PHQ-2 were considered to have depressive symptoms.63 Clinically significant anxiety symptoms were measured using the General Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2),56 a validated brief scale used to screen for probable anxiety. Respondents who scored 3 or higher on the GAD-2 were considered to have anxiety symptoms.56 Survey respondents were coded as having anxiety and/or depression symptoms (A/D) if they scored greater than or equal to 3 on the PHQ-2 the GAD-2, or both.

2.4. Missing data

Responses recorded as “Refused,” “Don't Know,” or “Not Ascertained” in responses to questions used for the above measures were considered missing data. Missing data for questions used to operationalize chronic pain CP, HICP, HICP-Family, and A/D symptoms included a total of 1101 observations, representing 3.7% of overall data. Complete case analysis was used given the small amount of missing data (<4%), the robust sample size, and the consequent expectation of minimal precision and bias loss67 in the context of prevalence estimation with observational data.80 As noted by Lee et al., when fewer than 5% of records have missing values, the method of handling missing data has negligible practical impact.66 Considering the characteristics of the data under study, deviations from the missing at random (MAR) assumption are unlikely to affect the validity of the estimates.73

2.5. Analysis details

Population prevalence and population mean estimates presented in all tables were calculated using SAS statistical software version 9.4 accounting for the stratification, clustering, and weighting procedures of the complex NHIS survey design. The R package eulerr and its associated shiny app were used to generate the proportional Venn Diagram.65,66

3. Results

Chronic pain with high family impact (HICP-Family) is experienced by an estimated 4.4% of the U.S. adult population—approximately 11.0 million U.S. adults experience chronic pain affecting their families most days or every day. Among U.S. adults with chronic pain, 18.2% experience HICP-Family.

In the U.S. adult general population, we observed that CP, HICP, and HICP-Family may be parsimoniously characterized as nested sets; only 2% of those with chronic pain would not conform to this characterization (Fig. 1).

This preliminary insight motivated our adoption of a (set-theoretic) approach to understanding the family impact of chronic pain in U.S. adults. Two proportions were assessed (1) the proportion of HICP among those with CP and (2) the proportion of HICP-Family among those with HICP. Among adults with CP, the prevalence of HICP was 34.9%. Among U.S. adults with HICP, the prevalence of HICP-Family was 46.3%. In other words, 34.9% of adults with CP were concomitantly included in the HICP set, whereas 46.3% of people with HICP were concomitantly included in the HICP-Family set (Table 1).

We examined sociodemographic characteristics of those with HICP, comparing those with and without concomitant HICP-Family. Among U.S. adults with HICP, those with concomitant HICP-Family differed significantly by age, family structure, and household composition (Table 2). The HICP-Family group displayed disproportionately high prevalence of middle-aged individuals (34–64 years). Regarding family structure, never-married individuals with children, never-married individuals without children, and married/partnered individuals with children were significantly more prevalent among those with HICP-Family, whereas never-married individuals without children were significantly less prevalent. Those living alone were significantly less prevalent in the HICP-Family group, whereas those residing with others were significantly more prevalent.

Employment status differed significantly in the HICP-Family group; status of disabled/unable to work for health reasons was substantially more prevalent, whereas being employed, retired, or looking for work was substantially less prevalent. Functional limitations in activities of daily living and social participation were significantly more prevalent and more severe in the HICP-Family group. Hispanic and (non-Hispanic) African American or Black individuals were more prevalent in the HICP-Family group, whereas non-Hispanic Whites were less prevalent, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P < 0.074). Differences between those who did and did not report concomitant HICP-Family were not observed by sex, educational attainment, or family income.

The prevalence of anxiety/depression symptoms differed statistically and substantively from end-to-end across the functional impact cascade: 12.0% among all U.S. adults, 24.4% among U.S. adults with CP, 37.1% among U.S. adults with HICP, and 46.1% among U.S. adults reporting HICP and HICP-Family (Table 3). Among those with HICP but without concomitant HICP-Family, the prevalence of anxiety/depression symptoms was 29.3%.

4. Discussion

The National Center for Health Statistics reported that among U.S. adults in 2023, the prevalence of CP was 24.3% and the prevalence of HICP was 8.5%.74 Using the same dataset, we estimated the prevalence of chronic pain with high impact on families of U.S. adults. An estimated 4.4% of the U.S. adult population, approximately 11 million U.S. adults, report that their chronic pain affects their families most days or every day (HICP-Family). U.S. adults with HICP-Family make up 18.2% of U.S. adults with CP.

Chronic pain, HICP, and HICP-Family were observed to be nested sets, which motivated our set-theoretic approach to subsequent analyses. Cascade analysis is the study of inflection points analyzed at the level of systems. In health services research, cascade analysis identifies key inflection points where improvement could provide meaningful population benefit.44,45,49,120 We observed that HICP-Family was almost exclusively reported by those with HICP, at the same time, the presence of HICP is not deterministic. Among U.S. adults with HICP, fewer than half endorsed concomitant HICP-Family. HICP can, therefore, be viewed as a necessary but not sufficient condition to produce HICP-Family.

Chronic pain is generally benchmarked using the proportion of U.S. adults experiencing one or more chronic pain states, eg, the National Center for Health Statistics reports that 24.3% of U.S. adults had chronic pain in 2023.74 We suggest there could be value in considering 2 additional proportions assessing functional impacts: (1) the proportion of HICP among those with CP and (2) the proportion of HICP-Family among those with HICP. To our knowledge, the current study is the first to operationalize and report these statistics: in 2023, the proportion of HICP among U.S. adults with CP was 35%, whereas the proportion of HICP-Family among U.S. adults with HICP was 46%. We suggest that meaningful consideration of chronic pain's individual and family-level impacts could provide a more robust picture of chronic pain's public health impacts and support identification of opportunities to mitigate them.

Sociodemographic determinants of health are well-established predictors of both CP and HICP; however, within the HICP group, comparing those with/without HICP-Family, no significant differences were observed by sex, educational attainment, or family income. In the HICP-Family group, the age distribution was disproportionately concentrated in middle-age, a life-course phase accompanied by relatively strict social expectations around provision of care and contribution of economic resources to one's family. Those with HICP-Family more frequently resided with others rather than alone, and more likely to be part of family structures with caregiving interdependencies—including individuals who are married/partnered, individuals who have children, and individuals for whom both are true.

Disrupted workforce participation because of health/disability was significantly more prevalent in HICP-Family, whereas being employed or having retired was significantly less prevalent. Functional limitations in activities of daily living and social participation were likewise significantly more prevalent and more severe in the HICP-Family group. Psychological distress was increasingly prevalent at each subsequent transition in the functional impact cascade; among those with HICP-Family, nearly half (46.1%) displayed clinically significant symptoms of anxiety or depression. Finally, although racial/ethnic differences did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.074), both Hispanic and Black or African American individuals were more prevalent in the HICP-Family group, whereas non-Hispanic Whites were less prevalent. We regard this result as suggestive given well-documented differences in cultural expectations around family caregiving.9,11,22,53,98,104

Taken together, this study's findings suggest that the impact of chronic pain on the families of U.S. adults operates via 2 interrelated aspects of global functioning: (1) reduced capacity of the person with pain to care for and provide for themselves and (2) reduced capacity of the person with pain to care for and provide for family members, such as children, older adults, and family members with disabilities.

5. Limitations

Several limitations are noted. First, persons with no permanent address, persons living on military bases, U.S. expatriates, and individuals residing in long-term care institutions, and correctional facilities are not represented in the NHIS. Second, the 2023 NHIS adult dataset included only the brief forms of the screeners for depression and anxiety; although these have been shown to be viable alternatives to the GAD7 and PHQ-8,52 it is still possible that using the brief form could in some way influence the measurement of anxiety/depression. Third, the NHIS measures the impact of the respondent's pain on the family as perceived by the survey respondent; however, not all individuals may easily recognize the impact of their pain on their families, which could lead to undercounting the true prevalence. Ideally, information on family impact would be collected from family members themselves. Last, contextual information such as how many family members are affected by the survey respondent's pain, whether they see themselves as caregivers, care receivers, or some other relationship, whether they currently cohabitate with the respondent, and whether respondents may have experienced CP-associated family estrangement, are unavailable in the NHIS public use files, limiting exploration of cohabitation vs kinship and preventing a more nuanced analysis of the subjective meaning respondents attributed to the survey item concerning family impact of pain.

6. Conclusion

Chronic pain has been memorably characterized as a “sensitive barometer of population health”121 that is disproportionately prevalent in under-resourced and stigmatized individuals, families, and communities.10,60,91,92,109,121 It is perhaps less widely recognized that functional impacts of chronic pain also exist along a social, cultural, relational, and psychological gradient.23,26,30,34,43,51,79,81,82,90 Despite calls for family-oriented approaches to the treatment of chronic pain,88 relatively little research has examined chronic pain's family impact. This gap is particularly striking outside of pediatric contexts. HICP-Family is seldom measured, yet it could play an underrecognized role in maintaining feedback loops of anxiety/depression, functional limitations, and chronic pain. Development of validated, person-centered, culturally resonant approaches to assess CP's impact on families is needed.41

Further research is needed to precisely identify the mutable factors that predict concomitant HICP-Family among individuals living with HICP, and to develop effective prevention and intervention strategies. Involving families and people with lived experience in services may have high potential value compared with typical service models that consider patients primarily as individuals.14,48,75–78,101 Such involvement could be particularly important in communities known to experience heavy burden of chronic pain with high impact on individuals and families. Adaptive recovery of meaningful, self-directed lives is critical to the sustained functional resilience of individuals and their families. Mitigation of HICP-Family represents an opportunity to improve quality of life for millions of children, adults, and older people who are directly and indirectly affected by high-impact chronic pain.
