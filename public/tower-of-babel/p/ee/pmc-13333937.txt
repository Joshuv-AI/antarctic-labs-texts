Reprogramming lipid metabolism for cognitive restoration in Alzheimer's via PLA2G4E
Authors: Sara Badesso, María Espelosín, Cristina Alonso, Alejandro Montilla, Maria Collantes, Mar Cuadrado‐Tejedor, Ana García‐Osta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONGrowing evidence implicates dysregulated brain lipid metabolism in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, influencing membrane integrity, neuroinflammation, and amyloid beta and tau pathology, thereby representing a promising therapeutic target. However, therapeutic strategies targeting lipid pathways remain largely unexplored.METHODSThe therapeutic potential of PLA2G4E, previously identified in our earlier work, was validated in the APPNL‐G‐F AD mouse model using a translational gene‐delivery approach with a blood–brain barrier–penetrant adeno‐associated vector (AAV) (AAVP31) to achieve widespread brain expression. Brain lipidomics was performed to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying treatment effects.RESULTSPLA2G4E expression rescued memory deficits, reduced tau phosphorylation, and improved brain glucose metabolism and cognitive performance in AD models and aged wild‐type mice. These effects were accompanied by partial normalization of disease‐associated lipid metabolic alterations.DISCUSSIONThese findings support PLA2G4E as a promising therapeutic target in AD and provide mechanistic evidence linking modulation of lipid metabolic pathways to synaptic and cognitive rescue.

INTRODUCTION

Emerging evidence indicates that lipid metabolism and composition play central roles in Alzheimer´s disease (AD), influencing amyloid and tau pathology, membrane integrity, and microglial function, establishing lipids as key modulators of neuronal health during aging. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 In particular, cholesterol and sphingolipid‐rich membrane microdomains (lipid rafts) regulate amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing by modulating secretase activity and localization, thereby influencing amyloid beta (Aβ) production. 4 , 7 In parallel, bioactive lipids such as ceramides and phosphoinositides drive synaptic dysfunction, tau phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation, while age‐related alterations in lipid turnover and transport exacerbate neuronal vulnerability. 8 , 9 , 10 These effects also impact neuron–glia interactions, where impaired lipid handling by astrocytes and microglia further exacerbate synaptic dysfunction and inflammatory. 11

Human genetics supports lipid dysregulation as a key mechanism in AD. Variants in lipid‐related genes, including apolipoprotein E (APOE ε4), ABCA7, and the phospholipase PLCγ2, are associated with AD risk and neuroprotection, suggesting that modulation of lipid pathways can influence the balance between neurodegeneration and resilience. 12 , 13 , 14 Accordingly, comparative analyses reveal significant differences in lipid composition among the brains of healthy controls, AD patients, and cognitively resilient individuals, 15 , 16 highlighting the key role of lipid metabolism in AD pathogenesis and potential prevention strategies.

In line with these observations, we identified the phospholipase PLA2G4E as a potential modulator of resilience mechanisms and a promising target for therapeutic intervention in AD. 17 PLA2G4E is a poorly characterized cytosolic phospholipase that is expressed specifically in neurons within the brain but is also detected in peripheral tissues such as the skin and testes. 18 Its primary function is the production of N‐acyl phosphatidylethanolamines (NAPEs), the precursors to N‐acylethanolamines (NAEs), endocannabinoids with neuroprotective and antioxidant properties. 18 Accordingly, in the skin, PLA2G4E functions as a counterregulatory enzyme that limits psoriatic inflammation by promoting the production of anti‐inflammatory NAEs; its upregulation in a model of psoriasis parallels increased NAE levels, whereas its deletion exacerbates disease severity and reduces these lipids. 19 A similar protective role has also been suggested in the context of ischemia, where PLA2G4E contributes to the formation of NAPEs in an ex vivo murine model of brain ischemia. 20 Consistent with a protective role of PLA2G4E, we demonstrated that adeno‐associated vector (AAV9)‐mediated overexpression of this enzyme in hippocampal neurons reversed dendritic spine loss and ameliorated cognitive deficits in aged APP/PS1 mice. 17 These effects are probably mechanistically linked to the role of PLA2G4E in dendritic development and neuronal maturation, 21 although the precise mechanisms underlying this link remain to be elucidated.

In this study, we extended our previous findings by delivering murine and human PLA2G4E via AAV9 in the APP knock‐in model (APPNL‐F‐G), 22 confirming therapeutic effects. Due to limited distribution with this approach, we tested a blood–brain barrier (BBB)‐penetrant serotype (AAV9P31) to achieve broader and more clinically relevant brain delivery. 23 Given the critical role of lipids in AD progression, we hypothesized that PLA2G4E would modulate lipid signaling to promote dendritic spine remodeling and synaptic function; this was investigated through UHPLC‐MS analysis of cortical tissues to elucidate underlying mechanisms and assess its therapeutic potential.

RESULTS

Overexpression of hippocampal PLA2G4E restores memory in APPNL‐F‐G AD mouse model

To assess the effect of PLA2G4E on cognition, 7‐ to 8‐month‐old APPNL‐G‐F mice were divided into three groups, a control group received AAV9‐null, while two experimental groups received AAV9‐expressing HA‐tagged human PLA2G4E (APP‐hPLA) or murine Pla2g4e (APP‐mPLA), under the synapsin I promoter. Age‐matched naïve wild‐type (WT) mice were included as a reference control group for behavioral test comparisons. Two months after injection, we applied the fear conditioning (FC) and the Novel Object Recognition (NOR) tests. Consistent with previous reports, 24 APPNL‐G‐F mice receiving the AAV‐null treatment (APP) exhibited a significant deficit in fear memory relative to WT controls (p < 0.01) (Figure 1A). In contrast, APPNL‐G‐F mice overexpressing either human or murine PLA2G4E performed comparably to WT mice (p = 0.4308 and p = 0.7970), indicating that viral‐mediated PLA2G4E expression partially rescued the fear memory deficit. A significant improvement versus control APPNL‐G‐F mice was observed only in the mPLA2G4E group (p < 0.05; Figure 1A). In the NOR test, APPNL‐F‐G control animals showed significantly poorer performance compared to WT mice (p < 0.05). Notably, the impaired cognitive deficit was significantly ameliorated by overexpression of either human or murine PLA2G4E (p < 0.01) (Figure 1B).

Improved memory performance following hippocampal PLA2G4E administration assessed by two behavioral paradigms. (A) Fear conditioning test: The percentage of time in the freezing state, used as a memory parameter, is significantly decreased in APPNL‐F‐G (APP) mice compared to age‐matched wild‐type mice (WT) but recovered upon the injection of 1.1 × 1011 vg/kg of AAV9 expressing either human (APP‐hPLA) or murine (APP‐mPLA) PLA2G4E in each hippocampus. (B) Discrimination index evaluated with the Novel Object Recognition (NOR) test. NOR test revealed that APPNL‐F‐G mice had a significantly impaired recognition memory that was rescued upon the injection of AAV9 expressing either human or murine PLA2G4E in each hippocampus. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparisons post hoc test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01. APP = APPNL‐F‐G; hPLA = human PLA2G4E; mPLA = murine PLA2G4E.

Hippocampal PLA2G4E overexpression regulates glutamatergic markers and tau pathology in APPNL‐F‐G mice

PLA2G4E overexpression was verified by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in mouse brain sections using PLA2G4E and HA‐tag antibodies, showing overlapping, robust staining in the hippocampus, especially CA1 and CA2 synaptic regions (Figure S1). Consistently, Western blot analysis of hippocampal tissue confirmed a robust increase in PLA2G4E protein levels in APP‐hPLA mice compared with controls (Suppl. Figure 1A).

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed studies on lipid metabolism dysregulation in AD, focusing on evidence linking altered phospholipid homeostasis to synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and Aβ and tau pathology. The literature highlights lipid pathways as contributors to disease mechanisms and supports lipid‐targeted interventions as a promising but still underexplored therapeutic strategy in vivo. Interpretation: Our findings demonstrate that brain‐wide expression of PLA2G4E rescues cognitive deficits, improves brain glucose metabolism, and reduces tau phosphorylation while partially normalizing disease‐associated lipid alterations in Alzheimer's models and aged mice. These data suggest that PLA2G4E‐driven remodeling of disease‐relevant lipid species may underlie the observed recovery of memory and normalization of key histopathological and metabolic features, providing a mechanistic link between lipid modulation and functional rescue. Future directions: Future work should assess long‐term efficacy and safety, define cell‐type‐specific mechanisms and lipid mediators, and test whether pharmacological modulation of PLA2G4E can reproduce therapeutic effects to enable clinical translation.

Although quantification of amyloid burden was not performed in this cohort, qualitative inspection of APP immunostaining did not reveal apparent differences between AAV9‐null and AAV9‐hPLA2G4E–treated mice (Figure 2A). This observation is consistent with our previous findings in the APP/PS1 model, 17 suggesting that the behavioral improvements induced by PLA2G4E occur independently of major changes in Aβ pathology. Phosphorylated tau (p‐tau; Ser202/396) levels were also assessed by Western blot using the PHF1 antibody; however, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups (Figure 2B).

Next, we examined pre‐ and postsynaptic components of glutamatergic signaling, a pathway strongly affected in AD, revealing a significant decrease in the levels of VGLUT1 in APPNL‐F‐G mice overexpressing hPLA2G4E compared to APPNL‐F‐G control mice (Figure 2C). This was accompanied by increased expression of GluN2B and GluA1 (Figure 2C), with no changes in GluA2/3 and GluN2A. These results link the beneficial effects of PLA2G4E to modulation of glutamatergic signaling alterations characteristic of AD.

Systemic administration of AAVP31‐PLA2G4E enhances memory in aged WT and in APPNL‐F‐G mice

Using the BBB‐penetrant AAV9P31 capsid, the construct was packaged to generate AAV9P31‐hPLA2G4E. 23 , 25 A prior systemic dose‐escalation analysis revealed a dose‐dependent increase in PLA2G4E expression, with minimal signal at 2.4 × 101 1 vg/kg, intermediate levels at 4 × 101 2 vg/kg, and robust, widespread expression at 2.4 × 101 3 vg/kg (Figure S2). Based on these results and consistent with commonly used doses in preclinical studies using this capsid for brain targeting, a dose in the 101 3 vg/kg range was selected for subsequent experiments. 25 , 26 We then compared PLA2G4E expression in the brains of adult C57BL/6 mice brain following intrahippocampal injection of AAV9‐hPLA2G4E (1.1 × 1011 vg/kg) versus retro‐orbital administration of AAV9P31‐PLA2G4E (1.1 × 1013 vg/kg). Thirty days after injection, HA and PLA2G4E IHC showed hippocampus‐restricted expression after intrahippocampal AAV9‐hPLA2G4E delivery, whereas systemic AAV9P31‐hPLA2G4E resulted in widespread brain distribution (Figure S3A). Notably, systemic AAV9P31‐PLA2G4E delivery led to expression in cortical layers III and V and hippocampus, resembling endogenous PLA2G4E patterns (Figure S3B). These results were confirmed by increased cortical PLA2G4E protein levels in treated mice by Western blot (Figure S3C).

Next, to assess the effect of PLA2G4E in the context of aging, aged WT animals (18 to 20 months old) received a retro‐orbital injection of AAV9P31‐PLA2G4E or AAV9P31‐null (1.1 × 1013 vg/kg). Two months after injection, memory function was evaluated using the FC test, revealing a significant improvement in treated animals (Figure 3A). To assess functional outcomes, 18F Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography [18F]‐FDG‐PET was used to measure brain glucose uptake, a marker of neuronal activity and metabolic function (Figure 3B). No significant differences were detected between AAVP31‐PLA2G4E and the null condition at the regional level. However, whole‐brain analysis, defined as the sum of regional measurements per animal, revealed a significant increase in glucose metabolism following treatment (Figure 3C).

Accordingly, Western blot of the prefrontal cortex demonstrated a significant increase of synaptic markers, including GluA1 and GluA2/3, in AAV9P31‐PLA2G4E‐treated mice, compared to control, while VGLUT1 levels were significantly decreased (Figure 3D). Tau pathology was assessed using the PHF1 antibody, revealing a significant reduction in p‐tau levels in PLA2G4E‐treated mice compared with controls (p < 0.05), in line with the observed improvements in memory and overall brain function (Figure 3E).

To further validate the beneficial effects of PLA2G4E in the context of AD, 4‐ to 5‐month‐old APPNL‐F‐G mice were administered AAV9P31‐PLA2G4E or AAV9P31‐null (1.1 × 1013 vg/kg) via retro‐orbital injection. Fear memory was assessed at 2 and 6 months after injection. At 2 months, improved fear memory was observed selectively in male AD mice, whereas females showed no significant changes, likely reflecting preserved cognitive performance at this age (Figure 4A). In contrast, at 6 months after injection, when mice had reached 12 to 14 months of age, PLA2G4E fully reversed memory deficits, indicating a robust and long‐lasting protective effect (Figure 4B). At this later time point, only female mice were analyzed, as male mice were allocated to lipidomic studies (Figures 5 and 6). Amyloid pathology was assessed by measuring Aβ42 levels in cortical tissue (prefrontal and parieto‐temporal regions), with no differences between groups in either soluble or insoluble fractions (Figure 4c–D). Consistently, 6E10 IHC revealed no changes in amyloid pathology (Figure 4E). Western blot analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in cortical p‐tau levels in AAV9P31‐PLA2G4E‐treated mice compared to controls, corroborating previous findings and supporting a potential therapeutic effect of PLA2G4E in AD (Figure 4F).

PLA2G4E expression normalizes dysregulated lipid metabolism in AD mice

Distinct lipid classes have been demonstrated to play crucial roles in brain development, 27 aging process, 28 and AD, 29 , 30 , 31 processes in which we recently demonstrated that PLA2G4E was also involved. 17 , 21 To determine whether PLA2G4E‐induced changes in lipid metabolism contributed to its therapeutic effects, we performed cortical lipidomic analysis using two UHPLC‐MS platforms for broad lipid coverage, identifying 372 lipid features.

We first compared cortical lipid composition between WT and APP NL‐G‐F mice at two stages: adulthood (6 to 8 months, males only) and old age (12 to 14 months), allowing assessment of both aging‐ and disease‐related effects. Figure 5A shows heatmaps illustrating the impact of age and genotype on the cortical lipidome, analyzed by two‐way ANOVA, with p values for each factor summarized on the right side of the heatmap. As shown, few metabolites were significantly associated with aging or with the interaction between factors, whereas stronger associations were observed with the disease model. Representative metabolites primarily affected by each factor are shown in Figure 5B.

Independently of the genotype, aging was significantly associated with metabolite‐dependent changes in diverse glycerophospholipids (Figure 5B). It was also associated with increased levels of monohexosylceramides (HexCer), lysophosphatidylglycerols (LPG), and sulfatides (SLF), as well as with decreases in some individual metabolites, such as the essential amino acid phenylalanine and palmitoleic acid. Moreover, important trends were only observed in the APP animals, like an important increment in cholesterol esters (CE) (Figure 5A).

Next, the analysis of pathology‐associated lipidomic changes, 12S‐Hydroxy‐5Z, 8E, 10E‐heptadecatrienoic acid (12(S)‐HHTrE), was significantly higher in the disease model. This oxidized fatty acid is a subproduct of arachidonic acid metabolism. Several additional lipid classes were also increased in association with pathology, including phospholipids, monohexosylceramides (HexCer), SLF, sphingomyelins (SM), and, most notably, ceramides (Cer). The increase in Cer was largely specific to the disease model, highlighting their potential relevance in AD pathogenesis. A representative example of this increase is depicted in the boxplot in Figure 5B. Indeed, this effect agrees with studies reporting increases in plasma and brain ceramides linked to AD progression. 1 , 32 , 33 Conversely, APP pathology was associated with a significant reduction in several non‐esterified fatty acids, including arachidonic acid. In addition, most NAEs showed a non‐significant decreasing trend in both adult and aged mice in the APP model.

Aging‐ and disease‐related effects were also assessed using multivariate analysis. Principal component analysis of the 12 samples is shown in Figure 5C. Consistent with the lipid differences observed, APP samples clustered separately from WT animals. The greatest lipid dysregulation was observed in aged mice with the most severe pathology.

Finally, we included a group of adult APPNL‐G‐F mice treated with AAV9P31‐hPLA2G4E to assess the effects of this treatment on cortical lipid composition. Interestingly, PLA2G4E overexpression opposed the effects of the AD model regarding NAEs, increasing the levels of most of them (Figure 6A). Specific changes corresponding to palmitoylethanolamide (PEA; NAE(16:0)) and n‐oleoyl ethanolamide (OEA; NAE(18:1)), two NAEs directly derived from PLA2G4E–dependent pathways, 20 are shown in the plots. In both cases the decreasing trend observed in APP mice relative to WT was opposed by treatment (Figure 6A).

As mentioned above, the levels of several Cer were also significantly increased in APP cortical samples compared to the WT ones (Figure 6B). Notably, AAV‐mediated overexpression of PLA2G4E effectively reversed the majority of Cer‐level alterations associated with the disease. Consequently, no significant differences were observed between AAV‐hPLA and WT mice for these lipid species, indicating a successful restoration of Cer homeostasis (Figure 6B). Similarly, the treatment also reversed the increments in the pro‐inflammatory, oxidized lipid mediators observed in the AD model (11‐HETE and 12(S)‐HHTrE) (Figure 6C). Of note, the original increases observed in the AD mice could be attributed to the elevated ceramides levels, which may contribute to lipid peroxidation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Last, similar outcomes were observed regarding sphingolipids such as SMs, HexCer, of SLF, whose levels were elevated in APP mice and decreased following treatment (Figure 6D,E). These latter effects were less pronounced and statistically less significant compared to the ceramide ones.

Collectively, our data suggest that PLA2G4E overexpression contributes to the normalization of lipid homeostasis in the cerebral cortex of APP mice, counteracting disease‐associated lipidomic alterations.

DISCUSSION

Building on our previous identification of PLA2G4E as a novel therapeutic target for AD and its essential role in memory and synaptic function, 17 this study further validates its therapeutic efficacy in an independent AD model. Furthermore, we demonstrated that systemic administration of a BBB‐penetrant AAV expressing PLA2G4E achieved widespread brain transduction and conferred functional benefits, highlighting the translational potential of PLA2G4E gene therapy beyond region‐specific interventions. Comprehensive lipidomic profiling revealed that PLA2G4E overexpression not only restored cortical disease‐associated alterations in sphingolipids, ceramides, and oxidized fatty acids but also normalized levels of certain neuroprotective NAEs, highlighting a previously unrecognized link between lipid homeostasis and cognitive rescue.

Together, these results extend our previous observations, demonstrating that PLA2G4E exerts multi‐level protective effects in AD through coordinated regulation of synaptic function, tau pathology, and lipid metabolism. Using both hippocampal and systemic gene delivery, PLA2G4E overexpression significantly improved cognitive deficits in APPNL‐F‐G mice, with benefits occurring independently of amyloid pathology, highlighting that amyloid alone does not explain cognitive decline in AD. Instead, PLA2G4E expression is associated with reduced tau phosphorylation and concomitant improvements in synaptic and metabolic parameters, which is particularly relevant given that tau pathology correlates more closely with synaptic loss and cognitive impairment than amyloid accumulation. 34 Considering that synaptic dysfunction, tau pathology, and metabolic alterations are increasingly recognized as key drivers of disease progression, 35 , 36 , 37 PLA2G4E‐based therapy may represent a disease‐modifying strategy for AD.

A key translational aspect of this study is the successful systemic delivery of PLA2G4E using the BBB‐penetrant AAV9P31 vector, which achieved widespread brain expression closely mirroring the endogenous distribution of PLA2G4E. Although formal toxicological evaluation was not performed, no overt adverse effects were observed, and treated animals showed improved behavioral and synaptic outcomes, suggesting good tolerability at the tested dose. Notably, despite systemic administration of the viral vector, the synapsin promoter restricts transgene expression to neurons, potentially reducing off‐target toxicity. 38 , 39 Further long‐term safety studies will be required for translation.

While it has certain limitations, the use of recently developed BBB‐penetrant viral capsids makes this strategy particularly promising for disorders such as AD, which involve widespread and regionally distributed brain pathology. 40 Systemic PLA2G4E delivery not only rescued memory deficits in aged APP mice but also improved cognitive performance and cerebral glucose metabolism in aged WT animals. Given that brain hypometabolism is a hallmark of both aging and AD and precedes neurodegeneration, 41 , 42 these findings suggest that PLA2G4E enhances neuronal metabolic competence at a systemic level, potentially improving the brain's ability to sustain synaptic activity and cognitive function across vulnerable neural networks.

At the synaptic level, PLA2G4E overexpression induced a selective remodeling of glutamatergic neurotransmission. The reduction in presynaptic VGLUT1 levels, together with the increased expression of GluA1 and GluN2B subunits observed in aged WT and APP‐treated mice, is consistent with a rebalancing of excitatory signaling rather than a generalized increase in synaptic activity. This pattern is consistent with the attenuation of excitotoxic stress, a prominent feature of advanced AD, while preserving postsynaptic mechanisms essential for synaptic plasticity and memory formation. 37 The specificity of these effects supports a regulatory role for PLA2G4E in maintaining synaptic efficiency.

To gain mechanistic insight, and considering that our therapeutic target is a phospholipase, lipidomic analyses were undertaken to explore disease‐associated metabolic alterations potentially susceptible to therapeutic modulation. These analyses revealed a marked dysregulation of lipid metabolism in the cortex of APP mice, particularly in aged animals with advanced pathology. Alterations affected multiple lipid classes, including NAEs, Cer, sphingomyelins, SLF, monohexosyl‐ceramides, lysophospholipids, and oxidized fatty acids – lipid species previously implicated in neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and synaptic failure in AD. 3 , 30 , 31 This lipid dysregulation may compromise the structural integrity of central nervous system (CNS) membranes, affecting neurotransmitter signaling and neuronal function. Notably, similar lipid alterations, including increased Cer and other pro‐inflammatory lipid species, were previously reported in this model and in human AD brain samples, where they accumulated in an age‐ and pathology‐dependent manner around Aβ plaques, supporting their pathological relevance. 33 Consistent with our findings, comprehensive lipidomic analyses in the 5xFAD mouse model have revealed a significant depletion of polyunsaturated NAEs, including NAE 20:5 and NAE 22:6, across serum, hippocampus, and cortex, highlighting a broader disruption of NAE homeostasis in AD pathology. 33

Strikingly, AAV‐mediated PLA2G4E overexpression reversed the majority of these disease‐associated lipid changes. In particular, Cer levels, markedly elevated in APP mice, were restored to WT levels following treatment. As Cer promote oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal apoptosis, 43 their normalization may underlie the observed neuroprotective and cognitive effects. In parallel, PLA2G4E reduced pro‐inflammatory and oxidized arachidonic acid derivatives, including 11‐HETE and 12(S)‐HHTrE, indicating attenuation of lipid‐driven inflammatory signaling.

Consistent with its known enzymatic function, PLA2G4E also restored reduced levels of NAEs such as PEA and OEA, bioactive lipids with well‐established anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties across multiple experimental paradigms. 44 Interestingly, PLA2G4E effects contrast with those of a high‐fat Western diet, which elevates Cer and hexosylceramides and reduces NAEs, promoting a pro‐inflammatory state. 45 This pattern is consistent with an anti‐inflammatory role for NAEs in the brain. Mechanistically, PLA2G4E may, at least in part, exert its effects through NAEs acting on PPAR‐γ signaling, as reductions in NAEs and PPAR‐γ have been associated with memory deficits that can be rescued by pharmacological elevation of NAEs or direct PPAR‐γ activation. 46 , 47 Interestingly, PEA, one of the most studied NAE, has been shown to mediate neuroprotective and anti‐inflammatory effects through PPAR‐α signaling. 48 A recent systematic review further reported that PEA improved memory and cognitive performance in animal models and showed promise in enhancing executive function, working memory, and daily living activities in humans with cognitive decline, highlighting its therapeutic potential in neurodegenerative disorders. 49 Together, these observations suggest that PLA2G4E may mitigate neuroinflammation and promote neuroprotection via NAE‐PPAR‐dependent pathways, involving both PPAR‐γ and PPAR‐α, thereby supporting neuronal function and cognitive resilience.

In this context, our findings gain additional relevance in light of recent clinical evidence linking circulating NAEs to cognitive performance in aging populations. 50 Longitudinal analyses from the PREDIMED‐Plus‐Cognition cohort show that plasma levels of several NAEs, including OEA, PEA, and DHEA, as well as their ratios to anandamide, are associated with both baseline cognitive status and longitudinal cognitive changes in a sex‐ and APOE‐dependent manner. 50 These findings suggest that the lipidomic changes observed upon PLA2G4E modulation are consistent with a mechanistically informative signature rather than a purely correlative effect. Normalization of sphingolipids, reduction of pro‐inflammatory mediators, and modulation of endocannabinoid pathways are all associated with improved synaptic function and reduced neuroinflammation. Increased ceramide levels have been linked to impaired synaptic plasticity and activation of tau‐related kinases, while inhibition of ceramide synthesis can improve cognitive and synaptic outcomes in animal models. 51 , 52 Taken together, the lipid changes observed in PLA2G4E‐treated mice support a causal, rather than purely correlative relationship, suggesting that the observed functional benefits arise as a downstream consequence of lipid remodeling.

Consistent with this interpretation, recent integrative omics studies have provided descriptive frameworks for PLA2G4E function in health and disease, highlighting associations with lipid metabolism, neuronal signaling, and neurodegenerative processes. 53 , 54

These pleiotropic effects position PLA2G4E as a promising disease‐modifying target in AD, capable of engaging multiple pathological pathways rather than a single mechanism. Importantly, while recently approved disease‐modifying therapies, such as anti‐Aβ antibodies, have shown limited and variable cognitive benefits, 55 PLA2G4E‐targeted strategies may complement these approaches by engaging additional mechanisms and promoting broader neuronal resilience.

Future studies should further elucidate the molecular links between PLA2G4E‐driven lipid remodeling and tau regulation and evaluate long‐term safety and optimal therapeutic timing. A key limitation is the early stage of CNS gene therapy, as challenges remain, including pre‐existing anti‐AAV antibodies, systemic biodistribution of BBB‐crossing vectors, and large‐scale production. Overall, our findings support PLA2G4E as a promising multi‐target therapeutic strategy for AD, warranting further validation in clinically relevant models.
