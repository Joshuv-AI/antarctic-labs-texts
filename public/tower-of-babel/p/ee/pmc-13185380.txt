Socio-cultural motives drive free-range livestock farming in Estonia
Authors: Raivo Kalle, Mare Kõiva, Zsolt Molnár, Annely Holm, Marko Kass, Jaan Liira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEuropean landscapes have developed into biocultural heritage over millennia of extensive livestock production. Much of this biodiversity and cultural richness has been preserved by small-scale farms; however, these farms are disappearing across Europe due to intense competition with industrial agriculture. Recognizing the interconnectedness of social and natural systems, this study examined the socio-cultural background of livestock keepers in Estonia. The main research question focused on understanding the context in which livestock keepers decide to begin or continue free-range livestock farming, viewed through the lens of culture and tradition.MethodsAn anonymous, voluntary online survey was conducted among beef cattle and sheep keepers in Estonia, who are the primary users of semi-natural grasslands. In total, 88 completed questionnaires were received and analyzed using descriptive statistical methods.ResultsNearly one-third of respondents reported continuing a family tradition of livestock keeping. However, 62% of respondents had no prior experience in agriculture before starting their activities, and 58% managed only a single type of agricultural animal. The most frequently reported emotional motive was the desire to maintain aesthetically pleasing landscapes around their homes through grazing (74% of respondents). In addition, 56% of respondents indicated that grazing is the most effective management practice for their farmland. The most commonly cited constraints preventing herd expansion were the lack of additional pastures and the inability to manage larger herds. A slight majority of livestock keepers valued the preservation of national culture, and nearly 40% considered local knowledge, such as unwritten rules, important in their farming practices. Furthermore, more than half of respondents reported the presence of a natural area near their farm that they regard as sensitive or sacred. Livestock keepers generally do not celebrate any specific holidays associated with the traditional folk calendar; the only customary practise reported was giving bread to animals during the Christmas season. Moreover, 76% of respondents declared that they do not engage in any historically significant magical protection rituals aimed at safeguarding their livestock.ConclusionsGrazing in heritage grasslands supported by nature conservation subsidies is increasingly carried out by a new generation of livestock keepers who are establishing their own traditions. To effectively understand and communicate with livestock keepers, it is essential to consider their socio-cultural background and the motives underlying their decisions. We conclude that, when transitioning to an agroecological system, livestock keepers are strongly motivated by the pursuit of personal well-being, which in turn contributes to the overall health of the environment and society. Supporting the social well-being of farmers therefore requires expanding the concept of livestock keepers as custodians of biodiversity beyond protected areas and recognizing the value of their everyday practices, which play a crucial role in slowing the decline of small farms and traditional livestock farming practices.Keywords: Beef cattle keepers, Sheep keepers, Farmer motives, Farmer perception, Folk calendar, Folk religion, Small-scale agriculture

Introduction

Over millennia, livestock farming has transformed the European landscape into a form of biocultural heritage, of which approximately 30% is now recognized as having high natural and cultural conservation value [1]. Present-day land-use and policy decisions should therefore consider historical and traditional contexts alongside conservation objectives [2, 3]. Conservation strategies must integrate traditional knowledge that has shaped landscapes over centuries, recognize knowledge holders, safeguard their social and cultural institutions, and support their capacity to maintain and revitalize traditional management systems [4].

Small farms are a vital component of European rural regions. Beyond subsistence production, they contribute to broader food security [5–7], often employing environmentally and socially more sustainable methods than large-scale agricultural enterprises [8]. The viability of small farms depends on the long-term stability of local resources and labour [9, 10]. Furthermore, small farms often function as custodians of local cultural heritage [11]. Although family farms remain the predominant economic unit within the European Union [12], many face increasing economic pressures that threaten their continued viability. In addition, the economic sustainability of small farms is insufficiently supported by existing agricultural policies [8]. In particular, small-scale livestock farmers—who more frequently employ extensive management practices—are disappearing across Europe, emphasizing the need to strengthen their well-being and socio-ecological resilience [13, 14].

The European Union supports farmers through agri-environmental schemes and related measures aimed at promoting biodiversity and rural vitality [15]. Accordingly, the majority of studies evaluating the impact of agri-environment schemes have focused on biodiversity, environmental outcomes, and cost-effectiveness [1, 16, 17]. However, subsidies intended to support traditional and extensive grassland management practices, particularly within protected areas, have often failed to achieve their stated objectives [18]. In some cases, these measures have instead contributed to increased management intensity and the erosion of traditional practices. Furthermore, despite the biocultural significance of many heritage landscapes, relatively few studies have addressed their socio-cultural dimensions [1, 15]. Babai with colleagues [19] highlighted the widespread decline of traditional grassland management practices across contemporary Europe. Similarly, Roellig with colleagues [20] found that farmers’ personal values—particularly those that support the preservation of heritage landscapes such as wooded meadows—are as important as financial incentives in shaping management decisions. Although traditional grassland management practices have largely disappeared over much of the continent, their reintroduction in the management of protected grasslands is widely recommended. Such approaches would not only support biodiversity conservation but also the preservation of social and cultural components of these landscapes [19, 21].

Livestock farming is an integral part of European culture heritage [22]. Sheep (Ovis aries) and cattle (Bos taurus), originally domesticated in West Asia, are among the earliest livestock species. They have long provided humans with meat, milk, hides, wool, and other products, and were introduced into Europe shortly after their initial domestication [23]. Archaeological evidence indicates that livestock were already being kept on a small scale in Northern Europe as early as 4800–4600 BC [24]. Over time, livestock farming brought about profound changes to the European landscape, and much of today’s cultural landscape reflects the long-term influence of pastoral practices [25]. Livestock keeping is also closely intertwined with intangible cultural heritage, as demonstrated by traditional vocal communication and shouts used in human-animal interactions [26–28], as well as by songs, rituals, and calendar-based practices associated with agricultural cycles [29, 30]. Such cultural connections are further reflected in the widespread presence of animal-related sayings and proverbs within European societies [31–33].

In Western Europe, livestock farming in peripheral areas is increasingly being abandoned, while small farms are progressively being replaced by large-scale agricultural operations [34, 35]. Both the intensification of agricultural land use and the abandonment of semi-natural grasslands (particularly pastures) have contributed to the loss of landscape heterogeneity and a decline in wild species populations [36]. In contrast, following the collapse of the Soviet Union, an opposing trend has emerged in Eastern Europe. The dissolution of soviet agrarian system, collective farms (kolkhoz) and state farms (sovkhoz), has given rise to new socio-cultural relationships and the emergence of numerous small-scale farms [37]. This development was shaped by differing economic starting conditions among entrants and, in some cases, by the continuation or revival of traditional practices. Similar processes can be observed elsewhere in Europe, where new farmers relocating from urban to rural areas introduce value systems that differ from those of long-established rural populations. These shifting socio-cultural dynamics highlight the need for new analytical approaches to better understand contemporary transformations in European farming systems [38, 39].

The European Union requires substantial policy reform in order to meet its food security and environmental objectives [13]. In this context, the EU Biodiversity Strategy has set an explicit target of transitioning toward agroecological farming systems by 2030. However, financial incentives alone are insufficient to motivate farmers to adopt more sustainable production practices [40], indicating that complementary measures are necessary.

Although the neo-rural phenomenon has attracted considerable scholarly attention in Europe in recent decades [39], socio-cultural studies that jointly examine neo-farmers and traditional rural farmers are still lacking. This study builds on the theoretical framework proposed by Berkes and Folke [41], which posits that social and ecological systems are inherently interconnected. Within this perspective, the distinction between social and natural systems is viewed as artificial and analytically limiting. Current standardized approaches to grassland conservation management have failed to halt the decline in species diversity. Instead, adaptive management strategies that integrate traditional ecological knowledge and long-standing practices are increasingly advocated [4, 21, 42]. However, such approaches do not address a fundamental long-term challenge: the ongoing decline in the number of people actively managing semi-natural grasslands in rural areas [42]. In order to meet environmental protection targets by 2030, the EU will need to adopt alternative policy approaches to shift the emphasis of existing frameworks [13]. Rather than relying on generalized and standardized measures, future sustainability initiatives should be collaborative processes that bring together researchers and local stakeholders to co-develop context-specific, pluralistic, goal-oriented, and interactive solutions [19, 43].

Objectives of this work

The study examines a regional context in Eastern Europe, namely Estonia, focusing on livestock keepers as the primary agents responsible for the management of semi-natural grasslands. In this context, the term grassland is used to encompass both meadows and pastures, unless otherwise specified. Semi-natural grasslands with high species richness are today largely incorporated into the Natura 2000 network, where intensive agricultural activities—such as fertilization, sowing high-yield hay seed, and harrowing—are prohibited. These areas are also commonly referred to as heritage meadows.

Stakeholder representatives were actively involved in the co-production of knowledge. This included their participation as co-authors (A.H.) and the involvement of two professional associations that contributed to the formulation of research questions and supplemented the study with sector-specific expertise. We posit that a deeper understanding farmers’ socio-cultural and socio-ecological relationships will provide greater insight into their decisions to begin and continue livestock farming, thereby contributing to the long-term stability of rural areas. The overall aim of the study was to adopt a broad, pluralistic approach by involving livestock keepers with diverse motives: (i) those managing species-rich protected pastures and receiving environmental subsidies, primarily motivated by nature conservation; (ii) those grazing livestock on cultivated grasslands and receiving agricultural subsidies, motivated by agricultural production; and (iii) those engaging in livestock farming as a hobby, for whom agriculture represents a secondary income source or a non-commercial activity. The study addressed the following specific objectives: (a) to identify whether, and which, specific cultural traditions are maintained by Estonian livestock keepers, and for what reasons; (b) to explore farmers’ motives for beginning and/or continuing livestock keeping; and (c) to assess whether, and how, a farmer’s socio-cultural background—such as education, participation in professional associations, place of origin, adherence to traditions, perceptions of nature—influence their decision-making processes and underlying motives.

Historical background and context of Estonian livestock farming

Sheep are generally considered to be the first livestock species introduced into Estonia, with their arrival dated to the Late Neolithic period (2900–1800 BC). More widespread livestock farming emerged during the Late Bronze Age (850–500 BC), with cattle husbandry becoming the dominant practice [44]. Written sources from the 13th century indicate that cattle farming was already well established and large herds common [45]. A notable transformation in livestock farming occurred at the beginning of the 19th century following the introduction of sheep breeds producing high-quality wool [46]. Wool sheep husbandry and wool processing became an important industry in the region and remained so until the mid-19th century. However, the profitability of wool production declined with the influx of inexpensive wool imports from Australia, which shifted economic incentives toward meat-oriented sheep farming [46]. Despite these changes, sheep farming remained a secondary agricultural activity prior to World War II, with most farm income deriving from dairy production and pig farming [46].

Until the early 19th century, cattle were primarily utilized for manure production and as draft animals (oxen) for fieldwork, while only bulls were typically fattened for beef [47]. From the mid-19th century onwards, livestock farming—particularly dairy farming—expanded on large estates and, by the end of the century, increasingly on small farms as well [48]. Following World War I and the Estonian War of Independence, estates were dismantled in the 1920s, and numerous small farms were established under new land reforms. Until Soviet occupation in the 1940s, dairy and meat products were important export commodities for Estonia [49, 50]. Forced collectivization during Soviet occupation led to the disappearance of small farms and the consolidation of production into large-scale collective systems, including kolkhoz and sovkhoz farms. From the mid-1970s onwards, livestock production became concentrated in large, purpose-built cattle facilities. After the collapse of the Soviet Union and the transition from a planned to a market economy in the early 1990s, the kolkhoz and sovkhoz systems were dismantled and replaced by small family farms. This economic transition was accompanied by a substantial decline in overall livestock numbers [37].

During the collective farm period, beef cattle production was not prioritized, and only limited experimental initiatives were undertaken, as the dairy industry took precedence [11]. Following the end of the Soviet period, state-owned dairies ceased milk production, forcing dairy farmers to seek alternative sources of income [11]. Ensuring the profitability of livestock farming has often required increases in herd size. For example, it has been estimated that, to achieve self-sufficiency while grazing semi-natural grasslands within protected areas, a farmer must manage at least 200 sheep on approximately 48.5 hectares (equivalent to four or five sheep per hectare) [51].

The Republic of Estonia joined the European Union in 2004, bringing the Common Agricultural Policy into force. Over the past decade, both livestock numbers and the number of herders have declined significantly and continue to decrease, according to official national statistics [52]. While cattle numbers increased annually until 2014, subsequent trends indicate a decline: approximately 66,000 beef cattle were recorded in 2015, compacted with about 64,000 in 2024. A corresponding reduction occurred in the number of beef cattle keepers, from 2,176 in 2015 (with an average herd size of 30 animals) to 1,684 in 2024, by which time the average herd had increased to 38 animals.

Sheep farming in Estonia has experienced an even stepper decline. Whereas nearly 90,000 sheep were recorded in 2014, their number fell to 58,000 by 2024 (including about 8,000 goats). By early 2025, only 1,568 sheep keepers remained active in Estonia, based on national statistical data [52, 53]. Production systems differ by livestock type: dairy cows are predominantly kept indoors year-round and fed a grass silage-based total mixed ration, whereas beef cattle and sheep are typically raised in free-range systems, with regular access to pastures during the grazing season and hay as winter fodder [29]. Given that beef cattle and sheep currently play a central role in maintaining and shaping semi-natural grasslands in Estonia through grazing, this study focuses specifically on these livestock keepers.

Material and methodology

Estonia is located in Eastern Europe on the Baltic Sea coast (Fig. 1). It covers an area of over 45,000 km² and has a population density of 29 inhabitants per km2. Its climatic and environmental conditions are favourable for livestock farming, which has historically contributed to the development of extensive open grasslands and wooded pastures. Since the 1950s, however, a substantial portion of semi-natural pastures and meadows has been abandoned and subsequently afforested [54].

Preparation and distribution of the survey plan

The online survey questionnaire was developed on the basis of prior qualitative interviews with beef cattle keepers [29] and in collaboration with two local stakeholder organizations (“MTÜ Liivimaa Lihaveis” and “Muhu Liha TÜ”). The initial testing of the questionnaire was conducted with representatives from both stakeholder groups. The final online survey was implemented on the server of the Estonian Literary Museum by Ilona Kolossova, a specialist in the institution’s IT department.

Given the relatively even spatial distributed of livestock farming across Estonia, the absence of pronounced regional cultural differences, and the country’s low population density, a nationwide survey approach was adopted. The survey was conducted between July and September 2025. Invitations to participate were disseminated through national-level organizations as well as local beef and sheep breeding associations, which then shared the surveys with their members via professional mailing lists and social media groups. Considering that most livestock keepers are affiliated with professional networks or relevant online communities, this dissemination strategy was expected to reach the majority of the target population. A follow-up reminder was sent one month prior to the survey’s closure. In addition, a direct mailing strategy was employed to contact hobby and tourism-oriented farmers listed in publicly available databases who may not be active in professional organizations.

The questionnaire opened with an explanation of the study’s objectives and the intended use of the collected data. Participation was completely anonymous, and no personal or sensitive data was collected. All respondents participated voluntarily and retained the right to terminate the survey at any point or to skip individual questions they preferred not to answer. Submission of the anonymous questionnaire was taken to indicate informed consent for processing of the data. The questionnaire consisted of 50 questions, of which more than half were used in the analysis presented in this study. It began with general socio-demographic questions, including education, gender, and profession. To preserve anonymity, respondents were asked only to indicate the decade of birth and the county in which their primary farming activities were conducted. The questionnaire was structured into the following thematic sections:

Social aspects and incentives, including the emotional dimensions of animal husbandry and membership in professional associations;

The survey included a combination of single-choice, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions, with respondents also given the opportunity to provide additional comments for each question. As local traditions are often maintained through folk calendar holidays, the questionnaire also included questions on the observance of traditional celebrations: Christmas, New Year’s Eve, Palm Sunday, Good Friday, Pentecost, and Midsummer. The selection of these six holidays was informed by earlier qualitative interviews with livestock keepers (R. K., unpublished data [29, 55]), .

Data analysis

All questionnaire responses were compiled into an MS Excel spreadsheet for data processing. Multiple-choice responses were organized into separate variables for each participant. Descriptive statistical methods were applied to summarize and evaluate the data. Textual responses to open-ended questions were analyzed separately using qualitative analytical approaches.

In total, 92 completed questionnaires were received. Data screening revealed that four participants had submitted duplicate responses; these were removed prior to analysis, resulting in a final dataset of 88 unique responses (Table 1).

The professional and educational backgrounds of the respondents suggest that agriculture was often not their initial field of training or career choice. Results

Socio-economic and socio-ecological benefits

Socio-cultural aspects of livestock farming are strongly influenced by the type of pasture used for grazing, as well as by land tenure arrangements. Attitudes toward land and maintenance differ depending on whether pastures are short-term leased or privately owned, particularly when ownership is associated with long-term familial ties. Among respondents, 28 indicated that they primarily graze livestock on their own land, 27 reported mainly using rented land, and 29 stated that they utilize a combination of owned and rented pastures. A small number of respondents (4) reported grazing exclusively on state-owned land, such as nature reserves.

Under agri-environment schemes, pastures are classified into several categories based on ecological and management criteria. Beyond differences in soil fertility—where more fertile areas typically have a higher hay yield production—this classification system indirectly contributes to social stratification due to variations in subsidy levels. Consequently, managers of more fertile grasslands and recipients of larger agricultural payments are financially advantaged compared to others, which may translate into broader social benefits, such as improved mobility (e.g. ownership of vehicles more suitable for rural conditions), housing improvements, or greater access to extracurricular activities for children. Within our sample, respondents managed several types of land: semi-natural grasslands (also referred to as heritage meadows and part of the Natura 2000 network); permanent grasslands, where land-use change is prohibited but which are not subject to natural conservation requirements; cultivated grasslands, which must be ploughed and re-established as fields at least once every five years; and forest land, where grazing is not permitted under the Estonian Forest Act. Agricultural subsidies are not available for grazing on forest lands at all.

The largest financial payments through agri-environment schemes are allocated to managers of protected semi-natural grasslands. In this study, 36 respondents (41%) reported managing these areas through mowing or grazing, indicating strong competition for access to these grasslands. Substantial subsidies are also available for cultivated grasslands, which were managed by 19 respondents. In contrast, the lowest level of support is provided for permanent grasslands located outside protected areas, a category reported by 59 respondents. Although no subsidies are granted for grazing on forest lands, 17 respondents nevertheless reported using these areas. Additionally, 21 respondents indicated that they managed pastures across all the aforementioned categories.

Land scarcity was identified as the most significant constraint on increasing herd size, followed closely by limitations related to labour and management capacity. Many respondents expressed reluctance to increase herd size beyond what could be sustained with existing family labour and resources. Only two livestock keepers with small herds (1–50 animals) identified difficulties in marketing agricultural products, such as sheep wool, and two others reported conflicts with neighbouring landowners, including restrictions on livestock movement and grazing access. In contrast, respondents managing large herds (201–300 animals) mentioned two primary challenges: inability to manage even larger herds due to labour shortages and limited free time, and increasing regulatory restrictions, including stricter standards for winter feeding facilities for cattle (Fig. 2).

In addition, nine respondents specified that they had already reached an optimal herd size and therefore do not intend to expand further. Three respondents explicitly articulated a “less is more” approach, emphasizing quality over quantity. For example, one respondent noted, “Livestock farming must not be so-called ‘breaking my bones’ but rather should be physically non-burdensome and enjoyable for me. I do not want to hire strangers to help” (woman born in the 1960s). Similarly, another respondent explained, “We focus on quality, not quantity; pastures and buildings are adequate for the current size” (woman born in the 1970s).

Labour-related constraints were also identified, with two respondents reporting a shortage of available labour and another two highlighting a lack of free time for farm work. Predation emerged as another concern, with three respondents reporting increased predator attacks in recent years. One sheep keeper described the severity of the issue as follows: “In the last two years, wolf attacks have had a tremendous impact on my livestock farming” (woman born in the 1970s).

Financial and infrastructure limitations were also noted. Three respondents reported insufficient resources to build livestock enclosures, two lacked the means to establish appropriate winter feeding areas, and two cited an overall shortage of agricultural equipment. Sheep keepers also mentioned that there is no longer anyone to whom they can sell wool. As one respondent noted, “I feel that wool production is greatly undervalued. In sheep farming, animals are kept solely for meat production. Sheep wool is not even classified as an agricultural product” (woman born in the 1970s).

Motives for starting livestock farming and feelings about their work

More than half of respondents (55) reported having no prior experience in livestock farming before beginning to raise beef cattle or sheep. Similarly, the majority (51 respondents) indicated that they had not previously kept any animals, other than pets, on their farms prior to establishing their current farming operations. Notably, a higher proportion of sheep keepers than beef cattle keepers reported having no prior experience in animal husbandry. Among respondents with earlier farming experience, several reported a transition between production systems. Those who previously kept dairy cattle had largely discontinued milk production in favour of beef production, with only one respondent continuing to keep dairy cows alongside beef cattle. In addition, 12 respondents indicated that they were initially grain farmers before taking up livestock production. Differences between livestock groups were also observed in terms of diversification. Sheep keepers were more likely than beef cattle keepers to raise additional animals, including goats, poultry, and less conventional species, such as alpacas, llamas, and ostriches. In contrast, beef cattle keepers were more likely than sheep keepers to also raise horses.

A key factor in taking up livestock farming was the continuation of family traditions in animal husbandry, as reported by 29 respondents. At the same time, nearly as many respondents (25) indicated that they became livestock keepers largely by chance. Personal aspirations were also evident, as illustrated by one respondent who described fulfilling a long-held ambition: “I fulfilled my childhood dream of owning my own flock of sheep” (woman born in the 1970s).

Additional factors influencing the decision to adopt livestock farming included: suitability for farmland management (10 respondents); less initial investment compared to arable farming (grain crops) or dairy production (8 resp.); and its perceived environmental benefits (7 resp.). Some respondents (5) also highlighted the advantages of livelihood diversification, noting that livestock farming provides economic flexibility.

In terms of lived experience, a majority of respondents (65) reported that working with animals gives them a pleasant feeling, as grazing livestock in free-range systems enhances the visual quality of their surroundings. Furthermore, 51 respondents emphasized that they particularly valued experiencing the rhythms of nature and the diversity of daily tasks associated with animal husbandry. A sense of cultural belonging was also prominent, with 48 respondents indicating that they feel connected to rural culture through their work. Additionally, 33 respondents expressed a sense of usefulness to society through their role as food producers. Despite these positive perceptions, a notable count of respondents (20) expressed negative experiences related to their work, particularly feelings of being undervalued by both society and government institutions.

Livestock keepers’ relationships to professional ethics and unwritten rules

Unwritten rules and professional ethics passed down from more experienced colleagues were considered important by 32 respondents. Nearly half reported that they had learned certain unwritten rules from their senior colleagues, although most of their basic knowledge had been acquired through professional training or other sources (e.g. specialist literature or the Internet). In contrast, 11 respondents stated that they do not value unwritten rules or professional ethics in their work. By “unwritten rules”, we refer to social norms transmitted orally and through practices that are not codified in formal regulations and that extend beyond the immediate boundaries of paid work. The etic category “local knowledge” (Estonian: kohalikud teadmised) is not readily understandable within the Estonian cultural and linguistic context; therefore, the term “unwritten rules” (Estonian: kirjutamata reeglid) was considered more appropriate for this cultural setting.

One-third of respondents (26) reported communicating with colleagues on a personal level outside of work hours and events. Twelve respondents indicated that they are active members of professional associations, which they primarily attend to acquire knowledge; however, they noted that they have not (yet) shared their own life experiences or practical wisdom with other livestock keepers. Here, the term life experience and wisdom (Estonian: elukogemus or elutarkus, included in the etic category “local knowledge”) refers to knowledge and experience that remain within the scope of a farmer’s paid work and do not extend to regulating broader social norms. Ten respondents reported sharing their knowledge through practical examples on the farm with trainees and interns. Finally, almost one-third (25 respondents) indicated that they still consider themselves too inexperienced in livestock production to share knowledge with colleagues.

Traditions and the folk calendar

The majority of respondents (70) agreed that livestock keepers should value national culture in their work. As no definition of “national culture” was provided, respondents were free to interpret the concept individually. In framing this question, we drew on the Constitution of the Republic of Estonia, which identifies the preservation of Estonian national culture as a core objective. In the comments section, respondents elaborated on this idea, for example: “As I am a livestock farmer, I want new biodiversity to be born from my ecological footprint” (woman born in the 1960s); and “I consider it important that I maintain the skills and ability to cope in nature; it is part of security” (woman born in the 1960s). Seven respondents reported that they are primarily guided by economic indicators rather than by the goal of preserving local culture. More than half of the respondents (49) agreed that farmers should value traditions in their daily work and that in doing so they contribute to the preservation of culture. Twenty-four respondents stated that they had not previously considered the preservation of traditions in relation to their work. Ten respondents indicated that they did not adhere to traditions as culture is constantly changing.

Respondents were presented with six folk calendar holidays associated with animal husbandry practices. Overall, 78% of respondents reported observing at least one of these traditions, while 22% selected none. Twenty-one respondents selected more than one holiday; of these, one reported observing four, while the remainder selected two or three. A total of 69% of respondents identified Christmas as an important holiday and reported giving animals a treat, most commonly bread, on this occasion. Eighteen respondents reported not working on Good Friday. Eight noted bringing young birch tree branches with fresh leaves into the yard and barn during Pentecost. Five respondents reported engaging in fortune-telling activities on New Year’s Eve. Four respondents mentioned making a flower wreath for their favourite livestock animal on Midsummer’s Day (24 June), and three reported placing willow twigs on the barn or barn door on Palm Sunday. No additional holidays were mentioned in the comments section, although one respondent noted that they observe the equinoxes and solstices.

Protection magic and the phases of the moon

The majority of respondents (63) selected the response: “I do not use activities that increase luck or success, nor protective amulets in my work”. Sixteen respondents reported that they rarely engage in such activities, while four stated that they actively use protective magic.

Several specific protective practices were described. Four respondents noted that they had sprinkled salt or ashes on the ground to protect their farm from evil influences. One respondent reported drawing a cross on the barn door using charcoal or chalk, while another mentioned seeking a blessing from a priest or an esoteric practitioner. Additional practices described in the comments included placing a horseshoe above doors, using rowan twigs tied with a red ribbon, and asking for a blessing from “nature”. Other protective practices included spitting three times over the shoulder to ward off evil and prohibiting the praising of unfamiliar animals. Some respondents mentioned that costumed beggars visiting on Saint Martin’s Day (10 November, Estonian: Mardipäev)) and Saint Catherine’s Day (25 November, Estonian: Kadripäev) are believed to bring good luck to the herd. However, it was emphasized that protective magic practices should not be publicly discussed. One respondent noted that his father had practiced magic to ward off evil spirits (by drawing crosses on doors), but that he himself had not. One female sheep keeper (born in the 1980s) noted, “I put peas on the windowsill, to protect against the Wiedergänger (Estonian: Kodukäija) [‘one who walks again’ – the soul of dead people who come to harm the living]”.

Nearly half of the respondents (41) reported that they do not follow the moon phases in their activities. Twenty-eight respondents indicated that they rarely consider the lunar phases, while 13 reported actively following them. Examples included starting new activities during the new moon and completing ongoing tasks during the waning moon. Seven respondents noted that they follow the full moon as animals tend to be more restless at that time. Two additional comments were provided, including: “Rather by chance [I follow the moon]. But I will not leave essential work undone because of the wrong phase of the moon” (woman born in the 1950s).

Special or sensitive natural areas

More respondents reported the presence of sensitive natural places near their homes than those who did not (49 and 39 respondents, respectively). By “sensitive natural place”, we refer to locations that are perceived as special, positive, and unusual, where individuals go to “collect and recharge” themselves. Thirty-two respondents identified such places as natural stones, trees, springs, or forests. Twenty-five selected the statement: “I do not have a specific sensitive place in nature, all of nature is alive for me and I interact with it everywhere”. In addition, seven respondents noted that trees planted in their own yards are important to them.

The most frequently selected description of a heritage landscape (67 respondents) defined it as area consisting of pastures and meadows that have existed for centuries. This was followed by the perception of heritage landscapes as small-scale farms with stone walls, old trees, and shrubs (51 respondents). Twenty-nine respondents emphasized that heritage landscapes are characterized by the presence of veteran trees.

A similar number of respondents agreed with the other two additional definitions: heritage landscapes as areas of “pure nature” ( 23 respondents) or as landscapes associated with local place names not found on official maps (22 respondents). Eighteen respondents emphasized that heritage landscapes are places containing sites of particular importance to a family. Four respondents agreed that any landscape surrounding a village constitutes a heritage landscape, while another four expressed the view that the entirety of Estonia is a heritage landscape. Several respondents provided more detailed explanations in the comments, for example: “A landscape with managed forests, native fields, wooded meadows, coastal meadows and pastures, with farmhouses and gardens” (woman born in the 1960s); “Heritage landscape means a landscape that has developed over millennia in friendly cooperation between humans and nature, including all types of meadows” (woman born in the 1960s); and “[A heritage] landscape is a place where there are no large wind turbines” (woman born in the 1950s).

Discussion

Opportunities and challenges

Previous studies among Estonian livestock keepers have shown that a lack of suitable land is primarily a limiting factor for small farms that graze animals in their immediate surroundings, whereas large-scale farms are able to move their herds to rented land located tens of kilometres away [56]. Our findings similarly suggest that land availability imposes restrictions on herd size. Nevertheless, 10% of respondents reported being satisfied with the current size of their livestock herd or farm and emphasized a commitment to quality.

Respondents were motivated to begin or continue livestock farming primarily because it allowed them to preserve or enhance the beauty of the surrounding landscape, but also because it allowed them to experience the rhythms of nature. This emotional motive aligns with observations from Wales (UK), where small-scale farmers similarly value working in natural environments and contributing to local biodiversity [57]. In both our study and in the Welsh case, only a small number of farmers perceived their role as being limited solely to food production. No longer defining oneself as a food producer contradicts the historical image of farmers working primarily to survive, support themselves, and meet market needs. Although local communities historically recognized the value of mixed forage and mixed hay [58], the accumulation of biodiversity in pastures and meadows largely occurred incidentally as a by-product of food production, rather than as an explicit goal. This stands in contrast to contemporary perspectives held by both farmers and the wider public. However, a recent analysis of historical literature indicates that rural populations were in fact well informed about biodiversity indicators [59].

A quarter of respondents indicated that they did not wish to increase their livestock herd, as expansion would increase the management burden on themselves, their families, and their farms. Sheep keepers in particular highlighted predator attacks on livestock, which result in both emotional distress—such as encountering injured or killed animals in pastures—and material losses. In line with Linnell and colleagues [60], predator attacks on livestock should also be viewed as a source of social tension (e.g. possible job losses when livestock farming is discontinued), particularly in regions with high nature value [1]. Consequently, tensions between two policy objectives—the maintenance of species-rich landscapes through livestock grazing and the protection of large carnivores—should be carefully balanced [1].

Beliefs and cultural customs

Earlier Estonian culture placed strong emphasis on protection magic and adherence to the folk calendar [61]. It is important to note that, until the beginning of the 20th century, agricultural life in Estonia was characterized by two parallel cultural groups: large landowners, primarily Baltic Germans (German-speaking), and the small-scale farmers, who were ethnic Estonians. The ethnographic source referenced here [61] reflects the traditional practices of Estonian peasants rather than those of Baltic German landowners.

Archival texts [62] indicate that in western and northwestern Estonia, drawing a cross on barn doors with soot was a common practice to protect animals. Interestingly, only two respondents in our study reported engaging in this practice. Decorating rooms, barn doors, or yards with birch twigs on Pentecost is another long-established custom in Estonia, closely associated with church holidays [63]. However, the decline of small-scale livestock farming has contributed to the disappearance of barn-related decorations. Similarly, placing willow twigs on barn doors on Palm Sunday was previously recorded in southeast Estonia [29] as well as in northeast Estonia [62]. In this study, two additional reports of this practice were received from Virumaa and Harjumaa counties. Regional variation in the gathering and placement of willow twigs is evident: this tradition has persisted primarily in areas with a living Orthodox heritage [64] and is closely connected to the Finno-Ugric and Slavic cultural spheres. Overall, our findings suggest that while some archaic regional traditions are still followed by a small number of contemporary livestock keepers, these are exceptions rather than the norm. Most respondents—particularly among the so-called new generation of livestock keepers—no longer use protective magic or consider lunar phases to be important in their work.

Although six folk calendar holidays were included in the questionnaire, Christmas clearly emerged as the most widely observed; all other holidays were selected far less frequently. Moreover, none of the respondents added additional holidays in the open-ended comments section. Earlier calendar-related studies conducted among young people (teenagers and young adults) from various regions of Estonia in 2007 (n = 1,300) and 2018 (n = 957) similarly identified Christmas as the most popular holiday [65]. However, those studies documented a much wider range of holidays considered meaningful and actively celebrated. In contrast, traditional spring festivities such as Pentecost (Estonian: suvisted) and the custom of bringing birch twigs indoors were not regarded as significant by younger generations. These findings point to a broader trend of declining engagement with traditional holidays, although practices associated with humour, masquerades, and mobile festivities have persisted. For example, Halloween has gained popularity, likely due to media influence and the availability of inexpensive holiday-themed merchandise [65].

Taken together, the results indicate that contemporary livestock keepers no longer organize their work according to the folk calendar. Historically, one of the most important days for a farmer was the spring release of livestock from winter housing [62]. Today, however, livestock are generally kept on pastures for much of the year and typically return to shelters only during winter to feed.

The emergence of livestock keepers with new social backgrounds and ethically guided network structures

Across Europe, the abandonment of agriculture in peripheral areas, along with the decline and ageing of rural populations, remains a significant challenge [13]. At the same time, there is a countertrend of people returning to the countryside and revitalizing local activities, including agriculture. For example, in the Alps, harsh climatic conditions contributed to a sustained population decline beginning in the second half of the 19th century. Over the past few decades, however, an immigration trend has emerged, resulting in a steady population increase—a phenomenon referred to as the “Alpine Renaissance” [66].

A similar dual process can be identified in Estonia, where the abandonment of livestock farming coexists with its revival. More than half of the respondents in this study reported having no prior experience with livestock farming. At the same time, more than half possessed higher education, indicating that individuals with advanced educational backgrounds are willing to undertake new challenges and risks by beginning livestock farming. In addition, a substantial proportion of respondents were of active working age—almost half (n = 42) were born in the 1970–1980 s—indicating that they potentially have up to 20 years of working life ahead.

In the Alpine context, where new rural cultures have emerged through immigration, questions remain regarding whether these practices will be sustained by future generations [67]. Similarly, our data raise questions about intergenerational continuity in Estonian livestock farming. Only 33% of respondents reported continuing a family tradition, indicating that the future of livestock production will remain highly dependent on new entrants to this sector.

At the policy level, the European Union has set a goal of transitioning from production-oriented agriculture toward agroecology [13]. In the former Eastern Bloc countries, where centrally planned agricultural systems—such as kolkhoz and sovkhoz— were dismantled in the 1990s [68], farmers have already experienced radical structural transformations, and may therefore be more willing to accept further changes and associated risks. In Estonia, the mid-1990s national campaign “Clear the old guard!” (Estonian: Plats puhtaks!), which promoted the replacement of “old” structures with “new” ones, exemplifies this broader societal transition [69]. At the same time, the freedom regained after the end of communist occupation enable individuals to pursue activities that they enjoyed. Within this context, livestock farming has been perceived as a means of transforming one’s surroundings and engaging with nature, as illustrated by one respondent: “I fulfilled my childhood dream and raised a flock of sheep”. New social groups of livestock keepers have thus emerged, developing alternative food systems based on the principles of agroecology. These new systems operate alongside the dominant global agricultural food model, which is largely shaped by industrial and corporate actors [70]. Identifying specific agroecological solutions for more sustainable food systems require that relevant actors have the freedom to self-organize [71]. Our findings suggest that a key driving force of such self-organization is the pursuit of personal well-being, expressed through access to healthy food and the creation of more beautiful landscapes. This aligns with earlier qualitative research [11], which found that, alongside financial incentives (e.g. nature conservation subsidies), a central motive for beginning livestock farming was the enhancement of landscape aesthetics through grazing. Beyond its aesthetic value, grazing also supports the maintenance of pastures and contributes to the conservation of wild species that depend on these habitats.

Pastures outside nature reserves also need conservation attention

A substantial proportion of respondents reported having no prior experience with animal husbandry. Given the ageing of the agricultural sector across Europe, understanding the motives and backgrounds of newcomers to the sector has become an increasingly important area of research. Studies of Spanish female farmers, for example, demonstrate that long-standing stereotypes in agriculture are disappearing as new social groups enter the sector and form their own professional networks [72]. These networks are often based on shared values, such as principles of sustainable agriculture. Although the authors of that study highlighted the issue of gender discrimination within Spanish livestock farming [72], this was not evident in the Estonian case, where both men and women are actively involved in livestock farming.

An overwhelming majority of respondents (80%) stated that livestock keepers should also bear responsibility for preserving national culture. This result may be interpreted in light of the Constitution of the Republic of Estonia, which explicitly emphasizes the preservation of national culture. This suggests that farmers are strongly influenced by socio-cultural factors, and that new livestock keepers entering the sector actively integrate into rural culture because they wish to contribute to the continuity and development of culture. Although it is often emphatically stated that only semi-natural grasslands constitute heritage meadows (i.e. part of indigenous culture), in practice, livestock keepers operating outside these areas also play an important role in maintaining and transmitting cultural values and practices.

The majority of respondents (76%) stated that in their opinion heritage landscapes are semi-natural grasslands. This perception may be partly explained by the fact that 41% of respondents grazed their livestock exclusively on such grasslands. However, it is important to note that only 31,000 hectares of semi-natural grasslands remain in Estonia today [51]. Given that the total area of agricultural land in Estonian is 983,447 hectares [52], semi-natural grasslands account for only about 3% of all farmland.

Previous research conducted in Estonia found that slightly more than half of all farmers perceived semi-natural areas as “beautiful and diverse landscapes” [73]. This appreciation has increased compared to earlier nationwide surveys of farmers. By contrast, recognition of grasslands in terms of “high species richness and the presence of species with conservation value” has remained relatively stable over a five-year period (48% in earlier surveys and 50% in more recent data) [73]. It is likely that livestock keepers’ high appreciation of semi-natural areas has been shaped by media attention, nature conservation efforts, and growing research interest. However, we wish to emphasize the importance of extending socio-cultural and socio-ecological research beyond formally protected areas.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, no statistical tests or procedures were applied to assess causal relationships between variables; all interpretations are based on qualitative analysis of descriptive statistics. As the aim was not to conduct a quantitative statistical study but to explore patterns and meanings using descriptive data, the findings involve a degree of uncertainty.

Second, as with all voluntary survey-based research, the data are subject to self-selection bias, over which the researcher has no control. For example, organic farmers were more likely to respond to the questionnaire than non-organic farmers, despite our intention to capture opinions from both groups. A total of 64 respondents indicated that they currently receive organic subsidies, while eight respondents (all managing herds of up to 50 animals) reported that they previously had been organic farmers but were no longer certified. Only 16 respondents stated that they had never practised organic farming (i.e. received organic subsidies); most of these managed herds of up to 50 animals, with three respondents keeping between 51 and 100 animals.

Finally, response rates to the questionnaire varied considerably by county, further limiting the representativeness of the sample.

Conclusions

A nationwide online survey of sheep and beef cattle keepers revealed that many respondents had higher education unrelated to agriculture and no prior experience in animal husbandry. The ongoing structural changes taking place in the agricultural sector have facilitated the emergence of a new group of farmers with diverse social backgrounds. This group is helping to reshape rural culture and no longer adheres closely to traditional folk calendar practices and folk beliefs. At the same time, nearly one-third of respondents reported continuing a family tradition in livestock farming, suggesting that, following the dismantling of kolkhoz and sovkhoz in Estonia, intergenerational continuity has emerged within certain farm systems.

The primary motives for keeping livestock were related to landscape enhancement and the opportunity to experience the rhythms of nature through this work. Identification with the role of food producer was less important, indicating that livestock keepers tend to view themselves as components of the surrounding agroecosystem. Respondents also emphasized the importance of feeling part of the rural culture, suggesting that socio-cultural factors play an important role in choosing a profession. Furthermore, more than half of respondents reported having sensitive or meaningful places in nature (e.g. tree, spring, rock, forest) located near their homes, reflecting their strong connection to the surrounding natural environment.

Contemporary livestock keepers no longer follow the traditional magical rituals, seasonal folk holidays, or moon phase-based practices that were characteristic of earlier peasant culture. These findings indicate that in order to effectively understand and communicate with livestock keepers, modern socio-cultural changes and the motives underlying farmers’ actions must be considered. Our analysis suggests that transitions to agroecological practices are most effectively supported when they align with farmers’ desire to prioritize their own well-being and that of their immediate surroundings, which in turn improves the overall health of both the environment and society. To further strengthen the social well-being of livestock keepers, we should broaden the concept of farmers as custodians of biodiversity beyond protected areas and recognize the value of their everyday practices, which help preserve small farms and livestock farming practices.
