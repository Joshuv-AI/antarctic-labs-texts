Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cell-Derived Exosomes Ameliorates Testicular injury to improve spermatogenic disorder through PRDX1/TRAF6/TGFβ signaling pathway
Authors: Yuan Tian, Li Quan, Qi Li, Xiao Zhou, Fang Peng, Yanfei Gong, Gaobo huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfertility is a significant global health challenge, with male factors contributing to approximately 50% of cases. Here, this experiment investigated the effects of Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells (UCMSCs)-Derived Exosomes Ameliorates Testicular injury and its molecular mechanisms. In a mouse model of testicular injury, treatment with UCMSC-Exos improved spermatogenic dysfunction. We found that these exosomes carried and released PRDX1 mRNA. PRDX1 expression was downregulated in the injured testis, primarily within testicular epithelial cells. Knockdown of PRDX1 (sh-PRDX1) exacerbated spermatogenic disorder, whereas PRDX1 overexpression attenuated oxidative stress in vitro. PRDX1 reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in mice model of Testicular injury. PRDX1 reduced Epithelial fibrosis in Testicular injury model. PRDX1 reduced mitochondrial induced ferroptosis in Testicular injury model. The inhibition of PRDX1 promoted mitochondrial induced ferroptosis in Testicular injury model. PRDX1 suppressed TRAF6/TGFβ Signaling Pathway in mice model of Testicular injury. PRDX1 protein interlinked TRAF6 protein to promote TRAF6 ubiquitination in vitro model of Testicular injury. TRAF6 inhibitor reduced the effects of si-PRDX1 on ferroptosis and Epithelial fibrosis in Testicular injury model through the inhibition of oxidative stress and inflammation. Our findings demonstrate that UCMSC-derived exosomes ameliorate testicular injury and improve spermatogenic disorder. The mechanism involves the exosomal delivery of PRDX1, which subsequently interacts with TRAF6 to promote its ubiquitination, thereby inhibiting the TRAF6/TGFβ signaling pathway. This inhibition attenuates oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction-induced ferroptosis, and epithelial fibrosis, ultimately promoting testicular repair.Keywords: Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells, Testicular injury, Spermatogenic disorder, PRDX1, TRAF6

Introduction

Spermatogenic disorders are a major cause of male infertility, often leading to severe sperm abnormalities or azoospermia. The resulting infertility and increased risk of birth defects affect an estimated 5 million families worldwide [1, 2]. Despite their clinical significance, our understanding of the underlying pathogenesis remains limited. To date, only a few etiologies—such as Klinefelter syndrome, Y chromosome microdeletions, cryptorchidism, and mumps orchitis—have been definitively linked to impaired spermatogenesis [3]. Current research on spermatogenic dysfunction primarily stems from rodent models and focuses on genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. In contrast, studies on the proliferation, differentiation, and regulatory mechanisms of human spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) are markedly insufficient, particularly regarding the molecular signaling pathways that govern these processes [4]. The key focuses and difficulties in the research on human spermatogenic disorders are as follows: the lack of research on pathogenic genes based on clinical pedigrees, the shortage of applied research supported by an adequate number of tissue samples, and the absence of non-invasive sperm assessment technologies and standardized diagnosis and treatment systems [5].

The testis is composed of somatic cells and germ cells, with spermatogenesis tightly regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis through endocrine signaling [6]. Spermatogenesis requires complex interactions between germ cells and somatic cells. Spermatogenesis proceeds through three sequential phases: mitosis, meiosis, and spermiogenesis [7]. Undifferentiated spermatogonia undergo multiple rounds of asynchronous mitotic divisions to generate primary spermatocytes. These diploid cells then enter meiosis, completing two successive divisions to produce haploid round spermatids [8]. Subsequently, during spermiogenesis—a differentiation process involving dramatic morphological remodeling—these spermatids mature into elongated, motile spermatozoa [9]. Mature sperm are then released freely into the lumen of the seminiferous tubules and migrate to the epididymis for further maturation [10]. Any abnormality at any point during the entire spermatogenesis process may lead to spermatogenic defects, thereby causing male infertility [11]. Recent research has increasingly focused on the molecular underpinnings of spermatogenesis, with particular emphasis on transcriptional regulation, epigenetic modifications, and post-transcriptional control mechanisms that govern gene expression throughout germ cell development [12].

In recent years, exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) —including those from bone marrow, umbilical cord, adipose tissue, and human amniotic fluid—have emerged as a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy for a range of diseases, offering novel avenues for the treatment of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) [13]. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HuMSCs) are multipotent stromal cells isolated primarily from Wharton’s jelly and the perivascular regions of the umbilical cord. Compared with MSCs from other sources, hUCMSCs offer distinct advantages, including ethically non-controversial procurement, abundant availability, and low immunogenicity [14]. Exosomes are vesicles produced by eukaryotic cells that participate in intercellular information exchange. Composed of lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins, they generally have a diameter of 30–150 nm [15]. Accumulating evidence indicates that MSC-derived exosomes can modulate immune responses, attenuate local inflammation, reduce oxidative stress, and enhance tissue repair—thereby improving outcomes in various injury models and extending post-transplant survival in recipients [16]. Studies have shown that exosomes containing miR-122-5p derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells can reduce the apoptosis of ovarian granulosa cells and improve ovarian function [17–19]. Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (HuMSCs-EXOs), compared with umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells themselves, possess characteristics such as better biocompatibility, low toxicity, low immunogenicity, and low tumorigenicity [20]. Research has found that HuMSCs-EXOs have the functions of alleviating inflammatory responses and reducing cellular oxidative stress [21]. Building on these advances, the present study investigates the protective effects of exosomes derived from hUCMSC-Exos against testicular injury and elucidates the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

hUCMSC, HUVEC Culture, Exosome Extraction and Uptake of hUCMSC-Exos

The hUCMSC was obtained from our Hospital, was cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA) containing 10% exosome-free serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco, USA). Exosomes were extracted using an exosome extraction kit. The supernatant of P3-P5 hUCMSCs was collected, and ExoQuick-TC (SBI, USA) exosome extraction reagent was added to the supernatant at a ratio of 1:5. The supernatant was discarded, and the mixture was centrifuged at 1500 xg for 5 min to remove all liquid. hUCMSC-exos were used for experiments or stored at − 80 °C.

Animal model, histological, immunohistochemical, and immunofluorescence analyses and electron microscopy

The animal studies were authorized by the Animal Ethic Review Committees of our hospital. All animal experiments were strictly implemented in compliance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. X-rays produced by a precision linear accelerator (VWAT Elekta, Sweden) in the Radiation Centre (PLA Rocket Force Characteristic Medical Center, Beijing) were used as radiation sources. Male C57BL/6J mice were subjected to 5 Gy total-body irradiation (TBI) at a dose rate of 4 Gy/min. Mice were treated with vehicle (saline) or DMSO (M08G704, Alfa Aesar, China) at a dose of 6 g/kg by intraperitoneal injection 1 h prior to irradiation. For the in vitro experiment, GC-2 cells were subjected to irradiation at a dose rate of 4 Gy/min and cultured with or without 0.5% DMSO (v/v) for 2 h prior to irradiation.

Fixed testes in Bouin’s solution were dehydrated and placed in paraffin. The 5 μm sections were cut from the testicular tissue and inspected using a light microscope after staining with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E). A spermatogenesis score by the Johnsen score method with grading from 1, no spermatogenesis to 10, full spermatogenesis was used as described previously. Moreover, a testicular injury score from 0 which stands for normal histopathology to 3 which stands for severe damage was used for the evaluation of seminiferous epithelial damage, interstitial edema, tubular necrosis, and congestion as previously reported. For immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses, mouse tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained as described in previous studies [22]. Samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Observer A1, Germany) and a transmission electron microscope (80 kV) (Hitachi H7650, Tokyo, Japan) as described in a previous study [23].

Cell culture and animal model

GC-2 cells were transfected with negative, PRDX1, si-nc or si- PRDX1 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, CA), followed by 10 and 12 Gy radiation. The cells were incubated for another 7 days to allow for colony formation and subsequently stained with crystal violet.

ELISA and cell viability assay

4-HNE (H268-1-2), CAT (A007-1-1), MDA (A003-1-2), SOD (A001-3-2), GSH (A006-2-1), GSH-PX (A005-1-2) were performed as described in a previous study [24]. Cell viability was determined using CCK-8 assay (C0037, Beyotime) as described in a previous study [23]. Absorbance was measured on the Microplate Reader (Bio Tek, Winooski). EdU kit (C0075S, Beyotime) or LDH activity (C0016, Beyotime), Caspase3 (G01513), Caspase7 (H080) and caspase9 (G01811) were quantified Commercial reagent kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute) and Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a fluorescent reader (Synergy H1 Microplate Reader, Bio Tek, Winooski).

Western Blotting Analysis and Immunofluorescence

Western Blotting Analysis and Immunofluorescence were executed as literature [23]. PRDX1 (1:1000, Abcam), TRAF6 (1:1000, Abcam), TGFβ (1:1000, Abcam), GPX4 (ab125066, 1:1000, Abcam), β-actin (1:10000, AC028, Company ABclonal, Inc.) and Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:5000, GB23303, Servicebio) were used in this study. Protein was measured using an BeyoECL Plus kit (P0018S) and analyzed using an Image Lab 3.0 (BioRad Laboratories, Inc.). PRDX1 (1:1000, Abcam), and TRAF6 (1:1000, Abcam) was used for immunofluorescence analyses.

Statistical analysis

P < 0.05 was considered significant and evaluated using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-test. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Extraction and characterization of UCMSCs-Derived exosomes

Exosomes were isolated from the conditioned supernatant of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) using ultracentrifugation. The yield was quantified via a BCA protein assay kit, resulting in approximately 2.5 mL of exosome suspension at a concentration of 1172.6 µg/mL. The morphological characteristics of the isolated particles were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which revealed a typical saucer-like or cup-shaped morphology consistent with exosomes (Fig. 1A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) confirmed that the particle size distribution predominantly fell within the expected range of 30 nm to 150 nm (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, CD63 and CD81 protein expression levels were significantly increased in UCMSCs exosomes compared with hUCMSCs (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these data verify the successful isolation of exosomes from hUCMSCs.

UCMSCs-derived exosomes alleviate spermatogenic dysfunction in a mouse model of testicular injury to release PRDX1 mRNA expressions

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of UCMSC-Exos on testicular injury, we established a mouse model and administered UCMSC-Exos. UCMSCs exosomes reduced the numbers of pups per plug, the straight-line velocity of sperm and the curvi linear velocity of sperm, inhibited the amplitude of lateral head displacement of sperm, Sperm number and motile sperm, and decreased progres-sive sperm in mice model of Testicular injury (Fig. 2A and H).

Concurrently, UCMSC-Exos treatment markedly upregulated both mRNA and protein expression of PRDX1 in the injured testicular tissues (Fig. 2I, J). These results indicate that UCMSC-Exos can ameliorate spermatogenic dysfunction in testicular injury, and this beneficial effect is associated with the restoration of PRDX1 expression.

PRDX1 expressions was down-regulation in testicular injury model

First, this study explored the effects of PRDX1 expression levels in Testicular injury model. PRDX1 expression was in mice model of testicular injury were down-regulated (Fig. 3A). PRDX1 protein expression in mice model of testicular injury was suppressed (Fig. 3B). This decrease in PRDX1 expression was further confirmed in an in vitro cellular model of testicular injury, where PRDX1 mRNA levels were markedly reduced (Fig. 3C). Consistently, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses of testicular tissues from the injury model showed a substantial reduction in PRDX1 protein expression (Fig. 3D and E). These data collectively demonstrate that PRDX1 expression is suppressed in both in vivo and in vitro models of testicular injury.

PRDX1 expressions in testicular epithelial cells of testicular injury model

To dissect the cellular specificity of PRDX1 expression, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on testicular tissues from a mouse model of testicular injury and analyzed a public scRNA-seq dataset from human testicular injury patients (Fig. 4A and B). PRDX1 was found to be expressed in Testicular epithelial cells of Testicular injury patients (Fig. 4C). In contrast, PRDX1 expression was negligible or absent in immune cell populations, including B cells, T cells, and macrophages, within the same datasets (Fig. 4D and F). These findings indicate that PRDX1 is specifically expressed in testicular epithelial cells under injury conditions.

Sh-PRDX1 exacerbates spermatogenic dysfunction in a mouse model of testicular injury

To investigate the functional role of PRDX1, we performed knockdown experiments using sh-PRDX1 in a mouse model of testicular injury. Compared with the control group, Sh-PRDX1 aggravated the numbers of pups per plug, the straight-line velocity of sperm and the curvi linear velocity of sperm, increased the amplitude of lateral head displacement of sperm, Sperm number and motile sperm, and promoted progres-sive sperm in mice model of Testicular injury (Fig. 5A and H). These results demonstrate that loss of PRDX1 function aggravates testicular injury and impairs sperm quality and fertility.

PRDX1 mitigates oxidative stress in a cellular model of testicular injury

To determine the role of PRDX1 in regulating oxidative stress, we manipulated its expression in an in vitro model of Testicular injury. Overexpression of PRDX1 (PRDX1-OE) significantly increased PRDX1 mRNA levels (Fig. 6A). PRDX1 up-regulation reduced ROS production levels, MDA and 4-HNE levels, and increased CAT, SOD and GSH-PX levels in vitro model of Testicular injury (Fig. 6B and G).

Conversely, si-PRDX1 effectively reduced PRDX1 mRNA expression in an in vitro model of Testicular injury (Fig. 6H). PRDX1 down-regulation promoted ROS production levels, MDA and 4-HNE levels, and reduced CAT, SOD and GSH-PX levels in an in vitro model of Testicular injury (Fig. 6I and N). These data demonstrate that PRDX1 is a critical regulator of the cellular redox balance in testicular injury, where its expression alleviates oxidative damage.

PRDX1 attenuates oxidative stress and inflammation in a mouse model of testicular injury

We next evaluated the effect of PRDX1 on systemic oxidative stress and inflammation in vivo. PRDX1 up-regulation decreased IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα and INF-γ activity levels in vitro model of Testicular injury (Fig. 7A and D). Meanwhile, PRDX1 down-regulation increased IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα and INF-γ activity levels in vitro model of Testicular injury (Fig. 7E and H). Next, In the testicular injury mouse model, sh- PRDX1 increased IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα and INF-γ activity levels, promoted MDA and 4-HNE levels, and reduced CAT, SOD and GSH-PX levels (Fig. 7I and Q). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PRDX1 plays a protective role in testicular injury by concurrently suppressing both oxidative stress and inflammatory responses.

PRDX1 Attenuates Epithelial fibrosis in a testicular injury model

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that PRDX1 plays a protective role against Epithelial fibrosis in Testicular injury. In an in vitro model of testicular injury, PRDX1 overexpression significantly upregulated mRNA levels of epithelial markers—including E-cadherin, ZO-1, and N-cadherin—while concomitantly downregulating key mesenchymal and fibrotic markers such as fibronectin, vimentin, and α-SMA (Fig. 8A). Conversely, siRNA-mediated knockdown of PRDX1 (si-PRDX1) in cultured cells suppressed the expression of E-cadherin, ZO-1, and N-cadherin and enhanced fibronectin, vimentin, and α-SMA mRNA levels, indicating induction of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like processes (Fig. 8B). Meanwhile, sh-PRDX1 suppressed E-cadherin, ZO-1 and N-cadherin mRNA expressions, and induced Fibronectin, Vimentin and α-SMA mRNA expressions levels in tissue samples of Testicular injury (Fig. 8C). Together, these data suggest that PRDX1 mitigates testicular fibrosis by preserving epithelial integrity and suppressing EMT, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for preventing fibrotic remodeling following testicular injury.

TRAF6 inhibition rescues PRDX1 deficiency-induced ferroptosis and epithelial fibrosis

To determine whether the detrimental effects of PRDX1 loss are mediated through the TRAF6 pathway, we applied the specific TRAF6 inhibitor C25-140 (10 µM) in the context of PRDX1 knockdown. In an in vitro model of testicular injury, treatment with C25-140 significantly attenuated the aggravating effects of si-PRDX1. Specifically, the inhibitor reversed the downregulation of GPX4 protein and the upregulation of TRAF6 and TGF-β proteins induced by si-PRDX1. Concurrently, it mitigated the associated increases in oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokine release, ferroptosis markers, and epithelial fibrosis (Fig. 13). These results demonstrate that the promotion of ferroptosis and fibrosis upon PRDX1 deficiency is largely dependent on TRAF6 activation, and that targeting this pathway can restore redox and fibrotic homeostasis.

Discussion

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) possess the capacity of self-renewal and multi-directional differentiation, enabling them to contribute to tissue regeneration and serve as a therapeutic tool in various pathological conditions. Accumulating evidence indicates that the primary mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) is not direct cell replacement but rather paracrine signaling—particularly mediated by MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) [25]. Exosomes derived from hUCMSCs (hUCMSC-Exos) have been shown to play a pivotal role in tissue repair through multiple mechanisms, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, pro-angiogenic, and trophic support functions [26, 27]. hUCMSCs-Exo significantly reduced the content of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) treated with Mifepristone, significantly decreased the proportion of cells positive for senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal), significantly downregulated the mRNA expression levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and significantly downregulated the protein expression levels of P16, P21, and P53 [28, 29]. These results indicate that hUCMSCs-Exo have the ability to delay the senescence of hESCs [30]. In the present study, we demonstrate that hUCMSC-derived exosomes ameliorate spermatogenic disorders in a murine model of testicular injury by delivering functional PRDX1 mRNA, thereby enhancing antioxidant defense and preserving germ cell integrity. This observation aligns with recent work by Amiri et al., who identified Sertoli cell–derived exosomes as promising diagnostic and regenerative tools in male infertility [31]. So, UCMSCs exosomes is a potential method for treating Spermatogenic Disorder in the Model of Testicular Injury. However, this study also has shortcomings. For example, we have not validated our results clinically. We will validate and explore our conclusions with clinical samples in future experiments, and provide a foundation for future clinical applications.

The testis is the primary reproductive organ in male mammals. Its seminiferous tubules are lined with a specialized epithelium composed of Sertoli cells and germ (spermatogenic) cells, which together orchestrate spermatogenesis [32]. This highly coordinated process—encompassing mitotic proliferation, meiotic division, and post-meiotic differentiation—is exquisitely sensitive to perturbations by endocrine signals, environmental toxins, and pharmacological agents such as chemotherapeutic drugs [33]. Cyclophosphamide (CP), a widely used alkylating agent with potent antineoplastic and immunosuppressive properties, is known to exert significant gonadotoxic effects. CP disrupts the delicate equilibrium among proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis of spermatogenic cells, thereby triggering germ cell loss and impairing spermatogenesis [10]. Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that CP not only compromises testicular architecture and function but also allows its active metabolites—such as acrolein and phosphoramide mustard—to directly target meiotic spermatocytes and haploid spermatids, leading to their depletion and consequent infertili [9, 12, 34]. In addition, CP can increase the risk of testicular cancer, reduce spermatogenic potential, and shorten reproductive lifespan [35]. In the present study, we observed that PRDX1 mRNA is expressed in testicular epithelial cells in a CP-induced testicular injury model, suggesting a potential role for PRDX1 in the testicular response to oxidative stress. Notably, Xiang et al. recently reported that exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC-Exos) attenuate inflammation and fibrosis in early diabetic nephropathy [36], supporting the broader therapeutic potential of hUCMSC-Exos in oxidative stress–related tissue injury—including the testis. These data indicated that hUCMSCs-Exo regulated PRDX1 in Testicular injury model. This study only used GC-2 cells, a cell line, as an in vitro model to investigate our conclusions. But this is also our shortcoming. We will use more cells, especially primary cells, to validate our conclusions in future research.

Peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1) is a critical antioxidant enzyme that modulates intracellular levels of ROS and influences immune cell function. By scavenging ROS and mitigating oxidative damage, PRDX1 plays a protective role in various pathological conditions, including inflammatory liver diseases [37]. Predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, PRDX1 regulates multiple redox-sensitive signaling pathways and participates in essential biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [38]. It participates in biological processes including cell proliferation and differentiation, and exerts a vital role in the oxidative stress response by regulating signal transduction pathways [39]. We found that UCMSCs exosomes increased PRDX1 mRNA expression and protein expression in mice model of Testicular injury. PRDX1 expressions were down-regulation in Testicular injury model. This finding is consistent with the work of Qu et al., who demonstrated that hUCMSC-derived exosomes can modulate PRDX1 expression in target cells [40]. Collectively, these results suggest that PRDX1 is a key downstream effector of hUCMSC-Exos in the context of testicular injury, mediating their antioxidant and cytoprotective effects. Thus, PRDX1 represents a promising molecular target for hUCMSC-Exo–based therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving testicular function and fertility.

Globally, infertility affects approximately 15% of couples, with male factors contributing to nearly half of these cases. The primary cause of male infertility is spermatogenic dysfunction—a disruption in the highly orchestrated process of spermatogenesis, which encompasses three key phases: mitotic proliferation of spermatogonia, meiotic division of spermatocytes, and spermiogenesis (the morphological transformation of round spermatids into mature, tadpole-shaped spermatozoa) [41]. A defect in any of these links can lead to male infertility. Clinically, spermatogenic disorders commonly manifest as azoospermia (absence of sperm in ejaculate), oligozoospermia (low sperm count), teratozoospermia (abnormal sperm morphology), and/or asthenozoospermia (reduced sperm motility), with azoospermia representing the most severe phenotype [42]. Azoospermia refers to the complete absence of sperm in the ejaculated semen, and it includes obstructive azoospermia (OA) and non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) [43]. The testes of OA patients usually have normal sperm-producing ability, and the abnormal sperm transport caused by obstruction leads to azoospermia [44]. In contrast, NOA arises from intrinsic testicular failure, characterized by impaired or absent germ cell development within the seminiferous tubules [45]. In the present study, knockdown of PRDX1 (via sh-PRDX1) significantly exacerbated spermatogenic dysfunction in a murine model of testicular injury. Consistent with this, pharmacological or genetic inhibition of PRDX1 further aggravated germ cell loss and disrupted spermatogenesis, underscoring the protective role of PRDX1 in maintaining testicular homeostasis under stress conditions.

Spermatogenesis in mammals is a highly coordinated and dynamic process that relies on intricate bidirectional crosstalk between germ cells and testicular somatic cells—including Sertoli, Leydig, and peritubular myoid cells [46]. Due to the influence of factors such as genetics or the environment, an interruption in spermatogenesis at any stage may result in male infertility [47]. The somatic cell niche within the testis functions analogously to soil for seed germination: it provides essential structural, metabolic, and signaling cues that sustain germ cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation [48]. In the present study, we demonstrate that peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1) attenuates both epithelial–mesenchymal transition–associated fibrosis and mitochondria-driven ferroptosis in a murine model of testicular injury. Notably, exosomes have recently emerged as promising biomarkers and mediators in male reproductive health; for instance, Maria Barceló et al. reported that testicular exosome profiles can predict the presence of viable sperm in azoospermic men [49]. Building on these insights, our findings indicate that exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC-Exos) exert protective effects against testicular injury by upregulating PRDX1, thereby suppressing oxidative stress–induced ferroptosis and fibrotic remodeling in the seminiferous epithelium.

Mitochondria are primarily responsible for generating adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation, thereby supplying the energy required for a wide range of cellular physiological processes [50]. Testicular mitochondrial damage induced by oxidative stress is a key factor causing testicular injury and spermatogenic disorders [51]. For instance, arsenic exposure triggers excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to structural and functional impairment of testicular mitochondria and subsequent bioenergetic failure [52]. The testicular ATP content in the model group was significantly reduced, while that in the experimental group was significantly increased [32]. This indicates that grape seed proanthocyanidins (GSPs) can promote ATP synthesis and improve arsenic-induced testicular mitochondrial dysfunction [53]. Previous studies have shown that mitochondrial biogenesis is closely related to ATP production, and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis can increase the intracellular ATP content [54, 55]. In the present study, we demonstrate that peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1) effectively attenuates oxidative stress and inflammation in both in vivo and in vitro models of testicular injury. Notably, Che et al. recently reported that exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC-Exos) alleviate LPS/H₂O₂-induced neuroinflammation and oxidative stress [20], highlighting their broad cytoprotective potential. Extending these observations to the reproductive system, our data indicate that hUCMSC-Exos exert protective effects against testicular injury by upregulating PRDX1 expression, thereby mitigating oxidative damage and suppressing inflammatory responses in the injured testis.

Transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) is a pleiotropic cytokine belonging to the TGF-β superfamily and serves as the central driver of fibrogenesis [56]. It orchestrates fibroblast activation, induces their transdifferentiation into α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)-positive myofibroblasts, and promotes excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as collagen, thereby playing a pivotal role in tissue fibrosis [57]. The inflammatory response and oxidative stress damage mediated by TGF-β1 can be either promotional or inhibitory [58]. TGF-β1 can mediate the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by inducing inflammatory factors and oxidative stress to damage cells, thereby increasing the formation and deposition of collagen [59, 60]. Downstream of TGF-β1 signaling, TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) functions as a key adaptor protein that activates multiple inflammatory pathways. Upon stimulation, TRAF6 triggers ubiquitin-dependent signaling cascades leading to the activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), culminating in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [61–63]. In the present study, we found that upregulation of peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1) significantly increased PRDX1 protein levels while concomitantly suppressing the expression of both TRAF6 and TGF-β1 in a murine model of testicular injury. This observation aligns with previous reports: Min et al. demonstrated that PRDX1 inhibits NF-κB activation by directly suppressing the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRAF6 [40]., while Sun et al. showed that genetic ablation of PRDX1 enhances TGF-β1 secretion in primary lung fibroblasts [40]. Zhang et al. reported that hUCMSCs-Exo mediated TGF-β receptor to prevent scar formation during wound healing [64]. Collectively, our findings indicate that hUCMSC-Exos ameliorate testicular injury and improve spermatogenic dysfunction by upregulating PRDX1, which in turn suppresses the TRAF6/TGF-β1 axis, thereby mitigating inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrotic remodeling in the injured testis. Of course, this is a fundamental research and there is still a lot of work to be done for its clinical application. This is also what we need to do next in our experiment.

Conclusion

In summary, exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC-Exos) ameliorate testicular injury and improve spermatogenic dysfunction by suppressing epithelial fibrosis and ferroptosis through modulation of the PRDX1/TRAF6/TGF-β signaling axis. Specifically, PRDX1 upregulation promotes TRAF6 ubiquitination, leading to inhibition of the TRAF6/TGF-β pathway and subsequent attenuation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling in the injured testis (Fig. 14). Our data demonstrate that PRDX1 acts as a critical negative regulator of this pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory cascade in both in vivo and in vitro models of testicular injury. These findings highlight PRDX1 as a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of testicular injury and related male reproductive disorders.
