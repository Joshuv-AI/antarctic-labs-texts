A robust deep learning framework for RNA 5-methyluridine modification prediction using integrated features
Authors: Naif Almusallam, Salman Khan, Fawaz Khaled Alarfaj, Nijad Ahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe discovery of RNA 5-methyluridine (m5U) modifications is vital in computational biology due to their essential significance in different biological processes. This study presents a powerful predictor named 5-meth-Uri, which improves the overall accuracy of m5U modification predictions. The proposed method combines a composite of dinucleotide and trinucleotide-based auto-cross covariance with six physicochemical parameters to generate a feature vector. We select essential features using an unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) technique to enhance the model’s efficiency. Finally, an intelligent computation Deep Neural Network (DNN) was utilized to classify RNA 5-methyluridine (m5U).ResultsThe model’s performance was evaluated on two benchmark datasets, namely mature mRNA and full transcript, using tenfold cross-validation and an independent test. The proposed model achieved an average accuracy of 95.13% and 97.36% on the training set and 95.73% and 96.51% on the independent test sets for the full transcript and mature mRNA datasets, respectively. 5-meth-Uri demonstrated around 7.15% and 3.98% higher accuracy on training data and 5.42% and 3.52% higher accuracy on independent samples for both datasets compared to existing models.ConclusionsIts high accuracy and reliability make 5-meth-Uri a promising tool for researchers with potential biomedical and pharmaceutical applications.

Background

Revealing approximately 170 cellular RNA chemical alterations [1, 2], RNA mapping tools have significantly advanced the study of RNA epigenetic changes. Among them are well-known signs such as 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N6-methyladenosine (m6A), and pseudouridine (Ψ), as well as less investigated changes including 7-methylguanosine (m7G) and dihydrouridine (D). RNA changes affect all four bases: uracil (U), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and adenine (A), as well as the ribose sugar [3]. tRNA and rRNA are the most highly altered species. These chemical alterations are crucial for protein synthesis, gene expression, and modifications to RNA-modifying enzymes [4]. While every method has restrictions in sequence context and modification specificity [5], more advanced recognition techniques, such as liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) [6] and next-generation sequencing (NGS), have enabled researchers to study these modifications more comprehensively [6].

Among these modifications, 5-methyluridine (m5U) stands out for its biological significance. 5-methyluridine (m5U) is primarily found in non-coding RNAs and transfer RNAs (tRNAs), such as ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and messenger RNA (mRNA) [7]. m5U modifications are catalyzed by enzymes such as TrmA in bacteria and TRMT2A in humans. This modification contributes to RNA stability and structure, particularly in the conserved T-loop motif. However, despite its prevalence, the biological functions and mechanisms of m5U remain underexplored. Research [8, 9] has connected m5U to processes like stress response, plant development, disorders including lupus and breast cancer, and stress response. Though frequently costly and providing limited data owing to technological difficulties, experimental approaches such as miCLIP-Seq [10, 11] and iCLIP [9] offer insightful analysis. Advancement of our knowledge of m5U sites in health and illness depends on the development of strong computational approaches to precisely identify them. To address the challenges in spotting RNA modifications [12–14], researchers have suggested using Machine Learning (ML) methods.

The experimental identification of all RNA changes is costly and complex, leading to the widespread use of computational approaches. Numerous machine learning-based techniques have been created to forecast RNA changes, i.e., RNA modification [15], ConsRM [16], iRNA-Methyl [17], SRAMP [18], WHISTLE [19], DirectRMDB [20], MODOMICS [21], m7GHub V2.0 [22], m5C site predictors (RNADSN [23], iRNA-m5C [24]), and pseudouridine predictors (RNAm5Cfinder [25], PPUS [26], and iRNA-PseU [27]). To enhance the detection of m5U-modified sites, a limited number of experimental and computational methods have been developed to handle the complexity of RNA modifications [28, 29]. Among them, m5UPred, created by Jiang et al. [30], uses a support vector machine method using sequence-derived characteristics. For mature mRNA, the suggested model achieved an accuracy of 89.91%, and for full transcripts, 83.60%. Based on an SVM classifier, Ao et al. [31] introduced the m5U-SVM model using tenfold cross-validation. The m5U-SVM achieved accuracies of 94.36% for mature mRNA and 88.88% for full transcripts. Both models have yielded promising results in terms of accuracy; however, these models are developed based on conventional learning methods, which are unable to accurately predict the m5U sites due to the high similarity between m5U and non-m5U sites. In addition, these models require substantial amounts of human expertise and capability to extract prominent features automatically. Recently, Noor et al. [32] proposed a computational method that leverages pseudo-k-tuple nucleotide compositions as a feature extraction method, enriching them with structural and sequence-order features and refining feature selection using SHAP to retain the most discriminative attributes. The proposed model achieved 91.47% and 95.86% accuracy, surpassing existing predictors for accurately identifying RNA’s 5-methyluridine (m5U) modifications on the same benchmark datasets. However, these models may struggle to capture deeper contextual relationships within RNA sequences and often lack integration with complementary encoding strategies, which can limit the overall performance and generalizability of the trained models.

This study introduces a computational predictor to accurately identify 5-methyluridine (m5U) modifications, utilizing efficient feature extraction methods. Firstly, the proposed model uses a composite feature extraction algorithm, i.e., Dinucleotide-based Auto-Cross Covariance (DACC) and Trinucleotide-based auto-Cross Covariance (TACC), to convert an RNA sequence into a feature vector. The DACC combines Dinucleotide-based Auto-Covariance (DAC) and Dinucleotide-based Cross-Covariance (DCC). Similarly, TACC combines Trinucleotide-based Auto Covariance (TAC) and Trinucleotide-based Cross-Covariance (TCC). Through this integration process, we develop new composite feature vectors that enable the model to represent biomedical information more effectively. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed as a feature selection method to enhance these feature vectors further and select the discriminative ones by removing noisy and irrelevant features. Moreover, a Deep Neural Network (DNN) was used to classify and accurately identify 5-methyluridine (m5U) modifications through a tenfold cross-validation. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed model outperforms existing prediction models on both datasets in terms of accuracy and other performance measurement parameters. Experimental setup

To experiment, we use an Intel Core i7, which is suitable for desk work and basic computing, paired with a 256 GB SSD for faster booting, reading, and writing than HDDs. The system includes 16 GB RAM, enabling smooth multitasking. This setup is ideal for individuals or small teams focusing on data tasks, though advanced operations with larger datasets may require CPU and RAM upgrades.

Analysis of nucleotide composition

In this section, we used Two-Sample Logo software [33] to analyze nucleotide composition differences between sequences with and without m5U modifications. This method highlighted significant residue enrichment or depletion at m5U-modified sites (Fig. 2). Statistical analysis using a t-test (p < 0.05) revealed distinct patterns in both modes. In the full transcript mode, sequences displayed an enrichment of G at locations − 3 (ratio: 50) and − 8 (ratio: 35), U at location 1, and C at locations 7 and 8. Similarly, the mature mRNA mode showed notable enrichment of G at − 8 and − 3, while C clustered around positions 6–9. Homopolymeric regions, such as G (− 10 to − 8) and C (6 to 9), were observed. Additionally, sequence-specific differences emerged: G was more concentrated in the Mature mRNA mode, whereas C was enriched at location 19 in the full transcript mode. Notably, when C was at position − 1, the mature mRNA mode showed a higher concentration of G, while the full transcript mode enriched for A. These findings suggest that nucleotide composition and enrichment patterns can be reliable indicators for predicting m5U modification sites in RNA.Fig. 2m5U sites nucleotide composition analysis

Conclusions

With 5-methyluridine (m5U) being particularly important post-transcriptionally, RNA modification plays a vital role in determining disease development and controlling biological activities. Understanding RNA function and its connections to disease depends on this change marked by methylation at the C5 site of uridine. To facilitate the precise identification of m5U sites, we present a novel 5-meth-Uri model based on deep learning, designed to leverage hybrid feature integration and rigorous evaluation through tenfold cross-validation and independent datasets. The 5-meth-Uri model addresses challenges such as overfitting by employing optimized hyperparameter tuning and feature selection techniques. It demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving 95.13% and 97.36% accuracy on both datasets, respectively, while maintaining robust accuracy levels of 95.73% and 96.51% on independent test datasets. These results significantly surpass conventional machine learning models and other state-of-the-art prediction methods. With its superior accuracy and reliability, 5-meth-Uri is poised to enhance research into RNA modifications and their biological roles, particularly in stress response mechanisms and disease pathogenesis, including breast cancer. Moreover, this model holds promise for aiding the development of therapeutic strategies targeting RNA modifications, paving the way for innovations in biomedical research and personalized medicine.

In the future, we plan to develop a user-friendly online web platform for the proposed 5-meth-Uri model, enabling biologists to access and utilize it for convenient prediction of RNA 5-methyluridine modifications. Additionally, we aim to explore advanced parallel deep-learning models[4, 40, 41] to enhance computational efficiency and accuracy.

Methods

Benchmark dataset

Reliable machine-learning models and validation of ML approaches depend on datasets. This work made use of the full transcript and mature mRNA databases, previously used for the classification of RNA 5-methyluridine (m5U) modification sites [30, 39]. From the positive sample, a 41-nt modified sequence with an experimentally identified U-site was produced; however, unmodified uridine sites were randomly chosen from the same transcripts to create the negative samples. The full transcript dataset initially included 3696 positive and negative samples, while the mature mRNA dataset comprised 1232 positive and negative samples. We used CD-HIT to exclude homologous sequences and filter out more than 80% of similar sequences, improving the data quality.

Ultimately, 20% of the data served as a separate test set, and 80% was used for training. The full transcript dataset, for example, contained 1534 positive and 2862 negative training samples. Similarly, 983 positive and 985 negative training samples were utilized for the mature mRNA dataset. Furthermore, for the independent test set, the positive and negative samples are 245 and 247 in the mature mRNA dataset and 500 and 731 in the full transcript mode.

PCA features selection

In statistical machine learning, feature vectors play a crucial role in making predictions. However, high-dimensional feature spaces can negatively impact model accuracy due to the presence of irrelevant, redundant, and noisy features. Additionally, such models require significantly more computational resources and memory for training and testing. Various feature selection methods have been developed to reduce the feature space by eliminating unnecessary features, thereby addressing these challenges and enhancing predictive performance. PCA is a widely used multivariate technique that transforms correlated features into a smaller set of uncorrelated ones, known as principal components. The primary objective of PCA is to reduce the dimensionality of feature vectors while preserving critical discriminative features. In this study, we employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce the original 144-dimensional feature space (as shown in Table 8) while preserving the most informative components. We gradually applied PCA, starting from the complete feature set and incrementally reducing the dimensions, evaluating model performance at each step (Fig. 6 A, B). The proposed model achieved the best performance using 80 principal components for both datasets. Deep neural network architecture

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are a type of machine learning model designed to inspire how the human brain processes information. The DNN structure consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. The hidden layers play a crucial role in recognizing features and patterns within the data that aren’t immediately obvious. Adding more hidden layers can improve the model’s ability to handle complex patterns, but it also makes the network more difficult to train, more resource-intensive, and prone to overfitting. One of the primary benefits of DNNs is their ability to automatically extract features from data, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering. They can learn directly from raw, unlabeled, or unstructured data using standard training methods, as noted in [47]. Due to their depth and flexibility, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) outperform traditional machine learning methods in complex classification tasks. They are widely applied in fields such as bioengineering [40], natural language processing [34], image recognition [35], and speech recognition, demonstrating their versatility and effectiveness across various domains.

Although the DNN model exhibited promising results, several limitations are associated with the model: (a) the model is sensitive to the number of neurons in the hidden layers; i.e., a few neurons may cause underfitting problems, whereas too many neurons may contribute to overfitting issues. (b) In general, a network with more hidden layers in a training or testing process can lead to better accuracy; however, it also raises significant issues, such as increased computation cost, complexity, and vanishing gradients. (c) Due to its high computational complexity, the model required high-performance computing to generate reasonable results within a reasonable time. To address these issues, we applied several techniques. For example, a dropout technique was employed at each hidden layer to overcome the issue of overfitting [51–53]. Similarly, a balanced number of hidden layers (i.e., four hidden layers) was configured in the network to overcome the computational complexity and vanishing gradient issue [54, 55].
