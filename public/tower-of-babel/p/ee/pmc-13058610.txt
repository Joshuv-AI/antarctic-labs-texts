Effective therapy for periprosthetic joint infection through sequential application of non-contact low-frequency ultrasonic debridement and antiseptic solutions
Authors: Fei Wang, Yicheng Li, Boyong Xu, Xiaogang Zhang, Wenbo Mu, Wentao Guo, Abudousaimi Aimaiti, Tuerhongjiang Wahafu, Dewen Xue, Yichang Li, Baochao Ji, Li Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsConventional mechanical debridement may be insufficient to fully eradicate biofilms adherent to prosthetic and surrounding tissue surfaces in cases of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). This study aimed to evaluate the eradication efficacy and safety of non-contact low-frequency ultrasonic debridement (NLFUD) combined with antiseptic solutions against Staphylococcus aureus biofilms.MethodsEx vivo, sequential NLFUD was followed by one of four antiseptic protocols (0.9% saline, 3% H2O2, 0.5% povidone-iodine (PI), or 3% H2O2 then 0.5% PI) to eliminate S. aureus biofilms on titanium discs, and evaluated eradication efficacy by live/dead staining and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In vivo, the approach was subsequently evaluated in a S. aureus-infected PJI rat model comprising four groups: mechanical debridement alone, mechanical and chemical debridement, mechanical and NLFUD debridement, and combined mechanical, NLFUD, and chemical debridement. In vivo, biofilm disruption was assessed by SEM, and safety by haematology, hepatorenal function, major-organ histology, local angiogenic/inflammatory markers, and periprosthetic bone integration.ResultsNLFUD combined with each antiseptic produced residual live and dead biofilm areas of < 1%. NLFUD + PI and NLFUD + H2O2 + PI groups exhibited no bacterial regrowth. SEM analysis confirmed effective biofilm disruption using NLFUD on titanium discs ex vivo and on titanium alloy prosthesis in vivo. NLFUD treatment significantly increased bacterial counts in surgical fluid (median 2,665 vs 195 CFU/ml, p = 0.002). Biofilms were reformed in all groups at two weeks after debridement surgery. No significant between-group differences were seen in local immunohistochemical markers (including periprosthetic bone integration and angiogenic markers), or systemic inflammatory indicators, and haematoxylin and eosin staining revealed no structural abnormalities in major organs.ConclusionAs an intraoperative adjunct to debridement in PJI, sequential NLFUD and antiseptic irrigation enhance immediate biofilm disruption while maintaining a favourable systemic and local safety profile, warranting clinical evaluation to reduce residual biofilm burden.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(4):397–410.

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is the most severe complication after arthroplasty, potentially leading to poor functional outcomes, reduced quality of life, or even mortality.1 The incidence of PJI is 2.1% after hip arthroplasty and 2.3% following knee arthroplasty in the USA.2 Surgical intervention is widely regarded as the mainstay treatment for PJI. The treatment outcomes for single-stage revision, two-stage revision, and debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention are unsatisfactory, with infection control rates of 87%, 83%, and 64%, respectively.3,4

Developing biofilms is widely acknowledged as a primary cause of treatment failure in PJI. The 2018 International Consensus Meeting emphasized the necessity of removing all infected inorganic and organic materials during surgical intervention to eradicate biofilms.5 Biofilms, formed on the surfaces of prostheses and surrounding tissues,6-8 protect bacteria from antibiotic infiltration and antiseptic solution efficacy and enable bacteria to evade attacks from host immune cells in PJI.9 Despite numerous efforts to develop treatments targeting biofilms, including modifications to prosthetic surface materials, quorum-sensing inhibition, and degradation of biofilm matrices, an effective method for treating biofilms remains elusive.10 Therefore, this condition underscores the urgent need for a method to effectively disrupt all biofilms that may exist within the joint cavity of patients with PJI.

Low-frequency ultrasound generates alternating compression–rarefaction cycles that create transient negative pressures and nucleate cavitation microbubbles.11 The collapse or fragmentation of bubbles adjacent to prosthetic surfaces yields microjets and high shear forces that disrupt and detach biofilms, thereby liberating bacteria into the lysate for culture.12,13 In 2007, Trampuz et al14 advocated for the use of low-frequency ultrasound in the pathogen diagnosis of PJI. We further demonstrated that lysates, derived from intraoperatively excised prostheses or infected tissues using non-contact low-frequency ultrasound, exhibited higher culture positivity rates than synovial fluid and lysates acquired from conventional water bath ultrasound.15,16 We hypothesized that non-contact low-frequency ultrasound disrupts biofilms through cavitation effects, transforming biofilm-embedded bacteria into planktonic forms, which subsequently improves the bactericidal efficacy of subsequently applied antiseptic solutions in chemical debridement. This study aimed to evaluate the following: the biofilm eradication efficacy of non-contact low-frequency ultrasonic debridement (NLFUD) combined with antiseptic solutions ex vivo and in vivo; and the safety of using NLFUD in combination with chemical debridement during surgery.

Methods

Detection of NLFUD cavitation effect propagation patterns using luminol sonochemiluminescence

The sonochemical experiment was performed with minor modifications.17 Two reaction systems with different volumes were established: 30 ml and 5 ml aqueous solutions containing luminol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 1 × 10⁻3 M, sodium carbonate (Merck, Germany) at 1 × 10⁻2 M, hydrogen peroxide (Merck) at 2 × 10⁻4 M, and carbon tetrachloride (Merck) at 1.38 × 10⁻2 M. The NLFUD device (Shanghai Weimi Ultrasonic Company, China) operated at 25 kHz with a 3 mm diameter titanium alloy probe. The NLFUD device was operated at 100% output power. The 30 ml reaction system was performed in a 50 ml centrifuge tube, and the 5 ml reaction system was conducted in a 15 ml centrifuge tube. Both tubes were from NEST Biotechnology (China). All experiments were conducted in complete darkness with video recording using an iPhone 15 (Apple, USA). Frame extraction was performed using Final Cut Pro software (Apple), and image contrast enhancement was achieved using FIJI software (ImageJ; National Institutes of Health, USA).

Development of ex vivo biofilm model

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus; American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)-12600) was selected for biofilm eradication analysis and animal experiments based on previous studies.18,19 High-purity titanium discs (8 mm diameter, 2 mm thickness, and > 99.99% purity) served as substrates for biofilm formation. A 48-hour incubation period was selected to achieve mature biofilms as established in previous studies.20,21 Sterilized titanium discs were inoculated with 1 × 106 CFU/ml S. aureus suspension and incubated statically at 37°C with 80% relative humidity. After 24 hours, fresh Luria-Bertani (LB) broth was replaced, and incubation continued for an additional 24 hours. Mature biofilms were gently washed thrice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) to remove planktonic bacteria before experimental procedures.

NLFUD combined with antiseptic protocol ex vivo

Biofilm-bearing titanium discs were immersed in 30 ml normal saline and subjected to NLFUD exposure (3 mm probe diameter, 25 kHz frequency, 100% output power, 10 s on/3 s off cycles) for 0 s and 10 s. For each exposure-duration condition, we then applied four post-NLFUD antiseptic immersion protocols (n = 6 per condition): 1) immersion in normal saline for five minutes; 2) immersion in 3% H2O2 for three minutes; 3) immersion in 0.5% povidone-iodine (PI) for five minutes; or 4) sequential immersion in 3% H2O2 (3 mins) followed by 0.5% PI (5 mins). Antiseptic immersion times were pre-specified to align with clinical practice and published evidence.22-24 After antiseptic immersion, the titanium discs were gently soaked thrice in LB broth for five minutes to mitigate the effects of residual antiseptic solutions. Subsequently, the plates were cultured in LB broth for 24 hours to assess the presence of viable bacteria.

Live/dead biofilm staining assay

Biofilms on treated titanium discs were stained using the FilmTracer LIVE/DEAD Biofilm Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The staining solution was formulated by adding 3 μl of SYTO 9 dye and 3 μl of propidium iodide to 1 ml of filtered sterile water. Treated discs were rinsed thrice with sterile PBS, incubated in a staining solution at room temperature in darkness for 30 minutes, and subsequently rinsed thrice with filtered sterile water. Stained samples were immediately examined and photographed using a Nikon SMZ25 stereomicroscope (Nikon, Japan). The quantification of the red and green fluorescence images was performed using FIJI.

Animals and study design

A total of 36 male Sprague-Dawley rats (250 to 280 g) were housed under standard laboratory conditions with controlled temperature (25 ± 2°C), humidity (55 ± 5%), and a 12:12 hour light-dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. Based on aprevious study with modifications,25 we created a S. aureus-infected PJI rat model. We then performed the study-specific interventions (Figure 1). The full procedural details corresponding to Figure 1 (including the mechanical and chemical debridement procedures for each group) are provided in Supplementary Materials S1 and Supplementary Figure a.

Treatment scheme during the animal experiments. CON, control (mechanical debridement); DC, mechanical debridement and chemical debridement; NLFUD, non-contact low-frequency ultrasonic debridement; DU, mechanical debridement and NLFUD; DUC, mechanical debridement, NLFUD, and chemical debridement; PJI, periprosthetic joint infection; SEM, scanning electron microscopy.

Scanning electron microscopy

Titanium discs (ex vivo) and harvested prosthetic implants (in vivo) were fixed in 2.5% electron microscopy EM-grade glutaraldehyde (Solarbio, China) at 4°C for 12 hours. Following fixation, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 85%, 95%, and 100%) for 15 minutes at each concentration, followed by critical point drying. After gold sputter coating, samples were examined using a JSM-7610F SEM (JEOL, Japan).

Systemic and local response analyses

Body temperature and weight were measured at baseline (day 0), intervention (day 14), and study endpoint (day 28). Blood samples were collected through a tail vein puncture under anaesthesia on day 28. Complete blood counts (CBC, n = 6/group) were conducted on 0.2 ml blood in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes utilizing a BC-5000VET automated haematology analyzer (Mindray, China). For biochemical analysis (n = 6/group), 0.5 ml of blood was centrifuged at 1,000× g for 15 minutes, and serum was analyzed using a BS-240VET automated biochemistry analyzer (Mindray). Biochemical parameters included hepatic enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase, AST; alanine aminotransferase, ALT; alkaline phosphatase, ALP), renal function markers (creatinine, CREA; uric acid, UA), and total protein (TP) levels. All assessments were conducted with blinded evaluation.

Immunohistochemistry and histology

Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours. Bone tissues were decalcified in 10% EDTA (Solarbio, China) for approximately four weeks, and other tissues were processed directly. Following decalcification, tissues were dehydrated using graded alcohols, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0; Proteintech, China) at 95°C for 15 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 minutes at room temperature. After PBS washing, sections were incubated with goat serum (Zhongshan Golden Bridge, China) for one hour at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. Sections were incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Details of primary and secondary antibodies are described in the Supplementary Materials S2. Visualization was performed utilizing DAB substrate (Zhongshan Golden Bridge), followed by Mayer’s haematoxylin counterstaining (Solarbio). Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach, and intestine) were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histomorphological examination. Stained sections were dehydrated and mounted with neutral gum.

Periprosthetic bone integration analyses

Peri-implant bone (PIB) adjacent to the prosthesis was evaluated using radiological imaging and immunohistochemistry analysis. Anteroposterior knee radiographs (n = 6/group) were acquired under anaesthesia at days 0, 14, and 28 using an HF400VA digital radiography system (MIKASA, Japan). The radiological assessment employed a modified scoring system developed by An and Friedman.26 Immunohistochemistry was employed to detect osteogenic and osteoclastic marker expression in PIB and trabecular bone (TB).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were assessed for normality within groups using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as means (SDs) and compared between two groups using the independent-samples t-test, or among the four groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as medians (IQRs) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups or using the Kruskal-Wallis test; when significant, Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Holm adjustment were applied. For variables measured at multiple timepoints, group comparisons were performed separately at each timepoint. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For visualization, quantitative variables were displayed using box-and-whisker plots with individual observations overlaid or line plots, as appropriate. Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using R (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria) and RStudio (version 2024.12.1 + 563; Posit, USA).

Propagation patterns of NLFUD-induced active cavitation

Figure 2 depicts the side-view luminol sonochemiluminescence images at 100% output power and 25 kHz frequency. With increasing time, both the intensity and spatial distribution of active cavitation phenomena expanded progressively. Initially, cavitation activity was most prominent at the ultrasound probe tip before progressively disseminating throughout the entire liquid volume. Qualitative observation revealed that cavitation extended throughout the entire liquid space for approximately three seconds in the 30 ml aqueous luminol sonochemiluminescence reaction system (Figure 2a) and for approximately two seconds in the 5 ml luminol reaction system (Figure 2b), after which the spatial extent of cavitation remained relatively stable through the remaining exposure period.

Effect of NLFUD combined with antiseptic solutions against S. aureus biofilms on titanium surfaces ex vivo

Compared to antiseptic solutions alone (0.9% saline, 3% H2O2, or 0.5% PI), ten seconds of NLFUD pretreatment followed by these solutions significantly diminished residual live and dead biofilm coverage to < 1% (all p < 0.01, Figure 3). SEM results demonstrated that NLFUD pretreatment plus antiseptic solutions effectively disrupted biofilm structures (Figures 3b, 3e, 3h, and 3k). NLFUD pretreatment with application of 3% H2O2 + 0.5% PI did not reduce live biofilm residuals but notably decreased dead biofilm coverage (Figures 3j and 3l). Without NLFUD pretreatment, the 3% H2O2 + 0.5% PI group exhibited the lowest residual coverage for live and dead biofilms (Supplementary Figure b). Following NLFUD pretreatment, live biofilm residuals varied among groups, but all remained below 1%, with no significant differences in dead biofilm coverage (Supplementary Figure b). In biofilm regrowth experiments, the following treatments completely prevented biofilm regrowth: 1) ten seconds of NLFUD followed by 0.5% PI; 2) 3% H2O2 combined with 0.5% PI; and 3) ten seconds of NLFUD followed by 3% H2O2 combined with 0.5% PI (Supplementary Figure c).

Effect of NLFUD combined with antiseptic solutions against S. aureus biofilms on implants in vivo

Bacterial count detection exhibited effective biofilm disruption, with the median bacterial count in the ultrasonic lysis solution from the mechanical debridement and NLFUD group (DU) reaching 2,665 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml, significantly higher than the 195 CFU/ml observed in the saline immersion solution from the control group (mechanical debridement only, CON) (p = 0.002, Figure 4a). SEM analysis corroborated these findings, indicating that on day 14, biofilm structures on titanium implant surfaces in the DU group and the mechanical debridement, NLFUD, and chemical debridement group (DUC) had essentially disappeared, with only scattered S. aureus monomers detected, whereas intact biofilm structures containing embedded S. aureus persisted on implant surfaces in the CON and DC groups (Figure 4b). On day 28, biofilm structures had reformed on titanium implant surfaces across all groups, with S. aureus detectable among the regenerated biofilms (Figure 4c).

Discussion

Conventional mechanical debridement aims to eliminate infected tissues and expose pathogens for subsequent chemical treatment. Current techniques fail to adequately expose biofilm-embedded pathogens, thereby compromising the efficacy of debridement surgery. This study employed low-frequency ultrasound cavitation effects to disrupt potential biofilms in the joint cavity, thereby releasing more free-floating bacteria for exposure to antiseptic solutions. Ex vivo and in vivo experiments further confirmed the efficacy of NLFUD in biofilm disruption and its synergistic effects with antiseptic solutions. Furthermore, we verified that NLFUD exhibited no adverse effects on systemic and local safety indicators in rats. This study offers a promising strategy for debridement procedures in the treatment of PJI.

Conventional low-frequency ultrasound technology primarily utilizes the cavitation effects of water bath ultrasound systems to disrupt biofilms on the surface of retrieved prostheses, thereby enhancing pathogen detection rates in PJI diagnosis.14 Building on diagnostic protocols using water-bath ultrasonication, we adapted a sterilizable probe sonication platform for intraoperative use and therapeutic testing. NLFUD was operated at 25 kHz, within the commonly reported 25 to 40 kHz biofilm-disruptive band in the diagnosis of PJI.27,28 Unlike conventional water-bath ultrasound systems, which cavitate primarily at the basin floor and act on surfaces facing the emitter, the probe of NLFUD concentrates forward-directed cavitation at its tip, enabling systematic insonation of complex implant geometries and recesses and reducing blind spots.29 By decoupling the power generator from an independently sterilizable probe/transducer module, the system can be used within the sterile field of the operating theatre – unlike integrated water-bath units, which cannot be sterilized as a whole.12,15,16 We previously demonstrated higher intraoperative pathogen recovery with the same sterilizable, handheld NLFUD device, improving recovery from 69.40% to 87.88%, outperforming conventional water-bath sonication in PJI.15 Here, we extend its use to therapeutic debridement and evaluate debridement efficacy in preclinical models. In therapeutic applications, low-frequency ultrasound (20 to 40 kHz) technology is extensively used in wound debridement,30 with studies demonstrating that ultrasonic debridement achieves higher biofilm removal rates compared to conventional mechanical debridement.31 Therapeutic ultrasound can be categorized by application method into contact and non-contact types and by frequency, into low-frequency and high-frequency types. Although contact ultrasound is frequently employed for necrotic tissue removal, achieving comprehensive coverage within complex joint cavities is difficult, and direct contact may cause mechanical injury.30 High-frequency ultrasound generates weaker cavitation effects than low-frequency ultrasound.32 NLFUD uses cavitation effects generated by 25 kHz ultrasound with a 3 mm diameter probe in saline within the surgical area, facilitating comprehensive coverage of all surfaces within complex joint cavities and effectively disrupting biofilm structures, prevents mechanical injury, and offers a safer and more effective strategy for PJI debridement.

Chemical debridement uses irrigation with 3% H2O2 and 0.5% PI in the surgical site. Research indicates that the effective eradication of S. aureus by H2O2 necessitates extended exposure,33 whereas the bactericidal efficacy of PI depends on the release of active iodine.34 The complex structure of biofilms significantly inhibits antiseptic solution activity,35 and no single solution can achieve complete bactericidal effects.36,37 We found that H2O2 and PI alone were ineffective in eradicating bacteria within biofilms; however, the combination of H2O2 and PI was beneficial in eliminating all bacteria within the biofilm, consistent with the findings of a recent study.36 This might be related to H₂O₂ initially compromising biofilm structure through the generation of oxygen bubbles by bacterial catalase,38,39 thereby facilitating subsequent PI to enhance its bactericidal efficacy. This synergistic mechanism has been validated by our SEM observations and bacterial viability assays (Figure 3, Supplementary Material). Similarly, we found that sequential application of NLFUD with PI or the combination of H2O2 and PI effectively eradicated residual live bacteria in biofilms ex vivo. This increase in efficacy is due to NLFUD facilitating more efficient biofilm disruption than H2O2 alone by SEM (Figure 3). In vivo experiments further confirmed this biofilm-disrupting capability, as NLFUD significantly increased bacterial load in surgical site fluid from 195 to 2,665 CFU/ml, signifying effective biofilm disruption. Consequently, based on the enhanced biofilm-disrupting efficacy of NLFUD, we assert that efficiently disrupting biofilm structure before applying effective antiseptic solutions is an essential strategy for achieving complete biofilm eradication.

This study demonstrated that ultrasound alone or in combination with chemical debridement (H2O2 + PI) effectively disrupted biofilms in vivo at day 14; nevertheless, bacterial remnants remained on prosthetic surfaces, leading to biofilm reconstruction on day 28 (Figure 4). This finding may be attributed to two main factors. First, bacteria may remain viable within host cells or in the canaliculi of infected bone.7,40 Second, the antimicrobial efficacy of H2O2 and PI might be attenuated in vivo by physiological conditions.41,42 These findings emphasize that successful PJI treatment requires effective biofilm disruption through debridement procedures and antibiotic management to eliminate intracellular bacteria and prevent biofilm recurrence.43-45

Beyond its therapeutic efficacy in disrupting biofilms, the safety of NLFUD treatment is important. In a study by White et al,46 patients with chronic venous leg ulcers were treated using NLFUD (thrice weekly for eight weeks) and it was found that this technology did not affect wound healing or patients' quality of life, nor did it increase wound-related adverse events. However, Wiegand et al47 reported that NLFUD can inhibit proinflammatory cytokine expression and enhance wound healing. Herein, NLFUD was used only once during debridement surgery for PJI, indicating a lower application intensity compared to the repeated treatments for chronic ulcers. Given the reduced exposure, a single application exhibited no significant differences in local inflammatory markers (TNF-α and IL-10 in peri-implant bone, trabecular bone, and synovium) or systemic indicators (WBC count and neutrophil percentage in CBC) across the groups, indicating no increased inflammatory response, which may support a favourable environment for tissue repair. Histological examination confirmed no organ damage. Furthermore, with the validated safety of 3% H2O2 and 0.5% PI in PJI,48 our results revealed that NLFUD and chemical debridement alone or combined do not compromise systemic safety, angiogenesis, inflammatory response, or periprosthetic osseointegration.

This study has some limitations. First, the limited rat blood sample hindered the detection of serum inflammatory markers; future studies should employ larger animal models to evaluate these markers. Second, our in vivo model employed a single bacterial strain, potentially constraining clinical application. Third, since this study specifically aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of NLFUD combined with chemical debridement, antibiotic treatment was deliberately excluded, leading to suboptimal infection control across all groups. Future investigations should evaluate the long-term efficacy of combining antibiotic therapy with NLFUD and chemical debridement for comprehensive PJI treatment. Additionally, future studies could benefit from time-matched comparative analyses of different antiseptic solutions to better optimize exposure durations and concentrations for clinical application. Furthermore, as this study was conducted exclusively ex vivo and in animal models, clinical trials are essential to validate these findings in human patients.

In conclusion, NLFUD significantly disrupts S. aureus biofilm structure through cavitation effects and enhances sequential antiseptic efficacy ex vivo and in vivo. This integrated approach has demonstrated safety in animal trials. Future studies should evaluate the addition of long-term antibiotic treatment to the NLFUD-antiseptic combination for comprehensive PJI management.
