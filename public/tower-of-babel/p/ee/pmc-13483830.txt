Prehospital data in the dark: a scoping review of the widening gap between EMS demand and the evidence to meet it
Authors: Raied Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrehospital emergency medical services generate data at unprecedented scale, yet the scientific evidence base underpinning this field remains thin relative to operational activity. This scoping review examines the challenges of prehospital data utilisation and research conduct, explores methodological limitations of the existing literature, considers the challenge of linking prehospital data to in-hospital outcomes, and discuss the economic and structural case for investment in prehospital research capacity.MethodsA structured literature search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE combining MeSH terms and free-text keywords across three conceptual domains: prehospital EMS systems, electronic health records and data infrastructure, and health services research capacity and evidence generation. The search was updated in May 2025. An integrated EMS systems audit examined publicly available annual reports and operational data from 15 EMS systems selected randomly to explore research output reporting practices within EMS systems.ResultsPrehospital research is constrained by the operational environment, ethical and governance complexity, fragmented data systems, absence of linkage infrastructure, inadequate funding mechanisms that remains underdeveloped in most services. The updated search retrieved 1,886 records; after duplicate removal and title/abstract screening, 11 peer-reviewed studies meeting the eligibility criteria were included. None of the 15 EMS systems audited reported research output as a formal key performance indicator, with the sole exception of Denmark. Publications per 100,000 patient contacts ranged from 3.65 in the USA to 0.40 in Saudi Arabia.ConclusionAddressing the prehospital evidence gap requires coordinated investment at organisational, national, and international levels in the technical, institutional, and cultural conditions that enable scientific research. The cost of operating without an adequate evidence base is reflected in the resource allocation decisions made by services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-026-01293-z.Keywords: Emergency medical services, Prehospital research, Prehospital data, Data governance, Research capacity, Evidence-based practice, Data linkage

Introduction

Every year, emergency medical services respond to hundreds of millions of calls worldwide, and that number is rising [1]. Across high, middle, and low-income countries alike, demand for prehospital care is growing faster than the systems designed to meet it, driven by ageing populations, rising chronic disease burden, and increasing public reliance on emergency services as the default entry point into healthcare [1, 2]. Each of these encounters generates data, including dispatch timestamps, clinical observations, vital signs, interventions, and outcomes, a vast and largely untapped reservoir of real-world information about some of the most acute and time-critical events. The annual number of publications indexed in PubMed increased substantially, from 419 in 2001 to 1,788 in 2020, this output remains remarkably thin relative to the scale of the development in the field [3]. Despite increasing demand, the evidence base supporting prehospital care continues to lag behind other medical disciplines. For example, the Saudi Red Crescent Authority (SRCA), one of the largest EMS providers in the Middle East, responds to an average of approximately 1.5 million patients annually, yet its annual report recorded only six peer-reviewed publications in 2023, a figure that appeared without context, target, or follow-up, and was absent from both preceding and subsequent reports [4]. This ratio alone illustrates the scale of the disconnect between operational activity and research output. The prehospital environment is inherently ill-suited for traditional clinical research due to its unpredictable nature. Paramedics must deliver immediate care in high-pressure, uncontrolled settings, making it exceptionally difficult to anticipate clinical scenarios or dedicate time to accurate data collection. To appreciate the scale of this disparity, it is instructive to compare prehospital research output against a field that has long commanded institutional attention and research investment. In 2022, approximately 20 million new cancer cases were diagnosed worldwide [5]. That same year, oncology journals indexed in the Web of Science produced an estimated 60,000 publications, equivalent to approximately 300 publications per 100,000 new cancer cases [6]. Admittedly, this parallel is imperfect; whereas oncology focuses on a chronic, heterogeneous disease requiring multidisciplinary laboratory, clinical, and epidemiological investigation, the EMS domain manages acute, transient, and operationally complex encounters. However, this distinction underscores the critical nature of the argument. Prehospital medicine does not possess less clinical significance than oncology; rather, it represents the primary portal through which acute conditions initially present to the healthcare continuum. Consequently, the existing literature deficit is consequence of a deficient structural infrastructure necessary to facilitate rigorous clinical inquiry.

This scoping review examines the challenges of prehospital data access and research conduct, explores the limitations that constrain the existing literature, and considers the specific challenge of utilising prehospital data for research. It also briefly explores the economic dimension of rising EMS costs, addressing not only the cost of conducting research, but the arguably greater cost of not conducting it.

To address these potential barriers, this scoping review aims to map the structural, institutional, and methodological factors contributing to the disparity between EMS operational volume and research output. Furthermore, it examines proposed or implemented strategies to mitigate these challenges and synthesizes how major international EMS systems currently integrate and report research output within their performance frameworks.

Methods

Study design and framework

This scoping review was conducted alongside an integrated EMS systems audit, following the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines [7]. A systematic search strategy was developed and executed in PubMed/MEDLINE to identify relevant peer-reviewed literature. The search strategy was structured using the PEO (Population, Exposure, Outcome) framework, which is the recommended variant of PICO for observational and health services infrastructure research. The conceptual domains were: (P) prehospital emergency medical services; (E) electronic health records, data repositories, and medical record linkage systems; and (O) research capacity, evidence generation, performance frameworks, and data governance. The search utilised a combination of controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH]) and free-text keywords restricted to title and abstract fields [tiab], with truncation wildcards (*) to capture word variants. Boolean operators were used to establish explicit relationships. The comprehensive PubMed search strategy, executed in May 2025, is provided in full in the Supplementary Material.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Literature was considered eligible for inclusion if it addressed the intersection of prehospital data and its translation into research evidence, public health policy, or organisational performance frameworks. Primary focus was placed on articles discussing data linkage challenges, data governance, information system silos, EMS data infrastructure, quality registries, study design in prehospital research, or institutional barriers to research output within an international context. Reference lists of included sources were hand-searched and grey literature was searched targeting EMS annual reports and governmental health system documents.

For the EMS systems audit, fifteen systems were selected randomly. For each system, the following items were extracted from publicly available sources: annual incident or patient contact volume; estimated annual cost where reported; whether research output featured as a key performance indicator in the most recent annual report or strategic plan; estimated annual research publications where determinable; and a calculated rate of publications per 100,000 patient contacts. Data charting was performed by the primary author. Where data were unavailable, cells were recorded as ‘not reported’ rather than estimated.

Results

The literature search retrieved 1,886 records from PubMed. After removal of one duplicate (n = 1,885), titles and abstracts were screened by the author, yielding 339 records for full-text assessment. Of the 153 full-text articles evaluated, 142 were excluded. The vast majority evaluated localized therapeutic and clinical trial interventions without mentioning the data infrastructure accessibility or challenges (n = 99). A further 24 studies were limited to in-hospital data only, while 11 explored exceptional, non-routine emergency settings such as tactical or disaster frameworks. Finally, 8 papers were excluded due to structural format constraints, being restricted to conferences abstract-only publications or editorial correspondence. Following full-text review, 11 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included (Figure S1 — PRISMA flow diagram). The volume of data generated by EMS systems is substantial. In England alone, NHS ambulance services respond to over ten million incidents annually [9]. In Australia, ambulance service organisations attended 4.5 million incidents in 2024-25 at a total cost of AUD 6.3 billion [10]. The Scottish Ambulance Service serves a population of over five million people with more than 5,000 staff [12]. These figures represent not only the scale of operational activity but the scale of routinely collected data that is generated daily, yet whose research potential remains largely unrecognized.

This is not to imply that operational data is automatically suitable for research. Prehospital data quality is frequently compromised by incomplete documentation, inconsistent data entry across crews and shifts, and the absence of standardised terminologies, a problem further complicated in countries where multiple EMS providers operate entirely separate recording systems within the same national boundary [24, 25]. The same clinical event may be defined, measured, and coded differently across services, severely limiting both the research utility of individual datasets and the possibility of meaningful international comparison. Cantwell et al., in a study of over 1.2 million ambulance records from Ambulance Victoria in Australia, demonstrated that no standard method existed for coding paramedic assessment data, with each service using locally devised systems that precluded reliable cross-service or cross-sector comparison [26]. There are notable exceptions: the adoption of the Utstein template for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest has enabled meaningful international comparison of resuscitation outcomes across services that would otherwise be impossible to reconcile [27]. The gap in data standardisation also affects performance data: O’Cathain et al. demonstrated significant unexplained variation in non-transport rates across English ambulance services, attributable in part to differences in how routine operational data are collected and coded [28]. Similarly, Byrsell et al.’s ten-year analysis of Swedish emergency medical dispatch centres highlighted that registry-based data linkage between dispatch records and cardiac arrest outcomes is achievable at national scale but requires deliberate infrastructural investment [29]. However, cardiac arrest remains one of the few prehospital conditions for which such consensus exists. The vast majority of emergency presentations continue to be recorded in ways that are inconsistent, locally defined, and resistant to aggregation.

Discussion

Challenges in conducting research in the prehospital environment

The environment itself

Unlike the controlled conditions of a clinical trial ward, the prehospital setting is inherently unpredictable, and the primary obligation is always immediate clinical care. Researchers have no direct access to or control over the data collection process, and attaching an independent observer to every ambulance crew is simply not feasible [17]. Prospective data collection by paramedics is subject to the competing priorities of clinical care, is vulnerable to protocol deviation, and places demands on time that many services are not resourced to support [24]. Retrospective extraction from operational records is limited by whatever was recorded initially, which in the prehospital setting is frequently incomplete.

Informed consent and ethical complexity

Obtaining informed consent from patients in the prehospital environment is one of the most intractable challenges in the field. Many of the patient groups most relevant to prehospital research are precisely those from whom consent cannot be obtained at the time of the encounter [24]. In the United States, the exception from informed consent provisions under 21 CFR 50.24 represents the most formally codified regulatory pathway for emergency research, yet it imposes mandatory community consultation and public disclosure requirements that add cost, complexity, and delay to study initiation [25]. These frameworks vary considerably across jurisdictions, and the absence of internationally harmonised standards remains a persistent obstacle to multicentre prehospital studies. Ethics approval in the prehospital context is further complicated by the involvement of multiple institutions, each with different requirements, timelines, and interpretations of acceptable risk [24].

Funding and institutional constraints

Lerner et al. identified six core constraints on prehospital clinical research: funding, ethics approval, data collection, protocol training and compliance, randomisation and blinding, and the collection of outcome data from receiving hospitals [24]. Funding was consistently identified as the most fundamental, as prehospital care has historically been poorly positioned to compete against hospital-based specialties with established academic medical centres. A mixed-methods consensus study of EMS stakeholders in the Netherlands identified funding and organisational culture as central themes, and further found that data registration, data sharing, legal frameworks, and research culture were the four dominant barriers to EMS research productivity [30], the Canadian national EMS research agenda reported identical priorities [13], and Australia convened a national research convention reaching similar conclusions [11]. The convergence of these findings across systems with markedly different structures suggests that the funding challenge is a structural feature of how prehospital care is positioned within health systems globally.

The paramedic as researcher: culture and capacity

EMS organisations are operationally rather than academically oriented, and research is rarely embedded in their governance structures or workforce expectations. Evidence from a systematic review of paramedics as researchers found that lack of protected time, absence of research training pathways, and limited access to academic mentorship were consistently identified as structural constraints on research engagement [31]. A cross-sectional study of paramedics attending a dedicated prehospital research conference in Switzerland found that willingness to engage in research increased significantly following the event, suggesting that targeted educational interventions may shift professional attitudes toward evidence generation [32].

Data accessibility and governance challenges

Ownership, fragmentation, and access

Prehospital data exists in a complex institutional landscape. Depending on the jurisdiction, ambulance services may operate as part of a ministry of health, as independent public agencies, or as components of fire and rescue services, and they may themselves be public, private, or hybrid organisations. This structural variation directly shapes the legal frameworks governing data ownership, the requirements for data sharing, and the institutional priorities assigned to research. In systems where ambulance services are contracted out, the question of who owns the operational data generated by a private provider delivering a publicly funded service is not always straightforward.

Navigating the prehospital data acquisition pipeline presents significant operational challenges for investigators. The absence of centralized, standardized data-access protocols serves as a primary institutional constraint; consequently, data retrieval often relies on idiosyncratic professional networks and localized stakeholder alignment rather than formalized, reproducible procedures [24, 33]. Furthermore, the intersection of multi-layered data governance frameworks—encompassing statutory data protection legislation, and proprietary service-level policies—introduces extensive regulatory friction, often extending the timeline from study conceptualization to data extraction by several months or years [24].

Research as a missing performance indicator

The absence of research output as a key performance indicator in EMS strategic plans and annual reports emerges as a consistent pattern across the systems examined in this review. Analysis of publicly available annual reports and strategic documents from EMS systems across North America [3, 8, 13], Europe [9, 12, 15, 16, 18], Australasia [10, 14], the Middle East [4, 21, 34], and Africa [22, 23] reveals that standard performance indicators focus almost exclusively on operational metrics: response times, call answering speeds, cardiac arrest survival rates, and conveyance rates. Research publications, academic partnerships, and contributions to the evidence base do not appear as measured or reported outcomes in any of the systems reviewed, with the sole exception of Denmark, where research is structurally embedded within the national quality framework for prehospital care (Table 1) [18]. In Africa specifically, Mould-Millman et al. found that only 3 of 25 identified EMS systems conducted any research activity, and the absence of standardised data collection systems was identified as a fundamental barrier to generating evidence relevant to the African prehospital context [22].

Methodological challenges in prehospital research

Even setting aside the challenges of conducting research in the prehospital environment, the methodology of existing prehospital research presents its own limitations. Cavanagh et al.’s review of paramedicine research identified that 40% of published studies used observational designs, with a further 28% being editorials or commentary [35]. Randomised controlled trials represented only 3% of the literature. This profile was similarly documented in a foundational ten-year analysis by Brice et al., who found that case series accounted for 44% of out-of-hospital research designs from 1985 to 1994, with randomised trials comprising only 15%, and outcomes beyond mortality rarely measured [36]. Spaite et al., through the Emergency Medical Services Outcomes Project (EMSOP), established the conceptual and methodological framework underpinning outcomes research in EMS, identifying that the absence of robust databases and the operational complexity of the prehospital environment were fundamental barriers to generating rigorous evidence [37]. These structural challenges have persisted for decades. This profile reflects in part the genuine difficulty of conducting experimental research in the prehospital setting, but it also reflects a philosophical and methodological conservatism that limits the questions that researchers can ask and answer.

McManamny et al. conducted a systematic review of mixed methods in prehospital research, updating an earlier review from 2014 [38]. Whilst the use of mixed methods had grown substantially over the intervening decade, the review found that quality was frequently compromised by a failure to justify the mixed methods approach, to describe data integration clearly, or to articulate the philosophical underpinnings of the design. Only 25% of included studies clearly justified why a mixed methods approach was necessary, and fewer than 20% described insights gained from integrating the qualitative and quantitative components.

The review by Satchell et al. on research involving family members and bystanders in paramedicine found a near-universal absence of theoretical discussion, a strong geographic bias toward Western and Nordic settings, and almost no genuine engagement of service users in the research process [39]. Family members and bystanders were studied as subjects rather than partners, reflecting both the emergency context and a broader cultural conservatism within paramedicine research about who gets to shape the research agenda.

Data linkage: the missing connection between prehospital and hospital care

Why linkage matters

The outcomes of prehospital care are determined not only by the interventions delivered before hospital arrival but by what follows, yet in most countries these two phases of care are recorded in entirely separate and unconnected data systems. Clinical decisions made by paramedics in the field shape outcomes that unfold over hours, days, and weeks in hospital settings that have no visibility of what preceded patient arrival. Equally, post-handover outcomes remain largely inaccessible to prehospital clinicians, as data systems recording in-hospital care are, in most countries, structurally disconnected from those recording the ambulance response. This bidirectional absence of information represents a fundamental limitation on the continuity of both care and evidence.

Data linkage, the process of connecting prehospital records to hospital admission, treatment, and outcome data using patient identifiers, is the technical solution to this problem and the methodological foundation of the most clinically meaningful research questions in prehospital care. The collection of in-hospital care and outcomes data has been identified as one of the key challenges in prehospital research [24]. Even in studies that successfully recruit and collect prehospital data, the inability to follow patients through the hospital stay creates a systematic truncation of outcomes that biases findings and limits clinical applicability.

The Danish model: a blueprint for linked prehospital data

The most developed example of national prehospital data infrastructure is Denmark’s Quality Database for Prehospital Emergency Medical Services (QEMS), established in 2014 [18]. The QEMS captures data on approximately 300,000 to 350,000 patient contacts annually from all five Danish regions, using standardised quality indicators covering emergency response timelines, prehospital interventions, and clinical outcomes for specific emergency conditions including cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, and stroke. This infrastructure has enabled registry-based studies including evaluation of the Danish Helicopter EMS database, which demonstrated that with deliberate database design and on-going quality assurance, near-complete data capture and high data completeness are achievable in prehospital systems [19].

The distinguishing feature of QEMS is its linkage architecture. Every Danish citizen is assigned a unique ten-digit civil registration number that is used universally across all healthcare records and national registries. This single identifier enables unambiguous linkage between the QEMS and hospital admission records, diagnostic registries, vital status data, and cause of death records, providing complete patient follow-up from the emergency call to hospital discharge and beyond [18]. Establishing QEMS required a deliberate national policy commitment, a working group of regional medical directors and administrative professionals, a formal steering committee with clinical and epidemiological expertise, and sustained investment in electronic patient record infrastructure across all EMS providers [18]. This level of coordinated institutional effort is not commonly found in prehospital systems, but the Danish experience demonstrates that it is achievable and that the research returns on that investment are substantial.

The economic argument: the cost of not doing research

The challenges described in this review are frequently framed as practical constraints: EMS systems simply do not have the resources, time, or infrastructure to invest in research. This framing, however, inverts the logic and ignores the mounting pressure of rising demand. Without research evidence, services may be unable to distinguish which elements of their practice deliver value from which do not, and resource allocation decisions may default to tradition, habit, and operational convenience rather than evidence.

The costs of prehospital care are substantial and rising. In Australia, ambulance service expenditure reached AUD 6.3 billion in 2024-25 [10]. In England, NHS ambulance services handle over ten million incidents annually, representing a significant and growing proportion of the NHS urgent and emergency care budget [9]. A substantial proportion of these costs are associated with responses, conveyances, and interventions whose effectiveness has never been rigorously evaluated. Non-conveyance and hear-and-treat protocols represent areas where the absence of evidence has direct economic consequences. Without linked outcome data showing what happens to patients who are not conveyed, clinicians may remain risk-averse, defaulting to transport even where it may not be necessary or beneficial.

Community paramedicine illustrates a similar dynamic. A growing body of literature suggests that community paramedicine programmes may reduce emergency department attendance and hospital admissions among high-need populations, though as Cavanagh et al. note, the evidence base remains limited in both volume and rigour, and the research capacity needed to build it is not currently in place in most systems [35]. The investment case for prehospital research is therefore not simply about improving clinical knowledge. It is about enabling a potential shift from a reactive, transport-based model of EMS to a more proactive, outcomes-focused approach, a transition that cannot be pursued safely or confidently without a more robust evidence base.

Promising approaches and international solutions

National databases and research registries

At the national level, the Danish QEMS, discussed in Sect. 7.2, represents a well-documented example of deliberately constructed national prehospital data infrastructure that has demonstrated the feasibility of linking prehospital records to in-hospital outcomes at population scale [18]. The conditions that enabled its development, namely national policy commitment, standardised electronic patient records, and a universal patient identifier, remain absent in the majority of EMS systems examined in this review.

At the service level, Cantwell et al. demonstrated that ICD-10 classification could be applied retrospectively to paramedic assessment data, producing more clinically detailed case definitions than point-of-call dispatch data alone [26]. The authors proposed that this method could enable comparison across ambulance services and with broader population health datasets. However, more than a decade after publication, this approach has not been widely adopted, and no standardised framework for its implementation across services has emerged. The challenge of data access is also present at the point of care: O’Connor et al. demonstrated that EMS-clinician access to hospital electronic health records via smartphone applications is both feasible and associated with high adoption rates, suggesting that bidirectional data connectivity between prehospital and hospital systems is technically achievable, though implementation remains rare [40]. Similarly, Mears et al. documented the development of the North Carolina EMS Data System, one of the most comprehensively integrated statewide prehospital data systems, showing that linkage of prehospital records to trauma registries, stroke registries, and hospital discharge data is feasible when supported by legislative mandate, standardised data elements, and sustained institutional investment [41].

At the international level, the Utstein template for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest represents the most extensively evaluated example of consensus-based data standardisation in the prehospital setting [27, 42]. Despite proposals to extend the Utstein principle to other time-critical conditions including major trauma, stroke, and sepsis, no equivalent consensus framework has been established for any other prehospital condition.

These examples represent a fraction of the potential approaches available. Rapid advances in data science, machine learning, and interoperability standards offer considerable untapped potential, from natural language processing applied to free-text clinical records, to federated learning frameworks that enable multi-site analysis without centralised data sharing, to application programming interfaces facilitating real-time linkage between prehospital and in-hospital systems. The field has yet to systematically engage with these methodological developments, and the literature describing their application in the prehospital context remains nascent. Realising this potential will require not only technical innovation but the governance frameworks, research culture, and institutional investment that the preceding sections have identified as persistently absent.

The research paramedic role

One of the most promising structural responses to the capacity problem in prehospital research is the development of dedicated research paramedic roles, clinicians with protected time and specific training to coordinate research activity within EMS services. These individuals serve as a critical interface between academic researchers and operational EMS providers, combining familiarity with the clinical environment, professional credibility among colleagues, and the research competencies required to collect, manage, and quality-assure data in ways that operational paramedics on shift cannot [31]. The research paramedic model has been particularly documented in the United Kingdom, where it has been associated with improved recruitment rates in prehospital trials, reduced protocol deviations, and the gradual building of research capacity within services. Evidence from a systematic review of paramedic perspectives on research engagement suggests that training opportunities were consistently valued when they were structured, supported, and clearly connected to patient benefit [31].

Implications and recommendations

The evidence synthesised in this review points toward a set of recommendations directed at different actors in the prehospital research ecosystem.

For EMS organisations and service leaders

Research output, measured as publications, academic partnerships, trial participation, and data access agreements, should be incorporated as a formal key performance indicator in EMS strategic plans and continuous assessment and development frameworks. This structural change would shift the institutional incentive structure in a direction that currently favours operational performance at the exclusion of knowledge generation. Services should also invest in dedicated research paramedic roles with protected time, dedicated funding, adequate training, and meaningful career pathways. The structural factors that facilitate research engagement, including professional development opportunities, quality improvement frameworks, provider motivation, and managerial support, are amenable to deliberate policy intervention and should inform the design of research capacity-building programmes within EMS organisations.

For policymakers and health system planners

The development of national prehospital data infrastructure, encompassing standardised electronic patient records, agreed data dictionaries, and linkage architecture connecting prehospital and hospital records, should be treated as a health system priority rather than a research luxury. The Danish QEMS experience demonstrates that this investment is achievable and that its returns extend well beyond academic research to include quality improvement, service planning, and sustainable cost management. Legal and governance frameworks for data access and linkage need to be clarified and streamlined, with transparent pathways for researchers to access prehospital data. Dedicated funding mechanisms specific to prehospital care research should be established, recognising that the field has historically been unable to compete equitably with hospital-based specialties for generic research funding streams. The economic argument for this investment is not only about the cost of conducting research; it is about the arguably greater and less visible cost of not conducting it, as EMS systems continue to allocate substantial resources to responses, conveyances, and interventions whose effectiveness remains unevaluated.

For researchers, academic institutions, and paramedic educators

The prehospital research community should broaden both its topical and methodological focus. Whilst observational designs have made important contributions to the existing literature, a more diverse methodological repertoire is needed to address questions about complex interventions, patient experience, workforce behaviour, and organisational practice, including greater use of qualitative and mixed methods approaches applied with appropriate philosophical rigour and clear justification. Collaboration with researchers from adjacent disciplines should be actively pursued to bring methodological diversity and interdisciplinary perspectives to a field that has historically operated within relatively narrow academic boundaries. Research agendas should be developed jointly between academic institutions and EMS services, ensuring that the questions being asked reflect the priorities of services, clinicians, and patients rather than those of external investigators alone. Research literacy, including an understanding of why research matters, how evidence is generated and evaluated, and what role paramedics play in that process, should be a core component of pre-registration paramedic education. Evidence suggests that inadequate organisational support and limited exposure to research during training are among the primary constraints on paramedic research engagement. Addressing this deficit at the foundational level of professional education offers one of the most sustainable pathways toward a workforce that is equipped and motivated to contribute to evidence generation throughout its career.

Strengths and limitations

This review addresses a topic that has received limited systematic attention, combining a broad synthesis of the academic literature with an original cross-national audit of EMS operational and reporting data across 15 systems. The dual methodological approach allows structural and institutional patterns to be identified and contextualized in ways that neither component could achieve independently.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the search was limited to PubMed/MEDLINE; while this database provides the most comprehensive coverage of the relevant MeSH vocabulary used in the search strategy, relevant studies indexed only in CINAHL, Embase, or Scopus may not be captured. Second, the literature screening and data charting were conducted by a single reviewer, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. This approach introduces the potential for selection bias, along with the potential for confirmation bias given the author’s national system being among those audited. To mitigate these risks, strict eligibility criteria and a standardized extraction form were utilized; however, the 11 included studies serve as an illustrative mapping of the available evidence rather than an exhaustive systematic index. Third, the EMS systems selected for the audit do not represent every global model; they were chosen based on the availability of public data, which introduces selection bias and means they may not reflect all international frameworks. Finally, the audit relies entirely on publicly available documents and annual reports that vary in how recently they were published, meaning internal service data or unpublished research outputs are not captured.

Conclusion

While prehospital emergency care generates an unprecedented volume of clinical and operational data, the structural informatics infrastructure required to translate these inputs into actionable evidence remains globally deficient. The systemic challenges documented across this scoping review, encompassing operational constraints, data governance barriers, methodological limitations, and cross-institutional linkage fractures, are substantial but resolvable through targeted health services investment. The consequences of continued infrastructure deficits are tangible, forcing emergency services to execute high-stakes daily resource allocations in an evidence vacuum. Mitigating this risk requires a coordinated, multi-level commitment to building the technical, institutional, and economic frameworks necessary for systematic evidence generation. Ultimately, bridging this information governance fracture is essential if EMS systems are to evolve into learning organizations capable of continuously optimizing clinical safety, operational efficiency, and overall healthcare quality.

Furthermore, the intersection of multi-layered data governance frameworks—encompassing statutory data protection legislation, and proprietary service-level policies—introduces extensive regulatory friction, often extending the timeline from study conceptualization to data extraction by several months or years [24].

Research as a missing performance indicator

The absence of research output as a key performance indicator in EMS strategic plans and annual reports emerges as a consistent pattern across the systems examined in this review. Analysis of publicly available annual reports and strategic documents from EMS systems across North America [3, 8, 13], Europe [9, 12, 15, 16, 18], Australasia [10, 14], the Middle East [4, 21, 34], and Africa [22, 23] reveals that standard performance indicators focus almost exclusively on operational metrics: response times, call answering speeds, cardiac arrest survival rates, and conveyance rates. Research publications, academic partnerships, and contributions to the evidence base do not appear as measured or reported outcomes in any of the systems reviewed, with the sole exception of Denmark, where research is structurally embedded within the national quality framework for prehospital care (Table 1) [18]. In Africa specifically, Mould-Millman et al. found that only 3 of 25 identified EMS systems conducted any research activity, and the absence of standardised data collection systems was identified as a fundamental barrier to generating evidence relevant to the African prehospital context [22].
