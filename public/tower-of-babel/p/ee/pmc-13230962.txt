An in vivo fitness gene of Toxoplasma, MIC11, is essential for PLP1-mediated egress from host cells
Authors: Yuta Tachibana, Xue Gu, Miwa Sasai, Hidetaka Kosako, Eizo Takashima, Daron M Standley, Vern B Carruthers, Dominique Soldati-Favre, Masahiro Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfter invasion and replication, intracellular pathogens must egress from infected host cells. Toxoplasma gondii facilitates this process by permeabilizing host cells through induced secretion of perforin-like protein 1 (PLP1). However, the precise mechanism of host cell permeabilization remains enigmatic. Here, we identify the secretory microneme protein MIC11 as a key factor for membrane disruption. A CRISPR-based in vivo screen identifies MIC11 as the top in vivo fitness-conferring gene. Deletion of MIC11 results in severe defects in membrane rupture and egress. Scanning mutagenesis identifies functional motifs in MIC11, and mechanistic analyses support an association between MIC11 and PLP1, suggesting that MIC11 is involved in PLP1-dependent membrane disruption. Moreover, the merozoite-specific paralogue MIC22 functionally complements MIC11 deletion, suggesting a conserved mechanism of egress in the feline-restricted stages of T. gondii. Collectively, the discovery of MIC11 advances our understanding of how parasites disrupt host cells to facilitate rapid egress and successful dissemination.Subject terms: Parasite biology, Infection

Introduction

Intracellular bacteria and protozoa utilize various strategies to exit infected cells, a process known as egress1. Egress is a critical step for the obligate intracellular apicomplexan parasites, such as Toxoplasma gondii and Plasmodium falciparum, which cause human disease worldwide and represent a major global health problem2. In apicomplexans, this process relies on a dynamic interplay between host and parasite factors and typically leads to host cell lysis, resulting in detrimental effects for the host.

In T. gondii, egress is initiated by a signaling cascade that triggers microneme exocytosis, ultimately leading to disruption of the vacuolar membrane surrounding parasites3,4. Recent studies have markedly advanced our understanding of the key regulators. Guanylate cyclase (GC)5,6 and protein kinase G (PKG)7 drive a signaling cascade that branches into two major pathways: calcium mobilization, which activates calcium-dependent protein kinases 1 and 3 (CDPK1 and CDPK3)8–11 to phosphorylate many substrate proteins involved in microneme exocytosis, and lipid signaling involving diacylglycerol kinase 1 (DGK1) and acylated pleckstrin-homology domain-containing protein (APH)12, which also facilitates microneme secretion. In addition to responding to extrinsic signals, the parasite generates intrinsic signals via the dense granule protein diacylglycerol kinase 2 (DGK2) to control natural egress13.

Upon activation of these signaling pathways, micronemes, small secretory organelles at the apical end of the parasite, release a variety of microneme proteins (MICs) essential for invasion and egress14,15. While rhoptry secretion is not required for egress16, and a dense granule phospholipase LCAT contributes to the process17,18, microneme exocytosis is considered the primary driver of vacuolar membrane disruption. Although T. gondii is estimated to contain over 40 MICs19, many of them remain functionally uncharacterized.

Among the characterized MICs, perforin-like protein 1 (PLP1), a microneme protein widely conserved among the apicomplexans, is essential for egress in T. gondii20. Parasites lacking PLP1 exhibit significantly impaired egress from host cells and a complete loss of virulence in mice, highlighting the critical role of efficient egress in the in vivo fitness of T. gondii. Discharged PLP1 likely forms pores on the parasitophorous vacuole membrane (PVM), a membranous vacuole where parasites proliferate, leading to membrane permeabilization and disruption21. A current model supported by genetic, biochemical, and structural studies suggests that PLP1 alone is sufficient for membrane disruption20,22–25. Although a micronemal metalloprotease toxolysin 4 (TLN4) is implicated in egress, its contribution is limited26. PLP1 is therefore currently considered the only micronemal protein responsible for membrane disruption. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain poorly understood, raising the possibility that additional unidentified factors may contribute.

A genome-wide in vitro screen in human fibroblasts identified many fitness-conferring genes during the lytic cycle27, but paradoxically found PLP1 to be dispensable. This was an unexpected outcome given its established role in egress. This discrepancy likely results from the screening protocol, which bypasses egress by mechanically lysing host cells, or from delayed egress of PLP1-deficient parasites. In contrast, published in vivo CRISPR screens, free from such interventions, consistently identified PLP1 as crucial for parasite fitness28,29, suggesting their utility in uncovering egress factors. As these show, CRISPR/Cas9-based genetic screens have been instrumental in identifying essential genes involved in diverse biological processes in T. gondii30,31.

Here, we performed a CRISPR-based in vivo screen targeting genes encoding proteins of the apical complex and pellicle, leading to identification of several previously unrecognized genes essential for T. gondii virulence. Among them, we identified microneme protein 11 (MIC11) as a key egress factor that likely cooperates with PLP1 to mediate membrane disruption and parasite egress from host cells. Our findings challenge the long-standing egress model of T. gondii and provide revised mechanistic insights into the molecular basis of parasite-induced membrane rupture during egress.

Results

CRISPR screen identifies MIC11 as the top-hit among in vivo fitness-conferring genes

The apical complex (including the conoid and apical secretory organelles) and the pellicle (inner membrane complex and parasite plasma membrane) are critical subcellular compartments of the apicomplexan parasites participating in motility, invasion, and egress32,33. This motivated us to search for genes essential for the parasite in vivo fitness by CRISPR-based screening targeting proteins localized to the apical complex and pellicle.

We employed a previously established in vivo CRISPR screen system using the type I RH strain and inbred C57BL/6 mice28 (Fig. 1a). We designed a new guide RNA (gRNA) library targeting selected genes encoding the apical complex or pellicle-localized proteins19 (Fig. 1b). RH∆hxgprt parasites were transfected with the gRNA library and subjected to pyrimethamine selection for four passages in cell culture. Parasite mutant pools from the fourth passage were subsequently inoculated into the footpads of C57BL/6 mice at a dose of 107 parasites per mouse. Seven days after infection, parasites were retrieved from the spleens, expanded in cell culture for one passage, and parasite genomic DNA was extracted for sequencing of genome-integrated gRNAs.

Fitness scores were calculated as the average log2 fold change of gRNA abundances between the input library and the fourth passage (in vitro), or the fourth passage and the spleen (in vivo). Fitness scores represent the relative abundance of each mutant within the parasite population, thereby reflecting the contribution of the targeted gene to parasite survival. Our in vitro fitness scores were highly correlated with published genome-wide in vitro fitness scores in human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs)27 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Our in vivo fitness scores were highly reproducible between independent infections in mice (Supplementary Fig. 1b) and correlated well with previously reported genome-wide fitness data in mice (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Together, these results support the robustness and reproducibility of our screening approach.

Comparing in vitro and in vivo fitness scores identified genes that contribute to parasite fitness during acute infection (Fig. 1c). The distance of each gene from the regression line was computed to identify top-ranking candidates (Supplementary Data 1). Known virulence factors such as ROP18 and GRA23 ranked among the top hits, further validating the robustness of the approach. Some microneme-associated candidates were highly ranked, such as MIC11, SPATR, TGGT1_272380, and TGGT1_221180. MIC11 is also one of the top hits of another in vivo CRISPR screen29. SPATR was reported to be a component of the MIC complex essential for rhoptry discharge and contributes to virulence34–36. TGGT1_272380 was recently shown to be localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and dispensable for virulence37; thus, it was excluded from further analysis. Conoid-associated candidates, such as PRP (TGGT1_291880) and CF6 (TGGT1_245640)38, were ranked highly. Consistently, pellicle-associated candidates, such as PAP2 (TGGT1_247360), ULK kinase (TGGT1_235750), TGGT1_200460, and RAB6 (TGGT1_310460), were also ranked highly. Of these, TGGT1_200460 was recently shown to be essential for virulence29.

We selected nine genes displaying impaired in vivo fitness scores for validation, generating knockout parasites and inoculating them to the mouse footpad (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Five knockouts (∆MIC11, ∆PAP2, ∆SPATR, ∆ULK, and ∆TGGT1_200460) were avirulent in mice. Four knockouts (∆RAB6, ∆PRP, ∆CF6, and ∆TGGT1_221180) showed attenuated virulence. These results demonstrate the high fidelity of the in vivo screen in identifying essential virulence factors. In this study, we further describe the characterization of the top-hit MIC11 in the following sections.

MIC11 is a microneme protein processed in an ASP3-dependent manner

MIC11 is a 16 kDa microneme protein conserved among coccidian parasites39, and its role remains unknown. MIC11 consists of a signal peptide and two additional peptides, α-chain and β-chain, separated by an internal γ-propeptide. During maturation, the γ-propeptide is removed, and the α- and β-chains are linked by a single disulfide bond to form mature MIC1139 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We confirmed that MIC11 localized to micronemes and its secretion was stimulated by calcium-mobilizing treatments (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Under non-reducing conditions, the anti-MIC11 antibody detected a ~16 kDa band consistent with the mature species, whereas under reducing conditions two bands of ~10–15 kDa and ~20–25 kDa were observed, likely representing dissociated forms (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Most secretory proteins of T. gondii are proteolytically processed either during trafficking to secretory organelles or after exocytosis. ASP3, an aspartyl protease, has been shown to process several rhoptry proteins and MICs, and is essential for parasite invasion and egress40. Previous proteomics data suggested that MIC11 might be processed in an ASP3-dependent manner40. To test this, we used the anhydrotetracycline (ATc)-inducible ASP3 knockdown strain tagged with Ty epitope tag (ASP3-iKD). Western blot analysis confirmed that ATc treatment for 48 h depleted ASP3 protein expression. In agreement with a previous report40, MIC6, a known substrate of ASP3, accumulated as the immature form, whereas MIC4 was not affected. Under this condition, MIC11 also exhibited reduced levels of the mature form (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), indicating ASP3-dependent processing at two predicted sites in accordance with the previous study40 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Taken together, these results demonstrate that MIC11 is a soluble microneme protein processed in an ASP3-dependent manner.

MIC11 is essential for parasite egress and vacuolar permeabilization

Plaque assays were performed to assess the impact of MIC11 deletion on the lytic cycle and ∆MIC11 produced smaller plaques compared to WT parasites. The complemented ∆MIC11::MIC11-Spot-HA parasites, C-terminally tagged with Spot and HA epitope tags, restored normal plaque formation (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), confirming that MIC11 is required for the lytic cycle of T. gondii. Further investigation revealed that MIC11 deletion did not significantly affect invasion or intracellular proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). In contrast, abnormal spherical structures where multiple parasites were entrapped, reminiscent of those observed in the ΔPLP1 parasite-infected cells20. These spheres were not observed in WT or the complemented strains (Fig. 2b). Considering that PLP1 is necessary for egress, we performed induced egress assays following stimulation with the Ca2+ ionophore ionomycin to assess the role of MIC11 in egress. Most WT parasites egressed from the infected cells within 5 min, while ∆MIC11 parasites remained inside vacuoles, indicating that ionophore-induced egress was severely impaired in ∆MIC11 parasites. ∆MIC11::MIC11-Spot-HA parasites recovered the egress phenotype (Fig. 2c, d). In addition to Ca2+ ionophore, other stimuli such as the phosphodiesterase inhibitor zaprinast and low pH also trigger parasite egress9,22,41. ∆MIC11 parasites also exhibited severe defects in zaprinast- and low pH-induced egress assays (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). After intraperitoneal injection, ∆MIC11 parasites lost virulence in mice, and ∆MIC11::MIC11-Spot-HA parasites restored virulence (Fig. 2e).

To unravel the mode of action of MIC11, we examined which steps are affected by its deficiency. Microneme secretion and gliding motility of ∆MIC11 parasites were normal (Fig. 2f, g, and Supplementary Fig. 3g). To evaluate the PVM integrity, we generated transgenic parasites expressing GRA1-mCherry. Under unstimulated conditions, the PVM is intact and GRA1-mCherry resides in the PV. Parasites are then immobilized with the actin polymerization inhibitor cytochalasin D (CytD). Upon Ca2+ ionophore treatment, microneme secretion and subsequent PLP1 discharge trigger PV rupture, followed by diffusion of GRA1-mCherry into the host cytosol20 (Fig. 2h). We next monitored the permeabilization of the PVM after stimulation with ionomycin by using time-lapse imaging. WT parasites caused PV rupture after ionophore stimulation within 5 min. In contrast, no PV rupture was observed in ∆MIC11 vacuoles within 10 min (Fig. 2i, and Supplementary Fig. 3h). These findings demonstrate that impaired membrane permeabilization accounts for the defective egress in ∆MIC11 parasites.

Scanning mutagenesis reveals functional regions in MIC11

MIC11 lacks homology to known proteins and has no apparent conserved domains other than a signal peptide. AlphaFold3 was used to model the mature MIC11 consisted of α- and β-chains. We then selected the model for which the complex was consistent with disulfide bond formation, which exhibited a four-helix bundle due to each chain forming an antiparallel pair of helices (Fig. 3a).

To determine which regions of MIC11 are required for egress, we performed scanning mutagenesis by replacing each set of five amino acid residues with five consecutive alanines42. In addition, we generated point mutations C90A and C194A to disrupt the disulfide bond between these residues39. Transfection of these constructs into ∆MIC11 parasites yielded mutant lines mut_01–mut_35, C90A, and C194A (Fig. 3b). Ionophore-induced egress assays were performed to assess the phenotype of each mutant parasite. The results mapped the critical and dispensable regions in MIC11 across the α-chain, β-chain, and γ-propeptide (Fig. 3c). Essential regions were concentrated at the anterior and central parts of the α-chain, whereas they were distributed throughout the β-chain. As expected, C90A and C194A mutations failed to restore the egress phenotype. Among the γ-propeptide mutants, mut_23 showed markedly impaired egress.

Localization of MIC11 mutants was validated by immunofluorescence assays (Supplementary Fig. 4). Some mutants exhibited altered localization in addition to micronemes. Among the γ-propeptide mutants, MIC11 in mut_23 (IRPHG137–141 to AAAAA) accumulated in the PV and was absent from micronemes (Fig. 3d, e). Since the γ-propeptide is removed during maturation and its role remains unknown, we hypothesized that it contains a determinant for microneme trafficking of MIC11. Dissection by two alanine substitutions showed that IR_AA and PH_AA mutants failed to target MIC11 to micronemes, whereas GF_AA mutant localized normally (Fig. 3e, f). These results demonstrate that the IRPH motif in the γ-propeptide is essential for proper trafficking of MIC11 to micronemes.

MIC11 likely cooperates with PLP1 to facilitate parasite egress

To understand how MIC11 contributes to the PVM disruption, we performed co-immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis. Comparison of protein abundance between immunoprecipitations from ∆MIC11::MIC11-Spot-HA parasites over the parental ∆MIC11 parasites revealed several MICs, including PLP1, MIC5, MIC10, MIC20, and MIC21 (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Data 2). It is well established that many MICs form functional complexes15. Among these hits, only PLP1 has been previously implicated in parasite egress, whereas MIC5, MIC10, MIC20, and MIC21 have not been implicated in this process43–46. Consistent with this, our in vivo CRISPR screen indicated that PLP1 was essential for parasite fitness, while the other four MICs were dispensable, at least upon single deletion28 (Fig. 4b).

Therefore, we generated ∆PLP1 parasites and a quadruple knockout strain lacking MIC5, MIC10, MIC20, and MIC21 (referred to as QKO) (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). As reported previously20, ∆PLP1 parasites exhibited severely impaired egress and virulence, while QKO parasites exhibited egress and virulence comparable to WT parasites (Fig. 4c, d).

To perform reciprocal IP-MS, we generated endogenously tagged PLP1-HA parasites, along with rhoptry protein 9 (ROP9)-HA parasites as a control (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e). IP-MS of PLP1-HA parasites resulted in the detection of MIC11 within the precipitated fractions (Fig. 4e, and Supplementary Data 2). To further examine the potential associations between MIC11 and PLP1, we performed reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation assays. In these experiments, MIC11 and PLP1 were reciprocally co-immunoprecipitated (Fig. 4f). These observations are consistent with the formation of a complex containing MIC11 and PLP1, supporting their essential roles during parasite egress.

To further investigate the functional relationship between MIC11 and PLP1, we generated double knockout parasites lacking MIC11 and PLP1 (∆MIC11∆PLP1, referred to as DKO). Complementation with either MIC11-HA or PLP1-Ty alone did not restore egress, whereas double complementation (DKO::MIC11-HA::PLP1-Ty) fully rescued the egress phenotype (Fig. 4g–i). These findings suggest that both MIC11 and PLP1 are required for parasite egress and cannot compensate for each other.

MIC11 is not required for PLP1 localization or secretion

We confirmed co-localization of PLP1 with MIC11 by immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 5a). To further investigate the role of MIC11, we examined the impact of gene deletions on reciprocal protein localization. MIC11 localization was not affected by PLP1 deletion (Fig. 5b), and conversely, PLP1 localization was not affected by MIC11 deletion (Fig. 5c). Next, we assessed the secretion and processing of PLP1 and found that they appeared normal in the absence of MIC11 (Fig. 5d). These results indicate that ∆MIC11 parasites fail to permeabilize the membrane despite normal secretion of PLP1.

A putative MIC11 paralogue is expressed in the feline-restricted stages of T. gondii

Sexual reproduction of T. gondii occurs exclusively during the infection of the definitive feline host47. The parasites of feline-restricted stages need to egress from the intestinal cells, as do tachyzoites in the intermediate hosts. However, PLP1 and MIC11 are not expressed during the feline-restricted stages48. A previously proposed model suggested that PLP2, a PLP1 paralogue, is involved in egress in the feline gut49. This motivated us to search for a homologue of MIC11 expressed during the feline-restricted stages. We identified one gene, TGME49_295662, encoding an uncharacterized hypothetical protein that shares 25% amino acid sequence similarity with MIC11 (Fig. 6a). TGME49_295662 possesses a signal peptide, α-chain, β-chain, and γ-propeptide-like sequences. Within the γ-propeptide-like region, an IAPH sequence resembles the IRPH microneme trafficking determinant in MIC11 (Fig. 6a). The structure of mature TGME49_295662 was modeled in an equivalent manner as MIC11. In spite of the low sequence identity (25%), the two models were found to have the same overall topology, with TGME49_295662 containing an additional N-terminal helix in the β-chain (Fig. 6b). The Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) was 2.47 Å over 56 residues for the α-chains and 2.49 Å over 48 residues for the β-chain. In contrast to MIC11, TGME49_295662 is highly expressed in merozoites48 (Fig. 6c).

To characterize the putative paralogue of MIC11 in tachyzoites, we expressed the open reading frame (ORF) of TGGT1_295662 tagged with Spot and HA epitopes under the control of the SAG1 promoter in ∆MIC11 parasites (Fig. 6d). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that TGGT1_295662-Spot-HA localized to micronemes in tachyzoites (Fig. 6e). We then compared the biochemical properties of MIC11 and TGGT1_295662 by subjecting ∆MIC11::MIC11-Spot-HA and ∆MIC11::295662-Spot-HA parasites to western blotting under non-reducing and reducing conditions. TGGT1_295662-Spot-HA showed a pattern like MIC11-Spot-HA (Fig. 6f), suggesting that the mature form of TGGT1_295662 consists of α-chain and β-chain, connected by a disulfide bond between C93 and C202 (Fig. 6b). Based on these findings, TGGT1_295662 was named microneme protein 22 (MIC22). Given the similarity of MIC11 and MIC22, we next examined whether MIC22 could functionally compensate for MIC11 deficiency. Indeed, the ∆MIC11::MIC22-Spot-HA strain fully restored both egress and virulence (Fig. 6g–i). Collectively, these results suggest that MIC11–PLP1-mediated egress in tachyzoites may be replaced by MIC22–PLP2-mediated egress in feline-restricted stages.

Discussion

Exit from host cells, known as egress, is a critical process in the life cycle of intracellular pathogens, essential for their dissemination and survival within the host. In this study, we performed an in vivo CRISPR screen and identified MIC11, a secretory protein uncharacterized for more than two decades, as a fundamental factor for parasite egress. While previous work demonstrated that recombinant PLP1 protein alone exhibited lytic activity and thus PLP1 was thought to be sufficient for membrane disruption23, our findings revise this model. We show that PLP1 alone is insufficient in cellulo, and that functional egress requires both PLP1 and MIC11 (Fig. 7).

Revised model of PVM pore formation and egress.

Schematic representation of the revised model of T. gondii egress. Upon stimulation, intracellular parasites secrete MIC11 and PLP1 from micronemes, leading to pore formation on the parasitophorous vacuole membrane (PVM), which ultimately results in PVM rupture. The host plasma membrane (HPM) is also disrupted by an as-yet unidentified mechanism, enabling parasites to exit from the host cell. Pore formation mediated by PLP1 is thought to involve multiple steps, including membrane binding, insertion, oligomerization, and pore formation. The specific steps at which MIC11 contributes to this process remain unknown and are a focus of future studies. Created in BioRender. Yamamoto, M. (2026) https://BioRender.com/ flgwrc4.

Various genetic screening approaches have been developed to identify critical factors involved in T. gondii egress, leading to the discovery of multiple egress-associated genes50–55. However, despite its well-established role in egress, PLP1 was missed in some screenings. We speculate that in vitro CRISPR screens are not well-suited to identify egress factors because the protocol bypasses this step by mechanically lysing host cells, thereby releasing parasites regardless of their egress capacity. As a result, mutations specifically impairing egress do not confer a selective disadvantage and thus yield neutral in vitro fitness scores. Indeed, both PLP1 and MIC11 scored as neutral in vitro27. In contrast, we and others have conducted in vivo CRISPR screens and successfully identified MIC11 and PLP1 as in vivo fitness-conferring genes28,29. These results suggest that the in vivo CRISPR screen serves as a robust phenotypic screen for egress-related genes, and additional yet unidentified egress factors may be present among the screening hits. Furthermore, host-derived factors such as calpain and G protein-coupled receptors have been implicated in the egress of apicomplexan parasites56–58. Understanding the complex host–parasite interactions that regulate egress remains a key focus for future investigations.

The RH strain tachyzoites naturally egress around 48 h post infection in vitro13. However, it was reported that T. gondii may egress earlier from infected host cells in vivo59. Additionally, extrinsic factors, such as cytokine stimulation or inflammation, can trigger premature egress of T. gondii3. Since ∆MIC11 or ∆PLP1 parasites exhibit a complete loss of virulence in mice, efficient egress appears to be essential for parasite fitness in vivo. The precise role of efficient egress in in vivo settings remains an important topic for future investigation.

We performed a comprehensive mutagenesis screen of full-length MIC11 and identified regions essential for parasite egress. Among these, the IRPH motif within the γ-propeptide was found to be critical for the trafficking of MIC11 to micronemes. Soluble MICs are known to rely on escorter proteins for proper trafficking to micronemes60. We therefore hypothesize that MIC11 may interact with an unidentified escorter through the IRPH motif to mediate its microneme targeting. Future studies will be required to uncover the sorting machinery of MIC11. Although we also identified essential regions across the α- and β-chains, their precise roles remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

In contrast to the well-studied tachyzoite and bradyzoite stages in intermediate hosts, the feline-restricted stages are less explored. Interestingly, many secretory proteins display marked expression dynamics between tachyzoites and merozoites48. Our findings demonstrate functional redundancy between MIC11 and MIC22. MIC22 and PLP2 may function as key mediators of egress in merozoites, although the role of PLP2 in feline-restricted stages remains unknown. Recently developed methods for in vitro production of merozoite-like parasites would enable further exploration61,62.

In Plasmodium spp., proteases and phospholipases are considered to play more critical roles than perforins in egress during the asexual blood stages63,64. The Plasmodium genome encodes a family of five PLP genes (PPLP1–5)65, but unlike T. gondii, Plasmodium parasites apparently lack MIC11-like homologues. It remains to be determined whether PPLPs alone are sufficient for membrane disruption or if additional factors contribute to this process. How the PVM is ruptured by apicomplexan parasites remains a central question in understanding the molecular basis of parasite egress.

A limitation of this study is that it is still unclear whether MIC11 and PLP1 interact directly or via cofactors and how MIC11 contributes to membrane disruption in concert with PLP1. Defining the precise binding mode and the spatiotemporal dynamics of their association will be a priority for future work. Membrane pore formation mediated by pore-forming proteins typically involves multiple steps, including membrane binding, insertion, oligomerization, and pore-formation66,67. Future studies with structural insights will be essential to elucidate the precise molecular mechanism of how MIC11 contributes to this PLP1-dependent process (Fig. 7). In summary, this study shows that MIC11 is the second factor indispensable for egress in T. gondii and revises the current model of parasite egress.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted under the approval of the Animal Research Committee of the Research Institute for Microbial Diseases in The University of Osaka. C57BL/6NCrSlc (C57BL/6N) mice were purchased from SLC. Female mice aged 8–12 weeks were used in all experiments.

Toxoplasma strains

All T. gondii strains were maintained in Vero cells by serial passage using RPMI (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; JRH Bioscience), 100 µ/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque) in incubators at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The parental strains used in this study were RHΔhxgprt28 and RHΔhxgprtΔku8068. ASP3-iKD was described previously40. Depletion of ASP3 was induced by addition of 1 µg/mL of anhydrotetracycline (Takara).

Host cell culture

Vero cells were maintained in RPMI (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 µ/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque) in incubators at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HFFs were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 µ/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque) in incubators at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Antibodies

The following primary antibodies were used in the immunofluorescence assay and immunoblotting. Mouse anti-HA (MBL, M180-7), mouse anti-HA.11 (BioLegend, 901514), mouse anti-Ty (BB2), mouse anti-GRA1 (TG17-43), mouse anti-MIC2 (T34A11)69, rabbit anti-MIC2 (this study), rabbit anti-MIC11 (this study), rabbit anti-MIC670, rabbit anti-MIC460, rabbit anti-PLP1 (N-terminus)23, rabbit anti-PLP120, rabbit anti-PLP1 (this study), rabbit anti-GRA1728, rabbit anti-ROP1871, mouse anti-SAG172, rabbit anti-GAP4572. We raised custom antibodies against synthetic peptides in rabbits as follows. MIC11 (RGEGRLDKEEAVDDESD), PLP1 (PTSKTMSSLKLAPVK), MIC2 (TKVVMEEEKETLV). The epitope identification, peptide synthesis, rabbit immunization, and serum collection were commercially conducted by Cosmo Bio. The following secondary antibodies were used in the immunofluorescence assay or immunoblotting. Donkey anti-mouse CF488A (Biotium, 20014), donkey anti-rabbit CF488A (Biotium, 20015), donkey anti-mouse CF594 (Biotium, 20115), donkey anti-rabbit CF594 (Biotium, 20152), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen, A21244), goat anti-mouse-HRP (Invitrogen, G21040), goat anti-rabbit-HRP (Invitrogen, G21234). Dilutions of antibodies used in each assay are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Parasite transfection

Parental parasites were filtered through 5-µm filter (PALL) and resuspended in transfection solution containing 2 mM ATP, 5 mM glutathione, DNA, and cytomix (10 mM KPO4, 120 mM KCl, 0.15 mM CaCl2, 5 mM MgCl2, 25 mM HEPES, 2 mM EDTA). Parasites were electroporated in a 2-mm cuvette at 1500 V and 25 µF by GENE PULSER II (Bio-Rad). Stable transgenic parasites were selected with the appropriate antibiotics or sorted by cell sorter, cloned in 96-well plates by limiting dilution, and verified by genotyping, mRNA expression, or protein expression. The primers used for generating transgenic parasites are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Generation of parasite transgenic lines

For generating knockout parasites, the specific gRNAs targeting each gene’s 5′ and 3′ ends were cloned into the pU6-Universal vector (Addgene #52694). The floxed HXGPRT construct was PCR amplified with 60 bp homology arms to each gene’s 5′ and 3′ UTRs. Parasites were transfected with two gRNAs and the PCR-amplified HXGPRT repair template. Transfected parasites were selected with 25 µg/ml mycophenolic acid (Sigma) and 50 µg/ml xanthine (Wako).

For generating quadruple knockout parasites (∆MIC5∆MIC10∆MIC20∆MIC21, QKO), parasites were transfected with eight gRNAs and the PCR-amplified HXGPRT, DHFR-TS, Venus, and mCherry repair templates targeting each gene. Transfected parasites were selected with 25 µg/ml mycophenolic acid, 50 µg/ml xanthine, and 3 µM pyrimethamine (Sigma) for serial passage. Stable resistant parasites were sorted for Venus and mCherry-double-positive parasites by BD FACS Aria III.

To generate endogenously tagged PLP1 and ROP9 strains, the 3HA-HXGPRT cassette was PCR amplified with 40 bp homology arms to the C-terminus of the respective loci. The resulting repair templates were co-transfected into parasites with gRNA vectors targeting the C-terminus of PLP1 or ROP9. Transfected parasites were selected with 25 µg/ml mycophenolic acid and 50 µg/ml xanthine.

To generate GRA1-mCherry expressing parasites, pUPRT-pTub-GRA1-mCherry plasmid was co-transfected with a gRNA vector targeting UPRT locus. Transfected parasites were selected with 10 µM fluorodeoxyuridine (Wako).

To complement PLP1 deletion, PLP1 cDNA was amplified from RH strain cDNA and cloned into the pUPRT vector. The pUPRT-PLP1-Ty construct was co-transfected with a gRNA vector targeting the UPRT locus. Transfected parasites were selected with 10 µM fluorodeoxyuridine.

To generate ∆MIC11∆hxgprt parasites, the loxP-flanked HXGPRT cassette inserted at the endogenous MIC11 locus was excised by transient transfection with Cre recombinase. Transfected parasites were then selected in medium containing 80 µg/ml 6-thioxanthine (Wako).

To complement MIC11 knockout parasites, wild-type MIC11 cDNA was amplified from the cDNA of the RH strain. MIC22 ORF was amplified from the genomic DNA of the RH strain. Each fragment was cloned into pHXGPRT vector. ∆MIC11∆hxgprt parasites were transfected with the pHXGPRT-pSAG1-MIC11/MIC22-Spot-HA plasmid and selected with 25 µg/ml mycophenolic acid and 50 µg/ml xanthine.

Scanning mutagenesis of MIC11

Mutated MIC11 cDNAs were commercially synthesized (FASMAC) and cloned into an expression vector in which MIC11 was expressed under the SAG1 promoter, while a Venus-P2A-HXGPRT selectable marker cassette was driven by the tubulin promoter. ∆MIC11∆hxgprt parasites were transfected with empty vector, wild-type MIC11, or each mutant construct, and selected with 25 µg/ml mycophenolic acid and 50 µg/ml xanthine. After drug selection, stable polyclonal populations were obtained. Flow cytometry analysis (Attune NxT, Thermo Fisher) confirmed that 70–90% of parasites were Venus-positive in all lines, indicating efficient transfection and selection. Each mutant population was then subjected to ionophore-induced egress assays.

Construction of the apical–pellicle gRNA library

The gRNA sequences of the apical–pellicle library were selected from the genome-wide gRNA library27 based on hyperLOPIT data19. The selected gRNA libraries were cloned into the modified pU6-Universal vector as previously described28. The construction of the gRNA library was performed by VectorBuilder.

In vitro and in vivo CRISPR screens

In vitro and in vivo CRISPR screens were conducted as previously described28. Briefly, the gRNA library was linearized with NotI (Takara) and transfected into approximately 1–2 × 108 RHΔhxgprt parasites, divided between four cuvettes. Transfected parasites were grown in 4 × 150-mm dishes of confluent Vero cells. Medium was replaced with fresh one containing 10 µg/ml DNase I and 3 µM pyrimethamine 24 h post transfection. Every 3 days until passage 3, parasites were scraped, mechanically lysed, and passaged onto new dishes. After 2 days (passage 4), parasites were syringe-lysed, filtered, and counted for either genomic DNA preparation or mouse infection.

For genomic DNA preparation, 1 × 108 parasites were pelleted and stored at −80 °C. For mouse infection, parasites were resuspended in PBS at 2.5 × 108 parasites/ml, and 1 × 107 parasites in 40 µl PBS were injected into the footpad of each anesthetized C57BL/6 mouse. Seven days post infection, mice were euthanized, and spleens were collected, homogenized by a plunger, and passed through a cell strainer to prepare cell suspensions. These suspensions were added to 2 × 150-mm Vero cell dishes per spleen. Parasites typically lysed out within 2–4 days. At least 1 × 108 parasites were pelleted and stored at −80 °C for genomic DNA extraction.

Parasite genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen). The gRNAs were amplified and barcoded with KOD FX Neo (TOYOBO) using Primer 1 and Primer 2. The resulting libraries were sequenced on a DNBSEQ-G400RS (MGI) using Primer 3 and Primer 4. All primers are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Data analysis of the CRISPR screen

Following demultiplexing, raw read counts for each gRNA were obtained after alignment to the library. To account for differences in sequencing depth, counts were normalized by dividing each gRNA count by the total number of aligned reads per sample, yielding relative abundances. These normalized values were used to calculate log₂ fold-changes between samples. For in vitro analysis, log2 fold-change values were computed between the passage 4 sample and the input library for each gRNA. For in vivo analysis, log2 fold-change values were computed between each in vivo sample and the passage 4 sample for each gRNA. The median fitness score across mouse replicates was used as the in vivo fitness score. P values were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test comparing passage 4 vs in vivo samples, and adjusted p values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The distance of each gene from the regression line was calculated as below.

All analyzes were conducted by R (v4.1.1) using the stats package (v3.6.2) and visualized by ggplot2 (v3.4.0).

Assessment of in vivo virulence in mice

Mice were infected with 103 tachyzoites in 200 µl (intra-peritoneum) or 40 µl (footpad) of PBS. The mouse health condition and survival were monitored daily until 30 days post infection.

Immunofluorescence assay

HFFs were grown on coverslips, infected with parasites for 24–30 h, and fixed in 3.7% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.1% Triton-X or 0.002% digitonin for 10 min and then blocked with PBS containing 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the coverslips were incubated with the primary antibodies for 1 h, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies and DAPI for 30 min. The coverslips were mounted using PermaFluor (Thermo Scientific). Images were acquired by confocal laser microscopy (Olympus FV3000 IX83). Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 IX83 confocal microscope equipped with oil-immersion objective lenses (UPLXAPO60XO, NA 1.42, and UPLXAPO100XO, NA 1.45). Fluorophores were excited with 405, 488, 561, and 640 nm laser lines, and emissions were collected using spectral detection windows set to 440–480 nm (DAPI), 500–540 nm (CF488A), 581–631 nm (CF594), and 654–692 nm (Alexa Fluor 647).

Western blotting

Parasites were lysed in lysis buffer (1% NP-40, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque). The parasite lysates were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Immobilon-P; Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk in 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. HRP was detected using the Immobilon Forte Western HRP substrate (Millipore) or SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), visualized by ImageQuant LAS 4000mini (GE HealthCare).

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted by RNeasy kit (Qiagen), and cDNA was synthesized by Verso reverse transcription (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with CFX Connect real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad) and Go-Taq real-time PCR system (Promega). The data was analyzed by the ∆∆CT method and normalized to ACT1 in each sample. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Microneme secretion assay

Freshly egressed parasites (1 × 108) were pelleted and resuspended in serum-free DMEM with 2% ethanol for 30 min at 37 °C. To suppress microneme secretion, parasites were also treated with 100 µM BAPTA-AM. After incubation, parasites were pelleted, and the supernatants were transferred to new collection tubes and re-centrifuged. The final supernatants (ESA) were resuspended in SDS loading buffer. The pellet lysates and ESA were subjected to immunoblot.

Plaque assay

A total of 400 parasites were infected into a well of a six-well plate of HFFs and grown for 7 days in DMEM supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated FBS. Parasites were fixed with 3.7% PFA, stained with crystal violet, washed, and dried overnight. Plaque images were visualized using a stereo microscope (SZX16, Evident) equipped with a 1× objective and a digital camera (AdvanCam-HD2sA, AdvanVision). Images were acquired using the camera’s default software, and plaque areas were measured manually using ImageJ.

Invasion assay

Parasites (1 × 106) were inoculated on coverslips with HFF monolayer in a 24-well plate. The plate was centrifuged for 5 min at 250 G, incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, and fixed with 3.7% PFA. Extracellular parasites were stained with anti-SAG1 antibody without permeabilization. After washing three times, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X, and all parasites were stained with anti-GAP45 antibody. At least 100 parasites were counted and determined as extracellular or intracellular parasites.

Replication assay

A total of 4 × 104 parasites were inoculated on coverslips with HFF monolayer in a 24-well plate. Twenty-four hours post-infection, the monolayers were fixed with 3.7% PFA and processed for immunofluorescence assay with anti-GAP45 antibody to stain parasites. The number of parasites per vacuole was counted for at least 100 vacuoles.

Induced egress assay

Freshly egressed parasites (1 × 105) were inoculated on coverslips with HFF monolayer and grown for 24–30 h at 37 °C. Egress was induced under the following conditions:

Ionophore-induced egress assay: monolayers were washed twice with serum-free DMEM and incubated with 2 µM ionomycin (Nacalai Tesque) in serum-free DMEM for 5 min at 37 °C.

Zaprinast-induced egress assay: monolayers were washed twice with serum-free DMEM and incubated with 500 µM zaprinast (Tocris) in serum-free DMEM for 5 min at 37 °C.

Low-pH induced egress assay: monolayers were washed twice with intracellular (IC) buffer (5 mM NaCl, 142 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM EGTA, 5.6 mM glucose, 25 mM HEPES, pH adjusted to 7.4 with KOH). The buffer was then replaced with IC buffer (pH adjusted to 5.4 or 7.4 with HCl) with or without 0.002% digitonin, and coverslips were incubated for 3 min at 37 °C.

Cells were fixed with 3.7% PFA and processed for immunofluorescence assay using anti-SAG1 and anti-GRA17 antibodies to stain parasites and PVs, respectively. At least 100 vacuoles were manually scored as occupied or egressed.

Gliding motility assay

Freshly egressed parasites (2 × 106) were resuspended in serum-free DMEM containing 2% ethanol and added to poly-L-Lysine coated coverslips in a 24-well plate. Following incubation for 30 min at 37 °C, the coverslips are fixed with 3.7% PFA and processed for immunofluorescence assay using anti-SAG1 antibody to stain parasites and trails. Parasite trail lengths were manually measured using ImageJ.

Parasite-induced PVM permeabilization assay

For time-lapse imaging, parasites (5 × 105) were inoculated on HFF monolayers in poly-lysine-coated 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (Matsunami) and grown for 24–30 h at 37 °C. HFF monolayers were pre-treated with 2 µM Cytochalasin D in DMEM for 5 min followed by the addition of an equal volume of 2 µM Cytochalasin D and 4 µM ionomycin in DMEM. Time-lapse images were acquired every 10 s for up to 10 min by a confocal laser microscopy (Olympus FV1200).

For quantification, infected HFF monolayers were pre-treated with 2 µM Cytochalasin D in DMEM for 5 min followed by the addition of an equal volume of 2 µM Cytochalasin D and 4 µM ionomycin in DMEM for an additional 5 min. Samples were then fixed and subjected to an immunofluorescence assay using an anti-GRA1 antibody to stain PV. At least 100 vacuoles were manually scored as permeabilized or intact.

Co-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

Parasites were fixed with 0.1% PFA for 10 min, quenched with 100 mM glycine for 4 min, washed with HEPES-buffered saline, and lysed in HEPES-RIPA buffer (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.5, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate, 0.05% SDS, 1% NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail cOmplete EDTA-free (Roche) and Benzonase (Merck).

After centrifugation, the supernatants were processed as follows. For MIC11 immunoprecipitation, lysates of ∆MIC11::MIC11-Spot-HA parasites and ∆MIC11 parasites (control) were incubated with Spot-Trap magnetic agarose beads (Proteintech, etma). For PLP1 immunoprecipitation, lysates of RH::PLP1-HA parasites and RH::ROP9-HA parasites (control) were incubated with anti-HA antibody (#3724, Cell Signaling Technology) and Sera-Mag SpeedBeads Protein A/G (Cytiva). All incubations were performed for 3 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with HEPES-RIPA buffer and twice with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Proteins on the beads were digested with 200 ng of trypsin/Lys-C mix (Promega) at 37 °C overnight. The digested peptides were reduced, alkylated, acidified, and desalted using GL-Tip SDB (GL Sciences). The eluates were evaporated in a SpeedVac concentrator and dissolved in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid and 3% acetonitrile (ACN). LC-MS/MS analysis of the resultant peptides was performed on an EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC connected to an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer through a nanoelectrospray ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were separated on a 75 µm inner diameter × 150 mm C18 reversed-phase column (Nikkyo Technos) with a linear 4–32% ACN gradient for 0–100 min followed by an increase to 80% ACN for 10 min and a final hold at 80% ACN for 10 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent acquisition mode with a maximum duty cycle of 3 s. MS1 spectra were measured with a resolution of 120,000, an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 4e5, and a mass range from 375 to 1500 m/z. HCD MS/MS spectra were acquired in the linear ion trap with an AGC target of 1e4, an isolation window of 1.6 m/z, a maximum injection time of 35 ms, and a normalized collision energy of 30. Dynamic exclusion was set to 20 s. Raw data were directly analyzed against T. gondii GT1 protein data (ToxoDB release 55), supplemented using Proteome Discoverer version 2.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with Sequest HT search engine. The search parameters were as follows: (a) trypsin as an enzyme with up to two missed cleavages; (b) precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm; (c) fragment mass tolerance of 0.6 Da; (d) carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification; and (e) acetylation of protein N-terminus and oxidation of methionine as variable modifications. Peptides and proteins were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% using the percolator node and the protein FDR validator node, respectively. Label-free precursor ion quantification was performed using the precursor ions quantifier node, and normalization was performed such that the total sum of abundance values for each sample over all peptides was the same.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Parasite lysates were prepared as described in the IP-MS section, but lysed in lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque). After centrifugation, the supernatants were incubated with anti-HA antibody (sc-7392, Santa Cruz) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with Protein G Sepharose 4 Fast Flow (GE Healthcare) for 1 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.2% Deoxycholate). Proteins bound to the beads were eluted by boiling at 95 °C for 10 min in SDS-sample buffer and then subjected to immunoblotting.

AlphaFold prediction of MIC11 and MIC22 structure

The structures of MIC11 and MIC22 were predicted using the AlphaFold server (https://alphafoldserver.com/)73 with the α- and β-chain sequences as input. The RMSD was computed using cealign in PyMOL (version 3.1.0). The resulting structural model was visualized using UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.10.1)74.

Homologue search and sequential alignment

VEuPathDB75 was searched for MIC11 homologues using BLAST. MAFFT76 was used to align and visualize MIC11 and MIC22.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyzes of the CRISPR screen were performed using R (4.1.1, https://www.r-project.org/). Statistical analyzes of proteomics were performed using Proteome Discoverer (version 2.5) and GraphPad Prism 10. Statistical analyzes of other experiments were performed using GraphPad Prism 10, and details of the analyzes are reported in each legend. Quantification of image-based assay, such as plaque assay, egress assay, and motility assay, was manually performed. All fluorescence microscopy experiments were conducted in at least two biological repeats.
