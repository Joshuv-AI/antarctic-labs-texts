Relationship Between Family and Friend Support and Psychological Distress in Adolescents
Authors: Margaret M. Fitzpatrick, Avery M. Anderson, Christopher Browning, Jodi L. Ford
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study examines the associations between family and friend support and depressive and anxiety symptoms among adolescents, considering potential sex differences.Methods:Secondary data from a longitudinal cohort study of 1,348 adolescents ages 11–17 were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression, controlling for relevant factors. Interaction terms between sex and support were created to examine moderation by sex.Results:Youth who reported higher family support reported lower depressive symptoms (b = −0.53, p < .001) and lower anxiety symptoms (b = −0.39, p < .001). Similarly, youth’s higher reported friend support was associated with lower depressive symptoms (b = −0.52, p < .001) and lower anxiety symptoms (b = −0.44, p < .001). Effects were not modified by sex.Conclusion:Understanding modifiable factors such as supportive systems is important to mitigate mental health disorders. Further investigation of protective factors for promoting adolescent mental health is needed.

BACKGROUND

The consistent and pronounced increase in youth depression and anxiety in the United States raises an urgent need to address this public health concern. The percentage of high school students who experience persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness, a primary symptom of depression, has risen dramatically between 2011 and 2021 (Centers for Disease Control [CDC] and Prevention, 2023). More than 4 in 10 (42%) high school students stopped engaging in their normal activities due to feeling sad or hopeless almost every day for at least two weeks in a row, and nearly one-third (29%) experienced poor mental health in the last 30 days. Furthermore, over 20% of adolescents ages 12–17 experienced a major depressive episode in 2021 (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2022).

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, studies estimated that nearly 2 million youth (ages 3–17) had current depression, and approximately 4.4 million had a current anxiety problem (Ghandour et al., 2019). It is well-known that mental health challenges, particularly among youth, have been of growing concern since the pandemic, and a meta-analysis revealed estimates of elevated depression and anxiety in youth have doubled compared to pre-pandemic (Racine et al., 2021). The same meta-analysis found prevalence rates of depression and anxiety were higher in females compared to males. Similarly, over half (57%) of high school females report past year persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness compared to 29% of high school males (CDC, 2023). Poor mental health and potential chronicity of symptoms can profoundly interfere with appropriate social, emotional, and academic development as well as quality of life and activities of daily living (López-López et al., 2024; Schlack et al., 2021). Enhancing knowledge on protective factors that contribute to lessening youth depression and anxiety is essential to inform interventions (Askeland et al., 2020; Dray, 2021).

Research suggests that social support is a protective factor for poor mental health (Cohen & Wills, 1985; Scardera et al., 2020), and interventions to promote social inclusion among youth have shown promising success in decreasing symptoms of depression and anxiety (Hunt et al., 2023). Perceived social support refers to the provision of emotional comfort from one’s relationships (Umberson & Montez, 2010). However, social support can manifest in various domains (e.g., family, friends, and school), and evidence has limited consistency in the effects of distinct domains of social support on youth mental health (Bi et al., 2021). For example, perceived social support from family and teachers reduced the likelihood of experiencing anxiety and depressive symptoms but not social support from friends in one sample of adolescents (Hellfeldt et al., 2020). Family is often an important source of social support, and a meta-analysis demonstrated that support from family was the domain of social support most consistently protective against depression for adolescents (Gariépy et al., 2016).

Additionally, support from friends is another domain of social support, with one integrative review finding positive peer support can be a protective factor for adolescent mental health reducing depression, anxiety, and suicidal behaviors (Roach, 2018). In one longitudinal study investigating multiple sources of emotional support, family, friend, and teacher support were all independently protective against later depression (Pössel et al., 2018). They also examined moderation of all three types of support on the relationship between stress and depression and found only families provided a buffer to later depressive symptoms. There is a need to further examine different domains of social support, and their relationships with anxiety and depression, given the continued increase in youth mental health concerns.

There may be differences by sex in the relationships between domains of social support and adolescent mental health. While prevalence rates of depression and anxiety differ by sex (Farhane-Medina et al., 2022; Slavich & Sacher, 2019), symptom trajectories are also thought to differ, with females having an increased likelihood of moderate to high symptoms at onset (Lewis et al., 2020; Ohannessian et al., 2017). Additionally, perceived levels of social support often differ by sex with female youth reporting higher perceived levels of social support than their male counterparts (Johansen et al., 2021). Prior research also finds the quality of peer support may predict depression among youth, with effects being greater for females than males (Letkiewicz et al., 2023). Limited research exists assessing sex as a moderator of the relationship between specific domains of social support and depression and anxiety in youth.

Cohen’s Social Support Theory (Cohen & Wills, 1985) posits social support directly affects psychological distress by acting as a protective factor, allowing individuals to cope with stressful experiences. Subsequently, higher levels of social support enable individuals to cope better with stress and adversity, experience lower degrees of harmful stress and have improved mental health outcomes. Driven by Social Support Theory to address gaps in the literature, the purpose of the current study is to examine the relationships between two domains of social support (family and friends) and psychological distress (depression and anxiety) in youth; and second, test sex as a moderating variable in the relationships between social support and psychological distress.

METHODS

Study Design and Sample

We analyzed secondary data from the Adolescent Health and Development in Context (AHDC) a longitudinal cohort study of urban and suburban youth ages 11–17 (n = 1,401) that focused on the effects of spatial and social exposures on health and well-being. The study was conducted in a large metropolitan area in the Midwest, and the sample of youth in the study was representative of same-age youth residing in the study area with respect to race and household income except for a slight over-representation of non-Hispanic Black/African American youth (36% in the AHDC study vs. 32% in study area) (Boettner et al., 2019). After the research team obtained informed consent and assent from caregivers and youth, respectively, trained interviewers collected the data in the home setting. Data collection methods included an entrance survey and face-to-face interview with both the youth and primary caregiver, a weeklong ecological momentary assessment and global positioning system data collection of the youth’s locations, and an exit interview with the youth and a caregiver survey at the end of the seven-day collection period. The present analysis examined wave 1 data from youth’s self-administered entrance surveys and primary caregiver interviewer-administered surveys. Prior work has extensively discussed the parent study design, sampling procedures, and data collection (Boettner et al., 2019; Browning et al., 2017, 2021; Ford et al., 2021). The AHDC study and current analyses received prior approval from the university institutional review board.

Dependent Variables of Interest

Psychological distress was operationalized as depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms. Depressive symptoms were measured using an adapted 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies—Depression Scale (Cole et al., 2004). Youth responded to a self-report questionnaire inquiring about how often they experienced various depressive symptoms within the last week. Example items include “I was bothered by things that don’t usually bother me” and “I felt I could not shake off the blues even with the help of my family or friends.” Responses ranged from 1 “Rarely/none of the time/1 day” to 4 “Most/all of the time/5–7 days.” In prior factor analyses, one item “I felt everything I did was an effort” had a negative and low correlation and factor loading with the other 9 items, therefore we did not include this item in the scale development (Ford & Stowe, 2017). We reverse-coded some items so that higher scores denoted more depressive symptoms. We then summed and averaged all 9 items to create a mean score for the analysis (α = 0.78).

Anxiety symptoms were measured using an 8-item Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System scale. Youth self-reported anxious symptoms within the last week. Example items included “I felt worried” and “I felt nervous.” Response options were adapted to be consistent with the depressive symptom items and ranged from 1 “Rarely/none of the time/1 day” to 4 “Most/all of the time/5–7 days.” Some items were reverse coded; higher scores signified more anxiety symptoms. We then summed and averaged the items to create a mean score (α = 0.87).

Independent Variables of Interest

Family emotional support was measured using a 6-item questionnaire assessing adolescents’ perceived emotional support from family members. Example items include “I know my family will always be there for me” and “Sometimes I’m not sure I can rely on family.” Responses ranged from 1—very true, 2—somewhat true, to 3—not true (Turner et al., 1983). Some items were reverse coded with higher scores indicating higher family emotional support. We then summed and averaged items for a mean score for analysis (α = 0.80).

Friend emotional support was measured using an 8-item questionnaire assessing perceived emotional support from friends. Example items included “My friends would take the time to talk about my problems, should I ever want to” and “I feel very close to some of my friends.” Responses ranged from 1—very true, 2—somewhat true, to 3—not true (Turner et al., 1983). Some items were reverse coded with higher scores indicating higher friend emotional support. We then summed and averaged the items for a mean score for analysis (α = 0.75).

Control measures: Youth and caregivers self-reported demographic information including age, sex, and race and ethnicity. Youth sex included male (reference) and female. Youth race and ethnicity included the following categories: non-Hispanic White (reference), non-Hispanic Black/African American, non-Hispanic mixed Black/African American and White, Hispanic only, and “other” due to the limited amount of people per group (this included youth who identified as Asian, American Indian, and other multi-race). Youth also reported on their pubertal development using a 7-item scale adapted from Petersen et al. (1988) (range of scores = 1–4; higher scores indicating more advanced pubertal development).

Caregivers also reported on youth prescription medications including psychiatric medications. Youth who their caregiver reported taking prescription medication, such as antidepressants or anti-anxiety medications (e.g., sertraline, fluoxetine, or citalopram) or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder medications (e.g., methylphenidate or dextroamphetamine-amphetamine) on the survey were categorized as affirmative to current psychotropic medication use. Furthermore, annual household income from caregiver reports was a categorical measure with response categories in $10,000 increments from 0–$10,000 to >$150,000. We collapsed categories into a 4-categorical measure including <$30,000, $30,000–$60,000, $60,000–$150,000, and >$150,000 (reference category). We included the season of data collection in the analysis as depressive and anxiety symptoms may vary by season (Lukmanji et al., 2019). We categorically created the seasons based on the date of the interview with Winter operationalized as December through February, spring as March through May, summer as June through August (reference), and fall as September through November.

Analytic Strategy

We imported data into Stata, Version 17. First, we conducted univariate statistics based on the variable type and included means and standard deviations for continuous measures and frequencies and percentages for categorical measures. We mean-centered the independent continuous variables of family and friend support for ease of interpretation of the interactions. We analyzed the relationships between family and friend emotional support and psychological distress with multiple linear regressions. All regression models controlled for age, sex, race and ethnicity, household income, season of data collection, pubertal development, and psychiatric medication use based on prior research and theory. We examined independent variables for missingness and used chained multiple imputation by creating 25 imputed data sets.

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

The inclusion criteria for this analysis required complete data on the dependent variables of anxiety and depressive symptoms, thus we did not impute data for cases with missing data on the dependent variables. The final analytic sample comprised 1,348 youth with a mean age of 14 years. As seen in Table 1, 48.37% of the youth in the analytic sample identified as non-Hispanic White, 37.76% non-Hispanic Black/African American, 6.23% non-Hispanic mixed Black/African American, and White, 2.82% Hispanic only, 4.45% as other, and 52.67% female. Of the sample, 32.57% had caregiver-reported household income less than $30,000, 29.15% participated in data collection in the summer, and 7.72% reported use of psychiatric medication. The mean pubertal development score was 3 (range 1–4, sd = 0.71). The means for depressive and anxiety symptoms were 1.72 (range 1–3.89, sd = 0.54) and 1.78, (range 1–4, sd = 0.69), respectively. The means for family and friend support were 2.65 (range 1–3, sd = 0.40) and 2.64 (range 1.17–3, sd = 0.35), respectively. The variable with the most missing data was household income, with 93 (6.9%) observations missing.

Descriptive statistics of the sample, stratified by sex, are shown in Table 1. The means for depressive and anxiety symptoms for females were 1.80 (range 1–3.67, sd = 0.57) and 1.91, (range 1–4, sd = 0.73), respectively, and for males they were 1.62 (range 1–3.89, sd = 0.50) and 1.62, (range 1–4, sd = 0.62), respectively. The means for family and friend support for females were 2.62 (range 1–3, sd = 0.43) and 2.66, (range 1.17–3, sd = 0.35), respectively, and for males they were 2.69 (range 1–3, sd = 0.37) and 2.61, (range 1.38–3, sd = 0.33), respectively.

Multiple Linear Regressions

As seen in Table 2, both family and friend support were negatively associated with youth anxiety and depressive symptoms, after controlling for age, sex, race and ethnicity, puberty, household income, psychiatric medication use, and season of data collection. Youth who reported higher family support reported lower depressive symptoms (b = −0.53, p < .001, 95% CI [−0.63, −0.43]) and lower anxiety symptoms (b = −0.39, p < .001, 95% CI [−0.53, −0.25]). Similarly, youth who reported higher friend support reported lower depressive symptoms (b = −0.52, p < .001, 95% CI [−0.64, −0.41]) and lower anxiety symptoms (b = −0.44, p < .001, 95% CI [−0.60, −0.29]). The results of the moderation analysis were not statistically significant, thus the relationship between both family and friend support and anxiety and depressive symptoms were not different based on sex.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the relationships between family and friend support, and youth depressive and anxiety symptoms using data from a large cohort study of adolescents. Our findings on the benefit of different sources of social support on adolescent mental health align with prior research (Bourion-Bédès et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2024). In one longitudinal study of high school students that examined three different types of social support and depressive symptoms, family support was negatively associated with depressive symptoms and buffered the negative effect of stress one year later (Pössel et al., 2018). However, the same study found that friend support was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms once integrated into a model with both teacher and family support, although when examined independently friend support was negatively associated with depressive symptoms. Similarly, the current study found both family and friend support to be independently negatively associated with both anxiety and depressive symptoms. Others have found the quality of social support, beyond just the presence of it, may impact psychological distress differently. For example, recent work finds the absence of positive support, or low familial support, increases the risk of depressive symptoms (Walker & Reisig, 2024).

The current analyses did not find significant differences in the relationship between friend and family support and anxiety and depressive symptoms based on sex. Higher family and friend support were both negatively associated with psychological distress for both females and males. A prior meta-analysis found parental support was more protective for females compared to males in cross-sectional studies (Rueger et al., 2022). However, the same meta-analysis reported no statistically significant gender differences in the relationship between parental support and depression in longitudinal studies (Rueger et al., 2022). It is important to note that the current study measured sex based on two response options—male or female. Thus, it did not take into account gender identity. Future studies should incorporate a broader view of gender identity to examine the potential for variation in the protective effects of different types of social support on psychological distress.

The implications of these findings suggest the need for additional research to further examine the complexities and characteristics of different types of relationships and how they may provide social support for youth. For example, insight into other dimensions of familial relationships may provide a more comprehensive picture of youths’ social and emotional well-being. This may include investigating emotional support, family cohesion, and familial conflict. For example, one study examined the relationship between positive adolescent family relationships (family cohesion and absence of familial conflict) and mid-life mental health (Chen & Harris, 2019). Results showed family cohesion and the absence of parent-child conflict were associated with a lower risk of depression throughout adolescence, young adulthood, and midlife. However, the study did not investigate mechanisms through which family relationships buffered mental health. The current analysis suggests investigating emotional family support as a potential protective factor. Furthermore, future research may examine the reciprocal relationships between support and psychological distress to elucidate the dynamic interplay between the two. For example, prior research has used cross-lagged statistical models to examine relationships between social support and family functioning, finding a bidirectional relationship between them (Yu et al., 2022).

With adolescent mental health concerns on the rise pre-pandemic, and most notably post-pandemic, it is important to look beyond solely risk exposures and identify protective factors. In one recent study, higher friend support pre-COVID-19 was associated with less internalizing problems during COVID-19 (Bernasco et al., 2021). These findings align with the current analysis, suggesting friend emotional support is an important supportive measure for adolescent health and well-being. Further research may investigate the mode of contact between friends, as in-person friendships versus virtual/online friendships may impact psychological distress differently for various groups of youth (Campbell et al., 2019; Rice et al., 2012). Future studies may query youth on the method of contact for each relationship to gain a more nuanced understanding of their support systems.

The current study has several clinical implications related to social support and psychological distress in adolescence, including screening and treatment. Preventative measures for anxiety and depressive symptoms among youth may include screening of existing supportive systems. Advanced practice pediatric nurse practitioners are in optimal positions to routinely assess peer and family support networks during well-patient visits in primary care offices. Additionally, providers are well suited to identify existing support and potential areas of improvement for youth during mental health screenings. Bolstering support early in the life course may be ideal to prevent cumulative psychological distress later. Importantly, both outpatient and inpatient services can incorporate a variety of tools to evaluate sources of existing social support (Arms & McCumber, 2023; Huang et al., 2018).

In addition to screening youth for different types of support systems, well visits may be an opportune time to provide caregivers education related to the importance of strong supportive relationships during adolescence, a critical period of development. For example, primary care providers can provide resources from The American Academy of Pediatrics including HealthyChildren.org which offers information for caregivers navigating their child’s transition through teenage years (The American Academy of Pediatrics, 2024). Pediatric nurse practitioners can reinforce the importance of parents’ role as a supportive caregiver and protective factors for psychological distress.

Public health interventions that strengthen existing supportive ties from family may be a fruitful area for both future research and practice to mitigate the increasing rates of mental health disorders among youth. For example, one study evaluated a caregiver intervention targeted toward improving family functioning among families with youth experiencing depression (Aitken et al., 2023). This group intervention emphasized the importance of family support in decreasing youth mental health problems. Future work should also examine social support interventions that begin earlier in the life course before youth develop depressive or anxiety symptoms to determine effectiveness in improving wellness and preventing psychological distress in addition to ameliorating symptoms once they occur (Halfon & Hochstein, 2002).

As many public health concerns necessitate a multi-level and multi-faceted approach, population-based interventions that incorporate not only family support but also friend support will be useful, as the current study also found higher friend support is related to lower levels of both depressive and anxiety symptoms (Lo Moro et al., 2020). Innovative strategies to prevent psychological distress among youth may include different modes of social support provision such as through technology. For example, one study implemented a peer-support mobile app-based intervention in a school (Birrell et al., 2023). Results showed a significant decrease in depressive symptoms after 12 months among the intervention group compared to the control group. Future intervention work may implement multi-pronged public health interventions that incorporate both friend and family support.

Strengths and Limitations

Limitations of this analysis include the cross-sectional examination of relationships. The findings do not indicate causality. There is a need for more longitudinal research to examine the prospective relationships between supportive systems and psychological distress, as it will be able to capture different aspects of relationships and the provision of social support. For example, longitudinal studies can capture temporal nuances, such as the length of the relationship, the positive and negative aspects of relationships over time, and how different types of relationships impact health during different stages of life. The social support measure in the current analysis only captured the perception of support from friends and family at one moment in time. Another limitation of the current study includes the self-report of health symptoms and social support. The perception of social support at the time of data collection may have impacted the analysis. For example, recent negative interactions with friends or family may have skewed the self-reported perception of support more negatively. Repeated measures of social support and psychological distress over time may be a direction for future research to address these limitations. Lastly, this study examined sex assigned at birth, versus gender identity. Future research is necessary to incorporate the individual experiences of gender and how this may impact the relationships between sex, social support, and psychological distress.

Despite the limitations mentioned above, this analysis had several strengths. The analysis utilized data from a large socioeconomic and racially and ethnically representative cohort study of adolescents residing in a metropolitan area. Furthermore, we examined two different types of support, both family and friend. The analysis found that both family and friend support were associated with lower anxiety and depressive symptoms. Thus, interventions targeted during a critical point in the life course may focus on the modifiable factor of social support from both family and friends.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this analysis highlight the importance of social support in mitigating psychological distress among youth. The implications of this research extend to clinical practice, emphasizing the need for routine assessment of social support networks among youth, such as during well-patient visits and mental health screenings. Identifying and bolstering existing support systems may serve as a preventative measure to reduce the cumulative burden of psychological distress during a critical period of development. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to explore the prospective relationships between social support and psychological distress, taking into account the dynamic nature of relationships and their impact on youth mental health over time. Additionally, this analysis informs future public health and advanced practice nursing interventions, emphasizing the utility of multi-level supportive systems for youth mental health. The findings of this study are in concordance with Healthy People 2030s goal to increase social and community support (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.). Overall, this research underscores the importance of additional research, intervention, practice implications, and policy work surrounding protective factors, such as supportive relationships, to enhance youth health and well-being.
