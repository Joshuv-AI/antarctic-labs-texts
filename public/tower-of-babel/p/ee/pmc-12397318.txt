Aerial Imagery-Derived Dataset of Manufactured Housing Communities in the North Central United States
Authors: Armin Yeganeh, Maria Marshall, Noah Durst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractManufactured housing communities (MHCs) are residential neighborhoods consisting mainly of factory-built homes, providing affordable housing for economically disadvantaged households. There are few, if any, publicly available, systematically created datasets that detail unit-level characteristics of MHCs, e.g., unit location, boundaries, footprint. This article describes a dataset of manufactured housing parks—where multiple units are situated on a single rented parcel—collected from the State of Wisconsin. The dataset is intended to support applications in urban planning, policymaking, and disaster response. The dataset was produced using high-resolution aerial imagery and computer vision techniques to systematically detect and map MHCs. Data collection began by dividing the study area into square tiles for systematic detection. Detected communities were then vectorized, overlaid on statewide parcel maps, and processed to extract relevant attributes. The dataset includes both tabular and geospatial formats, documenting park distribution, spatial extent, and housing unit counts. This dataset provides a reproducible, quality-controlled resource designed for broad reuse across disciplines.Subject terms: Socioeconomic scenarios, Environmental impact

Background & Summary

Manufactured housing communities (MHCs) consist of factory-built homes clustered in residential areas often with shared communal amenities1,2. These communities are crucial for providing affordable housing to diverse populations, including low-income families, retirees, and individuals seeking cost-effective accommodations and homeownership3,4. For academics, non-profits, for-profits, and government organizations, collecting data from these communities is essential to understand their dynamics, challenges, and contributions within the urban landscape5,6. Traditional data collection methods are labor-intensive and offer limited coverage, highlighting the need for innovative solutions. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) mobile home park dataset7, for instance, is a point dataset (x and y coordinates) that has limitations in completeness, accuracy, and standardization, with potential gaps, outdated information, and inconsistent classifications.

In this article, we present a dataset extracted from the Google Maps publicly available tile map service, accessed via the Leafmap Python package8 that systematically captures state-wide manufactured housing park (MHP) data, and we describe the methods used for its collection and processing using aerial imagery and computer vision techniques. The approach covered large geographic areas where manual inspection is impractical and official parcel boundary data lack consistent unit counts or land use classifications. The dataset was generated to improve accuracy and efficiency in data collection, reducing human labor while maximizing precision. It provides information on community distribution, spatial features, and growth patterns. The existing literature on MHCs is limited, with few publicly available, large-scale, systematically collected datasets detailing their physical characteristics. Additionally, there is a lack of comprehensive integration of aerial imagery and computer vision for data collection. This dataset addresses that gap by offering a structured and quality-controlled resource for researchers and policymakers. Documenting the dataset’s creation highlights its potential reuse across urban planning, housing policy, disaster preparedness, and related fields.

Broader Goals: (a) Improved Urban Planning: The primary goal of this dataset is to enhance urban planning efforts by providing accurate and detailed information on the distribution and characteristics of MHPs3. (b) Housing Policy Development: The dataset helps support the development and evaluation of housing policies targeted at MHPs. By understanding the spatial distribution and growth trends of MHPs, policymakers can devise strategies to address housing affordability, community resilience, and social equity9. (c) Environmental Impact Assessment: Understanding the geographical spread and density of MHPs can aid in analyzing factors such as land use, vegetation cover, and proximity to natural resources, which are crucial for sustainable development10. (d) Disaster Preparedness and Response: The dataset can be instrumental in improving disaster preparedness and response strategies. This is particularly important for communities that are often vulnerable to natural disasters11. (e) Academic Research and Innovation: The dataset and methodology are intended to foster academic research and innovation in fields such as geography, urban studies, and computer vision12.

Model training and labeling were conducted solely to produce the dataset, not to introduce a new classification method. Nonetheless, the proposed approach can be used for longitudinal studies to track changes in MHPs over time. Researchers can compare historical data with current information to identify trends, patterns, and the impact of various interventions. The dataset has potential applications across multiple disciplines, including environmental science, public health, sociology, and economics. For example, it can be used to study the relationship between housing conditions and health outcomes or to analyze the socio-economic dynamics of MHPs. Policymakers and government agencies can use the dataset to assess the impact of existing policies and programs on MHPs. Real estate developers, insurance companies, and infrastructure service providers can leverage the dataset to identify opportunities and risks associated with MHPs. The dataset can be used in educational settings to teach students about remote sensing, GIS, urban planning, and data analytics.

As shown in Fig. 1, MHCs typically feature housing units that are consistent in size, shape, orientation, and setback. There are two subtypes of MHCs: (a) Parks, i.e., MHPs, where multiple units are situated on a single rented parcel, and (b) Subdivisions, where individual units are located on separately owned parcels. Manufactured homes vary significantly in size, from smaller, single-section units to larger, multi-section homes. Overall, these homes tend to be more compact than traditional single-family houses. The unique geometric properties and organization of manufactured housing units can help distinguish them from site-built houses.

Methods

Figure 2 provides a schematic overview of the data collection workflow. The diagram outlines the process used for identifying and preparing the dataset of MHCs through image classification and geographic data processing. The process began with the calculation of regions and tiles, involving geographic region partitioning, tile generation, and image retrieval and pre-processing. Following this, a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) application was applied for image classification, which included preparing pre-trained CNN models, configuring training and validation processes, and evaluating model performance. The next phase involved overlaying tiles and creating MHC plots by detecting MHC tiles using deep learning, linking tiles based on geographical proximity, and overlaying images to create community plots. Finally, the process culminated in the preparation of the final dataset by overlaying parcels, cleaning the data, joining secondary data, and preparing the final MHP dataset for use.

Workflow for Identifying and Mapping Manufactured Housing Communities. The process included region and tile calculations, CNN-based image classification, overlaying tiles to create plots, and final dataset preparation. Each stage involved detailed steps from geographic region partitioning to joining secondary data for comprehensive analysis.

We started by dividing the region of interest—State of Wisconsin—into smaller tiles based on a fixed latitude increment (0.001 degrees), then calculated the longitude width of each tile based on the latitude and saved the tile coordinates to a CSV file (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 1) In the second step, the script read the recorded bounding box coordinates from the CSV file and processed each bounding box to download corresponding GeoTIFFs (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 2) In this workflow, the tms_to_geotiff function from the samgeo library retrieved raster tiles from a tile map service (TMS) and mosaicked them into a single georeferenced raster dataset in GeoTIFF format. The samgeo library extends the Segment Anything Model (SAM) for geospatial data processing, enabling object segmentation on satellite or aerial imagery. It interoperates with geospatial visualization libraries such as leafmap, which provides interactive mapping, basemap management, and analysis tools within Python environments8. In our case, aerial imagery tiles were retrieved from the Google Maps TMS between March–June 2024 and mosaicked into GeoTIFFs using samgeo. The resulting imagery was visualized interactively with leafmap, which leverages the ipyleaflet library for rendering and supports overlaying additional basemap layers such as OpenStreetMap. We first applied ROADMAP images to identify and filter the built environment, excluding natural features. We then used SATELLITE images as the primary input for the deep learning detection model. Finally, ROADMAP images were re-used for mapping individual structures and unit boundaries.

The third step had two objectives: first, to filter the downloaded statewide images to only include those that have content, such as buildings and roads. To achieve this, we excluded images with uniform pixel values (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 3) Second, to threshold images by transforming a grayscale version of the images into a binary image, where pixels were classified into two categories: white pixels that represented objects and black pixels that represented the background. Figure 3 presents different cartographic representations of an aerial image of a single tile, which we utilized to distinguish inhabited from uninhabited regions and reduce redundant processing. In the fourth step we further filtered the thresholded images, selecting images that were likely to contain relevant content (such as buildings or roads) for further analysis, while discarding images that were predominantly empty or uniform (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 4).

The typical aspect ratio of manufactured home structures ranges from approximately 2:1 to 3:1. Due to their design, “single-wide” manufactured homes generally have a distinct, linear floor plan. Conversely, “double-wide” manufactured homes consist of two “single-wide” sections that are assembled on-site to create a complete home. Double-wide homes are more difficult to differentiate from site-built homes in aerial images due to their similar aspect ratios. However, when the goal is to detect manufactured housing parks, rather than manufactured housing subdivisions, this difficulty can be addressed by overlaying the vectorized version of the records detected as MHCs onto parcel boundary maps. For example, records were excluded if located on parcels with fewer than an arbitrary threshold of 5 buildings per parcel. We obtained Wisconsin’s parcel boundary shapefiles from the Wisconsin Statewide Parcel Map Database (latest release v11.0.0), made publicly available through the State Cartographer’s Office at the Department of Geography at the University of Wisconsin-Madison13. Esri’s nationwide tile layer of parcel boundaries (Regrid) was additionally used for visual inspection within ArcGIS Pro 3.1.0 (Esri Inc., 2023)14.

After preprocessing, we filtered the bounding boxes file to extract the preprocessed records and retrieved their aerial versions. We then evaluated multiple pretrained architectures for classifying images to distinguish MHCs from non-MHCs with minimal fine-tuning. The models included VGG16, ResNet, MobileNet, Inception V3, EfficientNet, and DenseNet, among others. In this case, the dataset comprised 4,480 standardized images, evenly split between MHCs and non-MHCs. After training and testing, our preferred MHC detection model was MobileNet. MobileNet achieved the highest performance among all tested models, with a loss of 0.06, accuracy of 0.98, precision of 0.98, recall of 0.97, and F1 score of 0.98 using 128 neurons. Even with 32 neurons, it maintained strong performance, with accuracy of 0.97 and F1 score of 0.97, outperforming alternatives like DenseNet121 (F1: 0.96), InceptionV3 (F1: 0.94), and VGG16 (F1: 0.93).

In the fifth step, the script set up and trained a deep learning model to classify MHC versus non-MHC images. (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 5) All deep learning model training was conducted using TensorFlow and Keras in Google Colab (https://colab.research.google.com/), leveraging pretrained models (MobileNet, ResNet, VGG16, Inception V3, EfficientNet, DenseNet) available through the Keras Applications library. These images were combined into arrays, labeled, and split into 80 percent training and 20 percent testing sets. The pixel values were normalized, and a pre-trained MobileNet model (without its top layers) was loaded and modified by adding custom classification layers (a Flatten layer, a Dense layer with 256 units, a Dropout layer, and a final Dense layer with a sigmoid activation function). The model was compiled with the Adam optimizer and binary cross-entropy loss, trained on the image data for 20 epochs, and finally saved to Google Drive for future use. In the next step, we modified Source Code 2 to retrieve the SATELLITE version of the preprocessed images (Source Code 4 outputs) and processed the images to make predictions using the pre-trained model. The script initialized lists to store file names, interpretations, and prediction probabilities (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 6).

The MobileNet model efficiently processed an average of 375 tiles, each 250 × 250 pixels, per minute on a typical PC. It classified 4,000 new data points, evenly divided between MHCs and non-MHCs, with results similar to the abovementioned evaluation metrics. In our experiment, there were 11 percent true positives, 3 percent false positives, 83 percent true negatives, and 3 percent false negatives. The false negatives often occurred in low-density areas where housing units were obscured by buildings or natural elements, making them difficult to detect from aerial views. After classification, we performed a visual inspection to remove false positives from the results. Before mapping MHCs, we removed records that likely belong to the same community. For this purpose, we rounded the coordinates to three decimal places and removed images with duplicate rounded coordinates. Using three decimal places ensured records were close enough to be treated as belonging to the same community, leaving only one representative image per MHC. We then generated the input CSV file for Source Code 7 (mhc-inspected-duplicate-cleaned.csv). This file contained at least the following columns, each with numeric values representing geographic bounding boxes: north (northern latitude), south (southern latitude), east (eastern longitude), and west (western longitude).

In the seventh step we took the representative image from each MHC and expanded the original boundaries by three times the width and height to cover the entire community. A function was defined to determine if two bounding boxes overlapped. If an overlap was found, the script assigned the same group id (community id) to the overlapping bounding boxes, and the resulting DataFrame with the grouped bounding boxes was saved to a new CSV file (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 7) Next, we retrieved the aerial, ROADMAP version of the image—adapting Supplementary Information, Source Code 1—which included building footprints—eliminating the need for a separate building footprint detection and extraction algorithm, as required in the SATELLITE version. Then, we used the downloaded GeoTIFFs that belong to the same group (i.e., same community as defined by overlapping expanded boundaries) and plotted them on a common “canvas” (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 8) The script processed GeoTIFFs in a specified folder, grouped them based on a ‘group’ identifier in their filenames, and then overlaid the images within each group into a single composite image. The script iterated over the TIFF files to extract and group them by their ‘group’ identifier, and then for each group, it calculated the overall geographic extent and overlaid the images using their spatial information. The resulting composite image for each group was saved as a high-resolution PNG file in the output directory.

Once the grouped images were saved as high-resolution PNG files, we thresholded them— adapting the Supplementary Information, Source Code 3. We then, in Google Colab environment, reassigned their geographical coordinates to convert them back to GeoTIFFs (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 9) The script read a CSV file containing bounding box coordinates and group IDs, preserving specific columns. It summarized these variables by grouping the data based on group id to find the maximum and minimum coordinates for each group. The script extracted the group id from each PNG image file name in a specified directory, calculated the spatial resolution and affine transformation matrix for the images, and read the image data. Using rasterio, it created new GeoTIFFs with the geographical coordinates based on the summarized data, saving them in a specified output directory. In the next step (See Supplementary Information, Source Code 10) we wrote a script in ArcGIS Pro environment using the ArcPy library to convert the prepared GeoTIFFs into shapefiles.

By performing the steps, we prepared raw shapefiles of MHCs, which included both the detected MHC structures and the surrounding non-MHC structures (output stored as part of the ncr_states folder). We then cleaned the surrounding non-MHC structures using the base map and parcel map underlays (resulting in the wi_shapefile.shp file at Zenodo). Some of the detected manufactured housing structures might have belonged to manufactured housing subdivisions, while others belonged to manufactured housing parks. In the absence of official land use classification data, the distinction was made based on the number of structures located within a single parcel. If five or more single-wide or double-wide structures fell on a single parcel, we treated that parcel as a manufactured housing park rather than a subdivision. Data Records

The Wisconsin mobile home park dataset described in this article is publicly available at the Zenodo repository Repository for Aerial Imagery-Derived Dataset of Manufactured Housing Communities in the North Central United States15 (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.16459113) and is distributed under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License (CC BY 4.0). All data reused from external sources are either openly licensed or used in accordance with their terms of use. Imagery was retrieved from the Google Maps TMS between March–June 2024. The Wisconsin files and ncr_states folder correspond to the processing steps described in Methods. The Wisconsin dataset includes: (1) Excel file (wi_data.xlsx) containing one row per identified manufactured housing park. Variables include: “designation” (unique identifier for each park), “area” (total square footage of all units within the park), “cent_x” and “cent_y” (X and Y coordinates of the park’s geographic centroid), and “count” (number of housing units detected within each park). (2) Statewide shapefile (wi_shapefile.shp) delineating the geographic boundaries of all identified parks and individual building footprints. (3) Dynamic HTML map (wisconsin.html) allowing users to visually explore the location and spatial extent of the communities through an interactive web-based interface. The repository also contains raw shapefile data for other states in the North Central Region (ncr_states folder, ~320 MB), from which tabular data can be generated after overlaying parcel boundaries. Files are named using state abbreviations (e.g., MI for Michigan), and large states with more manufactured housing communities are split into multiple non-overlapping files (e.g., mi_p1.zip, mi_p2.zip) to reduce file size and simplify management. These files can be combined in GIS software or in Python or R using geospatial packages.

Technical Validation

A series of quality control procedures were implemented to ensure the accuracy, completeness, and reproducibility of the Wisconsin mobile home park dataset deposited at Zenodo. Corrupted and blank image tiles were systematically excluded from analysis. To avoid spatial duplication, tile coordinates were rounded to three decimal places and overlapping or repeated tiles were removed. Manual review was then conducted to filter out false positives and confirm the presence of MHCs. Detected tiles were inspected against parcel boundaries and grouped accordingly, and groups with fewer than five structures per parcel were excluded based on visual and empirical thresholds. During model training, class balance was maintained between MHC and non-MHC tiles, and labeling accuracy was verified against reference imagery. All geospatial outputs were reviewed to ensure proper georeferencing. Final shapefiles were visually reviewed over base maps to confirm spatial accuracy and correct any classification anomalies. All preprocessing and classification scripts used to generate the Zenodo files were version-controlled and are included in the GitHub repository to ensure full transparency and reproducibility.

We also note several limitations relevant to users of this dataset. Certain hyperparameters (e.g., threshold value, kernel size for dilation, white pixel range) were tuned for the imagery sources accessed between March–June 2024 and may require adjustment when applied to imagery from other sources or dates. High-density MHCs were detected with higher accuracy than small or low-density communities, which may have resulted in underrepresentation of the latter. In the case of the state of Wisconsin, we overlaid detected MHCs on official parcel data to distinguish between parks and subdivisions after detecting MHCs using aerial imagery. Users seeking complete coverage may wish to supplement these data with other sources, such as parcel-level unit counts or state-maintained lists of licensed parks, available either directly or upon request through the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). These datasets may include additional information, such as the ‘open’ or ‘closed’ status of parks, but they may not be fully updated and accurate. While the use of parcel boundaries and state datasets enhances the technical validity of the results, detecting small subdivisions remains a challenge. While the dataset has been validated to a high standard, it should not be used as the sole basis for legal determinations.
