Liver Safety of the Dual Neurokinin-1/-3 Receptor Antagonist Elinzanetant
Authors: James H Lewis, Raúl J Andrade, Dominique Larrey, Yves Horsmans, Richard A Anderson, Mila Trajanovic, Esther Groettrup-Wolfers, Lineke Zuurman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElinzanetant is a selective dual neurokinin (NK)-1/-3 receptor antagonist for the treatment of moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms associated with menopause or caused by endocrine therapy. Elinzanetant has been approved by health authorities in several countries. The occurrence of elevated liver enzymes has been observed in clinical use with NK-3 receptor antagonists, e.g., fezolinetant and pavinetant, with recommendations for liver monitoring in the fezolinetant labeling information. Therefore, a thorough evaluation of the liver safety data from clinical trials with elinzanetant was undertaken.ObjectiveTo summarize available hepatic safety data from the clinical development program of elinzanetant.MethodsThe potential risk of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) associated with elinzanetant was evaluated on the basis of the analysis of 24 phase 1 studies, three phase 2 studies (RELENT-1, SWITCH-1, NIRVANA), and four randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 studies (OASIS 1‒4). Liver safety assessments were conducted throughout the studies as per protocol. In the phase 3 studies, a liver safety monitoring board (LSMB) performed an independent and blinded review of cases meeting close liver observation criteria, in alignment with US Food and Drug Administration DILI Guidance and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences 2020 consensus on DILI. An independent data and safety monitoring board monitored the general safety of the participants.ResultsIn the clinical program (phases 1‒3), there was no imbalance between the elinzanetant and placebo groups in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) elevations up to 5×, 10×, or 20× the upper limit of normal (ULN). In the pooled studies that included postmenopausal women, during weeks 1‒52 of treatment, ALT or aspartate transaminase (AST) elevations ≥ 3× ULN were observed in 1.2% of participants treated with elinzanetant 120 mg and in 1.0% of participants treated with placebo. In addition, there were no cases meeting Hy’s law, and no indication of cholestatic injury. The LSMB assessed two cases on elinzanetant (2 out of 1697 participants, 0.12%) and one case on placebo (1 out of 1028, 0.10%) as probably related to study drug. For the cases on elinzanetant, one participant had a peak ALT level 7.4× ULN and the other had a peak ALT 3.5× ULN. Alkaline phosphatase and total bilirubin levels remained within reference range; both cases were mild and reversible. The peak ALT for the participant on placebo was 3.6× ULN. No case was classified as highly likely or definite.ConclusionsThe aggregated phase 1‒3 clinical data, supported by preclinical toxicology and individual case adjudications of participants with elevated liver enzymes, suggest that there is a low risk of liver toxicity for elinzanetant. Key Points

Current evidence shows that elinzanetant 120 mg carries a low risk of liver problems in women experiencing hot flashes related to menopause or related to the use of endocrine therapy for breast cancer.An independent liver safety board concluded that liver tests are not needed after approval of elinzanetant by health authorities because no serious liver issues were found, and mild changes were similar between the elinzanetant-treated group and the placebo-treated group.

Introduction

Up to 80% of postmenopausal women experience vasomotor symptoms (VMS), also known as hot flashes, which negatively affect their quality of life and can result in significant morbidity [1, 2]. VMS are also commonly reported adverse reactions to endocrine therapy for breast cancer and are often more severe than the VMS associated with natural menopause [3].

The kisspeptin, neurokinin B, dynorphin (KNDy) neurons, a group of estrogen-sensitive neurons present in the thermoregulatory center of the hypothalamus, have been implicated in the pathophysiology of VMS [2, 4, 5]. KNDy neurons express both the neurokinin (NK)-3 and NK-1 receptor ligands and are hypertrophied and hyperactive during menopause owing to estrogen decline [2, 5]. Selective NK-3 and NK-1/-3 receptor antagonists that can restore KNDy neuron activity have been investigated and approved by health authorities as nonhormonal treatment options for the management of VMS [5–7].

Elinzanetant is a nonhormonal, selective dual NK-1 and NK-3 receptor antagonist, demonstrated to be efficacious for the treatment of moderate-to-severe VMS associated with menopause (OASIS 1, 2, 3 studies) [7, 8], or with endocrine therapy (OASIS 4 study) [6]. During the development of elinzanetant, the potential risk for drug-induced liver injury (DILI) was continuously assessed. The elinzanetant molecule does not show any of the known structural features that have been associated with hepatotoxicity [9–12] and does not share similar substructures with other NK-3 receptor antagonists (Fig. 1). Pavinetant exhibits a substructure alert for S-alkyl-sulfonamide, which is strongly associated with DILI [9]. A possible metabolic mechanism of fezolinetant’s hepatotoxicity might be the formation of a reactive carbene moiety at the methyl-thiadiazole after oxidation of the methyl group, although the clinical relevance is not clear [13]. Rather, elinzanetant is also an NK-1 antagonist. Approved NK-1 antagonists (aprepitant, fosnetupitant, and rolapitant) are considered in LiverTox as having a likelihood score of E, meaning an unlikely cause of clinically apparent liver injury [10].

Structure of NK-3 receptor antagonists relative to elinzanetant. Red circles indicate structural features that are hypothesized to be associated with hepatotoxicity that has been observed with the respective molecules

Elinzanetant is primarily metabolized through oxidative biotransformation via cytochrome P450 (CYP)3A4 [14]. None of the major metabolites of elinzanetant (M30/34, M27, and M18/21) contain substructures known to cause hepatotoxicity [14]. Monitoring for the formation of conjugates of metabolites of elinzanetant with glutathione in humans gave no indication for significant formation of reactive intermediates in vivo [14].

A weak or nonsignificant increase of total bilirubin (TBL) concentration in the blood was observed in clinical and toxicologic studies. This suggests that no interaction with transporter proteins, particularly bile salt export pump and multidrug resistance-associated protein 2, is expected at clinically relevant exposure levels of elinzanetant (manuscript in preparation).

Liver tissue does not express the NK-3 (TACR3) receptor and there is only minor expression of the NK-1 receptor (TACR1) [15–18], indicating that hepatotoxicity should not be viewed as an on-target effect of the class of NK-1/NK-3 receptor antagonists. The metabolic pathways of elinzanetant also do not raise any concern for adverse effects on the liver [14]. During the development of elinzanetant, no clinically relevant interaction with relevant enzymes and transporters was identified, and toxicology studies in animals with repeated dosing of elinzanetant for up to 2 years did not raise any potential issues.

The hepatic safety findings reported during the clinical development programs of the NK-3 receptor antagonists fezolinetant and pavinetant [19–21] initially raised questions around elinzanetant because of blocking the same receptor, and fezolinetant having a likelihood score of D, meaning a possible rare cause of clinically apparent liver injury [10]. Fezolinetant was approved by multiple health authorities for the treatment of VMS associated with menopause, with and without requests for liver monitoring. However, postmarketing reports prompted the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to issue a warning about the rare risk of serious liver injury with the use of fezolinetant [20]. Additionally, the drug information for prescribers has been updated to reflect the need for monitoring of liver enzymes before and during treatment [19, 20, 22].

These findings highlight the importance of identifying drugs likely to cause DILI as early as possible during drug development, with close monitoring of liver enzyme elevations in clinical studies [23–25]. Here, we provide an overview of hepatic safety from the clinical development program of elinzanetant.

Methods

Elinzanetant Clinical Development Program

A total of 24 phase 1 studies in healthy male or female volunteers were conducted. Elinzanetant was administered using different formulations and different doses, in single or repeated dose regimens. Two studies included participants with preexisting hepatic or renal impairment. Overall, 537 participants were treated with elinzanetant and 245 with placebo.

Data were also reviewed from three placebo-controlled phase 2 studies in postmenopausal women with moderate-to-severe VMS: RELENT-1 (NCT02865538) [26], SWITCH-1 (NCT03596762) [27], and NIRVANA (NCT06112756). The NIRVANA study was conducted in parallel with the phase 3 program.

Phase 3 consisted of three pivotal randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled multicenter studies, OASIS 1, 2, and 4, that assessed the efficacy and safety of elinzanetant 120 mg in two different populations. OASIS 1 (NCT05042362) and OASIS 2 (NCT05099159) included postmenopausal women with moderate-to-severe VMS associated with menopause. OASIS 4 (NCT05587296) included women with moderate-to-severe VMS associated with endocrine therapy (either tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors, with or without the use of gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues) who were either diagnosed with, or at high risk for, hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. In OASIS 1, 2, and 4, women received elinzanetant 120 mg for 26 weeks or matching placebo for 12 weeks, followed by elinzanetant 120 mg for 14 weeks [6, 7]. Additionally, OASIS 4 extended to 52 weeks and is currently in a long-term extension phase with treatment up to 3.5 years. OASIS 3 (NCT05030584) investigated the efficacy and safety of elinzanetant 120 mg versus placebo for the treatment of moderate-to-severe VMS associated with menopause over 52 weeks [8].

Participants were excluded from enrollment in the OASIS 1–4 studies when one or more of the following was applicable: aspartate aminotransferase (AST) or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) > 2× the upper limit of normal (ULN), alkaline phosphatase (AP) > 2× ULN, TBL > ULN unless explained by Gilbert’s syndrome, international normalized ratio (INR) > ULN unless explained by, e.g., intake of anticoagulants, or a diagnosis of hepatitis B or C at screening.

In SWITCH-1, NIRVANA, the phase 1 Driving Performance study [28], and the phase 3 OASIS studies, the same formulation of elinzanetant was used with different dose strengths. All these studies included only postmenopausal women, except for OASIS 4, with a total of 1697 women participating.

Liver Safety Assessments

Hepatic safety was continuously monitored during the clinical studies using protocol-specified hepatic exclusion criteria, liver safety laboratory assessments, and predefined stopping criteria. In the phase 1 and 2 studies, participants were excluded if they had a history of liver insufficiency, significant liver disease, or other related conditions, except for one study that included participants with hepatic impairment. Each protocol specified additional exclusion criteria concerning important liver parameters (such as AST, ALT, TBL, gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT], or positive hepatitis B or C serology). During the studies, liver safety assessments were conducted pre-dose, repeatedly after the administration of elinzanetant, and during the follow-up period. The protocols defined liver safety stopping criteria, which were based on the occurrence of clinical events, or laboratory results that were grounds for discontinuation from the study per investigator assessment.

In the late-phase development program, close liver observation was implemented in alignment with the DILI Guidance from the FDA [25] and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) DILI Working Group [24]. Close liver observation was initiated, if confirmed by a retest, when levels of ALT and/or AST were > 3× ULN, and/or AP was > 2× ULN, to cast as wide a net as possible for potential DILI. All cases meeting close liver observation criteria were independently reviewed in a blinded fashion by a liver safety monitoring board (LSMB). Further details of the LSMB assessment are described in Sect. 2.3. In the phase 3 studies, instructions for treatment discontinuation were provided (Table 1). An extensive workup for viral, autoimmune, and other causes was also performed to exclude alternative etiologies. An overview of the samples to be taken for analysis of clinical laboratory parameters during close liver observation is provided in Supplementary Table S1. An assessment of concomitant drugs, including nonprescription medications, herbal and dietary supplements, alcohol use, recreational drug use, exposure to environmental chemical agents, and special diets, was also undertaken. Liver imaging and consultation with gastroenterologists or hepatologists at the study sites was conducted, as clinically indicated.

Review and Analysis of Clinical Liver Safety Data

Liver safety study data were reviewed on an ongoing basis to ensure that any potential risk would be identified promptly. To prevent bias, in the phase 3 studies, all cases meeting close liver observation criteria were reviewed independently by the LSMB, consisting of three external experts experienced with assessing clinical cases of potential DILI. LSMB members were blinded to treatment assignment as well as to each other’s assessments and could not change their assessment afterward. The close liver observation cases were assessed for severity of DILI on the basis of modified Aithal criteria [29]. The causal relationship (likelihood) to study intervention was assessed by the LSMB for each case according to the scoring categories defined in the US DILI Network prospective study (Supplementary Table S2) [30]. If there was no consensus among the three reviewers, a meeting was scheduled to discuss the case, which was still blinded to treatment, and to reach agreement. The consensus assessment was then approved and signed by all LSMB members, and the assessment could not be changed afterward. All assessments were documented in a standardized process in the clinical study database. The LSMB was informed about treatment assignments after database lock.

In addition to the close liver observation cases from the phase 3 studies, the LSMB also assessed, in a blinded fashion, three cases of increased liver enzymes occurring in studies conducted in parallel with the phase 3 program: one case from a phase 1 Driving Performance study and two cases from the phase 2 NIRVANA study.

An independent data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) monitored in an unblinded fashion the general safety, including hepatic safety, of the participants in the phase 3 studies while the studies were ongoing.

Data from the phase 3 studies OASIS 1, 2, and 3 were pooled. Since the same formulation of elinzanetant 120 mg was used in the phase 2b SWITCH-1 study, the 120 mg treatment arm was added to the pooled dataset. Data were analyzed up to week 12 (placebo-controlled period) and up to week 52. Data from the OASIS 4 study were analyzed separately up to week 12 (the placebo-controlled period) and up to week 52. For comparative analysis of elinzanetant versus placebo, exposure-adjusted incidence rates were calculated according to Crowe et al. [31]. These results are presented per 100 patient-years (PY), where 1 PY is defined as 365.25 days. For study participants with close liver observation, liver biochemistries over time plots were generated. Evaluation of drug-induced serious hepatotoxicity (eDISH) plots for hepatocellular and cholestatic injury were also generated. In the eDISH plots for hepatocellular injury, each data point represents a participant plotted by their maximum ALT or AST versus their maximum TBL values in the post-baseline period. A potential Hy’s law case was defined as having any post-baseline TBL ≥ 2× ULN within 30 days after a post-baseline ALT or AST ≥ 3× ULN, and AP < 2× ULN. All participants with at least one post-baseline ALT or AST and TBL are plotted in the eDISH plots for cholestatic injury, with each data point representing a participant plotted by their maximum AP versus their maximum TBL values in the post-baseline period. A potential cholestatic DILI case was defined as having a maximum post-baseline TBL ≥ 2× ULN within 30 days after a post-baseline AP ≥ 2× ULN.

Results

In total, 20 cases in the phase 1 Driving Performance study, phase 2 NIRVANA study, and phase 3 OASIS 1‒4 studies fulfilled close liver observation criteria. Of these, 0.9% (n = 15) occurred during elinzanetant treatment and 0.5% (n = 5) during placebo treatment. The LSMB assessed two cases on elinzanetant (2 out of 1697 participants, 0.12%) and one case on placebo (1 out of 1028, 0.10%) as probably related to study drug. Phase 1 Clinical Data

Overall, 8/782 participants in the phase 1 studies had clinically relevant increases in liver parameters, three of whom had preexisting hepatic impairment, and another three had increased liver enzymes > 1 week after the last dose of elinzanetant. One participant had increased liver enzymes on both elinzanetant and placebo treatment. Prespecified liver-related stopping criteria were met for two participants during elinzanetant treatment (2/537), who are discussed below. Case 1: On day 10 of treatment with elinzanetant, the participant was found to have increased liver enzymes meeting DILI criteria. Her blood biochemistry tests peaked at: ALT 261 U/L (5.7× ULN), AST 153 U/L (3.7× ULN), and GGT 318 U/L (5.0× ULN), while AP and TBL remained within the reference range. She was asymptomatic. Her viral and autoimmune workup was negative except for prior Epstein–Barr virus infection. Study treatment was withdrawn and she recovered. The sponsor considered the event unlikely to be related to study treatment owing to the participants’ elevated GGT levels (up to 2.4× ULN during the pretreatment screening period), the presence of fatty liver, and a history of elevated aminotransferase levels, and attributed the fluctuations in ALT and AST to her underlying liver disease. This case did not meet the criteria for Hy’s law or cholestatic injury (Fig. 2).

Case 2: On day 3 of treatment with elinzanetant 120 mg in treatment period 4 of the Driving Performance study, the participant was found to have increased liver enzymes not meeting DILI criteria [28]. Her blood biochemistry tests peaked at: ALT 125 U/L (3.5× ULN) and AST 89 U/L (2.9× ULN), while AP and TBL remained within reference range (Fig. 3). She experienced mild nausea lasting 2 days. Her viral and autoimmune workup was negative, while an abdominal ultrasound showed probable mild hepatic steatosis. Over the course of the study, including a preceding treatment period with elinzanetant 240 mg, the participant had intermittently elevated ALT, with and without AST elevations, that did not reach the close liver observation criteria. Study treatment was withdrawn, and she recovered. The LSMB assessed the causality as probably related to elinzanetant owing to a positive de-challenge response and the extensive negative workup for viral and autoimmune causes. ALT elevations were not considered to reflect steatosis fluctuations, since ALT was normal at screening, and no elevations were seen during the time when she might have received placebo. Bilirubin remained normal, and the severity of liver enzyme elevations was considered mild. A possible alternative etiology, however, was identified as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. This case did not meet the criteria for Hy’s law or cholestatic injury (Fig. 3, Table 3).

Overall, in the phase 1 studies (782 participants, of whom 537 were treated with elinzanetant), there were no cases meeting Hy’s law criteria or having cholestatic injury.

Phase 2 Clinical Data

No increases in ALT or AST exceeding 3× ULN were observed among the 76 participants in the RELENT-1 study. In SWITCH-1, 6/199 participants had elevated liver enzymes reported as adverse events, of which one occurred on placebo treatment. The severity of all events was assessed as mild or moderate by the investigator. Sporadic elevations in AST and/or ALT were observed, with increases of up to 3× ULN noted across all treatment groups, including the placebo group (although most increases were ≤ 1.5× ULN). One participant in the elinzanetant 80 mg arm had an ALT level ≥ 9× ULN at week 4; however, elinzanetant was not detectable in plasma samples collected at weeks 2 and 4. Additionally, another participant in the same treatment arm had an increase in creatine kinase, with a peak change from baseline of 2171 U/L, yet elinzanetant plasma concentrations were not measurable at any study visit. The absence of detectable elinzanetant plasma concentrations indicated noncompliance with the study medication, leading to the conclusion that these events were unrelated to elinzanetant.

In total, 2/110 participants in the NIRVANA study had ALT and/or AST ≥ 3× ULN. One occurred during elinzanetant 120 mg treatment (Case 3), and one occurred during placebo treatment. The case occurring on placebo was assessed by the LSMB as mild liver injury of hepatocellular pattern, with causality to study treatment considered unlikely.

Case 3: On day 92 of treatment, the participant was found to have increased liver enzymes meeting DILI criteria [29]. Her blood biochemistry tests peaked 6 days after the last dose of elinzanetant at: ALT 259 U/L (7.4× ULN) and AST 159 U/L (4.5× ULN), while AP was 1.2× ULN, and TBL remained within the reference range. She was asymptomatic. Her viral and autoimmune workup was negative except for immunoglobulin G at 1.2× ULN. The participant had a history of increased hepatic enzymes and metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease (MASH). Over the course of the study, including screening, the participant had intermittently elevated aminotransferases that did not reach the close liver observation criteria (ALT and AST peak values were: 1.9× ULN and 1.7× ULN, respectively). The participant recovered. The LSMB assessed the causality as probably related to elinzanetant, with MASH as a potential confounder. While TBL remained normal and the liver injury was considered mild and reversible, the case was assessed as consistent with DILI owing to the compatible time to onset, positive de-challenge, and lack of an alternative etiology. This case did not meet the criteria for Hy’s law (Fig. 4, Table 3).

Overall, in the phase 2 studies (385 participants; 265 treated with any dose of elinzanetant, 120 receiving placebo), there were no cases meeting Hy’s law criteria or cholestatic injury.

Phase 3 Clinical Data: VMS Associated with Menopause (OASIS 1, 2, and 3)

Data presented in this section include the pooled data from phase 3 studies OASIS 1, 2, and 3, along with the 120 mg treatment arm from the phase 2b SWITCH-1 study, as the same formulation of elinzanetant was used.

At baseline, elevated values ≥ 1× ULN but < 2× ULN for ALT, AST, and TBL were observed in 13.2%, 7.3%, and 0.9% of participants in the elinzanetant group and in 14.9%, 7.8%, and 1.3% in the placebo group. For AP, elevated baseline values ≥ 1.5× ULN but < 2× ULN were observed in 1.4% of participants in the elinzanetant group and in 1.1% in the placebo group.

Exposure-adjusted incidence rates (per 100 PY) for participants meeting predefined categories of liver enzyme elevations during weeks 1‒12 and weeks 1‒52 by treatment group are summarized for the pooled studies in Table 4. Case 4: This participant was receiving endocrine therapy with letrozole following a diagnosis of stage II breast cancer 9 months prior to starting study treatment (elinzanetant 120 mg); other medical history considered relevant to the hepatic findings included hepatic steatosis. On day 32 of elinzanetant treatment, the participant had increased aminotransferases, meeting DILI criteria: ALT 17.1× ULN and AST 24.7× ULN, with AP 1.8× ULN and TBL 1.25× ULN. Symptoms of yellow skin, fever, and upper abdominal pain were also reported. Study treatment was stopped on day 35. Ultrasound examination revealed hepatic steatosis, and further ultrasound and computerized tomography findings in the following weeks included narrowing bile excretory ducts. Close liver observation monitoring reported peak levels of: ALT 599 U/L (17.1× ULN), AST 866 U/L (24.7× ULN), TBL 3.3 mg/dL (2.8× ULN), and AP 629 U/L (6.1× ULN) (Supplementary Fig. S2), and liver biochemistries returned to normal range by 16 weeks after the initial report. Although these acute liver enzyme elevations met criteria for moderate liver injury as assessed by the LSMB, the case did not meet the definition of Hy’s law using a nR value (ALT or AST, whichever was highest × ULN/AP × ULN), which was < 5 according to Robles-Diaz et al. [32]. The LSMB considered the alternative etiology of a passed gallstone the more likely cause, on the basis of the acuteness of the rise in AST > ALT and AP with bilirubin > 2× ULN in the presence of acute abdominal pain. However, the causality related to study treatment was assessed as “possible” owing to the absence of choledocholithiasis on imaging, which was only performed 14 days after the initial detection of the elevated enzymes.

Discussion

DILI is a significant clinical concern in drug development, as it can lead to the termination of clinical development programs or to market withdrawal [33]. Various classes of medications have been associated with hepatotoxicity [34–36]. However, within drug classes, there is variability in the risk of hepatotoxicity [11, 37–39]. For example, among macrolide antibiotics, troleandomycin and erythromycin have different hepatotoxicity risks compared with midecamycin and josamycin. There are also differences among the quinolones. Ciprofloxacin and moxifloxacin are considered safe for use, while trovafloxacin is hepatotoxic and has been withdrawn from the market. Similarly, tolcapone is associated with a higher risk of hepatotoxicity than entacapone. In the case of thiazolidinediones, troglitazone poses a greater risk compared with rosiglitazone and pioglitazone. These examples demonstrate that it is not uncommon for drugs with similar mechanisms of action, including neurokinin therapies, to have different safety profiles. This could also apply to neurokinin therapies, where hepatotoxicity may have a molecular-specific effect rather than a class effect. On the basis of the preclinical data of elinzanetant, the risk for hepatotoxicity was predicted to be low, which was confirmed by the phase 1 and 2 data.

The present analysis shows that among 1697 women who received elinzanetant 120 mg for the treatment of moderate-to-severe VMS, only two cases meeting close liver observation criteria were assessed by the LSMB as being probably related to elinzanetant; both were mild and reversible and neither met Hy’s law criteria.

There was no indicator of a potential for severe DILI as defined by the FDA DILI Guidance, considering the absence of imbalances in ALT elevations, and there were no marked differences in elevations of ALT or AST up to 5×, 10×, or 20× ULN between the elinzanetant and placebo groups.

Owing to concurrent endocrine therapy with tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors, as well as potential prior chemotherapy (which may be hepatotoxic) for breast cancer, women experiencing VMS associated with endocrine therapy can be considered at a higher risk of developing liver toxicity compared with the generally healthy population. However, the liver safety of the OASIS 4 population treated with elinzanetant 120 mg was largely comparable to the liver safety of the OASIS 1, 2, and 3 population. Among the 465 women with VMS due to endocrine therapy who received elinzanetant, there was no case with a causality considered probably related to elinzanetant. One potential Hy’s law case did not meet the definition of Hy’s law since the nR value (ALT or AST, whichever was highest × ULN/AP × ULN) as per Robles-Diaz et al. [32] was < 5.

Taken together, these findings indicate a low risk of hepatotoxicity associated with the use of elinzanetant 120 mg in women with VMS associated with menopause and in women with moderate-to-severe VMS associated with endocrine therapy who were either diagnosed with, or at high risk for, hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. The risk of hepatotoxicity is considered low owing to the fairly balanced number of participants with ALT or AST elevations ≥ 3× ULN: 1.2% of participants treated with elinzanetant 120 mg and 1.0% of participants treated with placebo. For fezolinetant, a clearer imbalance was reported, 2.3% of participants receiving fezolinetant and 0.9% of participants treated with placebo (LiverTox). This low risk of hepatotoxicity should be balanced with the benefits of elinzanetant for the treatment of VMS. In all phase 3 studies, elinzanetant reached a clinically meaningful reduction in daily VMS frequency compared with placebo [6–8]. In addition, elinzanetant improved the severity of VMS, sleep disturbances, and menopause-related quality of life. These benefits of elinzanetant for the treatment of bothersome menopausal symptoms outweigh the low risk of hepatotoxicity associated with its use.

Conclusions

On the basis of the preclinical data of elinzanetant, the risk for hepatotoxicity was predicted to be low, which was confirmed by the clinical development program for elinzanetant. The present analysis shows that among 1697 women receiving elinzanetant 120 mg for the treatment of moderate-to-severe VMS, only 3 out of the 20 cases meeting close liver observation criteria were assessed by the LSMB as probably related to study treatment (one on placebo and two on elinzanetant). Both cases on elinzanetant were mild in severity, reversible, and neither met Hy’s law criteria. Overall, ALT elevations were very similar between elinzanetant-treated women and placebo recipients. On the basis of a totality of these data, the risk of significant hepatotoxicity with elinzanetant is low, and the need for liver test monitoring in clinical practice was considered unnecessary by the LSMB.
