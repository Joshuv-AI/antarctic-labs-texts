The 10‐year status of pediatric gastroenterologists' perception of their roles in the management of childhood obesity
Authors: Sara Karjoo, Senthilkumar Sankararaman, Fotini Tania Mitsinikos, Marwa Abu El Haija, Sheela Mahendra, Ayesha Baig, Ruba A Abdelhadi, Ruben E Quiros‐Tejeira, Timothy Sentongo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2013, a survey of NASPGHAN members on their involvement in managing pediatric obesity revealed that most pediatric gastroenterologists viewed their roles as primarily supportive, focusing on the management of obesity‐related gastrointestinal comorbidities. This 10‐year follow‐up survey, which targeted pediatric gastroenterology programs and practices, found similar perceptions, along with an increased interest in learning about obesity pharmacotherapy. Additionally, most programs and practices reported limited access to dedicated dietitians, a lack of funding, and a desire for greater institutional support for multidisciplinary programs addressing obesity. Notably, none of the funded programs cited philanthropy as a source of funding. These findings underscore the need for greater advocacy to increase dietitian support and educational opportunities in obesity pharmacotherapy, the development of multidisciplinary teams, and the exploration of philanthropic support for innovative programs in the management of children with obesity.Keywords: dietitian, nutrition education, pediatric gastroenterology, research survey

INTRODUCTION

The percentage of children and adolescents affected by obesity has more than tripled since the 1970s 1 and has reached epidemic levels in the United States. 2 Approximately 40 million children under the age of 5 years and more than 330 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years were overweight or obese. 3 In 2013, Lin et al. 4 on behalf of the NASPGHAN nutrition committee and the Obesity Task Force, conducted a web‐based survey of nutrition education among individual pediatric gastroenterologists and also specifically examined whether they were adequately equipped to deal with the pediatric obesity epidemic in both nutritional counseling and management of obesity comorbidities. Pediatric gastroenterologists expressed interest in education about the management of obesity; however, there was no consensus about their role in obesity care. Most identified their role in treating the gastrointestinal and hepatic comorbidities of obesity and indicated that management should require collaboration among multiple pediatric subspecialists in a multidisciplinary approach. Many respondents stated that they did not have obesity programs or centers.

During the 10 years since Lin et al.'s publication, the prevalence of pediatric obesity has increased, pediatric obesity classification guidelines have been revised, 5 and a new nomenclature for obesity‐associated hepatobiliary complications has been published. 6 A recent study compared childhood obesity rates between 1999/2000 and 2017/2018 and found that the prevalence of obesity in 6–11‐year‐old and 12–19‐year‐old children had increased further from 15.8% to 19.3% and from 16% to 20.9%, respectively. 7 Childhood obesity is associated with an increased likelihood of progression to adulthood obesity, premature death, and disability. 3 Management of pediatric overweight/obesity and related complications involves a multidisciplinary and multifaceted approach, including dietary intervention, increasing physical activity, other lifestyle modifications, medications, and bariatric surgery. 8 , 9 The rising prevalence of pediatric obesity and its association with potentially lethal long‐term consequences is a call for higher prioritization and implementation of effective treatment and prevention programs across all subspecialties. Informal reports suggest that there has been a correspondingly increased engagement of pediatric gastroenterologists in the management of obesity; however, this is not accompanied by appropriate levels of program funding, clinic staffing, and patient care reimbursement.

Therefore, the purpose of this follow‐up survey was to reassess the status of pediatric gastroenterologists' engagement in the management of obesity and to determine whether there are common programmatic and labor needs that can benefit from advocacy at the societal level. This is a short communication of the survey results, including general comparisons with Lin et al.'s 4 findings.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The survey idea was initiated by members of the NASPGHAN Nutrition Committee, approved by the NASPGHAN Survey‐Review Task Force, and subsequently approved by the local Institutional Review Board at the University of Chicago, Chicago, IL.

Development of survey

The survey was not commissioned by the NASPGHAN council. Therefore, the results and interpretation of the survey do not represent NASPGHAN guidelines or position on the topic. Several questions were extrapolated from Lin et al.'s 4 survey, into four sections: (1) Program/practice demographics and geographic location in the United States, Mexico, and Canada. The United States was divided into seven regions as follows: New England region (ME, RI, CT, NH, MA); Mid‐Atlantic region (NY, NJ, PA); Southern region (VA, WV, KY, DE, MD, NC, SC, TN, AK, LA, FL, GA, AL, MS); Mid‐west region (MI, ND, SD, IA, MN, KS, NE, OH, IN, IL, WI, MO); South‐west region (TX, AZ, NM, OK); Rocky Mountains (MT, ID, CO, UT, WY, NV); Pacific Coastal (CA, OR, WA, AK, HI). (2) Availability of an obesity program, involvement of pediatric gastroenterologists in obesity care, and their perceived roles. (3) Access to dietitians and supportive staff. (4) Perceived educational needs and correctable deficits limiting management of pediatric obesity. See Supporting Information: Appendices 1 and 2, for the survey questions and results.

Distribution of survey

The survey was distributed through the NASPGHAN webmail to Program directors or practice leaders of all the self‐identified pediatric gastroenterology programs and practices based in the United States, Canada, and Mexico, and listed in the NASPGHAN membership directory. The survey link included an invitation letter with instructions from the Nutrition Committee. For non‐responders, the invitation link was automatically resent every 2 weeks for 6 weeks (February 2023–March 2023) and then terminated. The program directors or practice leaders were requested to complete the survey themselves or delegate it to a representative on their team who was judged most engaged or knowledgeable in managing pediatric obesity. Therefore, only one response was permitted per program or practice. The NASPGAHN web‐based platform SurveyMonkey was utilized to collect data from respondents. The survey respondents were anonymous.

Data analysis plan

Reporting was limited to descriptive statistics, actual numbers, and percentages.

RESULTS

Demographics of the participating programs

Out of 125 pediatric gastroenterology programs listed in the NASPGHAN directory, 65 (52%) responded to the survey. Among the respondents, 50 (77%) were academic programs, with 43 (86%) having nine or more fellows. The 15 nonacademic programs (23%) included eight hospitals or group practices, with 11–50 physicians, three with ≥51 physicians, and four with 2–10 physicians. Most respondents (78%) were from the United States, with representation from various regions: New England (3%), Mid‐Atlantic (12%), Southern (26%), Mid‐West (26%), South‐West (4.6%), Rocky Mountains (1.5%), and Pacific Coastal (4.6%), along with 12.3% from Mexico and 9.2% from Canada. Involvement of pediatric gastroenterologists and their perceived roles

Thirty‐nine (60%) respondents had obesity programs, 15 (23%) had no program but had referral access to local treatment, and 6 (9.2%) had neither. The primary reasons for referring children with obesity to gastroenterologists included elevated liver enzymes (87%), ultrasound findings of fatty liver (71%), and obesity with comorbidities (40%). Commonly screened comorbidities were increased ALT (84%), dyslipidemia (84%), and pre‐diabetes (75%). Treatment for dyslipidemia included referrals to lipid experts (79%), dietary changes, and exercise (65%), along with medications (27%). Opinions varied on who should lead obesity management: 6% said gastroenterologists, 37% suggested pediatric obesity specialists, 29% preferred primary care providers (PCPs), and 11% favored pediatric endocrinologists. Most (74%) viewed their role as supportive, focused on managing gastrointestinal comorbidities (91%) and conducting nutritional assessments (58%).

Access to supportive staff

Only 34 (53%) respondents had access to a full‐time dietitian, while 11 (17%) had part‐time access, and 20 (30%) had no access. Despite this, 55 (84%) believed dietitians play a crucial role in managing obesity through routine care and counseling. Additionally, only 14 (21%) had access to a physical therapist or athletic trainer for obesity management.

Perceived educational needs and program deficits

Forty‐two respondents (70%) were neutral or felt there were insufficient treatment options for adolescents with obesity, while only 18 (28%) used weight loss medications. Sixty percent indicated a lack of funding for their obesity programs, with only 26 (40%) reporting funding: 15 from intramural grants, eight from unspecified sources, and three from research grants. No funding came from philanthropic support. The most highly prioritized topics for educational didactics included obesity pharmacotherapy, when to refer for bariatric surgery, and securing support for multidisciplinary obesity management teams.

DISCUSSION

In this regionally representative sample of pediatric GI programs and practices in North America, the most frequent reason for referring children with overweight/obesity to pediatric gastroenterologists was a history of elevated liver enzymes, abnormal hepatic ultrasound. Most respondents (91%) felt that their main role was managing the hepatic and GI comorbidities associated with obesity. Most programs (84%) felt that dietitians played a central role in managing childhood obesity; however, only 53% had a full‐time dietitian, and 30% had no access to a dietitian. While 70% were neutral or felt there were insufficient treatment options for adolescent obesity, only 28% used weight management medications. Many programs (60%) lacked funding for obesity care, with philanthropy not being a source for those that did. The top topics for further education included pharmaceutical management of obesity, bariatric surgery referral guidelines, and securing hospital support for a multidisciplinary team.

The findings in this contemporary survey of pediatric gastroenterology programs and practices demonstrate that perceptions among pediatric gastroenterologists regarding the management of pediatric obesity have remained unchanged over the past decade. The Lin et al. 4 survey of individual NASPGHAN members reported that most pediatric gastroenterologists perceived their role as primarily supportive in treating GI and hepatic comorbidities of obesity, rather than being the leader (see Figure 1). This similarity is not surprising, as the management of obesity involves multidisciplinary care with PCPs, pediatric subspecialists (endocrinologists, sleep medicine specialists, orthopedic surgeons, cardiologists, lipidologists), dietitians, physical therapists/athletic trainers, behavioral psychologists, and social workers. 10

A novel finding of this study was that only 1.54% of respondents felt that pediatric gastroenterologists had no role in managing obesity. Therefore, the overwhelming majority's acknowledgement of the need for gastroenterologists' participation in the care of pediatric obesity is likely not only due to the increasing prevalence of obesity in children, but also due to constant advocacy efforts from pediatric gastroenterology societies worldwide. 11 , 12 , 13 Other significant findings included the limited availability of dedicated dietitians in most programs. This is compounded by the increased demands on dietetic services due to the increasing prevalence of obesity in the population. 14 Therefore, increased advocacy for dietitians is crucial because weight management interventions led by multidisciplinary teams that involve dietitians are more effective in achieving greater weight loss and improved cardiometabolic outcomes. 15 , 16

Lifestyle interventions involving dietary approaches and physical activity remain the foundation for the management of obesity; however, recent studies have demonstrated that pharmacological therapies, such as GLP‐1 agonists, may play an important role in achieving significant weight loss, decreasing obesity‐related complications, and enhancing outcomes in severe stages of obesity. 10 , 17 As these newer agents enter clinical practice, gastroenterologists are responding appropriately to the knowledge gap, which lends itself to incorporating more education about obesity pharmacotherapy. Furthermore, there was significant interest in learning how to secure hospital support for developing multidisciplinary teams to manage obesity. Most programs and practices reported a lack of funding for their weight management programs, and there was a notable absence of philanthropic support. Philanthropy is frequently overlooked as a funding source for innovative programs. Additionally, many philanthropic funders tend to focus locally, suggesting that local relationships may exert an equal or greater influence than research excellence. 18 Therefore, there's an opportunity for providers and program developers to explore this often‐untapped funding source.

This report has limitations that should be taken into consideration. The Lin et al. 4 web‐based survey targeted 1784 active NASPGHAN members; however, it achieved a low response rate of 15.2% (n = 272). In contrast, the current survey employed a different approach, focusing on obtaining one anonymous voice most knowledgeable about or best‐representing obesity care at every self‐identified pediatric gastroenterology program and practice listed in the NASPGHAN directory. This approach also had limitations due to the possibility that the NASPGHAN program list was neither accurate nor comprehensive. It also maintained anonymity, addressing confidentiality concerns, which have been cited among the reasons for low physician responsiveness to surveys. 19 We achieved a response rate of 52%, which fell within the 40%‐75% threshold considered acceptable in physician surveys. 20 However, the responses from clinicians working in larger academic institutions with greater nutritional focus may have predominated over those from private practice. 21 Despite these limitations, the survey's findings represented programs and practices from Canada, Mexico, and all regions of the United States; therefore, it is unique and provides valuable insights into the perception of pediatric obesity management among pediatric gastroenterologists.

CONCLUSIONS

Pediatric gastroenterologists are interested in multidisciplinary obesity management programs; however, they perceive their role as primarily supportive to manage obesity‐related gastroenterology comorbidities. There is a critical need for increased access to dietitians dedicated to managing obesity, as they play a crucial role in the multidisciplinary team. This need, along with education about obesity pharmacotherapy, guidance in developing multidisciplinary management teams, and exploring philanthropic support, can benefit from more advocacy and educational resources at the local and societal levels.
