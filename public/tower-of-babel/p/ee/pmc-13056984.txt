Anti-PD-1 antibody penpulimab plus chemotherapy for recurrent or metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a randomized, double-blind phase 3 study
Authors: Shuang Huang, Feng Liu, Song Qu, Lisha Chen, Ping Zhou, Shenhong Qu, Xiaohong Ai, Yong Chen, Meilian Liu, Rensheng Wang, Kelvin KW Chan, Peng Zhang, Chunhong Hu, Jiyu Wen, Jian Zhang, Qin Lin, Xiaojiang Li, Kangsheng Gu, Li Xiang, Dongxia Wang, Jingao Li, Daren Lin, Desheng Hu, Jianwu Ding, Siyang Wang, Xiaoming Huang, Lin Wang, Feng Jin, David G Pfister, Milena Perez Mak, Pedro Rafael Martins de Marchi, Yi Jiang, Haihua Yang, Xiaoye Hu, Tianrun Liu, Dehua Wu, Aditya Shreenivas, Thiago Bueno de Oliveira, Carlos Eduardo Baston Silva, Gustavo Vasconcelos Alves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this phase 3 trial, penpulimab combined with chemotherapy was assessed against a regimen of placebo plus chemotherapy for the first-line treatment of recurrent or metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma (R/M NPC). 291 patients were randomised and allocated in a 1:1 ratio in order to receive penpulimab (n = 144; 200 mg) or placebo (n = 147; 200 mg), plus chemotherapy (cisplatin/carboplatin and gemcitabine) every 3 weeks. Patients followed by maintenance therapy with penpulimab or placebo after 6 cycles. The primary endpoint of this study was progression-free survival (PFS) according to RECIST v1.1, and a significantly longer median PFS in the penpulimab arm versus the placebo arm (9.63 versus 7.00 months; hazard ratio 0.45, 95% CI: 0.33–0.62, P < 0.0001) was demonstrated in this prespecified interim analysis. The key secondary endpoint was the overall survival (OS). However, the OS data were still immature, and the median OS was not achieved (hazard ratio, 0.94; 95% CI: 0.63–1.40). The occurrence of treatment-related adverse events (grade ≥ 3) was 89.0% and 85.9% in two arms, with the most common being reduced the quantity of neutrophil (56.2% vs. 62.0%), reduced the quantity of white blood cell (54.1% vs. 54.9%), and anemia (45.2% vs. 38.7%). In the penpulimab arm, 6 patients (4.1%) experienced immune-related adverse events (grade ≥ 3). Adding penpulimab to chemotherapy led to a notable enhancement in PFS for the first-line R/M NPC treatment, alongside a safety profile that was both manageable and tolerable. Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), an epithelial tumor originating in the head and neck region, is notable for unique patterns of regional occurrence.1 In endemic areas, the incidence of NPC can be as high as 30 per 100,000 individuals.2 In contrast, areas with a low incidence report fewer than 1 case per 100,000 population.3 Keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma mainly occurs in low-incidence areas like North America and Europe, while nonkeratinizing squamous cell carcinoma prevails in high-incidence areas such as China and North Africa.4,5 Due to its hidden anatomical position and vague symptoms, most NPC patients (over 70%) are diagnosed at locoregionally advanced or metastatic stage.1 After intensive treatment with chemoradiotherapy, 20–30% of advanced NPC patients ultimately develop treatment failure mainly owing to recurrence or metastasis.1 The high mortality in advanced and recurrent NPC highlights the pressing demand for novel therapeutic strategies.6

Immunotherapy has triggered a transformative shift in cancer clinical treatment. Three key phase 3 randomized trials have demonstrated that combining gemcitabine-cisplatin with anti-PD-1 antibodies induces comparable, significant PFS prolongation for R/M-NPC (JUPITER-02, CAPTAIN-1st and RATIONALE-309).7–11 The binding of the IgG4 fragment crystallizable (Fc) fragment to Fcγ receptors induces antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), which further triggers the release of inflammatory cytokines by inflammatory cells.12,13 This may potentially lead to clinical adverse events. In contrast, penpulimab is the sole anti-PD-1 antibody of the IgG1 subtype that features a modified Fc region.14,15 Compared with other anti-PD-1 antibodies, penpulimab exhibits a reduced rate of antigen-binding dissociation, enhanced structural stability and superior receptor occupancy, properties that may contribute to improved efficacy and safety profiles. It has undergone Fc mutation and shows no apparent binding to Fc receptors. This modification prevents ADCC and complement-dependant cytotoxicity, thereby theoretically reducing the incidence of immune-related adverse events.14,16,17

In China, penpulimab has been approved for the treatment of certain types of tumors.15,18 Additionally, penpulimab has obtain approval in China for the treatment of advanced NPC (as a first-line and subsequent-line option).19 Meta-analysis of six clinical trials involving 465 patients reported a 3.2% occurrence of immune-related adverse events (grade ≥ 3).14 Additionally, in patients with metastatic NPC who have undergone extensive prior treatment, penpulimab showed encouraging antitumor activity with acceptable toxicity profiles.19 Given the promising data on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and penpulimab’s manageable toxicity for R/M NPC, we conducted this study to compare the efficacy and safety of penpulimab versus placebo combined with gemcitabine and cisplatin/carboplatin for patients with RM-NPC as a first-line option.

Results

Patients

This multicentre study (36 sites in China; 10 sites outside Asia) screened 410 patients from 16 August 2021 to 29 April 2024 (data cutoff), and randomized 291 patients to take either penpulimab or placebo combined with chemotherapy, 144 patients in the penpulimab arm and 147 patients in the placebo arm. The penpulimab arm had 20 patients still on treatment at the data cutoff, while the placebo arm had 9 patients (Fig. 1). The baseline demographic characteristics and disease features in the two treatment arms were comparable, involving PD-L1 expression status, distant metastatic site and Epstein Barr virus (EBV) DNA level. 280 patients were from Asian countries, and 11 patients from countries outside of Asia were enrolled. A total of 76.6% of the patients had nonkeratinizing undifferentiated histology. All patients had previously received anticancer therapies; 41.6% of patients had bone metastasis, and 39.9% of patients had liver metastasis. The proportion of patients with PD-L1 positivity was 38.2% and 36.7% in the penpulimab and placebo arms, respectively; EBV DNA < 500 copies/ml was identified in 31.3% of patients in the placebo arm and 37.5% of patients in the penpulimab arm (Table 1).

The median follow-up duration was 19.1 months, during which time patients received a median of 13 penpulimab cycles and 12 placebo cycles. Specifically, 68 patients (46.3%) in the placebo arm switched to penpulimab monotherapy following PD, and 42 patients (28.6%) received other anti-PD-1 therapies. Furthermore, more patients in the placebo arm (60 [40.8%]) received two or more types of systemic antitumor treatment sequences or combinations (targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and other immunotherapy) than those in the penpulimab arm (39 [27.1%]). The subsequent anticancer therapies for all patients are listed in supplementary Table 2.

Efficacy

189 patients had suffered disease deterioration or death at the time of data cutoff. According to the evaluation by blinded independent central review (BICR) per RECIST v1.1, in the penpulimab arm, the median PFS was 9.63 months (95% CI: 7.13–12.45) and in the placebo arm was 7.00 months (95% CI: 6.90–7.29) among the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. Penpulimab demonstrated a statistically significant prolonged PFS compared to placebo (HR 0.45 [95% CI: 0.33–0.62], two-sided P < 0.0001). Additionally, in the penpulimab arm, the estimated 12-month PFS rate was 44.2% (95% CI: 34.9–53.0) and in the placebo arm was 15.4% (95% CI: 9.4–22.7). The Kaplan‒Meier-estimated PFS is presented in Fig. 2a. A superior PFS benefit with the penpulimab arm versus the placebo arm was observed across prespecified subgroups. The subgroup analysis results were presented in more detail in Fig. 2b.

Among these ITT population in the penpulimab arm, the median investigator-assessed PFS was 12.29 months (95% CI: 9.69–14.55) and in the placebo arm it was 7.20 months (95% CI: 6.97–8.28). A notable advantage was seen in terms of investigator-assessed PFS (HR 0.44 (95% CI: 0.32–0.60), two-sided P < 0.0001). At the data cutoff, 48 deaths (33.3%) in the penpulimab arm and 49 (33.3%) in the placebo arm were reported. In either penpulimab or placebo arm, the median OS was not achieved and a stratified OS HR was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.63–1.40). In the penpulimab arm, the result of estimated 24-month OS rates was 66.3% (95% CI: 57.1–74.0) and in the placebo arm it was 61.9% (95% CI: 52.2–70.2) (Fig. 3).

Safety

By the cutoff date, the median exposure cycles with penpulimab and placebo were 13.0 and 12.0 cycles, respectively. 288 patients made up the safety population (146 in the penpulimab arm and 142 in the placebo arm), defined as those who received ≥1 dose of the study drug. 98.6% (140/142) of patients in the placebo arm and all patients (146/146) in the penpulimab arm had at least one treatment-related adverse event (TRAE). The overall incidence of TRAEs (grade ≥3) was 89.0% and 85.9% in two arms, with the most common being decreased the quantity of neutrophil (56.2% vs. 62.0%), decreased the quantity of white blood cell (54.1% vs. 54.9%), anemia (45.2% vs. 38.7%), and decreased the quantity of platelet (30.1% vs. 35.9%). The TRAEs with a rate of at least 10% has been recorded in Table 3. The proportion of patients experiencing serious adverse events related to treatment (TRSAEs) was similar in both arms (43.8% vs. 40.1%), with decreased the quantity of platelet being the most common (18.5% vs. 21.1%), decreased the quantity of neutrophil (13.7% vs. 8.5%), decreased the quantity of white blood cell (12.3% vs. 11.3%) and anemia (8.2% vs. 9.9%). Immune-related adverse events (IrAEs, 30.8% vs. 8.5%) were more frequent in the penpulimab arm, most of these irAEs were grade 1–2, and the most common irAEs included hypothyroidism (17.8% vs. 4.2%), rash (7.5% vs. 2.8%), and hyperthyroidism (2.1% vs. 0). Grade ≥3 irAEs occurred among 6 patients (4.1%) in the penpulimab arm, including 2 patients reporting immune-related diabetes, and both of them were recovering following interruption or discontinuation of the treatment and diabetes medication. The details of all irAEs are shown in supplementary Table 4. Due to TRAEs, stopping treatment happened in 16 patients (11.0%) receiving penpulimab plus chemotherapy and 12 patients (8.5%) receiving placebo in combination with chemotherapy. Moreover, due to TRAEs, the interruption of the study treatment dose was conducted on 91 of 146 patients (62.3%) treated with penpulimab and 80 of 142 patients (56.3%) treated with placebo. No TRAEs leading to death occurred in the placebo arm and two patients with penpulimab experiencing TRAEs leading to death (one cardiac failure and one disease progression).

Health-related quality of life

142 patients in the penpulimab arm and 144 patients in the placebo arm with available baseline results were included in the health-related quality of life analysis. Besides, the quality of life (QoL) assessments with EORTC QLQ-C30 global health status score at baseline and week 30 (cycle 11) visit, as well as the score changes from baseline to week 30 were presented in supplementary Table 5. The two arms had similar global health status/QoL scores at baseline, and the scores in both arms showed a downward trend after the start of treatment, followed by a slight increase with disease progression. No notable difference was observed in the trend of score changes between the both arms. The median time to first deterioration in EORTC H&N43 was 1.87 months (95% CI: 1.61–2.92) in the penpulimab arm and 1.84 months (95% CI: 1.54–2.63) in the placebo arm (HR 0.86 [95% CI: 0.64–1.15]), and no significant difference was observed. The Kaplan‒Meier curves for time to disease deterioration were illustrated in supplementary Fig 3.

Discussion

This study confirms that for patients with R/M NPC, combination therapy with penpulimab and standard chemotherapy greatly enhances PFS versus chemotherapy alone, which met its primary endpoint. The observed clinical improvement in PFS remained consistent among prespecified subgroups. And the safety profile was manageable.

This study was originally designed to enroll 218 patients from Asian countries and 60 patients from counties outside of Asia. However, enrollment from non-Asian countries proved extremely challenging, primarily due to the low incidence of nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated enrollment difficulties during the study period. Ultimately, 11 patients from counties outside of Asia and 280 Asian patients participated in the study. Our findings align with results from previous studies (JUPITER-02, CAPTAIN-1st and RATIONALE-309), confirming anti-PD-1-based chemoimmunotherapy could benefit patients with advanced NPC.

Three key phase 3 trials demonstrated toripalimab-, camrelizumab-, or tislelizumab-chemotherapy combinations significantly extended PFS (JUPITER-02, RATIONALE-309 and CAPTAIN-1st), with corresponding HRs of 0.52 (95% CI: 0.36–0.74), 0.52 (95% CI: 0.36–0.74) and 0.54 (95% CI: 0.39–0.76), respectively. Our study yielded a comparable HR of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.33–0.62). It distinctly separated at 6 months post-treatment in the PFS curves, indicating that the benefit of PD-1 inhibitor-associated benefits emerged after this time point, consistent with results from the above phase 3 studies.

Penpulimab’s PFS benefit was consistent across all prespecified subgroups. Liver metastasis is a recognized independent predictor of OS in R/M NPC, linked to reduced OS.20,21 According to subgroup analysis results, all patients could benefit from penpulimab, with similar HRs of 0.50 and 0.54. Baseline EBV DNA is also a significant prognostic factor for R/M NPC,22–24 and the subgroup analysis indicated that both patients with EBV DNA levels ≥500 copies and <500 copies could benefit. In addition, the PFS benefit with penpulimab was consistent among patients grouped by PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) of 50% or higher versus less than 50%.

The OS data were still immature. The median OS was not achieved, with an HR of 0.94. The OS estimates may be obscured by subsequent treatment, including crossover. In our study, 46.3% of patients switched to receive penpulimab after PD in the placebo arm, and more patients received other anti-PD-1 therapies in the placebo arm than those in the penpulimab arm (40.8% vs. 27.1%). Additionally, crossover was not permitted in the JUPITER-02 and CAPTAIN-1st studies. And 49.2% of patients switched to undergo tislelizumab monotherapy in the placebo arm and reported an HR for OS of 0.60 in the RATIONALE-309 study. It seemed that in the RATIONALE-309 study, no substantial impact from the crossover was observed, probably because the interim analysis was conducted at a very early time point. Given that in other studies, the median OS with chemotherapy was more than 22 months. Therefore, this study requires a longer follow-up period.

The similar safety profiles presented in the two arms, with comparable rates of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs (89% vs. 86%), indicating that adding the penpulimab did not increase the adverse events when chemotherapy was used. The occurrenceof TRAEs (grade ≥3) with penpulimab was also comparable to that in other studies: 93% in CAPTAIN-1st, 90% in JUPITER-02, and 76% in RATIONALE-309. As expected, irAEs occurred more frequently in the penpulimab arm (30.8% vs. 8.5%). Most of them were low-grade (grade 1–2) and manageable, with no deaths attributed to irAEs. The overall irAE profile was similar to what has been reported in previous studies involving penpulimab. The incidence of grade ≥3 irAEs (4.1%) was much lower than that in the CAPTAIN-1st (15%) and JUPITER-02 (7.5%) studies and slightly higher than that in the RATIONALE-309 study (2.3%). The favorable safety profile may contribute to the molecular design of penpulimab.

This research also had limitations. First, patients from countries outside of Asia was relatively small, and it remains to be determined whether penpulimab plus chemotherapy will be effective in these patients. Second, the OS data were immature and need to be further followed.

In conclusion, penpulimab combined with chemotherapy could significantly improve PFS versus chemotherapy alone. Therefore, the combination of penpulimab with chemotherapy is a viable new treatment option for R/M NPC.

Methods

Study design and participants

This clinical trial (NCT04974398) was carried out following Good Clinical Practice standards and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and all its modifications received approval from the independent ethics committees of the all sites involved in this study. The ethical registration number and the name of the approving committee or Institutional Review Board (IRB) are presented in supplementary Table 1. Additionally, all patients gave written informed consent prior to participating in the study.

In this research, patients with R/M NPC were enrolled from 36 sites in China and 10 sites outside of Asia. The details of all sites are shown in supplementary Table S1. Patients were eligible for enrollment as follows: aged 18–75 years; had histologically or cytologically confirmed NPC; had de novo metastatic NPC unsuitable for local management and so on. Detailed inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria were outlined in the study protocol.

Randomization and masking

Eligible patients were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to take either penpulimab or placebo combined with chemotherapy. Permuted block randomization was carried out through an interactive web response system. Randomization was stratified based on disease stages (primary metastases versus recurrent), liver metastasis (present versus absent) and ECOG performance status (0 versus 1). The investigators, subjects, and other participants were blinded to treatment allocation unless unblinding was performed. To preserve the masking, the investigational drug and the matching placebo had identical packaging, labeling, appearance, and dosing regimen.

Procedures

Patients took intravenous penpulimab (200 mg on day 1) or matching placebo plus cisplatin (80 mg/m2 on day 1, Q3W) or carboplatin (AUC5 on day 1, Q3W) and gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2 on day 1 and day 8, Q3W) for a maximum of 6 cycles, followed by maintenance therapy with penpulimab 200 mg or placebo intravenously Q3W until no more clinical benefit was assessed by the investigator, intolerable toxic effects, completion of treatment, or meeting other criteria for discontinuation, whichever occurred first. In each arm, carboplatin was selected as a substitute for cisplatin based on the investigator’s discretion before randomization. Dose modification of penpulimab or placebo was not allowed during the treatment period. Dose modification and interruption of chemotherapy drugs for a maximum of 6 weeks were allowed. Subjects received penpulimab treatment for up to 24 months in this study. Subjects in the placebo group would have the option to transition to open-label penpulimab monotherapy after radiographic disease progression (confirmed by BICR) assessed by the investigators.

Tumor assessments based on imaging, including computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the nasopharynx, head, neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis, were conducted every 6 weeks (±7 days) within 54 weeks after the first dose and every 9 weeks (±7 days) thereafter until radiographic progression or discontinuation of study treatment (whichever occurred later), initiation of new antitumor therapy, loss to follow-up, death, withdrawal of consent, or study discontinuation, whichever occurred first. If the symptoms of the subject worsened, an unscheduled imaging assessment was needed. If bone metastases were found at the screening and could not be seen on CT or MRI scans, or if clinically indicated, bone scans should be reexamined when a complete response was found or when progression in bone was suspected. If brain metastases were found at screening, the assessment was repeated at each subsequent tumor assessment. If no brain metastases were found at screening, an additional head CT scan or MRI might be performed during subsequent treatment if brain metastases were suspected.

Hematology assessments were completed no more than 3 days before dosing in each cycle (including day 1 and day 8, within a span of 2 days on day 8) and at the end of EOT visit. Blood biochemistry and urinalysis were conducted no more than 3 days before dosing in each cycle (day 1 only) and at the EOT visit. Thyroid function tests were conducted ≤3 days before dosing in each cycle and at the EOT visit except for cycle 1 day 1. The coagulation test was performed ≤3 days before dosing every 3 cycles and at the EOT visit and might be optionally performed by the investigator as clinically indicated. Adverse events (AEs) surveillance was performed continuously during treatment and for up to 30 days (90 days for SAE) after the last dose of study treatment (including chemotherapy) or the initiation of other antitumor therapy, whichever occurred first, immune-related AEs (irAEs) were collected until 90 days after the last dose of penpulimab (regardless of initiation of other antitumor therapies) and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI CTCAE), version 5.0. Survival was assessed every 3 months (±7 days) after drug discontinuation. PK samples and immunogenicity samples were collected within 60 min before the initiation of penpulimab in cycles 1, 2, 4, 8, and 14 and every 8 cycles thereafter. The EBV DNA level was analyzed in the central laboratory at cycles 1, 3, and 10 and at the EOT visit.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of BICR-assessed PFS was measured from randomization until the earliest occurrence of disease progression per RECIST v1.1 or death from any cause. The key secondary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from the randomization date to death due to any cause. Other secondary endpoints included BICR-assessed objective response rate (ORR), defined as the proportion of subjects who achieved a best overall response (BOR) of CR or PR according to RECIST v1.1, duration of response (DoR), defined as the time from the first documented response (CR or PR) until disease progression based on RECIST v1.1 or death due to any cause, disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of subjects who achieved the BOR of CR or PR or SD based on RECIST v1.1; investigator-assessed PFS, ORR, DoR and DCR based on RECIST v1.1; safety assessment, pharmacokinetic profile, immunogenicity, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), expression of PD-L1 in tumor samples, the relationship between PD-L1 expression and treatment effectiveness, the level of blood EBV at baseline and their changes following administration of the study drugs, as well as the relationship between blood EBV levels and treatment efficacy.

Statistical analysis

BICR-assessed PFS was tested with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. The trial was designed to enroll 278 patients and target 222 PFS events to achieve 89% power for detecting a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.65. The interim analysis for the BICR-assessed PFS was conducted when 189 PFS events were observed.

Since PFS reached statistical significance in this interim analysis, as planned in the statistical analysis plan, a descriptive analysis of OS, the key secondary endpoint, was conducted by spending a nominal α of 0.0001. No formal statistical testing or multiplicity adjustment was applied to other secondary endpoints. All efficacy analyses followed the ITT principle.

Adopting a stratified log-rank test to compare BICR-assessed PFS between two arms at the interim analysis with a significance threshold of 0.030. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate the PFS, and HRs with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were obtained from a stratified Cox proportional hazards model. The same statistical approach was applied to OS, investigator-assessed PFS, and BICR-assessed duration of response. Randomization stratification factors-disease stage (primary metastases vs. recurrent), liver metastasis status (present vs. absent), ECOG performance status (0 vs. 1), and chosen chemotherapy regimen (carboplatin vs. cisplatin) were included in all stratified models.

Prespecified subgroup analyses by age (under 50 or over 50 years), sex (male or female), ECOG performance-status score (0 or 1), liver metastases (yes or no), stage of disease at study entry (primary metastases vs. recurrent), investigator’s choice of chemotherapy regimens (carboplatin or cisplatin), PD-L1 expression level (TPS ≥ 50%) or negative (TPS < 50% or NA), EBV-DNA level (<500 copies/mL or ≥500 copies/mL), and region (Asian countries or non-Asian countries) were performed for BICR-assessed PFS. Unstratified Cox models provided HR estimates for each subgroup.

Health-related quality of life was assessed using EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N43 instruments. Change from baseline to week 30 was evaluated with a mixed-effects model for repeated measures, adjusting for stratification factors, baseline score, treatment, time, and treatment-by-time interaction. Time to deterioration in symptom scales was analyzed using methods consistent with the primary PFS analysis. For patients without confirmed worsening from baseline, the observation cutoff date is the date of the last observation. The EBV-DNA analysis population included all patients with baseline EBV-DNA results and at least one postbaseline result. Three binary variables (EBV DNA copy number decreased from baseline, EBV DNA copy number became undetectable after treatment, and EBV DNA rebounded after the initial reduction) were analyzed by the same method as for ORR. The analysis method for time from the lowest EBV copy number to rebound was the same as that for the primary endpoint PFS. Patients without rebound were reviewed as of the last sampling date.

The complete statistical analysis plan can be found in the supplementary material. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).
