Relation of Visual Function, Retinal Thickness by Optical Coherence Tomography, and MRI Brain Volume in Pediatric-Onset Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Anna Sosa, Kimberly A. O'Neill, Ruben Jauregui, Ugo Nwigwe, Thibo Billiet, Rachel Kenney, Lauren B. Krupp, Steven L. Galetta, Laura J. Balcer, Scott N. Grossman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesWhile reductions in optical coherence tomography (OCT) pRNFL and ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thicknesses have been shown to be associated with brain atrophy in adult-onset MS (AOMS) cohorts, the relationship between OCT and brain MRI measures is less established in pediatric-onset MS (POMS). Our aim was to examine the associations of OCT measures with volumetric MRI in a cohort of patients with POMS to determine whether OCT measures reflect CNS neurodegeneration in this patient population, as is seen in AOMS cohorts.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study with retrospective ascertainment of patients with POMS evaluated at a single center with expertise in POMS and neuro-ophthalmology. As part of routine clinical care, patients with POMS are evaluated by a POMS expert and undergo volumetric brain MRI, including whole-brain (WB), subregional, and gray matter (GM) volume analyses. Patients with POMS are routinely referred to neuro-ophthalmology for evaluation that includes high-contrast visual acuity, color vision testing, and OCT. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models, accounting for within-patient, intereye correlations (both eyes of each patient were included), MS disease duration, and disease-modifying therapy efficacy, were used to determine the relationship between visual pathway structure and function and volumetric MRI measures.ResultsAmong 61 patients (122 eyes) with POMS, the mean age at the time of the volumetric MRI scan was 19.2 (SD = 3.7, range 10–27) years, with a median disease duration of 2.8 (range 0–14) years. Lower (worse) pRNFL thicknesses (mean 87.4 [17.2] µm) were associated with reduced volume percentiles of WB (p < 0.001, GEE models), total GM (p = 0.025), and thalamus (p = 0.038). pRNFL thinning was also associated with greater lesion (p = 0.006) and black hole (p = 0.028) volumes. Reduced color vision and decreased high-contrast visual acuity were associated with lower hippocampal volumes (p = 0.012 and p = 0.015, respectively).DiscussionOur results demonstrate that changes in visual pathway structures are associated with reductions in overall brain volume and GM volumes, as well as greater lesion and black hole burden. Collectively, our results emphasize the importance of visual assessment in POMS and suggest that OCT reflects overall CNS neurodegeneration in this cohort.

Introduction

Pediatric-onset multiple sclerosis (POMS), characterized by the onset of multiple sclerosis (MS) symptoms before the age of 18 years,1 has been linked to impaired age-expected brain growth during childhood and adolescence.2-4 Relative to adult-onset MS (AOMS), individuals with POMS show greater radiologic disease activity and increased rates of lesion formation,5,6 and it is likely that this increased inflammatory activity underlies the loss of brain tissue. On the contrary, individuals with POMS recover more fully from clinical relapses7 and often show fewer findings on standard neurologic examinations, despite underlying disease brain atrophy.8,9 As such, there is a clear need for more sensitive indicators of neurologic status and prognosis. While emerging regulatory-approved neuroimaging technology provides a way to quantify whole and regional brain volumes from standard clinical MRI acquisitions,10,11 frequent volumetric MRI scans can be challenging to obtain and are not without risks because some pediatric patients may require sedation or removal of dental hardware.12,13 Therefore, identifying additional markers of the clinical course beyond brain volume measurement addresses an unmet need for those with POMS.

Ophthalmologic measures offer promise as proxies of MS disease activity and may reveal signs of MS treatment response or silent progression.14 Subclinical demyelination leading to optic neuropathy may also contribute to visual impairment and serve as a marker for MS-related neurodegeneration.15 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a reliable, replicable, and sensitive method for measuring structural changes in the anterior visual pathway and offers a noninvasive, patient-friendly measure to track neurodegeneration occurring early in the disease process.16 For several decades, studies in patients with AOMS have shown an association between pRNFL thickness and brain atrophy,17-19 as well as a relationship between pRNFL thinning and increased disease-related disability.20 Studies incorporating OCT measures among individuals with POMS are limited,21 although these are particularly important given the increase in radiologic disease activity and decrease in sensitivity of standard neurologic examination in this population.6 A potential link between structural measures of the optic nerve using OCT and brain volume as assessed by MRI could detect early signs of neurodegenerative processes and subclinical progression in patients with POMS.22

In this single-center cohort of patients with POMS, we aimed to examine the cross-sectional relationships between visual function outcomes, structural ophthalmic measures, and whole and regional brain volumes to determine whether ophthalmic measures may reflect CNS neurodegeneration in POMS, as is seen in AOMS. We hypothesized that thinner pRNFL and ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GCIPL)thicknesses, and visual function as a result, would be associated with reduced whole-brain (WB) and gray matter (GM) volumes, and therefore, OCT measures would be able to detect neurodegenerative processes at the early stages of this disease.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Eligibility

This was a cross-sectional study with retrospective ascertainment of data by chart review for patients evaluated at the New York University (NYU) Langone MS Comprehensive Care Center who had undergone at least 1 brain MRI scan using icobrain-ms software (an FDA-approved volumetric scan protocol) between July 2018 and July 2023. MRI scans were ordered for all patients with POMS as part of routine clinical care and were acquired at an NYU neuroradiology facility. Eligibility criteria also included a diagnosis of pediatric-onset relapsing-remitting MS, as defined by symptom onset before age 18 years and the revised 2017 McDonald criteria1; seronegative status for myelin oligodendrocyte antibody; and at least 1 clinical evaluation by a neuro-ophthalmologist with concurrent paraclinical testing completed within 3 years of the volumetric MRI.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was approved by the NYU Langone Health Institutional Review Board. Ascertainment of data by retrospective chart review was approved with a waiver of informed consent.

Data Collection

Clinical, demographic, and treatment data were captured by electronic medical record (Epic) chart review. The collected data included date of birth, date of MS symptom onset, date of initiation of disease-modifying therapy (DMT), and level of DMT efficacy. DMT efficacy was categorized as high, moderate, or none (treatment-naïve), with high efficacy defined as ocrelizumab, rituximab, ofatumumab, or natalizumab and moderate efficacy defined as all other available DMTs (e.g., fingolimod, dimethyl fumarate, and ozanimod).23,24 Clinicians performed the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) assessment as part of routine clinical care, and we captured the score closest to the date of the volumetric MRI for each patient.

Visual performance measures performed by a neuro-ophthalmologist and technicians included high-contrast visual acuity (HCVA) obtained using a Snellen chart at distance and the proportion of correctly scored Ishihara color plates. For patients with multiple neuro-ophthalmic evaluations, we recorded the evaluation closest to the date of the volumetric MRI. HCVA was converted to a logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) score.25 For this conversion, a visual acuity of 20/20 was equivalent to a logMAR score of 0, visual acuity of 20/25 was equivalent to a logMAR score of 0.1, and so on, with each new line correctly read adding 0.1 to the logMAR score. For patients who missed a single letter in a row, a value of 0.02 was added to the logMAR score.25

For the subset of patients who had clinical OCT performed, and readily available, as part of their neuro-ophthalmic evaluation, pRNFL thickness, GCIPL thickness, macular volumes, and central foveal subfield thickness closest to the date of the volumetric scan were recorded. Most of the 26 patients excluded from the OCT analyses had undergone OCT in the past, but the data were not available because the tests were performed at an outside institution. One patient had macular volumes that were extremely low and was, therefore, excluded as an outlier. Clinicians used either Spectralis SD-OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) or Cirrus HD-OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA) to perform the clinical OCT. Patients with OCT performed on a different OCT machine (i.e., TopCon) were excluded. For patients with OCT performed on a Cirrus machine, a conversion equation was used to convert all RNFL thicknesses from Cirrus machines to their Spectralis equivalent.26Spectralis pRNFL value=5.0+(1.0×Cirrus pRNFL value)

For macular volume and central foveal subfield thickness, only those obtained from a Spectralis machine were included, because of the lack of an available conversion equation.

MRI Acquisition and Segmentation and Measures

MR images were acquired using a 20-channel head coil on a 3T Siemens Biograph mMR scanner. Brain tissue volumes (WB, white matter [WM], cortical gray matter [CGM]) and lesion volumes (WM fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) T2 hyperintensities and T1 hypointensities, “black holes”) were quantified using icobrain-ms software.27-29 T1 hypointensities are defined as regions within FLAIR hyperintensities whose T1 intensity falls significantly below normal WM intensities, specifically below the median minus 3 times the median absolute deviation, and with a minimum volume of 5 voxels (0.005 mL). The T1 sequences used have minimal acquisition restrictions aside from requiring a 3D resolution (e.g., 1×× 1 mm). Previous versions of icobrain have been validated on pediatric data.30 Further information is provided in the Supplement.

MRI brain measures included percentiles (based on age-related and sex-related norms) and tissue volumes for the WB, WM, CGM, GM, hippocampus, and thalamus, as well as total lesion and black hole volumes. For patients with multiple volumetric MRI scans performed, we used only the first volumetric MRI scan for analysis. Whole-brain and regional volume percentiles were used, as opposed to normalized volume, to account for age and gender differences.

Statistical Analyses

Data analyses were performed using Stata statistical software (version 17.0, StataCorp, College Station, TX). The relationship between vision measures (pRNFL, ganglion cell layer, macular volume, central foveal subfield thickness, HCVA, Ishihara color plates) and brain volume (whole and regional brain volume percentile) was evaluated using generalized estimating equation (GEE) regression models, accounting for within-patient, intereye correlations, as well as disease duration and DMT efficacy at time of ophthalmic evaluation.26 A type I error level of α = 0.05 was used for statistical significance. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied because the study was hypothesis-driven.31

Post hoc sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the impact of clinical optic neuritis (ON) and to determine whether the retina-brain associations seen were independent of previous ON. Eyes were split into 2 groups: those with a history of ON and those without. A few patients had potential evidence of a previous ON without a documented event, and these patients were excluded from these analyses. Sensitivity analyses were not run for GCIPL measurements because of the small number of patients with available data. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted on the subgroup of patients who were still younger than 18 years at the time of volumetric MRI. Post hoc analyses were run on all measures to determine the impact of accounting for FLAIR lesion volume. Results from the post hoc analyses can be seen in eAppendix 1.

Data Availability

Data not provided in the article because of space limitations may be shared (anonymized) at the request of any qualified investigator for purposes of replicating procedures and results.

Results

Clinical and Demographic Characteristics

As shown in Figure 1, among 138 patients with POMS who underwent at least 1 volumetric MRI scan using icobrain-ms software, 61 (44%) had previously been evaluated by a neuro-ophthalmologist within 3 years of the volumetric MRI and had at least visual acuity assessed during that visit. There were few differences between patients with POMS who did and who did not have neuro-ophthalmology evaluation, except that patients with POMS with neuro-ophthalmology evaluation were more likely to have a history of acute ON (62.3% vs 37.7%, p = 0.0002). Disease duration was also significantly different and was, therefore, accounted for in our models as a covariate. Additional comparisons are described in the Supplement (eTables 1–6). Of the 61 patients, 41 (67%) underwent Ishihara color plate testing and 35 (57%) had at least 1 OCT scan. Of the 35 patients with OCT scans, 34 (97%) had pRNFL measurements, 16 (46%) had macular volume results available, and 5 (14%) had GCIPL measurements available (Figure 1). Owing to the small number of patients with GCIPL measurements, this measure was excluded from further analyses.

Patient Flowchart

The demographic characteristics of the 61 patients (122 eyes) are given in Table 1. The mean age at MS symptom onset was 15.2 (SD = 2.4) years, and the mean age at the time of the volumetric MRI scan was 19.2 (SD = 3.7) years, with a median disease duration of 2.8 (range 0–14) years at the time of volumetric MRI scan. Patients were 75% female and diverse, with 44% self-identifying as White, 34% self-identifying as Black, and 31% self-identifying as Hispanic or Latino. Most (64%) were on high-efficacy DMT.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to evaluate the relationship between neuro-ophthalmologic assessments and clinically available brain volume measures in individuals with POMS. We found significant associations between visual indicators such as pRNFL thickness and regional and WB measures, suggesting that the visual examination may be a reliable proxy of neurodegeneration among individuals with POMS, similar to what has previously been seen in AOMS.16,18,19

Our study demonstrated significant associations between pRNFL thinning and reduced volume percentiles of WB, WM, thalamus, GM, and CGM. Greater degrees of RNFL thinning were also associated with greater burden of WM lesions and black hole volumes. Among individuals with AOMS, pRNFL thinning has previously been reported to significantly correlate with overall brain atrophy,18 decreased GM and thalamic volumes, and increased T2 lesion volumes (WM lesions),17,32,33 which underscores the sensitivity of OCT measures to underlying neurodegenerative processes in AOMS and mimics the results seen in this study. We also found that reductions in macular volume were associated with reduced volume percentiles of the WB (Figure 3) and, specifically, the hippocampus. Previous studies in AOMS showed an association between GCIPL atrophy and both WB atrophy and thalamic atrophy that was not observed in this study; however, in contrast to our cross-sectional design, these AOMS studies were longitudinal, which may have contributed to the differences in results.19 Furthermore, our low sample size limited our power to identify associations of lower magnitude. Future studies on macular volume, GCIPL thickness, and brain volume in POMS are essential to determine their changes over time. In AOMS, changes in OCT measures have been linked to long-term disability progression, further supporting the use of OCT measures to track subclinical progression in POMS, which may not otherwise be readily measured.34 Retinal and ganglion cell layer changes as observed through OCT scans likely reflect the widespread neuronal injury,33,35 as well as the overall inflammatory burden associated with MS, changes that also lead to retinal nerve fiber, regional, and WB atrophy.36

Macular Volume Correlates With Whole-Brain Volume

Macular volume positively correlated with whole-brain volume percentile (ρ = 0.01, p = 0.004).

As was expected, our sensitivity analyses showed that most of the associations were dependent on the inclusion of eyes with a history of ON, although pRNFL thickness remained significantly associated with WB and CGM volume percentiles for both non-ON and ON eyes. Furthermore, macular volume was seen to be significantly associated with WM lesion volume in eyes without a history of ON, but the low number of patients in this subgroup limits the reliability of conclusions. On the contrary, when taking into account the potential relationship between the ON eye and non-ON eye (fellow eye) in a patient with a history of unilateral ON, most of the relationships remained (eTable 1). Therefore, retrograde degeneration in a fellow eye cannot explain the observed associations.

In our sensitivity analysis of patients younger than 18 years at the time of volumetric MRI, almost all associations remained and additional associations were seen between HCVA, color vision, and WM lesion volume. On the contrary, participants older than 18 years showed notable associations between central foveal subfield thickness and WB and GM volume percentiles, which was not seen in any of the other groups.

Our findings are consistent with the ability of young individuals with MS to compensate functionally, despite structural defects.7 Neither high-contract visual acuity scores nor color vision performance was associated with regional brain volume loss, with the exception of hippocampal volume, where both visual function tests were linked to lower hippocampal percentiles in our cohort. The dissociation between visual performance and structural findings by neuroimaging and OCT assessed cross-sectionally may reflect the sensitivity of certain visual function tests that we use.37,38 In patients with AOMS, low-contrast letter acuity compared with high-contrast visual acuity is more strongly associated with visual disability.39,40 It is, therefore, crucial to incorporate low-contrast acuity testing into future neuro-ophthalmic examinations to ensure a comprehensive assessment of visual function. Further investigation in our POMS cohort using both low-contrast sensitivity (which captures aspects of quality of life and overall function missed by other MS outcome measures41,42) and color vision tests that are designed specifically to capture optic neuropathy, such as the D15-Desaturated or Farnsworth-Munsell 100 Hue tests, would be helpful, although such detailed color vision tests are less frequently used in clinical practice. Low-contrast acuity testing, however, is easily administered and sensitive to brain changes in MS.

Tracking neurodegenerative changes among individuals with POMS can guide assessment of disease progression as these patients age. OCT is a noninvasive and rapid means of assessing structure in the anterior visual pathway, in a manner similar to the application of brain volume MRI to assess tissue loss in other brain regions. OCT is relatively inexpensive compared with brain volumetric studies, which, although useful during routine clinical care, may be costly and difficult to obtain in some situations, especially outside the United States.43

Both OCT and brain volume assessment can identify subclinical and clinical signs of neurodegeneration in MS.16,44 While brain volume and OCT measures are reduced among people with AOMS, with greater neurologic impairment as measured by EDSS scores,33,45 there is less information regarding these measures among the POMS population.17,46 An association between a decrease in mean pRNFL thickness and a history of ON has been previously noted in patients with POMS.47 This study demonstrates an association between pRNFL thickness and brain volume changes among individuals with POMS.36

The sensitivity of OCT to detect neuronal injury before the appearance of clinical symptoms and to predict disease activity and brain atrophy through a noninvasive, cost-effective approach makes this measure a helpful tool for monitoring patients with POMS.48 Furthermore, the anterior visual system, although involved in >50% of people with MS,49 may only be captured by high-resolution OCT.

Our study has limitations. The relatively small sample size and single-center nature of our data collection limit the generalizability of our results, although the rarity of POMS compared with AOMS may necessitate studies in smaller cohorts. Future multicenter collaborative research is needed. This study included only patients who saw both an MS specialist and an ophthalmologist or neuro-ophthalmologist and, therefore, may be specific to patients with POMS who had visual concerns prompting referral. The cross-sectional design also limits our conclusions. Unfortunately, there are no current normative values for OCT pRNFL thickness in pediatric patients younger than 18 years. While this study focused on patients with pediatric-onset relapsing-remitting MS, we specifically excluded those with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disorder and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, which are important and relatively common MS mimics in pediatric populations. It is, therefore, essential for future studies to focus specifically on these populations to enhance and complete our understanding.

Future work should investigate longitudinal neuro-ophthalmologic assessments, OCT measures, and brain volume changes among individuals with POMS to better understand these measures' utility as prognostic biomarkers. This study demonstrates that OCT findings correlate with global and regional brain atrophy in patients with POMS, supporting its value in clinical care and ability to offer insight into the MS disease process. In summary, neuro-ophthalmology and OCT examination can play a key role in the care and monitoring of patients with POMS.
