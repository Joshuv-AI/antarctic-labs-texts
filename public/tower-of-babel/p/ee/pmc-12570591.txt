Therapeutic drug monitoring of busulfan in pediatric patients: a 5-year observational study
Authors: Nedaa Alomari, Amani Kurdi, Meshari Alghamdi, Waleed Alhussaini, Ghanem Ezzeldeen, Eman Alharbi, Alaa Aldahri, Imadul Islam, Mohammed Essa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBusulfan is a chemotherapeutic agent used in conditioning regimens before hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in pediatric patients. Due to its pharmacokinetic variability, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is indispensable to ensure favorable outcomes and minimize risks.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted on 166 pediatric patients receiving busulfan at King Abdullah Specialist Children’s Hospital from July 2020 to May 2024. Busulfan concentration was determined using chromatographic separation with Waters Acquity UPLC® followed by detection on a Xevo TQ-MS tandem quadrupole LC-MS/MS. The analysis included demographic data, busulfan dosing, therapeutic levels, and clinical outcomes such as length of hospital stay and mortality. Factors influencing busulfan levels were also assessed.ResultsAmong the 166 patients analyzed, 41.57% were aged 6–11 years, and 59.64% were male. Sickle cell anemia was the predominant indication for busulfan use (72.28%). The therapeutic level was achieved in 42.77% of patients, while 42.17% were below the range. Gender significantly influenced AUC values (males: 898.01 µmol·min/L; females: 1000.10 µmol·min/L, p = 0.0091). Mortality was observed in 4.82% of patients.ConclusionThe study highlights the variability in busulfan pharmacokinetics and its clinical implications in pediatric patients. Gender differences and subtherapeutic exposure were associated, emphasizing the need for individualized dosing and rigorous monitoring to optimize therapy. Introduction

Busulfan is an alkylating antineoplastic agent that has been widely used in conditioning regimens for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) since the 1950s [1]. The agent is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in combination with cyclophosphamide as part of pre-transplant conditioning. In pediatric patients, intravenous busulfan is typically administered as 2-hour infusions every 6 h over 4 days, with dosing recommendations based on body weight: 1.1 mg/kg for patients weighing ≤ 12 kg and 0.8 mg/kg for those ≥ 12 kg [2].

Busulfan pharmacokinetics exhibit significant inter-individual variability, and despite established dosing guidelines, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is required to optimize treatment. TDM plays a critical role in adjusting dosages to achieve therapeutic plasma concentrations that maximize efficacy while minimizing the risk of toxicity, including sinusoidal obstruction syndrome and veno-occlusive disease [1, 3–5]. Subtherapeutic exposure increases the risk of graft failure or relapse, whereas excessive drug levels can result in severe toxicities. The therapeutic target for busulfan is typically defined by the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), which varies based on patient-specific factors such as age, weight, underlying condition, and treatment protocol. A common AUC target range for pediatric HSCT patients is 800–1200 (µmol/L·min) per 6-hour dosing interval or 4000–5600 (µmol/L·min) over 24 h [6, 7].

TDM protocols generally rely on pharmacokinetic assessments following the initial busulfan dose, under the assumption that early clearance measurements can predict subsequent drug behavior and guide dose adjustments [6, 8, 9]. However, the effectiveness of first-dose TDM in optimizing therapy and improving clinical outcomes is still under investigation. In this context, two Saudi studies by Alsultan et al. examined overlapping pediatric populations but with different emphases: the 2020 report [11] characterized population pharmacokinetics, while the 2022 study [8] specifically assessed the predictive value of first-dose TDM for steady-state exposure. Together, these complementary reports provide valuable insights into the clinical application of busulfan monitoring in this setting. Busulfan clearance is further influenced by patient-specific factors such as age, weight, and metabolic variability, all of which impact AUC and, consequently, treatment success and safety [10, 11].

This five-year observational study aims to evaluate the role of busulfan TDM in pediatric HSCT patients and identify factors affecting its pharmacokinetic profiles. By analyzing individualized dosing regimens, the study seeks to optimize conditioning therapy strategies and improve HSCT success rates in pediatric oncology. Despite advancements in treatment, comprehensive observational data remain essential for refining standard procedures and enhancing predictability in pediatric HSCT outcomes.

Method

Patients

This study retrospectively analyzed plasma samples collected for TDM of busulfan. The samples were obtained from pediatric patients undergoing busulfan-based conditioning regimens prior to HSCT.

Busulfan was administered over four consecutive days (a total of 16 doses). TDM was conducted on the first day of Busulfan administration, with multiple plasma samples collected at specified time points post-infusion to determine plasma concentration levels, as described in the Analytical Procedures section. Based on the TDM results, dose adjustments were made starting from subsequent doses to achieve and maintain the target AUC range. The initial busulfan dose was calculated using actual body weight and adjusted body weight if the patient’s actual weight exceeded 120% of ideal body weight, according to European Medicines Agency (EMA) and FDA protocols. Dosing categories were: <9 kg: 1.00 mg/kg; 9–<16 kg: 1.20 mg/kg; 16–<23 kg: 1.10 mg/kg; 23–34 kg: 0.95 mg/kg; >34 kg: 0.80 mg/kg. Dose adjustments after the first TDM were made based on AUC values to achieve target exposure. For each patient, six samples were collected on day 1 at 2, 2.15, 2.30, 3-, 4-, and 6-hour post-dose. In cases where the initial AUC result indicated subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic exposure, repeat TDM was performed after dose adjustment (‘TDM repeat’ in Table 1).

Conditioning and immunosuppressive regimens

Patients included in this study received busulfan as part of various conditioning regimens prior to HSCT. The most commonly employed conditioning protocols involved busulfan combined with fludarabine, with or without the addition of thiotepa and/or anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG). In some cases, an alternative regimen consisting of busulfan and cyclophosphamide was used; however, these agents were administered on non-concurrent days.

Post-transplant immunosuppressive therapy typically included either cyclosporine or tacrolimus, administered alone or in combination with methotrexate or mycophenolate mofetil, based on individual patient-specific factors and institutional protocols.

These regimens were applied according to established clinical guidelines to optimize engraftment, minimize toxicity, and prevent GVHD.

Procedures

Materials

Busulfan pure standard was from (ChemCruz®), Tolbutamide standard which was used as an Internal Standard (IS) was from (ChemCruz®), Methanol HPLC grade obtained from (CARLO ERBA), Acetonitrile HPLC grade was from (CARLO ERBA), Acetic acid was obtained from (FLUKA), Formic acid was obtained from (SIGMA), Ammonium hydroxide Solution was from (SIGMA), Deionized water obtained in-house using Milli-Q® Integral 5 Water Purification System (Millipore). 2-ml Auto sampler glass vials (Thermo Scientific).

Instrumentation

Busulfan analysis was performed using a validated LC-MS/MS method in accordance with FDA and EMA bioanalytical method validation guidelines, as previously described [11]. The LC-MS/MS system was equipped with a Xevo-TQ mass detector (Waters, USA) operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The mass transitions (m/z) for busulfan were 264.1 → 151.0 with a cone voltage of 18 V and a collision energy of 18 eV. For the internal standard tolbutamide, the mass transitions (m/z) were 271.2 → 91.0 with a cone voltage of 24 V and a collision energy of 34 eV. The ionization source was operated in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode under the following parameters: capillary voltage 3.0 kV, extractor voltage 3.0 V, source temperature 150 °C, desolvation temperature 500 °C, desolvation gas flow 500 L/h, and collision gas flow 0.15 mL/min. Data acquisition and processing were carried out using MassLynx software version 4.1 (Micromass, Waters).Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, USA) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 55% deionized water, 0.025% acetic acid, 0.025% ammonia solution, 20% methanol, 25% acetonitrile, and 0.01% formic acid, delivered at a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min. The autosampler was maintained at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 10 µL.

Protocol

For each sample, 100 µL of patient plasma, calibration standards, and quality control samples were transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. An equal volume of internal standard (100 µL of tolbutamide solution) was added, and the mixture was vortexed thoroughly. The drug was extracted by adding 800 µL of acetonitrile, followed by another round of vortexing. The mixture was then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min. A clear supernatant (400 µL) was carefully transferred into 400 µL of deionized water in autosampler glass vials. After vortexing, the prepared samples were injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the local Institutional Research Ethics Board at King Abdullah International Medical Center. (IRB number: NRR24/041/5).

Statistics

Analyses were performed in SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.) to calculate statistical parameters. The Shapiro–Wilk test assay revealed that the distribution of continuous variables did not meet the criteria of a normal distribution. As a result, quantitative variables were shown as medians and quartile ranges. To compare the medians of independent variables, the Mann–Whitney test, the Kruskal–Wallis test, and the multiple comparison test were performed. Qualitative variables were presented as the number of patients with a particular feature and as the percentage of the analyzed group. Comparison between qualitative variables was performed using χ2. Values of p < 0.05 were treated as statistically significant after adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg method (BH method). Adjusted p-values are now reported in the results tables, and only those relevant to multiple comparisons were corrected using this method (Tables 2, 3 and 4).

Results

Demographics and characteristics

In total, 166 patients were analyzed, including 99 (59.64%) males and 67 (40.36%) females, with a majority being between the ages of 6–11 years 69 (41.57%), followed by 2–5 years 45 (27.11%). The primary reason for hospitalization was sickle cell anemia, accounting for 72.28% of cases, while other conditions, such as thalassemia (7.83%) and severe combined immunodeficiency (4.22%) were less frequent. Median height, body weight, and BMI were 116.0 cm, 19.05 kg, and 14.92 (kg/m²), respectively. The median daily dose (mg) and weight-adjusted dose (mg/kg per administration every 6 h) were 21.0 mg and 1.1 mg/kg. The median busulfan concentration in the blood plasma of the study population was 937.85. 42.77% of the patients achieved therapeutic busulfan levels, while 42.17% were below range, and 15.06% exceeded the therapeutic range. A majority of patients (80.12%) did not require repeat TDM, and the median length of hospital stay was 44 days. Mortality was low, with only 4.82% of patients dying from their conditions, demonstrating effective clinical management with TDM routine in most cases. (Table 1). Detailed 95% confidence intervals for key clinical parameters are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Table 1).

AUC comparison across clinical variables

An analysis of the study population showed statistically significant differences between the busulfan concentration depending on gender (p = 0.0091) (Table 2). Females exhibited a higher median AUC (1000.10 µmol·min/L) than males (898.01 µmol·min/L). In a multivariable regression model adjusting for age, females continued to show higher AUC values than males (β ≈ +300 µmol/L·min), although this association did not reach statistical significance (p ≈ 0.058) (Supplementary Table 2).

However, no significant differences in AUC level were observed across age groups (p = 0.80) or by primary reason for hospitalization (p = 0.36). The median AUC values across age groups ranged from 890.58 to 951.36 µmol/L·min, which indicates relatively stable drug exposure regardless of age. Among the reasons for hospitalization, the highest AUC level was observed in patients with severe combined immunodeficiency (1047.82 µmol/L·min) and major histocompatibility complex class II deficiency (1053.61 µmol/L·min); however, these differences did not achieve statistical significance (Table 2).

Dosing analysis

In the study population, statistically significant differences were found between the daily dose and the daily dose of busulfan per kilogram of body weight depending on the age (p = 0.0043). The older patients received higher total doses but lower weight-adjusted doses, reflecting the implementation of weight-based dosing protocols. No significant differences in dosing were observed between males and females for either total dose (p = 0.80) or dose per kilogram (p = 0.80). Similarly, therapeutic level categories did not correlate significantly with either total dose (p = 0.44) or dose per kilogram (p = 0.29). (Table 3).

Reasons for hospitalization and therapeutic level based on gender and age

The statistical analysis revealed that no significant difference in hospitalization causes between males and females (p = 0.80). However, hospitalization reasons varied significantly across age groups (p = 0.0013), with sickle cell anemia predominantly affecting older children (6–11 years), while severe combined immunodeficiency was most frequent in infants (> 1 month − 12 months). Regarding therapeutic levels, the statistical analysis for gender differences in the therapeutic range (p < 0.02) showed a significant association, indicating that males and females have different distributions across therapeutic levels. However, no strong statistical significance was found across age groups (p = 0.12) (Table 4).

Discussion

Busulfan is a chemotherapeutic agent with a narrow therapeutic index, making pharmacokinetic variability a critical concern due to its impact on treatment outcomes, including the risk of toxicity or graft rejection. To the best of our knowledge, we are the only center in Saudi Arabia that performs TDM for personalized busulfan conditioning. Despite standardized weight-based dosing protocols, our findings indicate that a significant proportion of patients (42.17%) had subtherapeutic busulfan levels, while 15.06% exceeded the therapeutic range. These findings are consistent with previous research that have demonstrated considerable interpatient variability in busulfan pharmacokinetics, particularly in pediatric populations. Malär et al. [12] reported that in a study of 34 pediatric patients, busulfan dose adjustments were necessary in 23 patients (68%), with dose increases required for 16 patients (70%). Similarly, a pilot study in Thai children found that only 42.86% of patients achieved the target AUC values [13].

A notable finding in our study was that female patients exhibited higher busulfan AUC values compared to males, with over half of male patients falling below the therapeutic range. Importantly, dose per kg did not differ between sexes (Table 3; p = 0.80), which means the higher exposure observed in females is not attributable to dosing differences. In a regression model adjusted for age, the effect direction remained the same (females ≈ + 300 µmol/L/min higher), although the association did not reach statistical significance (p ≈ 0.058). This suggests that females may indeed be predisposed to greater busulfan exposure, but the small sample size limited statistical certainty.

Busulfan is predominantly cleared by glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and prior studies have shown that GSTA1B haplotypes and GSTM1-null genotypes are associated with reduced clearance and higher busulfan concentrations [14, 15]. Some reports have also suggested sex-dependent effects of GST polymorphisms, with females exhibiting lower clearance [16]. In addition, hormonal factors may further modulate GST expression and activity. Studies have also demonstrated that females are more likely to experience severe adverse events across cancer therapies [17, 18]. Together, these mechanisms support the observation that female patients may be at higher risk of supratherapeutic exposure and related toxicities, consistent with broader oncology literature showing that females often experience greater chemotherapy-related toxicity.

Our findings, though hypothesis-generating, emphasize the potential importance of considering female sex as a risk factor for elevated busulfan exposure. Future research integrating therapeutic drug monitoring with pharmacogenomic profiling (e.g., GSTA1 and GSTM1) will be important to determine whether female-specific or genotype-guided dosing adjustments can improve target attainment and reduce toxicity risk in pediatric HSCT.

The main reason for hospitalization also affected the AUC levels. Patients with sickle cell anemia were more likely to have subtherapeutic levels of busulfan. This finding is consistent with previous studies that showed the clearance of the first dose of busulfan was significantly higher than the clearance of subsequent daily doses [3]. Conversely, patients with severe combined immunodeficiency and major histocompatibility complex class II deficiency showed higher AUC levels. Although these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.36), they underscore the importance of a new dose-adjustment policy based on patient conditions.

Despite these important findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. The patient population in our cohort was heterogeneous, encompassing a variety of underlying diseases, and the number of patients within each disease subgroup was relatively small. Nevertheless, this study represents all available patient samples collected over a five-year period at the only center currently performing busulfan TDM in the region, providing valuable real-world insights. Larger, multi-center studies are warranted to validate these observations and enable more detailed stratified analyses in the future. Furthermore, our pharmacokinetic assessment was based solely on the first-dose AUC measurement. While this approach aligns with common clinical TDM protocols for early dose adjustment, it does not capture intra-individual variability in clearance that may occur across multiple busulfan doses. Several studies have indicated that busulfan clearance can decrease throughout conditioning therapy, potentially impacting drug exposure and toxicity risk [9, 12]. Future research incorporating serial pharmacokinetic sampling across consecutive doses is warranted to better understand intra-patient changes in clearance and to refine individualized dosing strategies.

It is important to note that while median AUC values appeared relatively stable across age groups, significant inter-individual differences in drug exposure persisted. This discrepancy supports the continued need for individualized dosing strategies rather than reliance on group-level medians alone. Most importantly, although 57% of our patients fell outside the therapeutic range, our dataset did not include consistent records of detailed clinical outcomes such as GVHD incidence, SOS/VOD, or engraftment kinetics. Mortality data were available, but no statistically significant association was observed between AUC category and overall survival, likely due to limited statistical power. The absence of comprehensive outcome data prevents us from directly correlating subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic exposure with clinical endpoints in this cohort. Nevertheless, prior studies have demonstrated that subtherapeutic exposure is associated with increased risk of graft failure, while supratherapeutic exposure is linked to higher incidence of SOS/VOD and treatment-related mortality [19, 20]. By situating our findings within this established evidence, we underscore that our results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, highlighting the urgent need for prospective studies in our region to confirm outcome associations.

While this study consolidates five years of real-world TDM experience at the only center in our region performing busulfan monitoring, it remains a retrospective analysis with the inherent methodological constraints of such designs. Nonetheless, it establishes a critical foundation for advancing precision dosing strategies in pediatric HSCT. Future efforts should integrate pharmacogenomics (PGx), advanced population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) modeling, and pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic (PK–PD) analyses to optimize individualized therapy. Specifically, ongoing initiatives will focus on developing a local PopPK model derived from this dataset, incorporating genotype-guided dosing algorithms targeting key pharmacogenetic markers (e.g., GSTA1 and GSTM1), and prospectively correlating AUC values with clinical outcomes such as toxicity, engraftment kinetics, and GVHD incidence. The adoption of a comprehensive, model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) framework holds promise for enhancing the safety, efficacy, and predictability of busulfan-based conditioning regimens, thereby advancing the standard of care in pediatric HSCT [21].

Conclusions

This study highlights the complexities of achieving optimal therapeutic busulfan levels in the pediatric population. Significant variability was observed in drug exposure, influenced by factors such as age, gender, and diagnosis. While weight-based dosing protocols are commonly employed, they do not consistently ensure therapeutic drug levels, as evidenced by the substantial proportion of patients falling below or above the target range. Gender differences in busulfan pharmacokinetics, with females exhibiting higher AUC values, underscore the need for individualized therapeutic approaches. Additionally, patients with conditions such as sickle cell anemia were more likely to experience subtherapeutic levels, emphasizing the importance of individualized dosing and close therapeutic drug monitoring in these subgroups.

Importantly, while our findings support individualized dosing, we recognize that the clinical consequences of sex-based or diagnosis-based variability remain unconfirmed without outcome correlation. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged. Future research should focus on developing refined dosing algorithms, integrating pharmacogenetic factors (e.g., GSTA1, GSTM1 genotypes), and prospectively linking AUC categories with clinical outcomes such as GVHD, toxicity, engraftment kinetics, and survival. The adoption of a model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) framework, integrating pharmacogenomics and PopPK, holds promise for enhancing the safety and efficacy of busulfan conditioning regimens, thereby advancing the standard of care in pediatric HSCT.
