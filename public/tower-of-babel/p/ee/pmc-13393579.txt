Bioactive 3D-Printed valve scaffold promoting valvular regeneration via immunomodulation and endothelialization
Authors: Kun Yang, Xianzhen Dong, Linlin Guo, Chuan Gao, Shuyu Wen, Jiahui She, Wenliang Liu, Renyuan Sun, Weihua Qiao, Jiawei Shi, Jianglin Wang, Chao Xu, Hongjun Yang, Zhiqiang Luo, Nianguo Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe in vivo performance of tissue-engineered heart valves remains constrained by a persistent early failure triad at the blood-material interface, namely thrombosis, unresolved inflammation, and slow endothelialization. Here, a bioinstructive multilayer valve scaffold is engineered to address these interfacial barriers through spatially integrated structural and biological functions. The scaffold comprises a digitally programmable 3D-printed framework, a silk fibroin wrapping layer, and a hydrogel biointerface incorporating Arg-Gly-Asp/GelMA adhesive cues together with H2S-releasing microgels. This layered design endows the construct with valve-relevant tensile properties while simultaneously programming the immune-endothelial microenvironment. The hydrogel biointerface promotes endothelial migration, proliferation, and angiogenic activity, whereas sustained H2S delivery biases macrophages toward a pro-resolving M2-like phenotype and suppresses inflammatory activation. Transcriptomic analysis further reveals coordinated upregulation of endothelial programs related to endothelial repair, migration, and proliferation, accompanied by attenuation of stress- and inflammation-associated responses. In vivo, the scaffold mitigates thromboinflammatory reactions, shows preliminary anti-calcification performance, and supports endothelialization under blood-contacting conditions. Together, this work establishes a layered bioactive engineering approach that converts a passive 3D-printed structural scaffold into a regenerative, hemocompatible, and immunoregulatory biofunctional valve scaffold. This strategy offers a promising design principle for the further development of regenerative valve scaffolds.Keywords: Tissue-engineered heart valve, 3D-printed scaffold, Hydrogen sulfide, Immune-endothelial interface, Endothelialization

Introduction

Valvular heart disease remains a major global health burden and a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1,2]. This challenge is particularly acute for pediatric patients, for whom no existing prosthetic valve offers a definitive solution. Mechanical valves, while durable, necessitate lifelong anticoagulation and lack growth potential. Bioprosthetic valves, though avoiding anticoagulation, are prone to calcification and structural deterioration due to glutaraldehyde fixation, leading to poor long-term durability and limited endothelialization [[3], [4], [5]]. Consequently, pediatric patients often face repeated reoperations and cumulative surgical risks. This highlights the need for valve replacements that can function immediately while also supporting remodeling and growth. In pursuit of this goal, tissue-engineered heart valves (TEHVs) have emerged as a promising strategy to create living, growing replacements by harnessing the body's regenerative capacity [6]. However, a significant translational gap persists. Many TEHV designs excel in specific areas, such as mechanical integrity or bioactivity, yet fail to integrate these properties effectively into a holistic solution. This disconnect invariably manifests as a recurrent triad of early-stage complications: thrombosis at the blood-contacting surface, unresolved inflammation, and critically, delayed endothelialization. The persistence of these issues across platforms shows that optimizing single parameters in isolation is no longer sufficient. The future TEHV development may benefit from holistic design strategy that co-optimizes structural, immunomodulatory, and pro-endothelial functions.

The advent of additive manufacturing has revolutionized the design of cardiac biomaterials, enabling the production of digitally programmable scaffolds with the potential to be adapted to patient-specific anatomies [7]. Through three-dimensional (3D) printing, porous frameworks can be engineered to provide customized mechanical support and inherent scalability. Nevertheless, this structural fidelity constitutes only a passive template. To ensure clinical success, the implanted valve must be endowed with bioactive components that actively orchestrate hemocompatibility and guide tissue integration.

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is now recognized as an endogenous gasotransmitter that plays fundamental roles in cardiovascular and inflammatory homeostasis [8,9]. Owing to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and immunomodulatory activities, H2S has emerged as a cytoprotective mediator of tissue repair [10]. This therapeutic potential is further supported by recent studies using H2S-releasing nanoplatforms in diverse infectious and inflammatory disease models, including infected oral mucosal wounds [11], inflammatory bowel disease [12,13], and hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury [14]. In these settings, controlled H2S delivery attenuates ROS accumulation, reinforces endogenous antioxidant defenses, suppresses NF-κB/MAPK-related inflammatory signaling, and promotes macrophage repolarization toward reparative M2 phenotypes, thereby reducing tissue injury and facilitating functional recovery. These disease-spanning findings are particularly relevant to cardiovascular regeneration, where persistent inflammation and oxidative stress often impair endothelial recovery and drive maladaptive remodeling. Therefore, H2S holds great potential for cardiovascular regeneration by alleviating inflammation, inhibiting fibrosis, and enhancing endothelial cell proliferation and migration [15,16]. However, the use of H2S is hampered by its narrow therapeutic window, necessitating advanced delivery systems that can achieve sustained, localized release to harness its regenerative potential while avoiding cytotoxicity [17].

Herein, we propose a spatially organized PM/SF/hydrogel scaffold architecture that functionally integrates porous structural stability, interfacial biocompatibility, immunomodulation, and endothelial guidance (Fig. 1). The PM component was designed as a DLP-printable porous supporting framework, because photocurable PM enables high-resolution and reproducible fabrication of valve-like architectures while helping maintain a stable spatial geometry (Fig. 1a). This porous framework was conformally wrapped with silk fibroin (SF), which was selected as a biocompatible protein-based interfacial coating to improve the surface compatibility of the printed framework and provide a suitable substrate for hydrogel integration (Fig. 1b). The outer PGR/PGM@PGR hydrogel layer was positioned at the blood-contacting surface to directly regulate early thrombo-inflammatory reactions, oxidative stress, and delayed endothelialization (Fig. 1c). In this configuration, sustained H2S delivery biases the local immune milieu toward a pro-regenerative state by promoting M2 macrophage polarization, thereby mitigating early thromboinflammatory injury and contributing to anti-calcification behavior. Meanwhile, GelMA and RGDAA in the hydrogel coating supply a high density of adhesive cues that collaboratively drive endothelial cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and rapid monolayer formation. In addition, the PEGDA-rich hydrogel network resists nonspecific protein adsorption and platelet adhesion. This design combines a digitally programmable 3D-printed scaffold with multilayer bioactive components. The resulting construct improves hemocompatibility, modulates the immune response, and supports endothelial repair at the blood-contacting interface (Fig. 1d). More broadly, this strategy may help address several early complications after valve replacement and provides a biofunctional scaffold platform for further development toward regenerative valve engineering, particularly in pediatric settings where growth and remodeling remain major unmet needs.

Results and discussion

Development and characterization of PM resin and SF/PM scaffold

Digital light processing (DLP) enables the rapid and convenient fabrication of tissue- and organ-mimetic constructs with complex anatomies, tunable dimensions, and high spatial resolution [18]. Essential to the field are photoresins that couple biocompatibility with high precision. In this contribution, the photopolymerizable monomer polycaprolactone triol methacrylate (PM) was obtained by reacting polycaprolactone triol (PCLT) with methacrylic anhydride (MAAh) (Fig. 2a). Unmethacrylated PCLT and the resulting PM were characterized by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy [19]. Relative to PCLT, PM shows additional resonances at 6.09 and 5.55 ppm that correspond to the methacrylate vinyl protons, confirming the successful synthesis of PM (Fig. 2b). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum shows a new band at 1637 cm−1 in PM, which is absent in PCLT and corresponds to the methacrylate C=C stretching vibration, thereby corroborating the successful synthesis of PM (Fig. S1) [20]. Further, a high-precision, photocurable PM resin was obtained by homogeneously blending PM with diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide/2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (photoinitiator system) and β-carotene (photoabsorber), which readily polymerizes upon ultraviolet (UV) exposure (Fig. 2c).

Resin viscosity critically affects recoating and settling in DLP. Lower viscosity facilitates sufficient reflow for uniform layer coverage, ensuring consistent light exposure and reducing the risk of build failure [21,22]. Rheological measurements were then conducted to assess the PM resin's viscosity and photopolymerization kinetics. As shown in the temperature-dependent viscosity profiles (Fig. 2d), the resin displayed time-independent behavior at each constant temperature. Its viscosity was approximately 1.05 Pa s at 20 °C and progressively decreased with increasing temperature, while remaining within a DLP-compatible range across the entire interval. Based on this characterization, a temperature of 50 °C was selected for the subsequent printing experiments in this study. Photocrosslinking kinetic analysis (Fig. 2e) shows that the PM resin undergoes rapid UV-induced network formation, reaching a gel point within approximately 0.7 s. This finding suggests that relatively short layer exposure times are sufficient, which is advantageous for faster DLP builds. To evaluate practical print performance, calibration structures with varied geometries were designed in SolidWorks (SW) for assessment. DLP printing cures the resin layer-by-layer via projected light patterns (Fig. S2a), yielding constructs with intact architectures and sharp boundaries without visible delamination (Fig. S2b), indicative of robust interlayer adhesion and stable build quality. Across three representative porous lattice topologies (Fig. S3), the printed architectures showed close design-model agreement, supporting broad process compatibility for pore-architecture programming. A gradient feature model integrating thin-wall/gap arrays and microneedle arrays (Fig. S4) was further used to quantify the resolution limit, revealing continuous features with a minimum distinguishable size of ∼80 μm (arrows). Moreover, additional complex geometries were designed and printed (Fig. S5). The printed components showed excellent agreement with the original 3D models, underscoring the capability of PM resin to realize complex 3D topologies, curved surfaces, and multi-scale structural integration. Collectively, these assessments of printing accuracy, structural integrity, and multi-scale feature fidelity validate the potential of the PM resin/DLP platform for biomimetic scaffold manufacturing. Here, a PM-printed porous valvular scaffold was selected as the model system for subsequent experiments to provide a coherent demonstration of the platform.

Silk fibroin (SF) membranes are widely used in biomedical engineering owing to their excellent biocompatibility and tunable mechanics [23]. However, they cannot be directly fabricated into 3D biomimetic scaffolds. Inspired by the concept of reinforced concrete, where rebar defines the frame and concrete encapsulates and infills, we implemented an integrated fabrication process. An SF membrane enveloped a 3D biomimetic porous PM scaffold to form an SF/PM scaffold, and the detailed fabrication procedure is shown in Fig. S6. The conformation of the SF membrane was analyzed by FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 2f). Two broad bands were observed at around 1620 cm−1 and 1230 cm−1, which are attributed to the amide I (C=O stretching of peptide bonds) and amide III (C-N stretching and N-H bending vibrations of the peptide backbone) regions [24], respectively. Deconvolution of the SF amide I band (1600-1700 cm−1) quantified the secondary structure as 76.74% β-sheet, 16.33% random coil/helix, and 6.92% β-turn (Fig. 2g). The high β-sheet content underpins mechanical robustness, whereas the coil/helix fraction imparts flexibility. Analysis of the amide I Raman spectra (Fig. 2h) similarly indicates a β-sheet-dominated secondary structure [25]. In addition, as shown in Fig. 2i, the noncovalently mediated collective motions of tyrosine (Tyr) within amorphous strands were evaluated using the intensity ratio of its Raman bands at 846 and 818 cm−1, corresponding to the characteristic tyrosine doublet (I846/I818) [26]. These interactions collectively form a dynamically coupled network that preserves structural stability while providing high flexibility (Fig. S7) and reversible conformational adaptability in silk fibroin [26,27].

Given the high flow velocities and shear stresses experienced by native valves, tissue-engineered replacements must exhibit exceptional biomechanical robustness [28]. Accordingly, uniaxial tensile tests were conducted on all specimens to assess whether the SF/PM constructs provide the requisite mechanical performance. In uniaxial tensile testing (Fig. 2j and k and Fig. S8), the SF/PM scaffold exhibited an ultimate tensile strength of 16.24 ± 0.32 MPa, an elastic modulus of 89.02 ± 6.83 MPa, and a toughness of 5.79 ± 0.30 MJ m−3. The tensile strength and elastic modulus were higher than the reported values for native heart valves, which show an ultimate tensile strength of approximately 7.5 MPa and an elastic modulus of approximately 40 MPa [29]. These results indicate that the SF/PM scaffold possesses enhanced mechanical robustness, supporting its potential as a mechanically relevant valve scaffold. To evaluate the in vitro degradation behavior of SF membrane and PM cured sample, both materials were subjected to a PBS-based degradation assay under simulated physiological conditions for 3 months, and their degradation profiles were assessed by measuring mass loss. As shown in Fig. S9, SF and PM exhibited mass losses of 19.73% and 3.28% after 90 days, respectively. These results indicate that both materials underwent measurable degradation in the PBS environment.

Preparation and characterization of bioactive PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold

To further enhance the cytocompatibility of the SF/PM scaffold and suppress nonspecific protein adsorption, we applied a PGR hydrogel composed of 8-arm polyethylene glycol diacrylate (8-arm PEGDA), gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), and RGDAA to its surface through PDMA-mediated interfacial adhesion, analogous to a finishing layer on reinforced concrete. In parallel, an H2S donor was incorporated to enable sustained H2S release and thereby modulate the interfacial microenvironment. Peroxythiocarbamate (PTC), as an H2S donor, was prepared via the reaction between 4-(hydroxymethyl) phenylboronic acid pinacol ester and 4-fluorophenyl isothiocyanate (Fig. 3a). 1H NMR and FT-IR spectra (Fig. S10) jointly corroborate its successful synthesis. The boronic ester moiety of PTC is able to undergo oxidation by reactive oxygen species (ROS) to generate carbonyl sulfide (COS), and 4-hydroxybenzyl alcohol. Subsequently, carbonic anhydrase, which is broadly expressed in vivo, rapidly converts COS to H2S, enabling in situ H2S release. The in vitro kinetics of PTC conversion to COS were assessed using H2O2 as a surrogate oxidant, and reaction rates were calculated from time-course 1H NMR spectra by quantifying the decay of the parent signal at approximately 5.5 ppm (B) and the concomitant growth of the product signal at approximately 4.4 ppm (B’) (Fig. 3b). To enable sustained release, a photocurable GelMA was prepared as a PTC carrier, with methacrylation verified by 1H NMR and FT-IR spectra (Fig. S11). Specifically, PTC and GelMA were mixed to form an aqueous phase, while a surfactant-containing fluorinated oil served as the continuous phase. PTC-GelMA microgels (PGMs) were then produced by microfluidics (Fig. 3c and Fig. S12). Representative optical images are shown in Fig. 3d, and size analysis using ImageJ yielded a mean diameter of 58.63 ± 2.27 μm (Fig. 3e).

To enable robust grafting of the hydrogel onto the SF membrane substrate, dopamine (DA) and dopamine methacryloyl (DMA) were introduced as an interfacial coupling layer to mediate adhesion. The successful synthesis of DMA and 8-arm PEGDA was verified by the characterization results shown in Fig. S13 and Fig. S14. DA and DMA were deposited onto the SF/PM scaffold to form a PDMA interfacial coupling layer via catechol-mediated surface adhesion [30,31]. The scaffold was coated with a PGR pre-crosslinked solution and subsequently subjected to UV irradiation to yield the PGR/SF/PM scaffold. Similarly, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold was fabricated using a PGR pre-crosslinked solution containing PGM. Fig. 3f illustrates PDMA-mediated interfacial coupling between the PGR hydrogel and the SF substrate. The interface is reinforced by multiple noncovalent interactions together with UV-triggered acrylate copolymerization, resulting in a stable hydrogel coating. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of SF/PM, PDMA-modified SF/PM, and PGR/SF/PM showed a gradual attenuation of the characteristic SF (020) reflection with successive surface modification and hydrogel integration (Fig. S15). SEM surface imaging showed that PDMA treatment increased the surface roughness of SF, whereas subsequent PGR coating smoothed the interfacial features and yielded a more homogeneous coverage (Fig. S16). As shown in Fig. 3g, the cross-sectional SEM image of the hydrogel-coated SF revealed a uniform and continuous hydrogel layer that was well integrated with the SF substrate, without obvious cracks or delamination. The coating thickness, denoted as h, was approximately 7 μm. These morphological changes support successful stepwise modification. The adhesion strength between the hydrogel coating and the SF substrate was measured to be 31.8 kPa by the lap-shear test, indicating stable interfacial bonding (Fig. 3h). Surface wettability of the different scaffolds was evaluated via water contact angle (WCA) measurements (Fig. 3i). The results indicated that the WCA of the bare PM material was 115.62 ± 5.73°. Following encapsulation with SF, the WCA decreased to 71.23 ± 2.08°, indicating the hydrophilic nature of the SF layer. Subsequently, the PGR/SF/PM scaffold further reduced the WCA to 63.43 ± 2.76°, demonstrating that the PGR hydrogel confers enhanced hydrophilicity on the scaffold.

The material-level anti-calcification potential of the scaffold is critical for its subsequent implantation performance. Therefore, an in vitro calcification assay was performed. GV, a clinically used glutaraldehyde-crosslinked bioprosthetic valve material, was selected as reference material. As shown in Fig. S17, obvious and relatively uniform mineral deposition was observed on the GV surface, while SF also exhibited a certain degree of calcium deposition. In contrast, PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds showed limited calcium deposition, indicating favorable anti-calcification performance. ICP-OES quantification further showed that the GV group had the highest calcium content of 7.77 μg mg−1, whereas the PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM groups exhibited the lowest calcium levels, approximately 2.8 μg mg−1 (Fig. 3j). These results further demonstrate the anti-calcification potential of the scaffolds developed in this study. After 100 tensile cycles of the PGR/SF/PM scaffold (Fig. S18), SEM images showed no obvious cracks on the surface of the hydrogel coating, indicating that the coating possessed sufficient flexibility to accommodate repeated deformation of the substrate without causing immediate interfacial failure. In addition, pulsatile flow evaluation showed that most of the hydrogel coating remained tightly attached to the SF substrate after 10 days of cyclic mechanical loading. Although partial coating degradation or localized defects were observed, the coating retained relative structural stability under short-term dynamic mechanical conditions (Fig. S19). Together with the cyclic tensile results, the pulsatile-flow assessment provides preliminary support for the valve-scaffold orientation of the construct by demonstrating short-term dynamic adaptability, observable opening/closing behavior, and coating retention under cyclic loading conditions.

HPLC was used to monitor the decomposition kinetics of PTC released from the PGM system under ROS-triggered conditions. According to the decomposition pathway of PTC, ROS-mediated cleavage of PTC generates COS, a precursor for H2S formation, accompanied by the stoichiometric production of p-hydroxybenzyl alcohol (p-HBA). Therefore, the generation kinetics of p-HBA were measured as an indirect indicator of the H2S-generating capability of PTC released from PGM. The results showed that p-HBA generation in the PGM system was dependent on the H2O2 concentration. In the presence of 50 μM H2O2, the cumulative p-HBA generation reached approximately 16% within 2 days (Fig. S20), whereas only limited p-HBA generation was observed in the absence of H2O2. These results indicate that PGM serves as a reservoir for PTC release, and the released PTC can undergo ROS-triggered decomposition to support sustained H2S generation. This release profile supports prolonged donor availability for subsequent ROS-triggered conversion and H2S generation.

Cytocompatibility evaluation of scaffold materials

Cytocompatibility of the scaffold material is a primary requirement for developing biofunctional valve scaffolds toward regenerative heart valve engineering [32]. Rigorous assessment therefore needs to account for the key cellular constituents of native valve tissue. Native heart valves feature a complex cellular architecture predominantly comprising valvular endothelial cells and valvular interstitial cells, which together orchestrate valve homeostasis and function [33,34]. Valvular interstitial cells, residing beneath the endothelial layer, are the principal cell type responsible for synthesizing and remodeling the valvular extracellular matrix (ECM) [35]. In contrast, valvular endothelial cells form a continuous monolayer covering all valvular surfaces and are in direct contact with the circulating blood [36]. Through their coordinated interactions, valvular endothelial cells and valvular interstitial cells maintain the long-term structural integrity and function of the valves. When a scaffold is implanted in the body, endothelial progenitor cells in the bloodstream and adjacent endothelial cells can migrate and proliferate, ultimately leading to complete endothelialization of the scaffold surface [37]. To evaluate the concentration-dependent cytocompatibility of the H2S donor, a CCK-8 assay was performed by co-culturing HUVECs with donor-loaded scaffold discs at effective concentrations of 50, 100, and 200 μM. No significant decrease in OD450 was observed at 50 or 100 μM compared with the control, whereas 200 μM led to a marked reduction at later time points (Fig. S21a). Based on these results, the effect of 100 μM on RAW264.7 cells was further evaluated. As shown in Fig. S21b, no significant difference was observed between the 100 μM-treated group and the control group, indicating that this concentration was also safe for RAW264.7 cells and supporting the use of concentrations ≤100 μM in subsequent experiments.

The cytocompatibility of different scaffold materials was then assessed using paired live/dead staining and CCK-8 assays. In the presence of PGR/SF/PM or PGM@PGR/SF/PM, HUVEC cultures exhibited cell densities comparable to those in the SF/PM group and the tissue-culture control (Fig. S22a). Consistently, the CCK-8 results from the same set of samples showed that the proliferation trend in the presence of PGR/SF/PM closely matched the control, while SF/PM yielded lower OD450 values (Fig. S22b). Notably, by day 5, cultures containing PGM@PGR/SF/PM showed a modest but significant increase in OD450 relative to the control, which may be associated with adhesive cues from GelMA/RGDAA together with sustained H2S release reported to support endothelial cell survival and proliferation.

Immunomodulatory functionality of bioactive scaffolds

Persistent inflammation is a hallmark of cardiovascular disease progression, driving an imbalance between tissue injury and repair. In calcified valvular lesions, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages dominate the infiltrating immune population and release cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and ROS, which together amplify local inflammation and induce osteogenic differentiation of VICs, thereby accelerating calcification [38,39]. Moreover, implantation of prosthetic scaffolds during valve replacement introduces additional inflammatory stimuli, further disturbing immune homeostasis. Given the intrinsic plasticity of macrophages to switch phenotype in response to microenvironmental cues, regulation of macrophage polarization has emerged as an effective strategy to mitigate inflammation and promote tissue regeneration. The gaseous signaling molecule H2S has attracted increasing attention for its immunomodulatory activity. H2S suppresses inflammatory signaling through S-sulfhydration of key proteins such as the NF-κB p65 subunit, thereby inhibiting NF-κB, JNK, and ERK activation [40]. As a powerful redox regulator, it also helps restore mitochondrial function and modulates the epigenetic state of M0 macrophages, promoting their polarization toward the pro-resolving M2-like phenotype [41,42]. These alternatively activated M2 macrophages secrete transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), which collectively enhance fibroblast recruitment and collagen deposition, fostering a regenerative microenvironment [43]. Inspired by this mechanism, we incorporated an H2S donor PTC into the PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold to enable sustained H2S release, and its immunomodulatory effects were characterized by immunofluorescence staining, ELISA, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and related analyses (Fig. 4a).

Firstly, the H2S-responsive probe 7-azido-4-methylcoumarin (AzMC) was employed to assess the biological response of RAW264.7 macrophages to PTC. As illustrated in Fig. 4b and Fig. S23a, cells without H2O2 treatment showed no detectable fluorescence signal, whereas a dose-dependent increase in AzMC fluorescence, reflecting enhanced H2S generation, was observed under oxidative stress. These results indicate that PTC exhibits ROS-responsive behavior, enabling it to decompose and release H2S specifically in environments with elevated ROS levels. Such controlled H2S release enables more precise therapeutic action at inflamed sites while minimizing potential damage to normal cells and tissues. To further verify whether H2S contributes to macrophage polarization, GelMA microgels (GM) without PTC and PGM containing PTC were compared under oxidative stimulation (Fig. S23b and S23c). Compared with the GM group, macrophages co-cultured with PGM exhibited reduced CD86 expression and markedly increased CD206 expression, indicating that PTC-derived H2S suppressed M1-like polarization and promoted an M2-like reparative polarization phenotype while suppressing M1-like activation.

Macrophage phenotype polarization represents a form of functional reprogramming that dictates macrophage physiological roles. To investigate whether the PGM@PGR coating, potentially through sustained H2S release, could modulate this process, RAW264.7 macrophages were co-cultured with SF/PM, PGR/SF/PM, or PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds for 48 h under oxidative stress, and polarization was assessed by immunofluorescence staining for the M1-associated marker CD86 and the M2-associated marker CD206. As shown in Fig. 4c and d, macrophages cultured with SF/PM exhibited no pronounced phenotypic alteration compared with the Control group. By contrast, the PGR/SF/PM group induced a modest phenotypic shift relative to the SF/PM group, which may be attributed to the bioactive contribution of GelMA-derived cell-interactive motifs, including Arg-Gly-Asp sequences [44]. In contrast, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group exhibited a marked decrease in CD86 expression together with a pronounced increase in CD206 expression, indicating that the PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold, likely through sustained H2S release, promoted a shift of macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype. In addition, CD206-positive cells in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group displayed a more elongated morphology, a feature commonly associated with pro-resolving macrophage phenotypes. RT-qPCR and ELISA analyses further supported these observations, showing decreased levels of CD86 and TNF-α and increased levels of CD206 and IL-10 in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group relative to the other groups (Fig. 4e and f and Fig. S24a, S24b).

Scaffold-driven gene expression in macrophages

To further elucidate the molecular basis of these phenotypic changes, macrophage samples obtained under identical treatment conditions were subjected to RNA-seq. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation among the three groups, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. S25a). Relative to the PGR/SF/PM group, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group exhibited 71 upregulated genes and 72 downregulated genes (Fig. S25b). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that these differentially expressed genes were mainly involved in macrophage migration, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress-related biological processes (Fig. 4g). Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed positive enrichment of a ROS metabolism-related gene set in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, indicating enhanced oxidative stress adaptation in macrophages (Fig. 4h). Together, these transcriptomic analyses suggested that PGM@PGR/SF/PM not only attenuated inflammatory activation, but also promoted a broader pro-resolving and immunoregulatory macrophage program.

Compared with the SF/PM group, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group showed 121 upregulated genes and 326 downregulated genes (Fig. S25c), which were primarily enriched in pathways related to immune response, cytokine activity, and chemokine signaling (Fig. S25d). GSEA analysis suggests enhanced regulation of ROS metabolic processes in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group (Fig. S25e). Furthermore, a normalized heatmap revealed a distinct transcriptional pattern in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, particularly among genes associated with chemotaxis, macrophage activation, repair-related growth factors, ECM remodeling, and migration/adhesion (Fig. S25f). Notably, several repair-associated genes, including Hbegf, Gdf15, Il1rn, and Tgm2, were upregulated, supporting the activation of an immunoregulatory, tissue repair-associated macrophage program. To validate the activation of repair-related signaling pathways at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed (Fig. 4i). Compared with the Control, the functionalized groups exhibited increased phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR, with the strongest activation observed in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group. ImageJ-based densitometric analysis showed that the p-mTOR/mTOR and p-AKT/AKT ratios in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group were approximately 6-fold and 11-fold higher than those in the Control group, respectively (Fig. 4j). These results indicate that PGM@PGR/SF/PM activated AKT/mTOR signaling in macrophages, contributing to the immunoregulatory and tissue repair-associated phenotype.

Consistent with transcriptomic analysis implicating HBEGF-related signaling, the increased expression of Hbegf supports the involvement of the HBEGF-EGFR-MEK/ERK axis in downstream repair-associated cellular responses (Fig. S25g). Moreover, these findings implicate scaffold-reprogrammed macrophages as active paracrine regulators of endothelial repair. Upregulation of Hbegf positions macrophage-derived HBEGF as a candidate intercellular mediator capable of engaging EGFR signaling in adjacent endothelial cells, while the coordinate suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine output is expected to attenuate the hostile oxidative milieu that otherwise compromises endothelial integrity. The mechanistic consequences of this proposed immune-to-endothelial crosstalk are interrogated in the following section. Collectively, sustained H2S release from PGM@PGR/SF/PM reshaped macrophage polarization and transcriptional responses under oxidative stress (Fig. S25h), fostering an anti-inflammatory, tissue repair-supportive microenvironment.

Endothelial cell behavior mediated by bioactive scaffolds

Given that macrophage polarization toward a pro-resolving phenotype is expected to generate a repair-supportive condition, we next investigated whether the scaffold could directly support endothelial regeneration. The valvular endothelium serves as a multifunctional barrier that maintains tissue homeostasis, modulates inflammatory signaling, and prevents pathological calcification and thrombosis. Loss or delayed regeneration of this layer after injury disrupts these protective mechanisms and compromises long-term valve function [45]. Consequently, timely and effective endothelialization is critical for scaffold integration and restoration of a stable blood-contacting interface. To assess the endothelialization capacity of the scaffold, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were used as a representative endothelial model. Their migratory and tube-forming behaviors were examined to evaluate the scaffold's pro-angiogenic and pro-healing potential (Fig. S26a-S26c).

As shown in Fig. 5a, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group exhibited markedly enhanced cell migration at 24 h, with pronounced wound closure evident at 48 h. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 5d) revealed that the wound closure area in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group was significantly greater than in the SF/PM and PGR/SF/PM groups. Consistent with these findings, the transwell assay (Fig. 5b and e) demonstrated a significantly increased migration rate of HUVECs in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, suggesting that the integrated PGM@PGR biointerface promoted endothelial cell migration through the combined contribution of the hydrogel matrix, adhesive cues, and H2S-generating component. In the tube formation assay, early tubular structures were observed at 6 h, whereas more mature and interconnected networks had developed by 12 h. Notably, the PGR/SF/PM group exhibited improved tube formation compared with the SF/PM group, likely owing to the presence of GelMA and RGDAA that enhance cell adhesion and signaling [46]. Among all groups, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group generated the most extensive vascular-like networks, and the number of meshes was significantly higher than in the other groups (Fig. 5c and f).

Moreover, the relative expression levels of the endothelial marker genes CD31 and VEGF were markedly upregulated in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group (Fig. S26d). CD31 is widely used to evaluate endothelial coverage and junction integrity, whereas VEGF is a potent endothelial mitogen that enhances endothelial growth and activation [47,48]. The increased expression of CD31 and VEGF suggests an enhanced endothelial-associated phenotype in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group. Together with the migration results, these data suggest that the scaffold supports both endothelial movement and endothelial function, which are critical for repair and regeneration.

Scaffold-driven gene expression in endothelial cells

To further investigate the biological mechanisms underlying the multifunctional effects of the PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold, RNA-seq was performed on HUVECs cultured with different scaffolds for 4 days. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using an absolute log2(fold change) > 1 and p < 0.05 as thresholds. PCA revealed tight intra-group clustering and clear separation between groups (PC1 = 85.17%, PC2 = 12.34%), indicating robust and systematic transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 6a). The Venn diagram (left panel) in Fig. 6b illustrates the overlap of DEGs among the three comparison groups, whereas the bar chart (right panel) summarizes the number of DEGs in each comparison.

Volcano plots indicated that introducing the PGR coating onto SF/PM (PGR/SF/PM) elicited a modest yet directional shift in gene expression (Fig. 6c), marked by increased expression of immediate-early and motility-associated genes, including FOSB, EGR3, NR4A1, and WNT6. In contrast, incorporation of the H2S-releasing component (PGM@PGR/SF/PM) produced a broader remodeling of the transcriptomic landscape (Fig. 6d and Fig. S27a), featuring upregulation of genes such as NR4A3, HSPG2, and CPA4, together with downregulation of PTPRD and PRDM16, consistent with a shift toward a more pro-repair transcriptional state. Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes in the heatmaps further supported these systematic expression changes (Fig. 6e and f and Fig. S27b, S27c). Notably, PGM@PGR/SF/PM displayed the most distinct expression profile, with coordinated induction of gene programs related to endothelial functional activity and extracellular matrix remodeling, alongside reduced expression of stress- and inflammation-associated mediators.

GO enrichment analysis further supported the functional changes indicated by the transcriptomic data. Compared with the SF/PM group, DEGs in the PGR/SF/PM group were mainly enriched in biological processes related to responses to external and endogenous stimuli, cell differentiation and development, as well as chemotaxis and cell communication (Fig. 6g). These results suggested that PGR modification reshaped cellular responsiveness to the microenvironment and promoted cell behaviors associated with recruitment, phenotypic transition, and intercellular signaling. In addition, cross-group analysis showed that PGM@PGR/SF/PM preferentially enriched proliferation-associated programs, consistent with a transcriptional shift toward enhanced cell-cycle and growth-related gene regulation (Fig. 6h and Fig. S27d).

GSEA further demonstrated that, relative to SF/PM, both PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM exhibited coordinated activation of core biological programs, with gene sets linked to cell migration and proliferation significantly enriched (Fig. 6i–k and Fig. S27e–S27g). Together, these transcriptomic signatures aligned with our phenotypic observations and supported the conclusion that H2S augmented HUVEC motility and promoted branched, network-like organization on the PGR matrix. KEGG analysis of upregulated genes further indicated that the composite construct drove a concerted reprogramming of HUVEC transcriptional profiles toward endothelial functional responses compared with the control scaffold (Fig. S28). Consistently, Reactome chord plots revealed a coherent shift in differentially expressed gene-associated pathway programs, supporting a global remodeling of endothelial transcriptional states (Fig. S29).

Reactome pathway enrichment analysis further delineated the underlying signaling mechanisms (Fig. S30). In PGR/SF/PM, upregulated DEGs were enriched in NGF-stimulated transcription (FOSB, EGR3, EGR2) and Nuclear Receptor pathways (NR4A1/2/3), alongside chemokine receptor signaling (CXCR4, CCR7), indicating that PGR primarily activates immediate-early gene programs and chemotactic receptor signaling to enhance endothelial microenvironmental responsiveness. In PGM@PGR/SF/PM versus SF/PM, the additional enrichment of the GAB1 signalosome (EREG, AREG, HBEGF) and PI3K/AKT signaling revealed that H2S release engages a GAB1-PI3K/AKT axis, driven by upregulation of EGF-family ligands, to promote endothelial survival and migration [49,50]. Concurrent enrichment of YAP1/TAZ-stimulated gene expression (CCN2, RUNX2) and G1/S transition regulators (E2F1, CDC25A, CDC6) further indicated that H2S coordinately activates mechanosensitive transcription and cell cycle entry. Comparison of PGM@PGR/SF/PM with PGR/SF/PM was used to estimate the H2S-associated transcriptional contribution, revealing comprehensive activation of cell cycle progression, spanning G1/S entry (CCNE2, E2F1, MCM2-7), mitotic execution (PLK1, CDK1, AURKB), and homology-directed repair (BRCA1, RAD51), and providing a direct molecular basis for the H2S-driven proliferative phenotype.

RNA-seq showed that PGM@PGR/SF/PM produced the largest transcriptional changes in HUVECs among the three groups. The altered genes were mainly related to cell migration, proliferation, and matrix remodeling, whereas stress- and inflammation-related signals were reduced. Mechanistically, Reactome analysis established that H2S promotes endothelialization through a GAB1-PI3K/AKT signaling axis and YAP/TAZ mechanosensing, coupled with a coordinated cell cycle progression program spanning G1/S entry and mitotic execution. These findings agreed with the pro-repair phenotype observed in the functional assays.

Integrating the transcriptomic findings from both macrophages and HUVECs reveals a coherent intercellular signaling framework underlying scaffold-mediated tissue repair. H2S-driven upregulation of Hbegf in macrophages implicates macrophage-derived HBEGF as a paracrine mediator that engages the EGFR-GAB1-PI3K/AKT axis in endothelial cells, as supported by the concordant enrichment of the GAB1 signalosome in HUVEC Reactome analysis. Concurrently, attenuation of cytokine and chemokine signaling in macrophages aligns with reduced inflammatory gene expression in HUVECs, indicating that immune resolution creates a permissive microenvironment for endothelial functional recovery. Together, these findings support a model in which scaffold-orchestrated macrophage reprogramming drives coordinated endothelialization through a paracrine immune-to-endothelial repair axis.

Tissue compatibility of scaffold materials

To preliminarily assess the in vivo tissue compatibility of the SF/PM, PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds, a 4-week subcutaneous implantation study was conducted in Sprague-Dawley rats. Fig. 7a schematically illustrates the implantation procedure of the scaffold materials. Histological examination by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed good local tolerance in all groups, with no evidence of necrosis or abscess formation (Fig. 7b). Notably, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group exhibited the mildest inflammatory response. To further characterize the host immune response, macrophage polarization was assessed. M1-polarized macrophages were identified by co-staining for CD86 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), whereas M2-polarized macrophages were detected using CD206 and arginase-1 (Arg-1) as markers (Fig. 7c and d). Compared with the SF/PM group, the PGR/SF/PM scaffold elicited a lower proportion of M1 macrophages and a higher proportion of M2 macrophages in the surrounding tissue (Fig. 7f and g). This shift suggests that the PGR hydrogel coating may reduce inflammatory cell infiltration through its anti-adhesive properties, thereby enhancing material biocompatibility. Furthermore, the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, benefiting from the regulatory effects of H2S released via PTC decomposition, showed the lowest level of M1 macrophages and the highest level of M2 macrophages among the three groups. This can be attributed to the strong anti-inflammatory properties of H2S, which scavenges ROS, further reduces macrophage adhesion, and promotes their polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotype, thereby accelerating the restoration of immune homeostasis. Since anti-calcification performance is one of the critical evaluation metrics for valve-related materials [6], Alizarin Red staining was used to assess mineral deposition. Calcific deposits were readily observed in the SF/PM group, whereas markedly reduced calcification was detected in the PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM groups (Fig. 7e). To corroborate these findings, calcium content was quantitatively analyzed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) (Fig. 7h). The measured calcium contents were 2.2 ± 0.4 μg mg−1 for SF/PM, 1.7 ± 0.3 μg mg−1 for PGR/SF/PM, and 1.3 ± 0.1 μg mg−1 for PGM@PGR/SF/PM. These results suggest the anti-calcification potential of the functionalized scaffold groups, which may be associated with the PGR-modified biointerface and H2S-mediated regulation of the local inflammatory microenvironment. In total, PGM@PGR/SF/PM showed better tissue compatibility than the other groups, with reduced inflammatory markers, increased M2-associated markers, and favorable in vivo anti-calcification performance.

Performance evaluation of the scaffold under hemodynamic conditions

Hemocompatibility is a critical determinant of valve safety and function. This consideration is especially important because the persistently high-shear hemodynamic environment can potentiate interactions among platelets, coagulation factors and complement, rapidly triggering thromboinflammation and valvular dysfunction [51]. Beyond effectively reducing protein adsorption and platelet adhesion, a blood-contacting surface must also support rapid endothelial regeneration to establish endogenous antithrombotic control mechanisms [52,53]. Therefore, we conducted hemolysis assays, extracorporeal arteriovenous shunt experiments, and in vivo arterial implantation to evaluate blood-material interactions at multiple levels. In this context, the carotid artery implantation model was used as a preliminary in vivo arterial blood-flow model to assess blood-contacting interface performance, including thrombosis, inflammatory response, endothelialization, and tissue integration under flowing blood conditions.

Hemolysis assay results (Fig. S31) showed very low hemolysis ratios for the SF/PM, PGR/SF/PM, and PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds (0.93 ± 0.16%, 0.39 ± 0.14% and 0.35 ± 0.09%, respectively), in stark contrast to the positive control (deionized water, 100% hemolysis). All measured values were well below the 5% threshold specified in international (ISO 10993-4) and national (GB/T 16886.4) standards, indicating no significant damage to red blood cells. Further, an in vitro thrombus formation assay was performed to evaluate the antithrombotic properties of the scaffolds (Fig. S32a and S32b). The PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM groups exhibited the lowest thrombus burden, which was markedly lower than that of the clinically used GV group. These results suggest that the hydrogel coating substantially reduced thrombus formation on the scaffold surface.

An ex vivo arteriovenous shunt model in New Zealand White rabbits was then employed to investigate the antithrombogenic properties of the scaffolds under physiologically relevant blood flow conditions (Fig. 8a). The samples were attached to the inner wall of a tube connecting the carotid artery and jugular vein. After 2 h of blood circulation, macroscopic inspection (Fig. 8b) revealed clot formation and luminal occlusion in the SF/PM group. In contrast, the PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM groups maintained unobstructed blood flow with significantly reduced thrombus deposition, indicating their superior antithrombogenic properties. These hemodynamic results were further corroborated by SEM imaging (Fig. 8c). The surface of the SF/PM scaffold was covered by a dense mesh of fibrin and red blood cells, consistent with the macroscopic observations, whereas the PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM surfaces remained substantially cleaner. These results suggest that introduction of the PGR hydrogel coating substantially reduces thrombotic risk, likely attributable to the broad-spectrum antifouling properties of PEGDA, which suppress initial nonspecific protein adsorption [54,55]. In addition to the antifouling protection provided by the PGR coating, the controlled release of H2S from PGM@PGR/SF/PM imparts potent antioxidant and vasoprotective effects, which further reduce the risk of thrombosis and enhance overall hemocompatibility. Scaffold samples were incubated in platelet-rich plasma (PRP) on a shaker at 37 °C. SEM observations directly visualized the protective effect of the hydrogel coating, showing a substantial reduction in platelet adhesion and activation on the scaffold surface (Fig. 8d). Quantitative platelet adhesion was then evaluated using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (C0016, Beyotime) by measuring the LDH content released from platelets retained on the sample surfaces. As shown in Fig. S33, the PGR coating significantly reduced the number of residual platelets on the scaffold, indicating improved anti-platelet adhesion/antithrombogenic performance. Moreover, sustained H2S release further decreased the LDH signal, suggesting a combined contribution to inhibiting platelet adhesion on the modified scaffolds.

The performance of the scaffolds was further evaluated in a more physiologically relevant in vivo rabbit carotid artery implantation model to assess scaffold behavior in a dynamic arterial blood-flow environment (Fig. 8e). After 4 weeks of implantation, all scaffolds maintained structural integrity (Fig. 8f) and Doppler ultrasound imaging revealed continuous intraluminal blood flow signals in the implanted vessels across all three groups. H&E staining revealed a layer of cellular infiltration on the luminal surface of all implants (Fig. S34a). Compared with SF/PM, the PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM groups exhibited markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. Immunofluorescence staining for macrophage polarization markers (CD86 and TNF-α for M1; CD206 and IL-10 for M2) was performed to visually assess the post-implantation inflammatory response (Fig. 8g and h). The SF/PM group contained the highest number of M1 macrophages, whereas the PGR/SF/PM and PGM@PGR/SF/PM groups were dominated by M2 macrophages, which contribute to an anti-inflammatory and pro-healing response (Fig. 8j and Fig. S34b). This effect was most pronounced in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, reaffirming the beneficial regulatory role of H2S in guiding macrophage polarization toward a pro-repair M2-like phenotype. Rapid endothelialization is crucial for the success of valve scaffold implantation and functional repair. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated endothelial coverage of the scaffold surface and infiltration of vimentin-positive cells within the scaffold, suggesting ongoing cellular remodeling (Fig. 8i). The PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold demonstrated the most robust endothelialization and interstitial cell infiltration (Fig. 8k). This effect is likely attributable to the combined contribution of the RGDAA/GelMA-derived cell-adhesive motifs in the PGR coating and the regulatory functions of H2S, which together promote integrin-mediated attachment and subsequent cellularization [56,57]. At 3 months after carotid artery implantation, Von Kossa staining was performed to further evaluate the in vivo anti-calcification performance of the scaffolds (Fig. S35). Compared with the SF/PM group, calcification was barely detectable in the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, further supporting the anti-calcification potential of the PGM@PGR hydrogel coated scaffold. In addition, H&E staining of the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney revealed no obvious histopathological abnormalities in any group, suggesting favorable preliminary systemic biocompatibility of the implanted scaffolds after 3 months (Fig. S36). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold possesses favorable hemocompatibility and supports endothelialization in the models, highlighting its promise for valve-oriented cardiovascular applications.

Conclusions

Rather than serving solely as passive structural substitutes, tissue-engineered heart valve scaffolds may benefit from the incorporation of bioactive interfacial cues. By integrating a digitally programmable scaffold with a layered H2S-releasing biointerface, our study suggests that the early post-implantation response of valve scaffolds can be guided through the coordinated regulation of hemocompatibility, inflammation, and endothelialization. The multilayer PGM@PGR/SF/PM construct provides tensile properties comparable to those reported for valves while establishing a pro-regenerative immune-endothelial niche that suppresses thromboinflammatory responses, exhibits anti-calcification potential, and supports endothelialization. Importantly, these findings suggest that successful regenerative valve design should not be viewed solely as a mechanical or architectural challenge, but as a problem of interface programming across structural, immune, and vascular dimensions. As such, this study provides a promising scaffold and a generalizable design principle for future regenerative heart valve engineering.

Experimental section

Materials

Polycaprolactone triol (PCLT, ∼830), methacrylic anhydride (MAAh), 4-(hydroxymethyl) phenylboronic acid pinacol ester, 4-fluorophenyl isothiocyanate, ethanol, glycerol, sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF), sodium chloride (NaCl), potassium carbonate (K2CO3), sodium hydride (NaH), anhydrous ethyl acetate (EA), anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), sodium borate (Na2B4O7), calcium chloride (CaCl2), diphenyl (2, 4, 6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO), 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (1173), formic acid (FA) and dopamine hydrochloride (DA) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Triethylamine (TEA) and dichloromethane (DCM) were procured from J&K Scientific. n-hexane was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Gelatin (Type A, gel strength ∼300 g Bloom) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 8-arm polyethylene glycol (8-arm PEG, Mw = 20000) was purchased from Adamas Life. Drop-surf and droplet generation oil were obtained from Fluidiclab. Bombyx mori silkworm cocoons were acquired from a local market. RGDAA was supplied by Lingeltech. Lithium phenyl (2, 4, 6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphinate (LAP) was procured from EFL-Tech (Suzhou) Co., Ltd. Ultrapure water (resistivity ≥18.2 MΩ cm at 25 °C) was obtained using a Milli-Q purification system.

Preparation of photocurable PM resins

PCLT was dissolved in anhydrous DCM to prepare a 0.1 g mL−1 solution. TEA (3 equivalents) and MAAh (3 equivalents) were slowly added sequentially with an ice bath and nitrogen atmosphere. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 48 h. The mixture was then washed 3 times each with 1 M hydrochloric acid and deionized water, followed by precipitation in cold methanol. The precipitation-dissolution cycle (redissolving in DCM and precipitating in cold methanol) was repeated 4 times. The solvent was removed via vacuum filtration and rotary evaporation. The product was characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy (NMR, AV400, Bruker, Switzerland). The photocurable PM resin was formulated by homogenizing 2 wt% photoinitiator sourced from a 1:1 mixture of TPO and 1173 into PM. 0.05-0.08 wt% of β-carotene was subsequently incorporated as a photoabsorber to improve printing resolution.

3D printing of PM scaffolds

Scaffold models were designed in SolidWorks software and sliced using CHITUBOX. Photocurable PM resin was loaded into the vat of a digital light processing (DLP) printer (Bio-Architect®PR, Regenovo, China) for 3D printing. Printing parameters were set as follows: stage temperature 50 °C, layer thickness 50 μm, Z-axis lift distance 2 mm, lift speed 0.5 mm s−1, retract speed 0.25 mm s−1, 405 nm ultraviolet light (UV) intensity 100 mW cm−2, and exposure time 0.8 s per layer. For burn-in layers (2-4 initial layers), UV intensity was increased to 120 mW cm−2 with 1.6 s exposure. Post-printing, constructs were carefully extracted, ultrasonicated in absolute ethanol (400 W, 4 × 10 s) to remove residual resin, and post-cured with UV light.

Synthesis of PTC

4-(hydroxymethyl) phenylboronic acid pinacol ester and 4-fluorophenyl isothiocyanate at 5 mmol each were blended in anhydrous THF (10 mL), followed by the addition of 6.25 mmol NaH. The reaction was stirred until completion at room temperature, as confirmed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). The resulting mixture was washed with brine (2× volume), and the organic phase was extracted with an equal volume of EA. The combined organic layers were dried over MgSO4, and the solvent was removed by rotary evaporation. The crude product was redissolved in ethyl acetate and purified by silica gel column chromatography using a binary solvent system of n-hexane and ethyl acetate as the eluent. The obtained PTC was stored at 4 °C until use.

Synthesis of 8-arm PEGDA

Dry polymer 8-arm PEG was dissolved in anhydrous DCM to a concentration of 0.1 g mL−1. Under a nitrogen atmosphere, 4 equivalents of TEA followed by acryloyl chloride (4 equivalents) were added sequentially via dropwise addition. The reaction proceeded for 24 h with continuous light exclusion. After filtration, the solution was washed with an equal volume of 2 M aqueous potassium carbonate (K2CO3). The organic phase was isolated, and the product was precipitated by addition to chilled diethyl ether. The precipitate was redissolved in DCM, and reprecipitated in chilled diethyl ether. This dissolution-precipitation cycle was repeated three times. The final precipitate was vacuum-dried to yield the purified product.

Fabrication of multilayer integrated PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold

The PM porous scaffold was fabricated by digital light processing (DLP) printing. Degummed silk fibroin (SF) was dissolved in a 0.5 wt% calcium chloride/formic acid solution at 60 °C for 1 h to obtain a 10% (w/v) SF solution. The printed PM scaffold was immersed in the SF solution, and solvent evaporation was subsequently performed to deposit a conformal SF coating throughout the porous architecture, yielding the SF/PM scaffold. The SF/PM scaffold was incubated in a DA and DMA mixed solution (pH 8) for 1 h to form a coupling interfacial layer. The construct was then treated with a PGR precursor solution and exposed to 405 nm light to achieve complete photocrosslinking. The PGR precursor solution consisted of 5% (w/v) 8-arm PEGDA, 2.5% (w/v) GelMA, 0.25% (w/v) RGDAA and 0.08% (w/v) LAP. After photocuring, the constructs were rinsed with PBS to remove unreacted components, resulting in the multilayer integrated PGR/SF/PM scaffold. Further, PTC-GelMA microgels (PGM) were additionally incorporated into the PGR precursor solution, and the PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffold was fabricated following the same procedure.

Characterization of PTC decomposition and release

To assess the ROS-responsive decomposition behavior of the H2S donor PTC, a time-resolved 1H NMR analysis was performed. Briefly, PTC (1 mmol) was added to a solution containing H2O2 (5 mmol) in 10 mL of CD3CN/D2O (1:1, v/v). The reaction progress was monitored by acquiring 1H NMR spectra at predefined time intervals. The decomposition kinetics of PTC were quantified by monitoring the time-dependent decrease in the intensity of its characteristic proton resonances, normalized to an internal standard.

The in vitro H2S release kinetics from PGM were indirectly evaluated by monitoring p-HBA generation. Upon ROS-triggered cleavage, PTC generates COS together with an equimolar amount of p-HBA, and COS can be further converted to H2S in the presence of carbonic anhydrase. Therefore, the formation kinetics of p-HBA were used as an indirect indicator of H2S release from PGM. Briefly, 2.00 mL of PGM solution at a mass concentration of 1.0 mg mL−1 was sealed in a dialysis bag and immersed in 40.00 mL PBS release medium supplemented with 0.5% (v/v) Tween 80. The release study was conducted at 37 °C under constant shaking. Two release conditions were investigated: PBS and PBS containing 50 μM H2O2. The release medium was refreshed daily. At predetermined time points, aliquots of the release medium were collected, and the concentration of p-HBA was quantified by HPLC with UV detection at 275 nm. The cumulative p-HBA release profile was measured according to a pre-established calibration curve and used to indirectly characterize the H2S release kinetics of PGM.

Mechanical and rheological properties

The mechanical properties of the materials were evaluated on a computer-controlled CMT-4104 universal testing machine (Shenzhen Xinsansi Material Testing Co., Ltd., China). The samples were clamped in place and subjected to tensile testing at a constant displacement speed of 10 mm min−1 until rupture. Rheological behavior of the PM resin was analyzed using an MCR102 rheometer (Anton Paar GmbH, Austria). The temperature-dependent viscosity behavior of the PM resin was evaluated using time-sweep rheological tests at various temperatures. Samples were equilibrated at 20, 30, 40, 50, or 60 °C, followed by time sweeps at a shear rate of 50 s−1. The photocrosslinking kinetics were investigated by monitoring the evolution of the viscoelastic moduli (G′ and G″) of the samples under light exposure at 50 °C.

Visualization of H2O2-triggered H2S release from PTC

The murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until they reached 80% confluence. The culture medium was then removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated for 20 min in serum-free DMEM containing 100 μM PTC and 5 μM 7-azido-4-methylcoumarin (AzMC, an H2S-sensitive fluorescent probe; A191, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were then washed with PBS to remove excess reagents. To induce oxidative stress, the cells were further treated with H2O2 at 0, 25, or 50 μM in serum-free DMEM for 30 min. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and immediately imaged using an IX71 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) under 365 nm excitation.

Macrophage polarization assay

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to 80% confluence. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 50 μM H2O2 for 48 h. Cells cultured without scaffolds served as the Control group. In the scaffold-treated groups, cells were co-cultured with SF/PM, PGR/SF/PM, or PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds under the same conditions. For the PGM@PGR/SF/PM group, the PTC-equivalent concentration was 100 μM. Following treatment, the cells were processed for immunofluorescence as follows: fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 8 min, and blocked with 1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies, namely rabbit anti-CD206 antibody (24595, CST) and mouse anti-CD86 antibody (ab31630, Abcam) for macrophage polarization analysis. The cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (A11034, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (A11005, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI solution for 10 min. Between each step, the cells were gently washed with PBS to remove residual reagents. Fluorescence images were acquired using an FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan). In addition, the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-10) were determined using corresponding ELISA kits (RA20035 and RA2009, respectively; Bio-Swamp).

Wound scratch assay

HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured under standard conditions until a confluent monolayer was formed. A straight scratch was made in the monolayer using a sterile 10 μL pipette tip, followed by gentle washing with PBS to remove detached cells. After scratching, the cells were cultured in serum-free medium containing 50 μM H2O2. Cells cultured without scaffolds served as the Control group. In the scaffold-treated groups, cells were co-cultured with SF/PM, PGR/SF/PM, or PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds under the same conditions. At 0, 24, and 48 h after scratching, the cells were fixed and stained with TRITC Phalloidin (40734ES75, Yeasen) and DAPI (40728ES03, Yeasen). Images were then acquired using an FV3000 confocal microscope. Wound closure was quantified with ImageJ software, and the wound closure rate was calculated as follows: Woundclosurerate(%)=(A0‐At)/A0×100%, where A0 represents the initial wound area and At denotes the wound area at each time point.

Transwell assay

HUVECs (2 × 105 cells) suspended in 200 μL of medium containing 1% FBS (South America) were seeded into the upper chambers of transwell inserts. The inserts were placed in a 24-well plate, and the lower chambers were filled with 700 μL of complete medium containing 50 μM H2O2, with or without the indicated scaffolds. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the inner surface of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab. The inserts were washed with PBS, and the migrated cells on the outer surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (G1063, Solarbio), washed with PBS, and imaged under an optical microscope.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

RAW264.7 macrophages and HUVECs were independently cultured with SF/PM, PGR/SF/PM, or PGM@PGR/SF/PM scaffolds in medium containing 50 μM H2O2. Total RNA from each cell type was extracted using TRIzol reagent (9108, Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA purity was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer based on the OD260/280 and OD260/230 ratios, while RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation. Sequencing libraries were subsequently prepared from qualified RNA samples, quantified using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, diluted to 1 ng μL−1, and further evaluated for insert size using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries meeting the quality criteria were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. After quality filtering and adapter trimming, clean reads were mapped to the corresponding reference genomes for downstream differential expression analysis. The transcriptomic data were then visualized using standard bioinformatic methods.

RT-qPCR assay

After the respective treatments, mRNA levels of macrophage and endothelial-related genes were analyzed by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from cells using an RNA extraction kit. cDNA was synthesized from the RNA using the 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (RR047A, TaKaRa). RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (QPK-201, Toyobo) on a CFX96 Real-Time system (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1 (Supporting Materials).

Subcutaneous implantation

Male Sprague-Dawley rats were used for subcutaneous implantation studies. Briefly, rats were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (induced with a high concentration and maintained with a low concentration). An appropriate incision was made on the dorsal skin using a scalpel, and the skin was carefully separated with the underlying muscle to create bilateral subcutaneous pockets. The test materials were gently implanted into the pockets on both sides of the dorsal region. Four weeks after implantation, the animals were euthanized, and the tissue surrounding the implants was harvested for histological analysis. Tissue sections were subjected to the following staining procedures: (a) H&E; (b) immunofluorescence for pro-inflammatory (CD86, iNOS) and anti-inflammatory (CD206, Arg-1) markers; and (c) Alizarin Red. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Arteriovenous shunt assay

New Zealand White rabbits were anesthetized and administered heparin intravenously for systemic anticoagulation. Following a midline cervical incision, the carotid artery and jugular vein were surgically exposed and isolated. An external circuit containing the test sample was connected between the vessels: one end of the tubing was inserted into the artery and the other into the vein. This configuration established an extracorporeal shunt, allowing blood to flow from the artery through the circuit (passing over the sample surface) and return to the venous circulation. After 2 h of circulation, the sample was carefully retrieved, and its blood-contacting surface was examined macroscopically and by SEM (JSM-IT800, JEOL Company) observation.

Carotid artery scaffold implantation

Anesthetized New Zealand White rabbits were administered heparin for anticoagulation. The carotid artery was surgically exposed, and a segment was isolated by applying vascular clamps proximally and distally. The conduit sample was anastomosed end-to-end to the isolated arterial segment using sutures. After completing the anastomosis, the clamps were released to restore blood flow. The surgical incision was closed in layers. Four weeks after surgery, the explanted tissue-conduit complexes were collected for histological analysis by H&E staining and immunofluorescence. Measurements and statistical quantification were conducted using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Origin 2024 software was used for statistical analysis. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three replicates per group. Statistical analyses were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001); ns, not significant.
