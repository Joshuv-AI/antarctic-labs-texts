CAM-Net: a context-aware network for identifying reliable microbial relations via optimal consortium
Authors: Junhui Zhang, Wenfei Xu, Jieqi Xing, Yangyang Sun, Shi Huang, Xiaoquan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobes exist within complex community contexts, particularly for key functional species whose stable colonization critically depends on specific ecological partners. However, conventional microbial correlation analyses predominantly rely on isolated pairwise metrics (e.g. Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave), which ignore community-level dependencies. This limitation leads to spurious associations in large-scale datasets and obscures the true structure of microbial interactions. Here, we introduce CAM-Net, a context-aware framework that identifies a target microbe's optimal consortium, a fully connected network subset that accurately predicts its abundance. By constructing networks via multi-hop information propagation, CAM-Net effectively filters false positives from indirect associations and captures complex, context-dependent patterns that are inaccessible to traditional pairwise approaches. We evaluated CAM-Net on over 25 000 human gut microbiome samples using Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus acidophilus as representatives of indigenous and transient colonizers. CAM-Net identified a coherent and reproducible consortium for A. muciniphila, but only weak association structures for L. acidophilus, consistent with their ecological behaviors. In contrast, pairwise methods produced spurious associations for both species. Notably, despite substantial geographic heterogeneity, Alistipes shahii consistently emerged as a conserved core member of the A. muciniphila consortium, demonstrating the advantage of context-aware modeling. The source code is available at https://github.com/qdu-bioinfo/CAM-Net.Keywords: microbiome, context-aware network, optimal consortium

Introduction

The human gut microbiome is a complex ecosystem composed of diverse microorganisms that play important roles in host metabolism, immune regulation, and disease development [1]. Within this ecosystem, microbial species do not exist in isolation but coexist within a shared community environment, forming complex association patterns. Understanding these associations is essential for revealing the ecological organization and stability of microbial communities [2]. The increasing availability of large cohort datasets provides new opportunities to investigate microbial associations and construct correlation-based association networks that describe potential ecological relationships [3].

Accurately inferring microbial correlations remains challenging, as mainstream correlation-based methods, including Spearman, SparCC [4], and FlashWeave [5], infer pairwise associations as independent units, leaving them sensitive to compositional effects and zero inflation [6, 7]. Alternative dynamical or multivariate approaches can model higher-order relationships but require logistically challenging and resource-intensive perturbation experiments [8, 9], while graph-based frameworks generally focus on heterogeneous data integration [10–13]. Consequently, these existing paradigms fail to capture the complex, multi-hop relationships dependent on the broader community context, yielding incomplete representations of microbial ecosystems. CAM-Net addresses this limitation by introducing a target-centric, context-aware framework. By resolving multi-hop, non-pairwise relationships within large-scale microbiome data, CAM-Net effectively mitigates spurious associations to identify robust, optimal microbial consortia.

The construction of an optimal consortium is both biologically valid and practically necessary for understanding microbial dynamics. The correctness of identifying these highly connected, fully integrated structures is grounded in established ecological and genomic principles. In the context of gene co-expression networks, all fully connected genes indicate a strong co-expression relationship between them [14]. Moreover, densely connected clusters of microbes have been shown to reveal complex indirect interactions, such as symbiont-mediated competition or mutualism, which reshape community functioning. Crucially, species within these highly connected hubs tend to exhibit enhanced evolutionary resilience and adaptive capacity, leveraging symbiont-conferred phenotypic plasticity and interaction rewiring to respond rapidly to environmental stressors [15]. Furthermore, such structures often represent keystone microbes, which are defined as highly connected groups that exert a significant influence on microbiome structure and functioning regardless of their spatial or temporal abundance [16]. These microbes play a unique and critical role; their presence maintains the community’s organizational logic, and their removal would lead to drastic changes in the microbiome’s overall structure and stability.

The necessity of identifying an optimal consortium is particularly evident in the study of specific functional microbes, such as Akkermansia muciniphila [17]. While A. muciniphila is widely recognized for its host-protective roles, its successful colonization and functional stability are highly dependent on the overall composition of the gut microbiome and the presence of supportive ecological partners. Without its optimal consortium, comprising specific mucin-degraders or syntrophic partners, the colonization of A. muciniphila may fail to reach stable levels or provide its characteristic metabolic benefits, such as short-chain fatty acid production [18]. Therefore, identifying these good neighbors is essential not only for theoretical ecological modeling but also for practical applications in probiotic engineering and clinical diagnostics.

Methods

Overall algorithm design

Here, we propose CAM-Net, a context-aware network framework that departs fundamentally from pairwise association paradigms. By dynamically constructing a local ecological environment around a target microbe and identifying its optimal consortium through multi-hop relational propagation, CAM-Net captures higher-order interaction structures while effectively reducing spurious associations (Fig. 1).

The overall framework of CAM-Net. (A) Dynamic network construction via context-aware traversal. Starting from target microbe T, the algorithm builds the network by traversing all microbial nodes. In each phase, edges are established by considering an expanding set of context microbes (e.g. {T} in Phase 1, {T, A} in Phase 2, and {T, A, B} in Phase 3). This traversal across all nodes captures multi-hop associations to identify the optimal consortium in Phase n. (B) Identification of the optimal consortium. Within the pre-built network, the consortium is expanded through a step-by-step traversal process. Starting from an initial group (Step 1), new members are identified by calculating the intersection of the neighbor sets of all current members (Steps 2–4). This ensures the final optimal consortium is a fully connected dense subgraph, representing the most stable ecological neighborhood for target microbe T.

CAM-Net begins with a dynamic network construction procedure based on multi-hop traversal. Starting from a target species (e.g. microbe T in Fig. 1A), the algorithm iteratively expands from first-order neighbors to higher-order connections, progressively incorporating the surrounding community context at species level. An edge is only established if the correlation exceeds a 99th percentile threshold derived from an empirical distribution of 10 000 times random pairwise resampling (refer to Statistical thresholding for biological association specificity for details about threshold setting). This systematic traversal ensures that the resulting network captures refined ecological associations while eliminating spurious correlations.

Subsequently, the optimal consortium is identified within this pre-built network through a recursive expansion strategy (Fig. 1B). This process is initialized with a minimal seed set comprising the target microbe and a candidate neighbor. The consortium is then iteratively expanded by incorporating additional species that satisfy a strict common-neighbor constraint. Specifically, a candidate node is admitted only if it lies in the intersection of the neighbor sets of all current members and exhibits consistently strong associations (above the 99% threshold; refer to Statistical thresholding for biological association specificity for details about threshold setting) with the entire group. This procedure continues until a fully connected dense subgraph is obtained and no further nodes can be added, representing the most stable and reliable ecological neighborhood where all members exhibit coordinated functional activity within the local community context.

Results

Experimental design

To evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the identified optimal consortium, we established a rigorous cross-validation framework (Fig. 2) using over 25 000 human gut microbiomes. This framework comprises samples from geographically diverse cohorts spanning Asia, Europe, Australia, and North America (Table 1), derived from both metagenomic Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) and 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) sequencing. To mitigate inherent data heterogeneity across these diverse platforms and cohorts, all microbial abundance data were log-transformed prior to analysis.

Cross-validation framework. The dataset is partitioned repeatedly into internal (80%) and external (20%) datasets. CAM-Net is applied to the internal dataset to identify the optimal consortium; this identified consortium is then applied to the external dataset to calculate R values (predicted versus true abundance) through repeated resampling. Performance stability is assessed across three scenarios: Real consortium (consistent performance; N.S., P > .05), False positives (overfitting; sharp external performance drop; ***, P < .001), and False negatives (underfitting; internal R values significantly lower than external; ***, P < .001).

Specifically, to handle zero values and ensure numerical stability, a pseudo-count equivalent to half of the minimum non-zero abundance was added to each entry, following previous microbiome studies [20, 21]. This approach ensures that all values remain positive for log-transformation while keeping the imputed values smaller than any observed non-zero abundance, thereby minimizing distortion of the original abundance distribution, consistent with the general principles of compositional data analysis [22].

The dataset for each cohort was randomly partitioned into an internal subset (80%) and an external subset (20%). To ensure an unbiased evaluation and prevent any potential data leakage, the external subset was kept strictly isolated and was never mixed into the network construction or model training process in advance. We first ran CAM-Net on the internal dataset to identify the optimal consortium specific to a target microbe (microbe T, Fig. 1, Fig. 2). This consortium was then independently applied to both internal and external subsets to predict the relative abundance of the target microbe, denoted as Internal Validation (I. V.) and External Validation (E. V.), respectively (Fig. 2). Performance was quantified by the Pearson correlation coefficient (R values) between the predicted abundance (Predicted Abd.) and the true abundance (True Abd.) of the target microbe using a standard linear regression model without any regularization or secondary feature selection penalty. To ensure statistical rigor and eliminate sampling bias, the entire procedure was repeated five times through resampling, and the statistical significance of R values between I.V. and E.V. is measured using a two-tailed, unpaired Welch’s t-test [23] (Fig. 2).

For fair comparison with alternative approaches, we benchmarked CAM-Net against Spearman correlation, SparCC, and FlashWeave while controlling for consortium size across methods. Specifically, once CAM-Net identifies an optimal consortium of size N, we select the top N microbes with the highest absolute correlation values from Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave to form their respective comparative consortia. This normalization ensures that performance differences reflect methodological effectiveness rather than disparities in network scale or feature number. In the comparative analysis, we strictly adhered to the original false-positive exclusion protocol for the SparCC and FlashWeave methods. This setup highlights CAM-Net’s superior efficacy in identifying context-aware microbial relationships and in filtering spurious associations through its explicit modeling of community context.

The ecological validity of the identified consortium was assessed across three scenarios: Real consortium, False positives, and False negatives. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the Real consortium maintains consistent and high predictive performance across both datasets (N.S. indicates P > .05), proving that the captured associations are robust and reproducible. In contrast, the False positive scenario demonstrates clear overfitting, where the R value is significantly higher in the internal dataset but drops sharply in the external validation or their P values ​​are lower than the level of the true consortium. Similarly, the False negatives reflect underfitting or incomplete association capture, resulting in a lower R value for internal validation compared to external validation. This experimental design ensures that the optimal consortium identified by CAM-Net is a stable functional unit.

Robust optimal consortium discovery for the indigenous microbe A. muciniphila

CAM-Net delineated a sharply defined optimal consortium for A. muciniphila, characterized by a densely connected subgraph that was topologically segregated from the surrounding microbiome (Fig. 3A). Such structural isolation indicated a self-consistent ecological module and provided a basis for its stable predictive performance across both internal and external datasets (Fig. S1).

By contrast, pairwise correlation methods (e.g. Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave) yielded star-like, hub-centered structures that lacked internal connectivity. This topology reflected their inability to capture higher-order dependencies, which rendered them prone to indirect and spurious associations (Fig. S2, Fig. S3, and Fig. S4).

This distinction was further supported by cross-cohort validation (Fig. 3B), where CAM-Net exhibited consistent performance across geographically diverse populations, with no significant difference between internal and external validation (N.S., P > .05). These results indicated that the identified optimal consortium represented a conserved and robust ecological module across populations.

Notably, R values of Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave in Asia, Europe, and North America exhibited highly significant difference (P < .01 or P < .05). This statistical inconsistency highlighted the pervasive overfitting inherent in traditional pairwise methods when handling complex microbial data. By incorporating context-awareness and multi-hop traversal, CAM-Net successfully filtered out these unstable, spurious associations, ensuring the identified consortium was both authentic and globally generalizable.

Weak ecological integration of the exogenous microbe Lactobacillus acidophilus

The physical characteristics of the network topology directly mapped the ecological roles of microbes within the gut. The high-density, fully connected clique displayed by A. muciniphila represented its robust symbiotic nature as a core functional species (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the network for L. acidophilus appeared chaotic and sparse, which quantitatively validated its status as an exogenous or transient colonizer from a topological perspective, reflecting its unstable colonization state in the human intestine (Fig. 4A).

CAM-Net demonstrated exceptional specificity in identifying biological signals, effectively distinguishing between inflated correlations and true stability. Traditional methods, such as Spearman, often yielded inflated correlation values within specific cohorts that plummeted during external cross-regional validation, indicating significant false positives. Although CAM-Net captured lower R values when processing exogenous microbes (e.g. L. acidophilus), its high consistency between internal and external validation (Fig. 4B; N.S.) proved its ability to extract weak yet authentic global signals.

Furthermore, multi-country validation demonstrated that the robustness of CAM-Net was significantly superior to existing methods. Specifically, while SparCC avoided false-positive scenarios for certain datasets (e.g. Asia and North America cohorts), CAM-Net achieved stable performance across all cohorts without false-positive or false-negative cases. While Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave were highly susceptible to interference from cohort-specific noise in certain regions, the exceptional level of non-significant difference sustained by CAM-Net represented a clear manifestation of the algorithm’s cross-population generalizability (Fig. 4B).

Geographic and cohort heterogeneity shapes microbial consortium composition

The variations in the optimal consortia identified across different national cohorts were the result of a complex interplay between various heterogeneous factors, including dietary habits, environmental conditions, and host genetics [24]. Consequently, the distinct composition of these consortia suggested that localized, region-specific designs were essential for effective microbiome-based interventions (Fig. 5A). Notably, despite this regional divergence, Alistipes shahii consistently emerged as a universal core member across all geographic locations, indicating a globally conserved symbiotic relationship with A. muciniphila that transcends geographic barriers (Fig. 5B).

This robust association was underpinned by significant ecological and metabolic synergies. According to previous studies [25], A. shahii was highly enriched in healthy individuals and maintained a significant microbial network association with A. muciniphila. This co-occurrence was strongly correlated with over 50% of core functional pathways, suggesting a protective role in host health, particularly in oxalate metabolism and the prevention of kidney stone formation. These findings highlighted the potential of the AKK-Alistipes network as a critical biomarker for intestinal homeostasis.

This cooperative relationship was further elucidated by the mechanism of A. muciniphila-mediated degradation of host mucin, a process in which A. muciniphila cleaved mucin sugar chains to release tryptophan from the protein backbone [26]. As a tryptophan utilizer, Alistipes took up this released resource to produce indole and its derivatives, which subsequently activated the host aryl hydrocarbon receptor to modulate Th17 (T helper 17 cell) immune responses. Together, these findings highlighted a friendly and coordinated alliance where A. muciniphila provided essential metabolic precursors that Alistipes utilized to maintain host physiological homeostasis and immune regulation. Inter-sample heterogeneity constrains microbial correlation inference

Building on the observed geographic and cohort heterogeneity, our analysis revealed that this inherent variability imposed a severe constraint on the accuracy of microbial correlation inference. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the mean R values difference between internal and external validation was highly susceptible to inter-sample heterogeneity (smaller differences indicate more stable correlations).

By original relative abundance, correlation estimates for both A. muciniphila and L. acidophilus exhibited extreme volatility with substantial cross-cohort discrepancies (Fig. 6), highlighting how inter-sample heterogeneity fundamentally constrained microbial correlation inference. Conversely, the implementation of log-transformation effectively mitigated this variability, collapsing the internal and external validation gap (mean R values difference) to near-zero across all cohorts (Fig. 6; refer to Experimental design for details about log-transformation). This stabilization further suppressed false-positive associations (Fig. 3B and Fig. 4B), allowing CAM-Net to recover robust ecological signals that transcended dataset-specific noise.

WGS outperforms 16S rRNA sequencing in resolving functional microbial consortia

While both 16S and WGS showed consistent cross-cohort validation (Fig. 7, Fig. 3B, and Fig. 4B), functional analysis underscored the superior resolution of WGS for consortium identification. Specifically, across Asia and Europe, WGS uniquely identified functional partners like Eubacterium and Lachnospiraceae that leveraged A. muciniphila-mediated metabolism to produce propionate [27] and butyrate [28]. Conversely, 16S rRNA highlighted negative correlations with antagonistic microbe, including the pro-inflammatory Romboutsia [29] and Bacteroides [30] (Tables S1 and S2).

Consequently, although CAM-Net demonstrated consistent robustness in both internal and external validation across WGS and 16S datasets, WGS demonstrated higher biological specificity by identifying functional allies that facilitated colonization and metabolic cooperation, while 16S rRNA was more prone to capturing statistical noise or antagonistic microbe that did not constitute a true consortium (Tables S1 and S2).

Conclusion and discussions

In this study, we introduced CAM-Net, a context-aware framework designed for identifying the optimal consortium of a target microbe in the human gut microbiome. By addressing the limitations of traditional pairwise methods, CAM-Net captures complex, higher-order microbial interactions through multi-hop network construction and context-aware filtering. Our results demonstrated that CAM-Net outperforms traditional methods like Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave, which are often prone to false-positive associations and instability, particularly in heterogeneous datasets. While its re-samplings and multi-hop traversals increase computing complexity, CAM-Net delivers optimal precision without substantially inflating runtime or memory usage, which matches the efficiency of methods like SparCC (Fig. S5; refer to Experiment environment configuration for details about computing resources).

The ability of CAM-Net to maintain statistical consistency across diverse populations highlights its robustness, which is inherently validated against technical noise by our cross-platform and multi-cohort design. Despite distinct sequencing depths and sparsity levels across 16S rRNA and WGS datasets, CAM-Net consistently captured targeted core consortia, effectively mitigating false-positive associations under heterogeneous conditions. Additionally, the incorporation of log-transformation ensures accurate microbial correlation estimation under varying data conditions. We recommend the routine use of log-transformation to mitigate data heterogeneity, as CAM-Net yields more reliable results compared to conventional approaches.

Overall, CAM-Net provides a powerful tool for microbiome research, offering a more accurate and context-aware approach to deciphering microbial relationships and their broader impact on host health. For instance, discoveries like the A. muciniphila and Alistipes tryptophan synergy provide a clear blueprint for designing personalized, multi-strain probiotics.

Materials and datasets

Datasets and pre-processing

The datasets utilized in this study, consisting of human gut samples, were retrieved from the Microbiome Search Engine (MSE) [19] and are summarized in Table 1. For WGS sequences, taxonomic annotation was performed using Metagenomic Phylogenetic Analysis [31] based on the Reference Sequence database [32], with analyses restricted exclusively to bacterial taxonomic profiles. For 16S rRNA amplicon data, analysis was conducted using the Parallel-Meta Suite [33]: sequences underwent Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) denoising, followed by Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) clustering based on the denoised ASVs and mapping to the Greengenes (v13.8) [34] reference database. We also excluded microbial features that exhibited zero counts in more than 90% of the samples to reduce data sparsity in 16S rRNA data. Finally, both 16S rRNA and WGS taxonomic profiles were log-transformed prior to downstream analysis to mitigate inter-sample heterogeneity (refer to Experimental design for details about log-transformation). In addition, to prevent potential information loss and preserve the integrity of the ecological background, no heuristic feature selection or taxonomic filtering was performed in advance, allowing CAM-Net to dynamically evaluate all microbial entities within their complete community context.

Target microbe selection and context-aware network construction

To evaluate the ecological robustness of CAM-Net across distinct colonization dynamics, we selected A. muciniphila and L. acidophilus as representative focal microbe. For the WGS data, analyses were conducted at the species level to achieve high-resolution characterization. In contrast, for the 16S rRNA sequencing data, to enable precise characterization of the target microbes, the corresponding OTUs were mapped back to the genera Akkermansia and Lactobacillus. Meanwhile, to preserve the high-resolution background of the surrounding microbial community, all remaining microbe were retained at the OTU level. A. muciniphila was prioritized as a prototypical indigenous bacterium due to its stable colonization of the human gut and its well-documented role as a mucin-degrading metabolic hub [21]. In contrast, the common probiotic L. acidophilus was selected as a representative transient colonizer [22]. Its typically unstable engraftment and exogenous nature provide a weak-association scenario. This contrast provides a controlled framework to evaluate whether CAM-Net can distinguish stable, context-dependent microbial associations from transient or spurious correlations.

Co-occurrence networks were constructed using CAM-Net, Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave, with edge weights representing correlation magnitudes. To ensure a fair comparison, the top N associations for Spearman, SparCC, and FlashWeave (ranked by absolute correlation) were selected to match the size of the CAM-Net optimal consortium (refer to Experimental design for details about definition of top N microbes). While traditional benchmarks typically exhibited a star-like, hub-and-spoke topology that linked the target to isolated partners, CAM-Net identified a densely interconnected subgraph (red edges; Fig. 3A). This cohesive structure captured reciprocal inter-connections and higher-order dependencies, reflecting a stable ecological module that transcended simple pairwise associations(Fig. 1).

Experiment environment configuration

In this study, all computations were performed on a personal workstation (Apple M2 chip, 8-core CPU, 16 GB memory). Memory usage was quantified as peak memory consumption, and runtime was measured by the system clock millisecond.
