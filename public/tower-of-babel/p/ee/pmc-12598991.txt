Evaluation of the efficacy of PREVENIR (PREVention ENvIronment Reproduction) platforms on urinary markers of chemical exposure in pregnant women: protocol for an unblinded randomised clinical trial (PREVENIR-G)
Authors: Fleur Delva, Loïc Sentilhes, Florence Francis-Oliviero, Vincent Bessonneau, Claire Sunyach, Carine Audouin, Christophe Paris, Bassam Haddad, Mireille Matrat, Jean-Claude Pairon, Milia Belacel, Rémi Sitta, Tamara Roberts, Florence Bretelle, Ronan Garlantezec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIt has been reported that pregnant women used more cosmetics daily than non-pregnant women. Phenoxyacetic acid is the main metabolite of phenoxyethanol, the most frequent preservative in cosmetics used in Europe, previously associated with reproductive effects (longer time to conception, endocrine disruptors in newborns and poorer verbal comprehension in children). In France, specialised platforms (PREVention ENvIronment Reproduction (PREVENIR)) in university hospital maternity wards are dedicated to evaluating environmental and occupational exposures in patients with pregnancy-related pathologies and supporting targeted prevention efforts. These platforms are composed of occupational health physicians, obstetrician-gynaecologists, midwives, occupational health nurses, and occupational health and environmental engineers. To assess the efficacy of these platforms, we developed a randomised clinical trial, the protocol for which is presented in this paper. The primary objective of the PREVENIR-G Study is to compare the change in urinary phenoxyacetic acid concentrations from baseline to 3 months postintervention between an intervention group and a control group. To date, the intervention has been integrated into routine care in certain facilities; however, its efficacy remains unproven. We will include 300 pregnant women (aged 18 years or older) who are under 24 weeks of gestation (150 per group) referred to the participating PREVENIR platforms for management. The intervention will consist of clinical prevention management through the PREVENIR platforms, involving a consultation with an environmental health expert for an assessment of environmental and occupational exposures. During the consultation, targeted prevention messages will be provided based on identified exposures. The no intervention comparator will be a waiting-list control group. At the inclusion visit, patients will receive urine collection vials for samples to be collected at baseline and again at 3 months. Urine samples will be collected twice in a single day, on three separate days, during the collection week at home. In the week following the urine collection period, only participants in the intervention group will engage with the PREVENIR platforms. Introduction

Pregnancy is often associated with increased cosmetic use due to common skin changes, such as dryness and stretch marks. A 2013 study conducted in France found that pregnant women used more cosmetics daily (n=18 products) than did non-pregnant women (n=16 products).1 In this study, the proportion of users was similar for pregnant and non-pregnant women for most cosmetic products. Hair colouring was nonetheless more frequent among non-pregnant women (46% vs 38% among pregnant women). In contrast, products for stretch marks (6% vs 50%) and personal hygiene (48% vs 63%) were used more frequently by pregnant women.1 Similarly, a 2016 study in France reported that few women altered their cosmetic use during pregnancy, despite cosmetic changes often being made during this time.2 Thus, some women began using products they had not used before, such as body lotion and an intimate hygiene product (4.4% each). The use of body lotion during pregnancy increased (13.4%). Cosmetics contain various substances, including preservatives, fixatives and colourants, which can result in significant exposure to certain compounds with potential health effects. Phenoxyethanol, a glycol ether, is the most commonly used preservative in Europe, either alone or in combination with other preservatives, in a range between 20% and 50% of cosmetics.3 4 It is limited to a maximum concentration of 1% per product, as regulated by the Cosmetics Directive. This glycol ether metabolises into phenoxyacetic acid, which has a plasma half-life of approximately 2 hours, with 80% of elimination occurring through urine.5 Because of frequent and repeated exposure, urinary phenoxyacetic acid levels can serve as an effective biomarker of cosmetic exposure and have been associated with the use of cosmetics in biomonitoring surveys among the general population.6 Additionally, phenoxyacetic acid has been documented as a reliable indicator of occupational exposure to cosmetics.7 In the PELAGIE (Perturbateurs Endocriniens : Étude Longitudinale sur les Anomalies de la Grossesse, l’Infertilité et l’Enfance) mother–child cohort, urinary samples were collected before 19 weeks of gestation, and glycol ether measurements were available for 609 women. They found that higher urinary phenoxyacetic acid is associated with longer time to conception for the current pregnancy, changes in circulating steroid hormone levels in newborns and poorer verbal comprehension in children at 6 years of age.8,10 Animal data suggest that several glycol ethers, including phenoxyethanol, have been associated with impaired ovarian function11 and neurotoxicity by different mechanisms.8

Other chemical exposures are also significant in terms of health effects and risk levels. For instance, bisphenols—commonly used in plastics, epoxy resins, flame retardants and chemical developers—have been detected in the urine of pregnant women, indicating their ability to cross the placental barrier and accumulate in the placenta and amniotic fluid, thereby reaching the fetus.12 13 Studies have shown that exposure to bisphenol A, bisphenol F and bisphenol S during pregnancy can reduce birth weight.14 15 Furthermore, exposure to bisphenol A between the tenth and seventeenth weeks of pregnancy has been associated with an increased risk of cryptorchidism.16 Another relevant example is pyrethroids, which are among the most widely used insecticides globally. These are present in agricultural, veterinary and household products, including antilice shampoos and mosquito repellents. Data from the ELFE (French Longitudinal Study of Children) cohort showed that among five pyrethroid metabolites, all but one were detected in the urine of all pregnant women.17 The Pélagie cohort study, which measured the same pyrethroid metabolites in urine, found that exposure to two of them was associated with reduced cognitive performance in children.18

Intervention studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of targeted actions in reducing exposure to specific chemicals. A scoping review was conducted to identify interventions that successfully altered biomarker concentrations of phenols, glycol ethers and phthalates originating from dietary intake and personal care product use.19 Only three studies specifically aimed to remove or replace personal care products, to assess their impact on urinary concentrations of the endocrine-disrupting chemicals of interest. In all three studies, urinary concentrations of these endocrine-disrupting chemicals decreased following the intervention.

Previous studies have recommended preconception visits as a means of identifying and addressing environmental exposures.20 However, a study conducted in the USA found that fewer than 20% of surveyed obstetricians and interns routinely enquired about environmental exposures in pregnant women.21 In 2015, the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics recommended implementing policies to prevent exposure to environmental chemicals.22 Despite these recommendations, preconception visits—which could play a crucial role in identifying such exposure—remain uncommon.

In France, specialised platforms are dedicated to evaluating environmental and occupational exposures in couples seeking fertility and pregnant patients who are followed in university hospital maternity wards. These platforms are composed of occupational health physicians, obstetrician-gynaecologists, midwives, occupational health nurses, and occupational health and environmental engineers and support targeted prevention efforts, including guidance on the use of cosmetics.23 We designed a randomised clinical trial to assess the efficacy of these platforms, and the protocol for the study is presented in this paper.

Objectives

The primary objective of the PREVention ENvIronment Reproduction (PREVENIR)-G Study will be to compare the change in urinary phenoxyacetic acid concentrations from baseline to 3 months postintervention between the intervention group and the control group.

The secondary objectives of the PREVENIR-G Study are included in the following investigations:

Objective 2: We will evaluate the platforms’ effectiveness in reducing levels of pyrethroids (home-use pesticides), parabens, phthalates and bisphenols (commonly found in consumer products) by measuring urinary concentrations at baseline and 3 months postintervention in the intervention group and comparing them with the control group.

Objective 3: We will evaluate the platforms’ effectiveness to reduce occupational exposures over 3 months (an occupational health expert will assess occupational exposures—including chemical agents such as metals and solvents, as well as physical and organisational factors—from the data obtained during each consultation).

Objective 4: We will also explore changes in both the chemical exposome and the endogenous metabolome (using a non-targeted analytical approach) between baseline and 3 months.

Objective 5: We will monitor exhaled carbon monoxide (CO) levels in both groups over this period and the change between baseline and 3 months.

Objective 6: We will describe social distribution in phenoxyacetic acid exposure, examining variations according to income level, socioprofessional category (International Standard Classification of Occupations 2008), and education level in both the preintervention and postintervention groups.

Objective 7: We will evaluate the determinants of phenoxyacetic acid, pyrethroid, paraben, phthalate and bisphenol levels.

Objective 8: We will use qualitative analysis to examine factors influencing the implementation and transferability of the intervention, including individual, organisational and contextual (as platforms localisation) elements that may enhance or limit its effectiveness.

Objective 9: Through qualitative studies, we will assess the acceptance, perceived value and effectiveness of the intervention from the perspectives of professionals and patients, as well as its impact on professional practices and the adoption of health-promoting behaviours.

Methods and analysis

This study is an unblinded, randomised clinical superiority trial with two parallel groups (intervention vs no intervention). It is a national, multicentre study that will be conducted across four sites in France: the University Hospitals of Bordeaux, Rennes, Créteil and Marseille. A mixed-methods approach will be employed, combining this quantitative component with a complementary qualitative study involving professionals and patients to provide an in-depth understanding of the implementation, appropriation and perceived effectiveness processes.

Population

Eligibility criteria

We will include pregnant women aged 18 years or older and under 24 weeks of gestation (to minimise the number of participants giving birth during the study period) who are referred to the participating PREVENIR platforms.

We will exclude individuals under court protection, guardianship or curatorship; those with severely compromised physical and/or psychological health that, in the investigator’s opinion, may affect study compliance; and individuals who do not speak French.

Study procedures

The intervention consists of clinical prevention management through the PREVENIR platforms. This will involve a consultation with an environmental health expert from one of the PREVENIR platforms, who will conduct a structured, pre-established interview to assess exposure to environmental risk factors related to reproduction, both in professional and non-professional settings. During the interview, targeted prevention messages will be provided based on the exposures identified. A comprehensive report will then be created, outlining primary and supplementary preventive actions that may benefit parents, children and siblings. Standard care at each centre will remain unchanged to allow for a pragmatic evaluation of these platforms under real life conditions.

The control group will serve as a waiting-list control group. On inclusion, participants in the control group will receive standard pregnancy-related care but will not undergo any specific environmental health intervention. The intervention will be offered at the end of the study, 3 months after initial inclusion.

Enrolment in the study will be conducted by an environmental health expert (midwife, nurse or engineer specialising in environmental health) from the PREVENIR platform within the maternity wards associated with the PREVENIR platforms during 1 year. Enrolment may take place early in pregnancy, at prenatal diagnosis centres, or following referral from gynaecologists in community settings or other hospitals. Pregnant patients treated at university hospitals within the platforms are patients referred for first-trimester ultrasound scans or for pregnancy care consultations with midwives. The environmental health expert will explain the study details to potential participants and then randomly assign them to either the intervention group or the control group. The randomisation list will be prepared by the statistician at the methodology centre prior to the start of the study. The numbers in the two treatment groups will be balanced in a ratio of 1:1. Randomisation will be stratified by centre. A document describing the preparation of the randomisation list is kept confidential at the Methodology and Data Management Centre.

At the inclusion visit, patients will receive urine collection vials for samples to be collected at baseline and again at 3 months. A CO measurement will also be taken during this visit. Additionally, each patient will be provided with a diary to complete at home during the week of urine collection.

For 1 week following the inclusion visit, each day during the urine collection week, the patient will complete the first two sections of the diary. The first section records the cosmetics, hygiene and personal care products used that day, while the second section documents food intake and leisure activities that may involve contact with chemicals. Furthermore, urine samples will be collected twice in a single day on three separate days during the week, at home. The patient will record each collection on the urine collection form within the daily diary. Collected urine samples will be stored in designated containers in the participant’s refrigerator.

In the week following the urine collection period (days 8–15), only participants in the intervention group will engage with the PREVENIR platforms. Management in PREVENIR platforms includes a standardised interview performed by a nurse or midwife, based on a questionnaire that identifies potential activities or products (cosmetics, pesticides, home, perfumes, cleaning, housing, leisure activities…) that expose patients to reproductive risk factors. The consultation in which the standardised interview is conducted is not only a time for data collection but also for providing preventive advice. After each consultation, an environmental and occupational exposure analysis is performed. It is difficult in clinical practice to identify exposure to chemicals. Indeed, this requires a lengthy and complex complementary investigation (exact references of the products used by the patients, requests for safety data sheets from the suppliers, analysis of the composition, etc), which often results in obtaining only the approximate composition of the product. In clinical practice, the goal is to identify the circumstances of exposure that are documented in the scientific literature as potentially exposing individuals to one or more reproductive risk factors. After analysis, targeted preventive measures are proposed for each patient, along with the corresponding medical conclusions. If occupational exposure is suspected, the consultation report is sent to the patient’s occupational health department, following the patient’s consent, for the implementation of preventive actions in the workplace. If the patient does not consent or his/her workplace does not have an occupational health service, the hazard study is conducted by the platform that provides appropriate preventive advice. Preventive advice on proper actions is also provided to patients for non-occupational exposure.

At 3 months, a member of the project team will contact each participant to remind her to collect urine samples and complete the daily diary as she did at the start of the study. For 1 week (from day M3 to M3+6), urine collection and diary completion will follow the same protocol as at inclusion. In the week following this collection, all participants will undergo a follow-up interview using the same questionnaire administered at inclusion by the PREVENIR platforms. Additionally, a measurement of exhaled CO will be conducted.

In the no-intervention group, the full intervention will take place at this time, after the urine collection, including the interview and the provision of preventive advice.

The biological sample circuit is described in online supplemental file 1.

Qualitative data processes

Interviews with platform professionals: Semistructured individual interviews (n=15) will be conducted with professionals working across the four PREVENIR platforms, including the environmental health experts who carry out the consultations, the medical coordinators and the platform managers. At the start of each interview, sociodemographic and professional data (age, sex, occupation and length of service) will be collected.

Participant interviews: A sample of 40 participants (10 from each site) in the intervention group will be selected for semistructured interviews. Participants will be chosen primarily based on theoretical relevance to the study’s focus (patients benefiting from the intervention). Selection will also account for geographical representation across the four participating centres. The recruitment process will aim for a diverse sample based on several inclusion criteria: age, education level, living area (rural or urban), marital status and socioprofessional category.

Data collection and management

The principal investigator will oversee all aspects of data collection and management. All data management activities will be conducted using REDCap for the electronic collection and management of data. Only database managers, the Unité de Soutien Méthodologique à la Recherche (USMR) project team and auditors will have direct access rights to the database. Checks will be scheduled to verify the consistency of the data entered into the electronic case report form.

Statistical analysis

The main analysis will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. Multiple imputations will be used to handle missing data. Qualitative variables will be summarised in terms of frequency, percentage and 95% CIs, using the exact binomial distribution. Quantitative variables will be described using head count, mean, 95% CIs for the mean, SD, median, minimum, maximum, and first and third quartiles. Where applicable, the geometric mean and its 95% CI will also be reported. Quantitative variables will be compared between groups using Student’s t-test, provided the test’s assumptions are met (normal distribution and homogeneity of variances). If variances are unequal between groups, Welch’s t-test will be used. If the distribution is not normal, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test will be employed.

For the primary outcome and quantitative secondary outcomes, a linear regression model (with random effects if necessary, particularly for longitudinal measures or centre effects) will be used to account for adjustment variables and to identify factors that may predict and/or modify the intervention’s effect. The exposure variable will be the intervention, and confounders will include sociodemographic variables, implementation-related factors and determinants influencing patients’ engagement with the intervention. For the adjustment strategy, a directed acyclic graph will be constructed based on the scientific literature. It will be determined before the analysis in the statistical analysis plan, using possibly blinded data descriptions. The assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity of residuals will be systematically checked. If these assumptions are violated, variable transformation may be considered. Cross-sectional measures of concentration will be analysed a priori using a log-linear model.

Categorical variables will be analysed using a logistic or polytomous regression model with the same adjustment strategy as for quantitative outcomes. The assumption of log-linearity in the association between predictors and outcomes will be systematically verified.

In a French biomonitoring study, the median phenoxyacetic acid concentration was reported as 291 µg/g creatinine (IQR 96–958), with quantifiable levels detected in 99.8% of the population.6 This distribution closely approximates a lognormal distribution. Based on available data regarding associations between phenoxyacetic acid concentrations and exposure factors targeted by the intervention (such as deodorant and cosmetic use),6 7 we anticipate a 50% reduction in the median concentration for the intervention group at the 3-month mark, to an estimated median of 148 μg/g creatinine (IQR 48–454), also approximating a lognormal distribution. For the non-intervention group, we expect no change, with concentrations remaining at a median of 291 μg/g creatinine (IQR: 96–958). Using a two-tailed Wilcoxon test at a 5% alpha level, 133 subjects per group (totalling 266) would be needed to achieve 90% power. Accounting for potential loss to follow-up, given the 3-month period outside of the hospital setting, we have decided to enrol 300 participants (150 per group), which accommodates an estimated 11.3% attrition rate.

Qualitative analysis

The qualitative analysis will employ a thematic categorical approach with semantic coding, focusing on the empirical exploration of ideas, language and their meanings. This process involves a detailed, line-by-line examination of interview transcripts to describe, classify and transform the raw qualitative data based on an analytical framework. The construction of this framework, known as categorisation, involves defining categories of analysis informed both by the data themselves—through an open, inductive approach that generalises and abstracts from interview excerpts—and by the study objectives, prior data and theoretical frameworks from the literature. The inductive or exploratory component incorporates flexibility in analysis, drawing on Grounded Theory methods to explore emerging themes. According to proponents of this approach, data collection serves theory formulation; the researcher simultaneously collects, codes and analyses data to guide further data collection and inform the emerging theory. The deductive component, meanwhile, will apply established theoretical frameworks, specifically behavioural and organisational change models, with an emphasis on the Theory of Change. This framework offers a robust model for analysing determinants of behavioural change in preventive contexts.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the hospital ethics committee (CCP (Comité de Protection des Personnes) Ouest 2, no. 2023-A00941-44). All participants will receive information about the study and will provide their written informed consent (online supplemental file 2) before taking part in the study. All study procedures will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. When the trial concludes, the results will be shared through presentations and publications.

Regarding the ethics of the no intervention arm, to date, the intervention has been integrated into routine care in certain facilities, but its efficacy remains unproven. The control group will serve as a waiting-list control group. On inclusion, participants in the control group will receive standard pregnancy-related care but will not undergo any specific environmental health intervention. Nevertheless, the intervention will be offered at the end of the study, 3 months after initial inclusion.

Trial status

The text of this manuscript refers to protocol V.2.1, dated 3 February 2025. Recruitment will begin in September 2025 and is expected to continue until December 2026.

Discussion

To assess platform efficacy, phenoxyacetic acid—a metabolite of phenoxyethanol and the most prevalent preservative in cosmetics in Europe4 24 25—will be selected as the exposure biomarker, given its widespread presence in the general population. Previous studies have shown a high prevalence of phenoxyacetic acid exposure in the general population, including children, based on detectable levels in 85.7%–100% of urine samples. Individuals using cosmetic products exhibited significantly higher concentrations than those who did not use such products.9 26 27 Evidence from a mother–child cohort study demonstrated that urinary phenoxyacetic acid levels in early pregnancy were associated with effects on fertility, endocrine biomarker levels and cognitive outcomes in children,8,10 suggesting that it is an effective indicator of cosmetic product use with potential negative impacts on pregnancy outcomes. To monitor behavioural change and, thus, platform effectiveness, urinary phenoxyacetic acid levels will be measured as an objective marker of exposure, considering that 80% of excretion occurs via this route. Urine samples will be collected over a 1-week period, with participants randomly selecting 3 days during which they will provide two urine samples per day.28 This sampling method, previously applied in the PESTIRIV (Riverains de vignes) and HELIX (Human Early Life Exposome) studies, has shown high acceptance and feasibility among participants.29 30

This individually randomised trial will be stratified by centre, using a ‘waiting list’ control group. This control structure respects ethical considerations because it ensures that all participants ultimately receive the intervention. In particular, patients in the ‘no intervention’ group will receive the preventive advice 3 months later, allowing them to adopt measures to reduce environmental exposure. A cluster trial design is deemed unsuitable because of the limited number of centres and the relevance of focusing on patients referred to existing prevention platforms, which preclude the feasibility of establishing a distinct control cluster. Potential contamination bias will be minimised because the environmental health management provided by the PREVENIR platforms operates independently from pregnancy clinical management. Recruitment will be conducted during maternity consultations or hospitalisations, with PREVENIR team members explaining the intervention process. This set-up will ensure that patients not managed by PREVENIR will not receive targeted environmental prevention advice. Research has shown that pregnant women often independently make behavioural changes to mitigate environmental exposures;31 however, most report feeling insufficiently informed. This study will evaluate the impact of the PREVENIR platforms, supplementing the information typically available through routine care resources, such as the ‘1000 Premiers Jours’ website and environmental health information leaflets.

To date, the intervention has been integrated into routine care in certain facilities; however, its efficacy remains unproven. The strategy currently under study has not yet demonstrated benefits in terms of morbidity reduction or proxy outcomes such as reductions in substance exposure levels. It is therefore essential to assess the relevance of this intervention, considering both its potential benefits and any adverse effects, such as increased stress or anxiety. Messages should clearly communicate safer options during pregnancy to improve understanding among patients and clinicians without unnecessarily alarming pregnant women about self-care. In the scientific literature, environmental exposure has been linked to reproductive disorders spanning conception to child development, as well as various short-term, medium-term and long-term health effects, including impacts on psychomotor development and an elevated risk of cancer. The developmental origins of health and disease model reinforces the idea that many health outcomes and diseases may trace their roots to early developmental exposures to environmental risk factors.32 Strong scientific evidence supports this hypothesis, showing that prenatal exposure to toxic agents or adverse environmental conditions can have lifelong health consequences.33 The mitigation of these risk factors from the preconception period through embryonic and fetal life may favourably influence health outcomes across the lifespan and into subsequent generations. Furthermore, preventive actions taken early in life appear to yield more substantial effects than those implemented later in adulthood.

The analysis of the intervention’s effectiveness will adopt a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative and qualitative data. The qualitative analysis, drawn from semistructured interviews, will focus on two primary aims: to assess the perceived effectiveness of the platform and to identify the key determinants of this perception from the perspective of platform professionals; and to explore patients’ engagement with and appreciation of the intervention, as well as its impact on behaviour changes related to cosmetic use and exposure to other environmental reprotoxic factors. Qualitative insights from interviews with both platform professionals and patients will complement and contextualise the quantitative findings, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of the platform’s benefits and limitations. The sampling strategy for the qualitative study will ensure diversity by incorporating criteria such as centre, age, sex and socioprofessional categories to capture a broad range of perspectives. Individual interviews are particularly well suited to this study because they are an effective qualitative technique for exploring complex personal processes such as understanding, evaluation, decision-making and the adoption of behavioural changes.

Additionally, the ESTEBAN Study revealed a social gradient in phenoxyacetic acid exposure, with lower levels of education correlating with higher exposure. This finding supports the hypothesis that our individualised approach, tailored to each patient, may help to reduce health inequalities related to environmental exposures.

A key limitation of this protocol is the lack of double-blinding, which may introduce detection bias. However, because the primary outcome is a biological measure, the risk of bias is limited. Another limitation of the study concerns the generalisability of the results: the PREVENIR platforms are established within university hospitals in France and are intended for pregnant women receiving care in these hospitals. It would be important to involve patients in the study design to define the protocol. However, with the mixed approach, we could understand the barriers and drivers of care in patients. The strengths of this study include the implementation of a randomised clinical trial to evaluate, with a high level of evidence, the effectiveness of a preventive intervention in reducing exposure to household pollutants. In addition, the study employs both quantitative and qualitative approaches, which are expected to provide valuable insights into the implementation of the platforms.

At an individual level, the anticipated benefits of this study include fostering health-promoting behaviours among couples and supporting positive developmental outcomes for their children in the short term, medium term and long term. Currently, five PREVENIR platforms are operational across France, and four of them are taking part in this trial, and three more are under development in Toulouse, Rouen and Lyon. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of these platforms in reducing exposure to reproductive risk factors. Additionally, it aimed to deepen our understanding of the factors that enhance the efficacy of preventive actions when implemented. With the potential nationwide roll-out of these platforms, the findings from this study will help inform the optimal organisation of preventive care for couples, including the identification of target groups, determination of necessary platform staffing and refinement of care delivery models.
