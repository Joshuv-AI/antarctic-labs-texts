Efficacy and Influencing Factors of Short-Term Spinal Cord Stimulation for Postherpetic Neuralgia: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Hui Zhang, Ying Yuan, Kaisong Xuan, Mengzhen Xu, Jin Liu, Chenhui Xue, Jingjing Nan, Liwei Liu, Guohui Wang, Huifang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the efficacy and influencing factors of spinal cord stimulation (SCS)for postherpetic neuralgia (PHN).Patients and MethodsA total of 80 patients diagnosed with postherpetic neuralgiaand treated at the Pain Department of Shanxi Medical University between March 2022–March 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were divided into two groups according to theactual treatment received in clinical practice, with 39 cases in the SCS group and 40 cases in the PRF group. One patient in the SCS group was excluded due to loss to follow-up at 4 weeks after treatment The experimental group underwent spinal cord stimulation (SCS) treatment, while the control group received pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) treatment. The Visual Analog Scale, Self-Rating Anxiety Scale, Self-Rating Depression Scale, and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index were administered before surgery and at 1, 4, 12, and 24 weeks post-surgery, respectively.ResultsThe VAS, SAS, SDS, and PSQI scores in both groups were significantly reduced at 1, 4, 12, and 24 weeks after treatment (P<0.05), and the scores in the SCS group were significantly lower than those in the PRF group (P<0.05). Multivariate linear regression analysis indicated that gender, age, and disease duration had significant effects on the efficacy (P<0.05).ConclusionSpinal cord electrical stimulation is more effective than pulsed radiofrequency for treating postherpetic neuralgia. It significantly reduces pain levels, improves sleep quality, and alleviates anxiety and depression in patients. Factors such as gender, age, anddisease duration may serve as potential determinants of the effectiveness of SCS.Keywords: postherpetic neuralgia, herpes zoster, spinal cord stimulation, pain

Introduction

Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a neuropathic pain syndrome caused by nerve fiber injuryand is the most common complication of herpes zoster (HZ), resulting in a significant burden on individuals affected by HZ-related diseases.1 PHN is defined as the persistence ofpain for 90 days or more following a rash caused by HZ. While the pain usually subsides within a few weeks, it can persist for several months or even years.2 Incidence rates ofPHN range from 6.5% to 18% in retrospective studies conducted in the United States, and prospective studies have reported rates as high as 30%.3 Clinical symptoms of PHN primarily include persistent burning pain, shooting pain, stabbing pain, electric shock-like pain, hyperalgesia, paresthesia, and numbness.4 Neuralgia following shingles is associated with impaired mood, disrupted sleep, reduced social functioning, and difficulties carrying outdaily activities.5

Currently, there exists a variety of treatment modalities for PHN, such as subcutaneous blocks in the lesion area, dorsal root ganglion pulses, epidural blocks, paravertebral blocks, sympathetic nerve blocks, etc. These treatments can alleviate symptoms to varying degrees, but some patients experience recurrent symptoms and have poor long-term efficacy. Consequently, this significantly impacts the quality of life for these individuals.6 In recent years, spinal cord stimulation (SCS) has gained significant clinical application as a minimallyinvasive treatment. The mechanism of SCS involves electrical stimulation, which promote nerve cell regeneration and stimulates Schwann cells to express neurotrophic factors. Additionally, the electric field can electrolyze microtissues, resulting in the release of vasoactive substances. By triggering axon reflex and segmental reflex, SCS can induce vasodilation and enhance local blood flow around the nerves, thereby playing a significant role in nerve repair.7

Pulsed radio frequency (PRF) is a widely used minimally invasive method that can alleviate neuropathic pain by regulating nerve conduction and reducing inflammatory responses. However, high-quality comparative studies between short-term SCS and PRF for PHN remain limited. This retrospective study was performed to compare their efficacy and identify influencing factors. Furthermore, they regulate and control axonal development and regeneration, demyelination, and debris clearance through autophagy and phagocytosis during Wallerian degeneration.8

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Selection of Patients

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study. A total of 90 patients with PHN were treated between March 2022 and March 2023. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 80 patients were retrospectively analyzed. We retrospectively collected medical recordsof patients who received either SCS or PRF treatment. Patients were assigned to two groups based on the actual treatment administered in clinical practice.(not randomized). Each group initially consisted of 40 patients. One patient in the SCS group was excluded due to loss to follow-up; finally, 39 patients were included in the SCS group and 40 in the PRF group. All enrolled patients provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University in Taiyuan, Shanxi Province, People’s Republic of China and was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who were diagnosed with PHN according to the clinical diagnostic criteria;2 (2) Visual Analog Scale- (VAS)score ≥5 points; (3) Pain refractory to conventional pharmacological (such as opiate analgesics, tricyclic antidepressants, anticonvulsants, and topical analgesics) or physical (such as percutaneous electrical nerve stimulation and acupuncture) therapies; and (4) No spinal lesions or malformations.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe organ dysfunction, including brain, heart, lung, kidney, and liver diseases; (2) Complicated with severe liver and kidney dysfunction; and (3) patients who had intellectual problems and were unable to complete self-evaluations.

Therapeutic Method

Group SCS

After the patient was admitted to the operating room, ECG, blood pressure and oxygen monitoring were performed. Intravenous access was opened, and the patient was placed prone on the operating table. A self-made locator was placed at the intended puncture site and the intervertebral space was determined by DSA localization (Figure 2A). A 0.5-cm skin point near the affected spinous process was marked as the puncture point with reference to the locator grid. After applying local anaesthesia, a tunnel needle was inserted at a 30° angle to the epidural space. After confirming the appropriate puncture position by DSA (Figure 2B), the needle core was withdrawn, the stimulating electrode was connected and placed near the dorsal posterior midline of the spinal cord segment (Figure 3A and B). After confirming the position of the electrode in the spinal cord again by DSA without error (Figure 2C and D), connect the electrode extension cable and pulse generator. The electrical stimulation parameters were adjusted until the patient felt numbness and distension covering the painful area. Typical SCS parameters included a frequency of 50 Hz, pulse width of 200–300 μs, and current of 0.5–3.0 mA, which were adjusted according to individual paresthesia coverage. Subsequently, the electrode lead was sutured and fixed (Figure 3C), the connector was looped and fixed to the skin (Figure 3D), and the puncture site was covered with a sterile dressing. Vital signs and patient limb function were continuously monitored throughout the procedure. The suture is comfortable in daily life and during sleep without additional precautions. After completing 14 consecutive days of spinal cord electrical stimulation therapy, the electrodes were removed.

Group PRF

The patient was placed prone on the operating table and a homemade locator was placed at the intended puncture site. The position of the intervertebral foramen in the corresponding segment was determined by CT localisation. The puncture depth and paracentesis distance were calculated, the angle of needle entry, and the entry point and target point were marked. After routine disinfection and 0.5% lidocaine local anaesthesia, the skin was punctured at a fixed point, and the needle was inserted according to the predetermined puncture angle to reach the outer and lower edge of the transverse process through the fascia and muscle layers. The needle was slowly inserted into the intervertebral foramen site under CT confirmation again until the tip of the needle was located in the intervertebral foramen 1/3 (below the inferior notch of the superior pedicle). Connect the radiofrequency instrument (Beiqi R-2000B), test the resistance around the tip of the needle, and if it is between 250 and 550 Ω, then carry out the sensory and motor point stimulation test; for sensation, use the high-frequency current, 100 Hz,500 μs, 0.5 mA or less can induce acidity, distension, numbness or even electrocution sensation of the original pain area; for motor use, use the low-frequency current of 2 Hz, 1000 μs, 2 mA, and request that this stimulation shall not induce out of the Trunk and limb muscle twitching. After the impedance and electrical stimulation test and other physiological localisation were correct, pulsed radiofrequency was then used to further confirm that the needle tip was located in the dorsal root ganglion. A temperature of 42°C and 45V was set to administer pulsed radiofrequency to the patient. If the patient complained of throbbing pain in the original pain area without muscle twitching, the tip of the needle was confirmed to be in the dorsal root ganglion, and pulsed radiofrequency was performed at 42°C for 8 minutes, and then 3 mL of a mixture of methylprednisolone and lidocaine was injected at the end of the radiofrequency treatment and then the needle was withdrawn (Figure 2). During the operation, blood pressure, heart rate and oxygen saturation were monitored dynamically, and the muscle strength and activity of the limbs were observed. The next dorsal root ganglion was loosened in the same steps, and the puncture needle was removed at the end of the operation and observed for 30 minutes. (Figure 4).

Evaluation and Outcome

Primary outcome: The pain relief rate at 24 weeks after treatment, defined as the reduction in VAS score compared with baseline.

Secondary outcomes: VAS, SAS, SDS, and PSQI scores at 1, 4, 12, and 24 weeks after treatment. Scores on various scales including the visual analogue scale (VAS), self-assessment scale for anxiety (SAS), self-assessment scale for depression (SDS), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) were measured at five time points: pre-treatment (T1), 1 week (T2), 4 weeks (T3), 12 weeks (T4), and 24 weeks (T5). Additionally, the rate of pain relief, defined as the decrease in VAS scores compared to T1, was assessed at T5. The follow-up duration after intervention was 24 weeks for all patients.

Adverse Events

Adverse events, including hematoma formation at the puncture site, infection, pneumothorax, spinal cord injury, peripheral nerve injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and electrode displacement, were meticulously documented throughout the entire follow-up duration.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis All data were analyzed and visualized using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc). Measurement data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test. The primary outcome was the pain relief rate at 24 weeks after treatment. Secondary outcomes included VAS, SAS, SDS, and PSQI scores measured at 1, 4, 12, and 24 weeks after treatment. Within-group changes across time points were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA, after confirming normality, sphericity, and homogeneity of variance assumptions. Between-group comparisons at each time point were performed using independent-samples t-tests. A Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons to control type I error. Spearman correlation analysis was used to explore the relationship between related factors and efficacy. Multivariate linear regression was performed to identify independent influencing factors for the treatment efficacy of SCS. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No missing data were observed in the final analysis dataset. Given the exploratory nature of this retrospective study, sample size calculation and power analysis were not performed.

One patient in the SCS group was excluded due to loss to follow-up at 4 weeks after treatment. The SCS group comprised 39 cases, consisting of 18 females and 21 males, with a mean age of (69.4±8.5) years and a mean duration of (90.6±12.4) days. The PRF group consisted of 40 cases, comprising 17 females and 23 males, with a mean age of (69.3±10.5) years and a mean duration of (90.8±12.7) days. No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups regarding gender, age, disease duration, pain location, co-morbidities, and general data including pain scores, anxiety, depression, sleep quality, and pre-treatment analgesic medication dosage (T1) (P>0.05) (Table 1).

Influencing Factors of SCS Curative Effect

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the pain relief rate among patients in the SCS group and variables such as age and gender. The findings indicated a negative correlation between the pain relief rate and age, gender, disease duration, SAS, SDS, and PSQI (P < 0.05). Notably, no significant correlation was observed between the pain site, initial pain score, and pain relief (P > 0.05) (Table 6).

Adverse Events

Among all the patients included in the analysis, one patient in the SCS group had postoperative adverse reactions with symptoms of nausea and vomiting, while none in the PRF group had serious adverse reactions. All perioperative and postoperative complications were closely monitored and recorded. No severe procedure-related adverse events were observed except for one case of nausea and vomiting in the SCS group.

Discussion

Efficacy of SCS in the Treatment of PHN

The current study investigated the efficacy of short-term SCS in the treatment of PHN, and found that SCS significantly reduced pain levels, improved sleep quality, and alleviated anxiety and depression in patients with PHN, and was more efficacious than PRF. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously due to the retrospective design and methodological limitations.

The etiology of postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) following herpes zoster remains uncertain. Nonetheless, it is widely acknowledged that PHN emerges due to both peripheral and central sensitization induced by nerve damage. In clinical practice, first-line treatments such as pregabalin, nerve block, and pulsed radiofrequency are commonly utilized; however, a subset of patients persists in experiencing unresolved pain despite these interventions. Consequently, managing PHN remains a clinical challenge. Furthermore, SCS has been reported to reduce opioid consumption in patients with chronic pain, supporting its value as an opioid-sparing neuromodulation therapy.9. Notably, the perioperative interventions were not identical between groups. The SCS group received 14 consecutive days of spinal cord stimulation, whereas the PRF group was additionally administered methylprednisolone and lidocaine at the end of the procedure. This difference in adjuvant medication may have a potential impact on the comparison of therapeutic effects, which should be noted when interpreting the results.

In 1965, Ron Melzack and Patrick Wall first proposed the “Gate Control Theory”10 which provided the theoretical basis for the neuromodulation of analgesia in SCS. The hypothesis proposes that nociceptive signal transmission through the posterior horn cells of the spinal cord to the center is regulated by the balance of course and fine nerve fibers in the peripheral nervous system, and when the fine fibers are excited, the gate of the nociceptive upload signal is opened, and when the coarse fibers are excited, the gate was closed, and pain was suppressed. The excitation threshold of the coarse fibers is lower than that of the fine fibers. When the spinal cord is selectively excited by electrical stimulation of the coarse fibers, the gates are closed, blocking the transmission pathway of nociceptive information and achieving the effect of pain relief.11 The neurotransmitter mechanism of SCS for pain relief has also been proposed. SCS stimulation of the posterior spinal column increases the release of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which in turn significantly inhibits tactile nociceptive hyperalgesia, while decreasing the release of excitatory amino acids (Glutamate, Aspartate, etc.) in the posterior horn of the spinal cord and, to varying degrees, promoting the release of endogenous analgesic substances.12 Spinal cord stimulation is a reversible minimally invasive neuromodulation technique that has been successfully used to treat various chronic pains.13 A prospective case series study followed up 28 PHN patients treated with SCS for 29 months and found that pain scores decreased significantly in 23 cases (82%) and functional ability improved.14 Yanamoto concluded that spinal cord stimulation is an effective treatment for PHN patients with a course of 1 to 6 months.15 At present, studies at home and abroad have shown that SCS is safe and effective in treating PHN, can significantly reduce the pain level of patients and improve their sleep quality.16,17. These results align with international studies.

Possible Influencing Factors of Curative Effect in the SCS Group

In this study, we analyzed the factors influencing the efficacy of PHN patients in the SCS group, and studied such factors as age, disease course, gender, initial pain score, sleep quality, anxiety and depression. The results showed that age, sex and course of disease were significant factors affecting the curative effect. The rate of pain relief obtained from SCS treatment for PHN decreased with age and disease duration. Previous studies have shown that age is currently the only recognised risk factor for the development of postherpetic neuralgia.18 Several studies have found that the prevalence of herpes zoster ranges from approximately 3.4 to 5.3%, that the incidence of PHN is on the rise in people over 50 years of age, and that older patients have at least a 5.785-fold increased risk of developing postherpetic neuralgia (PHN).19 The lower rate of pain relief achieved in older patients with PHN may be due to the reduced tissue and nerve repair capacity of the elderly, as well as the progressive decline in varicella-zoster virus-mediated specific cellular immunity.20

In addition, our study found that disease duration was an important factor influencing the effectiveness of temporary spinal cord electrical stimulation therapy, suggesting that early intervention seems to improve outcomes. In a study by Harke et al,14 it was noted that SCS was not homogeneous in the treatment of PHN relative to the favourable results achieved by those with shorter disease durationA study by Kumar et al21 similarly noted that early intervention with spinal cord electrical stimulation (SCS) provided better pain relief and longer pain control. These studies, along with our current study, suggest that early intervention and effective pain management may be important for the prevention and control of PHN. However, further studies are still needed to deepen the understanding of the specific mechanisms.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be addressed. First, this was a single-center retrospective study without randomization, leading to potential selection bias. Second, minor inconsistencies in data presentation and sample size labeling have been fully corrected in the revised manuscript. Third, the regression analysis was only performed in the SCS group (n=39) with multiple predictive factors, which may increase the risk of overfitting. Fourth, no sample size calculation or power analysis was conducted due to the exploratory retrospective design. Therefore, the conclusions should be considered preliminary and need further verification by large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, short-term spinal cord stimulation was associated with greater improvements in pain relief, sleep quality, anxiety, and depression compared with pulsed radiofrequency in patients with postherpetic neuralgia. Gender, age, and disease duration may be potential factors related to the effectiveness of SCS. These findings are preliminary owing to the retrospective design and small sample size, and further large-scale, well-designed prospective studies are needed to confirm the results.
