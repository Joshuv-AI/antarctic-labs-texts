Nonlinear modeling of health checkup factors and future claims-based healthcare costs in Japan: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Haruto Nakamura, Kaisei Harada, Kohei Hirako, Toshitaka Sawamura, Kousuke Imamura, Masahiko Sagae, Hidetaka Nambo, Shigehiro Karashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation health checkups support disease prevention and health management. Clarifying how routinely collected checkup factors relate to subsequent healthcare costs may inform population-level risk assessment and preventive planning. We aimed to characterize nonlinear and sex-specific associations between routine checkup factors and near-term claims-based healthcare costs in Japan and to explore whether composite metabolic indices provide additional information beyond routine measures.MethodsWe linked Japan’s Specific Health Checkups (FY2012–FY2022) to subsequent administrative insurance claims in Hakui City and analyzed adults aged 60–74 years. The outcome was annual claims-based healthcare costs in the third and fourth fiscal years after baseline, expressed as fee schedule points. We fitted generalized additive models with a Gamma distribution and log link in the overall sample and separately for men and women. Predictors included routine checkup measures (including glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c]) and lipid- and adiposity-related indices (atherogenic index of plasma, arteriosclerosis index, non–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fatty liver index, and visceral adiposity index). Model fit and the stability of the observed association patterns were assessed using 50 repeated 80/20 individual-level split validations.ResultsThe dataset comprised 11,148 person-years from 6,757 residents. The models showed limited explanatory performance (mean R2=0.06), indicating that routine health checkup variables alone explained only a small proportion of variation in subsequent claims-based healthcare costs. Age and HbA1c showed consistent associations across groups. HbA1c showed a J-shaped association with costs, with a steeper increase above approximately 6.0%. Body mass index, waist circumference, and fatty liver index showed moderate association patterns, whereas the atherogenic index of plasma, arteriosclerosis index, and visceral adiposity index provided limited additional information beyond routine measures; the fatty liver index showed a notable association pattern in men.ConclusionsRoutine health checkup factors exhibited nonlinear, sex-specific associations with near-term claims-based healthcare costs in a Japanese community. These findings may inform population-level hypothesis generation and future indicator selection. However, routine checkup variables alone appear insufficient for precise individual-level prediction. External validation and integration of additional information on comorbidities, medications, healthcare utilization, and socioeconomic factors are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00818-9.Keywords: Healthcare costs, Generalized additive model, Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), Fatty liver index, Health checkup data, Waist circumference

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including diabetes, obesity, and metabolic syndrome, are major global public health challenges and are associated with increasing healthcare utilization and costs [1, 2]. In particular, obesity—especially visceral adiposity—is a central driver of cardiometabolic risk and is closely linked to the development of diabetes, fatty liver disease, and other metabolic disorders [3–5]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) projects that the number of people with diabetes will reach approximately 853 million by 2050 [6]. Diabetes-related health expenditures are increasing worldwide [7].

In Japan, rapid population aging has increased the demand for healthcare because of the rising prevalence of lifestyle-related diseases. In 2022, total national medical expenditure reached 46.7 trillion Japanese yen (JPY), including 1.2 trillion JPY attributed to diabetes-related care [8]. Against this backdrop, Japan introduced the Specific Health Checkups and Specific Health Guidance Program in 2008 for adults aged 40–74 years. This program focuses on visceral obesity (metabolic syndrome) and aims to prevent the onset and progression of lifestyle-related diseases and to reduce cerebrovascular and cardiovascular disease risk through standardized checkups and follow-up health guidance [9–11]. In addition to improving population health, prevention is also expected to contribute to sustainable healthcare financing. Standardized checkup data have been accumulated under this program and now constitute a valuable platform for risk assessment, as well as health services and health economic research [12–16].

In Japan’s reimbursement system, medical claims are recorded as fee schedule points, with 1 point corresponding to approximately 10 JPY. Claims points are influenced by multiple biological, demographic, and lifestyle-related factors, and their associations with routine health checkup variables may be nonlinear [17–20]. For example, glycemic status, adiposity, blood pressure, and lipid-related markers may show threshold-like, J-shaped, or U-shaped associations with healthcare utilization and costs. Spline-based approaches, including restricted cubic splines and generalized additive models, have been used in population health research to quantify nonlinear associations while retaining interpretability [20, 21]. Previous Japanese studies using generalized additive models have also demonstrated interpretable nonlinear associations in health checkup and clinical data, including nonlinear patterns related to atrial fibrillation in the general population and urinary albumin excretion in patients with type 2 diabetic nephropathy [16, 22]. Therefore, analytical approaches that can flexibly model nonlinearity while retaining interpretability are needed to characterize population-level associations between checkup findings and subsequent claims-based healthcare costs.

Routine health checkup variables were selected based on their clinical relevance and availability in Japan’s Specific Health Checkups program. Age and HbA1c are closely related to chronic disease burden, diabetes management, and healthcare utilization. BMI and waist circumference are standard indicators of general and abdominal adiposity, whereas blood pressure and lipid measures are established components of cardiometabolic risk assessment.

In addition to these routinely measured variables, we examined several metabolic composite indices (MCIs) calculated from standard checkup measurements. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated from triglycerides and HDL cholesterol, reflects atherogenic lipid balance and has been proposed as a marker related to dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk [23]. The arteriosclerosis index (AI), calculated from total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol, summarizes the balance between atherogenic and anti-atherogenic cholesterol fractions [24]. The fatty liver index (FLI), calculated using BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, is a noninvasive indicator of hepatic steatosis [25]. The visceral adiposity index (VAI), calculated from BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol, has been proposed as a surrogate marker of visceral adiposity and adipose tissue dysfunction [26]. These indices may provide more detailed metabolic information than individual routine measurements alone by summarizing lipid-related, visceral adiposity-related, and fatty liver-related risk profiles. However, the extent to which these composite indices provide additional information beyond routine measures for understanding subsequent healthcare costs in community-dwelling general populations has not been sufficiently investigated.

In this study, we aimed to characterize nonlinear and sex-specific associations between routine health checkup factors, including indicators of visceral obesity and fatty liver, and claims-based healthcare costs in the third and fourth fiscal years after baseline using interpretable generalized additive models. We additionally explored whether selected metabolic composite indices provided additional information beyond routine measures. Association patterns were interpreted using partial dependence plots, effective degrees of freedom, and P values for smooth terms, whereas permutation importance was used as a supplementary indicator of relative variable contribution.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study linked Specific Health Checkups with subsequent medical claims data in Hakui City, Ishikawa Prefecture, Japan, for fiscal years 2012–2022. Health checkups were conducted in accordance with national implementation guidelines issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan. Baseline data included anthropometric measurements, blood pressure, blood biochemistry (e.g., lipids, liver-related markers, and HbA1c), and urinalysis. Lifestyle items (smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity) were not analyzed because of high levels of missingness.

Anonymized data were obtained through a data-sharing agreement between Hakui City and Kanazawa University. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kanazawa University (No. 2018-129(053)). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived, and an opt-out opportunity was provided via the official website of Hakui City.

This study was reported in accordance with the RECORD statement for observational studies using routinely collected health data. A completed RECORD checklist is provided as Supplementary Table 1.

Outcome and predictors

The outcome was annual claims-based healthcare costs in the third and fourth fiscal years after the baseline health checkup, quantified as the total number of fee schedule points (“claims points”) from outpatient, inpatient, and pharmacy claims for each eligible fiscal year. Claims points were analyzed as recorded, without conversion to Japanese yen. We selected this 3–4-year window as a near-term follow-up period in which baseline cardiometabolic status could plausibly be reflected in subsequent healthcare costs, while avoiding reliance on costs immediately after the baseline checkup. When claims data were available for both fiscal years, each fiscal year was treated as a separate person-year observation.

Candidate predictors included age, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides (TG), HDL cholesterol (HDL-C), and HbA1c levels. In addition, metabolic composite indices (MCIs) were used to capture lipid- and adiposity-related profiles. Lipid-related MCIs included the AIP, calculated as log10[TG/HDL-C] [23], and the AI, calculated as (calculated total cholesterol − HDL-C)/HDL-C [24]. Total cholesterol (TC) was calculated as TC = LDL-C + HDL-C + TG/5, and non–HDL-C was calculated as TC − HDL-C. Adiposity-related MCIs included the FLI [25] and the VAI [26].

Analyses were restricted to participants aged 60–74 years because few participants were aged 40–59 years, and both metabolic indicators and claims points were strongly age dependent. Observations outside the 2.5th–97.5th percentile ranges for each continuous predictor were excluded. Outcome outliers were identified using the interquartile range rule, defined as values below the first quartile minus 1.5 times the interquartile range or above the third quartile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range. These observations were excluded from the primary analysis.

Dataset construction and feature-set variants

Dataset A was defined as the primary analytic dataset because it consisted of routine health checkup variables that are widely available in Japan’s Specific Health Checkups program and clinically relevant to cardiometabolic risk and healthcare utilization. Dataset A included age, BMI, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and HbA1c.

Datasets B and C were constructed as secondary analyses to examine whether clinically motivated lipid-related and adiposity-related composite indices provided additional information beyond routine measures. Dataset B evaluated lipid-related variables and indices, including HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, non-HDL cholesterol, calculated total cholesterol, arteriosclerosis index, and atherogenic index of plasma. Dataset C evaluated adiposity- and fatty liver-related variables and indices, including BMI, waist circumference, fatty liver index, and visceral adiposity index.

Because several composite indices share components with routine variables, we used pre-specified feature-set variants in which one lipid-related or adiposity-related measure was added at a time to reduce multicollinearity and preserve interpretability.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means (standard deviations), and categorical variables as percentages. Between-sex differences in baseline characteristics were assessed using Welch’s t-test for approximately normally distributed variables, or the Mann–Whitney U test otherwise; categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test, as appropriate. Analyses were conducted in Python (v3.12.4) using SciPy (v1.11.4) and pyGAM (v0.9.1). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Generalized additive modeling of nonlinear associations and model assessment

GAMs were used to characterize potentially nonlinear associations between routine health checkup variables and subsequent claims-based healthcare costs. The primary objective was to examine interpretable association patterns rather than to develop a maximum-performance prediction model. GAMs extend generalized linear models by allowing flexible smooth functions of continuous predictors while retaining an interpretable additive structure [27]. Because claims points were right-skewed, a Gamma distribution with a log link was specified. Continuous predictors were modeled using penalized cubic regression splines with 5 basis functions per term (n_splines = 5). This basis dimension was selected after sensitivity analyses comparing n_splines = 5, 10, and 15. Generalized cross-validation (GCV) was prioritized as the main model selection criterion, with AIC examined as a supplementary criterion. Although AIC favored n_splines = 10 in some models, GCV more consistently supported n_splines = 5 across analyses. Therefore, n_splines = 5 was used in the primary analyses to reduce excessive curve complexity and improve interpretability.

Models were fitted to the overall sample and separately for men and women using the same predictor set; sex was handled via stratified models rather than being included as a covariate in the pooled model. To avoid data leakage during repeated split validation, person-year observations from the same individual were assigned to the same split using GroupShuffleSplit by individual ID, with 80% of individuals used for model fitting and 20% used for model assessment in each of 50 iterations. R2 was summarized across iterations as a supplementary measure of model fit and explanatory performance. For each smooth term, effective degrees of freedom (EDF) and P values were extracted to summarize the complexity and statistical evidence of the modeled association. EDF values close to 1 were interpreted as approximately linear, whereas larger EDF values suggested more complex nonlinear patterns. Partial dependence plots were used to visualize the shape of the associations. Permutation importance was used as a supplementary measure of relative variable contribution. Primary analysis of nonlinear associations using routine health checkup variables

Using Dataset A, the coefficient of determination (R2) was 0.06 ± 0.022 in the overall sample, 0.047 ± 0.045 in men, and 0.054 ± 0.029 in women. R2 was interpreted as a supplementary measure of model fit and explanatory performance. Partial dependence plots, EDFs, and P values for smooth terms were used to interpret the nonlinear association patterns. EDFs and P values for smooth terms in the primary models are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Permutation importance rankings are presented as supplementary indicators of relative variable contribution; in the overall model, HbA1c and age showed high relative contributions, whereas in men, waist circumference showed a high relative contribution (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figures 1 A and 1 C).

Nonlinear effects estimated by the GAMs were visualized using PDPs. PDPs for the overall model are shown in Figure 2, and sex-stratified PDPs are shown in Supplementary Figure 1. In the overall model, HbA1c exhibited a steeper rise in partial dependence at higher values, with an apparent inflection at approximately 6.0%. Partial dependence increased with age. In contrast, other variables, such as BMI, blood pressure, and lipid measures, were associated with relatively modest changes in PDPs.

In sex-stratified analyses of Dataset A, clear sex differences were observed in nonlinear patterns across several baseline indicators (Supplementary Figure 1). HbA1c showed a J-shaped pattern in both sexes, with a more pronounced increase at higher values. Age increased monotonically in both sexes. In men, BMI was largely flat, whereas WC showed a U-shaped association. In women, BMI and WC were relatively flat across the middle range and increased mainly at the upper end. DBP tended to decrease at higher values in men but was largely flat in women. TG were generally stable in men but showed a decreasing trend at higher values in women. HDL-C exhibited a peak in partial dependence at approximately 70 mg/dL in men and was largely flat in women.

Sensitivity analyses comparing n_splines = 5, 10, and 15 showed broadly similar qualitative association patterns across settings. AIC favored n_splines = 10 in some models, whereas GCV more consistently supported n_splines = 5 across analyses. Therefore, the revised primary analyses used n_splines = 5, and the interpretation focused on broad nonlinear association patterns rather than minor local fluctuations (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study used generalized additive models to characterize nonlinear and sex-specific associations between routine health checkup factors and subsequent claims-based healthcare costs. Partial dependence plots, EDFs, and P values were used to interpret the shape and statistical evidence of these associations, whereas permutation importance was used as a supplementary indicator of relative variable contribution. HbA1c and age showed consistent associations across groups, and HbA1c showed a J-shaped association with a steeper increase at higher values. Sex differences were also observed in both relative variable contributions and nonlinear shapes, with waist circumference showing a higher relative contribution in men.

The present findings should be interpreted in the context of population-level association modeling rather than individual-level prediction. Spline-based methods, including restricted cubic splines and generalized additive models, are useful for describing nonlinear exposure–outcome relationships in an interpretable manner. Previous Japanese studies have applied generalized additive models to health checkup or clinical data and demonstrated nonlinear associations in cardiometabolic and renal outcomes [16, 22]. In addition, claims-based cohort studies in Japan have shown the value of routinely collected administrative data for examining treatment patterns and healthcare-related outcomes in real-world settings [17]. The present study extends this line of work by using interpretable nonlinear models to examine how routine health checkup factors are associated with subsequent claims-based healthcare costs.

The consistent association of HbA1c with subsequent claims-based healthcare costs is clinically plausible. HbA1c directly informs treatment decisions [28, 29], and higher levels are likely to increase healthcare utilization through treatment intensification and more frequent visits and examinations, thereby increasing the number of claims points [30, 31]. Large claims-based studies have similarly reported lower diabetes-related and total healthcare costs among individuals with well-controlled HbA1c levels compared with those with higher HbA1c levels [30, 31]. In the PDPs, the association became steeper in the higher HbA1c range, suggesting a threshold-like increase in claims-based healthcare costs beyond a certain level.

Age also showed a consistent association, likely reflecting the cumulative burden of chronic conditions, multimorbidity, and increased risk of hospitalization with advancing age [32, 33]. Japanese insurer data also show clustering of multimorbidity among high-cost individuals [32], which is consistent with our findings. As age and HbA1c are routinely measured during Specific Health Checkups, our results suggest that these core factors related to subsequent claims-based healthcare costs can be captured without additional testing, supporting pragmatic population-level assessment within existing checkup programs.

The higher relative contribution of waist circumference in men may be explained by sex differences in fat distribution in this age group. Magnetic resonance imaging analyses from the UK Biobank indicate that men tend to have more visceral adipose tissue, whereas women tend to have more abdominal subcutaneous and gluteofemoral fat. This suggests that the same waist circumference may reflect different fat compartments according to sex [34]. In Japan, waist circumference cutoffs (85 cm in men and 90 cm in women) are used to screen for visceral adiposity, and women are considered to have more subcutaneous fat at comparable levels of visceral fat, resulting in a larger waist circumference for the same visceral fat area [35]. Moreover, reports suggest that men can have higher visceral-to-subcutaneous or visceral-to-peripheral adiposity ratios even when BMI or waist circumference are similar, implying that waist circumference may function more effectively as a proxy for visceral fat burden in men [36]. Consistently, the Framingham Heart Study concluded that waist circumference and BMI capture visceral fat–related risk reasonably well in men but less adequately in women, for whom computed tomography–measured visceral fat may provide a more precise risk assessment [37]. Altogether, these observations support the interpretation that waist circumference may carry more additional information for subsequent claims-based healthcare costs in men, whereas its added information may be smaller in women and become apparent primarily at extreme values. Nevertheless, visceral adiposity can still be an independent risk factor in women, and the use of indicators that directly reflect visceral fat may improve risk stratification in women [38].

In analyses of additional lipid- and adiposity-related indices, calculated total cholesterol and non-HDL cholesterol (lipid-related), and BMI, waist circumference, and FLI (adiposity-related) showed moderate association patterns, whereas adding AIP, AI, or VAI alone provided limited additional information beyond routine measures. AIP and AI were proposed as convenient atherogenic markers derived mainly from lipid ratios such as triglycerides and HDL cholesterol, and VAI was proposed to represent visceral adipose “function” using BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol. However, these indices are not generally positioned as primary treatment targets in major guidelines, and their values rarely serve as direct triggers for treatment intensification or follow-up scheduling [39]. Therefore, although they may summarize metabolic risk, they may not translate into differences in claims points over a 3–4-year horizon. Notably, in men, FLI showed a relatively clear association pattern in the partial dependence plots, suggesting that fatty liver-related metabolic profiles may be more closely related to subsequent claims-based healthcare costs than some other composite indices. FLI is a noninvasive fatty liver marker calculated from BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase and has been validated in Japanese populations [40]. Cohort studies using health checkup data have also reported that higher FLI predicts incident diabetes [41]. Furthermore, Japanese claims-based studies indicate substantial comorbidity burden, healthcare resource use, and costs among patients with NAFLD/NASH [42]. These findings suggest that the association of FLI in men may reflect clustering of metabolic abnormalities linked to hepatic steatosis and liver dysfunction, leading to increased visits, examinations, and treatment within a relatively short period [43]. The absence of clear associations for some composite indices should not be interpreted merely as a modeling failure. Rather, these findings suggest that some indices may provide limited additional information beyond routine health checkup variables for understanding subsequent claims-based healthcare costs in this population and time horizon.

This study has several limitations. First, the models showed limited explanatory performance, indicating that routine health checkup variables alone explained only a small proportion of the variation in subsequent claims-based healthcare costs. This is expected because claims points are influenced by many factors not captured in checkups, including comorbidities, medications, acute events, prior healthcare utilization, healthcare-seeking behaviors, healthcare system characteristics, and socioeconomic factors [18, 44, 45]. Therefore, the present models should be interpreted primarily as exploratory association models rather than as tools for precise individual-level cost prediction. At the same time, this limited explanatory performance is informative because it indicates the extent to which routinely available health checkup variables alone can and cannot account for future claims-based healthcare costs. Second, this was an observational study using routinely collected real-world data from a single municipality among adults aged 60–74 years. Therefore, the analyses were associational rather than causal, and generalizability to other regions, age groups, or insurance systems may be limited. In addition, claims points derived from insurance claims do not capture out-of-pocket payments, long-term care costs, or indirect costs. Covariates were also restricted to health checkup variables and derived indices, and important determinants such as medication details, comorbidity severity, longitudinal lifestyle changes, healthcare-seeking behavior, and socioeconomic background may not have been adequately captured [46, 47]. Analyses were further restricted to complete-case observations, and the number of individuals at extreme index ranges was limited, which may have influenced the estimation of nonlinear effects. Third, some individuals contributed more than one person-year observation, and within-person correlation may have influenced model-based uncertainty estimates. Although GroupShuffleSplit by individual ID was used to prevent data leakage between model fitting and model assessment, this approach did not directly model within-person correlation; therefore, the results should be interpreted with this data structure in mind. Finally, the exclusion of outcome outliers may have reduced the influence of high-cost individuals, who are particularly relevant in healthcare cost research. Therefore, findings related to the upper tail of the cost distribution should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should include external validation in other settings and integration of additional data sources, such as comorbidities, medications, healthcare utilization patterns, and socioeconomic indicators, to improve explanatory performance and generalizability.

Conclusions

Using routine health checkup data, this study characterized nonlinear and sex-specific associations between selected checkup factors and subsequent claims-based healthcare costs using generalized additive models. HbA1c and age showed consistent associations across groups, and waist circumference and FLI showed additional association patterns, particularly in men. These findings may inform population-level hypothesis generation and future indicator selection. However, routine checkup variables alone appear insufficient for precise individual-level prediction. External validation and integration of additional information on comorbidities, medications, healthcare utilization, and socioeconomic factors are warranted.
