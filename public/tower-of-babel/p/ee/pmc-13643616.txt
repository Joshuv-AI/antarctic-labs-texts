Hippo pathway disruption and YAP1 activation in anogenital papillary squamous intraepithelial lesion
Authors: Gloria Zhang, Karen L Talia, W Glenn McCluggage, Robert A Soslow, Bin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPapillary squamous intraepithelial lesion (PSIL), also known as papillary immature metaplasia, is a rare anogenital lesion associated with low‐risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. The molecular pathogenesis of PSIL remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the activation of YAP1, a Hippo pathway co‐activator, in PSIL and compare it to condyloma and papillary HSIL.Methods and resultsYAP1 activation was evaluated by immunohistochemistry using an antibody specific for activated/unphosphorylated YAP1 (act‐YAP1) in 36 PSIL cases, 20 condyloma and 2 papillary HSIL cases. HPV genotyping and chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) were performed to determine HPV status. Low‐risk HPV, predominantly HPV6, was detected in 78.6% of PSILs. CISH revealed a diffuse fine punctate distribution of low‐risk HPV E6/E7 transcripts in PSIL, distinct from the large focal clusters seen in condyloma, but similar to high‐risk HPV in HSIL. All PSILs showed non‐block p16 expression. Act‐YAP1 immunostaining demonstrated YAP1 activation in 86.1% of PSILs (72.2% diffuse, 13.9% focal), resembling the pattern in papillary HSIL but differing from condyloma, which exhibited nuclear act‐YAP1 only in basal and granular layers. No significant associations were found between YAP1 activation and HPV status or immunoprofiles of p16, p53, Rb1 and CK7.ConclusionsPSILs demonstrate prominent YAP1 activation, implicating Hippo pathway disruption as a potential driver of PSIL pathogenesis. These findings support a distinct molecular profile of PSIL and provide insight into the biological mechanisms underlying this rare lesion.Keywords: anogenital, cervix, Hippo signalling pathway, HPV, papillary immature metaplasia, papillary squamous intraepithelial lesion, YAP1

Introduction

Papillary immature metaplasia (PIM), also referred to as immature condyloma, was originally described as a distinctive exophytic lesion of the uterine cervix. 1 , 2 PIM is a rare lesion and is characterized by papillary growth composed of morphologically bland immature squamous cells, without obvious koilocytotic features, and is typically associated with low‐risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. 3 Although initially described in the cervix, similar lesions have since been identified in the vagina, vulva, anal canal and endometrium. 4 , 5 Recent studies, including our own, have challenged the interpretation of PIM as a reactive metaplastic process and instead proposed the term papillary squamous intraepithelial lesion (PSIL), reflecting its rapid growth, high Ki67 labelling index and frequent recurrence; hereafter the term PSIL is used in this study. 5 , 6

The molecular mechanisms underlying PSIL pathogenesis remain largely undefined. Yes‐associated protein 1 (YAP1) is a central downstream effector of the Hippo signalling pathway, which plays a critical role in regulating cell growth, differentiation, cell density and organ size. 7 The Hippo pathway is a highly conserved signalling cascade that maintains epithelial homeostasis by restricting cell proliferation and promoting differentiation. Under pathological conditions, YAP1 activity is tightly regulated through phosphorylation by upstream kinases such as LATS1/2. Phosphorylated YAP1 is retained in the cytoplasm via interaction with 14–3‐3 proteins and targeted for proteasomal degradation. When Hippo signalling is disrupted, YAP1 becomes dephosphorylated, translocated to the nucleus and activates TEAD‐dependent transcriptional programmes that promote proliferation and cellular survival. 8 , 9

Recent studies have demonstrated that high‐risk HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins can disrupt the Hippo signalling pathway, resulting in YAP1 activation and promoting cervical carcinogenesis. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Our prior work demonstrated that YAP1 is progressively activated in high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) and invasive squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the cervix and vulva, but largely inactive in low‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL/CIN1). 14 However, it is unknown whether Hippo‐YAP1 pathway disruption contributes to the tumorigenesis of low‐risk HPV‐associated exophytic lesions.

To address this question, we assessed YAP1 activation in a large cohort of PSIL cases and compared the findings with conventional condyloma acuminata and papillary HSIL, derived from the cervix and non‐cervical sites. We further examined the relationship between YAP1 activation, HPV genotype and immunohistochemical profiles of p16, p53, Rb1, CK7 and Ki67.

Materials and methods

Study Cases and Clinicopathologic Data

This study was approved by our hospital's institutional review boards. A retrospective search of the hospital pathology database identified cases diagnosed as condyloma, PIM, immature condyloma, papillary squamous intraepithelial lesion, papillary HSIL and HSIL with papillary features of the anogenital sites. Clinical and pathological data, including patient age, specimen type, prior cervical cytology diagnosis, HPV testing results and immunohistochemical studies performed at diagnosis, were retrieved from electronic medical records. All haematoxylin‐and‐eosin–stained slides were reviewed to confirm the diagnoses. Representative tissue blocks with optimal preservation and adequate lesional tissue were selected for additional studies.

Immunohistochemical Studies

Immunohistochemistry was performed on 4‐μm‐thick formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded sections using an automated staining platform (Ventana Benchmark Ultrasensitive, Ventana, Tucson, AZ) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primary antibodies were activated YAP1 (clone EPR19812, rabbit anti‐human monoclonal, 1:3000, Abcam), p16 (clone E6H4, mouse monoclonal, predilute, VMS), p53 (clone DO7, 1:20, DAKO), Rb1 (clone G3‐245, 1:140, BD Biosciences), CK7 (OV‐TL 12/30, 1:40, Agilent Dako) and Ki67 (30‐9, predilute, Ventana), followed by detection with a DAB polymer‐based detection kit. Appropriate positive and negative controls were included.

The activated YAP1 (act‐YAP1) antibody specifically recognizes unphosphorylated YAP1, which is released from the cytoplasm and translocated into the nucleus. Accordingly, only nuclear staining—regardless of any concurrent cytoplasmic staining—was considered positive. Act‐YAP1 immunostaining was semi‐quantitatively graded according to the percentage of nuclear expression: <15%, 15%–49%, 50%–75% and >75%. For descriptive purposes, nuclear labelling of less than 15% was defined as limited activation, 15%–49% as focal activation and greater than 50% as diffuse activation. Staining intensity was classified as weak (1+), moderate (2+) or strong (3+). The distribution of staining across epithelial layers was also documented. p16 immunostaining was categorized as negative, focal/patchy or block‐type (diffuse). Nuclear staining for p53 and Rb1 was considered positive and evaluated for both intensity and distribution. CK7 cytoplasmic staining was described as negative, focal or diffuse. All immunostained slides were independently reviewed by two authors (GZ and BY), with any discrepancies resolved by consensus.

HPV Testing and Genotyping

HPV E6/E7 mRNA expression was assessed using the RNAscope chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) assay. The presence of E6/E7 transcripts was evaluated with RNAscope® probes (ACD, Newark, CA) on an automated Leica Biosystems BOND‐III stainer (Leica Biosystems, Richmond, IL), following the manufacturer's protocol. The probe sets detect E6/E7 mRNA from six low‐risk HPV types (6, 11, 40, 42, 43 and 44) and 18 high‐risk HPV types (16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73 and 82). HPV genotype analysis was further conducted on selected cases with available blocks and sufficient unstained tissue sections. HPV genotyping was performed using the Allplex HPV28 Detection Assay (Seegene Inc., London, UK) on whole tissue sections. The Allplex HPV28 detection is a multiplex reverse transcription‐PCR (RT‐PCR) assay that enables the simultaneous amplification and detection of target nucleic acids from 19 high‐risk HPV types (16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 69, 73 and 82) and 9 low‐risk HPV types (6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61 and 70), along with an internal control. Cobas HPV testing (Roche Diagnostics USA, Indianapolis, IN) was performed on cervical cytological materials collected 3–8 weeks before cervical and vaginal biopsies or resections. The Cobas HPV testing detects HPV16, HPV18 and 12 additional high‐risk HPV types (31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68).

Statistical Analysis

Association between two categorical or nominal variables was evaluated using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate. A two‐sided P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological Characteristics

The study cohort included 36 cases of PSIL from 28 patients, comprising 28 primary and 8 recurrent lesions. Of the patients, 25 were females and 3 were males, ranging in age from 27 to 85 years (mean age: 53 years). PSILs were most located in the cervix (21 cases), with additional cases in the vagina (6), anal canal (6), endometrium (2) and vulva (1). Clinically, all lesions present as exophytic, polypoid or papillary masses, measuring 1.2–5 cm at their greatest dimension. Specimens included 12 biopsies and 24 resections, comprising 13 excisions, 9 LEEP/conizations and 2 hysterectomies.

The study included 20 cases of condyloma acuminata from the vagina (3), vulva (13) and anus (4). All condyloma cases demonstrated exophytic growth, ranging from 0.6 to 4.5 cm in greatest dimension. Microscopically, condylomas exhibited broad papillary architecture with squamous maturation and prominent koilocytosis. None of the condyloma cases showed a pushing or infiltrating base. Additionally, two cases of cervical HSIL lesions were included, both showing papillary architecture with marked nuclear atypia, including enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei, irregular nuclear membranes, increased nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratio and frequent mitoses.

HPV Testing and Genotyping

The HPV CISH assay, designed to detect E6 and E7 mRNA from 6 low‐risk and 18 high‐risk HPV types, was performed on all cases of papillary HSIL, condyloma acuminatum and PSIL. All 20 condyloma cases were positive for low‐risk HPV and negative for high‐risk types, with large clusters of E6/E7 transcripts focally present in surface koilocytotic cells (Figure 1). Both papillary HSIL cases were positive for high‐risk HPV but negative for low‐risk HPV, with CISH revealing random fine dot signals of high‐risk HPV E6/E7 transcripts (Figure 2). Among PSILs, low‐risk HPV was detected in 78.6% (22/28) of cases; none were positive for high‐risk HPV and 21.4% (6/28) were negative for both (Table 1). Notably, PSILs showed a distinctive fine punctate distribution of low‐risk HPV E6/E7 transcripts throughout the lesion, resembling the pattern in HSIL but clearly distinct from the clustered pattern seen in condylomas (Figure 3).

Nineteen PSIL cases underwent additional HPV genotyping using the Allplex assay. HPV6 was identified in 10 cases, while high‐risk HPV (HPV16, HPV53, HPV73) was present in six—three of these with concurrent HPV6 and three with only high‐risk HPV. Immunohistochemistry of p16 and Act‐YAP1 in Condyloma Acuminata

All 20 cases of condyloma acuminatum showed non‐block‐type p16 immunostaining, with six cases negative and 14 cases exhibiting only focal cytoplasmic staining. Act‐YAP1 immunohistochemistry in condylomas displayed a limited staining pattern, predominantly localized to the basal and granular layers in all cases. This peculiar basal and granular layer pattern closely mirrored that of adjacent non‐neoplastic squamous epithelium, though staining intensity was greater in granular layers in condylomas (Figure 1 and Table 2). Most intermediate layers containing koilocytes were negative for act‐YAP1. One vulvar condyloma showed weak extension of staining into the intermediate layers and one vaginal condyloma was near negative for act‐YAP1.

Immunohistochemistry of p16 and Act‐YAP1 in Papillary HSIL

Both cases of HSIL with papillary features exhibited block‐type p16 immunostaining. Based on percentage of nuclear labelling, act‐YAP1 immunohistochemistry was categorized as limited activation (<15%), focal activation (15–49%) and diffuse activation (>50%). Immunohistochemical study for act‐YAP1 revealed prominent diffuse activation in both cases (Figure 2 and Table 2).

Immunohistochemistry for p16, p53, Rb1, CK7 and Ki67 in PSIL

All 36 PSIL cases underwent p16 immunohistochemistry: 17 were negative, and 11 showed only focal cytoplasmic staining. Importantly, none exhibited block‐type p16 staining. In 22 PSIL cases, further immunohistochemical analysis for p53, Rb1, CK7 and Ki67 was performed. Most demonstrated increased wild‐type p53 labelling with variable nuclear staining intensity, with the strongest staining in the basal and parabasal layers (Figure 2). Unlike HSIL, PSIL lesions did not display mid‐epithelial basal sparing or null‐like wild‐type p53 staining patterns. Rb1 immunostaining was also increased in PSIL compared to non‐neoplastic squamous epithelium, mirroring the p53 pattern. CK7 staining was negative in 14 cases and focally positive in 8, with no diffuse full‐thickness CK7 expression observed. The Ki67 proliferation index ranged from 20% to 75% (mean 55%) and 68% of cases (15/22) had a Ki67 labelling index greater than 50%. Ki67 positivity was mainly confined to the lower half to two‐thirds of the lesional epithelium.

YAP1 Activation in Papillary Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion

Act‐YAP1 immunohistochemistry was performed on all 36 PSIL cases, revealing YAP1 activation in 86.1% (31/36) of cases. Of these, 72.2% (26/36) demonstrated diffuse staining, 13.9% (5/36) had focal staining and 13.9% (5/36) exhibited limited staining patterns (Table 2). In cases with diffuse staining, act‐YAP1 nuclear labelling extended from the basal layer through to the intermediate and superficial layers of the lesional epithelium (Figures 3 and 4).

Correlation of YAP1 Activation in PSIL with HPV Status and Other Biomarkers

Among PSILs, YAP1 activation was observed in 90% (27/30) of cases positive for low‐risk HPV, with 11.1% (3/27) showing focal and 88.9% (24/27) showing diffuse staining patterns. In HPV‐negative cases, act‐YAP nuclear expression was detected in 66.7% (4/6), comprising 33.3% focal and 66.7% diffuse staining (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference in act‐YAP1 expression between HPV‐positive and HPV‐negative PSILs (P = 0.19). Additionally, no significant correlation was found between YAP1 activation and p16 staining patterns (P = 0.96), nor with the immunohistochemical profiles of p53, Rb1, CK7 or Ki67.

YAP1 Activation in Primary, Recurrent and Site‐Variant PSIL

Of the PSIL cases analysed, 21 originated from the cervix and 15 from non‐cervical sites. Histopathological examination revealed morphological variations—such as delicate versus broad papillae and varying degrees of surface maturation—yet all PSIL lesions were consistently characterized by a predominance of immature squamous cells, irrespective of anatomical site. Act‐YAP1 expression was uniformly elevated across all locations, including the cervix, vagina, vulva, anal canal and endometrium (Figure 4). Focal and diffuse YAP1 activation occurred in 85.7% (18/21) of cervical and 86.7% (13/15) of non‐cervical PSILs, with no statistically significant difference between these groups (P = 0.98). Among the eight recurrent PSIL lesions from four patients (three HPV‐positive, one HPV‐negative), one patient experienced three recurrences of anal PSIL within 30 months. Both primary and recurrent PSILs displayed comparable Act‐YAP1 staining patterns, although staining intensity was generally greater in recurrent lesions (Figure 5).

Two cases featured PSIL concurrent with high‐grade lesions: one anal PSIL with simultaneous HSIL and one cervical PSIL adjacent to AIS. In both cases, low‐risk HPV was detected exclusively in the PSIL, while high‐risk HPV was found only in the HSIL or AIS components. Discussion

Papillary immature metaplasia (PIM) is a distinctive anogenital epithelial proliferation characterized by exophytic papillary architecture, cytologic immaturity, high proliferative activity and a tendency to recur. Recent evidence indicates that PIM represents a true low‐risk HPV‐associated squamous intraepithelial lesion rather than a metaplastic process. Its papillary growth pattern is distinct from both high‐risk HPV‐associated LSIL/CIN1 and low‐risk HPV‐associated conventional condyloma acuminatum. 1 , 2 , 3 Accordingly, the term papillary squamous intraepithelial lesion (PSIL) has been proposed in line with LAST guidelines. 5 , 6 , 15 Although initially described in the cervix, PSIL has also been identified in the vagina, vulva, anal canal and endometrium. 5 , 6 , 16 Despite its low‐grade histopathological appearance, PSIL is a rapidly growing lesion with a high Ki67 labelling index, which can easily lead to misdiagnosis as papillary HSIL by pathologists. Our study found that a Ki67 labelling index greater than 50% was observed in more than 68% of PSIL lesions, a rate significantly higher than the approximately 25% seen in LSIL and rarely observed in condyloma. 17 Previously, we demonstrated that PSIL is a highly recurrent lesion (43%) and is often clinically mistaken as malignancy. 5 One anal PSIL case included in this study was initially diagnosed as papillary squamous cell carcinoma and recurred three times within 30 months following resection and radiation therapy. Despite its established association with low‐risk HPV, the molecular mechanisms underlying PSIL development remain largely undefined. In our previous research, we showed that the YAP1 oncoprotein, a Hippo pathway co‐activator, is activated in high‐risk HPV‐associated HSIL but largely inactive in LSIL/CIN1. 14 In this study, we further investigate YAP1 activation in low‐risk HPV‐associated PSIL, comparing our findings with those in condyloma acuminatum and papillary HSIL.

Approximately 79% of PSILs in our study harboured low‐risk HPV, predominantly HPV6. Previous reports have shown low‐risk HPV detection rates ranging from 70% to 100%, likely reflecting variations in methodological sensitivity, with the highest rates observed using PCR assays. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 18 In our cohort, high‐risk HPV was detected by PCR‐based genotyping in a small subset of PSILs, either alone or in combination with HPV6. However, high‐risk HPV was not confirmed by the CISH assay, suggesting possible contamination during tissue preparation for PCR, as recut sections lacked contamination prevention. Supporting this interpretation, in cases with concurrent PSIL and HSIL or AIS on the same slide, high‐risk HPV was identified exclusively in HSIL or AIS, but not in the PSIL components. Consistently, HPV CISH revealed a fine punctate distribution of low‐risk HPV E6/E7 transcripts throughout the PSIL epithelium, which was similar to the patterns in HSIL, but distinct from the large focal clustered signals seen mainly in superficial koilocytes of condylomas. These findings suggest that viral transcriptional regulation in PSIL may differ from productive episomal infection characteristic of conventional condyloma. Whether this reflects altered viral replication, early integration events or changes in viral‐host interaction remains to be elucidated.

The molecular mechanism driving PSIL development is largely unknown. Elucidating its pathogenesis is essential to better define this lesion, which has often puzzled pathologists and clinicians. In high‐risk HPV‐associated cervical carcinogenesis, E6 and E7 oncoproteins classically promote proteasomal degradation of tumour suppressors pRb and p53. 19 In our study, we examined the expression of p53, Rb1 and p16 in PSIL and observed a marked increase in wild‐type p53 labelling, particularly in the basal and parabasal layers. This pattern contrasts with the mid‐epithelial basal sparing or null‐like patterns typically seen in high‐risk HPV‐associated HSIL. 20 Wild‐type p53 is normally unstable and restricted to the basal layer in normal squamous epithelium, with a short physiological half‐life of 5 to 20 minutes, 21 , 22 but in PSIL its stabilization suggests an extended half‐life. This finding aligns with recent in vitro studies showing that low‐risk HPV E6 can trap and stabilize p53 without triggering its degradation—mechanism distinct from high‐risk HPV. 23 Furthermore, Rb1 protein was significantly elevated in PSIL compared to condyloma and non‐neoplastic squamous epithelium. Notably, none of the PSIL lesions showed block‐type p16 staining, indicating that, despite Rb1 stabilization, the pRb‐p16 regulatory pathway appears functionally intact.

The Hippo signalling pathway is essential for regulating cell proliferation, differentiation and tissue homeostasis. Disruption of this pathway by high‐risk HPV E6 and E7, leading to YAP1 oncoprotein activation, is well established in experimental models. 10 , 11 , 13 , 24 Our previous research showed that YAP1 is primarily inactivated in LSIL/CIN1, but becomes progressively activated, from focal to diffuse patterns in flat HSIL and squamous cell carcinoma. 14 In this study, we examined two papillary HSIL cases, due to their morphological overlap with PSIL, and confirmed diffuse nuclear act‐YAP1 staining with block‐type p16 immunoreactivity, consistent with our early findings. Notably, after observing similar fine punctate E6/E7 transcript signals in both PSIL and HSIL, we extended our investigation of YAP1 activation in PSIL lesions. We found that approximately 86% of PSILs exhibited YAP1 activation, mainly characterized by diffuse and intensified nuclear labelling from the basal to superficial layers. Interestingly, the immunostaining pattern for activated YAP1 in PSIL closely resembles that seen in HSIL. These results suggest that, while HSIL is associated with simultaneous inactivation of the pRb and p53 pathways and Hippo‐YAP1 activation, PSIL development involves Hippo‐YAP1 pathway disruption without inactivation of pRb and p53 tumour suppressors.

In contrast, the pattern of activated YAP1 expression in PSIL differs markedly from that seen in condylomas. Nearly all condylomas showed only limited Hippo‐YAP1 pathway disruption, with act‐YAP1 nuclear staining mainly restricted to the basal and granular layers and absent in the intermediate layers and koilocytes. This pronounced difference in YAP1 activation likely underlies the morphological distinction between these two low‐risk HPV‐associated exophytic lesions. Considering the Hippo pathway's key role in squamous differentiation and maturation, 25 , 26 our findings suggest that the limited YAP1 activation in condylomas supports proliferation while maintaining balanced differentiation. In contrast, the extensive YAP1 activation and Hippo pathway disruption in PSIL may drive the heightened proliferative activity and impaired differentiation with cytomorphologic immaturity characteristic of these lesions.

The discovery of YAP1 activation in PSIL was unexpected, as previous studies have mainly examined YAP1 in the context of high‐risk HPV. 11 , 13 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 The mechanisms underlying low‐risk HPV‐driven lesions remain poorly understood, partly because low‐risk HPV is generally thought to infrequently integrate into the host genome, a step considered crucial for upregulating viral oncoproteins like E6 and E7. However, our finding of fine punctate E6/E7 transcript signals in PSIL, similar to those in HSIL, suggests that viral integration may occur in PSIL as well. Supporting this, a recent study of recurrent respiratory papillomatosis, a disease associated with HPV11 and sharing morphological and clinical features with PSIL, demonstrated integration of low‐risk HPV into the host AKR1C3 gene, affecting key regulatory pathways including Hippo‐YAP1. 31 These insights raise the possibility that PSIL represents a distinct biological state in which low‐risk HPV disrupts cellular regulatory mechanisms, particularly the Hippo‐YAP1 pathway.

PTPN14 (Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Non‐Receptor Type 14) is a tumour suppressor crucial for regulating cell proliferation and differentiation. High‐risk HPV E7 protein can bind to the host E6AP (an E3 ubiquitin ligase) and target PTPN14 for degradation, which leads to YAP1 activation, impaired keratinocyte differentiation and promotes cell immortalization and cervical carcinogenesis. 10 , 32 , 33 , 34 However, while low‐risk HPV E7 can also bind PTPN14, it does not induce its degradation. 32 Another key regulator of the Hippo‐YAP pathway is NHERF1 (Na+/H+ exchanger regulatory factor 1), which normally promotes YAP1 phosphorylation and inactivation, sequestering YAP1 in the cytoplasm. Downregulation or degradation of NHERF1 results in increased YAP1 activation and its nuclear translocation, driving epithelial proliferation and blocking differentiation. 35 Recent evidence shows that NHERF1 is the main cellular target of low‐risk HPV E6, 36 which binds E6AP to form an E6‐E6AP complex. Unlike high‐risk HPV, this complex does not degrade wild‐type p53, but instead targets NHERF1 for degradation, leading to YAP1 activation and enhanced cell proliferation with impaired differentiation. 36 Whether YAP1 activation in PSIL is primarily driven by NHERF1 degradation by low‐risk HPV E6 remains to be clarified and warrants further investigation.

We systemically examined the relationship between YAP1 activation and clinicopathological factors, including disease location, recurrence and expression of other biomarkers. Our analysis revealed no significant differences in YAP1 activation between cervical and non‐cervical PSILs, between primary and recurrent lesions or with respect to HPV status. Moreover, YAP1 activation showed no correlation with the immunostaining patterns of p16, p53, Rb1, CK7 or Ki67. These findings suggest that YAP1 activation is a potential key driver in PSIL pathogenesis and operates independently of the canonical p53 and pRb‐p16 tumour suppressor pathways. The presence of YAP1 activation alongside intact p53 and Rb1 further distinguishes PSIL from high‐risk HPV‐associated HSIL. Integrating our current results with prior data on CIN lesions and with existing literature, 14 we propose a biologically stratified model on HPV‐associated exophytic squamous intraepithelial lesions, such as PSIL, condyloma and papillary HSIL (Table 3). In HSIL, malignant progression requires both YAP1 activation and canonical inactivation of p53 and pRb tumour suppressors. In contrast, in PSIL, YAP1 activation alone seems sufficient to drive epithelial proliferation without p53 and pRb tumour suppressor pathway disruption, potentially explaining its non‐invasive but highly proliferative behaviour. For LSILs with HPV episomal state, such as CIN1/AIN1/VaIN1 and condyloma, limited YAP1 activation likely supports proliferation while preserving differentiation and squamous maturation. This biological framework positions YAP1 activation as a central molecular event in HPV‐driven epithelial proliferation and underscores how the degree and context of Hippo pathway disruption shape the morphological phenotype and biological behaviour of these intraepithelial lesions.

While our findings suggest that YAP1 oncoprotein activation may play a pivotal role in PSIL pathogenesis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not conduct functional analyses or examine the involvement of cellular proteins, such as NHERF1 and PTPN14 and downstream targets like TEAD, in PSILs. Second, although we observed a diffuse fine‐dot distribution of low‐risk HPV E6/E7 transcripts in PSIL, it remains unclear whether this pattern signifies integration of low‐risk HPV into the host genome. Third, the clinical utility of act‐YAP1 immunohistochemistry remains limited for differential diagnoses. In distinguishing condyloma from PSIL, mature condyloma and immature PSIL display clear morphological differences. HPV CISH is instrumental in highlighting the contrasting distributions of low‐risk HPV E6/E7 transcripts in PSIL versus condyloma. Notably, the difference in act‐YAP1 expression between condyloma and PSIL may aid diagnosis in challenging cases. Although PSIL and papillary HSIL share certain morphological features and exhibit high KI67 labelling, careful microscopic examination reveals that PSIL consists of bland, homogeneous immature cells, while papillary HSIL is characterized by dysplastic, high‐grade nuclear features. Block‐type p16 immunostaining and detection of high‐risk HPV in papillary HSIL further distinguish it from PSIL lesions.

In summary, our study demonstrates that YAP1 activation is present in the majority of PSIL lesions, occurring independently of the canonical inactivation of p53 or pRb tumour suppressors. These results indicate that disruption of the Hippo‐YAP1 pathway through YAP1 activation may contribute to the pathogenesis of low‐risk HPV‐associated PSIL. Improved understanding of these mechanisms could facilitate more accurate diagnostic distinction from papillary HSIL and conventional condyloma, and may eventually support the exploration of YAP1 as a therapeutic target in the management of this rare but frequently recurring HPV‐associated lesion.
