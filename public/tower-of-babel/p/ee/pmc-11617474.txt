B-Cell Epigenetic Modulation of IgA Response by 5-Azacytidine and IgA Nephropathy
Authors: Shanshan Yu, Xiang Li, Ting Wang, Jingyi Li, Hongzhi Li, Ying Xu, Yanling Hu, Fubin Zhu, Jinwei Wang, Tianhe Wang, Bin Zhu, Xu-jie Zhou, Hong Zhang, Jicheng Lv, Jonathan Barratt, Binghai Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

IgA nephropathy is the commonest pattern of GN in the world and commonly leads to kidney failure.1 There is a well-described, but poorly understood, association between dysregulated mucosal IgA synthesis and IgA nephropathy.2 Changes in the IgA1 hinge region O-glycans have been widely reported to have pathogenic significance in IgA nephropathy,3,4 and increased levels of this galactose deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) are a central feature of the multihit hypothesis of the pathogenesis of IgA nephropathy.5,6 A number of genome-wide association studies have identified multiple risk alleles that congregate in pathways associated with maintenance of the intestinal epithelial barrier, mucosal IgA synthesis, and responses to mucosal pathogens.7 Simultaneously, multiple lines of evidence suggest that a significant proportion of mesangial IgA is derived from the gut-associated lymphoid tissue.8 Notably, recent phase 2 and 3 studies of Nefecon, a targeted-release formulation of budesonide designed to deliver the potent corticosteroid budesonide to the Peyer's patches-rich terminal ileum, have reported positive results, reinforcing the importance of a gut–kidney axis in the development and progression of IgA nephropathy.9,10 However, the exact molecular mechanisms responsible for the development of IgA nephropathy remain unclear, especially the role of epigenetic factors.

There is an increasing appreciation of the importance of epigenetic modification in the pathogenesis of kidney diseases, including IgA nephropathy.11 In particular, dysregulation of microRNA (miR), small non-coding RNAs capable of preventing translation of mRNA to protein, have been shown to play critical roles in IgA nephropathy, both in determining immune responses and likelihood of loss of kidney function.12 Previously, we have shown that miR-23b plays an important role in determining serum total and polymeric IgA levels and that dysregulated miR-23b synthesis results in both an IgA nephropathy–like phenotype in miR-23b−/− mice and potentiates generic downstream pathways of kidney inflammation and fibrosis in miR-23b−/− and db/db mice.13,14 In this study, miR-23b–mediated downregulation of serum IgA responses seemed to involve a direct effect on mucosal IgA synthesis, possibly mediated by an action on activation-induced cytidine deaminase.

A characteristic feature of mucosally derived IgA1 is a reduction in the galactose content of the IgA1 hinge region O-glycans, similar to that seen in IgA nephropathy.15 Hinge region O-galactosylation is under genetic control, and following class switch recombination (CSR) to IgA1 is regulated by the cytokine milieu within the local microenvironment.16–18 Ig CSR is primarily regulated by AID, a member of the AID/apolipoprotein B mRNA-editing catalytic polypeptide-like family, that is also important in somatic hypermutation and gene conversion.19 AID shuttles between the nucleus and the cytoplasm and is required for DNA cleavage of S regions in CSR.20,21 AID is itself under epigenetic regulation,22 and there is emerging evidence that AID-induced CSR is likely modulated by 5-methylcytosine (5mC) RNA modification.23

The aims of this study therefore were to determine how miR23-b regulates serum levels of IgA and IgA immune complexes and whether epigenetic modulation of IgA CSR determines serum and mucosal IgA responses and the likelihood of developing IgA nephropathy.

Methods

Full details of materials and methods, including cell culture experiments, microscopy and RNA analysis, Western blotting, and statistical analysis, are detailed in the Supplemental Methods.

Human Samples

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Peking University, Beijing. Peripheral blood was collected after obtaining written informed consent from patients with IgA nephropathy attending the First Affiliated Hospital of Peking University. The clinical characteristics of the patients with IgA nephropathy included in this study are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. Patients with IgA nephropathy with rapidly progressive/crescentic GN were excluded from the study. In all cases, no patient was receiving immunosuppression at the time of blood collection.

Animal Studies

miR-23b−/− mice were generated by the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University, China, using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat-Cas9 technology as reported previously.13 Aid−/− mice were purchased from the Cyagen Company (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Trdmt1−/− mice were from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and kindly provided by Dr. Fei Sun, Zhejiang University, China. Aid and Transfer RNA Aspartic Acid Methyltransferase 1 (Trdmt1) miR-23b double knockout mice were generated by mating miR-23b−/− with Trdmt1−/− or Aid−/− mice, respectively, and the double knockout was confirmed by genotyping (Supplemental Figures 5E and 9F). A second IgA nephropathy–like mouse model was induced in Kawasaki disease mice by injection of Lactobacillus casei (Chinese Industrial Microbial Culture Collection Center) cell wall extract (LCWE) at a dose of 5 μg/ml for 5 consecutive days as previously reported.24

For 5-azacytidine (5-Aza) injection of miR-23b−/− mice, 5-Aza (Sigma, Beijing, China) was administered by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg every other day (Figure 1A). For 5-Aza treatment of Kawasaki mice, 5-Aza was administered by intraperitoneal injection at 2 or 2.5 months, at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg every other day according to the protocol (Figure 2A). The dose of 5-Aza for animal injection was mainly based on other's and our previous report.25,26 For 5-methylcytidine injection, wild-type and miR-23b−/− mice were injected with 5-methylcytidine at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg every day from 2 months of age for 1 month. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Laboratory Animal Care and Welfare, Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals, Hangzhou Medical College (approval number: ZJCLA-IACUC-20040082). All mice were housed in sterile individual ventilated cages in specific pathogen-free conditions and fed water and sterile growth and reproduction food (KeAoXieLi Feed mice [littermates] were used in the studies unless otherwise stated). At the end of each study, the mice were sacrificed, and kidney, bone marrow, gastrointestinal tract, and serum samples were collected and stored at −80°C. The sample sizes (number of experimental units in each group) were calculated according to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guideline (Supplemental Methods and Supplemental Tables 2 and 3).

ELISA

ELISAs for human IgA1 (Abcam, Shanghai, China), murine IgA (GeneTex), IgG and IgM (Abcam, Shanghai, China), and IgG1 or IgG2b (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were performed as previously described.13 The data were normalized to human IgA1 (Abcam, Shanghai, China) and murine IgA/G/M (Southern Biotech, Birmingham) protein by a standard curve method to quantify concentration. ELISAs for total 5mC (Abcam, Shanghai, China) content of 500 ng of DNA and RNA were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. ELISA to assess the degree of IgA glycosylation was performed using the lectin Ricinus communis agglutinin I (RCA-I, Vector Laboratories). Plates were coated with anti-mouse IgA antibody (Gentex); diluted sera were added to each well. Captured IgA was probed with biotinylated RCA-I. After washing, avidin–horseradish peroxidase conjugate (ExtrAvidin; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well. The development reaction was stopped by adding H2SO4, and the plate read at 450 nm. Data were expressed as optical density values.

Statistical Analyses

Data are expressed as mean±SEM and compared using one-way ANOVA, t test, or Dunnett multiple comparisons test using Prism (version 8; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). A P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

miR-23b Downregulated Expression of Trdmt1 and Reduced 5mC RNA Modification and IgA Synthesis in B Cells

Bioinformatic analysis revealed that the 5′ end of the mature miR-23b had perfect complementarity with seven nucleotides of the 3′-untranslated region of Trdmt1 (Supplemental Figure 1A), and binding of miR-23b to the 3′-untranslated region of Trdmt1 was confirmed with a luciferase assay (Figure 3, A and B). Correspondingly, Trdmt1 mRNA levels were higher in bone marrow B cells of miR-23b−/− mice (Figure 3C and Supplemental Table 5). Consistent with an increase in Trdmt1, levels of 5mC (m5C) RNA were higher in miR-23b−/− bone marrow B cells (Figure 3D). These changes were mirrored in the gut (small intestine, ileocecum, and colon) and kidneys of 5-month-old miR-23b−/− mice (Figure 3E and Supplemental Figure 1B). Levels of TRDMT1 and m5C RNA seemed to be higher in B cells in the small intestine (Figure 3F) and nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (Supplemental Figure 2A) of miR-23b−/− mice. By contrast, levels of the other principal m5C-RNA methyltransferases, Nsun2 and Nsun6 mRNA, were unaltered in bone marrow B cells from miR-23b−/− mice (Supplemental Figure 2, B and C).

To determine the potential effect of excessive m5C RNA modification on IgA synthesis, the IgA1-producing human DAKIKI cell line was exposed to 5-Aza or decitabine (5-Aza-2′-deoxycytidine, Supplemental Figure 3A), which inhibit m5C RNA and DNA modification, respectively. Inhibition of m5C RNA modification with 5-Aza decreased DAKIKI cell IgA1 synthesis (Figure 3G and Supplemental Figure 3, B and C), whereas there was no change with 5-Aza-2′-deoxycytidine (Supplemental Figure 3D). By contrast, DAKIKI cell IgA1 synthesis was significantly higher after exposure to 5-methylcytidine, which increases levels of m5C RNA (Figure 3H and Supplemental Figure 3E). While 5-Aza exposure reduced TRDMT1 expression, NSUN2/6 expression was unaffected (Figure 3I and Supplemental Table 4), implying that the reduction in m5C RNA modification directly reduces the activity of TRDMT1, which further reduces m5C RNA modification.

Consistent with these observations, B-cell levels of m5C RNA and Trdmt1 mRNA strongly correlated with serum IgA levels in miR-23b−/− mice (Figure 3, J and K). There was no correlation with levels of Nsun2 and Nsun6 mRNA (Supplemental Figure 3, F and G).

Inhibition of 5mC RNA Modification Ameliorated Progression of an IgA Nephropathy–Like Kidney Disease in miR-23b−/− Mice

To determine whether inhibition of m5C RNA modification could ameliorate the elevated levels of serum IgA, IgA complexes, and the IgA nephropathy–like phenotype of miR-23b−/− mice, 5-month-old miR-23b−/− mice were administered 5-Aza for 3 months (Figure 1A). Serum IgA levels were reduced in 5-Aza–treated miR-23b−/− mice compared with nontreated miR-23b−/− mice (Figure 1B); however, there was no change in the pattern of glycosylation of IgA using the lectin RCA-I, which binds to galactose and N-acetylgalactosamine residues, between wild-type (WT) and miR-23b−/− or 5-Aza–treated miR-23b−/− mice (Supplemental Figure 3H). There were no changes in serum IgG and IgM levels (Figure 1, C and D). Notably, there was also a decrease in the polymeric IgA fraction in the serum of miR-23b−/− mice exposed to 5-Aza (Figure 1, E–G). There was no glomerular IgA or C3 deposition in 5-Aza–treated miR-23b−/− mice, while glomerular deposition of IgG and IgM was unchanged compared with WT (Figure 1H [first to fourth panel]).

Inhibition of RNA 5mC Modification with 5-Aza Was Able to Prevent or Ameliorate Progression of an IgA Nephropathy–Like Kidney Disease in Kawasaki Disease Mice

To confirm the observations in miR-23b−/− mice, 5-Aza was administered in an alternative model of IgA vasculitis and IgA nephropathy, which is mediated through the gut–kidney axis.24 LCWE-exposed mice were treated with either a prophylactic or therapeutic 5-Aza treatment regimen (Figure 2A). Whether administered early or late, treatment with 5-Aza resulted in significantly reduced serum levels of total and polymeric IgA compared with nontreated mice (Figure 2, B and C). There was no effect on serum IgM levels, whereas a significant decrease in serum IgG levels in the 5-Aza prevention treatment group was observed (Figure 2, D and E). Significantly, there was no mesangial IgA or C3 deposition seen in 5-Aza–treated Kawasaki disease mice. There were also no differences in mesangial IgG and IgM staining between WT and 5-Aza–treated mice (Figure 2F). Levels of intestinal m5C RNA were significantly reduced, and there was a trend to lower levels of intestinal TRDMT1 in 5-Aza–treated mice compared with LCWE-exposed mice (Figure 2, G and H).

Enhanced 5mC RNA Modification Resulted in an Exaggerated IgA Nephropathy–Like Phenotype in miR-23b−/− Mice

To confirm that RNA m5C modification contributes to the development of IgA nephropathy–like kidney disease in miR-23b−/− mice, 2-month-old miR-23b−/− and WT mice were administered 5-methylcytidine for 1 month before sacrifice (Figure 4A). While treatment with 5-methylcytidine did not result in any significant changes in total serum Ig levels (Figure 4, B–D), sustained increases in serum polymeric IgA levels were observed in both WT and miR-23b−/− mice exposed to 5-methylcytidine (Figure 4, E–G). Exposure to 5-methylcytidine resulted in more extensive mesangial IgA and C3 deposition in both WT and miR-23b−/− mice, supporting a contribution of RNA m5C modification to the development of IgA nephropathy (Figure 4H).

TRDMT1 Played a Key Role in the Development of an IgA Nephropathy–Like Kidney Disease in miR-23b−/−Mice

At 7 months of age, Trdmt1−/− mice had lower levels of serum total IgA compared with WT mice, whereas serum levels of IgG and IgM were no different and there was no detectable kidney phenotype (Supplemental Figure 4, A–D). In contrast to miR-23b−/− mice, which displayed elevated levels of serum total and polymeric IgA and mesangial IgA deposition, 5-month-old Trdmt1−/− miR-23b−/− mice (Figure 5A and Supplemental Figure 4E) exhibited normal serum IgA levels (Figure 5B) and significantly reduced levels of polymeric IgA (Figure 5, C–E) with no change in serum IgG and IgM levels (Figure 5, F and G). Trdmt1−/− miR-23b−/− mice did not develop mesangial IgA and C3 deposition, and there were no differences in mesangial IgG and IgM staining between WT, miR-23b−/−, Trdmt1−/−, and Trdmt1−/− miR-23b−/− mice (Figure 5H).

miR-23b Regulation of AID Was Likely Mediated through TRDMT1-Driven 5mC RNA Modification in B Cells

We had previously shown that activation-induced cytidine deaminase, an enzyme mainly expressed in B cells and intimately involved in somatic hypermutation and IgA CSR,20,27 was upregulated in the small intestine of miR-23b−/− mice. Levels of mRNA for Aid in bone marrow B cells were higher in miR-23b−/− mice and positively correlated with B-cell levels of m5C RNA and Trdmt1 mRNA, but not Nsun2 and Nsun6 mRNA levels (Supplemental Figure 5, A and B), and serum levels of IgA (Figure 6, A–D). Suppression of m5C RNA modification in DAKIKI cells with 5-Aza downregulated expression of AID (Figure 6E). Transfection of DAKIKI cells with siRNA to TRDMT1 or AID resulted in suppression of TRDMT1 or AID, respectively, with no change in NSUN2/6 (Figure 6F) and, in both instances, was associated with reduced IgA1 synthesis (Figure 6G).

Further supporting an association between m5C RNA modification and activity of AID, the absence of Trdmt1 in Trdmt1−/− and Trdmt1−/− miR-23b−/− mice was associated with a reduction in expression of AID, CD19, and IgA in the small intestine (Figure 6H) and a decrease in both the percentage of CD19+IgA+ B cells and TRDMT1 expression in CD19+ B cells in the small intestine (Supplemental Figure 5, C and D).

5-Aza–treated miR-23b−/− mice also displayed a reduction in the number of AID+CD19+IgA+ B cells in the ileocecum and small intestine (Figure 6I and Supplemental Figure 5E). A key step in RNA incorporation of 5-Aza is the linkage of pyrimidine triphosphate to the pentose of 5-Aza. This is performed by uridine/cytidine kinase UCK1 and 2.28 UCK1 and 2 were mainly expressed in the spleen, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tissues of miR-23b−/− mice (Supplemental Figure 6, A and B). Notably, UCK1 was predominantly expressed in CD19+ B cells in the gastrointestinal tract (Supplemental Figure 6C), identifying mucosal CD19+ B cells as being highly susceptible to the action of 5-Aza.

B-Cell 5mC RNA Modification and IgA CSR Were Dependent on Both TRDMT1 and AID

To better understand the relationship between TRDMT1, expression of AID, and IgA synthesis, Aid−/− and Aid−/− miR-23b−/− mice were generated and phenotyped. Aid−/− mice lack the ability for CSR and somatic hypermutation, resulting in markedly lower serum IgA and IgG levels and significantly higher serum IgM levels (Supplemental Figure 7). Consistent with previous reports, m5C DNA methylation was increased, and there was downregulation of DNA-methyltransferase 1 mRNA and upregulation of mRNA for Dnmt3b in bone marrow B cells in Aid−/− mice (Supplemental Figure 8, A–D).29 DNMT1/3a/3b was detected in the small intestine and specifically in IgA+ B cells in Aid−/− mice (Supplemental Figure 8E). Bone marrow B cells from Aid−/− mice displayed a significant decrease in Trdmt1 mRNA expression and m5C RNA modification, while there were no significant changes in Nsun2/6 mRNA levels (Figure 7, A–D), suggesting that activity of AID directly or indirectly influences expression of TRDMT1 and RNA m5C modification. Parallel changes were seen in TRDMT1 expression and m5C RNA modification in small intestine CD19+IgA+ B cells in Aid−/− mice (Figure 7E), with no changes in NSUN2 and 6 (Figure 7F).

RNA 5mC, TRDMT1, and AID mRNA Levels Were Increased in Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells and Correlate with Levels of Serum IgA1 and Gd-IgA1 in Human IgA Nephropathy

As a first step in translating our findings to human disease, we studied peripheral blood mononuclear cells as an accessible source of B cells as opposed to mucosal and bone marrow B cells, which were the focus of our murine studies. Although levels of m5C RNA were only modestly higher in peripheral blood B cells in IgA nephropathy, the extent of RNA m5C modification significantly correlated with serum levels of IgA1 (and Gd-IgA1) in IgA nephropathy (Figure 9, A–C, and Supplemental Figure 9A). B-cell expression of TRDMT1 and NSUN2 and NSUN6 mRNA were similarly modestly higher in IgA nephropathy (Figure 9D and Supplemental Figure 9, B and C). Consistent with our murine model data, B-,cell TRDMT1 mRNA expression significantly correlated with serum IgA1 (and Gd-IgA1) levels, whereas NSUN2 and NSUN6 mRNA expression displayed no such correlation (Figure 9, E and F, and Supplemental Figure 9, D–G). Consistent with our murine data, although we found no significantly increased expression of AID mRNA in peripheral blood B cells in IgA nephropathy (Figure 9G), and AID mRNA expression was strongly correlated with serum IgA1 (and Gd-IgA1) levels (Figure 9H and Supplemental Figure 9H) and with the expression of mRNAs for TRDMT1>NSUN6>NSUN2 (Figure 9I and Supplemental Figure 9, I and J). Furthermore, there was no correlation between proteinuria levels and RNA 5mC, TRDMT1, and AID mRNA expression in human IgA nephropathy, which parallels an absence of correlation between serum IgA1 and Gd-IgA1 and proteinuria (Supplemental Figure 10).

Discussion

In this study, we show that the extent of TRDMT1-mediated RNA m5C modification in B cells determines both serum levels of IgA and the formation of circulating polymeric IgA immune complexes in miR-23b−/− mice. We also show that inhibiting RNA m5C methylation in vivo with 5-Aza normalizes serum IgA levels and thereby stops the drive for mesangial deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes and the consequent development of kidney injury in miR-23b−/− and Kawasaki disease mice. Furthermore, TRDMT1-mediated m5C RNA modification regulates IgA CSR through a direct action on AID activity. This effect is seen both at systemic and mucosal sites of IgA synthesis.

Until recently, TRDMT1 had been considered to have only DNA methyltransferase activity, owing to the homogeneity of its catalytic domain with DNMT1/3.30 However, it has now been shown that TRDMT1 can modify mRNA and transfer RNA (tRNA) by using a single cysteine in its catalytic pocket.31,32 Mutation of Trdmt1 has been shown to result in developmental defects in zebrafish,33 is associated with stress signaling and RNA-mediated transgenerational inheritance,34–36 and is required for accurate polypeptide synthesis during hematopoiesis in mice.37

We hypothesize that loss of miR-23b–mediated suppression of Trdmt1 in miR-23b−/− B cells resulted in excessive TRDMT1-mediated RNA m5C methylation. 5mC modifications have been found in mRNA, ribosomal RNA, and tRNA; are reversible epigenetic modifications; and play important roles in RNA stability control, codon–anticodon pairing and protein synthesis and transcriptional regulation. Excessive m5C modifications in these experiments were associated with dysregulation of IgA synthesis, likely mediated through epigenetic modification of several targets, one of which was AID, and the development of IgA nephropathy–like phenotype.

While these effects were observed in all B-cell compartments, there is significant evidence for a dominant perturbation in mucosal B-cell homeostasis after the loss of miR-23b. We have previously reported that loss of miR-23b is associated with a significant increase in the number and percentage of CD19+IgA+AID+ EBF1+ cells in Peyer patches in the small intestine, ileocecal junction, and colon.13 In this study, we show that these changes are dependent on TRDMT1 because Trdmt1−/− miR-23b−/− mice display a significant reduction in CD19+IgA+AID+ cells in the small intestine compared with miR-23b−/− mice. Intriguingly, this increase in intestinal CD19+IgA+AID+ cells can similarly be reversed with the administration of 5-Aza, and importantly, this reduction in CD19+ IgA+AID+ B cells in the ileocecum and small intestine is associated with a reversal of the elevation in serum total and polymeric IgA levels and prevention of mesangial IgA deposition and kidney function decline.

Although there are differences between mouse and human IgA systems, in particular, mouse IgA does not have an O-glycosylated hinge region, and the studied mice did not develop a severe kidney phenotype with kidney failure, we believe that the consistent development of mesangial IgA deposition, a defining feature of IgA nephropathy, means that these experiments do provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying generation of pathogenic IgA in IgA nephropathy.

Dysregulation of mucosal IgA synthesis, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract, is believed to be a major driver for IgA immune complex formation in IgA nephropathy.2 We believe that our data now strongly implicate miR-23b and TRDMT1 as key regulators of mucosal IgA synthesis and that they may be dysfunctional in IgA nephropathy. Our preliminary data in human peripheral blood B cells support this hypothesis because we found that the extent of RNA m5C methylation, and both TRDMT1 and AID mRNA expression, strongly correlated with serum levels of IgA1 and Gd-IgA1 in IgA nephropathy.

It is established that DNA methylation, histone acetylation, and RNA m6A methylation occur during Ig CSR.22,38,39 The role of RNA m5C methylation, and in particular TRDMT1-induced RNA m5C methylation, in AID-regulated IgA CSR is, however, poorly understood. In this study, we found a consistent interaction between TRDMT1 activity, levels of RNA m5C methylation, and AID-mediated IgA CSR that was not seen with NSUN2 or NSUN6. We speculate that TRDMT1 could interact with AID in a number of different ways to influence IgA CSR. TRDMT1 could directly bind with AID during shuttling between the cytoplasm and nucleus and modify AID CSR activity.23,40 Alternatively, TRDMT1 might drive m5C methylation of AID mRNA41 or modulate levels of tRNA-derived small RNAs.36 Excessive levels of tRNA-derived small RNAs have been reported to form G-quadruplex structures, which play a pivotal role in AID-mediated CSR.42,43

The beneficial effects of limiting RNA m5C methylation with 5-Aza provide proof-of-concept data to support TRDMT1 targeting as a novel therapeutic option in IgA nephropathy. Azacytidine is currently approved for the treatment of myelodysplastic syndrome.44 It is unlikely that azacytidine itself will be used in IgA nephropathy because of the associated toxicity, but our data provide the first proof of mechanism for the targeting of epigenetic pathways in IgA nephropathy.

In conclusion, we show for the first time that TRDMT1-induced RNA m5C methylation plays a critical role in AID-regulated IgA CSR and likely contributes to the excess mucosal IgA production characteristic of IgA nephropathy.
