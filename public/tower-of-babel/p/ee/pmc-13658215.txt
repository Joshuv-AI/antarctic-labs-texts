CeraMIRScan: Mid-infrared OCT Scan Dataset for Ceramic Quality Assessment
Authors: Natalia P García-de-la-Puente, Fernando García-Torres, Andrés Laveda-Martínez, Coraline Lapre, Niels Møller Israelsen, Ole Bang, Dominik Brouczek, Niels Benson, Adrián Colomer, Valery Naranjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMid-infrared Optical Coherence Tomography (MIR-OCT) is a promising Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) technique due to its high-resolution imaging capabilities and extensive applicability across various industrial domains. Studies developing Deep Learning (DL) models to detect defects in MIR-OCT scans are scarce, and few have been used for ceramic quality assessment. To address this gap, we introduce the MIR-OCT Scan Dataset for Ceramic Quality Assessment (CeraMIRScan), including labels to detect and segment defects. The dataset comprises 29 volumes corresponding to MIR-OCT scans of 3D printed ceramic pieces, decomposed into 21,882 B-scan images, each paired with expert-annotated binary masks capturing defects such as pores, delaminations, and inclusions. Notably, 41.38% of the images contain visible anomalies. To illustrate the dataset’s applicability to DL, we provide baseline segmentation results using a U-Net architecture, achieving 80.55% precision, 80.00% recall, and an 80.27% Dice score. CeraMIRScan offers a benchmark resource for advancing automated quality assessment and supports the development of MIR-OCT-based defect characterization methods.

Background & Summary

Ceramic additive manufacturing (AM) is transforming the production of complex ceramic components by enabling direct, layer-by-layer fabrication from digital designs. In contrast to conventional subtractive methods such as machining or moulding, ceramic AM allows the creation of geometries and internal features that were previously impossible to make1, while reducing material waste and production time2–4. This versatility has driven rapid adoption across diverse fields, including microelectronics5,6, biomedical systems7,8, terahertz technology9,10, and aerospace engineering11,12, where ceramics are valued for their exceptional mechanical strength, chemical stability, thermal resistance13, and biocompatibility. Despite these advancements and its growing industrial impact, ceramic AM is still in an intensive development phase, as achieving consistent, defect-free manufacturing at the micron scale remains a significant challenge14,15.

Ensuring the structural integrity of AM ceramics relies on several Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) techniques, designed to detect both surface and subsurface defects16, including ultrasonic inspection17, infrared thermography18, X-ray computed tomography (XCT), acoustic emission testing17, and visual inspection19. However, these NDT techniques face intrinsic trade-offs between resolution, penetration depth, and throughput, limiting their suitability for ceramic AM components16. Ultrasonic and thermographic techniques can penetrate deep, but both offer very low resolution, limiting their ability to resolve fine structural features. Ultrasound further requires a coupling gel for contact, whereas thermography can heat the sample and provides only a surface projection, but not its precise location. XCT delivers rich structural detail but is restricted by high acquisition costs, long scanning times, and limited suitability for large or complex geometries. Acoustic emission testing remains inherently reactive, and visual inspection is confined to surface-level assessments. Together, these constraints underscore the need for faster, higher-resolution, and non-contact inspection approaches for AM ceramics.

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) offers a promising solution as a high-resolution, non-invasive, and non-contact imaging modality capable of providing cross-sectional and three-dimensional visualisations of internal microstructures without physical contact. Conventional OCT systems typically operate in the near-infrared spectral range, having been originally developed for optical biopsy and ophthalmic imaging in the late 1980s20. OCT has evolved into a versatile acquisition method with applications spanning cardiology21, dermatology22,23, and, increasingly, industrial inspection and materials science24,25.

For most conventional materials, however, high optical scattering at near-infrared wavelengths limits imaging depth. Mid-infrared OCT (MIR-OCT) overcomes this limitation by using high-bandwidth MIR supercontinuum lasers to improve depth resolution. Operating around a 4 μm (4000 nm) wavelength26, MIR-OCT achieves exceptional resolution of approximately ~11 μm in air and ~7 μm in alumina, offering detailed subsurface imaging of ceramic microstructures. Crucially, the achievable imaging depth in alumina using this system exceeds 1 mm, though exact penetration remains highly dependent on the specific material composition and surface topology. These capabilities have proven valuable for the inspection of ceramics27,28 and for evaluating coating performance29,30. Whereas visible and near-infrared-based OCT systems remain inadequate for such applications, as their shorter wavelengths cannot penetrate strongly scattering ceramics and coatings26,27,29–31, MIR-OCT represents state-of-the-art in NDT for the target industries, in particular for in-line operation. The Austrian Research Center for Non-Destructive Testing (RECENDT) has demonstrated several MIR-OCT systems, though the use of slow pyroelectric detectors has limited imaging speeds28,32–34. The NORBLIS MIR-OCT technology offers a significant performance advance through a patent-protected combination of a broadband MIR supercontinuum laser and an ultra-fast kHz A scan rate upconversion spectrometer35,36.

While MIR-OCT provides unprecedented imaging capability, automating the detection and classification of defects remains a critical challenge. Advanced computational methods, such as deep learning (DL), offer promising solutions for integrating NDT with real-time process monitoring19,37. However, the development of robust, automated inspection frameworks is fundamentally hindered by the lack of large, well-annotated datasets. To date, no publicly available dataset exists for applying DL to MIR-OCT-based defect detection in ceramics; the only related open resource is an OCT image dataset of textile fabrics38, which focuses on material classification rather than defect identification, leaving a significant gap in the field.

All measurements and characterizations were performed exclusively on specimens in the green processing stage. This strategy aligns with the Zero Defect Zero Waste (ZDZW) EU Horizon project objective of enabling early, in-line defect detection. In LCM, identifying fully formed flaws, such as air bubbles, delaminations, and inclusions, directly after printing is critical for resource efficiency. It allows defective parts to be filtered out before they undergo time- and energy-intensive thermal processes like debinding and sintering.

We introduce CeraMIRScan39, a curated MIR-OCT scan dataset of 29-labeled ceramic components with ground-truth defect annotations. While our previous preliminary work40 was limited to specific defect types (such as inclusions), CeraMIRScan addresses this gap by providing comprehensive labeling across all major defect categories. This resource establishes a standardized benchmark, facilitating the development and evaluation of high-resolution, non-invasive quality assessment methodologies in manufacturing.

Methods

Lithography-based printing

Ceramic samples were fabricated using a commercial Lithoz 3D printer operating on lithography-based ceramic manufacturing (LCM) technology (Fig. 1a). LCM, widely employed in aerospace and biomedical manufacturing7, follows stereolithography principles: a photosensitive ceramic slurry is cured layer by layer by projected light. A blue LED source (450 nm) initiates polymerization, while a digital micromirror device controls exposure geometry and lateral resolution41.

a. Schematic of the LCM process (© Lithoz) showing the main components: a blue LED light source, the optical system including the digital micromirror device that shapes the light pattern, and the building platform on which the ceramic slurry is cured layer by layer. b. Printed ceramic component in its green state, produced using this technology.

A total of 28 samples were prepared for this study. Each sample consisted of 102 layers with a green-phase layer thickness of 25 μm (Fig. 1b). While all samples underwent identical thermal processing to yield final sintered dimensions of 4.02 × 16 × 2.01 mm3, their internal structures varied due to differences in slurry formulation and the intentional introduction of flaws. We artificially generated these defects using two distinct approaches:

By design: Modifying Computer-Aided Design (CAD) files to include internal features, such as small voids or larger cavities with critical overhangs, to simulate natural defects within the bulk material.

Despite these deliberate efforts to balance the representation of major defect classes, the inherent dynamics of the printing process meant that the exact quantity and distribution of unintended micro-pores and bubbles could not be strictly controlled.

MIR-OCT system

To generate a high-resolution dataset suitable for DL-based defect detection, two mid-infrared optical coherence tomography (MIR-OCT) scanners were employed: the DTU MIR-OCT system and the industrial NORBLIS scanner integrated into a Lithoz printer. The DTU MIR-OCT scanner (Fig. 2a) is based on a free-space Michelson interferometer with an in-house SC fiber, from 1 to 4.6 μm, for the light source42. A long-pass filter is present in the input of the interferometer to cut the light below 3.2 μm. Inside the scanning head, there are two-axis galvanometric mirrors paired with a barium fluoride (BaF2) plano-convex lens of 30mm focal length. The chromatic dispersion introduced by the lens was compensated in the second arm of the interferometer with a BaF2 window; the residual dispersion is corrected numerically43. The signal was measured by the combination of an upconversion system and a spectrometer. using a fast and low noise spectrometer. The system achieved an axial resolution of ~11 μm in the air, ~7 μm in alumina, which has a refractive index of n = 1.68. The lateral resolution was approximately ~30 μm, with an A-scan rate of 5kHz. For more information about the DTU MIR-OCT system, see refs. 26,27.

a. Visualization of the complete DTU MIR-OCT system. b. Integration of the NORBLIS MIR-OCT hardware within the Lithoz 3D printer setup.

The NORBLIS scanner employs a similar SC source and upconversion detection scheme, with a lateral resolution of ~30 μm and an axial resolution of ~5 μm in alumina, and 8 μm resolution in air. The system operates at an A scan rate of 3 kHz and is integrated directly with the Lithoz printing platform (Fig. 2b).

Data acquisition

Data acquisition from aluminum oxide samples was performed in two stages, following the general measurement and alignment workflow illustrated in Fig. 3. In Stage 1, were fabricated using the Lithoz printer and characterized ex situ with the DTU MIR-OCT scanner. For each sample, 400 cross-sectional images (B-scans) were collected, each comprising 400 depth scans (A-scans), covering a 5.67 × 5.67 mm2 field of view with a spacing of approximately 14 μm between adjacent A- and B-scans. The acquisition time for each A-scan was 200 μs. Post-processing included curvature correction to compensate for lens-induced distortion27. Because the geometry of the scanning lens causes physically flat surfaces to appear slightly curved in the raw data, a numerical flattening procedure is required to ensure geometric accuracy. This correction was successfully applied to the data acquired with the DTU scanner, utilizing a spatial error profile mapped out in advance for this specific custom setup.

Conceptual schematic of the measurement and alignment workflow. a. Lateral alignment (X, Y). b. Axial focus and B-scan acquisition (Z).

In Stage 2, samples were both printed and imaged in-line using the NORBLIS MIR-OCT system custom-integrated into a Lithoz printer. Developed on-site at the Lithoz facility in Vienna under the ZDZW project, this bespoke hardware setup enabled scanning directly within the active production environment. Consequently, scanning was performed intermittently between printed layers rather than exclusively after the part’s completion. The timing of these in-line scans was strategically dictated by the material’s penetration depth limit and the location of targeted internal features, ensuring that specific regions of interest (e.g., newly closed cavities) were captured in a timely manner. Each resulting volume scan covered a 7.3 × 7.3 mm2 surface area and consisted of 700 B-scans—each comprising 700 depth A-scans—with a spacing of approximately 10 μm between adjacent scans and an A-scan acquisition time of 300 μs.

Defect categories

The annotations within this dataset focus on critical subsurface defects originating directly from the printing process, as these flaws can lead to major structural failures in the final sintered ceramic. The defect categories were predefined based on established expert knowledge within the ceramics AM field, particularly for vat photopolymerization processes (e.g., LCM)44,45. The primary defect types identified include air bubbles (pores), delamination, and foreign inclusions caused by contamination (see Fig. 4).

Representative examples of the three subsurface defect categories. Bounding boxes highlight instances of a. a pore, b. a delamination, and c. an inclusion.

Pores were identified as distinct, localized signal voids with high-contrast boundaries. Delaminations appeared as horizontal, highly reflective linear artifacts indicating physical separation of layers. Inclusions were characterized by localized regions of altered scattering intensity compared to the surrounding ceramic matrix. An LCM expert applied these predefined, field-standard categories during the manual annotation process. Data annotation

To accommodate the chronological development of the dataset, the annotation workflow varied slightly between the two acquisition stages. For Stage 1 samples (ID A in Table 3), the process was conducted in two sequential steps. First, the images were visualized in 2D using the VGG Image Annotator (VIA) software46, where the expert localized the general defect regions using bounding boxes (Fig. 6, top) and assigned the corresponding defect type. Second, precise pixel-level segmentation masks (Fig. 6, bottom) were generated within these bounding boxes using the MATLAB Volume Segmenter application47. For this step, the expert marked the specific B-scans where the defect started and ended, and the mask was interpolated across the intervening slices. Conversely, for Stage 2 samples (ID B in Table 3), the bounding box step was omitted, and pixel-level masks were generated directly using the MATLAB Volume Segmenter. Pixels corresponding to these delineated defects were marked in white, and background pixels in black, thereby creating a binary mask for each slice.

Finally, all segmentation outputs from both stages underwent a comprehensive expert review to ensure anatomical and defect-level consistency across the entire dataset. It is important to note the physical boundaries of the discernible regions. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the lower portions and outer edges of the scans frequently exhibit low contrast, making visual defect detection challenging in these specific areas. This is due to two primary variables: (1) signal attenuation, which causes contrast to naturally decrease with increasing imaging depth into the ceramic material, and (2) surface topology, as non-flat or curved sample surfaces cause signal scattering that lowers contrast at the physical edges of the part.

Data Records

The complete CeraMIRScan dataset described in this manuscript, including the raw volumetric MIR-OCT scans and their corresponding pixel-level annotations, has been deposited in Zenodo and is publicly accessible39.

The CeraMIRScan dataset comprises 29 volumetric scans acquired from 28 3D-printed ceramic parts, and has been carefully curated to support the development of DL models. Notably, IDs A.1 and A.2 correspond to the same sample, which was scanned twice under identical conditions to identify potential scanning-related artifacts. Of these, 22 volumes include bounding-box annotations to enable defect localisation and classification, while all volumes are accompanied by manually generated binary segmentation masks. In total, the dataset contains 21,882 individual scans, of which 41.38% exhibit detectable defects.

The dataset is organised into three primary directories. The first (images/) contains the raw 29 MIR-OCT volumes. The second (annotations/raw_labels/) provides bounding-box coordinates and defect-level labels for 22 of those volumes. The third (annotations/masks/) includes pixel-wise segmentation masks for all 29 volumes. Image and mask files follow the naming convention VolumeName_SlideNumber.png, and bounding-box annotations are stored as VolumeName.csv.

Note that B-scan indexing follows the default acquisition of the MIR-OCT system, in which indexing starts at 0; therefore, the first acquired slice is labeled as slide 000.

Data structure

The images/ folder contains one subfolder per volume, each with the complete set of slices in .png format. The images contain different types of defects in size and shape, and have the dimensions described in the table 3. The dataset includes both bounding box annotations and binary segmentation masks that highlight internal defects in the ceramic volumes.

The annotations folder is divided into two subfolders: raw-labels/ and mask/. The raw-labels/ subfolder contains one .csv file per volume that includes the coordinates of the bounding boxes and the defect categories for each image. The mask/ subfolder contains one folder per volume, with binary masks in .png format aligned with the original images that indicate defective regions (See Fig. 7). Although many types of defects are considered in the bounding box annotations, the segmentation masks are binary and distinguish only between defect and non-defect.

Technical Validation

Data usability validation

The CeraMIRScan dataset was designed to support the training of automatic defect segmentation models for MIR-OCT images of ceramic components. The dataset was divided into two subsets: 27 volumes for training (A.1-A.12, A.14-A.16, B.1-B.7, B.9-B.13) and 2 volumes for validation (A.13 and B.8), ensuring a balanced representation of samples with both sparse and abundant defects. This split ensured that the validation set included samples from both fabrication batches.

To establish a solid baseline, segmentation was performed using the U-Net architecture. This model was selected because it is a widely adopted standard in image segmentation. For consistency, a ResNet34 backbone with ImageNet pre-trained weights was used, providing a reproducible benchmark for future research on this dataset.

The model was trained with a batch size of 16, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 over 200 epochs. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 15 epochs, automatically saving the best-performing model, which in the optimal configuration occurred at epoch 23. A fixed random seed was set to ensure reproducibility. All experiments were run on an NVIDIA DGX system equipped with 8 × A100 GPUs (40 GB each), an AMD EPYC 7742 CPU (64 cores, 2.25 GHz, 256 threads), and 1 TB of DDR4 RAM.

Model validation

Segmentation performance was evaluated on the validation set using Precision, Recall, and Dice Score at the pixel level.

Two loss functions were considered: Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and Dice Loss (DLS), along with their equal-weight combination. Consequently, Table 4 provides a rigorous benchmark of the model’s robustness across diverse imaging setups and defect origins.

As shown in Fig. 8, the U-Net segmentation output demonstrates strong overall similarity to the manually annotated ground truth for both large and small defects. However, technical validation of the minor discrepancies reveals a direct correlation with specific imaging constraints. Specifically, minor segmentation errors, such as boundary blurring and occasional false positives, are localized to regions exhibiting low defect-to-background contrast, inherent speckle noise, and depth-dependent signal attenuation. Tracking these errors provides valuable insight into how specific imaging quality factors influence model accuracy and highlights areas where targeted image enhancement could further improve segmentation.

Usage Notes

When utilizing the CeraMIRScan dataset, researchers should first note that the defects were artificially generated rather than collected from an industrial manufacturing line. While this approach allows for controlled experiments and a purposely balanced representation of major defect classes, users should be aware that this may limit direct transferability to real-world manufacturing scenarios where defect generation is entirely stochastic.

Furthermore, researchers must account for instrumental and methodological distinctions between the two sample subsets. Initial imaging for Sample set 1 (ID: A) was performed using a custom DTU scanner, configured to provide optimal imaging convenience and customization. This subset was produced prior to the availability of in-line measurement capabilities, focusing on structural defects induced passively through the application of improper printing parameters. Subsequent measurements for Sample set 2 (ID: B) utilized a commercial NORBLIS scanner, parameterized to mirror the DTU setup as closely as possible. However, inherent hardware limitations and reduced customizability of the commercial system necessitated slight differences in the final measurement parameters. Additionally, Sample set 2 was specifically generated after in-line measurement capabilities were established, featuring defects intentionally generated by design. These operational differences should be carefully considered when conducting comparative analyses or training DL models across the combined dataset.

To allow for maximum flexibility in future analyses, the images are provided in their raw, untrimmed format. For researchers intending to train deep learning models, we highly recommend implementing specific preprocessing strategies to address the high vertical resolution (1024 pixels) and to remove deeper regions where signal contrast naturally diminishes. In our baseline analyses, optimal training performance was achieved using a vertical crop of 352 pixels, isolating the upper portion of the image where structural information is concentrated. Additionally, for compatibility with standard network architectures requiring dimensions divisible by 32, we recommend applying a horizontal padding of 4 pixels to the raw images, resulting in an effective width of 704 pixels.

Finally, this dataset provides an ideal foundation for advancing NDT via DL. Recent studies have successfully applied DL architectures to segment XCT scans of 3D-printed aluminum48 and classify defects directly from 1D ultrasonic signals49. While the baseline evaluations presented in this study predict defects strictly on a slice-by-slice (2D) basis and treat all flaws as a single class, future users are strongly encouraged to expand upon this. Researchers can leverage the full volumetric context by employing 3D convolutional architectures to improve spatial consistency and defect quantification. Moreover, this dataset can be used to develop multi-class segmentation models that automatically differentiate between specific structural irregularities, such as pores, inclusions, or delaminations, thereby establishing new computer vision benchmarks for automated ceramic quality assessment.
