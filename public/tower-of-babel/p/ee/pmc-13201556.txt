Multi-scale covariance filtering for edge-preserving medical image fusion
Authors: Shweta Sharma, Shalli Rani, Ayush Dogra, Mohammad Shabaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-modal medical image integration is necessary to improve the accuracy of diagnostic results by integrating complementary information between modalities such as Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging. Nevertheless, current methods of fusion fail to maintain the finer details of the anatomy, balance of contrast and eliminate artifacts along structural boundaries. In order to solve these issues, this paper will present a new fusion framework that is based on Covariance Filtering. It uses a two-scale image decomposition to extract the base and detail layers and proceeds with saliency-guided weight map creation and covariance-based refinement for spatial consistency and edge preservation. Assessments of multi-modal brain imaging data show that the suggested method is better than the state-of-the-art methods in terms of Entropy, Mutual Information, Average Gradient and QAB/F. All the research findings support its efficacy in maintaining anatomical boundaries, improving visual clarity, and offering a computationally efficient solution. This framework will be expanded to 3D image fusion in future work and dedicated mechanisms used in regions detection to enhance precision in diagnostic processes.

Introduction

Medical image fusion is now a necessary technology in modern healthcare, which integrates complementary information of different imaging modalities to enhance diagnostic accuracy, maximize treatment plans, and aid decision-making. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Computed Tomography (CT), Positron Emission Tomography (PET) and Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) are some of the most commonly used techniques which offer different anatomical or functional information. The restrictions of these modalities including the limited soft-tissue contrast of CT and the comparatively low spatial resolution of PET mean that a combination of multi-modal data is required to produce a more comprehensive and informative view of pathological states is needed1,2. This hybrid process has special significance in medical applications that are more complicated, such as oncology, neurology examination, and surgical navigation, wherein detection, localization, and characterization of abnormalities are highly important and require accuracy in all cases3,4.

Besides multi-modal fusion, the more recent developments have been directed to the enhancement of automated analysis of medical images with the help of efficient segmentation frameworks. Uncertainty-aware learning methods have been suggested to increase the accuracy of semi-supervised medical image segmentation when using less annotated data5, and methods following dual diversity learning and pseudo-label correction have also been proposed to further improve segmentation quality in under-annotation conditions6. Likewise, fusion of diversity features together with dual consistency learning has been studied to enhance feature representation and segmentation performance the field of medical imaging-based learning images and tasks to be learned are described as features, often termed feature maps or feature vectors, that are regularly accessed and evaluated throughout the learning process of the task7. These advancements emphasize the rising relevance of image analysis methods in the retrieval of meaningful information in medical images.

Medical image fusion techniques evolved over time from simple pixel-based and feature-based methods to more advanced Deep Learning (DL) methods. Before, wavelet transformations, Principal Component Analysis and sparse representation were used to extract features and linear combination rules by hand. Although these methods were relatively successful, they were prone to missing important structural information and adding artifacts to the fused images8,9. With the introduction of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), a data-driven view of image fusion has become available, allowing to preserve textures, edges, and critical functional information in a more successful way. As an example, conditional GANs (cGANs) and Laplacian pyramid-based GAN models have been highly effective in producing high-resolution fused images without compromising the modality-specific information by adversarial training processes10. These combinations can enhance the quality of fusion by using complementary decomposition approaches with adaptive fusion mechanisms, which are complementary to each other, thus providing a better fusion quality, which utilizes their combined benefits11,12.

Despite these advancements, significant issues still exist to address. Multi-modal datasets usually vary in terms of the degree of resolution, noise, and alignment, implying that they require robust pre-processing and registration systems13. A significant challenge remains with regards to the trade-off between computing efficiency and fusion quality particularly in cases where real time computation is required. Techniques such as Coati optimization strategy or multiscale adaptive transformers, attempt to optimize fusion parameters, with less computing power, as proposed in14. To illustrate, the outputs produced by self-attention models using transformers are more structurally sound since it finds long-range dependencies between modalities.

The resulting fused images is further complicated by the fact that there is no collective set of criteria to evaluate the quantitative accuracy and significance of the images. Measures such as Structural similarity Index (SSI), Mutual Information (MI) and Edge Preservation Index (EPI) are often used but are often not correlated with the subjective rating of radiologists15. Recent research highlights the need of task-related assessment systems based on domain knowledge, including tumor boundary definition in cancer or vessels in neurology16.

Explainable Artificial Intelligence, edge computing, and federated learning must collaboratively address challenges related to scalability, privacy, and interpretability in the future of medical image fusion. Lightweight GAN architectures and adaptive fusion rules optimized using swarm intelligence algorithms, such as the Chameleon Swarm Algorithm, exemplify emerging technologies poised to enhance real-time processing capabilities17. As customized medicine increasingly depends on multi-modal imaging, enhancing fusion techniques will be crucial for developing accurate diagnoses and delivering effective treatments.

This paper presents a new multi-modal fusion method based on the Laplacian Pyramid to meet the ongoing need for efficient and high-quality image fusion solutions. The suggested method is meant to preserve fine anatomical details better and make the contrast more consistent in the fused outcomes. This approach uses a multi-level Laplacian Pyramid decomposition instead of directed filtering, which is what most standard methods use. This collects image information at multiple spatial scales. Also, a Gaussian-based saliency detection method is used to make weight maps that are accurate and specific.

Related work

Medical image fusion aims at integrating complementary information of various imaging modalities in a single image to enhance the accuracy in diagnosis and assist in the treatment. The past decade has seen numerous fusion methods being developed, such as spatial domain and transform domain ones, and newer methods based on the use of DL. This part presents a literature review of the work that has been conducted and the fusion frameworks involving the guided filtering, saliency detection and Laplacian pyramid decomposition, as well as the recent advances in the sphere.

Guided filtering and image statistics based fusion

The pioneering work in medical image fusion with the help of filtering methods was done by Li et al.18 who came up with a guided filtering-based system that decomposed images into base and detail layers at two scales. Their approach integrated all these layers through the use of weight maps based on saliency cues and local consistency rules. This method was used by Bavirisetti et al.19 and applied to medical imaging. They combined CT images and MRI images with the help of guided image filtering and statistical techniques preserving such important details as bone structures and soft tissues. Their work demonstrated that guided filtering can provide a smooth spatial weighting as well as retain few features in the image which is even more significant as it can be applied to jobs that require fusing images of various sources.

Saliency-based and pixel significance fusion techniques

Similar to guided filtering saliency-based fusion techniques have been the focus of attention in their capability to highlight areas that are perceptually significant. Bavirisetti et al.20 proposed a two-scale image fusion scheme where visible and infrared images are combined, with saliency detection maps to direct the process of fusing detail layers. The technique was also related, and Kumar et al.21 applied it to enhance important features in both multi-spectral and multi-focus images, by employing Cross Bilateral Filtering (CBF). This was in a combination methodology of pixel significance. Kumar et al. had also previously applied the Discrete Cosine Harmonic Wavelet Transform (DCHWT) as well as pixel significance maps in fusing multi-focus images and multispectral images. This demonstrated the fact that localized significance information can be important in the creation of fusion results22.

Multi-resolution and laplacian pyramid-based fusion methods

Multi-resolution decomposition methods have been used in image fusion since long ago as they have the capability of retrieving the images at various scales. Li et al.23 compared the various multi-resolution transforms, including the Laplacian pyramid, the discrete wavelet transform, and the contourlet transform. They discovered that Laplacian pyramid and variants are the most reasonable trade between computational and perceptual qualities. When applied to edge-aware fusion, Paris et al.24 developed the Local Laplacian filter, which simultaneously applies a Laplacian pyramid structure on images to apply contrast and maintain edges.

Further to this, Zhu et al.25 suggested a multi-modality medical image fusion technique based on phase congruency and local Laplacian energy in Non-Subsampled Contourlet Transform (NSCT) domain with an accent on the advantages of Laplacian-based energy descriptors in preserving the structure of the image. Kurban et al.26 more recently, introduced a Gaussian of differences(GoD)-based fusion, which is an efficient computationally method of fusion images in general and preserves important structural aspects at the same time.

Recent advances in decomposition and saliency-driven fusion

Recent work has explored decomposition strategies enhanced by saliency-based weighting schemes. Song et al.27proposed a dual-decomposed low-rank representation method (D2-LRR) for medical image fusion, reducing redundancy while preserving complementary features28. designed a multi-exposure image fusion technique using structural weights derived from visual saliency maps. The fusion framework proposed by Zheng et al.29 employ edge detection and salient feature extraction in which the structural information is boosted on the basis of gradient-based edge maps which are complemented by saliency-driven weighting. They are successful in enhancing the preservation of the anatomical outlines and the quality of perceptions in fused images. Simultaneously, the decision methods based on DL have received critical interest in the possibilities to represent the complex nonlinear interactions between modalities. Kaur et al.30 proposed a deep neural network-based medical image fusion algorithm which depends on morphologically processed residuals. They used residual learning and morphological operations in their approach to boost feature representation, which allows better detail preservation and noise suppression in the fused result.

These recent developments highlight the growing trend toward combining structural feature extraction and learning-based approaches to improve fusion performance, particularly in terms of edge preservation, feature enhancement, and robustness across diverse medical imaging modalities.

Toward covariance filtering for image fusion

Guided filtering as well as Laplacian-Pyramid-based methods have delivered results that are practically useful but their use of external guidance images combined with their tendency to produce blocky artifacts at boundaries remain significant weaknesses. However, Covariance filtering compares to direct modeling of local statistical inter-dependencies between an image and a weight map31. Niehsen et al.24 in a previous study proved the usefulness of covariance-intersection filtering in data fusion, thus establishing a covariance-based local-consistency systems. Further investigations affirm the power of covariance-based modeling to adaptive filtering and signal-processing tasks. Chang et al.32, used adaptive covariance filter to increase the signal retrieval level, thus demonstrating superior robustness and noise resistance. Similarly, modeling methods and covariance estimation have demonstrated usefulness in the reconstruction and filtering of data as well as enhancement of stability of second order statistical dependencies in data modeling and reconstruction processes33.

Saliency-based approaches effectively highlight important regions but often fail to maintain spatial continuity, leading to discontinuous or noisy weight maps.Furthermore, multi-scale decomposition methods, although effective in separating structural components, do not inherently guarantee optimal weight refinement, which can limit detail preservation and introduce artifacts in the fused image34.

This method preserve fine structural information, contrast and less local inconsistency artifacts that are typical in guided and Laplacian Pyramid based methods. The suggested approach is able to provide spatial consistency utilizing local covariance-based linear models, which have a simplicity in computation and better detail preservation.

Dataset

The data set used in this study contains three pairs of multi-modal medical images, which were obtained using different modalities of brain imaging as shown in Fig. 2. Particularly, the dataset includes T1-weighted and T2-weighted MRI sequences, CT data, and post-contrast MRI data. These images span both cerebral anatomical structures and pathological areas, such as aberrant tissue intensities and lesion-affected areas which are studied in multi-modal neuroimaging studies. Images are all in grayscale, with a bit-depth of 8 bits, and spatial resolutions of between 256 to 464 pixels-per-unit. The given images are already aligned and visually clean enough. So, no additional preprocessing, including registration of image, denoising, or intensity normalization were considered before using the fusion algorithm. MRI T2-weighted 1(A) is an axial slice of the brain where cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is visible with a high intensity of brightness. The white matter (high content of water in its myelin sheaths) also has a high signal intensity and the lateral and third ventricles are clearly distinguished by the high signal intensity. Gray matter, on the other hand, is darker than white matter, which makes it a good contrast in order to localize the structures like the basal ganglia (caudate nucleus and thalamus) and corpus callosum. Sections of the brainstem (midbrain and pons) and cerebellum are seen inferiorly and the optic nerves and orbital globes are seen as the slice nears the orbital level. This T2 contrast has specific application in the visualization of pathologies associated with fluid (e.g. edema or demyelination) and as a rich source image in fusion pipelines.

Coupled with such T2-weighted MRI slice is a CT scan of the same axial level (1(B)). Skeletal structures, which are of the skull such as the calvarium, skull sutures and orbital walls, are hyperdense (bright white) in this type of bony-window reconstruction. The intermediate attenuation of brain tissue and lower attenuation of CSF give rise to a dull gray-white complex which, however, gives an accentuated emphasis on structural boundaries and cortical folding patterns. The ventricles still appear hypodense (dark) spaces, which complement fluid-filled spaces. No acute hemorrhage or fractures are present and this provides a baseline Normative template on which any T2 based fluid contrasts can be merged to provide better image of both soft tissue and bone.

Another key source image is the T1-weighted MRI (2(A)). Here, CSF is hypointense (dark), and white matter appears brighter than gray matter. Such contrast accentuates cortical and subcortical anatomy: the caudate nucleus, putamen, and globus pallidus in the basal ganglia stand out against adjacent white matter; the midbrain section of the brainstem is visible centrally; and the orbital globes appear hyperintense (bright) in their vitreous component. Sulci and gyri are symmetrically folded, indicating no mass effect or cortical atrophy. This T1 contrast is instrumental for detailed anatomical assessment, especially when subsequently fused with CT data that excels at highlighting bone and calcifications.

Its paired CT counterpart (2(B)) is a non-contrast axial brain scan. Bone structures–such as the calvarium and facial bones–appear hyperdense (white), while gray and white matter attenuation differences are subtler. However, this CT window still delineates sulcal patterns and ventricular margins, and it provides clear views of orbital and sinus anatomy (e.g., frontal and ethmoid sinuses appear as low-density areas). There are no hemorrhages, calcifications, or midline shifts, offering a complementary soft-tissue backbone to the T1-weighted MRI’s fluid depiction. Together, these two modalities (T1 MRI + CT) support fusion methods that aim to enhance both soft-tissue contrast and bony detail.

T2-weighted post-contrast MRI (3(A)) is added in order to highlight fluid and possible edema of the left temporal lobe. Within ventricles, the CSF is highly bright, the lateral ventricles well-identified and the periventricular white matter may be evaluated in terms of subtle intensive hyperintensities. Corpus callosum and basal ganglia retain their signaling patterns and one can enjoy the initial signs of lesion margins or fluid build-up due to the improved contrast. This slice thus creates a detailed source image in terms of fusion methods that are advantageous in terms of highlighting of inflamed or edematous relationships as well as structural clarity of the CT image.

The T1-weighted post-contrast MRI (3(B)) has a negative effect on the CSF by making it dark, whereas the white matter exhibits enhanced intensity after gadolinium. The ventricles are well delimited and any deep white matter or along the corpus callosum enhancement is easily noticed. This modality highlights areas characterized by broken blood 2 barriers or inflammation and is a point of reference when fusing lesion-enhanced T2 configuration with CT-based bone delineation. Together, these images allow multi-scale fusion plans to address the vascular/permeability changes as well as the underlying landmarks of the bone in the body.

Method

The multi-modal image fusion scheme is proposed which involves a two-scale image decomposition scheme and then weight map construction and refinement with the help of covariance filtering. The proposed method compares to the traditional methods that rely on guided filtering, where a covariance-based local linear model is used to compute spatially consistent weight maps, edge-preserving weight maps. The general process comprises three steps: two-scale decomposition, weight map construction and final image reconstruction depicted in Fig. 3.

The above three datasets, including SET-1, SET-2 and SET-3 are assessed on the proposed fusion framework, as outlined below. The datasets are each made up of registered multi-modal medical image pairs that contain complementary structural and intensity characteristics. Such datasets are utilized in the entire methodological pipeline which consists of two-scale decomposition, construction of saliency-based weight maps, construction of covariance filtering based refinement and reconstruction of final image.Fig. 3Summary of the proposed multi-modal methodology of medical image fusion. The model has three key steps, a two scale image decomposition, a saliency-based weight map development, and covariance filtering-based weight refining and image reconstruction. The initial step is to decompose every source image into base and detail layers in order to distinguish coarse intensity data and high-frequency structural details. The further step involves creating saliency maps by applying Laplacian and Gaussian filters to detect perceptually important areas to create initial weight maps. The covariance filtering is then used to refine these weight maps to enhance spatial consistency and maintain anatomical edges. Lastly, the refined weights are created to combine the base and detail layers resulting in the final fused image that preserves complementary structural and functional data of the origin modalities.

In order to successfully isolate the large-scale intensity structures and fine image details, the registered input images of each image, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_i$$\end{document}, are first broken down into a base layer and a detail layer. The base layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_i$$\end{document} is purposely configured to store the coarse variations in intensity and smooth background area, whereas the detail layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i$$\end{document} is configured to store the high-frequency detail of the image, including edges, contours, and texture.

The base layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_i$$\end{document} is calculated by averaging a filter Z on the source image:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} B_i = I_i * Z \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Where the Z represents an averaging filter of size 31 x 31 and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$*$$\end{document} represents a two dimensional convolution. It is effective when information with high frequencies is being suppressed and the low frequencies are retained during the outcome.

After obtaining the base layer, the detail layer corresponding to it, which is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i$$\end{document}, is created by subtracting the base layer to the original image:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_i = I_i - B_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This step separates the high-frequency residues - such as edges, lines and texture elements - by eliminating the rough structures embodied in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_i$$\end{document}. As a result, every image is divided into complementary representations which can be processed separately in the fusion framework.

Weight map construction

In order to successfully control the process of a fusion, the weight maps are built so as to identify which of the source images made a contribution to the final result of the fusion at a given spatial position. This is achieved through two processes; firstly generation of the weight map, which is done using saliency detection, and finally refinement on the weight map using covariance filtering.

First, a saliency map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} of each image is calculated in order to determine the perceptual significance of every pixel in the source images. It starts by using Laplacian filter to highlight high-frequency data:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H_i = I_i * L \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here L is a 3 x 3 Laplacian kernel that is used to accentuate edges and fine structures by increasing rapid intensity changes. The resultant high-pass image H i reveals anatomical edges and fine textures.

A Gaussian filter of high-pass image, the value of which is absolute, is then smoothed with the help of a Gaussian filter, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{r_s, \sigma _s}$$\end{document} high-pass image, which is used to suppress noise and guarantee local spatial continuity:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_i = |H_i| * g_{r_s, \sigma _s} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In this case, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{r_s, \sigma _s}$$\end{document} is a Gaussian kernel having a radius, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_s = 5$$\end{document}, and standard deviation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _s = 5$$\end{document}. The saliency map of the resulting 2D saliency map, denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document}, employs the saliency map to amplify the prominent structure whilst reducing the response of isolated noise.

After retrieving the saliency maps, preliminary weight maps, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_i$$\end{document} are obtained with the help of a pixel-wise maximum selection strategy. The original weight map M i is set to be defined at each spatial point k, i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_i$$\end{document}.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M_i^k = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & \text {if } S_i^k = \max \limits _{j} (S_j^k) \\ 0, & \text {otherwise} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The effect of this rule is that, in any pixel point, the image that has greatest local saliency prevails in the sense that the rest of the images are diminished. The resulting binary weight images are a pointer of the most informative source image in any pixel location.

These binary maps are, however, largely spatially inconsistent, and may be blocky or have artifacts along structural boundaries. In response to this, covariance filtering based refinement strategy is used. Unlike guided filtering, covariance filtering will not require a guidance image, but will instead learn directly local linear models between each image, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_i$$\end{document} and its original weight map, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_i$$\end{document}, in each direction, and this forces local consistency and retention of edges.

The covariance of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_i$$\end{document} within a local radius of r is obtained as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Cov}(I_i, M_i) = \text {mean}(I_i \cdot M_i) - \text {mean}(I_i) \cdot \text {mean}(M_i) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This quantity measures the degree of local linear correlation between the image and its weight map. Simultaneously, the local variance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_i$$\end{document} is obtained as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Var}(I_i) = \text {mean}(I_i^2) - \left( \text {mean}(I_i)\right) ^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Using these local statistics, the linear coefficients a and b are estimated according to:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} a = \frac{\text {Cov}(I_i, M_i)}{\text {Var}(I_i) + \epsilon } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} b = \text {mean}(M_i) - a \cdot \text {mean}(I_i) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} is a regularization term that prevents division by near-zero variances, preserving numerical stability in homogeneous regions.

The refined weight map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_i$$\end{document} is then computed by applying the locally averaged linear model to the corresponding image:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_i = \text {mean}(a) \cdot I_i + \text {mean}(b) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This process adaptively adjusts the weight map based on local intensity variations in the source image, enhancing alignment with structural boundaries and reducing noise artifacts. Different parameter values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(r, \epsilon )$$\end{document} are applied for base and detail layers, resulting in distinct refined weight maps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{B_i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{D_i}$$\end{document} for each image.

Finally, to ensure balanced contributions from all images, the refined weight maps are normalized such that their sum equals one at every pixel location:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i=1}^{N} W_i^k = 1, \ \forall k \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This normalization guarantees that the final fused image preserves consistent pixel intensities and avoids overemphasis or suppression of any individual image.

Image fusion and reconstruction

Once the refined weight maps are available, the fusion of base and detail components is performed by computing weighted sums. The fused base image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_B$$\end{document} is obtained as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_B = \sum _{i=1}^{N} W_{B_i} \cdot B_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Similarly, the fused detail image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_D$$\end{document} is computed as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_D = \sum _{i=1}^{N} W_{D_i} \cdot D_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Finally, the overall fused image F is reconstructed by combining the fused base and detail layers:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F = F_B + F_D \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This reconstruction is done to make sure that the fused image contains both the coarse structures and fine details of all the input images and the outcome would be a perceptually superior, artifact-free multi-modal fusion image.

Results

The section is a complete review of the functionality of the suggested fusion framework with special focus on the qualitative and quantitative results. The combination was used on pairs of CT and MRI images at various anatomical levels and contrast types (T1, T2 and post-contrast MRI).

Qualitative evaluation

The fuse result of the proposed method had much better visual resolution and structural fidelity in maintaining important anatomical structures, including ventricular boundaries, cortical sulci, and cranial sutures. This is also enhanced by means of a combination of a two scale decomposition with a saliency guided weight-map computation which enables to independently process coarse intensity structures using higher frequency information, prioritize diagnostically important areas, including the ventricular systems and the bone margins, throughout the process of fusion.

Figure 4, of fusion of the CT scan and T2-weighted MRI, clearly indicated that the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) had retained its rich and well-defined texture, and retained its characteristic high T2-weighted MRI appearance of hyperintensity. At the same time, the deep and sharp contrast of the osseous structures, such as the calvarium and cranial sutures, after the CT ensured that no details would be lost due to excessive smoothing as was usually observed in the classic Laplacian Pyramid or guided filtering technique. Covariance filtering-based weight refinement was the means to achieve such an ideal preservation by modifying saliency-based weight maps in an adaptive fashion to match the local image statistics, thus preserving sharp edges along anatomical lines and attenuate undesired background oscillation. Ablation study

To determine individual contribution of the key elements in the proposed fusion framework, an ablation analysis was conducted, which is a systematic removal of key modules, such as covariance filtering, saliency-based weight construction, and Laplacian-based edge extraction in the proposed framework. The results of these ablation experiments in a quantitative form are represented in Table 9.

Effect of Covariance Filtering: In order to measure the contribution of covariance filtering, the refinement step was eliminated and the original weight maps were used unfiltered. As seen, without covariance filtering, there is less edge preservation and spatial consistency, causing a decrease in QAB/F values in all the datasets. This backs up the fact that covariance filtering is vital in filtering weight maps and enhancing structural correspondence.

Effect of Saliency-Based Weight Construction: In order to look at the significance of saliency, even weights were put on all the input images, omitting the step of calculating saliency. The findings indicate a high degree of deterioration of the fusion performance, especially in the case of Average Gradient (AG) and QAB/F. It means that saliency-based weighting is the key to informative regions and to retain meaningful features of source images.

Effect of Laplacian-Based Edge Extraction: In order to consider the effect of the edge information, the step of the Laplacian filter was eliminated, and the saliency maps were directly calculated using the input images. The findings show that Laplacian filtering is essential to minimize edge representation and structural detail, which validate the use of Laplacian filtering.

Parameter Settings: The covariance filtering stage uses two parameters that are proposed: the window radius and the regularization parameter. Discussion

Experimental results demonstrates the ability of the proposed Covariance Filtering-based fusion method to preserve modality-specific content from multi-modal images while, at the same time, providing a high level of structural coherence and perceptual separability of the fused output images. The new approach is compared with traditional pixel-based and transform domain-based fusion methods that are often guided by problems of spectral distortion or local detail loss. The approach utilizes a two-scale decomposition with region-aware saliency weighting and adaptive covariance-based refinement. This allows for both the diagnostically important boosting of structures like lesions, bone edges, and gray-white matter interfaces, and, at the same time, the elimination of redundant.

The proposed solution works best in case with unquenchable anatomy contrasts. One example included the combination of post contrast T2-weighted MRI and CT images which brought out areas of edema and lesion margins without compromising bone information. The implications of these findings provide a deeper understanding of the fact that precise visualization of soft tissue pathology and bony anatomy is needed in pre-surgical planning, radiotherapy contouring, and neuro-oncological analyses. The high performance on parameters of mutual information, entropy, and QAB/F highlights that the method can maintain complementary properties of both modalities, fluid mechanics of the MRI and structural outlines of CT.

The strong point of our solution is that it is flexible with various MRI contrast modalities. Regardless of the choice of fusing T1-weighted or T2-weighted high-resolution images or post-contrast images, the Covariance Filtering framework invariably received the local fine-grained features as well as the global contextual structure. This is also possible due to the covariance-based weight refinement that offers an avenue to relax spatial consistency and maintain sharp boundaries. Also, the saliency detecting mechanism was implemented on the basis of Gaussian, which guaranteed that the mechanism of fusion more highly valued the areas that were associated with diagnostics rather than the noise or less essential background structures.

The comparison to the state-of-the-art algorithms, such as guided filtering-based and Laplacian Pyramid-based fusion, showed that our algorithm performed worse than others according to the objective measure, as well as subjective understanding. Although techniques, e.g. guided filtering15, also yielded a visual smoothness, they tended to produce low contrast and also could not highlight important boundaries particularly the ones surrounding a lesion. Contrastingly, the covariance-equipped saliency-clustered weight maps of our approach yield sharper edges and enhanced tissue distinctiveness which is particularly useful in the application of radiology where sharp edges and high precision in space are required.

In spite of such advantages, there are certain weaknesses that should be taken into consideration. To begin with, the offered method does not require as much computation as a deep learning model, nevertheless, it entails local window calls of covariance and multi-level fusion, which can certainly affect real-time applicability in a large-scale workflow without additional efforts. Also, even though saliency maps were effective to select regions, they are based on local contrast, and in images with low signal-to-noise or weak initial alignment can be suboptimal. Further development of the study in the future might involve combining adaptive learning-based saliency detectors or transformer-based attention systems to improve further region selection and semantic relevance.

In short, the Covariance Filtering-based fusion system discussed in the current paper could meet some of the major issues of the multi-modal medical image fusion by providing a structurally coherent, perceptually incisive and meaningful system. Its combination of spatial saliency, local covariance modelling and multi-scale detail retention make it a prospective approach to both real-world diagnostic and therapeutic workflows and has the potential to be used in the future in both 3D imaging and AI-based optimization.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising performance and the impressive results of the proposed covariance filtering-based fusion model using different datasets and evaluation criteria, it is possible to note several limitations of the presented model, which should be addressed in the future.

To begin with, the algorithm has greater computational efficiency than the DL techniques, whereas local window-based covariance and multi-stage processing, including saliency map generation and refinement, have an average level of computational demands. This will restrict its real time application in large scale pipelines or time sensitive application such emergency diagnosis or intraoperative imaging. The work in the future will be based on optimizing the computational pipeline through parallelization and effective approximations of covariance computation and processing using GPOUs to make this computation useable in real time.

Second, the strategy is operating in the 2D image space. Despite the fact that it is sufficient to most slice-based diagnostic uses, volumetric imaging is increasingly being used in daily practice, especially in cancer and neurosurgery. The model will be extended to allow 3D volumetric fusion, which will allow better anatomic continuity between slices and the space-related information in the areas of tumor outline and radiotherapy planning.

Besides, the framework uses pre-registered input images. In practice, fusion can be compromised by misalignment between modalities (e.g., the motion of the patient or variation in the time of acquisition). The method should be further enhanced by incorporation of a lightweight image registration module or learning-based alignment correction into the fusion pipeline so that the method becomes more robust and usable.

Overall, although the suggested fusion approach offers a well-balanced approach with high capacity in preserving edges, increasing detail, and a low-level of computation, future studies will focus on real-time processing, adaptive region picking, and parallelized appraisal to expand the use of it in precision medicine.

Conclusion

A new multi-modal medical image fusion model that uses Covariance Filtering was proposed in this paper to cope with the continuous issues of retention of fine anatomical details, spatial consistency and improvement of contrast in fused images. The proposed approach incorporates a two-scale decomposition scheme and saliency-based weight map formation, and adaptive covariance-based refinement, and thus is effective to preserve global contextual structures as well as local features without external guidance images.

The extensive qualitative and quantitative assessments on multi-modes CT and MRI data proved the superiority of the methodology to several state-of-the-art methods in the main performance indicators, such as Entropy, Mutual Information, Average Gradient and QAB/F. The suggested method always provided better anatomical edges, contrast, and perception, which qualifies it to be used extensively in neurosurgical planning, radiotherapy guidance, as well as multi-mode reporting.

Additionally, the framework attains this better performance at computational simplicity to provide a convenient trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. Further studies will involve the extension of this technique to include more sophisticated, region-sensitive attention mechanisms, which can further improve the diagnostic reliability and semantic significance of fused imagery in a wider range of medical imaging conditions.
