HIV and tuberculosis co-infection in non-European migrants in Europe: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Domenico Benvenuto, Elena Raffetti, Giancarlo Ceccarelli, Pierluigi Francesco Salvo, Simona Di Giambenedetto, Roberto Cauda, Massimo Ciccozzi, Carlo Torti, Anna Mia Ekström
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEven though HIV-TB co-infection is an emerging public health issue among migrants in European countries, the number of related articles has shown a decreasing trend.MethodsTo better estimate the extent of this problem, we analyzed 34 articles reporting both prevalence and odds ratio for HIV-TB co-infection in migrants in European countries. Heterogeneity analysis was conducted to assess potential bias, and a random-effects model was used to calculate the effect size.ResultsThe overall prevalence of HIV-TB co-infection was 9% (95%CI: 7% − 11%) in foreign-born individuals, with higher rates observed in specific subgroups: 14% (95%CI: 5% − 33%) in those from Sub-Saharan Africa, which is higher than the overall average, and 4% (95%CI: 2% − 7%) in those from Latin America, which is lower than the overall average. Compared to the native-born European population, foreign-born individuals had a twofold increased risk of HIV-TB co-infection, with a threefold increased risk for those from Sub-Saharan Africa.ConclusionsOur meta-analysis results highlight the disproportionate burden of HIV-TB co-infection among foreign-born people in Europe, particularly those from Sub-Saharan Africa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11746-0.Keywords: HIV, AIDS, Tuberculosis, Migrants, Public health

Introduction

In the past years, the global burden of HIV and Tuberculosis (TB) co-infection in the European Region significantly increased, and more and more nations are starting to report cases of this phenomenon [1, 2]. Notably, migrants displayed a greater risk of acquiring HIV and/or TB infection compared to the general populations in the host countries [3] and Europe was already the region with the highest number of international migrants in the world in 2020 (with 87 million of international migrants) and the number of international migrants worldwide has continued to grow rapidly partially explaining the increasing number of co-infection reports [4].

Due to crowded transport vehicles with poorly ventilated spaces, crowded living conditions (e.g., in refugee camps), poor nutrition, and co-infections with other pathogens such as HIV increases migrants are more vulnerable to acquiring TB [5].

Uncomfortable living conditions, proximity to each other, malnutrition, mental and physical stress, and limited access to health care services could enhance the vulnerability of migrants to TB [5]. Furthermore, delays in diagnosis and inadequate treatment adherence exacerbate the risk of multidrug-resistant TB transmission, a priority issue in the international public health agenda, but in practice, it may be overlooked [6]. It has been demonstrated that migrants are more prone to conditions that lead to the emergence of drug-resistant infections during mass gatherings or while they are in refugee camps and/or detention facilities [3].

Interestingly, there is increasing evidence that suggests that foreign migrants are healthier in profiles compared to their native-born counterparts in receiving countries – the ‘healthy migrant effect’ [7]. This phenomenon, though observed with various groups of migrants, is extremely heterogeneous depending on the migrant source countries [8, 9].

Considering this, it would be crucial to perform tailored surveillance for infectious diseases among migrants, especially those who have recently arrived. Still, there isn’t a clear definition of the period during which migrants are defined as such. Due to the complexity of the topic and the heterogeneity of definitions, it is still not entirely clear what the prevalence of co-infection is in migrants and how much migrants are predisposed to co-infection compared to the native population. Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to estimate HIV-TB co-infection prevalence and odds among migrants versus native-born persons in Europe, particularly focusing on studies mentioning that patients presented for the first time for this kind of problem as a proxy of a recent arrival to the host Countries, to provide critical information for targeted public health interventions.

Methods

Search strategy

This meta-analysis adhered to the PRISMA standard [10] (Fig. 1) and involved a systematic review of the following electronic databases: MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search terms were: (“HIV” OR “AIDS”) AND (“TB” OR “Tuberculosis”) AND (“migrants” OR “foreign-born”) AND (“ratio” OR “prevalence”). No time or language restrictions were applied. The search was last updated on June 12th, 2024.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only. During the identification of the articles 32 articles were excluded because the full text could not be found. ** articles including long-term migrants, tourists, short-term visitors, visiting friends and relatives and seasonal workers have been excluded

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established before reviewing articles and abstracts. To consider only those articles describing migrants in European countries, the abstract of all articles was reviewed, and only those studies that included European patients were taken into consideration. Furthermore, in the absence of official definitions of newly arrived migrants, the authors read the articles and excluded those studies whose aim was not to evaluate the prevalence of first diagnoses of HIV-TB co-infection in patients with no previous mention of co-infections. The meta-analysis included studies in any language regarding adult migrants from non-European countries to European countries, which estimated the rate of HIV-TB co-infection and Odds Ratio (OR) of an HIV-TB co-infection compared with locally born patients, and who had been diagnosed with co-infection for the first time. The studies that reported cumulative risk were excluded.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Newcastle-Ottawa quality assessment scale as a reference [11] (Table 1).

Data extraction

Authors, year of publication, country, sample size, study design, number of patients, duration of data collection in years, characteristics of the patients, risk factor, sampling, comparison, prevalence, and OR in all included studies were independently. The following were extracted and compared for every measure:

Prevalence of co-infection of HIV-TB among foreign-born.

Prevalence of co-infection of HIV-TB among foreign-born.

Prevalence of co-infection of HIV-TB among migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa (where available).

Prevalence of co-infection of HIV-TB among migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa (where available).

Prevalence of co-infection of HIV-TB among migrants from Latin America (where available).

Prevalence of co-infection of HIV-TB among migrants from Latin America (where available).

Risk of having co-infection of HIV-TB among foreign-born.

Risk of having co-infection of HIV-TB among foreign-born.

Risk of having co-infection of HIV-TB among migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa (where available).

Risk of having co-infection of HIV-TB among migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa (where available).

All the data were independently updated, and any discrepancy was discussed and agreed upon by all authors.

Statistical analysis

Random-effects models were used to adjust for heterogeneity in study-specific results [12]. Statistical significance concerning heterogeneity was determined by a p-value < 0.05 of the chi-square test, and high heterogeneity was indicated by an I² statistic >50% [13, 14].

Visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry and Egger’s linear regression test was used to assess the risk of publication bias across included studies. The study outcome was expressed in a single weighted estimate of the effect size, its upper and lower limits, and as a Forest Plot. The Z-statistic was used to compare the prevalence between all migrants, Sub-Saharan African migrants, and Latin American migrants. A post-hoc meta-analysis of OR and prevalence was also performed by dividing the studies into two groups: TB patient studies and HIV patient studies. The Z-statistic was used to determine statistically significant differences between the two groups with a reported effect size h of 0.3. Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc (version 23.2.0), GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.0), and ProMeta 3 software.

Results

Study characteristics

A selection process with inclusion and exclusion criteria was performed among 671 articles retrieved. Upon reading and evaluation of abstracts, 584 articles were excluded because irrelevant to the aim of this meta-analysis or not related to the population established in the inclusion criteria. The other 87 papers were subsequently carefully screened, and 53 studies were excluded from analysis based on them not being relevant to the topic of interest because it wasn’t not clear from the methodology whether these were patients with known HIV-TB co-infection (n = 7), not being conducted in European countries (n = 12), not reporting odds ratio (OR) or prevalence (n = 21), or indicating possible selection bias (n = 9). Thus, 34 studies were deemed eligible for the meta-analysis with a total of 49,081 patients. The study selection is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The main characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1 according to the PICO framework [15–49].

In total, this meta-analysis included 13 Italian, 10 Spanish, 2 German, 2 French, and 2 Portuguese studies, and 1 study each from the Netherlands, Switzerland, Sweden, and Poland. Studies initiated before 2000 (n = 20) were approximately twice as many as studies initiated after 2000 (n = 13). The sample size of the studies was 395 (range: 22 to 14,491) by median.

Incidence of HIV-TB among the foreign-born population

We had 31 articles that documented the incidence of HIV-TB co-infections among the foreign-born population. The analysis of heterogeneity showed a statistically significant high level of heterogeneity (I² = 97,44%). Heterogeneity was likely to be caused by true differences in the data rather than sampling error. Egger’s linear regression test was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) and revealed the absence of evidence for bias among the studies (Supplementary Fig. 1). The overall foreign-born pooled estimate using the random-effects model was 9% (95% CI: 7% − 11%) (Forest Plot 2a). The total pooled prevalence among Latin American migrants was 4% (95% CI: 2% − 7%) (Forest Plot 2c), and the total pooled prevalence among Sub-Saharan African migrants was 14% (95% CI: 5% − 33%) (Forest Plot 2b) (Fig. 2). There was no publication bias evidence obtained by visual inspection of the funnel plot symmetry and using Egger’s linear regression test. The post-hoc meta-analysis indicated a combined prevalence of 4% (95% CI: 2% − 9%) in 23,302 patients for HIV patient articles (Forest Plot 3a), and a combined prevalence of 12% (95% CI: 10% − 14%) in 22,827 patients for TB patient articles (Forest Plot 3b) (Fig. 3). The Z-test between the two prevalences was p < 0.001, indicating that there exists a statistically significant difference between the two groups.

Foreign-born population’s odds ratio of HIV-TB co-infections

For the odds ratio (OR) of an HIV-TB co-infection among the foreign-born population, 17 articles were used in this analysis. Heterogeneity analysis showed high heterogeneity (I² = 91,33%). Egger’s linear regression test was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting there was no indication of bias in the studies (Supplementary Fig. 2). The meta-analysis pooled odds ratio overall in the random-effects model was 2.09 (95% CI: 1.39–3.12) (Forest Plot 4a). The pooled odds ratio in migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa was 3.04 (95% CI: 1.70–5.58) (Forest Plot 4b) (Fig. 4). Post-hoc meta-analysis yielded an odds ratio of 2.35 (95% CI: 1.71–3.22) in 14,962 patients for HIV patient-focused articles (Forest Plot 5a), and an odds ratio of 1.98 (95% CI: 1.07–3.65) in 16,614 patients for TB patient-focused articles (Forest Plot 5b) (Fig. 5). The Z-test between the two odds ratios provided a p-value of 0.098, indicating that the difference between the two subgroups was not statistically significant.

Discussion

This meta-analysis highlights that individuals from Sub-Saharan Africa have a notably higher rate of HIV-TB co-infections compared to the overall foreign-born population. Besides, the risk of HIV-TB co-infection was approximately twice as high in foreign-born individuals compared to native-born populations, and the risk was higher than threefold in Sub-Saharan African migrants.

Interestingly, our study indicated heterogeneity of the estimated HIV-TB co-infection prevalence. This would most likely be due to variation in migration streams, social problems, and countries of origin [50]. Still, several factors could influence this, such as the characteristics of the migration phenomenon and different policies and applications of screening and testing at or after the arrival of the migrant population to the host countries.

Our findings align with trends reported by the World Health Organization (WHO) concerning migration and infectious diseases, indicating an increasing trend in HIV-TB co-infection among migrants in the European Region, particularly between 2000 and 2008 [16].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, a strong geographical bias exists in that the majority of included studies were from Italy and Spain. This limited geographic scope may not represent all European countries accurately, potentially affecting the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, despite our efforts to minimize bias, the possibility of publication bias cannot be eliminated because null or nonsignificant studies might be underreported. The decreasing trend in the number of articles written on this subject over time is also a cause for concern, reflecting a possible disregard for this important public health issue [51].

Further, the paucity of data that is available for individual migrant subgroups, i.e., Eastern Europeans, restricts us from making certain conclusions regarding these groups. Finally, the statistically significant difference in prevalence found between studies carried out among HIV patients and studies carried out among TB patients underlines the importance of future studies adequately defining the study population and exploring the underlying clinical and epidemiological rationale for such a difference. Another limitation is that the studies reviewed do not clarify at which stage of migration the migrants are at the moment of enrollment within the study. Nonetheless, the studies mention that patients presented for the first time for this kind of problem, which may be a sign of recent arrival. The European Commission glossary includes the definition of a long-term migrant as a person who has been in the new country for at least one year, but there is no definition for newly arrived migrants [52]. This consideration highlights how, in the future, it will be essential to provide globally accepted definitions and classifications in order to make the evidence found generalizable.

While acknowledging the limitations of the available data, our analysis incorporated several rigorous methodological steps to limit the impact of bias, including a systematic review adhering to PRISMA guidelines, independent data handling by multiple reviewers, and formal assessment of publication bias, thereby strengthening the confidence in our overall estimates.

In conclusion, this meta-analysis reports a high rate and elevated risk of HIV-TB co-infection among the foreign-born population in Europe, with a highly characterized burden in Sub-Saharan African migrants. This, combined with earlier published evidence indicating higher tuberculosis mortality in Eastern Europe [53], is a worrying situation for European public health, which requires further rigorous investigations and specific public health interventions for effective prevention of the spread of these co-infections.
