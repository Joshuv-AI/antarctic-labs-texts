Discrete Symmetry Metrics Mask Frequent Phase‐Specific Lower Limb Asymmetry During Bilateral Countermovement Jumping in Youth Football Players
Authors: Mark G L Sayers, Chloe Keogh, Kelly S Jensen, Bruno P Couto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInter‐limb asymmetry is frequently evaluated during bilateral tasks such as the countermovement jump (CMJ), typically using discrete peak‐based metrics. However, these approaches may inadequately capture the complex, time‐varying nature of inter‐limb coordination. This study examined inter‐limb asymmetry during CMJ in youth football players using both discrete and continuous assessments, with consideration of limb dominance. Forty‐three male youth academy football players (14 ± 1.1 years) performed three maximal bilateral CMJs with data collected using dual force platforms and three‐dimensional motion capture. Inter‐limb asymmetry was quantified using discrete symmetry indices for peak vertical ground reaction force (GRFvert), joint displacements, and joint moments, alongside time‐series approaches including statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D) and the normalized symmetry index (SInorm). Asymmetry threshold breaches (±10% and ±20%) and their duration across the movement cycle were assessed, with comparisons between dominant and non‐dominant limbs. Discrete metrics indicated low levels of inter‐limb asymmetry at the group level, with mean values typically within ±10% thresholds. In contrast, time‐series analyses revealed frequent, phase‐specific asymmetry within individual trials, particularly for joint moments, despite no significant group‐level differences identified using SPM1D. A ±10% GRFvert asymmetry breach occurred in every trial. Limb dominance had minimal influence, except for longer GRFvert breach durations on the non‐dominant limb. Inter‐limb asymmetry during bilateral CMJ is observed frequently when assessed using time‐series methods, despite apparently symmetrical discrete outcomes. These findings demonstrate that reliance on peak‐based symmetry metrics may overlook substantial phase‐specific asymmetry, with implications for the interpretation of bilateral loading during athlete monitoring and rehabilitation contexts.Keywords: athlete monitoring, biomechanics, countermovement jump, inter‐limb asymmetry, statistical parametric mapping, symmetry index, time‐series analysis, youth athletes

Introduction

The development of lower limb symmetry has long been considered a key factor in athletic performance, injury prevention, and applied decision‐making in sport and rehabilitation contexts, with numerous systematic and narrative reviews exploring its relevance across various sports and movement tasks [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In their systematic review, Bishop et al. [6] examined the relationship between inter‐limb asymmetry and measures of physical and sporting performance, reporting mixed findings across functional, morphological, kinematic, and kinetic metrics. They concluded that most asymmetry measures were either negatively or not associated with performance outcomes and emphasized the need for standardized metrics and task‐specific assessments. Similarly, a meta‐analysis by Fox, Pearson, Hicks [3] on the impact of inter‐limb asymmetry on athletic performance reports weak negative effects of lower limb asymmetries on sprint and change‐of‐direction ability, while vertical jump performance appeared unaffected. The interrelationships between lower limb asymmetry and sports injury are equally unclear, with systematic reviews showing inconsistent or weak associations between asymmetries and injury risk [2]. Together, these findings suggest that the performance impact of asymmetry may be both task and context dependent, raising questions about the sensitivity and practical relevance of current asymmetry metrics.

One of the primary challenges in this domain is the rarity of true lower limb symmetry, as inter‐limb asymmetries of 20% or more during lower limb tasks are observed frequently in otherwise healthy individuals [2, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Studies reporting associations between excessive inter‐limb asymmetries and injury risk address this ‘natural variation’ by using 15% asymmetry as a threshold [7, 8]. For return‐to‐play decision making following injury, a more stringent 10% asymmetry threshold has been recommended [11]. In their systematic review, McGrath et al. [12] suggest that < 4% asymmetries might be a more optimal symmetry benchmark for return to play following ACL reconstruction. Collectively, these findings highlight the lack of consensus regarding what constitutes a clinically meaningful inter‐limb asymmetry, suggesting that at least part of this variability may arise from differences in how symmetry is quantified.

A complication arising from the widespread use of traditional symmetry indices concerns their reliance on discrete variables (e.g., peak force). Of these indices, the Bilateral Symmetry Index [13] and Symmetry Index (SI) [14] are suggested as the only methods to accurately quantify limb strength symmetry during bilateral lower limb dominant tasks [6]. Numerous other indexes quantify movement symmetry using peak joint displacement and moment data (for a recent review see [15]), with a common approach quantifying symmetry as the difference between the left and right sides (or affected and unaffected limbs, or dominant and non‐dominant sides) before expressing these differences as percentages of the average of the two sides [16]. In bilateral tasks, apparently symmetrical peak values can arise from qualitatively different phase‐specific coordination strategies between limbs [17], meaning that symmetry at discrete instants does not necessarily reflect symmetry throughout the movement. This oversimplification may lead to misinterpretation of athlete readiness or injury risk, particularly when discrete metrics fail to capture compensatory strategies or interlimb coordination nuances.

To address the limitations imposed by these discrete symmetry measures, several time‐series‐based symmetry indices have been proposed, offering a more comprehensive view of inter‐limb coordination throughout the entire movement cycle [18]. The normalized symmetry index (SInorm) is similar to the SI, but with data normalized across the entire movement cycle [19]. This technique enables limb symmetry to be expressed as a function of time. Similarly, in one of our earlier projects we utilized statistical parametric mapping techniques (SPM1D) to quantify lower limb symmetry during back squat time‐series data [10]. This technique can quantify significant differences between data sets across the entire duty cycle and has been used successfully to analyze time‐series biomechanical data [20]. A key finding from our earlier SPM1D research was that asymmetries in discrete data do not necessarily translate into asymmetries across the time‐series. Importantly, time‐series approaches address distinct but complementary questions: whether systematic group‐level asymmetry exists across the movement cycle, and whether individuals exhibit meaningful phase‐specific deviations within trials. Our previous work demonstrated this during the back squat when SPM1D analyses revealed significant phase‐specific asymmetries that were not identified using conventional discrete symmetry measures [10].

The purpose of this study was to examine lower limb symmetry during countermovement jumps (CMJ) in youth‐level Association Football players (Football). This population provides a relevant case for examining symmetry interpretation, as youth academy settings frequently employ CMJ testing for neuromuscular monitoring during periods of rapid growth and motor development, where movement strategies may be highly variable. Given the unilateral demands of football‐specific activities such as kicking, accelerating, decelerating, and changing direction, understanding whether observed asymmetries represent functional adaptations or potentially maladaptive movement strategies is of particular importance for performance monitoring and rehabilitation. The analysis incorporates both discrete and continuous symmetry measures, with specific consideration given to participants' lower limb dominance (defined by their preferred kicking leg) to explore potential asymmetries in force production and movement patterns.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Thirty‐four male youth football players (age 14.0 ± 1.1 years, body mass 54.8 ± 9.1 kg, height 1.68 ± 0.09 m) volunteered to participate in this study. The sample represented a convenience sample comprising all eligible players from a youth football academy who were available during the testing period (training 7–9 sessions [12–16 h] per week). As this was an observational study of an existing athlete cohort, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Biological maturity was estimated as 0.2 ± 1.3 years from peak height velocity using the equations of Mirwald et al. [21] At the time of testing, all participants were free from injury and had no history of lower‐limb or spinal surgery, or current musculoskeletal complaints. The study was approved by the institutional Human Research Ethics Committee (S221687), and all participants and their parents/guardians provided written informed consent prior to participation. Limb dominance was determined by asking participants, “Which leg do you use when taking a free kick?” Five participants identified as left‐foot dominant and 29 as right‐foot dominant. Self‐reported limb dominance was subsequently confirmed using three‐dimensional motion capture to quantify ball velocity during a series of simulated left‐ and right‐foot free kicks.

Data Acquisition

Following the collection of standing height and body mass, 39 low‐mass retro‐reflective markers were placed bilaterally adjacent to anatomical landmarks of the torso, pelvis, and lower limbs [22]. An additional 3 markers were placed on a regulation football. Participants then completed a self‐directed warm‐up (~10 min) consistent with their usual pre‐training routine, including locomotor activities and several submaximal CMJ of progressively increasing intensity. Testing involved participants performing three maximal vertical CMJ, with a minimum of 2 min rest between trials to minimize fatigue effects. All jumps were performed with the participants' hands on their hips, with participants required to stand still for 1 s prior to commencing each jump [23]. No other constraints were imparted for CMJ depth and/or movement strategy, enabling participants to self‐select their preferred technique.

Marker trajectories were recorded in three dimensions at 500 Hz using a 10‐camera infrared motion capture system (Qualisys AB, Gothenburg, Sweden). Ground reaction force (GRF) data were collected at 1000 Hz from two force platforms (Bertec, Columbus, OH, USA) and synchronized with the kinematic data via an AD converter (BNC/USB/19 Rack; Qualisys AB, Gothenburg, Sweden), using standard biomechanical analysis software (Visual3D; C‐Motion Inc., Maryland, USA).

Captured marker trajectories were filtered using a low‐pass second‐order (zero lag), 12 Hz digital filter [22] and modeled in three dimensions using Visual3D (Visual3D; C‐Motion Inc., USA) to construct an eight‐segment model of the torso, pelvis, and lower limbs (single‐segment foot model). Force platform data were filtered using a low‐pass second‐order (zero lag), 50 Hz low‐pass digital filter [24]. The lower limbs were modeled using standard procedures [25] so that flexion (posterior pelvic tilt), adduction, and medial rotation were defined as positive rotations of the distal segment about the joint's respective x, y, and z axes. The three ball markers were used to develop a ball centroid, with the initial ball velocity calculated from the first derivative of unfiltered ball centroid displacements starting from 10 ms after the instance of initial foot–ball contact [26, 27].

All biomechanical variables of interest were extracted during the jumping phase of each trial (i.e., no landing data). The vertical acceleration of the Centre of Mass (COM) during the jumps was derived from the GRFvert data using well established procedures [28, 29]. COM acceleration was subsequently integrated with respect to time to obtain COM velocity and then integrated a second time to develop COM displacement. The onset of each CMJ was identified following the recommendations of Owen, Watkins, Kilduff, Bevan, Bennett [23], and take‐off was identified as the instant GRFvert fell below the 20 N threshold on the force platforms.

Statistical Analyses

To quantify the prevalence, magnitude, and temporal features of inter‐limb asymmetry threshold breaches, each trial was treated as a separate unit of analysis. This approach was adopted because the primary outcome variables (e.g., occurrence and duration of asymmetry threshold breaches) are defined at the individual trial level and not at participant‐level summaries. As multiple trials were obtained from each participant, the data possess a nested structure. However, the focus of the present analysis was to characterize within‐trial asymmetry behavior across repeated performances rather than to estimate between‐participant differences. Paired comparisons between dominant and non‐dominant limbs were therefore conducted within trials to minimize between‐subject confounding. Accordingly, inferential statistics should be interpreted as characterizing asymmetry behavior within repeated CMJ performances rather than estimating independent population‐level prevalence rates.

Within‐session reliability of the discrete symmetry indices was assessed using the three repeated CMJ trials completed by each participant. Testing included a two‐way mixed‐effects intraclass correlation coefficient for absolute agreement of single measurements (ICC [1, 3]) [32], with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Absolute reliability was quantified using the typical error of measurement (TEM), calculated as the square root of the residual mean square obtained from the repeated‐measures analysis of variance. The TEM and MDC95 values provided a framework for determining whether breaches of the predefined ±10% symmetry threshold were likely to reflect normal measurement variability or true changes in inter‐limb symmetry.

A custom R script was developed to analyze the SInorm data for each variable, quantifying whether a threshold breach occurred on the dominant and/or non‐dominant side and the duration of each breach as a percentage of the duty cycle. Threshold‐breach durations were initially calculated for each of the three countermovement jump trials and then averaged to produce a single participant‐level mean value for each threshold and limb, thereby accounting for the repeated‐measures nature of the data. Inferential comparisons between dominant and non‐dominant limbs were performed separately for the ±10% and ±20% thresholds using paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, appropriate for bounded, non‐normally distributed data. Effect sizes were expressed as the median paired difference with corresponding bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, as the median provides a robust measure of the typical paired difference for skewed data and aligns with the assumptions of the non‐parametric analysis.

Analyses of time‐series data were completed using the SPM1D technique [34, 35] and conducted through MATLAB. Scalar paired SPM{t} tests were conducted to compare dominant and non‐dominant GRFvert and ankle, knee, and hip joint displacement and moment wave forms. Prior to these analyses the time‐series data were interpolated to 101 points representing 0%–100% of the movement cycle. Statistical significance was assessed using random field theory with α = 0.05, with critical thresholds determined automatically from the estimated residual smoothness of the data. The SPM1D analyses identified statistically significant group‐level differences between dominant and non‐dominant waveforms across the movement cycle, whereas the participant‐level SInorm threshold analyses quantified the frequency and duration of symmetry threshold breaches. Time‐series data are presented as means (±1SD), with discrete analyses presented as means with 95% CI.

The analysis of the discrete joint symmetry indices revealed distinct patterns across the lower limb and ground reaction forces. The SI GRFvert data were relatively symmetrical, with mean values near zero (−0.24% ± 3.78%) and 95% confidence intervals well within the 10% thresholds. Similarly, peak flexion data for the knee and hip demonstrated high levels of symmetry, with mean values close to zero and 95% confidence intervals within the ±10% threshold (Figure 1). In contrast, peak ankle flexion symmetry data showed greater variability, with some participants exceeding the ±10% threshold and some breaching the ±20% threshold. Discrete measures of joint moment symmetry showed a wider spread compared to the comparable displacement data, with mean values within the 10% thresholds (Figure 2). The variability of these joint moment data was more pronounced that the joint displacement data, particularly at the hip where the 95% confidence intervals were substantially wider than those observed for the joint displacement data.

Breaches of the ±10% threshold were relatively common across trials for GRFvert (100% of trials), angular displacement (ankle 88%, knee 42%, hip 26% of trials), and joint moment data (ankle 96%, knee 95%, hip 88% of trials). Participant‐level mean breach durations, averaged across the three countermovement jump trials, were subsequently compared between the dominant and non‐dominant limbs (Figures 5 and 6). Discussion

This study quantified lower limb symmetry during CMJ in youth‐level football players using a combination of classical symmetry indices computed from discrete peaks and time‐series methods (SPM1D and SInorm) spanning the full movement cycle. Three principal observations emerged. First, true symmetry was uncommon at the individual level, despite group means often appearing symmetrical. Second, discrete indices and time‐series assessments did not always agree, with discrete metrics often suggesting “good symmetry” while SInorm revealed frequent, phase‐specific breaches of the 10% and 20% symmetry thresholds. Third, limb dominance exerted limited influence on lower limb symmetry, with the only consistent dominance‐related effect being a longer duration of 10% GRFvert breaches on the non‐dominant side. Together, these findings add to the prevailing assumption that bilateral tasks inherently promote symmetrical loading, and they reinforce contemporary calls for task‐specific, multi‐method assessments of asymmetry [3, 6].

The within‐session variability data provide context for the interpretation of breaches of the asymmetry thresholds. In the present study, our relatively small TEM and MDC95 values suggest that the proposed ±10% symmetry threshold exceeds normal within‐session measurement variability. Importantly, the relatively small measurement error compared with the predefined symmetry thresholds indicates that the frequent threshold breaches observed throughout the movement cycle are unlikely to represent measurement artifact. Instead, they most likely reflect genuine fluctuations in inter‐limb coordination during CMJ performance. Nevertheless, individual short‐duration breaches should still be interpreted within the context of normal trial‐to‐trial variability in movement execution.

Bilateral exercises such as CMJs are often assumed to encourage symmetrical loading, with CMJ assessments (typically using two force platforms) being common analytical tools in clinics and sporting teams. Our discrete symmetry data show this cohort of athletes having high levels of symmetry for GRFvert and peak knee/hip flexion with confidence intervals well within the ±10% threshold. Although ankle displacement and especially joint moments (notably at the hip) displayed less symmetry than the former, most of these data were also within the ±10% threshold. If interpreted in isolation, these findings might suggest this cohort presented with “acceptable” symmetry. However, time‐series analyses painted a different picture. SPM1D detected no group‐level differences across the duty cycle, yet SInorm demonstrated frequent threshold breaches within trials, particularly for ankle (displacement: 88% of trials; moment: 96% of trials), and for knee (moment: 95%) and hip (moment: 88%). Notably, given the high level of symmetry for the discrete SI data, there was at least one 10% threshold breach in GRFvert in every trial. These discrepancies illustrate the interpretive limitations inherent in discrete indices which, by compressing rich temporal patterns into a single value, oversimplify the dynamic inter‐limb coordination that occurs in even relatively simple movement tasks [15, 17, 18, 19]. The absence of group‐level asymmetry alongside frequent participant level threshold breaches suggests that bilateral coordination during CMJ is highly individualized, with asymmetries differing in magnitude, direction, and timing between athletes.

Our results also highlight some key differences in the time‐series based symmetry assessments used in this project. While SPM1D can quantify group‐level effects and delineate where in the duty cycle those effects occur [34, 35], SInorm can identify within‐trial, phase‐specific deviations in symmetry. The latter enables the quantification of whether and for how long pre‐set asymmetry thresholds are exceeded. Our findings of no group differences in symmetry (SPM1D) despite frequent participant level threshold breaches (SInorm) are not contradictory. Rather, these results highlight that asymmetry is heterogeneous across individuals and movement phases, and that deviations are not aligned in time or direction strongly enough to survive group‐level averaging. This is consistent with prior observations that discrete asymmetries need not translate into time‐series asymmetries and vice versa [15, 17, 18, 19].

A noteworthy finding in this project is that lower limb asymmetries are common in our population of healthy youth football players. This is consistent with earlier literature indicating that asymmetries are common, even among healthy, trained individuals [2, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The ubiquity of ≥ 10% breaches across variables suggests that motor solutions in CMJ are not constrained to symmetry. Athletes can meet task demands through limb‐specific coordination strategies, reweighting joint contributions across phases without necessarily altering discrete peaks beyond specific thresholds. This heterogeneity aligns with the broader recognition that the performance impact of asymmetry is both task and context dependent [3, 6]. Meta‐analytic evidence indicates weak or trivial associations with some performance outcomes, reporting minimal effects on vertical jump and small negative effects on sprint/change‐of‐direction ability [3]. Similarly, systematic reviews call for standardized, task‐specific metrics to better resolve when asymmetry is consequential [6]. The present findings add granularity by showing that even when peak metrics imply symmetry, phase‐specific asymmetries can be frequent and sizable. Notably, joint moments exhibited greater variability than displacements, consistent with moments reflecting the net outcome of multi‐joint, multi‐segment coordination, and therefore being more sensitive to compensatory redistribution of effort across limbs and phases [16, 18, 19, 20]. This distinction reinforces that discrete metrics primarily describe movement outcomes, whereas time‐series analyses provide insight into the underlying coordination strategies used to achieve those outcomes. Practically, this cautions against equating “symmetric peaks” with “symmetric mechanics.”

Contrary to common expectations in football populations, kicking limb dominance exerted minimal systematic effects on symmetry during CMs in our cohort of well‐trained youth football players. Although a small effect of kicking limb dominance was observed, our data showed that when a ±10% GRFvert symmetry breach occurred, the non‐dominant limb tended to exceed the threshold for longer than the dominant limb. Although this difference reached statistical significance, the absolute difference in breach duration between limbs was relatively small and should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution when considering its practical significance. While this finding was not consistent across all participants, it is worth noting that during kicking the non‐dominant limb acts to support and stabilize the body, enabling the kinetic chain to transfer momentum to the kicking limb [36]. Regardless, it is likely the complex coordination of kicking and CMJ tasks is task‐specific rather than representing a general characteristic across all movement metrics or phases [11]. Furthermore, given the mixed literature regarding limb dominance and inter‐limb asymmetry, including studies reporting null or only small effects [3, 6, 11, 12], these findings reinforce that limb dominance should not be assumed to dictate symmetry during bilateral vertical tasks, and should instead be evaluated empirically.

In both applied practice and research, ±10% and ±20% thresholds are used commonly to flag “meaningful” asymmetry with 10% being a conservative criterion often invoked in return‐to‐play decision‐making, and 20% approximating magnitudes frequently observed in healthy cohorts [2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Our findings support a more nuanced interpretation. First, thresholds should not be treated as binary pass/fail criteria. Instead, duration above threshold (as a proportion of the duty cycle), phase location, and which variable is breached (e.g., joint moment vs. displacement) may be more informative. Second, task context matters: a breach during propulsion may carry different implications than a breach during braking, and a breach in hip moment may differ practically from a breach in knee displacement. Third, individual asymmetry profiles and consistency across sessions may be more actionable than single‐session status. Collectively, these points align with recent recommendations to employ task‐specific, standardized, and context‐aware metrics for more meaningful inference [1, 3, 5, 6, 16, 19].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. The sample comprised youth male academy football players, limiting generalisability to females, older athletes, and non‐football populations. Playing position was not considered and may have contributed to inter‐individual variability in lower‐limb symmetry patterns. In addition, participants performed CMJs using self‐selected countermovement depth and movement strategy. Although this approach better reflects the ecological conditions under which CMJ testing is typically implemented in athlete monitoring programmes, it may also have contributed to variability in the observed symmetry patterns. The SPM1D analyses evaluate group‐level trajectories and, therefore, heterogeneous or unsynchronised individual deviations may average out to null effects. Similarly, the ±10% and ±20% SInorm thresholds, although widely used in applied settings, remain heuristic and their ecological validity may vary according to the movement task and outcome of interest [2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 20]. As data were collected during a single testing session, the present study provides only a snapshot of lower‐limb symmetry and cannot determine whether the observed asymmetry patterns represent stable individual characteristics or transient fluctuations associated with day‐to‐day biological variability. Finally, the analyses were limited to the propulsive phase of the CMJ and may not be representative of symmetry during landing.

These results have important implications for coaches and practitioners. Relying solely on discrete symmetry indices may not fully capture inter‐limb coordination throughout the movement cycle. Continuous measures or time‐series analyses should therefore be considered to provide a more comprehensive assessment of lower‐limb symmetry. Furthermore, the frequent occurrence of threshold breaches suggests that symmetry should not be viewed as a binary concept but rather as a dynamic characteristic that varies across individuals and movement phases. Although these findings have implications for athlete monitoring and rehabilitation, the present study did not directly assess injury risk or athletic performance outcomes. Accordingly, the practical implications should be interpreted within the context of symmetry assessment.

Future research should explore longitudinal changes in symmetry during growth and training, including the influence of biological maturation and playing position on discrete and time‐series measures of lower‐limb symmetry. Investigating the relationship between symmetry breaches and injury risk remains an important priority. Developing practical tools capable of translating time‐series symmetry assessment into field‐based athlete monitoring could further enhance screening and rehabilitation strategies. Additionally, examining whether asymmetry patterns differ across movement tasks or under fatigue conditions may provide further insight into their functional relevance.

Conclusions

In youth footballers performing bilateral CMJ, lower limb symmetry is rare when viewed through a time‐series lens. Discrete indices suggested relatively symmetrical GRFvert and joint displacements at the group level, yet SInorm revealed frequent, phase‐specific breaches, especially in joint moments. SPM1D identified no population‐level differences, underscoring that individual asymmetry patterns are heterogeneous and often unsynchronized in time. Kicking limb dominance had a minimal influence on symmetry, apart from longer GRFvert breach duration on the non‐dominant side. Collectively, these results challenge the assumption that bilateral tasks like CMJ produce symmetrical loading and argue for multi‐method, time‐aware, and context‐specific symmetry assessment in youth football. For clinicians and practitioners, this highlights that symmetrical peak outputs during bilateral tasks do not guarantee symmetrical loading throughout the movement, underscoring the need to interpret symmetry as a dynamic characteristic of movement coordination rather than a static outcome measure.

Perspective

The present findings add important nuance to how lower limb symmetry is interpreted in sports medicine and athlete monitoring contexts. Consistent with previous work highlighting the prevalence and heterogeneity of asymmetry in athletic populations [1, 3, 5, 6], this study demonstrates that apparently symmetrical peak outcomes during bilateral tasks can coexist with frequent, phase‐specific asymmetry in underlying joint loading. These results align with growing recognition that asymmetry is not inherently pathological, but context‐dependent and shaped by individual coordination strategies rather than solely limb dominance or injury status. The use of time‐resolved analyses complements discrete metrics by providing clinically relevant insight into when and for how long asymmetry occurs, which may be particularly important when interpreting monitoring data during growth, rehabilitation, and return‐to‐training phases. From a sports medicine perspective, the present findings caution against binary interpretations of symmetry thresholds and support a shift toward more dynamic, task‐specific assessment frameworks. The inclusion of the temporal features of asymmetry into athlete evaluation may enhance clinical reasoning by differentiating transient, task‐related adaptations from persistent or maladaptive patterns, thereby improving the ecological validity of symmetry assessment in both performance and rehabilitation settings. Although the present study utilized laboratory‐based motion capture and dual force platforms to characterize lower‐limb symmetry, the broader implications extend beyond laboratory settings. Rather than advocating the routine use of sophisticated biomechanical analyses, these findings emphasize the importance of considering movement symmetry as a dynamic characteristic rather than relying solely on isolated peak values. Even when using more accessible field‐based technologies, such as portable force platforms, jump mats, or wearable sensors, repeated monitoring of movement patterns over time may provide more meaningful information than single discrete symmetry metrics alone. As wearable technologies and field‐based analytical methods continue to evolve, the translation of time‐series concepts into routine athlete monitoring and rehabilitation practice is likely to become increasingly feasible.
