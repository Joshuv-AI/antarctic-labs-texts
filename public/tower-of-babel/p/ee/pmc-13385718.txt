Monolithic integration of p- and n-type doped 2D WSe2 for wafer-scale complementary logic circuits
Authors: Yan Hu, Shicheng Zeng, Yi Wang, Jinshu Zhang, Yuxuan Zhu, Zhejia Zhang, Xiangqi Dong, Qicheng Sun, Mingrui Ao, Xinlong Guo, Jieya Shang, Yuexi Wang, Chao Liu, Yuchen Tian, Haojie Chen, Xinliu He, Yufei Song, Yue Zhang, Sen Wang, Junlong Shi, Zhengjie Sun, Jiahao Wang, Jichao Li, Liping Jiang, Lin Wang, Chao Zhu, Zihan Xu, Saifei Gou, Yin Wang, Yin Xia, Xu Wang, Zhengzong Sun, Wenzhong Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtomically thin two-dimensional (2D) semiconductors are promising candidates for next-generation electronics, which could effectively suppress short-channel effects and consequently reduce static power consumption. However, the lack of effective doping methods for 2D semiconductors remains a significant challenge, impeding the realization of homogeneous complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) integrated circuits (ICs). Here, we report the monolithic integration of wafer-scale homogeneous top-gated WSe2 CMOS circuit arrays. The p-type and n-type doping methods could effectively modulate carrier polarity and concentration for WSe2, enabling the fabrication of wafer-scale CMOS inverter arrays via a proposed bilayer hard mask process. A representative CMOS inverter exhibits a voltage gain of up to 396 V/V, with a low static power consumption of ~ 30 pW and a noise margin exceeding 90%. Furthermore, more complex CMOS circuits, like XOR and a multiplexer (MUX), are successfully fabricated. This demonstration of homogeneous CMOS integration shows a promising strategy for the practical deployment of 2D semiconductors in low-power large-scale ICs.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic devices, Electronic devices, Electronic and spintronic devices

Introduction

The continued downscaling of silicon (Si) metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors faces fundamental limits due to short-channel effects (SCEs). 2D semiconductors, which possess atomic-scale thickness, a perfect surface without dangling bonds, and appropriate band structures, have been identified as the most promising channel material candidates for sub-1 nm technology nodes1. In recent years, substantial breakthroughs have been made in the wafer-scale 2D material synthesis, particularly MoS2 and WSe22–4, and high-performance 2D semiconductor devices5–16.

With the advancement of research on the material synthesis and devices, an increasing number of medium-scale ICs based on 2D semiconductors have gradually emerged in recent years17–23, indicating a steady transition of 2D semiconductor materials towards industrial-grade applications. However, most reported 2D semiconductor ICs were still implemented based on NMOS architecture23, which suffered from much higher power consumption compared to CMOS architecture. Unfortunately, conventional ion implantation doping techniques in Si CMOS are not suitable for 2D materials because of the severe damage to the atomically thin materials. The precise fabrication of p-type and n-type semiconductors is a critical step in the realization of CMOS ICs. Nowadays, mainstream 2D CMOS typically relies on the heterogeneous integration of n- and p-type 2D materials via a transfer process24–26, which makes it difficult to ensure high device yield and uniformity. Therefore, from a reliability and cost perspective, it is imperative to utilize a single-channel material to fabricate ICs27. Currently, a practical doping method for controllable p-type and n-type doping of 2D semiconductors is still lacking.

In this work, we report p-type and n-type doping methods, and unveil the doping mechanisms to fabricate wafer-scale homogeneous top-gate CMOS circuit arrays based on WSe2, which is a promising candidate for 2D CMOS due to its intrinsic bipolar behavior, featuring both high electron and hole mobilities26. By precisely introducing WO4 or N(CH3)4 dopant group, we can effectively tune the carrier type and concentration of the resulting monolayer WSe2 film. After optimizing the doping and fabrication processes, well-balanced current densities for both PMOS and NMOS devices under enhancement-mode operation are achieved. Moreover, by employing conventional atomic layer deposition, lithography, and etching techniques, we creatively develop a bilayer hard mask process, which could realize spatially selective patterned p-type and n-type WSe2 semiconductor channels. Utilizing this spatially selective doping approach, we fabricate a wafer-scale 2D CMOS inverter array that exhibits great performance. A representative CMOS inverter exhibits excellent electrical performance, with a voltage gain of 396 V/V (at a supply voltage of 5 V), ultra-low average static power consumption of ~ 30 pW, and noise margin up to 92.1% (at a supply voltage of 2 V), underscoring its capability of low static power consumption. Additionally, we fabricate more complex CMOS circuits, such as NAND, NOR, AND, OR, XOR, and MUX. This work demonstrates practical p- and n-type doping methods for 2D semiconductors, and paves a pathway for the development of fabricating homogeneous 2D CMOS ICs.

Results and Discussion

Film characterizations

The 4-inch monolayer WSe2 wafer was synthesized via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) with a thickness of ~0.8 nm characterized by atomic force microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1). The CVD growth processes follow the steps as described in Methods. To investigate the material quality and homogeneity of the as-grown monolayer WSe2, 40 spots uniformly distributed over the 4-inch wafer (Fig. 1a) were selected for PL (Fig. 1b) and Raman (Fig. 1c) characterization. Figure 1d shows a narrow PL peak position distribution centered at the bandgap of ~1.66 eV with an average full-width at half-maximum of only 0.09 eV, a standard deviation of 0.01 eV. Figure 1e also shows a statistical histogram of the peak position distribution in Raman spectra for the 40 sampling spots, yielding a narrow distribution (a standard deviation of 0.58 cm−1) with an average value of ~ 250.2 cm−1, which conformed to the fingerprint of monolayer WSe2.

The Se (3d) XPS spectra demonstrate distinct chemical shifts, n-type doped WSe2 displays a 0.3 eV blue shift in Se (3d) peaks compared to pristine WSe2, indicating EF upward movement toward the conduction band that increases binding energy. Conversely, p-type doped WSe2 exhibits a 0.2 eV red shift, indicating EF downward displacement toward the valence band (Fig. 2b). Additionally, we also conducted Raman spectra to support the XPS characterization results (Fig. 2c). For n-type doped WSe2, the E2g mode shows a distinct redshift of 0.9 cm⁻¹, attributed to enhanced electron-phonon scattering from increased electron density, which weakens lattice vibration. In contrast, p-type doped WSe2 shows a 1.2 cm⁻¹ blueshift, reflecting reduced electron-phonon coupling and strengthened covalent W-Se bonding due to p-type doping. Meanwhile, the Raman spectra of 1L-WSe2 films show good crystallinity for p- and n-type doping treatment, as evident from the absence of a significant peak broadening in the E2g vibrational mode.

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying p- and n-type doping, Density Functional Theory (DFT) simulations were conducted to reveal interfacial charge transfer dynamics between dopants and WSe2. Figure 2d–f presents the band structures and density of states (DOS) for pristine, p- and n-type doped WSe2, with insets depicting corresponding atomic configurations. As described in Methods, p-type doped WSe2 was achieved by immersing in a 10 mmol/L aqueous solution of ammonium tungstate pentahydrate at room temperature for 30 minutes. The WO4 group dissociated from ammonium tungstate solution was adsorbed at selenium vacancies, leading to the formation of oxygen-dominated energy states near the valence band maximum (VBM). Moreover, charge transfer of the CDD map in Fig. 1h illustrates that the WO4 group exhibits strong electron-withdrawing behavior, inducing hole accumulation in WSe2. In contrast, N(CH3)4 group dissociated from tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH) aqueous solution introduces electron-rich states near the conduction band minimum (CBM), demonstrating an electron-donating effect. Similarly, as shown in the CDD map in Fig. 1h, pronounced electron transfer towards WSe2, which is induced by the N(CH3)4 group at the N(CH3)4/WSe2 interface (Fig. 1h). More analysis of WO3/WO4 and N(CH3)4 groups' adsorption configurations and band structures are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 7.

Electrical characterizations

For monolithic integration of homogeneous 2D CMOS, it is critical to develop a selective-area n- and p-type doping process. Inspired by the hard mask process in Si CMOS technology, we proposed a bilayer hard mask process for selective-area doping. Initially, bilayer hard masks with Al2O3 (5 nm)/HfO2 (5 nm) stacks are deposited, where HfO2 acts as a protective capping layer. Subsequently, patterned regions are defined by using conventional photolithography and physical etching techniques. Finally, 1% TMAH treatment will selectively etch Al2O3 without etching HfO2, enabling localized n-type doping of WSe2 in patterned regions. Additional details regarding the fabrication processes are provided in the Methods, with the cross-sectional TEM images of the bilayer hard mask structure shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. The efficacy of the selective-area doping process is further illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 9, where n-type doped regions exhibit n-type behavior, while adjacent pristine areas retain p-type characteristics. Therefore, the selective-area doping process enables large-scale homogeneous integration of NMOS and PMOS.

Figure 3a presents transfer characteristics of ~70 fabricated p- and n-type transistors, demonstrating enhanced-mode operation with balanced on-state current (~10 μA/μm), high on/off ratio (>109), and great threshold voltage uniformity. The statistical distributions (Supplementary Figs. 10) confirm a high device-to-device homogeneity. Carrier concentration and field mobility of representative pristine, p-type doped and n-type doped WSe2 transistors are extracted in Supplementary Figs. 11−13, demonstrating doping-type dependent transport properties.

Moreover, we conducted a comprehensive study to systematically evaluate the robustness, long-term stability, and thermal reliability of the n- and p-type doping methods at different conditions, which are critical metrics for a doping method. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 14, 15, n-type doped WSe2 transistors exhibited exceptional doping efficacy with robust electrical performance at various TMAH aqueous solution treatment duration (5–600 s) and concentrations (0.1–25 wt%), which indicates WSe2 is highly sensitive to the n-type dopant and could be fully n-type doped. The tunability of doping concentration is a critical parameter for the doping process. Supplementary Fig. 15b demonstrates that reducing the doping concentration and treatment time results in a controllable shift of the threshold voltage and a simultaneous decrease in maximum current, thereby verifying the tunability of the effective doping level. Additionally, Supplementary Fig. 15c presents the transfer characteristics of transistors treated with various doping concentrations, with the extracted Vth compared in Supplementary Fig. 15d. The result reveals that for TMAH solution concentrations exceeding 10%, the variability in Vth escalates significantly with increasing doping concentration.

Furthermore, thermal stability (250 °C and 400 °C, 1 hour in Ar atmosphere) and long-term stability (12 months) of n- and p-type doping methods were also studied carefully. The performance of these n-type devices decreased slightly after 250 °C anneal thermal budget and fully failed after 400 °C anneal thermal budget. Notably, after 12-month aging treatment, n-type doped transistors demonstrated great stability, with negligible performance degradation (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). Meanwhile, the performance of the p-type devices remained stable after a 250 °C anneal thermal budget and decreased significantly after a 400 °C anneal thermal budget. After 12-month aging treatment, p-type doped transistors demonstrated relatively good stability, with slight performance degradation (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). The above results demonstrate that the proposed p- and n-type doping methods exhibit great doping performance and robustness, along with good long-term stability. Regarding the thermal stability bottleneck, several engineering strategies are proposed. First, chemical adsorption energy can be enhanced via defect engineering, for instance, by introducing controlled Se vacancies on the WSe2 surface to create stronger anchoring sites for dopants. Second, thermodynamic optimization of the doping solution, such as pH modulation, could improve stability. Finally, kinetic suppression of degradation can be achieved by encapsulating the channel with h-BN or Si3N4.

In a representative inverter, the transfer curves of p-type doped and n-type doped WSe2 channels at |VDS | = 1 V exhibit typical p-type and n-type semiconductor characteristics, with current on/off ratios of ~ 107 (Fig. 3c), and the output curves demonstrate excellent matched saturation current of 12 μA/μm (PMOS) and 13 μA/μm (NMOS) at VGS = ± 3 V (Fig. 3b). As a result, the representative CMOS inverter demonstrates superior electrical performance (Fig. 3d–g). The voltage transfer characteristics (VTC) in Fig. 3d exhibit full rail-to-rail output swing, with noise margin reaching 92.1% at supply voltage (VDD) = 2 V. The noise margins at various VDD are illustrated in Fig. 3e. Optimized threshold voltage matching between p- and n-channel transistors enables high voltage gains of 396 V/V at VDD = 5 V and 180 V/V at VDD = 2 V, as shown in Fig. 3f. A critical advantage of a CMOS inverter is its low static power consumption. Notably, the WSe2 CMOS inverter shows an ultra-low average static power consumption of ~30 pW at VDD = 2 V (Fig. 3g).

Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21 display the VTC and voltage gain of 35 randomly selected inverters in an inverter array, with low deviation and high device yield, demonstrating the potential for large-scale circuit integration. Figure 3h shows a benchmark that compares several critical performance metrics, like voltage gain, noise margin, average static power, homogeneity, and scalability6,28–30. Our results are notable, since most of the previous studies have struggled to achieve comparable performance across these key metrics; in contrast, our results exhibit a significant advantage in benchmark evaluations. The detailed comparison information is presented in Supplementary Table 1. In dynamic tests, the CMOS inverter demonstrates robust switching characteristics across a frequency range of 1 Hz to 100 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 22).

Monolithic integrated WSe2 CMOS circuits

To validate the scalability of the WSe2 CMOS fabrication process for digital circuits applications, we additionally fabricated more complex CMOS circuits. The supply voltage is maintained at VDD = 3 V and GND = 0 V for all circuit demonstrations. Supplementary Fig. 23 displays the optical microscope image of CMOS circuit arrays, including AND, OR, XOR logic gates, and 2:1 MUX. Figure 4a, b displays the NAND circuit schematic and optical microscope image of NAND devices. The static output characteristics of NAND (Fig. 4c) display a correct logic function, which switches into the low state only when both inputs are in the high state. The temporal response of NAND also outputs correct values at 100 Hz (Fig. 4d). The NOR circuit schematic diagram and optical microscope image of NOR devices are illustrated in Fig. 4e, f, respectively. Similarly, Fig. 4g, h shows fully correct static outputs and temporal responses of the NOR at 100 Hz. The experimental results of AND and OR logic gates (Supplementary Fig. 24) also display correct rail-to-rail voltage for all possible inputs. The four logic gates exhibit proper functionality, with clear differentiation between the logic levels. However, the operating frequency is limited by the large RC delay inherent to the long-channel architecture (L = 10 μm). To address this bottleneck for future high-speed applications, we propose three strategies: (1) scaling down the channel length via high-resolution lithography to reduce channel resistance and gate capacitance; (2) adopting a self-aligned gate architecture to minimize parasitic overlap capacitance; and (3) optimizing the metal-semiconductor interface to reduce contact resistance and boost the on-state current.

In summary, we have reported wafer-scale homogeneous top-gate WSe2 CMOS circuit arrays via doping methods. The p-type and n-type doping methods could effectively modulate carrier polarity and concentration for WSe2. Moreover, we employ the doping methods to fabricate a wafer-scale high-performance 2D CMOS inverter array and more complex CMOS circuits via a bilayer hard mask process, which could realize spatially selective patterned p-type and n-type WSe2 channels. The inverters achieve a high voltage gain (396 V/V), ultra-low static power (~ 30 pW), and superior noise margin (92.1%). In conjunction with other fast-paced developments of 2D semiconductor engineering, such as short-channel devices, reduced contact resistance, and high-k dielectric integration, our proposed monolithic integration of WSe2 CMOS could accelerate the resolution of current bottlenecks in thermal stability and operating frequency, contributing to the widespread utilization of 2D semiconductors in large-scale integration technology.

Methods

Monolayer WSe2 synthesis

4-inch wafer-scale monolayer WSe2 was synthesized via chemical vapor deposition using 0.1 g WO3 powder (99.999%) and 0.3 g Se powder (99.999%) as precursors in a dual-zone tube furnace under 100 sccm Ar and 10 sccm H2 carrier gas, with WO3 positioned in the high-temperature zone (990 °C) and Se in the low-temperature zone (250 °C). Sapphire substrates were placed downstream of the WO3 vapor source. The system was evacuated to <1 Pa before introducing the carrier gas and stabilizing the pressure at 500 Pa during heating. After reaching the target temperature, growth proceeded for 10 minutes, followed by rapid cooling to room temperature under an inert atmosphere.

Device fabrication

To fabricate top-gate WSe2 FETs and CMOS inverters, the source and drain contact electrodes were patterned by laser direct writing technology (Micro-Writer ML3) followed by the deposition of an Au layer (35 nm) by electron-beam evaporation. The WSe2 channels were defined by O2 etching followed by p-type doping (L = 10 μm, W = 30 μm). Then, a hard mask was fabricated via electron-beam evaporation, a 2 nm SiO2 seeding layer, atomic layer deposition (ALD) 5 nm Al2O3, and 5 nm HfO2 at 150 °C. Selective-area n-type doping was implemented before depositing the gate dielectric (20 nm HfO2). Another lithography/lift-off/deposition process cycle was repeated to form the gate metal layer (35 nm Au). The electrical characterizations of the WSe2 devices were performed in a probe station connected to an Agilent B1500A semiconductor analyzer. All electrical testing was performed at room temperature and ambient conditions. More details of the fabrication are in Supplementary Fig. 3.

P- and n-type doping for WSe2

To achieve p-type doping, the CVD-grown WSe2 film on sapphire substrates was immersed in 10 mmol/L aqueous solution of ammonium metatungstate or ammonium tungstate pentahydrate for 30 minutes at 75 °C, followed by drying with high-purity nitrogen gas. Similar to p-type doping, the thin film was immersed in 1 wt% TMAH solution for 10 minutes followed by drying with high-purity nitrogen gas to achieve n-type doping.

Selective-area n-type doping

The bilayer hard mask consists of sequential deposition of Al2O3 (5 nm) and HfO2 (5 nm) stack, where HfO2 serves as a protective capping layer for the underlying Al2O3. Subsequently, patterned regions are defined by using conventional photolithography and SF6 plasma etching techniques to selectively remove HfO2 in patterned regions. Next, 1 wt% TMAH treatment etches the exposed Al2O3 regions without etching HfO2-covered areas, which enables localized n-type doping of WSe2, while HfO2-covered areas retain intrinsic p-type behavior. In the end, spatially selective doped p-type and n-type WSe2 are produced.

Materials characterization

Raman spectra and PL spectra of the WSe2 film were obtained by Raman spectroscopy (Horiba HR320) with 532 nm laser wavelengths. XPS spectra of the pristine and doped WSe2 film were obtained by XPS spectroscopy (Thermofisher Nexsa). Atomic force microscopy (Veeco D3100) and SEM (Helios G4 UC) were used to characterize the surface morphology of the WSe2 devices. iDPC-STEM (Themis Z TEM) was carried out to observe the cross-sectional view of the as-grown monolayer WSe2 FET.

Computation details

The electronic band structure was calculated using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. To eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images along the z-direction, a vacuum layer of 30 Å was introduced. Structural relaxations were performed with a k-point mesh of 4 × 4 × 1, a plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV, and convergence criteria of 10−6 eV for energy and 10-3 eV·Å-1 for forces. Spin-polarized calculations were employed for all simulations to accurately model the material’s properties, and van der Waals interactions were accounted for using Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction.
