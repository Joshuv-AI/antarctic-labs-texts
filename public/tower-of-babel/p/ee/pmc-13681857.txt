Comparative evaluation of dentin microcrack formation after root canal preparation with different nickel-titanium instrument systems based on micro-CT: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ang Li, Jimin Zhang, Yijun Lin, Huiying Dai, Feiyang Guo, Min Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDentin microcracks following root canal treatment may progress to vertical root fractures, a severe clinical complication. Engine-driven nickel-titanium (NiTi) instruments are associated with dentin microcrack initiation, but current evidence remains inconsistent. This review systematically synthesizes micro-CT-derived evidence on the effects of different NiTi instrument systems on dentin microcrack formation.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and grey literature was performed from inception to October 1, 2025. Eligible studies were ex vivo investigations comparing dentinal microcrack incidence after root canal preparation with various NiTi instruments. Two reviewers independently conducted study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment using adapted JBI and CRIS checklists; meta-analysis was performed for sufficient comparable data.ResultsTwenty-four ex vivo studies were included from 256 initially retrieved records, covering all tested NiTi instrument subgroups with a pooled total sample of 1082 extracted mandibular and maxillary teeth. Studies testing instruments including RECIPROC, WaveOne, ProTaper Next, the HyFlex series, and TRUShape reported a 0% microcrack incidence under specific experimental conditions. Meta-analysis revealed the following findings: (1) Thermally treated instruments had significantly lower microcrack incidence than conventional ones (pooled incidence: 1% vs. 9%, p = 0.0046); (2) ProTaper Gold had significantly lower microcrack incidence than ProTaper Universal (pooled incidence: 3% vs. 16%, p = 0.0403); (3) ProTaper Universal had higher microcrack incidence in moderately to severely curved canals (20°-40°) (30%) than mildly curved canals (< 20°) (11%), though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.2230); (4) There was no significant difference in microcrack incidence between reciprocating and rotary instruments (p = 0.2095); (5) No significant difference in microcrack incidence between RECIPROC Blue and WaveOne Gold, both reciprocating systems (p = 0.3978); (6) WaveOne Gold did not differ significantly from conventional WaveOne (p = 0.6813), nor did RECIPROC Blue from conventional RECIPROC (p = 0.5334); (7) XP-endo instruments consistently demonstrated an almost 0% microcrack incidence across studies. They may effectively prevent dentinal microcracks even in moderately to severely curved canals. Funnel plot showed good symmetry, indicating no significant publication bias.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this ex vivo meta-analysis, instrument kinematics did not influence microcrack formation, while thermally treated NiTi instruments significantly reduced risk. Thermally treated systems are preferred, and highly flexible, advanced-design instruments should be prioritized for curved canals to minimize dentin microcracks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09363-7.Keywords: Root canal preparation, Dentin microcracks, Nickel-titanium instruments, Micro-CT, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Root canal treatment is currently the most effective and widely used approach for managing pulpal and periapical diseases. As the core procedural step, root canal preparation aims to thoroughly remove infectious contents and shape the canal into a continuous taper that preserves its original morphology and curvature, thereby creating optimal conditions for subsequent obturation [1].

During preparation, instrument contact with the dentinal wall induces stress concentration in the dentin. The highest stress levels typically occur in the apical third and at the point of maximum curvature, where the instrument experiences greater flexural and torsional loads. These localized stresses may initiate dentin microcracks, particularly in regions with thinner dentin thickness [2–4]. These microcracks can impair post-treatment seal integrity, leading to root canal reinfection and treatment failure. Furthermore, obturation forces, occlusal loads and other factors may cause microcrack propagation to the pulp chamber or periodontal ligament, resulting in vertical root fracture (VRF). Teeth with VRF are difficult to manage, have a poor prognosis, and are a common cause of extraction after root canal treatment [5, 6].

Currently, a wide range of NiTi instrument systems with distinct design philosophies are available, differing in kinematics, cross-sectional design, taper and alloy properties. These design variations directly influence an instrument’s cutting efficiency, cyclic fatigue resistance and the stress distribution it exerts on root canal walls [7, 8]. A systematic evaluation of the effect of different NiTi instrument systems on dentin microcrack formation is therefore critical to guiding clinical instrument selection and optimizing treatment protocols.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) provides a non-invasive, accurate gold-standard technique for evaluating dentinal structure before and after root canal preparation [9, 10]. Using this approach, many ex vivo studies have explored the association between different NiTi instrument systems and microcrack formation, yet results remain inconsistent. A systematic review and meta-analysis of the available evidence is therefore warranted.

This systematic review comprehensively searched and evaluated the available ex vivo evidence, with qualitative and quantitative analyses regarding the effects of different NiTi instrument systems on dentin microcrack formation during root canal preparation. The aim was to provide a reference for clinical practice and guidance for future research.

Literature search strategy

Electronic database searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and grey literature (including ProQuest and OpenGrey databases) with no language restrictions. Search terms included keywords related to nickel-titanium (NiTi) instruments and dentin microcracks. All databases and grey literature sources were searched through October 1, 2025, and manual searches were conducted to supplement any additional relevant literature. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

Search results were imported into EndNote 21, and duplicate records were removed. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to exclude obviously irrelevant studies, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible studies for final inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Corresponding authors were contacted via email if data or information was missing. Extracted data included: authors, publication year, tooth type, root canal type, root canal curvature, sample size, NiTi instrument type(s), dentin microcrack incidence, working length, final preparation size, and micro-CT parameters/voxel size. The primary outcome was the percentage of new dentin microcracks observed in micro-CT slices after root canal preparation.

Risk of bias assessment

Since the included studies were laboratory-based, the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was not fully applicable. A customized evidence model was therefore used to assess the risk of bias. This model was developed by following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) evaluation guidelines [12] and adapted the Checklist for Reporting In-Vitro Studies (CRIS) guidelines [13], and incorporating recommendations from a study evaluating the methodological quality of endodontic systematic reviews [14]. The final tool comprised 10 items: (1) No funding support; (2) Sample size calculation; (3) Random group assignment; (4) Description of the randomization process; (5) Standardization of working length; (6) Single operator; (7) Standardized micro-CT-based method for microcrack assessment; (8) Blinded outcome assessment; (9) Methodological reproducibility; (10) Statistical analysis. Items 3 and 4 were related to selection bias, and item 8 to measurement bias. Each item was rated as Yes/No/Not Reported, with a score of 1 assigned for “Yes” and 0 for “No” or “Not Reported”. Studies with a total score of 8–10 were classified as “high quality”, 5–7 as “medium quality”, and 1–4 as “low quality”. Two reviewers independently performed the assessments, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. Prior to tool application, reviewers completed calibration training. Pre-assessment of five randomly selected articles yielded an inter-rater agreement Kappa value of 0.78, indicating good reliability. Formal assessment of evidence certainty (e.g., GRADE) was not applicable due to the ex vivo nature of the included studies.

Data processing and synthesis methods

Prior to data synthesis, dentin microcrack incidence rates from individual studies were converted to event counts (number of teeth with microcracks) and total sample sizes. For studies reporting multiple subgroups, each subgroup was treated as an independent study arm for analysis.

Given anticipated heterogeneity in experimental design and clinical parameters across included studies, a random-effects model was used for meta-analysis. The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to proportional data to address statistical limitations associated with extreme proportions (e.g., multiple zero-event groups). Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using the I² statistic, with I² > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity.

Results

Literature search

A total of 256 records were identified, from which 168 duplicates were removed. Following title and abstract screening, 48 records were excluded. Forty studies were selected for full-text assessment, of which 16 were excluded for the following reasons: 5 studies involved only one type of NiTi instrument, 3 evaluated crack propagation length rather than the incidence of new dentin microcracks, 4 used cadaveric jawbone blocks as the experimental model, and 4 had data that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 24 studies were included in the final analysis. The study selection flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Risk of bias assessment of included studies

Using the customized assessment tool, among the 24 included studies, 15 (62.5%) demonstrated low overall risk of bias, 8 (33.3%) exhibited moderate risk of bias, and 1 (4.2%) presented high risk of bias. High and some medium risks were mainly attributed to inadequate reporting of methodological details. For example, most studies did not describe the randomization process in detail, approximately one-third did not perform or report blinded outcome assessment, and more than half did not conduct or report sample size. Influence of instrument material and design

Modern NiTi instruments can be broadly categorized into two groups based on their room-temperature phase composition: those mainly containing the austenite phase (conventional NiTi, M‑Wire, R‑Phase) and those mainly containing the martensite phase (CM‑Wire, Gold, Blue heat‑treated NiTi). Martensitic alloys have been reported to be more flexible with improved cyclic fatigue resistance and greater angle of deflection at failure when compared to conventional NiTi [39, 40]. The unique mechanical behavior of NiTi derives from reversible solid‑state phase transformations between austenite and martensite, conferring superelasticity and shape memory properties [41].

Consistent with the findings of previous studies [42, 43], qualitative analysis indicated that alloy composition and thermal treatment were key factors affecting dentin microcrack formation (Table 2). Instruments manufactured using CM-wire, Gold, or EDM technology exhibited enhanced superelasticity and flexibility, with very low microcrack incidence reported in most studies. Consistent with this observation, meta-analysis demonstrated that thermally treated instruments were associated with a significantly lower microcrack incidence than conventional NiTi treated instruments (pooled incidence: 1% vs. 9%, p = 0.0046). Direct instrument comparisons further supported this trend; for instance, ProTaper Gold (thermally treated) showed a significantly lower microcrack incidence than ProTaper Universal (conventional) (3% vs. 16%, p = 0.0403).

Role of kinematics

Discrepancies persist regarding the effect of instrument kinematics on dentin microcrack formation of endodontically treated teeth. Some studies have demonstrated that reciprocating rotary files release torsional stress periodically and reduce dentinal wall stress, inducing fewer dentinal microcracks compared with continuous rotary files [34, 44]. Conversely, existing literature suggests that single-file reciprocating systems perform intensive material removal within a short operative period, which tends to exacerbate pre-existing inherent defects in dentin. While such instruments experience diminished torsional load, the redistributed composite stress may be transferred onto root canal walls, potentially elevating the incidence of microcrack formation [45, 46].

Within the limited conditions of this ex vivo study using micro-CT scanning on root canal-prepared teeth, no significant differences in the formation of dentinal microcracks were observed between reciprocating files and continuous rotary files. Qualitative analysis revealed no clear superiority of either continuous rotation or reciprocation with regard to dentin microcrack formation, as zero microcrack incidence was reported in some studies for instruments utilizing both kinematic modes [15, 20–22, 27, 28, 31, 32, 36]. Quantitative meta-analysis confirmed no statistically significant difference in dentin microcrack incidence between the two kinematic groups (reciprocating: 1.0%, rotary: 2.0%, p = 0.2095), indicating that kinematics alone is not a decisive factor in dentin microcrack formation.

Role of root canal anatomy and preparation parameters

Root canal curvature is an important modifying factor. Qualitative analysis suggested that the risk of dentin microcracks generally increases in moderately to severely curved canals (20°–40°), particularly when using instruments with higher rigidity. Although quantitative analysis did not reach statistical significance due to limited data availability, a trend was observed wherein the incidence of dentin microcracks was higher in moderately to severely curved canals (20°–40°; pooled incidence: 30%) compared with mildly curved or straight canals (< 20°; pooled incidence: 11%). XP-endo instruments performed favorably across different canal curvatures, with multiple studies reporting 0% microcrack incidence in both straight and curved canals, demonstrating excellent canal conformity [18, 24, 25, 37].

Preparation parameters also play a crucial role. Across the 24 included studies, final apical preparation sizes varied considerably, ranging from #25 to #40. The majority of studies used #25 as the final size, but several studies (e.g., Ceyhanli et al., 2016; Cakici et al., 2019) prepared canals to #30 or #40. Larger preparation sizes were generally associated with higher microcrack incidence, likely due to greater removal of dentin and increased mechanical stress on the remaining root structure [26, 47, 48]. This variation in preparation size may contribute to heterogeneity across studies and should be considered when comparing different NiTi systems. All included studies emphasized the importance of glide path establishment using small K-files.

Overall incidence and heterogeneity

Pooled meta-analysis of all included experimental arms yielded an overall dentin microcrack incidence of 2% (95% CI: 1%–3%), with negligible between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%). Qualitative analysis, however, identified important sources of heterogeneity: identical instruments exhibited variable incidence across studies (e.g., HyFlex EDM showed 0% incidence in most studies but 18.33% in Mandava et al., 2018 [23]), likely attributable to differences in preparation parameters, canal morphology, and assessment methodologies.

Assessment of publication bias

The funnel plot was largely symmetrical, and Egger’s test was not statistically significant (p = 0.3203), indicating no evidence of significant publication bias.

Discussion

This systematic review analyzed 24 ex vivo studies investigating the impact of various NiTi instrument systems on dentin microcrack formation following root canal preparation. The key finding was that not all NiTi instruments generate microcracks; rather, instrument design, alloy composition, and associated preparation techniques collectively determine the resultant biomechanical effects.

Summary of main findings

Comprehensive analysis identified instrument alloy composition and thermal treatment as the key influencing factors. NiTi instruments subjected to thermal treatments such as M‑Wire, CM‑Wire, Blue, Gold, and EDM exhibit altered phase transformation temperatures. Unlike conventional superelastic austenitic NiTi, which is relatively stiff at body temperature and returns to a preset shape, martensitic alloys remain predominantly martensitic or R‑phase at intracanal temperature, thereby enhancing flexibility and canal conformity [39]. Such unique phase characteristics of thermally treated martensitic NiTi wires confer enhanced superelasticity and flexibility, enabling the instrument to better adapt to the original root canal anatomy, particularly in curved canals. This effect reduces excessive dentin removal and stress concentration on canal walls, ultimately resulting in a lower incidence of microcracks. The quantitative findings of the present study further corroborate these qualitative observations. Meta-analysis demonstrated that thermally treated NiTi instruments (e.g., ProTaper Gold) were associated with a significantly lower microcrack incidence than conventional instruments (e.g., ProTaper Universal), a difference directly attributable to the improved flexibility and superelasticity of the modified alloys. Comparisons between reciprocating thermally treated instruments (RECIPROC Blue, WaveOne Gold) and their conventional counterparts (traditional RECIPROC, WaveOne) further supported this trend: for instruments with identical kinematics, limited improvements in alloy and thermal treatment may produce only modest reductions in microcrack risk.

Secondly, instrument kinematics (rotary or reciprocating) demonstrated no clear direct causal association with dentin microcrack incidence. Both reciprocating systems (e.g., RECIPROC/WaveOne) and rotary systems (e.g., ProTaper/HyFlex) may achieve zero microcrack incidence when optimized in terms of design and alloy composition. The geometric design of the instrument, including cross‑sectional geometry, core diameter, and variable taper configuration, collectively determines the instrument’s behavior within the canal and the stress pattern exerted on root dentin.

Thirdly, root canal anatomy, particularly curvature, plays a key role in dentin microcrack formation. In straight canals (< 5°), differences in microcrack incidence among various NiTi instruments are less pronounced, with most modern systems achieving 0% incidence. As demonstrated by Bayram et al. [19], HyFlex CM, HyFlex EDM, and other instruments induced no dentin microcracks in single-canalled mandibular incisors with < 5° curvature. In moderately to severely curved canals (20°–40°), the mechanical interaction between the instrument and root dentin is amplified. Instruments with higher rigidity (e.g., ProTaper Universal) or those with suboptimal performance under extreme curvature (e.g., One Curve and VDW.ROTATE in Soltaninejad et al. 2025, with incidences of 67.2% and 71.7%, respectively [38]) are more prone to generating dentin microcracks. In contrast, XP-endo instruments maintained 0% microcrack incidence in most studies involving curved canals; their unique adaptive design effectively distributes stress across the canal walls, rendering them an ideal choice for managing complex root canal anatomy. These findings underscore the importance of clinicians selecting instruments based on specific preoperative canal conditions.

The findings highlight the importance of the minimally invasive endodontics concept. Over-enlargement of root canals was identified as a significant factor that increases root structure vulnerability and microcrack risk. For instance, Cakici et al., 2019 [26] reported that ProTaper Next and RECIPROC instruments used for preparation to size #40 resulted in microcrack incidences of 5.68% and 9.95%, respectively, which were higher than those observed in other studies using a final apical size of #25. Additionally, available literature indicates that the working length of root canal preparation is correlated with the initiation of dentinal microcracks. Fewer microcracks develop when the apical termination of canal preparation is set at 1 mm coronal to the anatomical apical foramen, whereas excessive apical enlargement elevates the risk of microcrack formation in the apical region [29, 48, 49].

Therefore, clinical procedures should adhere to biological principles, aiming for effective cleaning and shaping while avoiding unnecessary removal of dental hard tissue. Furthermore, standardized coronal access preparation, proper glide path establishment, and accurate working length control are critical steps for maintaining the integrity of the radicular root structure.

Comparison with previous systematic reviews

The conclusions of the present study are generally consistent with those of recent systematic reviews, all of which support the potential benefits of thermally treated NiTi instruments in reducing the risk of dentin microcracks [50, 51]. However, the contributions of this review are: First, based on the most recent literature up to 2025, it incorporates evidence on newer generation instruments like XP-endo and TRUShape, updating the evidence base in this field. Second, all included studies employed micro-CT technology, potentially reducing mechanically induced cracks from sectioning, thus providing more direct evidence [9, 52]. Third, this review not only focuses on instrument type but also delves into the interaction between specific operational parameters (e.g. preparation size, working length) and instrument design, offering more actionable insights for clinical practice. Fourth, compared to previous systematic reviews relying solely on qualitative analysis, this study provides evidence with greater statistical power through quantitative meta-analysis.

Limitations of this study

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. First, the validity of micro-CT-detected dentin microcracks remains controversial, as observed defects may represent pre-existing cracks, post-extraction artifacts, or dehydration rather than true instrumentation-induced damage [53]. Moreover, outcome evaluation was limited by inconsistent definitions of microcracks and the lack of baseline scans to distinguish new from pre-existing cracks, with most studies reporting only presence/absence without quantitative analysis [54–56]. Detectable heterogeneity existed across studies in both experimental design (tooth type, canal curvature, micro-CT parameters, hydration status) and preparation protocols (final apical size ranging from #25 to #40) [53, 57]. Third, although 62.5% of studies were graded as low risk of bias, common reporting deficiencies were still observed: more than half did not include sample size calculation or detailed randomization procedures, and one-third failed to report blinding methodologies, which may have introduced selection and measurement bias. Fourth, and most critically for clinical translation, all data were derived from extracted teeth without an intact periodontal ligament, occlusal loading, or the physiological intraoral environment. Therefore, extrapolating these ex vivo microcrack findings to the clinical outcome of VRF is highly limited [57–60]. Fifth, small sample sizes in certain subgroups (e.g., ProTaper Universal in curved canals) and residual heterogeneity reduce statistical power, warranting cautious interpretation. The generalizability of findings for specific instruments (e.g., RECIPROC Blue, WaveOne Gold) requires validation in future large-scale studies.

Future directions

Based on the findings and limitations of the present systematic review, future research should prioritize the standardization of experimental protocols (e.g., unified tooth selection criteria, standardized preparation endpoints, and consistent dentin microcrack detection criteria) to enhance the comparability of study results. Concurrently, efforts should be directed toward strengthening clinical translation, including the development of ex vivo models that simulate periodontal ligament support and occlusal loading, as well as the conduct of long-term clinical follow-up studies to validate the clinical relevance of ex vivo findings [61]. Furthermore, timely evaluation of newer-generation NiTi instruments and their performance in complex root canal anatomies, retreatment scenarios, and other high-risk clinical settings is warranted [62, 63]. The exploration of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted automatic microcrack identification and quantitative analysis technologies could facilitate the automatic detection, classification, and quantification of dentin microcracks, thereby improving the objectivity and efficiency of future research. Ultimately, the overarching goal is to integrate high-quality evidence to develop evidence-based clinical guidelines that assist clinicians in selecting appropriate instruments and preparation parameters based on specific root canal conditions, thereby enabling personalized and minimally invasive endodontic treatment.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of ex vivo evidence, it can be concluded that the selection of NiTi instrument systems and preparation parameters exerts a significant influence on dentin microcrack formation following root canal preparation. Clinicians should prioritize instruments with advanced designs and high flexibility, particularly when managing moderately to severely curved root canals. While achieving effective infection control, preparation parameters must be strictly regulated, and the standardized establishment of a glide path using small K-files is essential. Preoperative assessment of root canal anatomy via radiographic examination, coupled with targeted instrument selection, is recommended to avoid a “one-size-fits-all” approach. This strategy can help minimize the risk of dentin microcrack formation and preserve the long-term structural integrity of the root.
