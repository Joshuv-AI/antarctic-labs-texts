IL-17A and IFN-α associated with female genital schistosomiasis lesions
Authors: Edmond François Tchago, Chris Marco Mbianda Nana, André Tchoffo Kemlong, Clauvis Ngainang, Eto Darlhin Tertullien, Albert Bayibeki, Rosette Megnekou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFemale genital schistosomiasis (FGS) remains an underdiagnosed consequence of Schistosoma haematobium (S. haematobium) infection, with limited understanding of its associated immunological responses. This study investigated the prevalence of FGS-related cervical lesions and their relationship with systemic cytokine profiles, as well as soluble immune activation biomarkers, among women living in endemic areas of Cameroon.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between November-December 2023 and May-June 2024, in two endemic municipalities in Cameroon. Sexually active women were screened for S. haematobium infection using urine filtration microscopy. Cervical examination and digital imaging were performed to document FGS-associated lesions, which were classified into neovascularization (NVA), grainy sandy patches (GS), yellow sandy patches (YS), and rubbery papules (RP). Blood samples were collected and plasma concentrations of cytokines (IFN-α, TNF-α, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-33) and soluble immune activation biomarkers (sCD25, sCD163) were quantified using a magnetic Luminex assay.ResultsA total of 218 women agreed to participate in this study, of whom 54 (24.8%) were infected with S. haematobium. Infected women presented significantly higher proportions of FGS (81.5%), NVA (53.7%), GS (42.6%), YS (7.4%), and RP (9.3%) compared with uninfected women (0.61%–13.4%; p ≤ 0.014). Itching and pelvic pain were not associated with the presence of FGS-related cervical lesions. S. haematobium infection was associated with increased plasma levels of IL-17A (p = 0.039). Women with FGS, NVA, and GS lesions exhibited significantly higher IL-17A and IFN-α concentrations (0.0001 ≤ p ≤ 0.039). IL-17A levels strongly correlated positively with NVA (rs = 0.568, p < 0.0001), while levels of IFN-α correlated with both NVA (rs = 0.390, p < 0.0001) and GS (rs = 0.500, p < 0.0001). Interesting, the immune activation biomarker sCD25 negatively and significantly correlated with RP (rs=-0,228, p = 0,042).ConclusionS. haematobium infection is associated with FGS-related cervical lesions, which occur independently of common symptoms such as itching or pelvic pain. Elevated IL-17A and IFN-α levels and their strong correlation with lesion types suggest that these cytokines may play key roles in FGS immunopathogenesis. These findings highlight the need to improve screening strategies by taking into account immunological markers to better detect and manage FGS in endemic areas.

Background

Female genital schistosomiasis (FGS) is a neglected gynaecological parasitic disease that affects more than 56 million women and girls in the world [1, 2]. It is a chronic manifestation of urogenital schistosomiasis, a disease linked to water and poverty that exposes women to risks to their sexual and reproductive health. FGS caused by egg-laying worms that live in the veins draining the main pelvic organs, including the bladder, uterus, and cervix [3]. The chronic manifestations appear as a result of the development of adult shistosomes in the body, which begin to lay eggs that become trapped in the genital tissue, promoting the formation of granulomas in various organs around the eggs, due to an excessive immune response [4] This usually leads to the visible lesions on the cervix [5, 6], which can be inspected using a colposcope or smartphone, revealing abnormal structures characteristic of schistosomiasis infection [7]. This has been shown to cause complications during pregnancy or infertility, as well as increased susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections such as HIV [8, 9]. Depending on the structure, there are four types of lesions associated with FGS: grainy sandy patches, yellow homogeneous sandy patches, rubbery papules and neovascularization [2]. According to a consensus recommendation, at least one of the four types of lesions should be present to establish the clinical diagnosis of FGS [10]. However, the immunopathology evidenced by elevated levels of immune biomarkers has not been sufficiently studied when female genital schistosomiasis is defined by characteristic cevical lesions associated with Schistosoma. which can progress to precancerous lesions [11–13]. In fact, high IL-10 level was observed in both urine and vaginal secretions, indicating that it may prevent immunopathology in FGS and limit severity of the disease, from a shift to Th2 immune response. In addition, schistosome infection can significantly influence cytokine levels, leading to CD4 T cell expansion and increased production of cytokines such as IL-13, which directly contribute to fibrogenesis and macrophage polarization [14]. TNF-α is also considered as a key cytokine mediator in the inflammatory environment and modulates tumor-promoting factors in genital schistosomiasis, mainly in men [15]. But, several of these studies did not sufficiently address the immune response of FGS in relation to cervical lesions. Also, the mechanisms involved in the development of severe forms of the disease in the female reproductive system have not yet been clearly defined. As a matter of fact, the formation and development of uterine lesions associated with schistosomiasis is directly linked to the presence of eggs in the genital tissue [16, 17], but the immune mechanisms involved in their development are still poorly understood. However, numerous studies have revealed the role of certain immune markers in hepatic and intestinal schistosomiasis, without specifying their role in genital schistosomiasis, mainly in women and girls [18–20]. Several cytokines have been found to be markers of susceptibility to fibrosis formation in intestinal and hepatic schistosomiasis [21, 22]. In point of fact, the direct correlation between the levels of IL-9 and IL-17 cytokines and the spleen size, portal vein diameter, and thickening of the periportal space has been shown to reinforce the association of these cytokines with the immunopathogenesis of human schistosomiasis [23]. In addition, the observation that IL-10 is also increased in patients with advanced fibrosis and the correlation found between IL-10 levels only with thickening of the periportal space suggest the hypothesis of an attempt of fibrosis modulation by this cytokine [24]. Thus, similar studies are needed to understand the mechanisms of cervical lesion formation resulting from granuloma in genital tissue. It should be noted that the diagnosis of FGS is not based on specific and precise immune markers, but on urogenital symptoms, which reduces the effectiveness of prevention, and on the endemic nature of bilharzia. The aim of this study was therefore to determine the plasmatic profile of immune biomarkers IL-10, IL-13, IL-33, IL-17A, TNF-alpha, IFN-alpha, sCD25, and sCD163 using the Luminex assay, in relation to schistosomal lesions of the cervix, in two endemic regions in a context of insufficient diagnostic. Results will contribute to monitor tools for female genital bilharzia in Africa and Cameroon in particular.

Methods

Study area and population

This cross sectional study was carried out between November-December 2023 and May-June 2024 in two municipalities, representing endemic areas of female schistosomiasis in Cameroon. The two municipalities include: the municipality of Loum in the Moungo department in the Littoral region and the municipality of Matta Barrage belonging to the Malantouen health district in the West region of Cameroon [1, 3]. All sexually active women residing in these municipalities who tested negative for soil-transmitted diseases and Schistosoma mansoni infection were invited to participate in the study.

Study design and sample selection

Our sampling was non-probabilistic quota sampling; the women included in this study were those who were sexually active, had signed an informed consent or assent form for minors, and resided in one of the study’s endemic areas. Women were excluded if a clear image of their cervix could not be obtained or if they tested positive for Schistosoma mansoni and/or STH. Participants were recruited at health facilities following community outreach efforts by community health workers. The sample size was calculated using the G* power 3.1.9.4 software. This software takes the following factors into account when determining the sample size: the alpha risk (α), which is equal to 5% ; the power, which is equal to 1-ß (this can be 80%, 90%, or 95%) ; and the effect size. For the calculation of the sample size in the present study, the effect size was set at 0.5.The resulting sample size was 130 participants. The sample size of this study was 218 women.

Sample collection and processing

All participants were screened for Schistosoma haematobium eggs and other parasitic co-infections in urine and stool samples. Urinary schistosomiasis was diagnosed using the urine filtration and microscopy method, while additional parasitic infections were assessed using the Mini-FLOTAC technique.

For immunological assessments, venous blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes, and plasma cytokines as well as immune activation biomarkers were quantified using a magnetic Luminex assay. Cervical lesions were evaluated through visual inspection, and high-quality digital images were captured for documentation. All images were reviewed by trained personnel and compared with the WHO Pocket Atlas for FGS to identify characteristic lesions. Each image was grouped by case and anonymized using a unique identification code, consistent with the coding system used for urine samples. All data were systematically recorded in the study questionnaires.

Diagnosis of Shistosoma haematobium

A midstream urine sample from each participant, was collected in clean container during the day and 10 ml were passed through whatman N°3 (Whatman International, Maidstone, England) filter paper in a filter holder. The filter paper was then stained with 1% Lugol’s iodine solution to detect the presence of S. haematobium eggs under a microscope. Participants were classified as infected if at least one Schistosomiasis haematobium egg was detected in the urine.

Cervical inspection and collection of digital images

A gynaecological examination using a speculum was performed at the start of the study and during each follow-up visit. The personnel conducting the inspection were healthcare professionals, including general practitioners under the supervision of a gynecologist, all experienced in performing pelvic examinations. Photographic documentation of the cervix was obtained using a smartphone camera equipped with an LED light. A polarising filter was installed to eliminate light reflections on the surface of the cervix. The camera was placed on a tripod at a distance of 30 cm from the surface of the cervix. For each patient, the camera was adjusted so that the cervical os was located in the centre of the smartphone screen, with a free margin of 3 to 5 mm around the edges of the cervix. All digital images captured during the visual inspection were stored in the REDCap data management system (Research Electronic Data Capture Version REDCap version 9.5.6).

Measurement of cytokines and soluble activation biomarkers

Plasma level of cytokines IFN-alpha, TNF alpha, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17 and IL-33 as well as soluble immune activation biomarkers sCD25 and sCD163 were measured by magnetic luminex screening assay, using Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (Cat no LXSAHM-08, R & D System, Inc. Mineapolis, MN, USA). Briefly, plates were incubated on the horizontal shaker with 50 µL/well of different diluted samples and concentration of standard cytokine or soluble activation biomarkers provided alongside with 50 µL of human, magnetic, premixed, microparticle cocktail with antibodies specific for each cytokine and soluble immune activation biomarkers. After washing using luminex magnetic plate separator (Luminex, Austin, TX, USA, Cat# CN-0269-01), plates were incubated with 50 µL/well of human premixed biotin-antibodies cocktail specific for each analyte. Complexes were revealed using Streptavidin-PE. Plates were read using a Luminex MAGPix Analyzer (XMAP Technology, SN, USA) and results expressed as median fluorescence intensity (MFI). A standard curve was generated for each analyte to convert MFI into corresponding analyte relative concentrations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.3. Results were reported as medians with the interquartile range for continuous variables. Mann–Whitney rank sum test was used to evaluate inter-group differences. Spearman rank order correlation (rs) was used to evaluate parameter association between the different groups. Proportions were compared using Fisher’s exact test. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 218 participants were enrolled, including 164 who were negative for schistosomiasis by microscopy and 54 who were infected (Table 1). Infected women represent those presenting a positive result for the detection of Schistosoma haematobium eggs in the urine, while uninfected women are defined as those with a negative result for eggs in their urine. The age of women not infected was not significantly different compared to those infected (p = 0.522). Schistosomiasis is often associated with itching and pelvic pain. In this study, no significant difference was found between the proportion of non-infected and infected women reporting itching or pelvic pain (p = 0.527 and p = 0.630, respectively). Urinary schistosomiasis can be associated with FGS, neovascularization (NVA), grainly sandy patches (GS), yellow homogenous sandy patches (YS), and rubbery papules (RP) lesions. In the present study, the proportions of these lesions were significantly higher among infected women compared to non-infected women (0.0001 ≤ p ≤ 0.014).

The four types of cervical lesions observed during the study were distinct from one another

The four types of lesions appearing in FGS were observed (Fig. 1) during this study and are consistent with those in the WHO FGS pocket atlas [25, 26].

Digital image showing female genital schistosomiasis associated pathognomonic lesions of the cervix. Circles and arrows indicate the different lesions

Figure 1 shows Neovascularization (NVA), indicating small, unevenly sized venules with a convoluted appearance. These deformed venules are the result of eggs released by a female worm that failed to penetrate the blood vessel wall, thereby blocking the natural blood flow [27]. Grainy sandy (GS) patches, appear as areas with distinct more or less elongated, slender or oblong grains, arranged generally in clusters or isolated in the cervicovaginal mucosa. Yellow homogeneous sandy patches (YS), defined as homogeneous colonies or foci without detectable grains at high magnification appearing overall as yellow hues lining the cervix. Rubbery papules, previously described in the urinary bladder mucosa only, defined as beige, spheroid, firm protrusion, smooth papules in the cervicovaginal mucosa [2].

The different types of lesions were not associated with clinical parameters

Lesions caused by Schistosoma haematobium can lead to common initial symptoms such as itching and pelvic pain. We compared the proportions of women presenting these symptoms according to the presence of FGS, NVA, and GS lesions (Table 2). Results showed no significant differences in the percentage of women reporting itching or pelvic pain between those with lesions and those without. These data suggest that FGS, NVA, and GS lesions occur independently of itching or pelvic pain, indicating that such symptoms are not reliable clinical indicators of lesion presence.

Schistosomiasis infection leads to an increase in the cytokine IL-17A

To assess whether cytokines and soluble immune activation markers differed according to schistosomiasis infection, their plasma levels were compared between uninfected and infected women (Fig. 2). It can be seen from this figure that, the plasma level of cytokine IL17A was significantly higher in infected women compare to uninfected women (p = 0.039). No significant differences were observed for other cytokines or soluble immune activation biomarkers. These data suggest that schistosomiasis infection selectively increases IL-17A levels without broadly altering other systemic inflammatory biomarkers studied.

IL-17A and IFN-α levels were associated with the presence of FGS, NVA and GS lesions

To investigate whether lesions due to schistosomiasis were associated with the cytokines and soluble immune activation biomarkers, their levels were compared between women with the lesion and those without (Fig. 3). The results obtained show that plasma concentrations of the cytokines IL-17A and IFN-α were significantly higher in women with FGS, NVA and GS lesions (0.0001 ≤ p ≤ 0.003 as shown in Figs. 3 and 4A-B). Moreover, IL17A levels positively correlated with lesion NVA (rs=0.568, p < 0.0001) while IFN-α levels positively correlated with NVA and GS lesion [(rs=0.390, p < 0.0001) and (rs=0.500, p < 0.0001), respectively] (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that schistosomiasis-related lesions, especially FGS, NVA, and GS, are strongly driven by heightened IL-17A and IFN-α responses, with both cytokines closely correlating with lesion presence and severity. Interesting is that, the soluble immune activation biomarker sCD25 negatively and significantly correlated with RP (rs=-0,228, p = 0,042), suggesting that the cervical lesion RP increase in Schistosoma infection without soluble activation markers of lymphocytes T.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the clinical, parasitological, and immunological features of women infected with Schistosoma haematobium and investigated how these parameters relate to FGS-associated lesions. Overall, our findings show that S. haematobium infection is associated with the presence of FGS, NVA, GS, YS, and RP lesions, while common clinical symptoms such as itching and pelvic pain were not reliable indicators of lesion presence. These results explain the role of schistosoma eggs in the formation of cervical lesions. Indeed, these lesions are caused by the host’s response to dead or viable schistosomiasis eggs. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first formal attempts to document the pathology of FGS in primary setting in Cameroon using smartphone based imaging. Notably,10% of women diagnosed with FGS did not excrete eggs in their urine. Similarly, Poggense et al.reported that 23% of women with FGS had no detectable egg excretion in urine [28]. Although typical genital changes, such as sandy patches and neovascularization for example, can make women susceptible to superinfection, causing contact bleeding, decreased fertility, miscarriages, discharge, and bleeding, the significant lack of correlation between these symptoms and cervical lesions in this study could be explained by the high average age of the participants, who no longer presented the same symptoms as younger girls. Higher infection amongst this age group has been reported as reason of younger women having more acute infections, compared to older women who have more severe chronic disease from life-time duration of chronic sequelae of infection [29].

Consistent with previous reports [1, 30, 31], this study showed that infected women exhibited a substantially higher frequency of genital lesions compared with uninfected women, highlighting the continued burden of FGS in endemic areas. However, itching and pelvic pain symptoms often attributed to urinary schistosomiasis did not differ between infected and uninfected individuals, nor between women with and without lesions. These observations suggest that, lesion development occurs independently of overt symptoms, underscoring the need for systematic genital inspection rather than symptom-based screening alone. Furthermore, according to current results and the general literature [32, 33], one of the observed effects of FGS is its impact on menstrual health. Indeed, more than half of the women who participated in the study conducted by Christine et al., 2023 reported poor menstrual health (FGS = 73.5%; UGS = 54.6%), whether irregular, painful, or absent periods, showing that more women who are FGS-positive report abnormal periods than those who are UGS-positive. This suggests close links between menstrual health management and FGS [32].

Our immunological analyses revealed a selective elevation of IL-17A in infected women, whereas other systemic cytokines and activation markers studied remained unchanged. IL-17A is known to contribute to granulomatous inflammation and tissue remodeling in schistosomiasis [34, 35], and its increase in infected women supports its potential role in driving local pathology [36, 37]. More importantly, both IL-17A and IFN-α levels were significantly associated with the presence of FGS, NVA, and GS lesions, in the present study. The positive correlations observed between these cytokines and lesion types especially with NVA and GS, suggest that they may participate in lesion formation or persistence. The formation of lesions is often the result of cellular fibrosis, and result from this study may indicate that IL-17A is a pro-fibrogenic cytokine which could have direct effect on cervix cells. Like liver, It may also induce cervix fibrosis progression via other means, including recruitment of macrophages through enhancement of chemokine secretion. Our results may also indicate that NVA and GS present a high risk of cervical fibrosis [38]. These findings are in line with studies implicating IL-17A-mediated responses in chronic egg-induced pathology and highlight IFN-α as a possible additional mediator of mucosal tissue damage or angiogenesis in Schistosomiasis [39, 40]. Together, our results indicate that schistosomiasis-associated genital lesions are linked to a specific inflammatory profile characterized by elevated IL-17A and IFN-α. The absence of broad systemic inflammation suggests that these cytokines may reflect localized pathological processes rather than generalized immune activation. Identifying IL-17A and IFN-α as potential biomarkers of lesion presence may improve future diagnostic strategies, especially in settings lacking advanced imaging tools. The negative correlation between sCD25 levels and RP suggest that cervical lesion RP increase in Schistosoma infection without soluble activation markers of T lymphocytes. RP lesions appear to be promoted by another activation process that suppresses sCD25. These data identify the inability of the immune activation marker sCD25 to promote the pathogenesis of Schistosoma cervical lesions, particularly RP lesions.

The limitations in this study are the fact that, its relied on visual inspection and urine microscopy, which may have underestimated FGS prevalence, particularly in younger girls or low-intensity infections. Another limitation is the fact that the study did not consider co-infections, STI’s such HIV and others. In addition, cytokines were measured in plasma at a single time point, which may not reflect local genital immune responses. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and the limited cytokine panel did not include other mediators relevant to schistosomiasis pathology.

Conclusion

Cervical lesions caused by S. haematobium remain highly prevalent in endemic regions of Cameroon. Neovascularization (NVA) and grainy sandy (GS) patches were most common and associated with elevated IL-17A and IFN-α cytokines, highlighting their potential as biomarkers for FGS. Symptom-based screening is insufficient, emphasizing the need for genital inspection, improved diagnostics, and continued public health interventions to reduce disease burden.
