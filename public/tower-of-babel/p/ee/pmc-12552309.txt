A cohort of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for pediatric rare diseases (allo-HSCT-PRD): design and preliminary results
Authors: Shiwen Hu, Ping Wang, Zifeng Li, Wenjin Jiang, Lian Liu, Wenjuan Ma, Ying Huang, Xiaochuan Wang, Xiaonan Du, Xiaowen Qian, Hongsheng Wang, Yi Wang, Yunhui Zhang, Xiaowen Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRare diseases in children have attracted widespread attention worldwide due to their rarity and difficulty in diagnosis and treatment. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is emerging as a promising and curable therapy for multiple rare diseases. However, rare disease research in China is relatively backward, prompting us to construct the first cohort of allo-HSCT for pediatric rare diseases (allo-HSCT-PRD) involving those who underwent allo-HSCT at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University from 1 January 2014 to 31 October 2024. We concurrently developed a standard data collection, management, and follow-up process, and a dedicated multidisciplinary team to support the work. The allo-HSCT-PRD consisted of 480 participants who were categorized into inborn errors of immunity (IEI) and inborn errors of metabolism (IEM), with 428 and 52 cases, respectively. Among all subjects, the engraftment rate was 84.6%, and the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate was 78.5% (95%CI 74.9% − 82.4%). In particular, patients in the IEM group had greater age and higher implantation and 5-year OS rate. Our next step is to extend our work experience to build allo-HSCT-PRD into a multi-center cohort to boost the development of disease models, exploration of pathogenesis, and clinical trials for new medications.Keywords: Rare disease, Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Children, Cohort

Backgrounds

According to the China Rare Disease Definition Study Report 2021, rare diseases are defined as those with an incidence rate of fewer than 1 in 10,000 live births, a prevalence rate below 1 in 10,000, or a total affected population not exceeding 140,000 individuals within the national population.

Orphanet, the largest global database dedicated to rare diseases, reports the existence of over 7,000 identified rare diseases, collectively affecting an estimated 263 to 446 million individuals worldwide. Approximately 70% of rare diseases are hereditary and have onset in childhood [1]. Based on the large population base, China has one of the world’s largest populations of rare disease sufferers, with about 20 million individuals [2]. Of the 121 rare diseases listed in China’s First Catalogue of Rare Diseases, 108 manifest during childhood [3]. Precision diagnosis and treatment of rare diseases remain a significant challenge, primarily due to the unknown etiology and pathogenesis of most rare diseases, as well as the wide heterogeneity in phenotypes and genotypes. Notably, only about 2% of the genes responsible for hereditary diseases cataloged in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database have been identified by Chinese researchers [4]. According to reports, the misdiagnosis rate of rare diseases in China is as high as 42%, with a delay of around 4.26 years before receiving an accurate diagnosis [3]. Drugs used to treat rare diseases are called orphan medications. They are pricey and unavailable to general patients due to high research costs, limited market demand, and inadequacies in existing medical insurance systems. European Organization for Rare Diseases revealed that 24% of patients with rare diseases do not receive treatment because of the inaccessibility of drugs, while 15% are untreated due to financial barriers [5]. Presently, only 10% of rare diseases are treated with effective medicines [6]. Especially, only 27 out of 60 FDA-approved and 8 out of 27 EMA-approved orphan medications were approved in China [7]. Due to the complexities and uncertainties in diagnosis and treatment, rare diseases account for 35% of infant deaths in the first year of life, with 30% of affected children dying before the age of five [8]. It can be seen that pediatric rare diseases impose significant psychological, economic, and health burdens on their families and society.

As mentioned earlier, the majority of rare diseases are marked by Mendelian inheritance and have no curable drugs. Therapies based on small molecule drugs, protein replacement, antibodies, oligonucleotides, as well as cell and gene therapies, have demonstrated clinical efficacy in the treatment of multiple rare diseases [9]. However, the unknown disease targets, high production costs, the possibility of lifelong treatment and invasive manipulation, off-target effects, and toxic effects have somewhat limited their applications [9]. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), involving grafts of fetal liver cells, bone marrow, peripheral hematopoietic stem cells, and umbilical cord blood cells, can help restore hematopoiesis and rebuild the immune system [10]. Allogeneic HSCT (allo-HSCT) accounts for the vast majority of the total HSCT cases in China [11] and is regarded as a developing and promising treatment for rare diseases when other options are ineffective or inappropriate [10]though challenges such as infections and graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) persist.

High-quality, disease-specific cohorts are essential for driving breakthrough advancements in research. China lags relatively behind in building rare disease cohorts as it did not formally establish a national rare disease registry system until 2016 [12], while the European Union launched the Orphanet database as early as 1997 [13]. It is worth emphasizing that the spectrum of rare diseases differs widely across countries, regions, and races, making it impossible to replicate foreign study experiences. Moreover, although there have been successive case series or case reports of HSCT for pediatric rare diseases, no dedicated disease-specific cohort has yet been established.

Building on the above background and our center’s clinical experience, we are developing a high-quality, single-center cohort on allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for pediatric rare diseases (allo-HSCT-PRD) with the following overarching goals:

Building a cohort of allo-HSCT for pediatric rare diseases with national characteristics.

Data collection

Multi-omic and multi-modal baseline data were extracted from the medical records of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. The variety and heterogeneity of rare diseases and the fact that each subtype has specific focus indices pose a challenge to the data collection process. Given that our center’s clinical practice works primarily with two broad categories of disease, inborn errors of immunity (IEI) and inborn errors of metabolism (IEM), we summarize the baseline indicators that need to be collected for these two categories of disease respectively, including demographics, medical history, family history, laboratory tests, imaging pre-HSCT, and therapy pre-HSCT, although the specifics are different (Tables 1 and 2).

The allo-HSCT information (characteristics of the stem cells, conditioning regimen, GvHD prophylaxis strategies, the condition of chimerism and engraftment, and HSCT-related complications, Table 3) and long-term follow-up information (height and weight at several time points, laboratory tests, imaging post-HSCT, HSCT-related long-term complications, outcomes, and cause of death, Table 4) are key components of the allo-HSCT-PRD and great concern in our research. Notably, we routinely monitored lineage-specific chimerism in peripheral blood leukocytes of patients at 2 weeks, 1 month, 2 months, 100 days, 6 months, 9 months, and 1 year post-transplantation, using short tandem repeat technology. Complete donor chimerism was defined as more than 95% donor-derived cells, while mixed donor chimerism was defined as host-derived cells accounting for 5–95%. Together, these data form a key metric for evaluating the efficacy and safety of allo-HSCT, helping physicians better develop patient-specific treatment and conditioning regimens to reduce toxicity and complications and improve implantation success. In addition, the researchers can keep track of the conditions and changes of participants dynamically in conjunction with baseline data and identify critical factors impacting outcomes and prognosis, with the ultimate goal of improving patient survival and providing precise therapy and medical care for pediatric patients with rare diseases.

Data management

To ensure the standardization and normalization of data, we set up a Quality Control Expert Committee and strictly complied with the following codes during the data collection and processing. Firstly, completeness, meaning that the dataset achieved the maximum comprehensive coverage of rare disease categories and data types as far as possible; secondly, uniformity, where the naming, format, units, and reference ranges of the data obeyed the standard to ensure the consistency and comparability between data uploaded at different times or by different researchers; thirdly, continuity, in the sense that the follow-up information was also collected according to the same standards in addition to baseline data to permit continuous tracking throughout the whole disease course; and finally, security, including the encryption of sensitive data and patients’ personal identifiers, the regular backup of crucial outcomes, rigorous training of data management personnel, the establishment of strict security auditing and monitoring mechanisms, and the application of identity authentication mechanisms and hierarchical access policies.

Any data generated by this study will be stored at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University for an extended period and used solely for research into rare diseases in children. It will not be disclosed to anyone other than the researchers and will not be used for commercial purposes.

Team support

Unlike the disease spectrum of adults, pediatric rare diseases are characterized by variety, complexity, multi-system involvement, and challenges in diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, in addition to consultation with the clinical specialists from relevant departments, the comprehensive guidance of auxiliary departments is also indispensable, that is, a multidisciplinary team (MDT). After a long period of effort and practice, we have established an MDT dedicated to the guidance and management of HSCT for refractory rare diseases in children. The team consists of doctors from clinical departments such as hematology, immunology, infection, gastroenterology, neonatology, endocrinology, intensive care medicine, psychology, nutrition, and so on, nursing staff, specialists in laboratory and pathology, and secretaries. Following the principles of comprehensive evaluation, precise diagnosis and treatment, team collaboration, and continuous tracking, we have benchmarked internationally to achieve a closed loop of the whole disease course of diagnosis, treatment, care, rehabilitation, and follow-up (Fig. 1). Relying on our MDT team, patients are allowed to receive precise, individualized, and efficient medical services, and the medical resources can be utilized and allocated rationally.

Results

A total of four hundred and eighty patients with rare diseases who received allo-HSCT at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University from 1 January 2014 to 31 October 2024 were enrolled in the allo-HSCT-PRD, consisting of 370 males and 110 females. At the time of HSCT, the median age was 19.00 (9.00, 50.25) months, and the median weight was 10.000 (7.975,15.825) kg. Umbilical cord blood (UCB) is the most common donor source, accounting for 71.9% of patients (345/480), with the remaining donor sources including matched sibling donors (31/480, 6.5%), unrelated donors (60/480, 12.5%), haploidentical donors (28/480, 5.8%), and T-cell-depleted haploidentical donors (16/480, 3.3%) (Fig. 2a). The engraftment rate is 84.6% (406/480). We obtained prognostic information from 477 of 480 patients. Over a median follow-up of 49 (22, 72) months, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate was 78.5% (95%CI 74.9% − 82.4%) (Fig. 2b).

There were 428 patients in the IEI group with a median age at HSCT of 17.00 (9.00, 39.00) months and a median weight of 9.500 (7.600, 13.025) kg. The engraftment rate was 83.4%, and the 5-year OS rate was 77.20% (95%CI 73.23% − 81.38%) (Fig. 3a). Genetic deficiency results of 425 patients were available, who were further classified into eight subgroups based on the classification criteria of the International Union of Immunological Societies Expert Committee [15]. The clinical characteristics and outcomes of each group were summarized in Table 5. Patients with combined immunodeficiencies had the lowest age (P = 0.0001) and weight (P = 0.0001), and the worst 5-year OS rate (Log-rank P = 0.0041, Fig. 3b). As for the specific disease, the top three were monogenic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) caused by IL-10RA deficiency, chronic granulomatous disease, and severe combined immune deficiency. Remarkably, we respectively developed a nomogram based on pre-transplantation factors for IEI receiving UCB transplantation (UCBT) (C-reactive protein level, history of sepsis, albumin level, and absolute CD4-positive cell counts) [16] and for IBD receiving UCBT (fecal occult blood, length or height-for-age Z-score, history of sepsis, and total nucleated cells in umbilical cord blood) [17] to predict prognosis and guide clinical practice.

Our experience with allo-HSCT for IEM was limited to a relatively small number of 52 cases, with a median age at HSCT of 73.50 (41.75, 96.50) months and a median weight of 22.750 (15.000,27.875) kg, significantly older (P < 0.0001) and heavier (P < 0.0001) than that in IEI. The engraftment rate was 94.2%, and the 5-year OS rate was 89.94% (95%CI 81.95% - 98.71%), both significantly higher than that of IEI (Pearson chi [2] P = 0.041, Log-rank P = 0.041, Fig. 3a). Patients in the IEM group were further divided into five groups based on specific diseases (Table 5). Although the age (P = 0.0001) and weight (P = 0.0019) varied between the groups, there was no statistically significant difference in 5-year OS (Log-rank P = 0.37, Fig. 3c). In an analysis of 12 patients with hereditary leukodystrophy, it was concluded that UCBT contributed to neurological function in patients with juvenile globoid cell leukodystrophy, while only beneficial in those who had no or mild neurological symptoms in patients with cerebral adrenoleukodystrophy [18].

Discussion

In this article, we provide detailed information on the design and primary outcomes of the allo-HSCT-PRD.

It is reported that lineage-specific rather than total peripheral blood leukocytes chimera analysis can serve as an assessment indicator for disease recurrence, risk stratification, and graft failure, whose value has been validated in hematologic malignancies, benign hematological diseases, and primary immunodeficiency [19]. Graft failure, especially rejection, remains a major obstacle to the allo-HSCT for non-malignant diseases. Considering the importance of continuous monitoring of lineage-specific chimerism, we routinely performed detection and recorded detailed data to enable preemptive intervention and improve the risk identification capabilities.

In our study, most patients received UCBT, whose low cell counts were well suited to the infants and toddlers who made up the majority of our cohort. Our data revealed an overall engraftment rate of 84.6%, which falls within the expected range for UCBT procedures. Clinical evidence demonstrates that UCBT achieves comparable OS rates while exhibiting superior GvHD, relapse-free survival compared to other allo-HSCT modalities [20]. The substantially lower incidence and severity of GvHD are particularly relevant for non-malignant conditions where GvHD provides no therapeutic benefit and may potentially compromise patient outcomes. More to the point, the risk of viral transmission associated with UCB is low, making it particularly advantageous for patients with congenital immune deficiencies in our cohort. As for lysosomal storage disorders in the IEM group, UCBT offers superior clinical outcomes through enhanced donor chimerism and more effective enzyme delivery mechanisms [21].

Limitations do exist. First, although some of the cohort studies for a single rare disease have been published [16–18, 22, 23]the integration of all rare disease cases undergoing allo-HSCT has not yet been completed, which is the next step in our targeted work. What’s more, the patients in our cohort were from a single center, which introduced a certain amount of bias inevitably. Moreover, rare diseases in children are receiving increasing attention and concern throughout the country and the world, which implies the necessity and importance of establishing a multicenter cohort. Relying on the National Key R&D Program of China, we have contacted more than ten hospitals to join our multi-center cohort. Our experience in cohort building and data management can be extended and applied to various centers, helping to convert fragmented and heterogeneous data from different centers into an integrated and homogeneous database. Nevertheless, developing secure and effective data-sharing channels will be a significant barrier for us to overcome.

In addition to the retrospective study, we have the expectation to enroll more participants in the allo-HSCT-PRD prospectively. This permits us to obtain biological samples from patients and their families to conduct larger-scale multi-omics studies. What’s even more promising is that it will also aid in the development of animal and cellular models, allowing us to investigate the pathophysiology and therapeutic targets of pediatric rare diseases, broaden the range of genetic variation, and even take on the clinical validation of novel medications. Of course, the data for this group of patients will be collected and managed as described above. The biological samples will be stored in our center for a long period and will only be used for research related to rare diseases.

In fact, we have achieved sporadic success in HSCT for certain rare diseases, such as the world’s first case of HSCT for immunodeficiency disease caused by IRF4 mutation, and the first domestic case of UCBT for DOCK8 mutation associated high immunoglobulin E syndrome, albeit with too few individuals to form a cohort. As we strive to expand the coverage of our cohort, we are also attempting to explore the indications for HSCT intending to cure more patients with rare diseases for which there is currently no effective therapy strategy.

Conclusions

The allo-HSCT-PRD is the first cohort of rare diseases in children undergoing allo-HSCT. We sincerely hope our current and future efforts will contribute to the establishment of standardized clinical pathways and a national quality control system of HSCT for rare diseases in children.
