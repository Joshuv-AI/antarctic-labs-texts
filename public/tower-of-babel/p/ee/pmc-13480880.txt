Effects of social isolation on all-cause mortality in older adults: do age, gender, and measurement matter?
Authors: Lydia W Li, Jay Kayser, Kyungeun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesResearch has suggested that social isolation increases mortality risk. However, findings have been mixed regarding gender differences, and few studies have explored age differences among older adults. We examined whether the association of social isolation and all-cause mortality varied by gender and age and compared two established measures of social isolation.Research Design and MethodsParticipants (N = 7,026, age 65+) in the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) were followed up to 12 years. 2 NHATS-based social isolation measures (Pohl and Cudjoe), both with 3 categories (socially integrated, somewhat isolated, severely isolated), were used. Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for sociodemographic, health, and health behavior factors, were applied. Interaction terms tested the moderation by gender and age.ResultsDuring follow-up, 2,457 (35.0%) participants died. The 2 social isolation measures had substantial misalignment, with only 36.6% of the sample being classified in the same category. Using Pohl’s measure, both somewhat and severely isolated groups had significantly higher mortality risks than socially integrated individuals, and effects were stronger for men than for women. No age interaction was observed. Associations using Cudjoe’s measure were non-significant after adjustment.Discussion and ImplicationsSocial isolation hastens death in older adults, particularly among men, but findings depend on how isolation is measured. Measures emphasizing family/friend contact may better capture mortality-related risk. Healthcare providers should consider screening for, and using social prescribing to address, social isolation when treating older patients. Reducing social isolation in older men requires particular attention.

This study found that social isolation is associated with increased mortality risk in older adults, particularly among men. The strength of this association differs between the two social isolation measures examined. Findings suggest that both gender and operationalization of social isolation are important considerations when assessing mortality risk associated with social isolation in older adults. Screening for social isolation and interventions to strengthen social connectedness may help reduce premature mortality in older adults, particularly men. Tailoring programs and outreach strategies to gendered patterns of help-seeking may increase men’s willingness to participate in organized activities and access formal services.

Background and Objectives

Social isolation, characterized by limited social contact or interaction with others, increases the risk of all-cause mortality (Barnes et al., 2022; Laugesen et al., 2018; Lennartsson et al., 2022; Naito et al., 2021; Vallée, 2025). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have estimated a 29%-33% increase in mortality risk associated with social isolation in the general population (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Naito et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Despite the breadth of this evidence, existing studies have been mixed regarding gender differences in the effect of social isolation on mortality (Laugesen et al., 2018; Lennartsson et al., 2022; Naito et al., 2023). Age differences in the social isolation-mortality association in late adulthood, a stage of life spanning several decades, are underexplored. In addition, there are no gold standards for measuring social isolation, and it is unclear how differences in operationalization relate to health outcomes, such as mortality. The present study has three objectives: (a) to examine the association between social isolation and all-cause mortality (mortality hereafter) in older U.S. adults (≥ 65 years old); (b) to investigate whether the association varies by gender and age; and (c) to compare the results using two established measures of social isolation.

Theoretical framework and empirical research

Social isolation affects health and, ultimately, mortality through multiple mechanisms, including biological, behavioral, and social ones. First, social isolation is a chronic stressor. As captured by the concept of allostatic load (McEwen, 2017), social isolation over-activates the body’s stress response, resulting in dysregulation of physiological systems, including elevated C-reactive protein and lipid levels, as well as cardiovascular, immune, and metabolic damage (Eisenberger et al., 2007). According to the stress process model (Pearlin et al., 1981, 1990), as a primary stressor, social isolation can lead to secondary stressors, such as loss of meaning and purpose, anxiety, and depression, that also increase mortality risk (Santini et al., 2020).

Second, social ties serve to motivate and monitor health-related behaviors. The absence of social ties can lead to the erosion of healthy lifestyle habits, such as maintaining a balanced diet and adhering to medication regimens. It can also lead to an increase in risky behavior, such as smoking and drinking, that negatively affects health (Kobayashi & Steptoe, 2018).

Third, social isolation makes it challenging to access social support when needed. Socially isolated older adults may lack tangible assistance, such as navigating the healthcare system and assistance during an acute health crisis, which could translate to unmanaged chronic conditions and higher mortality (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015). The lack of emotional support also reduces the ability to cope with the stress from primary and secondary stressors.

The association between social isolation and mortality risk has been widely reported; however, substantial heterogeneity in effect sizes was observed, potentially due to differences in covariate selection (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2023). For example, a study in the United Kingdom reports that the hazard ratio for social isolation on all-cause mortality decreased by 34%, 35%, and 32% after adjusting for behavioral risk factors, socioeconomic factors, and self-rated health, respectively (Elovainio et al., 2017).

Gender and age differences

The stress process model highlights that background characteristics, such as gender and age, are expected to influence the entire stress process, including the exposure to stressors, the availability of personal and social resources, and the manifestation of stress (Pearlin et al., 1981, 1990). These propositions justify investigating gender and age differences in the effect of social isolation on mortality.

Regarding gender, studies have shown that the prevalence of social isolation is higher in older men than in older women (Cornwell & Waite, 2009; Cudjoe et al., 2020). While both genders react physiologically to chronic stress, research suggests that older men show greater sensitivity to isolation-induced physiological stress than older women, attributed in part to a heightened inflammatory response (Yang et al., 2013). In addition, gender role socialization may provide isolated older women with stronger motivation and ability to maintain healthy behavior than their male counterparts, as women have been prescribed the roles of caretaker and nurturer throughout their lives (Grzywacz et al., 2012). Socially isolated men may also be constrained by internalized gender norms, such as self-reliance and toughness, that discourage seeking help (Addis & Mahalik, 2003). In contrast, similarly isolated older women may draw on gender norms that are more compatible with help-seeking and acceptance of support.

Findings on gender differences in the effects of social isolation on mortality risk have been mixed. While some studies report no gender differences (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Laugesen et al., 2018), others have found that effects are more pronounced in women (Lennartsson et al., 2022; Ward et al., 2021). Still others find the effects to be stronger in men (Gronewold et al., 2020; Naito et al., 2023; Vallée, 2025). The inconsistent findings may be related to differences across studies in their target populations, sample sizes, measurement approaches, analytic methods, and follow-up durations. Some prior studies have used gender-stratified analyses to infer gender differences (Gronewold et al., 2020; Ward et al., 2021). Given that the prevalence of social isolation differs between men and women (Cornwell & Waite, 2009; Cudjoe et al., 2020), it is necessary to statistically test the interaction effect of gender and social isolation on mortality in order to accurately characterize gender differences.

With respect to age, research has shown that the prevalence of social isolation increases with age among older adults (Cudjoe et al., 2020). The adverse health consequences of social isolation may also increase with age among older adults, as the physiological systems of those in advanced old age may already have been compromised by decades of biological aging. In addition, health behaviors, such as inadequate nutrition and missed medication doses, likely have an outsized impact on health among the oldest-old than among younger-old adults. Furthermore, with advanced age, the need for care and support increases, making deficits in social support particularly detrimental to health (Cornwell & Waite, 2009).

Most previous studies did not include significant numbers of old-old and oldest-old adults in their samples (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Naito et al., 2023). A recent study including a large sample of adults aged between 65 and 85+ in the United States (Barnes et al., 2022) found that mortality risk for those with a limited social network is 2.3 times higher than that of those with a diverse social network, controlling for sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and loneliness. Another study in Sweden that followed a large cohort of older adults (age 69+ at baseline) for five years found mortality risk 2.5 times higher for those at the highest level of social isolation compared to those at the lowest level (Lennartsson et al., 2022). The estimates from these two studies are higher than the pool-effect estimates reported in the above-mentioned meta-analyses (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Naito et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). However, a systematic review reports that the association between social isolation and mortality is greater in younger (< 60) than older (≥ 60) adults (Naito et al., 2021). Participants included in the systematic review averaged 53 years at study entry, likely underrepresenting adults of advanced old age.

Operationalization of social isolation

Prior studies have employed various assessment tools, many of which are based on the Social Network Index (SNI), a composite measure of connections across four domains: marriage, close friends and relatives, church membership, and group associations (Berkman & Syme, 1979). Rooted in the SNI, two measures of social isolation (Cudjoe et al., 2020; Pohl et al., 2017) based on data from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) have been developed and utilized in a large body of scholarship (e.g., Cudjoe et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023; Xiang et al., 2021). While sharing similarities, the two measures differ somewhat in how they operationalize social isolation (see Table S1). A major difference is in the indicators for the close friends and relatives’ domain—Pohl’s measure uses three items capturing visiting family/friends, and having friends and relatives, respectively, in the social network; whereas Cudjoe’s measure uses one item based on the network size (see the Method section for descriptions of the two measures). Research has shown that family, particularly spouses/partners and adult children, serve as the front-line caregivers for older United States adults (Wolff et al., 2016), and older adults who lack positive family ties face a high mortality risk (Shor et al., 2013). Although not as well studied as family ties, existing evidence suggests that friendships may be especially important protective resources for older adults (Chopik, 2017), due partly to their voluntary nature. The socioemotional selectivity theory holds that older adults increasingly prune their social networks, meaning that surviving friendships in later life are likely to be emotionally meaningful (Carstensen et al., 1999). The greater emphasis on the family and friend domain in Pohl’s measure may translate into greater sensitivity to mortality risk relative to Cudjoe’s measure.

Research design and methods

Data and sample

The data for this analysis were collected by the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), which recruited a representative sample of Medicare beneficiaries (age 65+) in 2011 for in-person interviews (National Health and Aging Trends Study [NHATS], 2011–2023). Specifically, NHATS used a stratified three-stage sample design at baseline (2011), selecting primary sampling units (PSUs) at the county level, ZIP codes (or clusters of ZIP codes) within sampled PSUs as secondary sampling units (SSUs), and Medicare beneficiaries who were 65 and older within sampled SSUs. The sample was re-interviewed annually. If a study participant was deceased, a proxy respondent (such as a family member or caregiver) was interviewed to ascertain the date of death. Details concerning the NHATS conceptual framework, survey design, and content have been reported elsewhere (Montaquila et al., 2012).

A total of 7,609 individuals received the sample interview in 2011 (baseline), including those living in the community and residential care facilities (not nursing homes). Among them, 583 were proxy respondents who were excluded from our analytic sample because they were not asked questions about the social networks used to construct the social isolation measures. The exclusion left a sample of 7,026 individuals who were observed up to 2023 (Wave 13). By then, 2,457 (34.97%) had died.

Variables and measures

Survival time

Survival time was calculated as the number of months from the baseline interview date to the date of death. Participants who were alive at Wave 13 or were lost before then were censored, and their survival time was calculated using the date of their last interview.

Social isolation

We used two measures of social isolation (see Table S1). Both combined multiple items from the NHATS to create a scale, which was then classified into three categories. We called the first Pohl’s measure (Pohl et al., 2017) and the second Cudjoe’s measure (Cudjoe et al., 2020). Their baseline measures were used to predict all-cause mortality, as we were interested in the long-term effects of social isolation on mortality (Dekker et al., 2008).

Pohl’s measure

This measure included four social domains: marriage/partnered, family and friends, church attendance, and club-like participation (see Table S1). Marriage/partnered (currently married/living with a partner), church attendance (attended religious services in the past month), and club-like participation (participated in clubs/classes/other organized activities in the past month) were each dichotomously coded (Yes, No). Three items constituted the family-and-friends domain. Two of which were derived from a social network question: “Looking back over the last year, who are the people you talked most often about important things?” Responses that identified family (relatives by blood/marriage) and friends (neighbors/colleagues/friends) were recoded (Yes = Included in networks, No = Not included) to represent family and friend inclusion, respectively. A third family-and-friend item was based on the question: “In the last month, did you ever visit in person with friends or family not living with you, either at home or theirs?” In total, Pohl’s measure uses six dichotomously coded items. The “no” response to each item was summed (range 0–6) and grouped into tertiles: socially integrated (score 0–1), somewhat isolated (score 2–3), and severely isolated (score 4–6). Limited psychometric work has examined Pohl’s measure, but it has shown convergence with depression risk and divergence with well-being (Pohl et al., 2017).

Cudjoe’s measure

This measure was based on four domains: living arrangement, close ties, church attendance, and social participation (see Table S1). One point was awarded for each of the following: living with at least one other person, having two or more people in the social network, attending religious services in the past month, and participating in club-like/volunteer activities in the past month. The sum score of Cudjoe’s measure ranged from 0 to 4 and was classified as socially integrated (score 2–4), somewhat isolated (score = 1), and severely isolated (score = 0). Psychometric properties of the measure are unclear (Cudjoe et al., 2020).

Covariates

We have included sociodemographic factors and multiple indicators of health status and health behavior, all measured at baseline, as covariates to reduce potential confounding.

Sociodemographic characteristics

These include participants’ age, gender, race, education, income, birthplace, and residential settings. Age was a 3-category variable representing young-old (65–74), old-old (75–84), and oldest-old (85+). Gender was dichotomously coded (Male or Female), race had four categories (White, Black, Hispanic, or Other races), as did education (Below high school, High school graduate, Associate degree, or Bachelor’s degree and above). Income was based on an imputed income variable provided by the NHATS research team and was categorized into tertiles to represent low, moderate, and high income. Birthplace (U.S.-born or Foreign-born) and residential setting (Community or Residential care facility) were coded dichotomously.

Health status

We included physical, emotional, and cognitive health status as covariates. Physical health indicators included chronic conditions which was a count of eight diseases (heart disease, high blood pressure, arthritis, osteoporosis, diabetes, lung disease, stroke, and cancer) that participants reported to have been diagnosed with; limitations in ADL (eating, toileting, bathing, and dressing) and IADL (laundry, meal preparation, shopping, and banking) that were each dichotomously coded (No limitations or Any limitations), and self-rated health which was collapsed into two categories (Good or Poor) from a single-item with five response categories.

Emotional health was assessed by the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) (Kroenke et al., 2009). Two dichotomous variables—anxiety and depression—were constructed based on the scoring scheme suggested (Kroenke et al., 2009). Cognitive health was assessed via a recall task. Respondents were given ten words and asked to recall them immediately and again after a brief delay. We summed the total number of correct recalls and used the sample mean to classify individuals as having good (at or above the mean) or poor memory (below the mean).

Health behaviors

We constructed two health behavior variables–smoking and physical activity. Smoking was dichotomously coded (Yes = Currently smoking, No = Not). Physical activity was assessed by questions that asked participants about engagement in walking and vigorous physical activity in the past month and was categorized into three groups: Vigorous (engaged in vigorous activity), Some (walked but did not engage in any vigorous activity), and None (neither walked nor engaged in any vigorous activity).

Data analysis

We conducted descriptive analyses to examine the distributions of health status, health behaviors, and mortality across the two social isolation measures, as well as the overlap between the two measures. To examine the effect of social isolation on mortality, we first conducted a Kaplan-Meier analysis and a log-rank test. Next, we used Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine the effect of social isolation and its interactions with gender and age on mortality. Five models were estimated. The first model included social isolation and sociodemographic characteristics as covariates. Health status (Model 2) and health behavior factors (Model 3) were subsequently added as covariates. We then added a product term, Social Isolation × Gender, to the fully adjusted model (Model 3) to test whether the effects of social isolation on mortality vary between men and women (Model 4). Likewise, a product term, Social Isolation × Age Group, was added to Model 3 to test the interaction effects of social isolation and age. Wald tests were used to test the main and interaction effects of social isolation.

To assess the proportional hazards assumption of the Cox regression models, we used Schoenfeld residuals, log-log plots of survival, and Kaplan-Meier and predictor survival plots. These tests suggested that the proportional assumption might be violated and that IADL limitations might be responsible. We stratified the Cox regression models by IADL limitations, allowing the baseline hazard to differ between the two strata, thereby resolving the non-proportional hazards issue (George et al., 2014). For example, for the model including the Social Isolation × Gender interaction term (Model 4), the Schoenfeld residuals showed a non-significant global test (p > .05); the log-log plots showed roughly parallel curves for all categorical variables; and the Kaplan-Meier and predictor survival plots showed almost overlapping curves. We used the weighting method suggested by the NHATS team to adjust for the complex survey designs and sampling methods (Freedman et al., 2020). All analyses were conducted using Stata 18, and the svy prefix and (subpop) option were used for estimation whenever appropriate (StataCorp, 2023). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Sample characteristics

Approximately 56.5% of participants were women; a majority (81.4%) identified as White; and more than half (51.5%) had postsecondary education. A vast majority was born in the United States (89.1%) and lived in the community (95.2%).

Bivariate associations of two social isolation measures with health status, health behavior, and mortality

For both Pohl and Cudjoe’s measures of social isolation, severely isolated individuals fared the worst across all indicators of health status and health behavior, followed by those who were somewhat isolated and those categorized as socially integrated (Table S2). Both social isolation measures were significantly associated with all health status (chronic conditions, self-rated health, ADL limitations, IADL limitations, memory, depression, and anxiety) and health behavior (smoking and physical activity) variables (p < .001). The severely and somewhat isolated group had higher percentages of deaths than the socially integrated group for both measures (Table S2).

Alignments between two measures of social isolation

The two social isolation measures were significantly correlated (p < .001). However, there was substantial misalignment between the two (see Table S3). Only about 36.6% of the total sample was classified in the same category by both measures. A large percentage (48.7%) was classified as somewhat isolated by Pohl’s measure and socially integrated by Cudjoe’s measure. Overall, Cudjoe’s measure had a larger proportion of the sample in the socially integrated category than Pohl’s measure (74.7% vs. 20.9%), and Pohl’s measure had a larger proportion in the somewhat (60% vs. 20.7%) and severely (19.1% vs. 4.6%) isolated categories than Cudjoe’s.

Main effects of social isolation on mortality

We first estimated Kaplan-Meier curves to check whether different levels of social isolation were differentially associated with survival times. As shown in Figure 1, for both Pohl’s (left panel) and Cudjoe’s (right panel) measures, the severely isolated group had the shortest survival time, followed by the somewhat isolated and socially integrated groups. The differences were statistically significant (log-rank tests using Pohl and Cudjoe’s measures, respectively, were χ2(2) = 255 and χ2(2) = 125; p < .001 for both).

Gender and age differences in the effects of social isolation on mortality

We added a product term, Social Isolation × Gender, to the fully adjusted model to test the interaction effect of social isolation and gender on mortality (Model 4, Table 2). The results show that when using Pohl’s measure, the interaction effect was statistically significant (adjusted Wald test: F [2, 55] = 13.56, p = .02). Figure 2 illustrates the interaction effects. The adjusted survival curves for men who were somewhat and severely isolated were steeper, indicating shorter survival time than that for socially integrated men. However, the differences among the three categories of social isolation were minor in women. Specifically, for men, those who were somewhat and severely isolated had HRs of 1.465 (95% CI = 1.192, 1.800) and 1.752 (95% CI = 1.415, 2.169), respectively (Table 2, Model 4). There was no statistically significant difference in the hazard ratios in women. However, when using Cudjoe’s measure of social isolation, the interaction effect of social isolation and gender was not statistically significant (Table 3, Model 4; adjusted Wald test: F [2, 55] = 1.10, p = .34).

We also tested the interaction effect of social isolation and age groups, which was not statistically significant using either Pohl’s or Cudjoe’s measure of social isolation (not shown in Tables 2 and 3).

Exploring discrepant effects between two social isolation measures

To examine the discrepancy between Pohl’s and Cudjoe’s measures, we used individual items from both measures to predict mortality, adjusted for all covariates (Table S4). Results show that two items had statistically significant effects (p < .05): friend/family visit (HR = 0.807, 95% CI = 0.703, 0.927) and religious service attendance (HR = 0.907, 95% CI = 0.830, 0.991). The former had the largest effect size among all the items. Marital status (HR = 0.905, 95% CI = 0.809, 1.013) and club/volunteer activity participation (HR = 0.909, 95% CI = 0.818, 1.011) had a p-value < 0.1. Friend/family visit and marital status were included only in Pohl’s measure, religious attendance was in both measures, and club/volunteer activity participation was used in Cudjoe’s measure.

Sensitivity analysis

When social isolation was operationalized as a continuous rather than categorical variable in the same Cox proportional hazards regression models, the results were unchanged. Specifically, the HR was statistically significant when using Pohl’s (1.09, 95% CI = 1.04, 1.14) but not Cudjoe’s measure (1.04, 95% CI = .99, 1.09), adjusted for all covariates (comparable to Model 3 of Tables 2 and 3). Likewise, the interaction effect with gender was statistically significant for Pohl’s (HR = 1.10, 95% CI = 1.01–1.21) but not for Cudjoe’s measure (HR = 1.07, 95% CI = .98–1.17). Note that the inter-item reliability for both measures was low (α = .35 for Pohl and α = .26 for Cudjoe).

We have also used age as a continuous variable, including age and age2 in the models. While both age and age2 were statistically significant in predicting mortality, the results regarding the interaction effect of social isolation and age remained unchanged (non-statistically significant for both social isolation measures).

Discussion and Implications

Based on a 12-year follow-up of a representative sample of Medicare beneficiaries in the United States, we found that social isolation significantly predicts all-cause mortality after adjusting for sociodemographic, health, and health behavior factors, and that the effects are stronger for men than for women, consistent with our hypotheses. However, these results are evident only when Pohl’s measure of social isolation, but not Cudjoe’s, is used. Contrary to our hypothesis, we did not find the effect of social isolation to vary by age group, suggesting that social isolation has a similar impact on mortality across all ages in later adulthood. We focus on the findings pertaining to Pohl’s measure below.

Our findings, which indicate that social isolation increases mortality risks, are consistent with prior research (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Naito et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Previous studies have reported mixed findings on gender differences (Lennartsson et al., 2022; Vallée, 2025). Our study provides evidence that the effect of social isolation on mortality is greater for older men than for older women. Specifically, the mortality risks are 47% and 75% higher for somewhat and severely isolated men, respectively, relative to socially integrated men. In contrast, the comparable hazard ratios in women are not statistically significant. As discussed in the introduction, a possible, theoretically grounded explanation for the observed differences is that biological, behavioral, and social pathways associated with social isolation vary across genders (Addis & Mahalik, 2003; Grzywacz et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2013). Future research is needed to examine how gender influences the pathways linking social isolation to mortality.

The two social isolation measures (Pohl and Cudjoe) showed substantial misalignment (see Table S3), despite both being rooted in the Social Network Index (SNI; Berkman & Syme, 1979). Unlike the results with Pohl’s measure, using Cudjoe’s measure yields no statistically significant main effect of social isolation and no interaction effect with gender after adjusting for health and health behavior factors. Our analysis suggests that including friend/family visit and marital status, as Pohl’s measure does, likely contributes to its stronger association with mortality compared with Cudjoe’s measure, in which the comparable items are network size and living arrangement (Tables S3 and S4). In-person visits with friends and family denote geographic proximity and the availability of tangible assistance, such as transportation to medical appointments and support during an acute health crisis, which can be critical for health maintenance and survival. Network size might not have captured the availability of tangible assistance, as family and friends living far away are unable to provide such help. In addition, for many older adults, a spouse serves as the primary caregiver and confidant. Although living arrangements overlap with marital status, non-spousal co-residents may contribute less reliably than spouses to care and support.

We also found that church attendance, which was included in both social isolation measures, was a significant predictor of mortality. Of note, participation in club/volunteer activity, which is used by Cudjoe’s measure, had a larger effect size than participation in club activity only, which is used by Pohl’s measure. In additional analysis, we explored the effect of volunteer activity participation on mortality and found it to be statistically significant (HR = 0.840, 95% CI = .738, .956). It is plausible that a composite measure of social isolation for older adults that includes friend/family visits, church attendance, marital status, and volunteer activity could better capture the construct and, at a minimum, improve predictive performance. We constructed such a measure and used it as a continuous variable in the fully adjusted Cox regression model (analogous to Model 3 in Table 2). Compared with the continuous measures of Pohl and Cudjoe, this new measure had a larger effect size (HR = 1.121 vs. 1.090 and 1.043 for Pohl and Cudjoe). As a caveat, this new data-driven measure is a by-product of this study; further conceptual and empirical scrutiny would be needed to determine its value. Nonetheless, the misalignment between Pohl and Cudjoe’s measures and their discrepant results regarding associations with mortality shows that how social isolation is operationalized matters. More work is needed to advance the measurement of social isolation.

We have included a wide range of covariates in the analysis, some of which may serve as mediators between social isolation and mortality (Elovainio et al., 2017). Using a formula to calculate the percentage of excess risk mediated (Lin et al., 1997), health factors (seven variables) and health behavior factors (two variables) are similar in their contribution to excess risk mediated. Specifically, health factors explained approximately 6.2% and 16.4% of the effects of somewhat and severe isolation, respectively, and health behavior factors explained about 7.8% and 14.9% of the comparable effects. Our findings support previous studies suggesting that health behaviors may be central to understanding the mechanisms by which social isolation affects health (Kim et al., 2024; Luo & Hendryx, 2022). We also note that controlling for health behaviors might have attenuated the moderating effect of gender on the association between social isolation and mortality.

Some limitations of this study should be noted. First, due to data limitations, we have included only smoking and physical activity as indicators of health behaviors. Some potential confounders and mediators, including alcohol consumption, diet, and use of preventive healthcare, are not considered in the analysis. Second, social isolation may have changed over the follow-up period. Our additional analysis suggests that social isolation was relatively stable, with about 74% of the sample remaining socially integrated or isolated across all assessment points. We did not use social isolation as a time-varying variable because we were interested in the long-term, rather than the short-term, effect of social isolation on mortality (Dekker et al., 2008). Third, the findings may not be generalized to contexts outside the United States. Societal structures, cultural values, and norms vary across societies and may influence the impact of social isolation.

This study has several strengths. First, it included a large representative sample of Medicare beneficiaries in the United States at baseline, providing more robust findings that can be generalized to the overall population of U.S. older adults. Second, the sample was followed up relatively frequently (annually) and vigorously (regardless of relocation), and proxy interviews were conducted for deceased respondents, increasing confidence in the data. Third, the follow-up period was relatively long (12 years), allowing for a more complete picture of event occurrence (death) and increasing the stability of estimates. Fourth, two previously NHATS-based measures of social isolation were examined, highlighting the importance of in-person contact with friends and relatives in assessing social isolation among older adults. Ultimately, the study reveals that social isolation is associated with increased mortality in later life, with particularly pronounced effects among older men.

Our findings suggest that healthcare providers should consider screening for social isolation when treating older patients and use social prescribing to address the problem. Several brief instruments, such as the Social Network Index, the abbreviated Lubben Social Network Scale, and the Social Interaction subscale of the Duke Social Support Index, are available for screening purposes (Berkman & Syme, 1979; Koenig et al., 1993; Lubben et al., 2006). However, social prescribing is not as well established in the United States as in other countries (Scarpetti et al., 2024). While recommendations for adopting social prescribing exist (Marshall et al., 2025), its systematic adoption in the United States will require provider education, individualized planning, resource mobilization, and cross-sector collaborations. Resources are needed to establish and operate such a system.

Reducing social isolation in older men requires particular attention, as our findings suggest that they have greater mortality risks associated with social isolation than women. Older men are less likely than women to participate in senior center activities (Pardasani & Berkman, 2021). Service providers could explore whether tailored and gender-specific approaches, such as Men’s Sheds (Milligan et al., 2016), that bring men together for a shared purpose, would enhance their social participation. Socially isolated older men may also be reluctant to acknowledge deprivation and seek support or services. Reaching them may require a more proactive outreach strategy in addition to tailoring and presenting services in ways that better align with gendered understandings of independence and help-seeking.

In this large, nationally representative cohort of older adults followed across twelve years, social isolation is associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality, particularly among men. The strength and significance of this association vary across the two measures of social isolation, with Pohl’s measure, which captures in-person contact with family/friends and marital status, showing a stronger association with mortality than Cudjoe’s. These findings highlight that both the choice of social isolation measure and consideration of gender differences are important when assessing mortality risk among older adults. Screening for social isolation and implementing targeted interventions to strengthen social connectedness may help reduce premature death in older adults. Future research should investigate the mechanisms underlying the relationship between social isolation and mortality, including the gender differences, and assess whether tailored social support strategies can improve survival outcomes.
