Investigating the relationship between blood metabolites and diabetic retinopathy using two-sample mendelian randomization and in vivo validation
Authors: Yihuan Zeng, Guangmeng Mo, Xiaoyv Wang, Yan Yang, Yan Dong, Ruiying Zhong, Ni Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe addressed fundamental questions about the influence of metabolites on the development of Diabetic retinopathy (DR), and explored the related pathological mechanism. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) database data for metabolites and DR were used to perform Mendelian randomization (MR) studies. The inverse variance weighting (IVW) was chosen as the primary analysis method. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using MR-PRESSO, leave-one-out and Cochran’s Q test. Confounding factors were eliminated to ensure robustness. We also conducted metabolic pathway analysis. In vivo experimental validation was conducted using Sprague Dawley rats. The serum metabolites of the DR group rats and normal group rats were examined to evaluate the MR results. The screen identified eighteen metabolites associated with DR risk, twelve of which were known components. Seven metabolites were positively correlated with DR risk, while five could reduce it. Eight metabolites associated with proliferative DR (PDR) risk were identified, four of which are known components. Three of these were positively associated with PDR risk and one metabolite reduced PDR risk. Additionally, two possible metabolic pathways involved in the biological mechanism of DR were identified. The ELISA results showed that the serum levels of isoleucine and 4-HPA were significantly increased in DR rats, while the level of inosine was decreased. This study offers novel insights into the biological mechanisms underlying DR. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a prevalent and severe complication among patients with advanced diabetes mellitus (DM)1. Global epidemiological research indicates that there were approximately 5.37 billion people living with diabetes in 2021, and it is projected to increase to around 6.43 billion by the year 20302. With the increase in blood glucose levels in patients with diabetes, oxidative stress (OS) and various inflammatory reactions occur in the eye, causing various types of retinal damage3. Early-stage DR is also referred to as non-proliferative DR (NPDR), with the main pathological changes being damage to capillary endothelial cells and pericytes. Proliferative DR (PDR) is the late stage of DR, characterized by the formation of new vessels and exudation in the retina, which can lead to irreversible vision loss and is not fully curable4,5. These pathological lesions gradually lead to irreversible visual impairment and even blindness. Globally, about 12% of people with diabetes go blind each year due to PDR, which is one of the significant contributors to vision loss and blindness1,6. Despite numerous systematic studies on DR, our knowledge of the early diagnosis, treatment and advance prediction of its deterioration of DR is still very limited.

Currently, the diagnosis of DR in clinical practice often relies on patient history and imaging data. But imaging diagnosis is only effective when specific pathological changes are detected and confirmed7. The combination of clinical risk factors is helpful to determine the onset, course and classification of DR. A long duration of diabetes, hypertension, obesity and other risk factors will increase the incidence and risk of DR8. However, the progression of DR in patients with similar clinical conditions is not the same9. Investigating potential biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity for the early detection, treatment, monitoring, and prognosis of DR holds significant clinical importance.

In recent years, the emergence of metabolomics has provided a new avenue for characterizing the underlying mechanisms of diseases by reflecting molecular processes that are closer to the states of disease10,11. A potential association between metabolites and DR has been identified in numerous studies, suggesting that certain metabolites are implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of DR9,12,13. A 12-year follow-up study found that various metabolites can affect retinal function, the onset, and progression of DR by modulating related pathways such as the activation of inflammatory pathways and angiogenesis12. Hanying Wang’s team found significant changes in 15 metabolites in the plasma of DR patients, mainly for the pantothenic acid and CoA metabolite biosynthetic pathways13. Exploring metabolites related to the development of DR is not only helpful for its early screening and prevention but also helpful for understanding the biological mechanism of DR treatment and mining new therapeutic targets.

However, the causal relationship between metabolites and the onset of DR and PDR has not been prospectively analysed or systematically evaluated yet. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological approach to research. It utilizes genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV) to infer causality through a genetic association between a hypothesized causal factor (i.e., exposure) and an outcome. Compared with traditional retrospective studies, MR is less affected by confounding factors and has more reasonable causal sequences14. MR can exclude the influence of various confounding factors. Therefore, in the absence of rigorous randomised controlled trial (RCT) conditions, MR is the most persuasive method for probing the causal relationship between exposure and outcome15.

In this study, two-sample MR was used to investigate the relationship between metabolites and onset or progression of DR at the genetic level. Moreover, in vivo experiments were used to verify the results. This study aims to provide a reference for exploring new biomarkers, clinical therapeutic targets and potential therapeutic mechanisms of DR.

Methods

Study design

We employed metabolites as exposure and DR as outcome in a two-sample MR design. Summary statistics from independent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were used to investigate potential causality between metabolites and DR incidence. A convincing MR design must adhere to three assumptions: (1) the instrumental variables must exhibit a strong correlation with the exposure; (2) the instrumental variables affect outcomes solely through exposure factors; and (3) the instrumental variables are unaffected by any confounding factors for expose-outcome associations16 (Fig. 1). Sprague-Dawley rats were selected to validate in vivo experiments for MR analysis.

GWAS data for human serum metabolites and DR

The chosen GWAS data on human blood metabolites are sourced from Shin et al.17, the most comprehensive and up-to-date scientific report on the genetic loci of blood metabolites. This data was obtained from the metabolomics GWAS server (https://metabolomics.helmholtz-muenchen.de/gwas/). The investigation comprised 7824 European participants, including 1768 individuals from Germany and 6056 from the United Kingdom. A total of about 2.1 million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the human population were analysed for associations, and 486 blood metabolites were successfully obtained. A subset of 177 unknown metabolites were named after X-, and 309 known metabolites were subdivided into 8 categories, namely, amino acids, carbohydrates, cofactors and vitamins, energy products, lipids, nucleotides, peptides and xenobiotics.

The GWAS statistics for DR were obtained from the FinnGen data freeze 8 results and summary statistics (https://r8.finngen.fi/). The data was obtained from a sample of 190,594 European individuals, consisting of 14,584 cases as well as 176,010 controls (GWAS ID: finngen_R8_DM_RETINOPATHY_EXMORE).

The GWAS data selected for PDR was sourced from the FinnGen data freeze 7 results and summary statistics (https://r7.finngen.fi/). The data encompass a sample of 212,889 European individuals, comprised of 8,681 cases and 204,208 controls. (GWAS ID: finngen_R7_DM_RETINA_PROLIF).

All three studies received ethical approval from the local committee, and all participants provided their voluntary informed consent prior to their involvement in the research.

Selection of instrumental variables

Genetic variation SNP sites displaying genome-wide significance (P < 1 × 10− 5) for blood metabolites were selected for aggregation. The threshold for the linkage disequilibrium (LD) coefficient r2 was set at 0.001, and the genetic distance was set to 10 000 kb18,19. Unqualified SNPs were screened to ensure their independence and exclude the influence of linkage disequilibrium on the results. The formula F=[(N-K-1)/K]×[R2/(1-R2)] was used to calculate the average F statistic and quantify the intensity of SNPs20. Here, N denotes the sample size of the exposure factors, K represents the number of IVs, and R2 indicates the proportion of variation in exposure factors that is accounted for by the IVs. SNPs with F > 10 have sufficient strength to ensure their effectiveness on the trait and avoid weak tool bias. Subsequently, exposure SNPs and outcome SNPs alleles were aligned to remove SNPs containing palindromic sequences. Finally, we used the Steiger filter method21 to determine whether the directionality of a single SNP was correct to remove IVs that might lead to an inversion of causality.

MR analysis

This research mainly adopts the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, which deems each SNP a credible natural experiment, assesses the causal impact of each SNP on the outcome, and utilises the outcomes as indicators in the meta-analysis to assess the collective causal influence. In the absence of horizontal pleiotropy or in the presence of equilibrium, IVW (fixed effects) methods provide unbiased estimates15. The results are presented as the model odds ratio (OR) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI).

Sensitivity analysis and directional detection

In addition to the IVW analyses, the robustness of the causation estimates was tested using both MR-Egger regression and Weighted Median (WM) models. The coherence of distinct MR analysis models’ direction enhances causal inference’s credibility. A consensus of three analysis methods in tandem with a statistically significant IVW outcome ascertains a positive result22. We used the MR-Egger intercept test to determine horizontal pleiotropy23. MR-PRESSO was also used to detect outliers and correct horizontal pleiotropy. P < 0.05 was deemed as statistically significant24. If so, IVs can directly affect results without exposure. Cochran’s Q test was utilised to appraise the heterogeneity among individual genetic variants25 and to determine the differences between various IVs. P < 0.05 represents the presence of heterogeneity. The SNPs with excessive heterogeneity were eliminated. The leave-one-out (LOO) approach was utilised to assess any potential bias caused by individual SNPs in the MR estimations26. Conduct a test on the data using MR Steiger, P > 0.05 indicates that the directionality of the causal assumption is correct.

Confounding analysis

Despite the robustness of the metabolites screened by sensitivity analysis for causal associations with DR, we searched for SNPs related to metabolites, using the Phenoscanner V2 website (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/). To ascertain whether it is linked with various prevalent risk factors that may potentially skew the MR results, including smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, heart rate and other retinal diseases10. Once these individual SNPs were linked to possible confounders at a threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5, they were removed for subsequent repeat MR analysis to ensure the reliability of the results.

Metabolic pathway analysis

The potential biological mechanisms of metabolic pathways causally involved in DR were further explored using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/)27. Pathway analysis datasets were obtained from the small molecule pathway database (SMPDB) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Animals

Twelve healthy male Sprague Dawley rats (SPF grade) (180–210 g) were purchased from Guangdong Medical Experimental Center and kept in a ventilated isolation environment with a 12 h:12 h day to night light cycle, humidity of 40% ~ 70%, and temperature of 21 ~ 25 ℃. Rats with normal blood glucose levels underwent random allocation into two groups: DR (n = 6) and control (Con, n = 6). The DR group received intraperitoneal injections of 2% streptozotocin (STZ) solution at a dose of 60 mg/kg, while the Con group received the same amount of citrate buffer. Rats that achieved blood glucose levels ≥ 16.7 mmol/L were deemed to have successfully modelled the target condition. The rats in both groups were fed normal rat food. The diet and drinking water were sufficient, and the changes in the rats’ general condition were closely observed and recorded. Blood was drawn from the tail vein once a week to record the rats’ blood glucose levels.

After 8 weeks of successful modelling, rats were fasted for 12 h and then anaesthetised by intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital at a dose of 40 mg/kg. Fresh blood was obtained from the rats by abdominal aorta sampling. The eyeballs of the rats were cut using ophthalmic scissors and fixed in FAS eyeball fixation solution for 24 h.

All animal testing procedures in this study were strictly performed according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (GZTCMF1-20230018). The study was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining of the retina

The dehydrated eyeballs were embedded in paraffin. Then, an ultrathin semiautomatic microtome was used to continuously cut the paraffin-embedded sections into 5 μm sections parallel to the optic nerve on the sagittal plane. Five discontinuous retinal sections were obtained from each rat. The sections were dehydrated with gradient ethanol for 2 min, stained with haematoxylin for 10 min, differentiated with 75% hydrochloric ethanol for 15 s, stained with eosin for 15 s, removed with xylene for 10 min, and then sealed with neutral resin. The cells were observed under a light microscope.

Detection of apoptosis in retinal tissue by TUNEL assay

Retinal cell apoptosis was identified using the transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) method. The TUNEL Staining Kit provided by Elabscience (China) was employed following manufacturer’s directions. Paraffin sections of retina were dewaxed with xylene, rehydrated with gradient alcohol, cleaned with PBS, and treated with ProteinaseK working solution for 20 min. After washing with PBS, an equilibration working buffer of 100 µl TdT was added for 20 min. The liquid was aspirated, and TdT enzyme working solution (50 µl) was added to each slide. Incubation followed in a dark wet box at 37 ℃ for 1 h. After washing, DAPI was incorporated and cells were stained for 5 min. Subsequently, pieces were sealed and re-washed. The staining results were observed using a fluorescence microscope with an excitation wavelength of 450–500 nm and a detection wavelength of 515–565 nm (green fluorescence).

Detection of serum levels of metabolites in rats by ELISA

Serum from rats was collected. The serum was analysed using isoleucine (MM-92672901), inosine (MM-92729102), and 4-hydroxyhippurate (4-HPA, MM-927547O2) ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd, China). The optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was determined using an enzyme marker. The levels of some metabolites in rat serum were estimated according to the standard curve recommended by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

The code packages in R (V.4.2.0) (TwoSampleMR, data.table, MRPRESSO, tidyverse, ggplot2, circlize, and ComplexHeatmap) were used for statistical analysis and mapping of two-sample MR.

Data obtained from in vivo experiments were analysed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and SPSS 22.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, whilst significance was determined by employing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the data conformed to a normal distribution, the difference between the two groups was evaluated using the T test, and these data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the two independent experiments. In cases where the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Selection of IVs

In total, 486 blood metabolites were analysed in this investigation and SNP details for each metabolite are provided in Supplementary file 1: Table S2. The SNPs generated by 486 blood metabolites ranged from 3 to 493, summing up to a total of 10,936 SNPs. The minimum F statistic value of blood metabolite genetic IVs was 17.64. This suggested that there was no instrument weak bias in this study, as all of these values were above the threshold of 10.

Effect of genetically determined metabolites on DR

The IVW method preliminarily identified 41 metabolites significantly related to DR (detailed MR analysis results are provided in Supplementary file 1: Table S3). Of these, 12 metabolites had unknown chemical composition, and the remaining 29 metabolites could be divided into 7 categories according to their chemical composition (17 lipids, 5 amino acids, 3 xenobiotics, 1 peptide, 1 nucleotide, 1 energy, and 1 carbohydrate) (Fig. 2).

Confounding analysis

We conducted various sensitivity analyses to ensure the soundness of the analysis results. However, to further ensure that IVs are not related to the remaining common risk factors for DR (smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, heart rate and other retinal diseases), we examined each of the 26 metabolite-associated SNPs using Phenoscanner. A total of 34 SNPs associated with confounding factors were screened (details are available in Supplementary file 1: Table S4). After deleting the corresponding SNPs, MR analysis and sensitivity analysis were performed again. The IVW results suggested that 8 metabolites lost significant association with DR (palmitate (16:0), heptanoate (7:0), X-02973, mannitol, X-12063, palmitoleate (16:1n7), decanoylcarnitine, and 1-oleoylglycerophosphoethanolamine). The estimated values of the remaining 18 metabolites were still significant after discarding the promiscuous SNPs, and the direction of conclusion was consistent with the primary analysis. Specifically, serum levels of isoleucine (OR 4.79, 95% CI: 1.55–14.83, P = 0.006), pipecolate (OR 1.7, 95% CI: 1.06–2.71, P = 0.027), hydroxyisovaleroyl carnitine (OR 2.08, 95% CI: 1.24–3.46, P = 0.005), 1-docosahexaenoylglycerophosphocholine* (OR 1.89, 95% CI: 1.04–3.46, P = 0.038), 1-arachidonoylglycerophosphoethanolamine* (OR 1.95, 95% CI: 1.21–3.15, P = 0.006), hexadecanedioate (OR 1.25, 95% CI: 1.04–1.49, P = 0.017), n-Butyl Oleate (OR 2.19, 95% CI: 1.2–3.98, P = 0.011), X-09706 (OR 1.97, 95% CI: 1.16–3.33, P = 0.012), X-11,876 (OR 1.39, 95% CI: 1.02–1.9, P = 0.036), X-12,450 (OR 1.46, 95% CI: 1.03–2.05, P = 0.031), X-12,844 (OR 2.12, 95% CI: 1.29–3.48, P = 0.029) and X-14,189 (OR 1.28, 95% CI: 1.05–1.57, P = 0.015) may increase the risk of developing DR. In addition, serum levels of cis-4-decenoyl carnitine (OR 0.65, 95% CI: 0.44–0.95, P = 0.027), inosine (OR 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.98, P = 0.023), pro-hydroxy-pro (OR 0.53, 95% CI: 0.32–0.88, P = 0.014), X-12,833 (OR 0.92, 95% CI: 0.87–0.97, P = 0.001), hippurate (OR 0.75, 95% CI: 0.58–0.98, P = 0.003), and 4-hydroxyhippurate (OR 0.61, 95% CI: 0.4–0.94, P = 0.025) may help reduce the risk of developing DR. General condition of rats

Con group rats grew steadily and symmetrically and were responsive and docile. The hair was relatively clean, white and smooth. Water intake, food intake, and urine output values were not high. The bedding was changed every 3 days. There were no significant changes in ocular characteristics. The DR group showed some typical symptoms of diabetes, including polydipsia, polyphagia and polyuria, from approximately 3 days after the model was established. The bedding material was replaced once a day, and the excreta was smelly and viscous. After 2 weeks, the rats in the DR group exhibited weight loss, and the body shape gradually showed a difference from that of the Con group. After 3 weeks, the rats in the DR group showed abdominal distention, messy hair, yellow and dull hair colour, and an irritable mood. In the DR group, metabolic cataracts gradually appeared in some rats from the 7th week of modelling, but there were individual differences (Fig. 6A).

The rats in the Con group gained weight steadily, averaging 37.25 ± 2.875 g/week over the 8 weeks following intraperitoneal injection. In contrast, the rats in the DR group had a slower average weight gain of 6.67 ± 2.08 g/week (Fig. 6B). Additionally, the random blood glucose level in the Con group remained consistently within the normal range. That of the DR group significantly increased one week after modelling but then slightly decreased, yet it remained consistently higher than 16.7 mM and tended to be stable(Fig. 6C).

Rat retinal tissue was observed by H&E staining

The inner limiting membrane of the retina in the control group was found to be smooth and intact by (H&E) staining. The ganglion cell layer showed an orderly and regular arrangement with good morphology and no apparent abnormalities in its monolayer structure. Additionally, the inner nuclear layer was neatly arranged, whereas the nuclei of the outer nuclear layer were darkly pigmented, orderly, and closely placed. The borders between the layers were well defined and sharply demarcated. This contrasted with the DR group, which showed thinning of the retinal tissue. The nerve fiber layer demonstrated an elevated thickness, with the presence of oedema and a disorganized and sparse distribution of cells. The ganglion cell layer showed cavitation. The outer nuclear layer was pale, while the inner and outer nuclear layers were disorganised. Additionally, the boundaries between layers were unclear (Fig. 6D). These observations align with the established features of rat retinopathy.

Apoptosis of rat retinal cells

The TUNEL signal (green) in the retina of the DR group was obvious and overlapped with the DAPI signal (blue), indicating that cells in multiple layers of the retina had undergone apoptosis (Fig. 7A). No obvious apoptotic signal was observed in the Con group. The optical density values of the staining results for the total retinal thickness of each group were quantified. The apoptosis signal of retinal tissue of rats in the DR group (n = 6) was significantly increased, and the optical density was 1.8 times that of the average optical density of rats in the Con group (Fig. 7B). According to the H&E and TUNEL results, the DR group showed early diabetic retinopathy-related lesions.

Changes in serum metabolite levels of rats in each group

Serum samples were collected for determination of isoleucine, 4-HPA and inosine levels. Isoleucine levels were 259.7 ± 26.4 ng/L and 303.6 ± 22.2 ng/L in the Con and DR groups, respectively, as determined by ELISA. The 4-HPA levels measured were 331.0 ± 31.8 ng/L and 371.2 ± 30.9 ng/L. The inosine levels were 1115.1 ± 165.9 ng/L and 856.3 ± 244.6 ng/L, respectively. Serum isoleucine (Fig. 7C) and 4-HPA (Fig. 7D) levels were markedly increased (P < 0.001, P = 0.01), whereas inosine levels were reduced (P = 0.031) among DR group compared with Con group rats (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

DR is a complication of systemic disease and is closely related to systemic circulation. In this study, through two-sample MR analysis and rigorous robustness testing, we ultimately identified 12 known metabolite species that were significantly associated with the onset of DR. Among them, higher levels of serum isoleucine, pipecolate, 1-docosahexaenoylglycerophosphocholine, 1-arachidonoylglycerophosphoethanolamine (1-arachidonoyl-GPE), hydroxyisovaleroyl carnitine (3-HIC), hexadecanedioate, and n-butyl oleate were associated with an increased risk of developing DR. Levels of inosine, hippurate, pro-hydroxy-pro, 4-HPA, and cis-4-decenoyl carnitine levels were linked to a reduced risk of DR. Meanwhile, we screened 4 known metabolites associated with PDR. Among them, alpha-ketoglutarate, N2,N2-dimethylguanosine, 1-arachidonoyl-GPE may increase the probability of PDR occurrence while salicylate decreases the incidence risk of PDR. Two metabolic pathways that may be implicated in the biological mechanisms of DR were identified. Subsequently, three of these metabolites were tested by ELISA using rat serum to verify the MR results.

According to the MR results, the positive causal relationship between isoleucine and DR was the strongest, and the valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis pathways to which it belongs were also significantly correlated with the onset of DR. Isoleucine belongs to the branched chain amino acids (BCAAs), which are essential amino acids in the human body. Previous studies have indicated that the vitreous and plasma levels of leucine and isoleucine are higher in the DR population28,29. Wang et al.30 additionally discovered that the concentrations of isoleucine and the corresponding gene expression levels identified in the retina of the mice with DR were also higher than those of the normal group. This is consistent with our findings and suggests that serum isoleucine content may be useful in assessing DR development. BCAAs can activate the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway and exert their effects31. Activation of this pathway induces the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)32. VEGF is a core molecule that induces the onset and development of DR. It can induce leukocyte stasis in the inner wall of retinal vessels in patients with DR, resulting in an imbalance in the expression of proangiogenic factors/ antiangiogenic factors. VEGF may increase retinal microvascular permeability and induce pathological neovascularization and retinal barrier (BRB) rupture in DR patients33,34. Additionally, studies have shown that under conditions of stress, isoleucine is released by the body and can promote the formation of glutamate, which can promote insulin resistance35. The accumulation of glutamate in the retina can lead to the death of retinal cells in various ways and has excitatory neurotoxicity to the retina, which plays a crucial role in the neurodegeneration of DR36,37.

Hippurate is the end product of polyphenol metabolism in the gut microbiota38. Hippurate participates in the phenylalanine metabolism pathway and is converted from benzoic acid produced by the breakdown of phenols, which is excreted in the urine by the kidneys39. 4-HPA is a 4-hydroxyl derivative of hippurate, and these factors are strongly correlated with each other40. Our study found that both factors had a significant effect on delaying the onset of DR. Franois Brial’s team found that hippurate enhances insulin secretion in response to glucose, reduces insulin resistance, and improves glucose homeostasis in mice41. Benjamin F. Bitner’s team found that hippurate effectively protects beta cells, stimulates insulin secretion, and reduces OS in cells42. When body oxidation and antioxidant imbalance occur, many reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced, resulting in increased protease secretion, neutrophil inflammatory infiltration, and damage to lipids, nucleic acids and proteins. Excessive accumulation of ROS and other oxidative stress products can lead to mitochondrial damage, cell apoptosis, inflammation, and lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing retinal vascular permeability and promoting angiogenesis. This causes damage to retinal function and structure and accelerates the onset and development of DR43. Besides, the Ana Rodriguez-Mateos team found that hippurate and phenolic metabolites can increase the bioavailability of NO in the body, thereby mediating the improvement of endothelial function and restoring endothelial-dependent vasodilation44. It may also alleviate the damage to retinal vascular endothelial cells caused by a high glucose environment and delay the course of DR. However, our study found that serum 4-HPA levels were higher in DR rats than in normal rats, which may be related to diabetes-related chronic kidney injury40. Thus, the excretion of hippurate and 4-HPA was blocked, and they accumulated in the serum.

Our research shows that inosine can also effectively inhibit the development of DR. Inosine is an inert purine nucleoside, which is an intermediate in purine metabolism45,46. Inosine can prevent the generation of OS, while suppressing the generation of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β, and alleviate the inflammatory response45. IL-1β can cause damage to retinal structure and function by causing pyroptosis of retinal cells47. IL-6 is secreted by macrophages in the inflammatory response and is involved in leukocyte recruitment and apoptosis as well as T cell activation. Its expression levels in serum and aqueous humour were positively correlated with the degree of macular oedema in DR patients48. TNF-α can induce the expression of proinflammatory proteins in DR, promote the production of reactive oxygen species, cause irreversible adhesion of white blood cells to the vascular endothelium (leukocyte stasis), and has been shown to be associated with the destruction of the BRB49. In conclusion, inosine and phenolic metabolism may be a new direction for DR treatment.

Furthermore, we identified several metabolites that have an effect on DR. Pipecolate is mainly derived from the catabolism of lysine50,51. Lysine metabolism and high levels of serum pipecolate are associated with activation of oxidative stress and increased levels of proinflammatory factors and may indicate the development of type 2 diabetes-related complications50. 3-HIC is a leucine-derived metabolite. When 3-HIC production is increased in the body, leucine intake is increased, or serum biotin deficiency leads to abnormal leucine metabolism52. Biotin deficiency causes an increase in the level of proinflammatory cytokines and exacerbates the effects of inflammation in the local environment. In addition, prolonged biotin deficiency leads to a reduction in AKT and mTOR, which are two major proteins in the insulin signalling pathway, a reduction in glucose utilization, and an increase in insulin resistance52. Hexadecanedioate is a long-chain dicarboxylic acid produced during the oxidation of omega fatty acids, and its level is associated with vascular endothelial dysfunction and neovascularization53. We speculate that it may promote retinal vasculopathy in patients with diabetes.

In addition, we have identified several metabolites associated with PDR. Previous research has shown that salicylate can effectively reduce insulin resistance in the retina of type 2 diabetic rat models and improve retinal function, which is consistent with our findings54. As part of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, the accumulation of alpha-ketoglutarate can lead to the inhibition of glycolysis within cells55, potentially exacerbating damage to retinal cells caused by high glucose levels. N2,N2-dimethylguanosine is a critical step in the guanine metabolic pathway, playing an essential role in stabilizing tRNA structure, and is an indicator of tRNA half-life in tissues. Circulating tRNA fragments are associated with ROS56. Furthermore, it has been found that serum concentrations of 1-arachidonoyl-GPE are positively correlated with both the onset and progression of DR. 1-Arachidonoyl-GPE is a lysophospholipid (LP)57. LP is an important signalling molecule, and its participation in signal transduction pathways may be related to stimulating neovascularization, enhancing vascular permeability and stimulating VEGF production58,59. All these metabolites may serve as potential predictive and therapeutic targets for the deterioration of DR.

Unfortunately, there are few studies on the associations between 1-docosahexaenoylglycerophosphocholine, n-butyl oleate, pro-hydroxy-pr, and cis-4-decenoyl carnitine and DR, and the related pathological mechanisms of their influence on DR needs to be further analysed and verified. These unknown metabolites related to DR have not been clearly reported, and we know little about their chemical composition and mechanism of action. However, this does not diminish our results but indicates a new direction for further research on the pathogenesis and therapeutic targets of DR.

Compared with previous studies, we used Mendelian analysis to exclude changes in metabolic substances induced by lifestyle changes after diagnosis, long-term intake of certain drugs, and other complications of diabetes. It also avoids the difficulties of implementing strict RCTs due to ethical issues, observation time, expensive funding, and other constraints. The results were verified by rigorous robustness analysis. Therefore, the current findings should be fairly convincing. Furthermore, compared to previous relevant MR analyses, we prefer to consider the MR method as an exploratory tool to identify metabolites that may affect DR. The results were then further validated in animal studies. This approach provides more comprehensive and reliable results. Meanwhile, we conducted a search for metabolites associated with the onset of PDR, which provides a reference direction for future research to predict and intervene in the deterioration of DR.

However, there are some limitations in our study. First, the GWAS we selected were limited to European populations only. This circumvents the bias that exposure and outcome factors are not of the same race and also limits generalizability to other populations. Second, because a linear relationship between exposure and outcome is assumed in MR analyses, potential non-linear associations between metabolites and DR may not be fully captured. Besides, the mechanism of some metabolites is unknown and needs further study.

Conclusions

Our study suggested that changes in the levels of some blood metabolites may have a causal effect on the development of DR. We identified 12 known metabolites, 6 unknown metabolites, and two metabolic pathways that can influence DR at the genetic level. We discovered new potential markers and therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of DR. The specific mechanism of action of these metabolites and the clinical efficacy of related drugs need to be further studied and verified.
