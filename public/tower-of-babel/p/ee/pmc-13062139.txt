Effects of robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation on motor function and daily living activities in acute stroke: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mehmet Ali Sunnetci, Burak Menek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStroke is a leading cause of disability worldwide, often resulting in upper limb impairments that hinder daily living activities. While conventional rehabilitation methods focus on motor recovery, recent advancements in robotic rehabilitation offer promising alternatives. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of hand robot-assisted rehabilitation on functionality, fine motor skills, and daily living activities in patients with acute stroke. A parallel-group, single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted with 30 stroke patients aged between 40 and 60. Participants were randomly assigned to either the Robotic Hand Rehabilitation (RHR) Group or the Neurodevelopmental Therapy (NT) Group, with both groups undergoing an 8-week rehabilitation program. Primary outcomes were hand function, assessed by the ABILHAND questionnaire, and fine motor skills, measured using the Nine Hole Peg Test (NHPT). Secondary outcomes included activities of daily living evaluated by the Barthel Index (BI), upper limb disability measured by the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) questionnaire, and spasticity assessed via the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS). All assessments were performed before and after the intervention period. Both groups demonstrated significant improvements in all functional parameters except spasticity (p < 0.05). However, the RHR group showed significantly greater improvements in fine motor skills (NHPT improvement: 4.44 ± 1.18 s vs. 1.93 ± 0.90 s, p < 0.001), hand function (ABILHAND: 7.27 ± 1.39 vs. 4.87 ± 2.23, p = 0.002), and daily living activities (BI: 16.33 ± 2.97 vs. 7.67 ± 4.95, p < 0.001) compared to the NT group. There was no significant difference in spasticity outcomes between the two groups (p > 0.05). The findings suggest that hand robot-assisted rehabilitation is an effective complementary approach in acute stroke rehabilitation, particularly for enhancing fine motor control and daily living function. Given these benefits, robotic hand therapy may serve as a valuable adjunct to conventional neurodevelopmental therapy, supporting improved motor recovery in individuals with acute stroke. Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), stroke is a severe cerebrovascular event resulting from a hemorrhagic or ischemic interruption in the blood flow that supplies oxygen and nutrients to the brain1. The disruption of cerebral blood flow due to vascular causes leads to focal or global functional impairments, resulting in a clinical condition that affects individuals’ motor and cognitive abilities. Following a stroke, 60–80% of individuals experience persistent upper extremity functional impairments, which significantly limit their ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs), particularly in areas such as hygiene, eating, and dressing2,3. Loss of hand function severely affects independent mobility and overall quality of life. Studies indicate that while proximal movements (shoulder and elbow) show partial recovery in stroke survivors, distal movements (hand and wrist) remain significantly limited, further exacerbating ADL-related challenges4. The primary causes of these limitations include muscle weakness, reduced range of motion, sensory deficits, and spasticity5.

The primary goal of stroke rehabilitation is to restore motor function, enhance individuals’ independent participation in activities of daily living (ADLs), and facilitate their reintegration into society6. Stroke recovery is closely linked to neuroplasticity, which occurs most actively in the early stages and progresses rapidly during the acute phase7. Therefore, early initiation of rehabilitation immediately after stroke is of great importance8. In acute stroke rehabilitation, the two most critical factors are the intensity and frequency of treatment9. Clinical studies have demonstrated that initiating rehabilitation immediately after a stroke accelerates neuromotor recovery and contributes positively to overall health outcomes 7. Literature reviews indicate that frequent and intensive rehabilitation programs following a stroke contribute more significantly to motor function recovery10,11. Clinical studies have demonstrated that initiating rehabilitation immediately after a stroke accelerates neuromotor recovery and contributes positively to overall health outcomes12. While the first six months post-stroke are characterized by a rapid recovery phase, the rate of functional gains declines after this period, as the effects of neuroplasticity mechanisms begin to diminish. Therefore, early and intensive rehabilitation programs are crucial for optimizing functional recovery13.

Rehabilitation methods encompass exercise therapy, neurophysiological approaches (PNF, Brunnstrom, Bobath, Rood), biofeedback, occupational therapy, virtual reality, and mirror therapy14,15. However, these approaches often have limitations, such as patients’ inability to sustain prolonged repetitive movements, treatment monotony, and decreased motivation, which may hinder rehabilitation outcomes16. These constraints highlight the need for more effective interventions to support the recovery of motor function impairments caused by neurological damage. Although upper extremity dysfunction is common after stroke, impairments in hand and fine motor skills are particularly disabling and tend to be more resistant to recovery than proximal functions such as shoulder or elbow movements. Recovery of hand function is essential for regaining independence in ADLs. However, conventional rehabilitation methods often fall short in restoring fine motor abilities, necessitating innovative interventions specifically targeting hand function. Other approaches such as mirror therapy and constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) have been shown to improve hand function; however, they typically require a certain level of voluntary movement and cognitive cooperation, which may not be suitable for all patients in the early post-stroke phase. In this context, robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation offers a promising solution to address the limitations of conventional and voluntary movement-based therapies. Robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation offers a promising solution to address the limitations of conventional therapy. Hand-specific robotic devices provide task-specific, high-intensity, and repetitive training, which facilitates motor learning and promotes neuroplasticity. These devices can be customized to the patient’s functional level and provide immediate sensory and visual feedback, making them ideal for fine motor rehabilitation17,18.

With advancements in technology and the increasing interest in robotic rehabilitation, robotic devices designed to support the rehabilitation process for both upper and lower extremities are being developed. These robotic systems offer significant advantages, such as reducing labor requirements, enabling the implementation of intensive and frequent therapy programs, allowing for the creation of individualized movement patterns, and enhancing patient motivation and performance through visual feedback. In this context, robotic rehabilitation has emerged as an innovative approach that enhances motor learning and provides intensive movement practice19. Robotic devices are designed to support functional movements, facilitate repetitive motion in structured patterns, and offer individualized assistance based on patients’ needs. The literature suggests that robotic rehabilitation promotes neuroplasticity, accelerating motor control and movement learning20,21. Studies emphasize that high-repetition practice is essential for enhancing neuroplasticity, and incorporating this principle into rehabilitation is critical22. Robotic rehabilitation addresses this need by enabling patients to perform a high volume of repetitive movements within a limited timeframe, making the rehabilitation process more efficient23.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of robotic rehabilitation in improving upper extremity functions. It has been reported that intensive repetitions, sensorimotor integration, and cognitive engagement significantly enhance functional motor performance24,25. However, most existing research has primarily focused on upper and lower extremity robotic systems, while studies specifically targeting hand function in robotic rehabilitation remain limited. This gap underscores the need for further investigation into the effectiveness of robotic rehabilitation interventions aimed at restoring hand function in post-stroke patients26. In general, the combination of robot-assisted therapy with different rehabilitation programs can enhance the effectiveness of rehabilitation training27,28. Robotic therapy has been reported to produce significant improvements in upper extremity motor function after stroke. For example, a meta-analysis reported gains in upper extremity functionality in patients receiving robot-assisted therapy29. Electromechanical and robot-assisted arm training for improving activities of daily living, arm function, and arm muscle strength after stroke30. In the literature, robotic systems that generally involve shoulder and elbow movements have been widely used, whereas studies focusing exclusively on hand and finger function remain limited. Therefore, more evidence is needed regarding the effectiveness of hand-focused robotic therapies31.

Despite growing interest in robotic therapy, few studies have focused on robotic interventions specifically designed for hand function, particularly in the acute phase of stroke recovery. Moreover, comparative studies evaluating the effectiveness of robotic hand therapy combined with standard neurodevelopmental Therapy (NT) versus conventional rehabilitation are lacking30,31. NT was selected as the comparator in this study due to its comprehensive approach targeting postural control, muscle coordination, and functional movement patterns. While interventions like CIMT or mirror therapy have demonstrated efficacy in improving hand function, they often require higher levels of voluntary motor control and cognitive engagement, which may limit their applicability in the early post-stroke period. In contrast, NT can be safely applied to a broader patient profile in the acute stage. Furthermore, the lack of studies investigating the combined use of robotic hand therapy with NT represents a notable gap in the literature. Therefore, NT was chosen to explore whether integrating robotic assistance into a standard therapy model could provide additional benefits.

The present study aims to investigate the effects of hand robot-assisted rehabilitation combined with NT, compared with conventional rehabilitation, on motor function, fine motor skills, and activities of daily living in individuals with acute stroke. The outcome measures included the ABILHAND Stroke Questionnaire, Nine-Hole Peg Test (NHPT), Barthel Index (BI), and the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand Questionnaire (DASH). We hypothesize that this combined approach will lead to superior outcomes compared to conventional therapy alone.

Methods

This parallel-group, 1:1 allocation ratio, single-blind (participant), randomized controlled study was conducted at NP Brain Hospital. Ethical approval for the research was secured from the Non-Interventional Ethics Committee at Istanbul Medipol University (Approval Number: E-10840098-202.302-620). The research adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for studies involving human participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. This consent was obtained in writing at the beginning of the study and adhered to all ethical standards regarding participant privacy. The study was conducted between January 2024 and November 2024. The study protocol was also registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06805955; date of registration on 03/02/2025).

Participants

This study included individuals diagnosed with stroke by a neurologist and aged between 40 and 60 years. Participants were selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria required participants to have a confirmed diagnosis of stroke, be between 40 and 60 years old, possess adequate communication skills, and achieve a minimum score of 20 on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). Additionally, participants were required to have full range of motion in the metacarpophalangeal (MCP), proximal interphalangeal (PIP), and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints and a Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) score of ≤ 2 in the elbow, wrist, and finger flexor muscles. Furthermore, upper extremity muscle strength had to be between 2 + and 4, and the post-stroke duration could not exceed six weeks. These criteria were determined to ensure the safe and effective implementation of exercises using the hand robotic device. Although robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation is theoretically suitable for patients with limited voluntary movement, the specific device used in this study required participants to have a minimum level of motor function to ensure safe and effective interaction. Therefore, inclusion criteria such as full passive range of motion in hand joints, a Modified Ashworth Scale score of ≤ 2, and upper extremity muscle strength between 2 + and 4 were applied. These criteria were selected to ensure both safety and compatibility with the robotic device. While some robotic systems may accommodate individuals with more severe motor deficits, the present study was limited by the functional requirements of the device used.

The exclusion criteria included inability to meet the study requirements due to insufficient verbal or written communication skills, presence of deformities preventing participation in exercises, and comorbid cardiovascular, rheumatological, or orthopedic conditions. Additionally, individuals who had undergone surgery within the past six months were excluded from the study.

A total of 32 individuals aged between 40 and 60 years with a diagnosis of acute stroke were included in the study. Two individuals were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Participants who were informed about the study and provided written informed consent were randomly assigned into two groups: Robotic Hand Rehabilitation (RHR) Group (n = 15) and NT Group (n = 15). All assessments and treatment sessions were conducted by a physiotherapist. Evaluations were performed before the treatment and at the end of the intervention period, in the 8th week. Randomization and blinding

Numbers from 1 to 30 were randomly assigned to two groups using the randomizer.org website. Participants in the study were assigned numbers based on their order of presentation at the clinic. Each participant was allocated to a group according to the random assignment conducted on randomizer.org based on their assigned number. Group allocation was performed by an independent individual who was not directly involved in the study. To minimize bias, participants were blinded to their group assignments and remained unaware of the interventions applied in the other group.

Interventions

Neurodevelopmental therapy group

Participants in the NT Group received only NT, administered three days per week for eight weeks, totaling 24 sessions, each lasting 60 min. This approach primarily focused on reducing muscle tone, improving hand functions, and enhancing daily living activities. Within this framework, exercises aimed at developing fine motor skills and improving upper extremity functionality were implemented. The program included hand and wrist mobilization, stretching and strengthening exercises, functional grasp training, and education on daily living activities. The progression of the exercises was adjusted under the supervision of a physiotherapist according to the patient’s condition. The exercise content was structured from simple to complex and from basic to more challenging activities, tailored to the individual needs of the patient. A detailed explanation of the exercises, along with corresponding illustrations, is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Robotic hand rehabilitation group

Participants in this group received 25-minute sessions of robotic hand therapy in addition to NT applications. The device used was the SYREBO model, manufactured in China, designed to support functional activities such as wrist and finger flexion–extension movements and grip functions. This advanced rehabilitation device operates through five distinct modes, which allow tailored interventions based on individual therapeutic needs. In the passive mode, the device independently performs flexion–extension movements of all fingers, which is particularly beneficial for patients with limited or absent voluntary motor control. The individual finger mode enables each finger to move independently, targeting selective motor control and finger isolation. In the pinch mode, the thumb continuously performs flexion–extension movements while the other fingers sequentially execute similar motions, thereby enhancing coordinated grip patterns. The active-assistive mode combines the patient’s voluntary movements with robotic assistance, facilitating motor engagement in individuals with partial movement capabilities. Lastly, in the mirror mode, the patient wears a sensorized glove on the unaffected hand and the robotic device on the affected hand; movements performed by the unaffected hand are mirrored by the impaired hand, promoting bilateral coordination and motor relearning. The specific mode(s) utilized during therapy were determined by an experienced physiotherapist based on each participant’s motor function level, muscle strength, and coordination capacity. During robotic hand therapy, a series of fine motor skill exercises were performed with the support of the device. These included tasks such as transferring buttons from one container to another, opening a loosely sealed jar lid, stacking checkers, flipping over cards placed on a table, and placing small cubes onto surfaces of varying heights. These exercises were designed to enhance manual dexterity, grip strength, and hand-eye coordination. Upon completion of the 25-minute robotic intervention, the same set of fine motor tasks was repeated without robotic assistance. This approach was intended to reinforce task-specific motor learning and promote functional independence. Overall, the structured protocol aimed to optimize sensorimotor feedback and facilitate improvements in upper extremity functionality. (Fig. 2) Throughout the intervention period, no serious adverse events or training-related discomfort were observed or reported by the participants.

Outcome measures

Outcome measurements for all participants were conducted prior to the treatment and again following eight weeks of treatment.

Primary outcomes

ABILHAND stroke hand function questionnaire

The ABILHAND Stroke Hand Function Questionnaire, developed by Penta et al., was used to assess manual ability. This instrument evaluates the degree of difficulty individuals experience when performing daily activities such as dressing, household tasks, and feeding. It comprises 23 items, and participants are asked to rate their perceived ability to perform each task based on their experiences over the past month. The response options are “Impossible” (0 points), “Difficult” (1 point), and “Easy” (2 points), with higher total scores indicating better hand function32.

Nine hole Peg test

NHPT is a timed test with established reliability and validity in individuals with stroke, was used to assess manual dexterity and fine motor activities. The test consists of a standard wooden board containing nine holes (10 mm in diameter) and nine wooden pegs (9 mm in diameter). The participant is instructed to pick up the pegs one by one and place them into the holes in any order as quickly as possible, followed by removing them one by one. The time required to complete both the placement and removal of the pegs is recorded. The stopwatch starts when the participant first touches a peg and stops when the last peg is placed back on the Table33 (Fig. 3).

Nine hole peg test.

Secondary outcome measure

Barthel index

The BI was used to assess independence in daily activities without assistance. The BI is scored between 0 and 100, where 0–20 points indicate complete dependence, and 100 points represent full independence. This index is widely used worldwide for planning, assessing, and monitoring rehabilitation programs in individuals with neurological disabilities34.

Disabilities of the arm, shoulder, and hand questionnaire

The DASH is used to assess physical limitations and function in upper extremity disorders. The DASH questionnaire consists of three subcomponents, with the first section comprising 30 questions. Of these, 21 questions evaluate the difficulties encountered in daily activities, 5 questions assess symptoms, and 4 questions measure social function, work, sleep, and self-confidence levels35.

Modified ashworth scale

The Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) is the most commonly used method for assessing spasticity. The evaluation should be performed while the individual is lying supine in a relaxed position. The examiner moves the joint passively, repetitively, and rapidly, assigning a score based on the resistance encountered during movement. The scale ranges from 0 to 4, where 0 indicates no increase in muscle tone, and 4 represents severe rigidity in both flexion and extension. Intermediate scores assess varying degrees of resistance, from mild catch and minimal resistance (1, 1+) to significant tone increase that restricts passive movement (2, 3). In this study, the spasticity levels of the participants were evaluated using the MAS to determine muscle tone variations and assess their impact on functional movement36. The following muscles, which are commonly involved in wrist and hand function and are suitable for MAS assessment, were examined bilaterally: flexor carpi radialis, flexor carpi ulnaris, extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis, extensor carpi ulnaris, and pronator teres. These muscles were selected due to their clinical relevance in upper extremity function and their appropriateness for passive movement assessment using the MAS.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcomes of the study was NHPT, evaluated through the time × group interaction. A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 for a mixed ANOVA design with two groups (RHR vs. NT) and two measurement points (pre/post). Based on previous literature, the assumptions included an effect size of f = 0.30, α = 0.05, power (1–β) = 0.80, an inter-measurement correlation of r = 0.50, and a nonsphericity correction of ε = 1.00. These parameters indicated a minimum required total sample size of n = 28. Considering a potential 10% attrition rate, the target sample size was set at n = 32 (16 per group). Following eligibility screening, two participants were excluded, and the final analysis was conducted with n = 30 participants37.

The data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User V 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) statistical software package. Descriptive statistics were presented as sample size (n), percentage (%), mean (X), standard deviation (SD), median (M), minimum (min), and maximum (max) values. In the decision-making process, data were considered to be normally distributed if the absolute skewness value was below ± 2.0 and the kurtosis value was below 7.038. Baseline comparisons between groups were performed using the Independent Samples t-Test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For within-group comparisons across time (pre- vs. post-treatment), the Paired Samples t-Test was applied when assumptions were met.

To examine treatment effects over time, a two-way mixed ANOVA was conducted with Time (pre, post) as the within-subject factor and Group (RHR, NT) as the between-subject factor. The Time × Group interaction was the main effect of interest. In addition, to account for potential baseline imbalances, an ANCOVA was performed for each outcome with post-treatment scores as the dependent variable, baseline scores as covariates, and Group as the fixed factor.

All hypothesis tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes for t-tests were reported as Cohen’s d (interpreted as small = 0.20–0.50, medium = 0.51–0.80, and large = 0.81 and above). For ANOVA and ANCOVA, partial eta squared (ηp²) was calculated and interpreted as small (≈ 0.01), medium (≈ 0.06), and large (≥ 0.14)39.

Discussion

In this study, the effects of robotic hand rehabilitation and neurodevelopmental therapy on upper limb function, fine motor skills, and daily living activities were compared in stroke patients. The results indicated that both treatment approaches led to significant improvements in all functional parameters except for spasticity. However, the robotic hand rehabilitation group demonstrated greater improvements in functionality, daily living activities, and fine motor skills compared to the neurodevelopmental therapy group. These findings suggest that robotic-assisted therapy may offer greater benefits in enhancing fine motor control and functional independence in stroke rehabilitation. The superior outcomes observed in the robotic hand rehabilitation group may be explained by several interrelated mechanisms. First, robotic devices enable highly repetitive, goal-directed, and task-specific training, which is essential for promoting neuroplastic changes in the acute phase of stroke. Second, early-stage implementation of robotic rehabilitation may maximize recovery potential by leveraging heightened cortical reorganization during the subacute window. Third, the device’s ability to provide real-time visual and proprioceptive feedback likely enhanced motor relearning, especially in fine motor tasks. Moreover, the integration of gamified tasks may have increased patient engagement and adherence, contributing to improved outcomes. These factors combined may explain the superior functional recovery in the RHR group compared to conventional NT alone. The superior efficacy of robotic hand rehabilitation may be further explained by its capacity to activate and reinforce neuroplastic processes. Through high-frequency, task-oriented practice, robotic interventions stimulate cortical motor areas, facilitate synaptogenesis, and support long-term potentiation, all of which are essential for motor recovery post-stroke. Moreover, the combination of visual, tactile, and proprioceptive inputs during robotic tasks enhances sensorimotor integration, thereby reinforcing motor learning and adaptive reorganization of sensorimotor networks. These multimodal stimulations, paired with real-time feedback and error-based learning mechanisms, provide an enriched environment that conventional therapy alone may lack. In this regard, robotic rehabilitation not only facilitates motor repetition but also promotes the central nervous system’s capacity for reorganization and recovery29,40.

One of the key elements in stroke rehabilitation is the ability of robotic rehabilitation to provide intensive and high-quality movement practice10,27. Rehabilitation robots, developed to support neurodevelopmental therapy methods, are becoming increasingly common in clinical practice due to their ability to facilitate repetitive movements and offer an alternative therapeutic approach41. In contrast, our study demonstrated that the robotic hand therapy group exhibited greater improvements in motor function compared to the neurodevelopmental therapy group. This may be attributed to the early-stage intervention and the intensive training program conducted three days per week. Supporting these findings, Liao et al. and Taveggia et al. demonstrated that robot-assisted upper extremity rehabilitation led to significant improvements in motor function, arm activities, and bilateral upper extremity skills compared to neurodevelopmental therapy42,43. A systematic review and meta-analysis study has demonstrated that integrating portable robotic rehabilitation (PRR) with standard therapy significantly enhances upper limb functional rehabilitation. It has been reported that robotic devices capable of performing repetitive and intensive tasks reduce the workload of therapists, allowing them to provide more personalized rehabilitation sessions, thereby decreasing labor demand and increasing efficiency44. However, most previous studies have investigated short-term interventions or primarily focused on proximal upper limb functions; evidence regarding long-term, hand-specific robotic rehabilitation in the acute phase of stroke remains limited1,18. Unlike previous studies, our research covered a longer treatment period and aligned with existing literature by confirming that adding robotic hand rehabilitation to neurodevelopmental therapy enhances hand function recovery in stroke patients. Many studies highlight that robotic rehabilitation is often used as an adjunct to neurodevelopmental therapy1,45. Our findings suggest that robotic hand therapy alone may not be sufficient, but it serves as an effective complementary approach to neurodevelopmental therapy.

Although the RHR group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in functional outcomes such as ABILHAND, the lack of group difference in MAS scores warrants consideration. This discrepancy may reflect the distinct nature of the constructs being measured. While ABILHAND captures perceived manual ability in functional contexts enhanced through task-specific, repetitive training inherent to robotic therapy MAS evaluates spasticity, which may be less responsive to such interventions, especially in individuals with low baseline tone. The relatively low initial MAS scores in both groups may have led to a floor effect, limiting detectable changes. These findings suggest that while robotic therapy effectively improves functional independence and motor control, its short-term effects on spasticity may be limited. It is also important to note that participants enrolled in this study were selected based on their ability to perform specific fine motor tasks, which inherently required low to moderate levels of spasticity. Consequently, baseline MAS scores were already low, limiting the potential for observable reductions in spasticity. This floor effect may explain the lack of significant change in spasticity scores following either intervention. Furthermore, the therapeutic emphasis of this study was placed more on restoring wrist and hand functionality than on directly addressing spasticity. Therefore, the absence of notable improvement in spasticity should be interpreted within this clinical context.

Robotic rehabilitation has been shown to be effective in enhancing both fine and gross motor functions in stroke rehabilitation. It is suggested that robotic devices, particularly those targeting wrist and finger movements, further enhance motor recovery46. Stein et al. evaluated the effects of robotic rehabilitation on fine motor skills and reported that while 46% of participants showed improvement, no significant differences were found in pre- and post-treatment fine motor skill assessments. In contrast, Takahashi et al. emphasized the effectiveness of robot-assisted therapy specifically in improving hand function, demonstrating that bilateral robotic training led to significant gains in distal upper extremity performance in patients with chronic stroke47. In our study, fine motor skill evaluations using the NHPT demonstrated superior outcomes in the robotic rehabilitation group compared to the neurodevelopmental therapy group48. This improvement in NHPT performance may be partially attributed to enhanced motor coordination; however, the contribution of proprioceptive factors remains uncertain, as they were not directly assessed in the present study. The observed increase in fine motor activities in the RHR group may be attributed to the device’s pneumatic-assisted movement support, which enables adjustable assistance based on the individual’s motor capacity. The hand robot provides selective motor support for each finger and offers various training modes including passive, active-assistive, and active allowing for a graded therapeutic approach tailored to the patient’s functional level. Additionally, its capacity to facilitate repetitive movements, promote neuroplasticity, and incorporate a gamified interface may have positively influenced patient motivation, adherence, and motor learning. This study has several limitations. The most significant limitation is the unequal distribution of stroke etiology between the groups, with a higher number of individuals with hemorrhagic stroke in both the NT and RHR groups. This lack of homogeneity in stroke cause is considered a major constraint of the study. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Another notable limitation is that both the interventions and outcome assessments were conducted by the same physiotherapist. This may introduce observer bias and compromise the internal validity of the study. However, this methodological choice was necessitated by institutional requirements, as the study was carried out within the scope of a master’s thesis, and all procedures were performed by the thesis student. Furthermore, the absence of long-term follow-up, as the study only evaluates the effects of the intervention over a specific period, presents another limitation. Moreover, the study design included only two time points for evaluation (pre- and post-intervention), which may not fully capture the progression or sustainability of treatment effects. Including intermediate or follow-up assessments in future studies would provide more comprehensive insights into rehabilitation outcomes. Additionally, the total treatment duration for the RHR group was longer than that of the NT group due to the addition of 25 min of RHR. This discrepancy in treatment time may have influenced the observed outcomes, and therefore, should be considered a methodological limitation. The lack of a standardized treatment protocol for RHR also poses a challenge. Although participants were informed that they were receiving a standard treatment program, it is likely that individuals in the RHR group recognized the use of a technology-assisted intervention due to the distinctive nature of robotic therapy. Therefore, complete participant blinding could not be achieved. Another important limitation is that the robotic device used in this study required a minimum level of motor function for safe and effective operation. As a result, individuals with more severe upper extremity impairment were excluded based on the device’s technical specifications. This restricts the generalizability of our findings to patients with higher levels of motor recovery and limits applicability to the broader stroke population. Future research should focus on achieving more homogeneous group distributions, assessing long-term treatment effects, and comparing different robotic hand devices with standardized rehabilitation protocols. Additionally, studies involving individuals with different types of stroke may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment efficacy.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings support the growing body of evidence indicating that robotic rehabilitation is an effective method for improving motor function, fine motor skills, and daily living activities in stroke patients. However, since this study did not include a group receiving robotic therapy alone, no conclusions can be drawn about its efficacy as a standalone intervention. Future research should aim to investigate the isolated effects of robotic therapy and determine the most effective strategies for integrating it into neurorehabilitation protocols.
