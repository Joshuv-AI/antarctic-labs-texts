The visualizable and marker-free gene editing platform mediated by CRISPR/Cas9 in Coleophoma empetri
Authors: Li Xie, Ping Men, Yu Zhou, Jiang Li, Xuan Zhang, Min Wang, Xuenian Huang, Xuefeng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractMicafungin, an echinocandin antifungal agent, has been extensively utilized for the treatment of invasive fungal infections. It is semi-synthesized from the lipohexapeptide FR901379, a natural product from the filamentous fungus Coleophoma empetri. The genetic manipulation of C. empetri has been significantly hindered by the scarcity of both effective neutral sites and selectable markers, which has impeded the systematic metabolic engineering of the industrial strain. Here, a visualizable and marker-free gene editing platform was developed in the industrial strain C. empetri MEFC09. Firstly, the biosynthesis gene cluster of melanin in C. empetri MEFC09 was characterized by gene knockoutin vivo, which comprises a core polyketide synthase gene cemelA and a transcriptional regulator cemelR. Based on this, two neutral sites were designed at the genomic loci of cemelA and cemelR, both demonstrating high-intensity expression of the luciferase reporter. Furthermore, a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated marker-free gene editing platform was developed. When the protoplast concentration was adjusted to 104 cells/mL, a positive transformation rate of 12.4% was achieved on antibiotic-free screening plates. Finally, the transcriptional activator McfJ was overexpressed at the neutral site cemelR using the visualizable and marker-free gene editing platform. It substantially increased the FR901379 titer to 1807.8 mg/L, corresponding to a 3.3-fold improvement over the parental strain. This genetic manipulation system is poised to serve as a versatile platform for accelerating metabolic engineering and functional genomics investigations in industrial strains C. empetri. It will also shed light on the development of genetic manipulation platforms in other filamentous fungi.Key points• Two visualizable neutral sites were characterized in Coleophoma empetri.• The CRISPR/Cas9-mediated marker-free gene editing platform was developed.• The transcriptional activator McfJ was efficiently expressed at the neutral site. Introduction

Filamentous fungi have proven to be a crucial class of industrial microorganisms, used for the production of antibiotics (Skatrud 1992), enzymes (Tani et al. 2014), organic acids (Geng et al. 2023; Xi et al. 2023), and unsaturated fatty acids (Zhao et al. 2022). These products have a wide range of applications in the pharmaceutical, agricultural, food, and materials industries (Bush and Bradford 2016; Hou et al. 2023; Tyśkiewicz et al. 2022; Vendruscolo et al. 2016). The biosynthetic pathways of these compounds are progressively elucidated; rational strain engineering based on metabolic network analysis has attracted increasing attention (Tong et al. 2019). For eventual industrial application, chromosomal integration represents a fundamental strategy in the construction of genetically stable microbial cell factories (Chen et al. 2018; Mikkelsen et al. 2012). Therefore, the selection and characterization of integration sites that maintain cellular viability remain paramount. Systematic characterization of neutral sites has been achieved in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, whereas only a limited number of neutral sites have been reported in filamentous fungi (Mikkelsen et al. 2012; Yang et al. 2020). Notably, filamentous fungi exhibit various pigment biosynthesis abilities, such as DHN-melanin (Chiang et al. 2011; Eisenman and Casadevall 2012), aflatoxin (Cao et al. 2022), azaphilones (Huang et al. 2020), and fusarubin (Tang et al. 2022). The biosynthesis genes of these pigments provide attractive candidates for the development of visualizable neutral sites.

Coleophoma empetri is an industrial strain for producing FR901379, the precursor of micafungin which is extensively utilized in treating invasive fungal infections (Hashimoto 2009; Iwamoto et al. 1994). The fermentation titer of FR901379 plays a pivotal determinant in assessing the economic feasibility of micafungin production at the industrial level (Iwamoto et al. 1994; Kanda et al. 2009). Recently, the biosynthetic mechanism of FR901379 has been elucidated. Two separated biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), a core mcf gene cluster and a sulfonated gene cluster, collectively mediate FR901379 biosynthesis under the coordinated regulation of the transcriptional activator McfJ (Men et al. 2022). The titer of FR901379 was significantly enhanced by overexpressing McfJ and the rate-limiting enzymes (Men et al. 2023). Building on these findings, the homologous transcriptional activators regulating the biosynthesis of pneumocandin B0 and echinocandin B have been subsequently identified, and both have been successfully implemented in the rational engineering of industrial strains (Jiang et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). However, increasing the copy number of mcfJ failed to further improve the FR901379 titer in strain MEFC09-J, which already overexpresses mcfJ, suggesting that systematic engineering of the metabolic network is required. This necessity underscores the demand for developing more efficient genetic manipulation toolkits, particularly those enabling multigene modifications and sustainable metabolic engineering.

In 2021, we validated the functions of selectable markers (hph, neo, and nat) and promoter elements (PgpdA, PgpdAt, and Ppgk) in C. empetri MEFC09 (Men et al. 2021). Nevertheless, the limited selectable markers remain a significant bottleneck for systematic metabolic engineering. Furthermore, the absence of well-characterized neutral sites in C. empetri compromises reliable functional validation of heterologous genes. The CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology has been implemented in C. empetri SIPI1284 via integrating Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation (ATMT) method (Wei et al. 2022). In contrast, protoplast-mediated transformation (PMT) is simpler and more efficient (Li et al. 2017). Therefore, the combination of PMT with CRISPR/Cas9 technology allows for high-precision genomic modifications with enhanced transformation efficiency.

In this study, we established a visualizable and marker-free gene editing system in the FR901379-producing strain C. empetri MEFC09. Firstly, the melanin biosynthesis genes were identified and characterized as neutral sites. Furthermore, a marker-free screening method was established by integrating CRISPR/Cas9 editing technology and the PMT method. Finally, this genome-editing platform was successfully applied to engineer industrial strains, thereby achieving effective overexpression of the transcriptional activator McfJ through marker-free selection and targeted integration. This system will significantly advance investigations into the high-yield mechanisms of FR901379 while enabling sequential metabolic engineering.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

All strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. The strains were cultivated at 25 °C on Potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates for 5–6 days. The transformation of C. empetri MEFC09 was performed by (PEG)-CaCl2-mediated PMT (Men et al. 2021). Hygromycin B resistant colonies were selected after incubating on PDAS-H plates (PDA with 0.8 M D-sorbitol and 100 µg/mL hygromycin B) for 5–7 days.

Gene cloning and plasmid construction

All primers used in this study are listed in Table S1. All plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S2. The strategy for gene disruption was carried out in mutant Δku80 as shown in Fig. S1. For gene disruption, about 1.2 kb fragments of upstream and downstream of the target genes were amplified from the genomic DNA of C. empetri MEFC09. The marker gene hph was amplified using the primers hph-F/hph-R with the plasmid pXH2-1 as the template (Huang et al. 2014). Subsequently, the upstream and downstream fragments were fused with marker hph by fusion PCR. The targeting fragments were then amplified from the fusion PCR using nested primers.

The fragment PgpdAt-luc-Ttrpc was amplified using the primers PgpdAt-F/Ttrpc-R with the plasmid pUC19-PgpdAt-luc-Ttrpc as the template. The upstream and downstream homologous arms of cemelA and cemelR were amplified from the genomic DNA of C. empetri MEFC09. Subsequently, the upstream and downstream fragments were fused with fragment PgpdAt-luc-Ttrpc and marker hph by fusion PCR. The upstream and downstream homology arms of cemelR were fused to generate the fragment UcemelR-DcemelR. In addition, the fragment PgpdAt-mcfJ-Tpgk was amplified using the primers PgpdAt-F/Tpgk-R with the plasmid pU-mcfJ as the template, which was then fused with the upstream and downstream of cemelR to construct the fragment UcemelR-PgpdAt-mcfJ-Tpgk-DcemelR.

The 20 bp gRNA of cemelR was screened through E-CRISP (http://www.e-crisp.org/E-CRISP/index.html, Fig. S2). Using plasmid pUcm-gRNA scaffold-eGFP as template, gRNAcemelR-eGFP fragment was amplified with primer gRNAcemelR-F/eGFP-R, and then connected to vector pEASY-T1 to create the recombinant plasmid pEASY-gRNAcemelR-eGFP. Then the sgRNA-related fragment was amplified from pEASY-gRNAcemelR-eGFP using the primers PEASY-F/gRNA-R, and the purified fragment was recovered by gelatinization.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and RNA transcription in vitro

Mycelia were collected from the MKF cultures for 2 days and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was isolated using a MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) following the supplier’s instructions. The RNA samples were treated with RNase-free DNase I (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) for 15 min to eliminate the genomic DNA. cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gRNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). All of the primers used for reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) are listed in Table S1.

The sgRNA-related fragment of cemelR was transcribed into RNA in vitro using the T7 RiboMAX™ Express Large Scale RNA Production System kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol; the RNA was then purified using the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

Measurement of hyphal growth and biomass

To observe the morphology and measure the colony diameters, the strains C. empetri MEFC09, ΔcemelA, ΔcemelR, and OEcas9 were cultured on PDA plates at 25 °C. The diameter of colonies was measured on days 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11, respectively. Each experiment was repeated three times.

To measure the biomass of OEcas9 and C. empetri MEFC09, the packed mycelial volume (PMV) of fermentation broths from OEcas9 and MEFC09 was analyzed at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 days. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Measurement of luciferase expression intensity

The expression intensity of luciferase was measured in 96-well microtiter View-Plates (white with transparent bottom, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) using a microplate reader. The strains were cultured in 20 mL MKS medium, and 230 µL culture was taken into 96-well plates at 0, 12, 15, 18, 21, and 24 h, respectively. Then 70 µL luciferin solution (diluted with MKS medium to 6 mM, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was added, resulting in a final volume of 300 µL. The measurement protocol recorded luminescence intensity (luminescence counts per second, LCPS) and optical density (OD) values at 595 nm (Wanka et al. 2016). Three independent experiments were performed for each transformant.

Fermentation and HPLC analysis

For the fermentation of all C. empetri strains, the fresh mycelia were crushed and inoculated into 50 mL of MKS seed medium, and cultivated at 25 °C, 220 rpm for 40–44 h. The MKS seed medium was formulated with the following components (per liter): 15 g soluble starch, 10 g sucrose, 5 g cottonseed meal, 10 g peptone, 1 g KH2PO4, and 2 g CaCO3, pH 6.5. Then 5 mL of seed culture was inoculated into 50 mL of MKF fermentation medium, and cultivated at 25 °C, 220 rpm for 8 days. The MKF medium was formulated with the following components (per liter): 10 g glucose, 30 g corn starch, 10 g peptone, 40 g D-sorbitol, 6 g (NH4)2SO4, 1 g KH2PO4, 0.3 g FeSO4·7H2O, 0.01 g ZnSO4·7H2O, and 2 g CaCO3, pH 6.5. The fermentation broth of all C. empetri strains was extracted with a threefold volume of methanol, followed by shaking for 1 h at room temperature (Men et al. 2021). Three independent experiments were performed for each transformant.

The samples were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a reverse-phase C18 column (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA, 4.6 × 150 mm, 5 µm) monitored at 210 nm. For HPLC analysis, solvent A was acetonitrile with 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid, and solvent B was deionized water with 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid. The following gradient was used at a flow rate of 1 mL/min: 5%–40% solvent A for 3 min, 40%–60% solvent A for 15 min, 100% solvent A for 5 min, and 5% solvent A for 3 min (Men et al. 2021). The amount of FR901379 was quantified based on the peak area.

Results

Identification of melanin biosynthesis genes in C. empetri MEFC09

In 2021, we identified the core gene of melanin in C. empetri MEFC09, when the gene cemelA (pks 11.2) was disrupted, the mutant strain exhibited white colonies (Men et al. 2021). Further bioinformatics analysis showed that there are another eight genes in the biosynthetic gene cluster of melanin (Fig. 1a). Here, the remaining genes were disrupted respectively in Δku80 to identify their function. When the genes of cemelB, cemelC, cemelD, cemelE, cemelF, cemelG, and cemelH were disrupted (Fig. S3), the colonies of mutant strains remained black (Fig. 1b). While the gene cemelR was disrupted, the colonies color of mutant strain ΔcemelR turned to white (Fig. 1b). There are two genes, cemelA and cemelR, involved in the biosynthesis gene cluster of melanin, which may serve as potential visualizable neutral sites.

The functional identification of biosynthesis genes of the melanin in C. empetri MEFC09. a The predicted biosynthesis gene cluster of the melanin. b The morphology and color of colonies of the mutant strains

Characterization of the visualizable neutral sites

To develop melanin biosynthesis genes as visualizable neutral sites, we first evaluated physiological parameters of the mutant strains ΔcemelA and ΔcemelR, including growth rate and FR901379 titer. Through daily monitoring of individual colony diameters on PDA plates, we demonstrated that knockout of either cemelA or cemelR caused no statistically significant influence on cellular growth (Fig. 2a). Additionally, no obvious differences were observed in FR901379 titers among ΔcemelA, ΔcemelR, and MEFC09 (Fig. 2b). These results demonstrated that the genomic loci of cemelA and cemelR indeed meet the fundamental requirements for the neutral sites.

Identification of visualizable neutral sites in C. empetri MEFC09. a Comparison of colony diameters between strains ΔcemelA, ΔcemelR, and MEFC09. b The FR901379 titers of strains ΔcemelA, ΔcemelR, and MEFC09. All data are presented as mean ± SD from n = 3 biologically independent samples. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. ns: no significant difference. c Schematic diagram of site-specific integration of the expression element of luciferase into genomic sites of cemelA and cemelR. d Comparison of fluorescence activities between strains ΔcemelA::luc, ΔcemelR::luc, and MEFC09

To further compare the expression level of exogenous genes at the cemelA and cemelR sites, luciferase was employed as a reporter gene, enabling continuous monitoring of transcriptional activity over time. The Δku80 strain was used as the chassis cell, and the target fragments were integrated via homologous recombination to avoid off-target events. The expression element of luciferase was inserted into the genomic sites cemelA and cemelR respectively (Fig. 2c), resulting in the recombinant strains ΔcemelA::luc and ΔcemelR::luc. Luciferase was successfully expressed in both strains, with significantly higher expression levels (20- to 60-fold) observed in the ΔcemelR::luc mutant compared to the ΔcemelA::luc mutant (Fig. 2d).

Development of the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated marker-free screening platform

Current genetic engineering in C. empetri MEFC09 relies on merely three validated selectable markers, severely restricting multi-round editing in industrial strains (Men et al. 2021). To overcome this bottleneck, we developed a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated marker-free editing platform enabling iterative genome modifications. First, a chassis cell with stable expression of Cas9 needed to be established. The plasmid containing the cas9 expression cassette was transformed into C. empetri MEFC09 (Figs. 3a and S4a). RT-PCR analysis confirmed strong transcriptional activity of cas9 in all three transformants (Fig. S4b), resulting in the mutant strain OEcas9. To further evaluate the potential metabolic burden imposed by Cas9 expression, we determined the growth curves, biomass, and FR901379 titer of OEcas9. Measurement of single colony diameters on PDA plates revealed no significant difference in vegetative growth between strain OEcas9 and MEFC09 (Fig. 3b). The packed mycelial volume (PMV) of fermentation broths from OEcas9 and MEFC09 was measured to evaluate the biomass. The results demonstrated that the biomass of the OEcas9 strain was comparable to that of the control strain (Fig. 3c). In addition, the titers of FR901379 were 473.0 mg/L and 466.8 mg/L in OEcas9 and MEFC09, respectively (Fig. 3d). These findings indicated that the introduction of the Cas9 did not affect the growth, development, or FR901379 biosynthesis in C. empetri. Therefore, the OEcas9 strain could serve as a suitable chassis strain for the manipulation of targeted genes.

To develop a visualizable marker-free screening platform, the gene cemelR was selected as the target neutral site. The guide RNAs for CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing were designed using E-CRISP. The optimal guide RNA was ligated into the vector pEASY-T1 to form the plasmid pEASY-gRNAcemelR-eGFP. Then the sgRNA-related fragment amplified from pEASY-gRNAcemelR-eGFP was transcribed into sgRNAcemelR in vitro. The sgRNAcemelR was then transformed alone into the mutant strain OEcas9 by PMT (Fig. 4a). In the absence of antibiotic selection, precise protoplast concentration optimization emerges as a critical parameter for ensuring efficient transformation while minimizing background regeneration. Quantitative profiling established a dose-dependent correlation between protoplast density and editing efficiency under marker-free conditions. Protoplast density optimization revealed threshold-dependent editing efficiencies. At 1.0 × 105 cells/mL, 6.7% white transformants were observed (54/805 colonies), rising to peak efficiency of 12.4% (15/124 colonies) at 1.0 × 104 cells/mL, whereas further dilution to 1.0 × 103 cells/mL reduced efficiency to 5.6% (1/18 colonies) (Fig. 4b). No white transformants were observed in the Cas9-only control group and the sgRNA-only control group (Figs. S5a, b), which confirmed that the positive editing events in the experimental group were indeed mediated by the combined action of Cas9 and sgRNA, rather than by non-specific effects of individual components. These results indicated that the protoplast concentration of 104 cells/mL is optimal for implementing marker-free screening in C. empetri, which is significantly lower than the protoplast concentration required for conventional transformation methods (> 107 cells/mL). Four white transformants randomly selected were subjected to sequencing validation, which confirmed precise editing at the cemelR site with specific deletions of 7, 22, 49, and 13 base pairs, respectively (Fig. 4c).

The marker-free screening platform mediated by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. a The flowchart of CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology used in MEFC09. b The transformants obtained by disrupting the gene cemelR with the different concentrations of protoplasts. c Sequencing results of white transformants obtained by disrupting the gene cemelR

In addition, to clarify the CRISPR-Cas9 editing efficiency between homology-directed repair (HDR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) events in the OEcas9 strain, two experimental groups were designed, one containing donor DNA with the cemelR homology arms, and the other lacking the donor DNA. Both groups included sgRNA. The proportion of white transformants was 11.4% in the group without donor DNA and 10.9% in the group with donor DNA (Figs. S5c, d). Further PCR verification confirmed that 31.4% of white transformants achieved HDR at the cemelR site in the group with donor DNA (Fig. S6), while the remaining positive transformants were generated by NHEJ-mediated repair at the cemelR site.

Overexpression of the transcriptional activator McfJ at the cemelR neutral site

The regulation of FR901379 biosynthesis is mediated by the transcriptional activator McfJ (Men et al. 2023). To simultaneously validate the utility of the platform and improve the biosynthesis capacity of FR901379, we performed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeted integration of the mcfJ expression cassette at the cemelR neutral site in OEcas9 strain, achieving a 12.7% editing efficiency (32/252 colonies) without antibiotic selection (Fig. 5a). Ten white transformants were randomly selected for verification of site-specific integration, and the mcfJ expression cassette was successfully integrated at the neutral site cemelR in two transformants (20% probability) through homologous recombination (Fig. S7). The engineered strain ΔcemelR::mcfJ achieved an FR901379 titer of 1807.8 mg/L in shake flask fermentation, representing a 3.3-fold enhancement over the parental strain MEFC09 (Fig. 5b). These results demonstrated that this visualizable and marker-free gene editing platform can reliably achieve site-specific integration of the target gene and maintain highly efficient functional expression.

Discussion

The genetic engineering of C. empetri presents significant challenges due to its inherently slow growth rate and stringent sporulation conditions, especially when compared to model filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus and Penicillium. Conventional transformation methods, including Agrobacterium-mediated and protoplast-based systems, have been established in C. empetri, enabling targeted genetic modifications (Men et al. 2021; Yamada et al. 2009). Nevertheless, the current genetic editing systems in C. empetri are hindered by several limitations, including insufficient neutral sites and selection markers, thereby imposing significant barriers to stable expression of heterologous genes and iterative genomic editing. To address the genetic manipulation limitations in the industrial filamentous fungus C. empetri, this study developed a visualizable and marker-free gene editing platform.

Neutral sites play an important part in metabolic pathway engineering, which can ensure the stable, reliable, and efficient overexpression of target genes (Mikkelsen et al. 2012; Yang et al. 2020). Systematic characterization of neutral sites has been achieved in plants, bacteria, and yeast (Albert et al. 1995; Härtl et al. 2001; Yu et al. 2021). However, research on filamentous fungi in this area remains limited compared to other microbial systems. Owing to the diverse pigment-producing capabilities of filamentous fungi, genes involved in pigment biosynthesis serve as ideal loci for establishing visually monitorable neutral sites in genetic engineering applications (Dufossé et al. 2014). The biosynthesis of melanin in C. empetri is mediated by cemelA and cemelR. However, time-course analysis revealed that the albino phenotype of the ΔcemelR was unstable, with melanization initiating at the colony margins by day 12 and progressing to complete pigmentation around day 28 (Fig. S8). This dynamic pattern starkly contrasted with the ΔcemelA, which exhibited stable and persistent albinism throughout cultivation. This phenotypic divergence likely originates from the regulatory complexity of melanin biosynthesis. Cellular physiological states undergo dynamic changes during prolonged cultivation, which may be attributed to the activation of the melanin biosynthetic pathway through alternative compensatory signaling mechanisms at the later stage (Qin and Xia 2024). In the biosynthesis of many pigments, disruption of a single gene typically leads to a stable and distinct colony color, allowing a single genomic site for visualizable gene expression (Geib et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2015). Given the differences in colony color changes resulting from the knockout of cemelR and cemelA at the later stages of cultivation, both genetic loci of cemelR and cemelA can be developed as visualizable neutral sites, which enables the iterative integration and expression of two genes. Notably, the level of luciferase expression in the ΔcemelR::luc mutant was lower during early growth but showed continuous elevation, ultimately reaching a 100-fold increase by the 24th hour (Fig. 2d). These findings demonstrated that the cemelA site is suitable for moderate expression of target genes, whereas the cemelR site supports high-intensity expression, particularly in metabolic engineering applications requiring production-growth decoupling.

Selection markers also serve as critical determinants in fungal transformation systems, yet their inherent scarcity frequently constitutes a primary constraint for iterative genome-editing in filamentous fungi. While the Cre/loxP system has demonstrated capacity for genetic marker recycling in filamentous fungi, its operational efficacy exhibits pronounced host dependency on HR efficiency (Forment et al. 2006; Krappmann et al. 2005). In addition, targeted disruption and functional complementation of the pyrG (encoding orotidine-5′-phosphate decarboxylase) enables bidirectional reuse of this auxotrophic selection marker in filamentous fungi (Huang et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2010). However, the growth rate of ΔpyrG mutant strains typically is slower than that of the parental strains. Owing to the inherently slow growth rate of C. empetri, if pyrG is disrupted, the screening cycle of positive transformants will be prolonged. To address the insufficiency of resistance screening markers in C. empetri, the CRISPR/Cas9 editing technology was integrated with melanin synthesis genes to achieve visualizable and marker-free gene editing. The protoplast density was at 1.0 × 104 cells/mL, and a 12.4% positive rate was achieved using marker-free selection. It was fully acceptable for genetic modifications, effectively addressing the critical bottleneck of limited screening markers.

The low transformation efficiency of filamentous fungi necessitates the use of high-quality protoplasts for genetic transformation, which has long posed a significant technical challenge. Conventional approaches relying on antibiotic selection typically require a high protoplast concentration (~ 1.0 × 107 cells/mL) to achieve effective transformation. With our optimized protocol, however, transformation can now be performed with only 1.0 × 104 cells/mL, dramatically reducing the required protoplast density. Consequently, whereas previously the protoplasts prepared in one batch could only support a few transformation experiments, the same quantity can now accommodate thousands of transformations. This advancement effectively overcomes the technical bottleneck associated with high-quality protoplast preparation for the genetic transformation of filamentous fungi. When integrated with high-throughput analytical techniques, this approach facilitates large-scale, parallel gene editing. Despite its advantages, this method faces some limitations, particularly in cases where the edited genes lack visible phenotypic markers, requiring extensive validation of transformants. To address this, the integration of ku80 deletion with CRISPR/Cas9-based editing may significantly enhance the efficiency of marker-free selection systems.

Finally, the efficiency and convenience of this platform were validated by overexpressing the transcriptional activator responsible for FR901379 biosynthesis at the cemelR neutral site, resulting in a 3.3-fold enhancement in FR901379 production. In subsequent studies, a wider array of promoters with different types and strengths will be evaluated with a visualizable and marker-free gene editing platform to achieve a balance between FR901379 biosynthesis and cell wall integrity.
