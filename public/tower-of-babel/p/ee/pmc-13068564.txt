Gene testing and prognosis in post-operative recurrent non-small-cell lung cancer: subgroup analyses of WJOG15421L
Authors: T Matsubara, T Sakamoto, T Takahama, T Yokoyama, I Yoshino, H Akamatsu, M Yamaguchi, J Baba, T Tokito, M Tachihara, Y Sato, T Takenaka, K Sugio, M Mori, T Takeuchi, K Okazuka, H Kenmotsu, J Fujimoto, M Shimokawa, N Yamamoto, K Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the status of biomarker testing and prognosis in patients with post-operative recurrent non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC).Patients and methodsThis retrospective multicenter study included 1500 patients with advanced or recurrent NSCLC from 29 institutions between July 2020 and June 2021. A post-operative recurrence subgroup analysis was carried out on 229 patients. The main focus was on the testing rate for biomarkers, including multigene testing. Survival analyses were also conducted.ResultsThe median age of the study cohort was 74 years (range 40-92 years). Of the patients, 46.5% had stage I NSCLC, and 67% had adenocarcinoma. Median time from surgery to recurrence was 511 days, and 169 patients had distant metastases; 23% of the patients underwent rebiopsy to confirm recurrence. Multigene testing was conducted in 43.7% of the patients, and single-gene testing in 56.8%. Multigene testing was more frequent in cases with distant metastases (47%) compared with local recurrences (35%). No significant difference in testing success was observed between the resected preserved specimens and the rebiopsied recurrent specimens. Multigene testing was less likely if recurrence occurred >3 years after surgery. Post-operative recurrent patients had better overall survival than advanced-stage patients in both the overall cohort and the driver-positive subgroup.ConclusionsThe rate of multigene testing in post-operative recurrent NSCLC cases was low despite the availability of sufficient surgical specimens. The further widespread adoption of multigene testing is one of the most critical challenges for improving resected patient outcomes along with recent advances in perioperative personalized medicine.Key words: non-small-cell lung cancer, multigene testing, thoracic surgery, biomarker, recurrence, driver mutation

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally,1 with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) being the most prevalent subtype. While pulmonary resection remains the primary treatment for early-stage NSCLC (stages I and II) and select stage III cases,2 post-operative recurrence occurs in ∼30%-50% of patients.3 In such cases, systemic chemotherapy, similar to that used for advanced stages, is required to improve patient prognosis. Recent advances in chemotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and targeted therapies have revolutionized NSCLC treatment by focusing on driver gene mutations. Screening for mutations in genes such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1), v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1 (BRAF), mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (MET), rearrangement during transfection (RET), v-Ki-ras2 Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor (NTRK) is essential for selecting the most effective targeted therapies. Agents targeting these gene alterations have been approved globally, including in Japan.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

With the discovery and increasing prevalence of driver gene mutations, multigene testing has become a critical component of personalized medicine. In Japan, the introduction of the Oncomine Dx Target Test Multi CDx system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) in 2019 and the subsequent approval of the AmoyDx Pan Lung Cancer PCR Panel (Riken Genesis, Tokyo, Japan) in 2020 have facilitated simultaneous testing for multiple gene mutations. Multigene tests offer significant advantages over traditional single-gene tests, such as PCR, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and fluorescence in situ hybridization, by allowing the comprehensive analysis of a single specimen. However, these tests are not without challenges: they require a sufficient number of tumor cells in the specimen, and archived surgical specimens may deteriorate over time, potentially compromising the accuracy of genetic testing.

Generally, there are two scenarios for diagnosing post-operative recurrence: clinical diagnosis during follow-up imaging (e.g. computed tomography or positron emission tomography scans) and pathological confirmation through rebiopsy of suspected lesions. In many cases, archived specimens from the initial surgery are used for genetic testing, particularly when rebiopsy specimens are insufficient. However, the quality of these archived specimens may decline over time, raising concerns about their suitability for accurate mutation testing.13

Despite the critical role of multigene testing in NSCLC patients, including post-operative patients, its implementation in clinical practice remains inconsistent. Notably, no study has specifically examined the status of genetic testing in patients with post-operative NSCLC recurrence. To address this gap, we conducted a subgroup analysis of the WJOG15421L trial to investigate the status of gene testing and the prognosis of patients with post-operative recurrence.

Patients and methods

Patient eligibility

The WJOG15421L/REVEAL study enrolled patients with advanced or recurrent NSCLC between July 2020 and June 2021. Eligibility was confirmed by histological or cytological analysis. Of the 1500 initially enrolled patients, 21 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria and, ultimately, 1479 patients were analyzed. This cohort included 1192 patients with advanced-stage disease, 57 with recurrence after chemoradiotherapy, and 230 with post-operative recurrence. One patient in the post-operative recurrence cohort was excluded from the subgroup analysis (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106890). This study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the central review board of the Tottori University Faculty of Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. The trial is registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Trials Registry (UMIN000046079). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Study design

The WJOG15421L (REVEAL) study was a multicenter, retrospective observational study with a prospective plan to assess prognosis and clinical outcomes. The study recruited patients from 29 institutions affiliated with the West Japan Oncology Group (WJOG).14 The primary endpoint was to evaluate the status of the biomarker testing. This preplanned subgroup analysis focused on the patients with post-operative recurrence. This study aimed to assess the implementation and success rates of genetic biomarker testing, positivity rates for each biomarker, and the proportion of patients receiving targeted therapy. The collected clinical data included patient demographics, sample collection methods, biopsy sites, systemic drug therapy details, survival outcomes, pathological stage at primary surgery, presence of rebiopsy at recurrence, and interval from primary surgery to recurrence.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and clinical backgrounds were analyzed for all patients. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, along with backward stepwise regression, were used to identify the clinical factors associated with the likelihood of multigene testing. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death or last follow-up, with survival data censored accordingly. Statistical analyses were carried out using Python 3.11.0 and Python data analysis libraries: pandas version 1.5.3, NumPy version 1.24.2, SciPy version 1.10.0, statsmodels version 0.13.5, and lifelines version 0.27.4. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

The characteristics of 229 patients with post-operative recurrence are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 74 years (range 40-92 years), and ∼70% of the patients were male. Of the total patients, 197 (86%) had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score of 0 or 1, while 11 (4.8%) had a worse ECOG PS score (3 or 4). The most common histological types were adenocarcinomas (67.7%) and squamous-cell carcinomas (21.0%). At the time of primary surgery, 107 patients (46.7%) were diagnosed with stage I disease, 58 with stage II disease, and 63 with stage III disease.

During the primary surgery, over 80% of the patients underwent lobectomy, with only four cases involving pneumonectomy. The median time from surgery to recurrence was 511 days [95% confidence interval (CI) 427-571 days], and three-quarters of the patients experienced distant metastasis at recurrence. Rebiopsy was carried out in 53 recurrence cases. At the data cut-off point, 77 patients (33.6%) died.

Genetic biomarker tests

Figure 1A summarizes the implementation rates of the genetic biomarker testing. Biomarkers were confirmed in 86.1% of patients. Multigene testing was conducted in 43.7% of cases, while single-gene testing was carried out in 56.8%. Notably, 18.8% of all cases underwent both multigene and single-gene testing. Genetic biomarker testing was not conducted in 13.9% of the cases. There was no significant difference in the rate of multigene testing between the submitted specimens (42.0% for surgically archived samples versus 49.1% for rebiopsy samples; Figure 1C). Although more genetic mutation tests were carried out in cases of distant metastasis recurrence, the difference was not statistically significant (35.0% in local recurrence cases versus 46.7% in distant metastasis cases; Figure 1C). The proportions of patients who underwent biomarker testing for EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, and MET were 84.3%, 77.2%, 68.2%, 55.4%, and 51.6%, respectively (Table 2). Figure 1B shows the detection rates of each driver gene mutation in the 100 post-operative cases in which multigene testing was conducted. EGFR was detected in 19% of the cases, whereas ALK and BRAF were detected in 2.0% of cases. MET exon 14 skipping was detected in 4.0% of cases, and the KRASG12C mutation was detected in 3.0% of cases. No genetic mutations were detected in 62.0% of the patients.

The success rate of multigene testing

We investigated the success rate of Oncomine Dx Target Test (Oncomine DxTT) multigene testing. Among the 78 cases submitted, both DNA and RNA analyses were successful in 83.3%. RNA analysis failed in 12.8% of cases, and both DNA and RNA analyses were unsuccessful in 3.9% of cases.

There was no significant difference in the success rates based on the type of submitted specimens (surgically archived or rebiopsy samples, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106890). Additionally, no correlation was found between the storage duration of archived specimens (i.e. the period from surgery to sample submission) and the success rate of Oncomine DxTT (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106890).

Clinical factors for non-submission of multigene testing

Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted to identify trends in cases in which multigene mutation testing was not carried out (Table 3). Univariate analysis revealed that patients with histological subtypes other than adenocarcinoma were less likely to undergo multigene testing [odds ratio (OR) 0.55, 95% CI 0.31-0.98, P = 0.043]. Additionally, patients with a time interval from surgery to recurrence of >3 years were less likely to undergo multigene testing (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.26-0.93, P = 0.029). No significant correlation was observed between age, performance status, comorbidities, recurrence type (local versus distant metastasis), or likelihood of multigene testing submission. Multivariate analysis confirmed that histological subtypes other than adenocarcinoma and a time interval from surgery to recurrence of >3 years were significant predictors of non-submission for multigene testing (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.21-0.77, P = 0.006 versus OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.21-0.89, P = 0.023, respectively).

Survival analyses

Survival analysis of the post-operative recurrence prognosis was conducted from four perspectives. Firstly, we compared the prognosis of patients with post-operative recurrence with those with advanced disease in the REVEAL cohort. Patients with post-operative recurrence had significantly better OS than those with advanced disease [hazard ratio (HR) 0.60, 95% CI 0.47-0.76, P < 0.005; Figure 2A]. Among the post-operative recurrence cases, those with driver mutations exhibited significantly better OS than those without mutations (HR 0.28, 95% CI 0.16-0.50, P < 0.005; Figure 2B). We further analyzed the prognosis of post-operative recurrence and advanced cases in the driver mutation-positive and -negative groups. As shown in Figure 2C and D, post-operative recurrence cases showed significantly better prognosis than advanced cases, regardless of the presence of driver mutations (driver-positive: HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.28-0.86, P = 0.01; driver-negative: HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.54-0.92, P = 0.01).

Discussion

In this study, the rate of multigene testing in cases of post-operative recurrence was below 50%, with 14% of the patients not undergoing any biomarker testing. Additionally, nonadenocarcinoma histological subtypes and a time interval of >3 years between primary surgery and recurrence were independent factors associated with the lack of multigene testing. Survival analysis showed that patients with post-operative recurrence had a significantly better prognosis than those with disease progression, regardless of the presence of driver mutations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to elucidate the status of biomarker testing and prognosis of patients with post-operative recurrence in a real-world setting.

Multigene testing offers the significant advantage of simultaneously screening for multiple driver gene mutations. In NSCLC, multigene panel testing is recommended by multiple guidelines and is considered critical for improving patient prognosis.15,16 However, one limitation of multigene testing is that it may fail to detect mutations if the sample lacks sufficient tissue. In this study, despite surgical specimens containing sufficient tumor tissue, the implementation rate of multigene testing was only 43.7%. This discrepancy can be attributed to two factors.

Firstly, some patients may have undergone biomarker testing before tumor recurrence, particularly in cases where Oncomine DxTT has not yet been approved in Japan. This limited access to multigene testing may have affected our results. In addition, previous studies have also suggested that the adoption of multigene testing in Japan remains suboptimal, regardless of specimen type (surgical or biopsy).14 Secondly, tissue quality may have affected the success of multigene testing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels, including Oncomine DxTT, have a failure rate of 10%-30%17, 18, 19, 20, 21 primarily because of small sample sizes or poor nucleic acid quality (DNA, RNA, or both). Iwama et al. reported that a tumor-slide (TS) value >4000 {[number of tumor cells on a section of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue (T)] × [number of slides subjected to testing (S)]} directly correlates with a sufficient concentration of DNA or RNA for Oncomine DxTT analysis.22 Although surgical samples typically contain high-quality nucleic acids and have a higher NGS success rate than bronchoscopic biopsy samples,21 long storage times from surgery to NGS analysis can degrade DNA and RNA quality.13,23 It is recommended that FFPE samples be used within 3 years of preparation for NGS according to Japanese regulations for handling pathological tissue specimens for genome analysis. Indeed, a storage period of >3 years was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of multigene testing submission, highlighting the need for timely testing.

Our survival analyses confirmed that patients with post-operative recurrence had a more favorable prognosis compared with those with advanced disease, consistent with previous studies.24, 25, 26, 27 Furthermore, generally, post-operative recurrence cases lack primary lesions due to lung resection and have fewer metastases than advanced cases, which likely contributes to the improved survival outcomes observed in these patients.28 In addition, this survival difference may be partly attributable to earlier detection of recurrent disease through structured post-operative follow-up, in contrast to primary metastatic cases that are often diagnosed at a later stage when the disease is more extensive and symptomatic. Treatment efficacy of ICIs and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) is often related to pretreatment tumor burden or primary tumor resection; however, the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood.29,30 To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to analyze the prognosis between post-operative recurrence cases and advanced cases in a cohort with the largest number of cases while simultaneously examining the prognosis in patients with and without driver mutations.

Recently, personalized medicine has become increasingly prominent in perioperative settings. In the adjuvant setting, atezolizumab following cisplatin-based chemotherapy has been shown to improve disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with resected NSCLC expressing programmed death-ligand 1 on IHC in tumor specimens.31 Additionally, adjuvant osimertinib has demonstrated significantly longer DFS and OS in patients with stage IB-IIIA tumors harboring sensitive EGFR mutations32,33 leading to its approval in several countries, including Japan. Neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy has shown better pathological response and longer event-free survival than neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients without EGFR mutations or ALK alterations.34 For patients with resectable NSCLC with driver mutations, several clinical trials are ongoing to investigate the efficacy of preoperative TKIs.35,36 In this era of perioperative personalized medicine, comprehensive biomarker testing at the preoperative diagnostic stage, which determines resectability, is critical for the selection of perioperative treatment strategies. While some patients may not be eligible for these perioperative treatments based on biomarker testing, comprehensive genome profile testing for recurrence has been significantly associated with the selection of first-line treatments after recurrence.37

Our study highlighted that multigene testing is often not carried out in cases >3 years after surgery and that biomarker testing is not conducted in some cases. Although comprehensive multigene testing is clearly required for all patients at recurrence, our findings underscore the importance of performing multigene testing at the perioperative stage to facilitate prognostic stratification. As recommended in current guidelines,38 biomarker testing should be implemented regardless of histological subtype, including squamous-cell carcinoma, and liquid biopsy should at least be considered when tissue samples are unavailable or inadequate. As personalized perioperative medicine continues to evolve, it is crucial to consider the use of cancer genome profiling tests on tumor tissues or blood samples from patients with recent post-operative recurrence to avoid missing therapeutic opportunities related to genetic alterations.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, selection bias may have occurred by not including consecutive cases; however, this was mitigated by the inclusion of a large, multicenter sample size. Secondly, biomarker test timing varied, with some patients undergoing testing before recurrence, which may have biased the population toward more advanced stages at the time of surgery. Finally, as the surveyed facilities were specialized cancer centers, the results may not be generalizable to the broader population, particularly in settings without specialized expertise.

Conclusions

This subgroup analysis of the WJOG15421L study highlights that multigene testing remains underutilized in cases of post-operative recurrence. Notably, patients with post-operative recurrence, irrespective of driver mutation status, exhibited a more favorable prognosis compared with those with advanced disease. As personalized medicine increasingly influences perioperative care, early implementation of multigene testing during the diagnostic phase is critical for optimizing treatment strategies and drug selection for patients with resectable NSCLC. Future research should focus on identifying barriers to the widespread adoption of multigene testing and exploring its long-term impact on patient outcomes.
