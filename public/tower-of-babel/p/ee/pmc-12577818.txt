Late closed or open reduction of developmental dysplasia of the hip: a systematic review and meta-analysis of outcomes
Authors: Lee R. Benaroch, Patrick Thornley, Maia Ross, Brynn Charron, Debra Bartley, Timothy Carey, Thierry E. Benaroch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFor late-detected developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) (6–24 months), it remains unclear whether open reduction (OR) offers better outcomes than closed reduction (CR), despite being more invasive. The primary concern for families and surgeons is the need for secondary surgery to correct residual dysplasia (RD) following delayed treatment. This meta-analysis compares OR and CR by evaluating their association with the need for secondary surgery, RD, and avascular necrosis (AVN).MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted. Studies published prior to December 2024 comparing OR and CR for DDH in children aged 6–24 months with ≥2 years of follow-up were included. Two authors independently screened studies based on predetermined criteria, performed data extraction, and assessed quality.ResultsNine high-quality retrospective studies yielded data from 1044 hips (CR: 540 hips; OR: 504 hips). The mean age at intervention was 8.62 months for CR and 9.64 months for OR. Pooled analysis showed that CR significantly increases the risk for the need of secondary surgery when compared to OR (risk ratio [RR] = 1.48; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.18–1.85; I2 = 69%). A sensitivity analysis removing 1 study showed that the new RR was 1.88 (95% CI, 1.46–2.43; I2 = 0%). OR showed a trend toward greater radiographic improvement when compared to CR. No significant difference in AVN or RD was identified between the OR and CR groups.ConclusionOur results indicate that CR for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is linked to a higher risk of secondary surgery in children aged 6–24 months compared to OR. These findings may help surgeons better counsel families regarding long-term outcomes and the likelihood of further intervention following CR or OR for late-presenting DDH. Future research comparing OR versus CR, the need for secondary surgery, and the potential influence of surgeon bias in treatment decisions would further help the decision-making process in the treatment of late-detected DDH.Level of EvidenceLevel III

The importance of early detection and intervention in patients with DDH is well known[2,3]. Early treatment typically allows patients to avoid surgical intervention, as well as the long-term consequences of residual dysplasia (RD)[3]. Delayed detection and treatment of DDH is associated with the early development of hip osteoarthritis, diminishing function and quality of life, and in turn, the likely need for total hip arthroplasty at an early age[4,5].

Despite screening efforts, DDH may be detected late, defined in this review as 6–24 months of age, and has been estimated to occur at a rate of 2.2 in 1000 in a Canadian cohort[6]. Yet, it remains unclear whether the treatment of late-presenting DDH with open reduction (OR) leads to better outcomes later in life, justifying the use of this more invasive and resource-intensive procedure over closed reduction (CR). Although historical studies suggest that CR is associated with higher rates of RD and subsequent hip arthroplasty[7,8], these typically include patients outside of the 6- to 24-month age range, limiting their generalizability and suitability for guiding clinical practice guidelines. Conversely, OR has been thought to represent a risk factor for the development of avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head[9,10].

Nonetheless, the primary concern for families and surgeons is the need for secondary surgery to correct RD following delayed treatment. Most commonly, secondary interventions entail major surgeries such as pelvic reconstruction and/or femoral shortening osteotomies. It remains unclear how the method of primary procedures affects the rate of reintervention. The need for revision surgery to correct late-presenting DDH has been shown to lead to adverse outcomes, with a high rate of serious complications such as AVN, limb length discrepancy, and recurrent instability[11,12].

Therefore, this study aims to provide a systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature and compare the outcomes of OR and CR for the treatment of late-detected DDH (6–24 months).

Methods

A systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions[13]. Findings were reported in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement[14].

Literature search

A health science librarian-assisted search of the Ovid, MEDLINE, and Embase databases was performed, focusing on studies comparing OR versus CR in patients presenting with late-detected DDH (Supplementary Digital Content Table 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A942). All identified records from the electronic search were downloaded and imported into Covidence systematic review software.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they compared the use of OR versus CR for the treatment of DDH in children 6–24 months of age at intervention with follow-up ≥2 years postoperatively. Studies published from 1 January 1946 to 9 September 2024 were eligible for review.

Articles that met any of the following criteria were excluded from our review: non-English language articles; case reports; case series with less than five patients; nonclinical studies; reviews; abstracts; conference presentations; studies lacking a comparative arm; patients with hip dislocation due to trauma, teratologic, neuromuscular, or known genetic causes; patients who had undergone previous DDH correction surgery; and patients who underwent simultaneous femoral or pelvic osteotomy, or other bony procedures during OR were excluded, unless the OR without osteotomy data were presented separately. Case reports and case series with less than five patients were excluded to enhance generalizability and reduce the impact of individual variability from smaller studies.

Article screening

Duplicate articles were removed from the systematic search results via Covidence. The remaining titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors based on the pre-determined criteria. Outstanding discrepancies were resolved via the study’s senior author. The full texts of the articles were then reviewed. All remaining full-text articles that met criteria were included in the review.

Data extraction and risk of bas

Data extraction was performed from the included studies. Contingent on their availability, the following data and outcomes were recorded: study title, authors, year of publication, study type, age at intervention, length of follow-up, number of participants, total number of hips, number of male versus female participants, number of CR and OR performed, surgical technique, pre- and post-op acetabular index (AI) and lateral central edge angle (LCEA), secondary procedures, RD, and AVN. Risk-of-bias was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS)[15]. As per prior studies, articles scoring less than 9 or 14 were considered poor quality, those scoring between 9 and 14 or 14 and 22 as moderate quality, and those above 14 or 22 as good quality for non-comparative and comparative research, respectively[16].

Data synthesis and evaluation of heterogeneity

The meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4.1 (Nordic Cochrane Centre, Cochrane Collaboration). We employed the Mantel–Haenszel method with a fixed-effect model to generate forest plots, appropriate for dichotomous data types, to estimate the common effect size across studies. We used a risk ratio (RR) as our effect measure to provide a consistent relative effect across studies with similar baseline risks. We quantified heterogeneity using the χ2 test for heterogeneity and the I2 statistic. I2 values of ~25% represented low, ~50% medium, and ~75% represented high heterogeneity[17]. Sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the influence of studies on heterogeneity. We planned to conduct a funnel plot to test for publication bias, but it was omitted given the lack of interpretability associated with small numbers of included studies[18]. We calculated interobserver agreement for the assessments of study eligibility using the Cohen κ coefficient and interpreted the κ values according to Landis and Koch[18].

Results

Literature search and study selection

Based on our primary literature search, 622 studies were returned with 175 duplicates identified and removed. The remaining 447 studies were screened for their title and abstract, and 108 studies progressed to full-text review, with 9 studies finally selected for data extraction and analysis[19–24] (Fig. 1). All nine selected studies were retrospective chart reviews, ranging from 10 to 158 participants. Secondary surgery

Eight of the nine studies reported on secondary surgeries. Reasons for secondary surgery included subluxation, redislocation, and/or RD. Surgeries included OR and pelvic osteotomies[20,24], isolated pelvic osteotomies[23,26], or pelvic osteotomy with femoral shortening osteotomies[25].

Out of the 514 hips treated with CR, 152 required secondary surgery compared to 94 out of 467 hips treated with OR, demonstrating an absolute risk for the need of secondary surgery greater in the CR group compared to the OR group (29.6% vs 20.1%). Furthermore, the pooled analysis showed that CR significantly increases the risk for the need of secondary surgery when compared to OR (RR = 1.48; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.18–1.85; I2 = 69%) (Fig. 2). Given the high heterogeneity, a sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically removing individual studies to assess their influence on the overall results and determine whether any single study disproportionately impacted the findings. This analysis showed that by removing Martino et al[25], the new RR was 1.88 (95% CI, 1.46–2.43; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3).Figure 2.Pooled analysis of the need for secondary surgery after closed reduction versus open reduction. Residual dysplasia

Seven studies assessed RD. Of the 285 hips treated with CR, 142 were classified as residually dysplastic compared to 116 out of 229 hips treated with OR. Although the absolute risk of RD was marginally less for OR compared to CR (49.8% vs 50.7%), the pooled analysis of these results did not show any significant difference with regards to risk of RD after treatment (RR = 1.00; 95% CI, 0.84–1.20; I2 = 36%) (Fig. 4).Figure 4.Pooled analysis of residual dysplasia after closed reduction versus open reduction. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of treatment for late-detected DDH provides valuable insights into the outcomes of CR versus OR in children aged 6–24 months. We analyzed data from 1044 hips across nine high-quality retrospective studies, revealing important findings that may influence clinical decision-making. The results demonstrate a significantly higher risk of secondary surgery with CR compared to OR, with an associated trend toward greater radiographic improvement as measured by AI and LCEA. Although the differences in AI and LCEA did not always reach statistical significance, the observed trends suggest that OR may facilitate improved acetabular remodeling, likely due to more reliable concentric reduction. Clinically, this could translate to lower risk of late RD or reduced need for secondary surgery. However, given that only a limited number of studies reported comparable radiographic outcomes and could be pooled, more research with standardized radiographic reporting and long-term follow-up is needed to confirm whether these observed differences translate into meaningful clinical benefits.

The concentric reduction of the femoral head is widely recognized as an integral component for proper acetabular development[28,29]. Therefore, the ability of OR to allow for direct visualization and removal soft tissue impediments that may preclude a concentric reduction appears to be a key factor in the trends and outcomes observed in our study. While one potential downside of OR is its increased risk of AVN relative to CR[30], our results demonstrate no significant difference in AVN rates when comparing treatment modalities for this specific patient population.

Although our findings indicate that OR resulted in fewer secondary surgeries and similar AVN rates compared to CR, there remains a plausibility of potential unknown long-term sequelae associated with violating the native hip capsule in OR procedures. A recent retrospective review by Desai et al[31] assessed the prevalence and risk factors for developing stiffness following OR in 170 DDH hips at a mean age of 21.6 months and mean follow-up of 46.8 months. They found the incidence of arthrofibrosis to be 36%, with about two-thirds of those cases resolving within 6 months of surgery with physiotherapy (PT) alone. However, the remaining 23 hips developed significant arthrofibrosis, and all required >6 months of PT for persistent range of motion concerns, with 7 requiring manipulation under anesthesia, and 2 requiring inpatient rehabilitation admission. All patients with postoperative arthrofibrosis were eventually able to achieve a functional range of motion in this study. However, the toll incurred by delayed/impaired ambulation, multiple months of PT, and occasionally reoperation or readmission can be significant for caregivers and should not be disregarded. The present included studies did not evaluate long-term functional outcomes, and therefore, it is possible that OR might lead to more functional limitations than CR over time. Future research should prioritize objective assessments such as gait analysis, radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis, and validated patient-reported outcome measures to capture the full spectrum of long-term sequelae. Larger prospective studies with long-term follow-up beyond skeletal maturity and with standardized PT protocols are needed to better understand the role OR may play in contributing to postoperative stiffness, altered biomechanics, and overall functional limitations.

While our findings demonstrate a significantly higher rate of secondary surgery following CR compared to OR, it is important to note that this review was not designed to determine optimal treatment selection criteria but rather was intended to compare outcomes between the two approaches using available pooled evidence. However, our results do suggest that certain patient-specific factors may help guide clinical decision-making. Age at presentation remains a critical determinant; older children within the 6- to 24-month window may be less likely to achieve a stable concentric reduction via CR due to progressive soft tissue contracture, thus favoring an OR approach. Additionally, the severity of dislocation, as quantified by radiographic grading systems such as the Tönnis or International Hip Dysplasia Institute (IHDI) classifications, may influence success rates. Higher-grade dislocations (e.g., Tönnis grade III–IV or IHDI grade III-IV) are less likely to achieve a CR, suggesting a lower threshold for proceeding to OR in this subgroup. Therefore, future studies should stratify outcomes by these variables to inform more precise treatment algorithms and identify which patients are most likely to benefit from one approach over the other, ultimately improving individualized care for children with late-presenting DDH.

Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature aimed at optimizing outcomes for late-detected DDH. A recent review by Vaquero-Picado et al[32] reemphasized that achieving a concentric and stable reduction is essential for normal acetabular development and long-term hip preservation, reinforcing the importance of selecting the most appropriate reduction method based on individual patient characteristics. Complementing this, a recent scientometric analysis by Shu et al[33] underscores that DDH literature remains dominated by studies with lower levels of evidence, noting that high-quality comparative data to guide treatment decisions, particularly in late-presenting cases, are still lacking. Although our study is Level III evidence, it helps address this gap by systematically synthesizing comparative data from a narrowly defined patient population and highlighting key variables for risk stratification that warrant prospective evaluation.

Given the fact that there was no statistical difference in RD between treatments, yet CR demonstrated significantly higher secondary surgery, there remains a possibility of surgeon knowledge of prior treatment and bias confounding the results. However, one included study, Bolland et al[26], attempted to assess for bias because of the surgeon not being blinded to whether an OR or CR was carried out. They compared the mean AI over time in OR and CR cases that ended up having a secondary procedure and found no difference in the AI values between groups. This suggests that surgeon decision-making in this study was solely based on clinical and radiological assessments, not knowledge of a prior OR or CR[26]. No other included studies assessed whether surgeons who performed a CR had a lower threshold and/or bias to perform a secondary surgery than when performing an OR. Therefore, future prospective research is warranted to assess the role surgeon bias may play in regard to treatment modality decisions.

This study should be interpreted considering its limitations. Non-English-language studies were excluded, though evidence suggests language barriers generally do not significantly affect intervention estimates[34]. Additionally, the small number of included studies may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, the large sample size – 540 CR and 504 OR hips – and low heterogeneity provides substantial statistical power, reducing the risk of overfitting[34]. By addressing key gaps in the literature, summarizing the most relevant evidence, and maintaining transparency about the limitations, the findings provide a robust foundation for guiding future research and clinical practice without overstating their significance.

Conclusion

This study provides further evidence that late CR of DDH is associated with a 48% higher risk of requiring secondary surgery later in life compared to OR. Additionally, OR showed no significant difference in risk of RD or development of AVN after treatment. These results will allow surgeons to better inform patients and caregivers regarding the long-term outcomes and the need for secondary surgery following late CR or OR in DDH. However, future studies comparing OR versus CR, the need for secondary surgery and the potential influence of surgeon bias in treatment decisions would further help the decision-making process in the treatment of late-detected DDH.
