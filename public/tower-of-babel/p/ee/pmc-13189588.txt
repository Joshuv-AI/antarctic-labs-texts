The Worst of Both Worlds: A Case Series of Adenosquamous Carcinoma of the Esophagus
Authors: Khaled H. Husain, Rachael Hagen, Bianca Thakkar, Norbert Promagan, Minh Thu T. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEsophageal adenosquamous carcinoma (EASC) is a rare, aggressive malignancy with histologic features of both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. We report a case series of 2 patients with EASC. The first case involves a middle-aged woman with traditional risk factors, including a 46-pack-year smoking history, frequent alcohol use, and long-segment Barrett's esophagus, managed with neoadjuvant chemoradiation, esophagectomy with lymphadenectomy, and radiofrequency ablation. The second case involves a young man without traditional risk factors who presented with advanced disease. These cases highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of EASC and the need for comprehensive management and vigilant surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer ranks as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, primarily comprising adenocarcinoma (AC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Esophageal adenosquamous carcinoma (EASC), a rare and aggressive variant featuring both AC and SCC components, accounts for 0.37%–1% of cases.1,2 EASC carries a poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate between 19.4% and 37.5% and a median survival of 21–44 months.3 Here, we report 2 cases of EASC.

CASE REPORT

Case 1

A 59-year-old woman presented with a 2-year history of progressive esophageal dysphagia to solids and liquids and a 40 lb weight loss. She reported a 46-pack-year smoking history and weekly consumption of 6 beers. Carcinoembryonic antigen was elevated (6.3 ng/mL; reference range 0–5.0 ng/mL); otherwise, laboratory results were unremarkable. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed a 15-cm segment of Barrett's esophagus and an ulcerated midesophageal mass (Figure 1). Pathology confirmed invasive EASC, composed of 20% AC (Figure 2) and 80% SCC (Figure 3).

Histopathology findings demonstrating the adenocarcinoma component of esophageal adenosquamous carcinoma. (A) Glandular component with intracellular mucin. (B) CK7 cytokeratin marker highlighting the glandular component.

Histopathology findings revealing the squamous cell carcinoma component of esophageal adenosquamous carcinoma. (A) Squamous metaplasia with keratinization. (B) P40 stain highlighting the squamous component.

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) revealed a T2N1 lesion invading the muscularis propria. Computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography (PET)-CT showed a 5-mm right paraesophageal lymph node without evidence of distant metastasis. She was ultimately diagnosed with EASC (staged as cT2N1M0) and started neoadjuvant chemoradiation with carboplatin and paclitaxel.

Six months after initial presentation, she underwent a complete esophagectomy with cervical anastomosis of the esophagus and gastric conduit. Pathology showed a favorable treatment response, with over 15 lymph nodes negative for malignancy, and repeat PET-CT showed no evidence of residual disease. Three months postoperatively, EGD and pathology analysis demonstrated low-grade dysplasia in the cervical esophagus and Barrett's esophagus at the anastomosis, requiring radiofrequency ablation. She continues to undergo oncological surveillance with CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, which thus far has shown no recurrence/metastatic disease.

Case 2

A 26-year-old man with no medical history presented with progressive dysphagia to solid foods and a 50-pound weight loss over 4 months. Laboratory findings revealed iron deficiency anemia: hemoglobin 8.4 g/dL (reference range 13.0–17.7 g/dL), mean corpuscular volume 76 fL (reference range 80–100 fL), ferritin 12 ng/mL (reference range 30–400 μg/L), and iron saturation 13% (reference range 20%–50%); laboratory results were otherwise unremarkable. Initial EGD showed esophageal stenosis requiring dilation to 10 mm and a large, irregular gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) lesion (Figure 4). Pathology revealed erosive carditis and Helicobacter pylori but was negative for malignancy. Ten days later, abdominal CT identified a large GEJ mass with liver and lung lesions.

(A) Esophagogastroduodenoscopy showing esophageal stenosis in the lower third of the esophagus, requiring dilation. (B) A large, irregular mass was visualized where the gastric cardia meets the gastroesophageal junction with a reticular pattern of vessels.

Two days after CT imaging, EUS demonstrated esophageal narrowing, for which stent placement and aspiration were performed. Pathology confirmed invasive moderately differentiated EASC (Figure 5), which was also identified on liver biopsy (Figure 6). The malignancy was staged as cT4N3M1. The patient denied alcohol use, nicotine use, or family history of malignancy. Genetic testing revealed a BRCA2 gene mutation.

He was started on chemotherapy and radiotherapy at 2 weeks and 3 months postdiagnosis, respectively. He later required biliary sphincterotomy and stent replacement. Despite treatment, he developed liver failure and uncontrolled pain due to cancer progression. He then opted for a comfort-focused approach, and the patient ultimately passed away.

DISCUSSION

EASC represents a distinct histologic subtype of esophageal cancer, combining features of AC and SCC, with an aggressive clinical course and limited data guiding its management. The origin of EASC remains unclear but is frequently associated with Barrett's esophagus, likely driven by genetic alterations, including loss of heterozygosity, missense mutations, and reflux-induced metaplasia.1,4,5 In the first case, Barrett's esophagus increased the patient's risk of AC, while smoking and alcohol use elevated her risk of SCC. By contrast, the second case illustrates an aggressive disease phenotype in a young patient without traditional risk factors. This atypical presentation raises the possibility of underlying genetic susceptibility contributing to disease development and progression. The presence of a BRCA2 mutation in this patient is notable, as BRCA2 variants have been associated with an increased risk of esophageal malignancies, although their role in EASC remains poorly defined.6 Rapid progression to metastatic disease despite multimodal therapy highlights the heterogeneity of EASC and suggests that younger patients with atypical features may represent a distinct high-risk subgroup. Together, these cases demonstrate the broad clinical spectrum of EASC, ranging from potentially curable localized disease to fulminant metastasis. This contrast raises the question of whether EASC represents a single clinicopathologic entity or a heterogeneous group of biologically distinct malignancies that share overlapping histologic features. While definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from these cases, the marked differences in presentation and clinical course highlight this important consideration.

Numerous theories exist about the origin of EASC. One suggests it arises from glandular cells that transition from AC to SCC during foregut embryogenesis, coinciding with the development of epithelial and submucosal glands.7 Another theory posits that EASC originates from the mucosa, developing into SCC first, with glandular cell differentiation occurring later.8 The AC component is typically located deeper than the SCC component, contributing to frequent misdiagnosis due to sampling error which is a key diagnostic challenge.9

Endoscopically, EASC resembles other esophageal cancers, typically presenting as ulcerated lesions, polypoid masses, or nodular growths. Histologically, it is characterized by the coexistence of AC, exhibiting glandular morphology and mucin production, and SCC, identified by keratinization and intercellular bridges. Immunohistochemical staining helps differentiate the components, with AC positive for cytokeratin-7 and mucin, while SCC is positive for P40 and other markers.10 For staging, EUS is the gold standard, complemented by CT to evaluate metastatic spread and PET-CT for further assessment. EASC follows the standard tumor, node, metastasis classification for esophageal carcinomas, incorporating depth of invasion, nodal involvement, and distant metastasis, although histologic grading is often limited by its biphasic composition.11

Although no EASC-specific management guidelines exist, current treatment options include chemotherapy, radiation, and/or surgery, with transthoracic esophagectomy with lymphadenectomy remaining the primary approach.1 Lymph node metastasis is a critical prognostic factor linked to poorer outcomes.12 The prognosis of EASC is generally poor, although comparable with esophageal SCC, with 1 study reporting a 5-year overall survival rate of 29.6% for EASC vs 42.5% for SCC of the esophagus.3 Adjuvant chemotherapy may improve survival in postesophagectomy patients.3 In this context, tumor location may represent an additional source of heterogeneity. The second case involved a GEJ tumor, whereas the first arose in the proximal esophagus. In esophageal AC, GEJ tumors are associated with more aggressive behavior and a poorer prognosis, potentially reflecting differences in tumor biology.13 Although this has not been specifically demonstrated in EASC, tumor location may similarly contribute to the divergent clinical outcomes observed in these cases.

Recognizing this rare, aggressive entity is crucial due to significant diagnostic challenges and poor prognosis. The overlapping histologic components increase the risk of misdiagnosis, with a preference for SCC due to sampling limitations. Although the prognosis is generally poor, multimodal treatment may improve outcomes. These cases highlight the marked heterogeneity of EASC, ranging from localized disease responsive to multimodal therapy to rapidly progressive metastatic cancer in young patients without traditional risk factors. This variability underscores the need for individualized management and further study of disease biology and EASC-specific management.
