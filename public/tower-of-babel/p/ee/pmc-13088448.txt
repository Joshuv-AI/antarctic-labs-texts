Differential diagnosis between oral squamous cell carcinoma and osteomyelitis through mandibular canal changes on panoramic radiographs
Authors: Jung Eun Park, Kyung-Hoe Huh, Ju-Hee Kang, Jo-Eun Kim, Won-Jin Yi, Min-Suk Heo, Sam-Sun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating between oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and osteomyelitis (OM) through imaging is essential due to the overlapping clinical and radiological presentations of these conditions, complicating diagnosis. This study assessed the adjunctive diagnostic value of mandibular canal–related radiographic features on panoramic radiographs to aid in differentiating OSCC from OM, and to propose a simple and efficient approach for general dental practitioners.MethodsA total of 166 panoramic radiographs from patients with histopathologically confirmed OSCC (n = 83) and OM (n = 83), involving the mandibular third molar region and/or retromolar trigone with bone destruction, were retrospectively analyzed. The border changes of the mandibular canal and adjacent sclerosis were assessed on the panoramic radiographs. Statistical analysis was performed using chi-squared tests to determine the significance of differences between OSCC and OM for each radiographic feature. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was also performed to evaluate the discriminative performance of a composite scoring model for distinguishing OSCC from OM, in which one point was assigned for each high-risk imaging feature for OSCC.ResultsDestruction of the borders of the mandibular canal was significantly more frequent in OSCC cases (P < 0.001), while border thickening of the mandibular canal and adjacent sclerosis were more commonly associated with OM (P < 0.001). Additionally, a composite scoring model based on these imaging features demonstrated high discriminative performance, with an area under the curve of 0.92, further supporting differentiation between OSCC and OM.ConclusionThe changes in the mandibular canal, including border destruction, border thickening, and adjacent sclerosis, observed on panoramic radiographs may serve as useful adjunctive indicators for differentiating OSCC from OM. Recognition of these features on routine panoramic imaging may help guide appropriate further diagnostic evaluation, particularly in clinical settings where immediate access to three-dimensional imaging is limited.Keywords: Mandibular canal, Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Osteomyelitis, Panoramic radiography, Third molar

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains a significant public health concern, with its aggressive nature contributing to a survival rate of approximately 50%, even with advancements in diagnosis and treatment [1]. Particularly vulnerable to OSCC is the retromolar trigone, a region proximal to the wisdom teeth known for its dense epithelial presence and susceptibility to malignant changes [2, 3]. The predilection of OSCC for this area complicates differential diagnosis, especially when considering inflammatory conditions like pericoronitis and resultant osteomyelitis (OM) that also manifest in the jaw [4, 5].

Osteomyelitis of the jaw, primarily resulting from bacterial infections, often originates from periapical abscesses or exacerbation of periodontal diseases [6]. The complexity and susceptibility of the area surrounding the wisdom teeth to impaction and infection make it a common site for OM [7–10]. This condition can lead to significant bone destructions, sometimes closely mimicking that observed in OSCC. Moreover, OSCC and OM often exhibit similar clinical symptoms such as swelling, pain, trismus, and paresthesia, particularly in the posterior mandible [11–16. These nonspecific signs can delay accurate diagnosis and treatment, especially in the early stages of disease progression.

The challenge in differentiating OSCC-induced bone destruction from inflammatory destruction seen in OM is further compounded by their overlapping radiographic features [17]. Both conditions can present with irregular bone destruction, periosteal new bone formation, and varying degrees of radiolucency and radiopacity. Additionally, the differentiation becomes even more complex when considering cases where SCC arises from pre-existing OM [16, 18, 19]. The potential for malignant transformation in OM patients adds another layer of difficulty, making it crucial to employ detailed and vigilant diagnostic strategies to accurately distinguish between these two conditions. This emphasizes the importance of integrating multiple radiographic features and clinical insights to enhance diagnostic accuracy and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Panoramic radiography, a staple in dental diagnostic imaging, is widely used for its convenience and ability to provide a comprehensive view of the jaw, aiding in the detection of lesions and assessment of bone structures. On panoramic radiographs, the mandibular canal is a typical well-defined normal anatomic structure in the third molar region, which is the predisposing site for OSCC and OM. Previous studies have analyzed the anatomical variation of the mandibular canal or the relationship between the mandibular canal and the third molar root apex on panoramic images [20–22]. Some previous studies have used mandibular canal widening or displacement for diagnostic aid [23, 24]. However, to our knowledge, there has been no research to differentiate OSCC from OM through changes in the mandibular canal on panoramic radiographs.

The present study aimed to evaluate the adjunctive diagnostic value of mandibular canal–related radiographic features on panoramic radiographs to aid in distinguishing OSCC from OM, and to propose a simple and efficient approach that general dental practitioners can apply in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of [blinded for review], and the requirement for informed consent was waived. All procedures adhered to relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Patient selection

Patients with histopathologically confirmed OSCC from January 2000 to December 2023 and those with OM from January 2011 to December 2023 were retrospectively reviewed through our institutional data system. Patients with a lesion arising in the mandibular third molar region and/or retromolar trigone and causing bone destruction were included. Patients of OM group were selected to match those of OSCC group regarding age and gender. Patients who had a history of taking antiresorptive or antiangiogenic agents and who received radiotherapy or chemotherapy before the panoramic radiographs were taken were excluded. Patients who had a recurrent lesion and whose panoramic radiographs did not reveal the mandibular canal clearly or were of poor quality, thereby making evaluation difficult, were also excluded.

The final diagnosis of OSCC and OM was established based on histopathological confirmation and advanced imaging including computed tomography (CT) or cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). However, the present study focused exclusively on the evaluation of panoramic radiographs, and three-dimensional imaging findings were not included in the imaging analysis.

Image acquisition

All panoramic radiographs were obtained using a consistent digital panoramic machine (OP100, Instrumentarium Corp., Tuusula, Finland) with settings of 66–73 kVp, 6.4–12 mA, and an exposure time of 16.8–17.6 s. Patients were positioned in the machine as per the manufacturer’s guidelines, ensuring the machine’s vertical line aligned with the patient’s sagittal plane and the Frankfort horizontal plane remained parallel to the floor. The images were taken using PSP image plates (12 × 10 inches) and processed through an FCR system (Fuji Computed Radiography 5000R, Fuji Photo Film Co. Ltd., Düsseldorf, Germany).

Diagnostic performance of general dentists

In order to assess the diagnostic performance of general dentists to differentiate OSCC from OM based on panoramic radiographs, three general dentists with 3 to 5 years of clinical experience and no specialized training in oral and maxillofacial radiology were enrolled in the present study. They were blinded to the clinical and histopathological information and asked to classify panoramic radiographs from selected patients as either OSCC or OM. The radiographs were presented in random order. The diagnostic performance of each dentist was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.

Analysis of imaging features related to the mandibular canal

The mandibular canal changes near the mandibular third molar and retromolar trigone were analyzed and the area of greatest bone destruction/resorption was used as a reference point for assessment. The imaging features assessed were border destruction and thickening of the mandibular canal, and finally adjacent sclerotic bone change.

Border destruction was defined as abrupt loss of superior or inferior cortical border of the mandibular canal. Border thickening was defined as blurring and increased width of superior or inferior border of the mandibular canal. Sclerosis was assessed as loss of trabecular architecture and homogeneously increased bone density surrounding the mandibular canal. Presence or absence of each imaging feature was determined based on the comparison with the corresponding normal mandibular canal on the opposite side.

All panoramic radiographs were independently evaluated by two oral and maxillofacial radiologists with over 10 years of experience using a picture archiving and communication system (INFINITT PACS; INFINITT Healthcare, Seoul, Korea). They were blinded to the clinical and histopathological information. In addition, they were blinded to the purpose of this study as well. In cases of disagreement, images were reassessed to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis

Interobserver agreement between two oral and maxillofacial radiologists for each radiographic variable was assessed by calculating Cohen’s kappa coefficient for qualitative variables [25]. The strength of the Cohen’s kappa coefficient was interpreted as follows: 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and > 0.81, almost perfect agreement [26].

Comparisons of each imaging feature between the OSCC and OM groups were performed using chi-squared tests, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. For statistically significant imaging variables, ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the discriminative performance. The AUC was calculated to determine its discriminative ability. An AUC of 0.5 was considered to indicate no discrimination, 0.5 to 0.7 poor discrimination, 0.7 to 0.8 acceptable discrimination, 0.8 to 0.9 excellent discrimination, and greater than 0.9 outstanding discrimination [27].

A composite scoring model was then developed to enhance discriminative accuracy. For this model, a score of 1 or 0 was assigned to each statistically significant imaging feature. The presence of mandibular canal border destruction was considered a high-risk feature for OSCC and assigned a score of 1. In contrast, border thickening and adjacent sclerosis were OM-favoring features and were assigned a score of 0 when present; conversely, the absence of these OM-favoring features was assigned a score of 1. A composite score was calculated by summing the points, resulting in a possible score range of 0 to 3.

ROC curve analysis was also performed on the composite scores to evaluate the overall discriminative performance of the model in differentiating OSCC from OM. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index to maximize both sensitivity and specificity.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.10.12.

Results

Patient demographics

Table 1 provides an overview of the demographic characteristics of patients. A total of 83 patients (24 female; 59 male) with OSCC and 83 patients (26 female; 57 male) with OM were enrolled in the present study. Gender distribution shows a predominance of males in both groups (71.1% in OSCC and 68.7% in OM), with no significant difference between the groups (P = 0.63). The mean age of the patients with OSCC was 69.51 years (range 38–97 years) and the mean age of patients with OM was 68.53 years (range 36–94 years). Age distribution reveals that both conditions are most common in older age groups, particularly in the 60s (36.1% for both OSCC and OM), followed by the 70s and 80s. The overall age distribution shows no significant variation between the two groups (P = 0.69), reflecting a similar demographic profile across the conditions. These comparable age and gender distributions reflect the matched selection between the OSCC and OM groups, minimizing demographic influence on the radiographic comparisons.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed whether panoramic radiographic features of the mandibular canal can aid in differentiating OSCC from OM—an important challenge given the overlapping clinical and radiologic presentations of these conditions. The results demonstrated that OSCC cases were significantly more likely to show destruction of the mandibular canal borders, whereas OM cases typically exhibited cortical border thickening and pronounced surrounding sclerosis. Moreover, combining all three radiographic indicators further improved diagnostic discrimination between OSCC and OM.

As demonstrated by the low diagnostic performance of general dentists in this study, differentiating between OSCC and OM on panoramic radiographs without specific radiographic guidance is challenging. Given the overlapping clinical and radiological presentations of these conditions, the mandibular canal changes observed on panoramic radiographs demonstrated substantial potential as practical diagnostic indicators.

Clinical factors may substantially influence the radiographic appearance of mandibular canal alterations and should be considered when interpreting panoramic findings. In particular, the duration of lesion evolution and disease progression play a critical role in the development and detectability of radiographic changes. Early-stage lesions, whether malignant or inflammatory, may not yet produce evident alterations of the mandibular canal, thereby limiting the sensitivity of panoramic radiographs for early diagnosis. Conversely, more advanced disease is more likely to demonstrate cortical disruption, thickening, or adjacent sclerosis, as observed in the present study.

Symptomatology also affects both the timing of imaging referral and the radiographic presentation. Oral squamous cell carcinoma is often asymptomatic in its early stages, which may delay clinical suspicion and subsequent imaging, whereas osteomyelitis is more frequently associated with pain or inflammatory symptoms. As a result, the cases included in this retrospective analysis may represent relatively progressed stages at the time of panoramic examination, a factor that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Although three-dimensional imaging modalities such as computed tomography or cone-beam computed tomography provide superior anatomical detail and remain essential for definitive diagnosis, their availability is limited in many clinical settings. In routine dental practice, panoramic radiography is often the initial imaging modality obtained. Within this clinical context, the mandibular canal–related features identified in this study should not be regarded as standalone diagnostic criteria, but rather as adjunctive indicators that may raise suspicion and guide the decision for further advanced imaging. Therefore, the proposed radiographic features may contribute to improved clinical triage in settings where immediate access to three-dimensional imaging is not feasible.

A key insight from this investigation is the diagnostic importance of the mandibular canal as a radiographic landmark. The canal’s course is consistently visible on panoramic images, making any alteration in its outline a readily observable clue. Displacement or widening of the mandibular canal have been used as a diagnostic aid [16–18]. However, to our knowledge no prior study has specifically focused on using canal alterations to distinguish OSCC from OM. Our findings fill this gap by demonstrating that an abrupt disappearance of the canal border without accompanying sclerosis or cortical thickening of the canal is suggestive of malignancy. Recognizing this pattern on a routine panoramic radiograph should alert the clinician to the possible presence of OSCC and prompt timely confirmatory evaluation to rule out cancer or other aggressive disease.

In clinical practice, panoramic radiography is often the first-line imaging modality for evaluating jaw pathology because it provides a comprehensive view of the mandible and maxilla. It is widely available and is routinely used not only for detecting pathology but also for general dental screening and treatment planning, for example, in dental implant evaluations where an overview of bone anatomy is required [28, 29]. Given that intraoral radiographs cannot adequately visualize the posterior mandible, a panoramic radiograph is usually obtained when patients present with pain or swelling in the third molar region or retromolar trigone. The critical concern is that OSCC in this area can radiographically mimic osteomyelitis or other inflammatory bone diseases, risking a misdiagnosis and a delay in proper treatment. Indeed, there are documented instances where oral carcinomas were initially mistaken for chronic infections on imaging [17, 30], leading to postponed biopsy and worsened outcomes. Our results indicate that careful scrutiny of mandibular canal changes on these initial panoramic radiographs could help avert such errors. If suspicious radiographic signs are identified early, clinicians can promptly pursue confirmatory diagnostic steps, such as biopsy or advanced imaging, thereby improving patient prognosis and potentially reducing unnecessary procedures or delays.

A pronounced loss of the cortical outline of the mandibular canal (erosion of its superior and/or inferior border) emerged as a strong indicator of OSCC in this study. Such canal border destruction was observed significantly more frequently in the OSCC group than in the OM group, reflecting the propensity of malignancies to invade and obliterate bony landmarks. This observation is consistent with prior reports that aggressive bone loss is a classic radiographic hallmark of oral malignancies [31, 32]. In practice, noticing an undefined or absent mandibular canal border on a panoramic radiograph should therefore raise concern for a neoplastic process and can be a key criterion for distinguishing OSCC from OM in ambiguous cases.

Conversely, the presence of cortical bone thickening along the mandibular canal was strongly associated with OM rather than carcinoma. In our series, this cortical bone reaction, manifesting as a radiopaque, thickened canal wall, was seen in the majority of OM cases but in very few OSCC cases—a difference that was highly significant (χ² = 79.4, P < 0.001). This finding is biologically plausible: chronic inflammatory lesions like OM often stimulate new bone formation and sclerosis, whereas aggressive tumors such as OSCC destroy bone without eliciting such reactive thickening. Our results align with previous reports that cortical border thickening is a hallmark of chronic osteitic processes and is seldom seen in malignant jaw lesions [33, 34]. Evaluating this feature in conjunction with other radiographic markers thus adds confidence to the differential diagnosis, especially when clinical symptoms are non-specific.

In the present study, generalized medullary sclerosis adjacent to the mandibular canal was another distinguishing feature: it was prominent in most OM cases [35] and typically minimal or absent in OSCC. This pattern accords with prior observations that extensive sclerosis reflects chronic inflammation rather than malignancy [36, 37]. However, sclerosis alone proved to be an imperfect indicator, since many older patients have baseline sclerotic changes and OSCC can arise in areas of pre-existing bone sclerosis. In other words, the presence of sclerosis does not exclude OSCC if another aggressive sign, such as border destruction, is present. Our findings underscore that sclerosis must be interpreted in context with the status of the canal borders. For instance, the combination of sclerosis with an abrupt loss of the mandibular canal should raise concern for malignancy, whereas an intact canal border accompanied by reactive sclerosis strongly favors a diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis. These nuances highlight the need for a comprehensive radiographic assessment that considers both destructive and reactive changes to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Combining all three key radiographic indicators into a simple composite score further improved diagnostic accuracy. This multi-feature model outperformed any single sign, reflecting the multifactorial nature of these disease processes. By integrating both destructive and reactive bone changes into one assessment, the composite approach provides a robust and practical tool that could be applied in clinical settings to enhance differential diagnosis.

The present study has some limitations. First, the shape and clarity of the mandibular canal can vary among individuals, which may limit the objectivity in evaluating changes in the mandibular canal. However, in this study, the changes were compared with the mandibular canal on the contralateral side of the same patient, and assessed through consensus by two experienced oral and maxillofacial radiologists, which showed substantial interobserver agreement. Second, our analysis was based solely on panoramic radiographs. While this approach reflects the realities of initial dental evaluations, it means we did not incorporate data from other imaging modalities such as computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Furthermore, we did not integrate other clinical information or laboratory findings that might aid diagnosis. Other clinico-radiologic features and histopathological details were beyond the scope of this study, although they could be important in a comprehensive diagnostic workup. Nevertheless, this approach using only panoramic radiographs is expected to provide a simple and intuitive method of differential diagnosis for general practitioners in dental clinics.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that specific changes in the mandibular canal on panoramic radiographs—particularly border destruction, border thickening, and adjacent sclerosis—may serve as useful adjunctive indicators in differentiating OSCC from OM. The composite scoring model integrating these imaging features can provide valuable insights into the differential diagnosis, underscoring the clinical utility of detailed radiographic evaluation of the mandibular canal in clinical triage and guiding further diagnostic evaluation. Accordingly, the findings of this study should be interpreted as adjunctive screening indicators rather than definitive diagnostic criteria.
