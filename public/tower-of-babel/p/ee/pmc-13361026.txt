Background

Description of the condition

Dengue is a viral disease that affects mainly tropical and subtropical regions. It is considered endemic in these regions, meaning the virus circulates continuously at a baseline level within the population, punctuated by periodic outbreaks [1]. It is transmitted primarily by the Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes, which act as vectors (organisms that carry and transmit the virus from one person to another) [1]. The disease presents mildly in 90% to 95% of cases, with symptoms such as high fever, headache, pain behind the eyes, and rashes, and usually resolves without complications [1]. However, approximately 5% of people with dengue progress to severe disease [2], which can cause significant complications such as dengue shock syndrome and severe bleeding, often accompanied by intense abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, rapid breathing, bleeding from the gums or nose, fatigue, restlessness, blood in vomit or stool, intense thirst, pale and cold skin, and generalised weakness [1]. Annually, nearly half a million people with severe dengue require hospital care, and approximately 2.5% of these hospitalized cases succumb to the illness [3].

Traditionally, urban areas acted as transmission hotspots for the disease due to a combination of high‐density human populations and a proliferation of mosquito breeding sites, a concentration driven in part by ongoing urbanization and globalization. However, recently, the number of dengue cases has increased in rural areas, where socioeconomic and environmental conditions often hinder effective vector control [4]. Globally, the incidence of dengue has increased significantly; the World Health Organization (WHO) reported a rise in cases from 505,430 in 2000 to 14.6 million in 2024 [1]. By 2024, the incidence of dengue reached historic levels, affecting over 80 countries in Africa, the Americas, the Eastern Mediterranean, Southeast Asia, and the Western Pacific [5]. That year, over 12,000 dengue‐related deaths were recorded [1]. Multiple factors have led to this rise in incidence. Firstly, vector distribution has widened to countries that were previously free of dengue. This is due to natural aspects of climate, such as the 2023 El Nino (a climate phenomenon that affects worldwide weather patterns [6]), and increases in temperatures and humidity caused by global warming that favour the proliferation of mosquitoes [7]. Secondly, in several nations, health systems that are strained by political and financial instability have struggled to respond [1].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

In the absence of specific antivirals and broadly available vaccines, vector control remains the primary strategy to prevent and reduce dengue transmission [8].

Vector‐control interventions reduce dengue transmission by lowering the density and survival of Aedes mosquitoes, and so reducing human contact with them, thus interrupting the virus transmission cycle [9]. Mathematical transmission models describe how entomological indicators relate to dengue incidence through changes in the basic reproductive number. When interventions decrease the density or survival of mosquitoes, the reproductive number decreases, which prevents sustained transmission [10].

Systematic reviews and modelling studies have clarified this mechanism [9, 10]. They show that reductions in adult mosquito density and larval indices lead to measurable decreases in dengue incidence, and that integrated control, which targets both adult and aquatic stages, substantially reduces infection prevalence. Logic models and empirical evidence further support this causal pathway, demonstrating that interventions that reduce vector density or breeding sites lead to proportional declines in entomological indices and subsequent decreases in human infection rates [11].

Community organizations, public health specialists, and entomology experts implement vector‐control strategies to reduce dengue transmission in urban and semi‐urban communities with high vector density [12]. Vector‐control teams adapt their actions to local conditions, taking climatic factors and predominant breeding sites into consideration [13]. Continuous supervision of vector‐control strategy implementation, together with entomological and epidemiological monitoring, helps optimize strategies and maximize their impact [8].

Following the framework proposed by the Pan American Health Organization [13], vector‐control interventions can be grouped into three main categories: chemical, biological, and environmental, each targeting either adult or aquatic stages of Aedes mosquitoes.

Biological interventions

Biological interventions include biological agents or organisms that naturally reduce mosquito populations through targeting the aquatic or adult stages [13]:

aquatic stages: interventions include larvivorous (larvae‐eating) fish, copepods, Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (BTi; a bacterium), or other microbial larvicides (agents applied to bodies of water to kill or harm larvae or pupae).adult stages: interventions include the release of genetically modified or sterile male mosquitoes to reduce adult mosquito fertility, and other biological control methods such as entomopathogenic fungi, which reduce adult mosquito survival and subsequent population density.

Chemical interventions

Chemical interventions include synthetic substances that are used to kill or repel mosquitoes [13]. These can target aquatic or adult stages:

aquatic stages: interventions include larvicides such as organophosphates, insect growth regulators, or other chemical agents (e.g. temephos, chlorpyrifos, fenthion, pirimiphos‐methyl, or pyriproxyfen) applied to water containers or breeding sites to eliminate larvae and pupae before they reach adulthood.adult stages: interventions include killing adult mosquitoes (adulticiding) through space spraying, thermal fogging, or indoor residual spraying, using insecticides such as pyrethroids; and the use of insecticide‐treated materials such as curtains or bed nets to reduce adult mosquito populations and human–vector contact.

Environmental interventions

Environmental interventions aim to modify or manage environmental conditions to reduce mosquito breeding opportunities and contact between humans and vectors [13]; again, these can target aquatic and adult stages:

aquatic stages: interventions include the covering, cleaning, or eliminating of containers that serve as breeding sites; improvements in solid waste management, such as the collection and proper disposal of discarded items in which water can accumulate; and ensuring proper water storage practices.adult stages: interventions include the use of physical barriers, such as window or door screens.

Combination of two or more types of intervention

Chemical, biological or environmental methods are implemented together as part of an integrated vector management approach [14]. By acting on different life stages of Aedes mosquitoes, the three categories of interventions work synergistically to reduce vector density, limit virus transmission, and prevent dengue outbreaks [14]. Their combined and sustained application under an integrated vector‐management framework provides the greatest long‐term impact in reducing disease burden [15].

Why it is important to do this review

Understanding how vector‐control strategies impact the prevention of dengue transmission is necessary in order to combat the increasing global incidence of the disease and its potentially severe complications [1]. In a context where climate change, urbanization, and globalization drive dengue’s geographic expansion, the effectiveness of vector‐control strategies is more relevant than ever. Effective interventions can significantly reduce the disease burden, prevent outbreaks, and protect at‐risk communities [13].

Health professionals need up‐to‐date information on best control practices to advise and treat affected communities more efficiently. For communities, reduced transmission means lower exposure to the disease and an associated decrease in dengue‐associated morbidity and mortality. Within communities, implementing effective control measures can improve quality of life and encourage proactive public health practices [16].

Policymakers face the challenge of allocating resources efficiently to vector‐control programmes and need solid evidence to support their decisions [17]. Vector‐control strategies can be more cost‐effective than clinical treatment [18], reducing healthcare expenditures associated with hospitalizations, case management, and outbreak response [19, 20], as indicated by a cost‐effective analysis conducted in México that reported savings up to 4400 USD per disability adjusted year [21]. Strengthening preventive approaches can, therefore, yield long‐term health and economic benefits [22]. Despite significant progress, uncertainties remain in the evidence base regarding the sustainability and long‐term effectiveness of different strategies, especially considering changes in climate conditions and their application in regions with limited resources. Continuous research and the evaluation of integrated approaches remain essential to address these uncertainties and optimize public health responses [23]. This protocol will support a series of reviews.

Methods

This protocol details the methods we expect to use for all our reviews. We will follow the Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting them [24], and PRISMA 2020 for reporting them [25].

We will exclude cross‐over trials as they are not relevant to the review question.

We will not exclude studies based on year or language of publication.

Types of participants

We will include studies that include people of all ages and genders living in dengue‐endemic regions such as Africa, the Americas, South‐East Asia, the Western Pacific and Eastern Mediterranean, including tropical and subtropical areas where Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are prevalent [5]. We will include people living in a variety of demographic compositions, including both urban and rural areas with different socioeconomic levels.

Types of interventions

We will include studies on vector‐control strategies using biological, chemical, and environmental methods or any intervention that combines two or more methods (see Description of the intervention and how it might work).

We will include studies focused on vector‐control strategies administered for at least three consecutive months, as we consider this period to be the minimum required to demonstrate a sustained impact on the vector population or impact on dengue transmission, or both [27].

We will not include vector‐control strategies focusing on Wolbachia‐carrying mosquitoes, as this is addressed in another Cochrane review [28].

This protocol describes the approach for individual reviews of our four intervention strategies of interest (biological, chemical, environmental, and combined). Outcome measures

We will not exclude studies that report only one of our critical or important outcome measures. We will only exclude studies that do not report on any outcome of interest for this review, provided there is evidence to support this, such as contact with the study authors, or access to the original protocol or study register. When studies report multiple time points for each outcome, we will include the longest follow‐up period in each case.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

Two review authors (EQ, JPR) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the identified studies and discard those that do not meet the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, two review authors (EQ, JPR) will independently assess the full text of the remaining articles using predefined criteria. We will resolve any disagreements through discussion, and if no consensus is reached, we will consult a third review author (LIG). If the eligibility of a study is unclear, we will contact the study authors for further information. We will record all reasons for exclusion of full‐text‐evaluated studies in a Characteristics of excluded studies table, and provide a list of all references to excluded studies. We will summarize the selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram. We will use Covidence software for study selection [42].

Data extraction and management

Two review authors (EQ, JPR) will independently extract data from the included studies. Any disagreements between the authors will be resolved by discussion, with input from a third review author (LIG) if needed. We will develop a specific form for data extraction and evaluation, tailored to the needs of each review, which we will pilot ahead of time in one included study following the guidelines of the Cochrane Handbook. For data analysis and storage, we will use Review Manager (RevMan) [43].

For all eligible studies, we will extract general data, such as author name, title, year of publication, country, geographical location (urban or rural area), number of units included, adjustment for clustering effects, and duration of follow‐up. We will also extract participant data such as the total number of participants, recruitment method, dropout or loss rate during follow‐up, age, sex distribution, and socioeconomic status.

With respect to the intervention, we will extract data on the type of vector control applied, the life cycle stage of the mosquito at which the intervention is aimed (egg, larva, or adult), the number and frequency of applications, field monitoring strategies, the presence of co‐interventions, and the comparison group used. We will obtain data on our critical and important outcomes by group for each study.

We will report these data in the Characteristics of the included studies table and in a summary table in the main text.

Measures of treatment effect

We will report the effect of the intervention within the studies using the risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for dichotomous outcomes (e.g. medical‐attended events (i.e. issues that prompt people to seek advice or treatment from a healthcare professional)). We will use the mean difference (MD) with 95% CI for continuous outcomes (e.g. dengue‐incidence rate). When data from studies that employed different instruments to assess the same outcome are combined, we will use standardized mean differences (SMDs) along with 95% CIs. We will input data presented as a scale with a uniform direction of effect and multiply the SMD by a standard deviation that accurately reflects the combined studies, such as the standard deviation (SD) derived from a widely recognized scale used by several of the studies included in the analysis that formed the basis of the result. If studies report continuous outcomes as rates (e.g. Breteau index as the number of positive containers per 100 houses) we will use rate ratios.

Unit of analysis issues

When a single trial has more than one treatment arm, we will only consider the arms pertinent to the review topic. If a study includes two comparisons, we will adhere to the recommendations in section 6.2 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions to prevent double counting [47]. Our preferred approach will be to combine groups to create a single pairwise comparison.

In cRCTs, we will treat the cluster as the unit of analysis rather than the individual participants to prevent unit‐of‐analysis errors, as described in section 23.1.1 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [45]. If the effect measure for the cluster is not determined by appropriate methods in the included studies, we will adjust for design effects, considering the clustering effect by using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The design effect is approximately: 1+ (M‐1) x ICC, where M is the average cluster size and ICC is the intracluster correlation coefficient. We will obtain estimates of ICCs by contacting study authors, or by imputing ICC values, using either estimates from other included studies that report ICCs or external estimates from empirical research [45].

We will use mixed‐effects models or regressions with robust estimates, which take into account the variability between clusters.

Dealing with missing data

Where data are missing, we will contact the study authors to request information. We will assess key participant numbers and analysis populations (e.g. intention‐to‐treat, as‐treated, and per‐protocol) when evaluating risk of bias. To accomplish this, we will examine attrition rates, such as dropouts, losses to follow‐up, and withdrawals, and we will critically assess concerns related to missing data and the application of imputation techniques, like last observation carried forward. For our main analyses, we will perform available‐case analyses, taking these matters into account when evaluating the risk of bias and the reliability of the evidence.

If we cannot acquire continuous outcome data from the investigators or study sponsors, we will substitute the mean with the median (treating the median as the mean) and derive the standard deviation from the standard error, interquartile range, or P values, following the methods outlined in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews Interventions [47]

Reporting bias assessment

To detect potential reporting bias, we will use graphical representations such as funnel plots when appropriate, that is, when at least 10 studies are included in a meta‐analysis. Several explanations may account for funnel plot asymmetry. Therefore, we will interpret the results carefully. We will not perform statistical analysis for funnel plot asymmetry, as these tests typically have low power, see Chapter 13 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [ 48]

Synthesis methods

We will conduct all syntheses using RevMan [43]. For data synthesis, we will primarily employ a random‐effects model [49]. We will interpret the results by considering the full distribution of effects and presenting confidence intervals. Statistical analyses will follow the methodological recommendations from Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [49].

We will only perform a meta‐analysis if we determine that there is sufficient similarity among participants, interventions, comparisons, and outcomes to provide a meaningful summary. In the primary analysis, we will include all studies regardless of their risk of bias. If a meta‐analysis is not feasible, findings will be presented through narrative synthesis, adhering to the Synthesis Without Meta‐Analysis guidelines instead of a combined statistical synthesis, as outlined in Chapter 12 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [50].

Equity‐related assessment

We will assess if any of the PROGRESS‐Plus criteria were considered in the analysis of the included studies [51], such as country‐income classification or place of residence. See Supplementary material 3 for additional details.

Dengue primarily affects communities located in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where socioeconomic conditions such as poverty, limited access to public health services, and poor socio‐environmental conditions increase exposure to vector mosquitoes [52].

The dynamics of dengue transmission and other arthropod‐borne viruses (arboviruses) often vary depending on the place of residence. Urban areas typically have higher population densities and infrastructure that facilitate mosquito proliferation, thereby increasing incidence of disease. Rural areas may exhibit different dynamics: while they generally have lower population densities, factors such as limited access to healthcare, varying levels of public health education and inadequate infrastructure for implementing control strategies, can significantly influence disease incidence [4].

If we have sufficient data, we will highlight the differences associated with these criteria in our summary of findings table.

Certainty of the evidence assessment

To assess the certainty of the evidence for each outcome of interest, we will apply the GRADE approach [53], which considers various factors that may influence confidence in the results. These factors include the risk of bias, the consistency of observed effects, the precision of estimates, the applicability of findings to the target population, and the potential for publication bias.

For evidence derived from non‐randomized studies of interventions (NRSIs) and observational studies, we will also consider factors that may increase certainty, including large magnitude of effect, dose‐response gradient, and effect of plausible residual confounding. In line with GRADE guidance, we will initially rate the certainty of evidence from non‐randomized studies at a high level, similar to randomized trials, given that risk of bias will be assessed using the ROBINS‐I tool. The overall risk of bias judgement derived from this assessment will guide any decisions to downgrade the certainty of the evidence.

Two reviewers (EQ, JPR) will independently conduct this assessment. If any discrepancies arise, they will be resolved through discussion, and if disagreements persist, a third reviewer (LIG) will be consulted to reach a consensus.

We will generate summary of findings (SoF) tables to summarize the most relevant outcomes for decision‐making. These tables will include participant‐centered outcomes (dengue‐incidence rate, severe cases of dengue, all‐cause mortality, and adverse events) and two key entomological outcomes (adult mosquito density and BI) for the long‐term follow‐up.

As this work will be conducted as a series of independent reviews (biological, chemical, environmental, and combined vector‐control strategies), we will generate separate SoF tables within each review for the following comparisons:

vector‐control strategies targeting aquatic stages compared with non‐use of any vector‐control strategies;vector‐control strategies targeting adult stages compared with non‐use of any vector‐control strategies; andcombined vector‐control strategies compared with non‐use of any vector‐control strategies.

Consumer involvement

We will not involve consumers in the development of this protocol or in the planned reviews because of limited resources. Additionally, we will review reports and contact organizations involved in the development and implementation of vector‐control strategies (see Searching other resources) to ensure that the evaluated results are relevant to public health decision‐making and dengue‐prevention strategies.
