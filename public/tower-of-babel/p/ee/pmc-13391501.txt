Metabolic characterization of the tumor microenvironment orchestrates therapeutic strategies and clinical outcomes in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Rong Tang, Yangyi Li, Cong Zhou, Chunbin Zhu, Chen Chen, Liquan Jin, Yueyue Chen, Yingna Liao, Yuan Liu, Qiong Du, Yubin Lei, Zijian Wu, Jin Xu, Wei Wang, Xiaoyu Yin, Chenghao Shao, Si Shi, Xianjun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic reprogramming and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) are hallmark features driving pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) progression. Despite the therapeutic potential of targeting immunometabolism, effective strategies remain scarce in clinical practice, likely due to cell-specific metabolic heterogeneity within PDAC TME. Here, we show integration of three algorithms to estimate metabolic fluxomes and pathways using scRNA-seq data, generating a comprehensive cell type-specific metabolic atlas. Leveraging 460 PDAC samples, we establish a TME-metabolism subtyping system, classifying PDAC into three subtypes (TMS1-3) with distinct immune-metabolic profiles and clinical outcomes. TMS1, characterized by low immune infiltrates, is susceptible to ferroptosis inducers. TMS2, enriched in macrophages, responds to chemoimmunotherapy with inhibition of glutamine synthetase. TMS3, characterized by matrix remodeling, responds to glycolysis inhibitors and albumin-paclitaxel. Finally, we develop a computational classifier for subtype discrimination. Together, this study delineates the metabolic heterogeneity of the PDAC TME and proposes a classification system that suggests promising therapeutic targets.Subject terms: Pancreatic cancer, Cancer microenvironment

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the deadliest malignancies, with a five-year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 10%1,2, primarily due to the scarcity of actionable therapeutic targets3. Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of PDAC, facilitating nutrient acquisition and energy production to support rapid proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance4–6. Among the approximately 20,000 protein-coding genes in humans, over 10% are involved in metabolism7. Consequently, targeting PDAC’s metabolic vulnerabilities represents a promising avenue for therapeutic development.

However, clinical trials targeting tumor metabolism have largely failed to demonstrate significant survival benefits8. A critical factor underlying this limitation is the intricate metabolic similarities and heterogeneity among cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME)9–11. For example, while glycolysis inhibition in tumor cells markedly reduces proliferation and metastasis12, it is also essential for the differentiation of T effector cells13,14. Additionally, tumor progression in PDAC heavily depends on glutaminolysis15, with inhibitors targeting glutamine transporters or enzymes showing promising anti-tumor effects in preclinical models16,17. Nevertheless, while some studies suggested that glutamine inhibition preserves T cell function18, others emphasized the critical role of glutamine in dendritic cell antigen presentation19. Notably, the TME varies significantly across cancer types and even among patients with the same cancer type20, suggesting that tumors with TMEs dominated by distinct cell types may respond differently to metabolic interventions.

In this work, to delineate cell type-specific metabolic heterogeneity in PDAC, we integrated multiple algorithms to estimate cell type-specific fluxomes and metabolic pathway activities using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, creating a comprehensive cell type-wise metabolic atlas. Additionally, we classified 460 PDAC samples into three subtypes (designated as TMS1–3) based on their metabolic and TME features, each associated with distinct clinical outcomes and therapeutic strategies. Collectively, our work not only provides a referable cell type-specific metabolic atlas but also establishes a PDAC classifier with the potential to guide therapeutic decision-making in clinical practice.

Results

Metabolic heterogeneity in the TME of PDAC

Although bulk-level analyses have highlighted stringent metabolic reprogramming in PDAC compared to the normal pancreas21, the metabolic heterogeneity across distinct cell types in PDAC remains underexplored. To investigate the metabolic characteristics of tumor-infiltrating cells within the TME, we integrated scRNA-seq data from 81 pancreatic tissues, including 67 PDAC samples and 14 adjacent normal pancreas tissues, encompassing 109,986 cells across 16 major cell types, of which pancreatic ductal cells (PDCs), myeloid-lineage cells, and stromal cells exhibited higher overall metabolic scores (Fig. 1A–C). To examine specific metabolic features for each cell type in detail, we applied three scRNA-seq-compatible algorithms: scMetabolism (for metabolic pathway activity), scFEA (for metabolic flux), and COMPASS (for the probability of metabolic reactions), collectively generating 7120 metabolic features (Fig. 1D). Unsupervised clustering based on these features revealed that proximal cell linkages shared similar metabolic patterns (Fig. 1E). Using a Random Forest model, we identified the metabolic features that are most important for distinguishing each cell type (Fig. 1F). For example, we demonstrated that cycling cells, characterized by the overexpression of proliferative genes, exhibit increased glycolytic flux, consistent with the high energy demands of rapid proliferation. In macrophages, we observed elevated glucose utilization, which aligns with previous studies identifying macrophages as the primary consumers of glucose within the TME22,23. For fibroblasts, which are abundantly enriched in PDAC, the metabolic flux from spermine to β-alanine is a distinctive feature. Although the significance of this metabolic dependency in PDAC-associated fibroblasts has not been fully elucidated, studies have shown that β-alanine serves as a precursor of carnosine, a molecule that may scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells and has the potential to protect fibroblasts from oxidative stress-induced damage24. The metabolic features associated with each cell type are detailed in a spreadsheet (Supplementary Data 1).

Leveraging the MEBOCOST algorithm25, we computed the metabolic communications between cell types. Overall, metabolic communications among cell types were more frequent in the tumor compared to the adjacent pancreas, with fibroblast-to-neutrophil communication being the most significant in PDAC (Fig. 1G, H). A recent study revealed that tumor-derived PGE2 programs cDC1 dysfunction, impairing the intratumoral orchestration of anti-cancer T cell responses26. In our analysis, T and NK cells, identified as the primary receivers of metabolites in the TME (Fig. S1A), were found to receive PGE2 from 14 cell types via PTGER4 (Fig. 1I). This finding suggests that PGE2 in the TME may be directly sensed by T and NK cells, potentially influencing their anti-tumor immune functions. Further in vitro experiments showed that PGE2 treatment impaired the tumor-killing ability of both T cells and NK cells, whereas Grapiprant, a PTGER4 antagonist, restored their cytotoxicity (Fig. S1B). Additionally, we observed that glutamine was another key metabolite received by T and NK cells from other cell types, supporting the notion that these cells are important consumers of glutamine in the TME (Fig. S1C, D). Results from the COMPASS analysis further corroborated the highly upregulated signals related to glutamine exchange (Fig. S1E). Given that T and NK cells are the major immune cells directly responsible for tumor cell clearance, the design of metabolic inhibitors that may impair their function requires careful consideration. Overall, we created a referable spreadsheet documenting the metabolic communications among cell types in PDAC (Supplementary Data 2).

To investigate metabolic discrepancies among different cell types during PDAC tumorigenesis, we compared the metabolic fluxes of each cell type between adjacent pancreatic and PDAC tissues, revealing that 80.5% of cell-type-specific metabolic fluxes and pathways were deregulated (Figs. 1J and S1F, G). Notably, acinar cells, which maintain the exocrine function of the normal pancreas, exhibited significantly increased lactate efflux and AMP-to-adenosine transitions. These metabolic changes have been reported as mechanisms that are unfavorable for anti-tumor immunity27,28. In contrast, B cells displayed a metabolically inactive phenotype in PDAC (Fig. S1G). Many metabolic pathways, including nucleotide, fatty acid, and central carbon metabolism, were downregulated, suggesting that the metabolic functions of tumor-infiltrating B cells may be restricted (Fig. S1H). Overall, the differential metabolic fluxes and pathways between PDAC and adjacent pancreas at the cell-type level are documented in a referable spreadsheet (Supplementary Data 3).

Construct a classification system for PDAC based on metabolic and TME features

From an integrated perspective, PDACs may exhibit heterogeneous metabolic phenotypes due to variations in cell proportions within the TME. To comprehensively profile the metabolic and TME characteristics of PDACs and evaluate their impact on clinical outcomes and therapeutic strategies, we established a PDAC cohort comprising 460 resected samples with at least transcriptomic data (Fig. 2A). Using previously reported TME gene sets, which include four sections comprising a total of 29 knowledge-based functional gene expression signatures that represent the immune, stromal, and other cellular populations of the tumor20, we identified extensive correlations between metabolic pathways and the TME (Fig. 2B). For example, gene signatures related to purine and pyrimidine synthesis showed a positive correlation with malignant cell properties but were negatively associated with anti-tumor immunity. A detailed correlational network is available in a referable spreadsheet (Supplementary Data 4). Using 232 screened differential metabolic features and 29 TME signatures, we classified PDAC samples into three subtypes through consensus clustering, termed TME-metabolism subtypes (TMS) (Fig. 2C). The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the flattening of the CDF curve and the distinct block patterns observed in the consensus heatmap, indicating stable and well-defined clusters (S2A, B). The TMS1, TMS2, and TMS3 subtypes comprised 32.2%, 25.2%, and 42.6% of PDAC cases, respectively. Compared to the adjacent pancreas, each subtype displayed both shared and unique upregulated genes, with 36.5% to 62% of the enriched pathways being related to metabolism or the TME (Fig. S2C–E). Further analysis revealed that more metabolic fluxes were upregulated rather than downregulated in PDAC compared to the adjacent pancreas. The downregulated fluxes were subtype-independent and shared common pathways, such as “β-alanine -> acetyl-CoA”, a characteristic feature of acinar cells identified previously (Fig. S2F, G). The differential genes and metabolic fluxes between PDACs and adjacent pancreas are documented in a referable spreadsheet (Supplementary Data 5 and 6).

Previous studies have proposed several molecular subtypes for PDAC29–31. By analyzing the gene signatures of each subtype within these systems, we found that both TMS1 and TMS3 exhibited higher signature scores for the “Classical” subtype in Collisson’s system, which reflects more integrated epithelial function and overlaps significantly with the “Progenitor” subtype in Bailey’s system (Figs. 2D and S3A-B). However, compared to TMS1, TMS3 displayed higher scores for “QM-PDA” in Collisson’s system and “Squamous” in Bailey’s system, both indicative of mesenchymal activation. GO analysis further supported this observation, showing that TMS3 was enriched for genes related to extracellular structure and matrix organization compared to TMS1 (Fig. S3C). We found that TMS2 most faithfully captured the “Immunologic” subtype characteristics first proposed in Bailey’s system, which highlights therapeutic opportunities with immune modulators (Fig. 2D). From a metabolic perspective, Karasinska et al. classified PDACs into four subtypes based on distinct glycolytic-cholesterol activity. Within the TMS system, TMS3 demonstrated the highest glycolytic-to-cholesterol ratio and the least metabolically quiescent phenotype, suggesting a high energy demand for this subtype (Fig. S3D). Additionally, we evaluated the mutation status of four PDAC driver genes in each subtype by calling variants from RNA-seq data using GATK. TMS1 exhibited relatively stable mutation rates across all four genes. In contrast, TMS2 showed a lower frequency of KRAS mutations, while TMS3 was characterized by a higher incidence of co-occurring KRAS and TP53 mutations (Fig. S3E–F).

The TMS classification is associated with the prognosis of PDAC patients

Through follow-up on the OS of 460 PDAC patients, we found that the TMS1 and TMS3 subtypes were associated with worse prognosis compared to TMS2 (Fig. 2E). Univariate COX regression analysis identified N stage, vessel invasion, advanced age, higher stage, preoperative CA125 and CEA levels, and postoperative CA199, CA125, and CEA levels as unfavorable factors for OS. In contrast, TMS2 emerged as a protective factor associated with prolonged OS. Further multivariate COX regression analysis confirmed TMS2 as a protective factor for prolonged OS in PDAC, underscoring its independent prognostic value (Fig. 2F). To validate this finding, we conducted a meta-analysis across six independent PDAC cohorts, which further confirmed the positive association between the TMS2 signature and improved prognosis in PDAC patients (Fig. 2G).

Targeting ferroptosis in TMS1 PDAC synergistically with paclitaxel to enhance therapeutic efficacy

From the perspective of TME, TMS1 manifested with low abundance of both immune and fibroblast cells (Fig. 3A). This features TMS1 subtype as a classic dessert “cold” tumor32. From the perspective of metabolism, integrated transcriptome-wise metabolic features and metabolome data, we found many disturbed metabolic signatures were associated with antioxidant defenses and fatty acids (Fig. 3B), which are reminiscent of a recently reported cancer phenotype benefited from ferroptosis inducer33. On one hand, enhanced signatures of antioxidant defenses like glutathione synthesis and elevated GSH/GSSG inhibit ferroptosis by increasing the clearance of lipid peroxidation, on the other hand, activated fatty acid metabolism, including enriched PUFAs and enhanced membrane lipids, could become the building blocks for ferroptosis execution (Fig. 3C–F).

To validate the susceptibility of TMS1 tumor cells to ferroptosis inducer, we generated seven patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from human PDAC samples. TMS1 organoids exhibited a significantly increased GSH:GSSG ratio, as well as elevated MDA fold changes and increased cell death following treatment with RSL-3, a ferroptosis inducer that inhibits glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). These findings suggest a heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis in TMS1 PDACs (Figs. 3G and S4A). Deregulated glutathione metabolism is frequently associated with drug resistance34, and inducing ferroptosis has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance chemotherapeutic responses35,36. Using the psSubpathway tool to identify subtype-specific subpathways37, we observed elevated CYP450-dependent and -independent drug metabolism in the TMS1 subtype (Fig. S4B), which has been previously linked to chemotherapeutic responses in PDAC38,39. The upregulation of drug metabolism activity in TMS1 PDAC was verified using the MetaFlux algorithm40 (Fig. S4C). These observations imply that TMS1 may exhibit distinct chemotherapeutic responses compared to other subtypes. To further explore this, we used oncoPredict41 to predict drug sensitivity for five standard chemotherapeutic agents and found that TMS1 showed higher resistance to gemcitabine and paclitaxel (Fig. 3H). To argue against the effect of non-tumor component transcripts on drug sensitivity evaluation, a common issue in bulk analysis, we performed snRNA-seq, which preferentially captures tumor cells compared to conventional scRNA-seq42. In total, we captured 101,258 cellular nuclei from seven frozen PDAC samples, of which 48,402 originated from TMS1 PDACs (Fig. S4D–F). Drug sensitivity predictions confirmed that TMS1-derived PDCs are more resistant to paclitaxel (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these bulk and single-cell level analyses suggest that TMS1 is resistant to at least paclitaxel treatment.

Consistently, GPX4 was stringently upregulated in TMS1 PDAC cells (Fig. S4G). To further explore whether GPX4 is related to paclitaxel resistance, we aimed to detect GPX4 protein levels in chemotherapy-resistant PDAC cell lines. Using a pulsatile approach, we established five drug-resistant cell strains for common therapeutic regimens in two PDAC cell lines, with IC50 values > 10-fold higher than their parental cells for each drug (Fig. 3J). Notably, paclitaxel-resistant PDAC cells exhibited the highest GPX4 protein levels compared to other strains (Fig. 3K), and GPX4 protein was elevated in PDACs that underwent neoadjuvant AG (a regimen containing paclitaxel) compared to samples with upfront resection (Fig. 3L). Together, these results indicate that GPX4 inhibition may enhance the response to paclitaxel in PDAC treatment. To validate this hypothesis, we isolated patient-derived primary PDAC cells (PDPPCs) from 22 resected PDAC samples and performed synchronous RNA-seq on the whole tissues (Fig. 3M). Within 10 passages of in vitro culture, we performed drug sensitivity tests using RSL-3 and/or paclitaxel on these PDPPCs. Synergistic analysis revealed that the combination of RSL-3 and paclitaxel showed a higher synergy score in TMS1 PDACs (Figs. 3N and S4H). Additionally, the combination of RSL-3 and paclitaxel demonstrated a lower IC50 in TMS1 PDAC organoids (Fig. 3O). In vivo studies using patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models further demonstrated that TMS1 PDACs were more sensitive to the combination therapy, as shown by their slower tumor progression (Fig. S4I). Since RSL-3 is not clinically approved, we also evaluated the combination of paclitaxel with sorafenib, which is an FDA-approved regimen known to induce ferroptosis43,44. This combination showed a higher synergy score in TMS1 PDAC organoids compared to non-TMS1 organoids, supporting the notion that targeting ferroptosis may enhance the efficacy of paclitaxel in future clinical applications (Fig. S4J–K).

In real-world clinical practice, we studied a PDAC case who achieved only stable disease (SD) after neoadjuvant AG (Fig. 3P) but still received radical resection. This patient experienced a rapid relapse within six months post-operation, despite receiving post-operative adjuvant AG, suggesting resistance to AG prior to surgery. Drug sensitivity analysis for the organoids derived from this patient revealed a low response to single paclitaxel treatment but susceptibility to the combination of RSL-3 and paclitaxel (Fig. 3Q). Taken together, given that AG is one of the recommended chemotherapeutic regimens for PDAC, the combined application of a ferroptosis inducer may represent a promising strategy for TMS1 PDACs.

Targeting glutamine de novo synthesis in macrophages synergistically promoted chemoimmunotherapy in TMS2 PDAC

From a microenvironmental perspective, both transcriptomic deconvolutional estimation and spatial proteome quantification (IMC) revealed that TMS2 subtypes exhibited an enrichment of M2-polarized macrophages (Figs. 4A, B and S5A). Although higher infiltration of CD8+ T cells and increased TCR diversity (particularly among small and rare clonotypes) were observed in the TMS2 subtype (Figs. 4A and S5B, C), exhaustion markers such as PD-1 were simultaneously elevated compared to non-TMS2 PDACs (Fig. S5D–F). These findings suggest that TMS2 represents an immunoregulatory phenotype with the potential to benefit from immunotherapy, as supported by a higher predicted immunotherapeutic score (Fig. S5G). In terms of metabolism, TMS2 demonstrated enhanced de novo synthesis of glutamine from glutamate (Fig. 4C). Notably, the flux from glutamate to glutamine was highest in macrophages compared to other cell types (Fig. S5H), as shown by spatial transcriptomics (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, this flux was most upregulated in tumor-infiltrated macrophages relative to macrophages in the adjacent pancreas (Fig. 4E). GLUL, the gene encoding glutamine synthetase (GS)45, was significantly upregulated in TMS2 subtypes and highly expressed in macrophages (Fig. S5I–J).

The TME is characterized by a paradoxical state of glutamine addiction and shortage46,47, and TMS2 PDAC tissues exhibited lower abundance of glutamine compared to the non-TMS2 subtype (Fig. S6A). Furthermore, in contrast to tumor and T/NK cells, macrophages demonstrated low levels of glutamine influx, highlighting the potential significance of de novo glutamine synthesis over extracellular uptake, particularly under conditions of glutamine deprivation (Fig. S6B). Using isotopic tracing assays with ¹³C₅;¹⁵N-labeled glutamate, we observed that GLUL inhibition by MSO increased the proportion of labeled metabolites in the TCA cycle while reducing the proportion of labeled nucleotides (Fig. 4F, G). Given the role of TCA cycle-derived metabolites in chromatin accessibility remodeling48–50, we performed ATAC-seq and RNA-seq analyses on GLUL-WT and GLUL-KO THP-1 cells (Fig. S6C). Integration of ATAC-seq peaks with RNA-seq data identified 215 overlapping genes that were upregulated in GLUL-KO THP-1 cells (Fig. 4H). Enrichment analysis revealed that GLUL knockout in THP-1 cells enhanced a pro-inflammatory phenotype, with pathways such as “TNF signaling pathway,” “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” and “NF-kappa B signaling” significantly enriched (Fig. 4H). In contrast, the downregulated genes in GLUL-KO THP-1 cells were primarily involved in cell cycle regulation, such as MKI67, highlighting the critical role of de novo glutamine synthesis in supporting macrophage proliferation (Fig. S6D, E). The pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes of macrophages are often associated with M1/M2 polarization. Under glutamine-deprived conditions, ATAC-seq results revealed that M2-associated genes, such as HAVCR2 (Tim-3), exhibited increased promoter activity, whereas M1-associated genes, such as CCL5, showed decreased promoter activity (Fig. 4I). Since GLUL-KO significantly increased the abundance of α-KG in THP-1 cells under glutamine deprivation conditions (Fig. S6F), which plays a critical regulatory role in TET enzyme activity and downregulates genomic DNA methylation levels, we performed WGBS on GLUL-WT and -KO THP-1 cells. Unexpectedly, the results revealed that the methylation level of these pro- and anti-inflammatory genes did not change significantly after knockout of GLUL (Fig. S6G, H). However, GLUL loss significantly reduced the H3K27Me3 abundance (Fig. S6I), which is directly affected by α-KG and has been reported to correlate with inflammatory regulation51,52. The H3K27me3 demethylase inhibitor GSK-J4 reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory genes and enhanced the expression of anti-inflammatory genes, as identified by the intersection of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data in our previous study. In contrast, supplementation with α-KG produced the opposite effect (Fig. S6J).

Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of GLUL with MSO in THP-1 cells resulted in reduced expression of M2 markers, while increasing the expression of CD86 and MHC-II, suggesting that GLUL inhibition promotes M1 polarization under glutamine deprivation (Fig. 4J). However, GLUL loss did not significantly affect phagocytic activity, implying that inflammatory regulation may play a more critical role in mediating the effect of GLUL loss on anti-tumor activity (Fig. S6K). Additionally, GLUL loss was associated with a decreased SPP1:CXCL9/10 ratio, which has recently been reported as an unfavorable factor for anti-tumor immunity (Figs. 4K and S6L). To further investigate whether GLUL in macrophages influences anti-tumor immune responses, we leveraged a co-culture system comprising CD8⁺ T cells derived from OT-1 mice, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), and OVA⁺ KPC-0116 cells. In this system, MSO-treated BMDMs were co-cultured with OT-1 CD8⁺ T cells and OVA⁺ PDAC cells (Fig. S7A). Flow cytometry analysis showed that MSO treatment elevated the expression of IFN-γ, Ki67, and Granzyme B in CD8⁺ T cells within the co-culture system under glutamine deprivation (Fig. S7B, C). These in vitro findings underscore the potential impact of GLUL inhibition in macrophages on anti-tumor immune response.

Single-agent immunotherapy has demonstrated limited efficacy in PDAC53. In contrast, chemoimmunotherapy has shown favorable safety profiles in clinical investigations and holds promise for benefiting at least a subset of PDAC patients54,55. Notably, we found chemotherapy-treated PDACs exhibit increased TMS2 subtype enrichment and reduced glutamine abundance, indicative of a more severe glutamine-deprived TME (Figs. 4L and S7D). This condition likely enhances reliance on de novo glutamine synthesis via GLUL, suggesting that targeting GLUL could represent an effective therapeutic strategy for PDAC patients undergoing chemoimmunotherapy. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated whether GLUL inhibition enhances the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy. In murine models, tumor volumes in the group treated with chemoimmunotherapy combined with MSO (referred to as CB) were significantly smaller compared to those treated with chemoimmunotherapy alone (referred to as CI) (Figs. 4M and S7E). Meanwhile, macrophage depletion mitigated the additional efficacy of MSO (Fig. S7F, G), supporting the hypothesis that GLUL primarily acts on macrophages to undermine the anti-tumor immune response. Using an orthotopic model, we further demonstrated that the triple-combination therapy (AG + MSO + α-PD1) markedly enhanced tumor apoptotic cell death, leading to prolonged survival compared with single or dual treatments (Fig. S7H, I). These findings bridge the gap between metabolic reprogramming and transcriptomic phenotypes (Fig. S7J), highlighting GLUL inhibition in macrophages as a promising strategy to augment chemoimmunotherapy in PDAC.

To investigate changes in immune cell composition following MSO treatment during chemoimmunotherapy, we performed cytometry by time-of-flight mass spectrometry (CyTOF) on harvested tumors (Fig. S8A–C). The analysis revealed a significant increase in the percentage of CD8⁺ T cells and their granzyme B expression in the CB group (Fig. 4N). Similarly, macrophages in the CB group exhibited elevated CD86/CD206 ratios and enhanced MHC-II expression (Fig. 4O). To further validate these findings, we employed CD34⁺ humanized mice (huCD34), which harbor a reconstituted human immune system (Figs. 4P and S8D). PDX of PDAC were transplanted into these huCD34 mice, which were then treated with either CI or CB regimens. The results showed that the addition of MSO significantly inhibited PDX growth compared to chemoimmunotherapy alone (Fig. 4Q). Moreover, in a PDAC cohort receiving combination immunotherapy, high GLUL expression was associated with worse OS (Fig. 4R).

Targeting glycolysis in combination with AG synergistically restrained the progression of TMS3 PDAC

From a metabolic perspective, TMS3 tumors exhibited significantly elevated expression of glycolysis-related enzymes and transporters (Fig. 5A). Consistently, the TMS3 subtype demonstrated the highest levels of glycolytic activity and lactate efflux (Fig. S9A, B). From the TME standpoint, TMS3 tumors were characterized by heightened matrix deposition and matrix remodeling signatures (Fig. 5B). Collagenolysis, a critical process in matrix remodeling, involves the cleavage of intact collagen (iCol) into cleaved collagen (cCol), which has been implicated in promoting PDAC progression56. A panel of collagen-cleaving matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), including MMP1, MMP2, MMP8, MMP9, MMP13, and MMP14, was found to be upregulated in the TMS3 subtype (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, we observed a positive correlation between matrix remodeling and glycolytic fluxes in PDAC tumors (Fig. 5D). Using a single-cell pipeline to eliminate interference from non-tumor components, we confirmed a strong correlation between collagen-cleaving MMPs and glycolytic signatures in PDCs (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these findings identify TMS3 as a subtype characterized by excessive glycolysis and matrix remodeling.

Although the links between ECM deposition and tumor glycolysis have been widely reported, most studies have focused on cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and the physiological properties of the matrix57,58. However, whether and how collagen cleavage affects tumor glycolysis remains largely unknown. The primary target of MMPs in desmoplastic tumors is Col I, the predominant ECM protein. Using an antibody capable of distinguishing cCol I from iCol I, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analysis revealed that cCol I abundance positively correlates with the expression of HK2 and MCT1 in PDAC tissues—key components for glycolysis and lactate efflux, respectively (Fig. 5F, G). Tumor cells sense cCol I through activation of DDR1, a collagen-activated receptor tyrosine kinase56. scRNA-seq data showed that DDR1 is primarily expressed in pancreatic ductal cells and is upregulated in TMS3 PDAC tissues (Fig. S9C, D). To investigate whether collagen cleavage influences glycolysis, we seeded cells on ECM deposited by PDAC-derived CAFs and measured the expression of glycolytic proteins (Fig. 5H). Cells cultured on ECM demonstrated upregulation of DDR1, phosphorylated DDR1, and several glycolytic enzymes and transporters; these effects were mitigated by an MMP inhibitor (MMPi), Ilomastat (Figs. 5I and S9E). Furthermore, isotopic tracing assays using ¹³C-labeled glucose revealed a higher proportion of glycolytic products in cells cultured on ECM, an effect that was attenuated by MMPi. Conversely, TCA cycle metabolites showed the opposite trend (Fig. 5J–K). Similar patterns were observed for extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and lactate efflux (Figs. 5L, M and S9F, G). Importantly, genetic ablation of DDR1 inhibited ECM-induced expression of glycolytic proteins (Figs. 5N and S9H), while overexpression of DDR1 restored glycolytic activity specifically under ECM-treated conditions, but not in ECM-negative or MMPi-treated settings (Fig. 5O). Furthermore, under ECM-treated conditions, p-Akt levels decreased upon DDR1 ablation and increased upon DDR1 overexpression (Fig. S9I). Inhibition of the PI3K/Akt pathway downstream of DDR1 with a classical inhibitor demonstrated that neither ECM treatment nor DDR1 overexpression could restore glycolytic activity (Fig. S9J).

In this context, combinatorial targeting of glycolysis and matrix remodeling may optimize therapeutic efficacy for TMS3 PDAC. Lonidamine, one of the few clinically approved glycolysis inhibitors, has shown limited anti-tumor activity and potential efficacy primarily in tumors with high glycolytic levels59. The target of lonidamine, HK2, is overexpressed in the TMS3 PDAC subtype (Fig. S9K) and has been reported to regulate lactate efflux60. Among first-line treatment regimens for PDAC, our previous study demonstrated the superior efficacy of AG in a subset of PDACs characterized by high stromal content61. Given the prominent glycolytic activity and matrix remodeling observed in the TMS3 subtype, we hypothesized that a combination of AG and lonidamine could enhance treatment outcomes for TMS3 patients. Initial experiments revealed that lonidamine exhibited increased anti-tumor effects in cells cultured on ECM, while its synergistic effects with AG were less pronounced in ECM-deficient conditions (Figs. 5P and S9L). Furthermore, the tumor-fibroblast co-culture model demonstrated that TMS3 PDAC cells exhibited a significantly higher apoptotic rate following treatment with AG and lonidamine compared to non-TMS3 cells (Fig. S9M, N). Notably, this therapeutic strategy also showed superior efficacy in PDX models bearing TMS3 tumors, further underscoring its potential clinical relevance (Fig. 5Q, R). Alternatively, the MMP inhibitor Ilomastat also exhibited increased sensitivity in TMS3 compared to non-TMS3 PDACs, suggesting a potential clinical application for MMP inhibitors in this subtype (Fig. S9O).

Predicting TMS subtypes using a gene-pair-based random forest model

To predict TMS subtypes in a batch-independent and single-sample-compatible manner, we employed a gene-pair-based relative expression algorithm, the practicality and effectiveness of which were demonstrated in our previous study62. Briefly, genes were cyclically singly paired (GeneA|GeneB), and the value of GeneA|GeneB is defined as 1 if the expression level of Gene A was higher than Gene B; otherwise, GeneA|GeneB is defined as 0 (Fig. 6A). To ensure the final model is compatible across various racial groups and sequencing platforms, we combined the transcriptome-based FUSCC cohort with the microarray-based, predominantly non-Asian MA cohort, creating a large training dataset (N = 1014) to fit a Random Forest classifier (Fig. 6A). Ten-fold cross-validation and cohort-split analysis demonstrated the stable performance of this model, with all AUCs exceeding 0.8 for each subtype in every sampling (Fig. 6B and S10A). To improve clinical applicability and minimize economic and time costs, we investigated whether a smaller subset of gene pairs could accurately predict TMS subtypes via qPCR, providing a more efficient approach. As a result, the top 15 most important gene pairs for TMS prediction were identified by evaluating the mean decrease in the Gini coefficient (Fig. 6C). We designed qPCR primers for the 20 genes associated with the 15 gene pairs. To ensure consistency in the relative expression of gene pairs between RNA-seq and qPCR, we calculated the PCR-RNA-seq concordance for each gene pair and retained the 10 pairs with the highest accuracy (Figs. 6C and S10B). The predictive accuracy of this simplified model was first confirmed in the TCGA cohort (Fig. S10C). To prospectively validate the accuracy of the simplified qPCR panel for predicting TMS, we collaborated with two additional centers, which provided surgically resected PDAC samples for both qPCR and RNA-seq analyses (Fig. 6D). The results demonstrated high accuracy for TMS prediction at both centers, highlighting the feasibility of using qPCR based on the top gene pairs (Fig. 6E). To streamline the TMS prediction process, we developed an R package named “Pandora” (short for “PANcreatic Ductal adenOcarcinoma-RAndom forest classifier”) for public access.

A limitation of the TMS classification system is that some optimal treatment regimens for each TMS subtype cannot be immediately applied in clinical practice. Nevertheless, we sought to investigate whether the TMS system could guide clinical decision-making within the current clinical framework, particularly for advanced PDAC patients who are no longer candidates for upfront surgery. To address this, we prospectively established a PDAC cohort of 148 advanced-stage patients. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) was performed for simultaneous pathological diagnosis and RNA-seq sampling (Fig. 6F). TMS1 and TMS3 accounted for the majority of advanced PDAC cases (90.1%) (Fig. S10D). Consistent with our previous findings, TMS1 is primarily predicted to be resistant to AG treatment. In contrast, TMS3, characterized by matrix deposition and remodeling, is expected to respond favorably to AG therapy, as we have reported previously in ref. 61. Hence, we hypothesized that TMS3 patients may derive greater benefit from AG treatment. The results showed that 21.4% of TMS3 patients who received AG therapy successfully underwent surgical resection, compared to only 7% of non-TMS3 PDAC patients (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, even among patients who did not achieve surgical transformation (Fig. 6H), progression-free survival (PFS) was significantly longer for TMS3 PDACs than for non-TMS3 PDACs (Fig. 6I). For TMS2 PDACs, characterized by relatively enriched immune infiltrations, GS inhibitors and chemoimmunotherapy are expected to provide therapeutic benefit. Although no GS inhibitors have been approved for clinical use, these patients should be prioritized for enrollment in clinical trials involving chemoimmunotherapy. To evaluate the potential benefits of chemoimmunotherapy in TMS2 PDACs, we generated 22 patient-derived tissue fragments (PDTFs), an ex vivo platform to investigate responses to PD-1 blockade in cancer. During treatment, we measured cytokines associated with immune responses in PDTF supernatants and classified samples into responders and non-responders based on cytokine fold changes (Fig. 6J). The results revealed that a higher proportion of TMS2 samples demonstrated the potential to respond to a combination of AG and PD-1 inhibitors (Fig. 6K). Together, the TMS system shows preliminary promise in clinical practice for guiding the selection of therapeutic strategies at the current stage.

Discussion

PDAC is characterized by profound metabolic reprogramming and an immunosuppressive TME, with its intricate interplay significantly shaping disease progression and clinical outcomes. However, the metabolic heterogeneity among distinct cell types within the TME, particularly its impact on responses to immune- and metabolism-targeted therapies, remains poorly understood. In this study, we explored the metabolic characteristics of various cell types within the TME and developed a comprehensive cell type-specific metabolic atlas for PDAC. Additionally, we proposed a classification system, identifying three distinct PDAC TMS subtypes, each defined by unique metabolic and TME characteristics.

As widely reported in the literature, PDAC exhibits significant metabolic reprogramming. Beyond the commonly observed Warburg effect and glutamine addiction shared by many tumors, Badgley et al. demonstrated that cysteine depletion inhibits PDAC progression by inducing ferroptosis in pancreatic tumors63. Moreover, we and others have shown that activated glutathione metabolism, a key internal resistor against ferroptosis, contributes to chemoresistance in PDAC. In this study, we identified TMS1 PDACs as characterized by enhanced glutathione metabolism and enriched PUFAs, making them suitable targets for ferroptosis-inducing therapies. Besides, this subtype exhibited low infiltration of effector T cells. Given that ferroptosis is increasingly recognized as an immunogenic form of cell death64, ferroptosis inducers are expected to enhance T cell infiltration and stimulate anti-tumor immune responses in TMS1 PDACs. Emerging evidence has also highlighted the interplay between immune cell metabolism and tumor progression. For example, lactate originating from both the TME and T cell-intrinsic production has been shown to impair the anti-tumor functions of T cells65–70. Based on the cell-type-wise metabolite atlas we created for PDAC, we showed T cells exhibit the highest levels of lactate abundance (Supplementary Data 7). This finding supports the rationale and significance of studies investigating lactate and lactylation in T cells, particularly given the intracellular accumulation of lactate. Additionally, for immune-enriched TMS2 PDACs, we observed high levels of glutamine de novo synthesis in macrophages, which promotes M2 polarization and suppresses anti-tumor immunity. PDAC is well-known for its desmoplastic nature, with the highest stromal proportion among common solid tumors71. This aligns with the TMS3 subtype, which we identified as having upregulated matrix-related signals, constituting more than 40% of all PDACs. Metabolically, TMS3 PDAC is characterized by hyperactivated glycolysis and lactate efflux. Numerous studies have reported mechanisms through which CAFs and matrix remodeling promote tumor glycolysis. Notably, CAFs themselves are highly glycolytic, partially contributing to the high SUVmax observed in PET-CT, as we recently reported. Here, we uncovered a mechanism whereby tumor cells sense cleaved collagen in the ECM to upregulate their glycolytic activity, further explaining the link between matrix remodeling and enhanced glycolysis in TMS3. Importantly, AG regimens have shown robust anti-tumor efficacy in stroma-enriched tumors due to their anti-stromal effects72. Building on this knowledge and findings, we propose that combining AG regimens with glycolysis inhibitors could optimize treatment strategies for TMS3 PDACs. Furthermore, given that MMP-mediated collagen cleavage is essential for the matrix’s effect on tumor cell survival, potent MMP inhibitors represent a promising alternative therapeutic strategy for TMS3 PDACs. Characteristics and potential therapeutic strategies for TMS subtypes

PDAC subtypes have been reported to co-exist within a single tumor sample73. Previous studies using spatial omics have revealed significant intra-tumoral heterogeneity in the TME, indicating that different regions of the same PDAC sample may exhibit distinct TME phenotypes74. Therefore, while the TMS subtyping system reflects overall metabolic and immune characteristics, multiple TMS features can co-exist within one sample. Furthermore, under therapeutic pressure, transitions between TMS subtypes may occur, as evidenced by our observation of an increased proportion of TMS2 in post-treatment samples. Meanwhile, we acknowledge that obtaining paired pre- and post-treatment tissues in PDAC is more challenging than in cancers like breast cancer or melanoma. Future studies with such paired samples will be invaluable to fully elucidate these subtype transitions.

Although it is increasingly common to use molecular features to guide clinical decisions in studies and even clinical practice for many cancers, such as melanoma75, breast cancer76, and colorectal cancer77, the precision medicine strategy has rarely been applied to PDAC. This is primarily due to the limited availability of actionable targets for PDAC and the fact that most patients do not benefit from targeted therapies, such as ICI-based immunotherapy78,79. As a result, chemotherapy remains the standard regimen for most PDAC patients80. Recent studies, such as the COMPASS study81 and the work by Jiang et al. 82, have focused on identifying reliable biomarkers to guide the selection of chemotherapy types. However, solely tailoring chemotherapy selection is unlikely to significantly improve the poor prognosis of PDAC. To revolutionize precision medicine for PDAC, it is equally important to identify therapeutic targets with the potential to improve long-term survival outcomes. Given that both the TME and metabolism have provided an increasing number of anti-tumor targets10,83,84, our study aimed not only to rationalize chemotherapy selection but also to identify promising therapeutic targets. For the TMS3 subtype, we proposed that the addition of the clinically approved glycolysis inhibitor lonidamine could enhance the therapeutic efficacy of AG, a hypothesis that warrants confirmation in future clinical trials. Furthermore, based on our sequential data for a cohort of advanced PDACs, we demonstrated that TMS3 PDACs had better survival outcomes compared to non-TMS3 patients, underscoring the preliminary translational value of our findings. We also recommended prioritizing TMS2 PDACs for inclusion in clinical trials evaluating immunotherapeutic strategies. In this context, GLUL expression in macrophages may serve as a potent biomarker predictive of immunotherapeutic efficacy. Although ferroptosis inducers have not yet been incorporated into clinical practice, certain approved drugs with ferroptosis-inducing potential, such as sorafenib and sulfasalazine85–87, may enhance the therapeutic efficacy for TMS1 PDACs. Additionally, given their demonstrated resistance to paclitaxel, TMS1 PDACs are likely to benefit more from alternative regimens such as FOLFIRINOX.

Previous studies have developed classification systems for cancers based on metabolic features. However, these approaches typically rely either on metabolism-related genes or on integrated scoring of metabolic pathways88,89. Since metabolic activities are often driven by the coordinated action of multiple genes, gene-based classification methods may struggle to capture the complexity of specific metabolic processes. On the other hand, pathway-based classification methods, which utilize sets of metabolism-related genes, can be influenced by inconsistencies in gene-pathway directionality. For example, it is common to include both ALDOA and ALDOB in glycolysis-related gene sets, such as those in the Hallmark and KEGG series that are widely used in studies. However, while ALDOA promotes glycolysis, many studies have shown that ALDOB acts as a tumor suppressor, inhibiting glycolytic activity75,90. In contrast, methods based on metabolic flux estimation91,92, validated by metabolomics data, can more precisely capture the intensity of specific metabolic events. Beyond incorporating algorithms for metabolic flux analysis, we also performed in vitro and in vivo experiments to validate the key metabolic features for each subtype, enhancing the reliability of our core conclusions. Notably, we also presented a comprehensive biogenetic resource comprising the transcriptomes of 460 resected PDACs, 78 adjacent pancreatic tissues, and 101 advanced PDACs obtained via EUS-FNA, accompanied by follow-up data. This represents the largest Asian PDAC transcriptomic cohort to date. The limited number of adjacent pancreatic samples is largely due to the challenges associated with preserving RNA integrity in these tissues, which are rich in various enzymes, and improper preservation can result in significant RNA degradation. For instance, the TCGA database includes data for only four adjacent pancreatic samples.

In conclusion, we not only generated a comprehensive cell type-specific metabolic atlas but also proposed a TME-metabolism-based PDAC classification with significant implications for clinical translation. Additionally, we developed a single-sample, cost-efficient classifier for subtype prediction to facilitate its widespread application.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations. On one hand, the immune and metabolic features were predicted by transcriptome-based algorithms due to the absence of mature single-cell metabolome technology. For bulk samples, while proteome and phosphorylated proteome have been widely applied for molecular profiling of tumors, the RNA-based approach is still the most cost-effective and informative tool for tumor stratification and promising for clinical translation, as most molecular classification-guided clinical trials preferentially choose RNA-seq/PCR-guided strategy for tumor stratification, and several of them have gradually yielded satisfactory results76,77,93–96. On the other hand, like many other molecular classification systems, some optimal therapeutic regimens in the TMS system we proposed are not approved in current clinical decisions, except for lonidamine for TMS3 subtypes, which could be considered in clinical trials. Nonetheless, for TMS1, while the ferroptosis inducer is still in an experimental stage, FOLFIRINOX may be preferred if patients could stand with, due to their resistance to AG-based regimens. Certain FDA-approved drugs with ferroptosis-inducing potential, such as sorafenib and sulfasalazine, could also be considered. For TMS2 subtypes, while GS inhibitors are not approved, these patients could be included in the clinical trials of chemoimmunotherapy due to their immune-enriched TME. Moreover, given the complexity of the TME, future studies should develop more systematic in vitro models that incorporate key parameters like hypoxia, as well as more complex architectures and cell types, such as blood vessels and immune cells.

Methods

Ethical statement

The study was conducted with the approval of the FUSCC Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient (ID: 2109243-17). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of FUSCC (ID: FUSCC-IACUC-2023561).

Single-cell transcriptome datasets and analysis

scRNA-seq data were integrated using external public datasets, including Steele et al. (Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE155698)97, Peng et al. (Genome Sequence Archive (GSA): CRA001160)98, Lin et al. (GEO: GSE154778)99, and ours (Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) cohort, NODE: OER330659)100. After elimination of a case of omentum metastasis, a total of 81 human resected pancreas tissues were analyzed (67 PDACs and 14 adjacent pancreas tissues). All samples were sequenced using the 10× Genomics platform and integrated using the Harmony algorithm (v0.1.0)101. Briefly, dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using the Seurat workflow (v4.3.0). The mitochondrial gene percentage was calculated using the PercentageFeatureSet (object, pattern = “^MT-“) function in Seurat, and cells with a mitochondrial gene percentage exceeding 10% were excluded (The cut-off for the snRNA-seq data was set at 20%). Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function, yielding the top 3000 genes for downstream analysis. Expression data were visualized using t-SNE or UMAP plots. Initial cluster annotations were performed using SingleR (v1.6.1) and subsequently refined manually based on insights from published literature.

Estimate the activity of metabolic features and intercellular metabolic communications

Three algorithms were applied to estimate the metabolic activities of cells: scMetabolism, scFlux, and COMPASS. For scMetabolism102, the “AUCell” method was selected to compute scores based on “KEGG” metabolic gene sets. The scFEA algorithm91, a graph neural network model designed to estimate cell-wise metabolic flux, was executed in Python (version 3.7.15) using the “scFEA” package. The file “module_gene_m168.csv” was used for the model, and the input was a pseudobulk matrix derived from the scRNA-seq data. To generate the pseudobulk matrix, the “AverageExpression” function in the “Seurat” package was employed to calculate the average adjusted gene expression per cell type for each sample. For COMPASS92, an algorithm that characterizes cellular metabolic states using single-cell RNA-seq data and flux balance analysis, the analysis was performed in Python (version 3.7.15). The output reactions underwent a -log2 transformation, followed by differential analysis. METAFlux (version 0.9000) was also used for verification40. The analysis was performed using TPM-normalized transcriptomic data and default parameters, as specified in the software instructions. Metabolic differences between cells derived from PDAC and adjacent pancreatic samples were identified after applying Min-Max Scaling to the input data. Random forest analysis for metabolic traits was conducted using the “randomForest” R package, with scaled input data and the number of trees (ntree) set to 100. To evaluate metabolic communication between cell types in PDAC, MEBOCOST25 was implemented in Python (version 3.8.19). For MEBOCOST, the input was an adjusted cell-wise expression matrix instead of a pseudobulk matrix.

Study cohorts

The present study established a study cohort of 460 PDAC patients who received surgical operation from 2015.3 to 2018.12 in FUSCC. All samples were obtained from patients who underwent upfront surgery without prior treatment and were pathologically verified as PDAC, and with at least a bulk transcriptome was performed. 190 samples were performed untargeted metabolome, and 129 samples were performed spatial proteome (Imaging Mass Cytometry). The study was conducted with the approval of the FUSCC Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient (ID: 2109243-17). We used the term “gender “ in the manuscript, and gender difference did not matter in the present study. No sex or gender factor into model selection. Our study is compliant with the ‘Guidance of the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) for the Review and Approval of Human Genetic Resources’, which requires formal approval for the export of human genetic material or data from China. The detailed clinicopathological features were described in Supplementary Data 8. The bulk transcriptomic and proteome data of samples with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) and the spatial transcriptome (10x visium) of a sample with NAC treatment were derived from our recent article100. In addition, 43 frozen PDAC tissues collected from Second Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University (SHCZ cohort) and 36 frozen PDAC tissues collected from The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (GDZS cohort) were collected and qualified for bulk transcriptomic sequencing, as the validation cohorts. To predict TMS subtypes for FUSCC-NAC, SHCZ, and GDZS cohorts, we first merged each of them with the primary FUSCC-TMS cohort with batch effect adjustment using the Combat algorithm103. Then, a randomforest model was trained to predict the TMS classification for each sample. Public PDAC cohorts with bulk transcriptomic data included TCGA-PAAD and an integrated microarray cohort (referred to as the MA cohort), comprising 554 PDAC samples with survival information. The MA cohort was constructed by integrating microarray expression datasets, including GSE71729, GSE57495, GSE85916, and E-MTAB-6134, with batch effects corrected using the Combat method. An immunotherapy cohort for PDAC was obtained from a previously published clinical trial104, with bulk RNA-seq data available in the GEO database (GSE179351).

Patient-derived PDAC organoids (PDOs)

PDAC organoids were generated as previously detailed in our earlier study105. Human surgically resected PDAC tissues were collected and digested into single-cell suspensions using the PDAC dissociation reagent (bioGenous, K601003). The resulting suspensions were embedded in Cultrex PathClear BME (R&D) and cultured in PDAC organoid culture medium (bioGenous, K2101-PC). The TMS classification for organoids was determined based on RNA-seq analysis of their parental PDAC tissue.

PDTFs

The methodology for constructing and culturing PDTFs was adapted from a previous study in ref. 106. In brief, solid tumor lesions were macroscopically selected by a pathologist from resected tumor material, and a portion of the tumor was collected in ice-cold RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 2.5% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin for subsequent PDTF cultures. Another portion of the tumor was embedded in paraffin for histological analysis. Tissue materials for PDTF cultures were immediately processed by manually cutting into small PDTFs of 1–2 mm³ size on ice. PDTFs from different regions within a tumor were mixed to ensure uniform representation of the tumor lesion. For PDTF cultures, individual PDTFs were embedded in an artificial extracellular matrix prepared by mixing sodium bicarbonate, collagen I, tumor medium, and Matrigel (Matrix High Concentration, Phenol Red-Free, 4 mg ml−1 final concentration; BD Biosciences). Each well of a precooled 96-well plate was coated with 40 μl of matrix to serve as a bottom layer and solidified by exposure to 37 °C for 20–30 min. PDTFs were extensively washed in tumor medium by flushing them multiple times on a cell strainer. One PDTF per well was placed on top of the pre-solidified matrix, after which a second layer of 40 μl matrix was added. Plates were then placed in a 37 °C incubator for 20–30 min, and after solidification, 120 μl of tumor medium was added on top of the matrix. The culture medium was supplemented with DMSO or drugs (paclitaxel (4 nM), gemcitabine (50 nM), and pembrolizumab (10 μg/ml) for 24 h. Then, cytokines were detected by Luminex technology based on the panel (Bio-Rad, 171AK99MR2). Briefly, standards are diluted according to COA instructions, mixed, and allowed to sit for 15–20 min, creating seven standards and one blank. Beads are mixed, diluted, and prepared for use. Detection antibodies and PE-streptavidin are similarly diluted and mixed, while the wash buffer is prepared by diluting the 10× solution with deionized water. During the assay, beads are resuspended, followed by washing. Standards or samples are added and incubated at 800 rpm for 60 min at room temperature with sealed plates. The beads are washed again, incubated with biotin-labeled detection antibodies for 30 min, washed, and incubated with PE-streptavidin for 10 min. After a final wash, beads are resuspended in assay buffer and incubated for 30 s.

Isolation and culture of patient-derived primary pancreatic cancer cells (PDPPCs) from tumor tissue

We generated 23 in-house PDPPCs at low passage values (<10) from resected human PDAC tissues that are pathologically verified. Briefly, the resected tumor samples underwent rigorous cleaning with PBS three times, followed by meticulous dissection into minute fragments of approximately 1 mm³. These fragmented tissues were then subjected to a 60-min enzymatic digestion process using collagenase IV in a shaking incubator maintained at a constant temperature of 37 °C. To terminate the digestion, 10% heat-inactivated FBS was introduced. The dissociated cellular suspension was subsequently filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 g for a duration of 10 min. Enrichment of tumor cells was facilitated using the Tumor Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi, Cat. No. 130-108-339). The isolated cells were then cultured in a controlled environment with 5% CO2 at 37 °C and passaged periodically.

Isolation and culture of primary human PDAC CAF cultures

The protocol for isolating and culturing primary human PDAC CAF cultures followed a procedure similar to that described in a previous study107. In brief, tissues were sectioned into 1 mm³ portions and transferred into 5 ml microtubes. These sections were subsequently minced using scissors and subjected to enzymatic digestion with collagenase IV for a duration of 60 min at 37 °C on a rotor. The dissociated cells were then centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. Following the removal of the supernatant, the pelleted cells were plated onto plastic dishes and incubated in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. After a few days, primary CAFs were harvested and treated with Fc Receptor Blocking Solution (Biolegend). Next, cell surface staining was performed in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS using a cocktail of antibodies (anti-EPCAM, anti-CD45, and anti-CD31) for 30 min at 4 °C. After two washes with PBS containing 2% FBS, the cells (EPCAM- CD45- CD31-) were sorted using the Moflo Astrios EQ cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Cell culture

KPC-0116 cells were derived from KrasLSL-G12D/+; Trp53LSL-R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mice and cultivated in our laboratory in vitro as we previously described100. Panc-1, MIA PaCa-2, NK-92, and THP-1 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). KPC-0116, Panc-1, and MIA PaCa-2 cells were cultured in DMEM, while THP-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium. NK-92 was cultured in specialized medium (Aoyinbio, SAPM0336). Both PDPPCs and CAFs were cultured in DMEM/F12. Drug-resistant cell lines were established from MIA PaCa-2 and KPC cells by gradually exposing them to increasing concentrations of gemcitabine (MCE, HY-17026), oxaliplatin (MCE, HY-17371), paclitaxel (MCE, HY-B0015), irinotecan (MCE, HY-16562), and 5-fluorouracil (MCE, HY-90006)108. Cells were considered drug-resistant when the IC50 ratio between resistant cells and parental cells exceeded 10. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Culture media were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Bulk transcriptome

Pathologically verified PDAC samples were preserved in a −80 °C refrigerator. RNA from PDAC tissues was extracted using a Trizol-based approach. RNA degradation and contamination were monitored on 1% agarose gels. RNA purity was checked using the NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). A total amount of 1 μg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system), and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. StringTie was used to quantify the number of reads mapped to each gene. The TPM (transcripts per million) for each gene was then calculated by first normalizing the read count by the gene length (in kilobases) and subsequently scaling these values so that the sum of all TPMs in the sample equals one million. Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) were identified from the RNA sequencing data using a standard variant calling pipeline. For each sample, the aligned reads in BAM format were processed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.2.2.0). Variant calling was performed with the GATK HaplotypeCaller. Key parameters applied during this step included --emit-ref-confidence GVCFto output a genomic VCF (gVCF) for joint genotyping, --dont-use-soft-clipped-basesto improve specificity by excluding potentially misaligned soft-clipped sequences, and --standard-min-confidence-threshold-for-calling 30to set a quality threshold for calling variants. The resulting variant call format (VCF) files were subsequently annotated to predict the functional consequences of the identified variants. This annotation was carried out using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP v104.3).

Nucleus isolation from frozen samples and snRNA-transcriptome

For frozen PDAC tissue, RNA quality control was performed by extracting RNA using the TRIzol method, with samples considered qualified if they had an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 7.0 and a 28S/18S ratio ≥ 0.7. Single-nucleus isolation for nine qualified PDAC samples began by cutting the tissue into small pieces with pre-chilled scissors in 1 ml of lysis buffer, followed by homogenization in 2 ml of lysis buffer using a homogenizer set to both loose and tight modes for 25 strokes each. The homogenate was transferred to a pre-chilled 15 ml centrifuge tube, and the homogenizer and pestle were rinsed with an additional 2 ml of lysis buffer, yielding a total of 5 ml. The mixture was lysed on ice for 5 min, centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. This process was repeated with fresh lysis buffer, after which the nuclei were resuspended in 5–10 ml of buffer and filtered through pre-rinsed 70 µm and 30 µm cell strainers to minimize nuclear loss. The nuclei suspension was centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the nuclei were resuspended in 1 ml of wash buffer for quality control (QC). Nuclei concentration was adjusted to 700–1200 nuclei/µl using a microscope and fluorescence cell counter, and density gradient centrifugation was performed if debris was present. Library construction for QC-passed nuclei followed the 10× Genomics standard operating procedure (Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ v3.1, Rev D) in three main steps. First, single nuclei were encapsulated into GEMs using a microfluidic Chromium Controller, where cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were introduced during reverse transcription. Second, cDNA was recovered, amplified by PCR, purified, and assessed for concentration and integrity using Qubit 4.0 and Agilent 2100. Finally, library construction included fragmentation, end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and index amplification, with library QC performed using Qubit 4.0 and Qseq400. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a PE150 strategy for QC-passed libraries. Sequencing data were quantified using Cell Ranger (v7.1.0, 10× Genomics) with the GRCh38-2020-A reference genome, using the parameter --include-introns=true to include intronic regions.

Untargeted metabolome

190 pathologically confirmed PDAC samples were accurately weighed and processed for untargeted metabolomics analysis. Tissues were individually ground with liquid nitrogen, and the homogenate was resuspended with prechilled 80% methanol by well vortex. UHPLC-MS/MS analyses were performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Germany) coupled with an Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer with a full scan range of m/z 100–1500, and data-dependent MS/MS acquisition was performed for structural elucidation. The Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode. The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS/MS were processed using the Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, ThermoFisher) to perform peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation for each metabolite. The main parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal/noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, et al. After that, peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity. The normalized data were used to predict the molecular formula based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks, and fragment ions. And then peaks were matched with the mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), mzVault, and MassList database to obtain the accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Metabolite annotation was conducted using the KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID Maps databases (Supplementary Data 9).

Imaging mass cytometry (IMC) data acquisition and analysis

The procedures for IMC data acquisition from FFPE PDAC tissue samples were adapted from our previous study. Briefly, tissue slides were dewaxed and rehydrated through xylene and a graded alcohol series. The slides were then stained with metal-labeled antibodies (Fluidigm) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclei were counterstained using a DNA intercalator, followed by washing in ultra-pure water with gentle agitation and air-drying at room temperature. Multiplexed images of the tissue sections were acquired using an imaging mass cytometer (Fluidigm, Hyperion). Cell segmentation was performed using CellProfiler software (Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research and MIT’s CSAIL) to generate mask files, which were subsequently analyzed in histoCAT software for data processing and cell neighborhood analysis. This allowed the conversion of image data into single-cell resolution. Differential analysis of IMC data was conducted using Bioconductor packages, referencing methods described by Malgorzata et al. 109. The list of antibodies used is provided in Table S1.

TME signatures and correlation with metabolic features

The TME signature refers to a previously reported manually curated list of 29 knowledge-based functional gene expression signatures, covering known cellular and functional TME properties20. Each TME signature and metabolic feature were cycling paired, and Pearson correlation coefficient and p values were computed for every pair. The network for the correlation between TME signatures and metabolic fluxes was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.0).

Estimate cell proportions, gene-set activity, and immunotherapeutic score from bulk transcriptome

We profiled cell type abundance from bulk RNA-seq data using CIBERSORTx110. The genematrix file, which includes genes and their weights, is provided in Supplementary Data 10. The R package “immunedeconv (2.0.4)”111 was utilized to implement various algorithms, including quanTIseq112, CIBERSORT113, TIMER114, MCPcounter115, EPIC116, and xCell117 algorithms to analyze PDAC transcriptomic data, which are essential for estimating the abundance of cell infiltrates in bulk tissues. Furthermore, the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm was applied to calculate enrichment scores for specific gene sets118. The gene-set for collagen-cleaving MMPs was obtained from a previously published study56. The gene-sets of metabolic pathways were derived from the KEGG database and summarized by Gong et al. 88. The immunotherapeutic score was calculated using the ImmuneCellAI algorithm, a predictive model with high accuracy for assessing immunotherapy responses119. Ferroptosis inducers and suppressors were downloaded from FerrDb V2, which is a manually curated database of ferroptosis regulators and ferroptosis-disease associations120. Ferroptosis-related signatures, including glutathione, BH4, complexes I-IV, CoQ10, fatty acids, and membrane lipids, were derived from a previously published study33.

Consensus clustering for PDAC samples

Normalized values for 261 signatures of metabolic fluxes (scFEA), metabolic pathways, and TME features were used for clustering PDAC samples. The R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” (version 1.64.0) was employed, with parameters set to clusterAlg = “pam”, distance = “euclidean”, reps = 50, and pItem = 0.8. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the decreasing trend of the relative change in the area under the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve.

Immune repertoire reconstruction from bulk RNA-Seq data

Immune repertoire sequences were reconstructed using the published algorithm TRUST4121, which can be installed from its GitHub repository (https://github.com/liulab-dfci/TRUST4). The input files for TRUST4 were FASTQ format transcriptomic data from PDAC samples. The resulting output files were processed using the R package “Immunarch”, which was employed to compute clonotypes and TCR diversity metrics for each PDAC sample.

Gene set functional enrichment analysis

For gene set functional enrichment analysis, we employed both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis for DEGs. The enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 3.14.3) to obtain the gene set enrichment results. We set the minimum gene set size to 5 and the maximum to 5000. Besides, the R package psSubpathway37 was utilized to compute differentially activated pathways for each TMS subtype.

In silico drug resistance prediction

Drug resistance analysis for bulk or pseudo-bulk datasets (derived from snRNA-seq) was conducted using the “calcPhenotype” function from the R package41. Resistance scores were calculated for 545 drugs, where a higher score indicates a lower predicted drug response.

Survival analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to assess the impact of clinicopathological factors and TMS on patients’ OS. The Cox proportional hazards model was built using the R package survival, and forest plots were generated with the R package “forestplot”. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were created in GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0), with statistical significance evaluated using the log-rank test.

Western blot

Equal amounts of protein were electrophoresed on 10% SDS‒PAGE gels and were then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore), which were soaked for 2 h in 5% skim milk or BSA for phosphorylated proteins. Subsequently, the membranes were probed with the indicated primary antibodies: Anti-GAPDH (1:10000, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), Anti-ACTB (1:20000, Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig), Anti-GPX4 (1:2000, Proteintech, 67763-1-Ig), Anti-phospho-DDR1 (pTyr513) (1:500, Sigma, SAB4504671), Anti-DDR1 (1:500, Santa Cruz, sc-390268; 1:1000, Abcam, AB288675), Anti-HK2 (1:5000, Proteintech, 66974-1-Ig), Anti-MCT1 (1:2000, Proteintech, 20139-1-AP), Anti-ALDOA (1:5000, Proteintech, 11217-1-AP), Anti-PFKP (1:2000, Proteintech, 13389-1-AP), Anti-ENO1 (1:2000, Proteintech, 11204-1-AP), Anti-LDHA (1:5000, Abcam, ab52488), Anti-PGK1 (1:5000, Proteintech, 68035-1-Ig), Anti-Akt (1:2000, abclonal, A18675), Anti-Phospho-Akt (Ser473) (1:1000, CST, D9E), cleaved-caspase3 (1:400, CST, 9661S) and Anti-GLUL (1:1000, Abcam, ab176562). Then, the PVDF membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies (1:1000, CST) for 60 min. After washing three times (15 min each time), the targeted proteins were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Millipore, MA, USA). Uncropped and unprocessed scans of Western blots can be seen in the source data file.

qRT-PCR

cDNA generation and RT-qPCR were performed as described earlier122. The reverse transcription was performed using HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect qPCR kit (Vazyme, R333-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis was performed with SYBR Green qPCR mix (Yeasen, 11184ES08) and a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative mRNA expression levels between different genes within a single sample were evaluated using cycle threshold (CT) values (if the CT value of Gene A is greater than that of Gene B, then the expression of Gene A is lower than that of Gene B). The primers for the genes were synthesized by KIAN Biotechnology (Suzhou) Co., Ltd. and are listed in Table S2.

Screening differentially expressed genes among PDAC subtypes

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.32.0). First, the counts expression profile dataset was processed by removing genes with more than 50% of their expression values set to zero. The input matrix was constructed using the DESeqDataSetFromMatrix function, followed by normalization with the DESeq function. Differential analysis was carried out using the results function, yielding the statistical significance of differences for each gene. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. The TMS2 signature comprised DEGs that were upregulated compared to non-TMS2 samples.

Prediction of PDAC subtypes based on gene pairs

4879 DEGs between two subtypes (TMS1 vs TMS2; TMS2 vs TMS3, and TMS1 vs TMS3) were retained and used for gene pairing after 2301 duplicated genes were removed. These genes were cyclically singly paired (GeneA|GeneB), and a 0-or-1 matrix was constructed by comparing the expression level of Gene A and Gene B. The value of GeneA|GeneB is defined as 1 if the expression level of Gene A is higher than that of Gene B; otherwise, GeneA|GeneB is defined as 0. When the number of gene pairs with an expression quantity of 0 or 1 accounted for >20% but <80% PDAC samples, it was considered a valid match. To ensure the final model is conserved and compatible across various races and sequential modes, we combined the transcriptome-based FUSCC cohort and the microarray-based MA cohort with the training data with a large sample size (N = 1014). Shared gene pairs that showed the same expression trend across distinct subtypes in two datasets were intersected and kept for further analysis. Randomforest analysis was applied to construct a multiclass predictive model using the R package “caret”. Ten-fold cross-validation was conducted to evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the predictive model. The top fifteen gene pairs with the largest importance coefficient (MeanDecreaseGini) were kept and used for simplifying predictive models by PCR. Primers of genes in the fifteen-gene pairs panel were synthesized co. Gene Adv (Suzhou, China). The consistency of gene expression in PCR and RNA-seq was detected for each gene pair; only the gene pairs with a consistent rate > 0.9 were kept for final model construction. To enable the prediction of TMS using RNA-seq and PCR data, we developed an R package named Pandora (short for “PANcreatic Ductal adenOcarcinoma-RAndom forest classifier”), which is available at https://github.com/982341689/pandora.

Animal experiments

C57BL/6J mice (female, 6 weeks of age, 5 mice per group) were housed in ventilated caging units in the Shanghai Cancer Center specific pathogen free (SPF) facility with standard housing and husbandry. The mice were fed a standard laboratory chow and had free access to food and water. For subcutaneous transplantation, the left flank of the mice was transplanted with 0.5 × 106 cells. Tumor volume was measured every four days using calipers and calculated using the following formula: length × width × height. In the orthotopic murine model, a left flank incision was made, followed by a stepwise dissection to exteriorize the spleen from the abdominal cavity. The pancreas, located posterior to the spleen, was then everted by gently rolling the spleen with a sterile cotton swab. Upon adequate exposure of the pancreas, 20 μl of cell suspension containing 0.3 × 10⁶ cells was injected along the organ’s long axis, starting from the splenic end. The formation of a transient, non-leaking translucent bleb was considered indicative of a successful injection. Euthanasia was performed using CO2 inhalation prior to tumor stripping. Gemcitabine powder (MCE, LY 188011) was resuspended in sterile normal saline at 10 mg/ml. The drug was administered at 50 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection every four days. Nab-paclitaxel (Abraxane) was resuspended in sterile normal saline and intravenously administered at 30 mg/kg at the same time as gemcitabine treatment. MSO (MCE, HY-B1692) was administered at 20 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection every other day. Mice in the corresponding groups were treated with 200 μg PD-1 monoclonal antibody (InVivoMab, RMP1-14) and were injected intraperitoneally every other day. The control group was treated with the corresponding isotype control (InVivoMab). For macrophage depletion, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 100 μl clodronate liposomes (Yeason, 40337ES08) or vector control (Yeason, 40338ES08) every three days.

HuCD34 humanized mice (NSG, female, 6 weeks of age, 5 mice per group) were generated based on the study conducted by the Chen lab123 and were fed a standard laboratory chow and had free access to food and water. Briefly, cord blood was pre-enriched, diluted 1:1 with PBS, layered over Ficoll solution, and centrifuged to isolate mononuclear cells. CD34+ cells were enriched using magnetic bead selection, and their purity (>95%) was confirmed by flow cytometry. Neonatal NSG mice were irradiated with 120 cGy using the X-RAD 225XL system (Precision X-ray, USA), followed by liver injection of 100,000 hCD34+ cells. Ten weeks post-engraftment, peripheral blood was analyzed by flow cytometry for murine CD45+ (BioLegend, Cat# 103112) and human CD45+ (BioLegend, Cat# 304008). Mice with >25% human CD45+ cells in peripheral blood were classified as humanized and included in further studies. The procedures for generating PDAC PDX were described in our previous study105. The initial xenograft generation from patient to mouse was designated as F0, serving as the source for subsequent passages. All PDXs were housed in ventilated caging units at the Shanghai Cancer Center SPF facility under standard conditions with free access to food and water. To establish PDX growth in huCD34+ humanized mice, an F2 generation PDX, previously propagated in NSG mice, was transplanted subcutaneously into humanized mice. The rate of humanization was calculated using the formula: Humanizationrate(%)=hCD45(%)hCD45%+mCD45(%)×100.

NSG mice (female, 6 weeks of age, n = 5 for each PDX case) were housed in ventilated caging units in the Shanghai Cancer Center SPF facility with standard housing and husbandry. They were fed a standard laboratory chow and had free access to food and water. To investigate the therapeutic efficacy of lonidamine and AG in TMS3 and non-TMS3 PDXs, we first classified the parental resected PDAC tissues for PDXs using RNA sequencing to determine their TMS subtype. F2 PDXs were then engrafted into the right flanks of recipient NSG mice. The initial tumor volumes for all PDXs in each group were approximately 300 mm³, with no significant differences between groups prior to treatment. Mice were treated every three days for a total of five doses with lonidamine (100 mg/kg), gemcitabine (25 mg/kg), and paclitaxel (10 mg/kg). The endpoint was defined as when tumor volumes reached 1500 mm³. The maximal allowable tumor burden permitted by the ethics committee was 2000 mm³, and this limit was not exceeded in any animal during the study.

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of FUSCC (ID: FUSCC-IACUC-2023561). Only female mice were included in this study due to animal housing requirements. Sex did not matter in the study design, since this study centers around cancer signaling and drug targets that generally do not require sex-based consideration.

Lipid peroxidation assay and GSH/GSSG ratio measurement

Lipid peroxidation assays were performed using a lipid peroxidation (MDA) assay kit (Dojindo Laboratories, M496) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All the data were quantified fluorometrically (Ex/Em = 540/590 nm). The GSH/GSSG ratio was calculated using a GSH/GSSG Detection Assay (Dojindo Laboratories, G263) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 405 nm.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and lactate detection

To analyze secreted proteins, we performed ELISA on the cell supernatants of Thp1 cells treated as indicated, following the manufacturer’s instructions for each ELISA kit. The following kits were used: SPP1 detection (R&D Systems, DOST00) and CXCL10 detection (Absin, abs510027). Lactate concentrations in the cell culture supernatants were measured using the Lactate Assay Kit-WST® (Dojindo, L256), also following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Viral transduction

Lentiviral constructs targeting human GLUL, DDR1, and corresponding vector controls were obtained from Shanghai Bio-Lifespan Co., Ltd. The constructs included pLV-U6-sgGLUL-EF1α-Cas9-CMV-mCherry-T2A-Puro for GLUL and pLV-U6-sgDDR1-EF1α-core-Cas9-PGK-Puro for DDR1. Lentiviral particles with titers exceeding 10⁷ TU/ml were used to infect target cells in the presence of 8 μg/ml polybrene (Merck Millipore, TR-1003-G). Following infection, drug selection with puromycin was performed to ensure stable integration.

Cell viability assay

PDPPCs or cell-lines were cultured in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C and plated at indicated density in 96-well plates. Optimal seeding densities were established for each cell type to reach 75-80% confluence at the end of the assay. The subsequent day, the cells were exposed to serial dilutions of various drugs. Drug concentrations for paclitaxel were set as 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 nM, for RSL-3 was set as 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 60 and 80 nM. After a treatment period of 48 h, cellular viability was evaluated utilizing the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8, Selleck), and incubated for 1 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a microplate reader (Spectra Max Plus). The viability of organoid with indicated treatment was evaluated by a CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell viability assay (Promega, G9683) based on the manufacturer’s instructions.

mIHC

mIHC was performed using tyramide signal amplification (TSA) for multiplex fluorescence detection. Paraffin-embedded slides were baked at 62 °C for 1 h, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol solutions. Endogenous peroxidase activity is blocked with hydrogen peroxide, and antigen retrieval is performed using Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) in boiling conditions. After blocking with 5% BSA, slides are incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, followed by labeling with a secondary antibody and TSA using fluorophore-conjugated tyramide. For multiplex staining, the process is repeated with different antibodies. Finally, slides are counterstained with DAPI and mounted with antifade medium. A modified labeling score (H score) which is calculated by using percentage of positive stained cancer cells and their intensity per tissue core as before. Intensity of stain was determined by dominant staining and was divided into four categories (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong). By multiplying staining intensity and percentage of positive stained tumor cells ranged from 0 to 100%, the H score ranged from 0 to 300. Cores with overall scores of 0–5, 5–100, 101–200, 201–300 were classified as negative, weak, intermediate and strong expression level. Negative and weak were viewed as low expression level and intermediate and strong were viewed as high expression level. Two independent pathologists evaluated the staining status. The following antibodies were used: anti-HK2 (Proteintech, 66974-1-Ig, 1:2500), anti-MCT1 (Proteintech, 20139-1-AP, 1:1000) and anti-Collagen type I, cleavage site (ImmunoGlobe, 0217-050, 1:200). Additionally, masson trichrome staining for PDX slices was performed by technicists in Ruiyu Biotech, Shanghai.

Phagocytosis assay

After differentiation into macrophage-like cells via 100 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), THP-1 cells were exposed to 100 µM MSO for 48 h. Phagocytic activity was then assessed using the Cell Meter™ Fluorimetric Phagocytosis Assay Kit (AAT Bioquest, 21225). Briefly, the pH-sensitive Protonex™ Red 600-Latex Bead Conjugate was added to the cells and incubated for 4 h to allow for particle internalization. The cells were then stained with the CytoTrace™ Green viability dye for 30 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader, with internalized beads detected in the Texas Red channel (Ex/Em = 570/600 nm) and viable cells quantified in the FITC channel (Ex/Em = 490/525 nm). The phagocytic index, defined as the ratio of red (bead) to green (cell) fluorescence intensity, was calculated for each well to normalize phagocytosis to the number of viable cells.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of effector cells against PDAC cells was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using a commercial kit (Cayman, 601170). Peripheral blood T cells were isolated from patients with PDAC using magnetic cell sorting (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-096-535) and pre-activated for 48 h with a T cell Activation/Expansion Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-441). For the co-culture assay, pre-activated T cells and NK92 cells served as effector cells, while Panc-1 cells were used as target cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 5:1. Prior to the 24-h co-culture, the effector cells were pretreated for 24 h with either PGE2 (100 nM), Grapiprant (1 μM), or a combination of both.

ECM preparation

ECM preparation was referred to a previous study56. CAFs were seeded on 6-well or Seahorse XF96 culture plates. One day after plating, cells were switched into DMEM (with pyruvate) with 10% dialyzed FBS supplemented with 100 μM vitamin C. Cells were cultured for five days with renewal of the medium every 24 h. Then fibroblasts were removed by washing in 1 ml or 500 μl or 100 μl per well PBS with 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 and 20 mM NH4OH. The ECM was washed five times with PBS before cancer cell plating. MMP inhibition in tumor cells was achieved using Ilomastat (MCE, HY-15768) at a concentration of 20 µM.

Glycolysis/OXPHOS assay

The relative activity of glycolysis and OXPHOS was measured using a commercial kit (Dojindo, G270). Briefly, Panc-1 and Mia-Paca2 cells with distinct ECM-treated statuses and DDR1 expression levels (5×10³ cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was then removed. Subsequently, 100 μl of medium containing 1.25 μmol/l Oligomycin was added to half of the wells, while the remaining wells received 100 μl of medium without Oligomycin. Then, 100 μl of the prepared ATP working solution (prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions) was added to each well. The plate was placed on a microplate shaker and mixed for 2 min. Following this, the plate was transferred to a microplate reader set at 25 °C and incubated for 10 min. Luminescence (RLU) was measured, and the resulting values were used to calculate Total ATP, Glycolytic ATP, and Mitochondrial ATP. Glycolytic ATP is defined as the ATP level in wells treated with Oligomycin, and Mitochondrial ATP is calculated as Total ATP minus Glycolytic ATP.

Isotope tracing analysis

L-Glutamate (13C5,99%; 15 N,99%) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. THP-1 cells were treated with PMA (100 ng/ml) for 48 h followed by culturing in glutamine-free RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS and 2.5 mM 13C5-L-glutamate, with and without MSO (100 μM) for 24 h. D-Glucose (U-13C6,99%) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. Panc-1 cells with indicated treatment were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS and 4.5 g/l 13C6 Glucose for 12 h. Then, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with cold PBS three times. Liquid nitrogen was added to the dish to quickly terminate the cell viability. Each sample is treated with 1 ml of an extraction solvent mixture consisting of methanol, acetonitrile, and water in a 4:4:2 volume ratio and then centrifuged at 15,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness and then reconstituted in 50 μl of 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution (1:1, v/v) for subsequent UHPLC-HRMS analysis. Chromatographic separation was conducted by an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). The eluents were analyzed on a Thermo Fisher mass spectrometer in Heated Electrospray Ionization with negative ion mode (HESI−).

Statistics and reproducibility

For comparisons between two groups, numerical variables were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while paired data were evaluated with a paired two-tailed Student’s t-test. When comparing three or more groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was utilized, followed by post hoc multiple comparison tests for pairwise analyses. Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R or GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0). Survival differences between the two groups were assessed using the log-rank test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, with Fisher’s exact test applied when necessary. Details of the statistical methods for each figure are included in the respective figure legends, with significance set at P < 0.05. Statistical significance is denoted as follows: “*” for P < 0.05, “**” for P < 0.01, “***” for P < 0.001, and “****” for P < 0.0001. Sample sizes (n/N), where “n” represents replicates per group and “N” indicates the total number of replicates (e.g., number of participants), are provided in the figures and their legends. All experiments corresponding to the representative micrographs shown in the figures were independently repeated more than three times.
