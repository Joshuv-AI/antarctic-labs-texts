ABCC2 as a novel therapeutic target in lung adenocarcinoma: a machine learning-driven discovery linking ammonia metabolism to prognosis and drug resistance
Authors: Shuangqing Liao, Ziqi Huang, Xiaobing Liu, Kai Wang, Zhi Zheng, Yanqi Li, Sijin Liu, Zhuoxin Dai, Miao Yang, Li Jiang, Jigang Dai, Quanxing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmmonia, long regarded as a metabolic waste product, has recently been recognized as a pivotal oncometabolite in the tumor microenvironment, contributing to cancer progression and immune evasion. However, its prognostic value and therapeutic relevance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsTranscriptomic data from multiple LUAD cohorts were obtained from public databases. Ten machine learning algorithms were integrated into 101 combinations to construct predictive models and identify key genes associated with prognosis. The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and immunotherapy sensitivity were evaluated using established computational methods. The functional impact of the top candidate gene was validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Additionally, candidate agents whose efficacy correlates with ABCC2 expression were screened, and molecular docking was performed to analyze binding affinity and interaction modes.ResultsThe Ammonia Metabolism Score (AMs) emerged as an independent prognostic index for LUAD. A high AMs was associated with a suppressed TIME, characterized by fewer tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes such as CD8⁺ T cells, and showed resistance to immunotherapy. Consistently, ABCC2 itself also demonstrated significant potential as a prognostic biomarker in LUAD. Overexpression of ABCC2 altered the expression of core ammonia-metabolism genes and drove the proliferation of lung adenocarcinoma cell lines both in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionThis study identifies tumor ammonia metabolism as a critical determinant of prognosis and immunotherapy resistance in LUAD. ABCC2 is further established as a key driver of this adverse phenotype, positioning it as a novel prognostic biomarker and a promising therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08357-x.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, ABCC2, Machine learning, Ammonia metabolism, Prognostic biomarker, Drug resistance

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in males and the second leading cause in females [1]. In China, it also poses a major threat to public health, representing a primary contributor to both cancer incidence and mortality [2]. Representing about 40% of all non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cases, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) stands out as the most common histological subtype [3]. Typically arising in the peripheral airways, LUAD exhibits glandular differentiation and is characterized by a high frequency of driver gene mutations [4]. Accurate tumor staging is crucial for guiding treatment decisions and predicting patient outcomes. While the overall 5-year survival rate for lung cancer in the United States remains approximately 19% [5], this figure varies dramatically by stage. For instance, patients with stage IA1 NSCLC have a 5-year survival rate of up to 90%, which plummets to below 10% for those with stage IV disease [6]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD initiation and progression, identifying effective prognostic biomarkers, refining combinatorial therapeutic strategies, and ultimately improving patient survival are imperative research priorities.

The human body produces ammonia through the catabolism of glutamine and asparagine as well as through the biosynthesis of cysteine and nucleotides [7]. Elevated levels of ammonia in plasma are toxic to human cells. Under normal conditions, enzymes can transform ammonia into urea based on the urea cycle (also known as ornithine cycle), which is primarily excreted from the body through urine [8]. Ammonia, a waste product of cellular metabolism, has inherent toxicity and exerts anti-tumor effects. Accumulated ammonia may induce autophagy in tumors [9]. Tian et al. found that glutamine synthetase (GS) inhibitors (MSO) loaded with self-propelled protein motors (MUHNPs) inhibit the enzymatic activity of GS, preventing the conversion and clearance of toxic ammonia. This induces and amplifies the toxicity of ammonia, thereby achieving universal anti-tumor therapy [10]. Carbamoyl phosphate, catalyzed by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS1), can be used for pyrimidine synthesis. Inhibition of CPS1 in LUAD cells may cause impaired pyrimidine formation, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [11]. However, Spinelli et al. found that breast cancer cells can convert accumulated ammonia into glutamate via glutamate dehydrogenase, and glutamate is further metabolized to produce essential amino acids such as aspartate and proline, which are necessary for cell proliferation; this ammonia metabolic cycle accelerates the dissemination of breast cancer cells [12]. In addition to directly affecting tumor cell proliferation, ammonia has a significant impact on many cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Glutamine and its metabolites, especially ammonia, influence metabolic processes in cancer cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts [10]. During the activation of CD8⁺ T cells, intracellular ammonia accumulates and then leads to T cell death, revealing that there exists a novel pattern of effector T cell death [13]. Nevertheless, the contribution of ammonia metabolism to lung adenocarcinoma cell biology and the tumor microenvironment remains incompletely understood.

ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 2 (ABCC2), also known as multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2), is a well-characterized efflux transporter located on the apical membrane of epithelial cells. In oncology, ABCC2 is predominantly recognized for its pivotal role in mediating multidrug resistance (MDR) [14]. In NSCLC, the upregulation of the efflux transporter ABCC2 is associated with chemotherapy resistance to agents such as paclitaxel and carboplatin [15]. Emerging evidence suggests a role for ABCC2 beyond multidrug resistance. In gastric cancer, ABCC2 expression, influenced by genetic polymorphism, has been linked to the induction of metabolic vulnerability and ferroptosis through enhanced glutathione export, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target [16]. A recent study by Eriko Koide and Jue Chen et al. revealed how ABCC2 recognizes diverse compounds, suggesting its potential involvement in tumor metabolic reprogramming. However, its role in ammonia metabolism in lung adenocarcinoma remains unexplored [17]. Investigating whether ABCC2 serves as a functional nexus between metabolic adaptation and therapeutic resistance could unveil novel mechanistic insights and therapeutic vulnerabilities in LUAD.

In the present research, we applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to investigate the ammonia metabolism-related gene modules related to staging. We systematically characterized the ammonia metabolism genes related to the prognosis and progression of LUAD via advanced machine learning methods. After validation in five independent cohorts, we developed a robust prognostic model for ammonia metabolism score (AMs). In addition, we analyzed the molecular features and mutation patterns of various AMs subgroups. Moreover, this research integrated multiple methods to study the response of AMs subgroups to immunotherapy and pharmacotherapy. We also identified ABCC2 as a novel therapeutic target. Experimental validations confirmed that ABCC2 influences the expression of key ammonia metabolism-related genes and promotes the proliferation of LUAD cell lines. Furthermore, we predicted several drug candidates with potential for targeting ABCC2. Through the above comprehensive exploration, our work enhances the understanding of ammonia metabolism’s function in LUAD and provides potential biomarkers for prognostic stratification and personalized treatment.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

We utilized the TCGA database to acquire transcriptome RNA-seq data for 600 LUAD cases. The STAR-Counts data from 541 tumor tissues and 59 adjacent normal tissues were collected, and differential analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.46.0). After removing samples lacking staging information, we analyzed the expression data of the remaining 503 LUAD patients with available clinical features. The subsequent analysis primarily used TCGA-LUAD data in transcripts per million (TPM) format.Additionally, we downloaded 11 raw gene expression datasets (GSE29016 [18], GSE30219 [19], GSE31210 [20], GSE42127 [21], GSE50081 [22], GSE35640 [23], GSE173839 [24], GSE135222 [25], GSE189357 [26], GSE123902 [27] and GSE131907 [28]) from the GEO repository [Supplementary Table S1]. The limma package (v3.62.1) was used to normalize the data from the GEO database, and samples with missing clinical data were excluded. In addition, using the keyword “ammonia metabolism”, ammonia metabolism-related genes with a relevance score of ≥ 1.0 were retrieved from GeneCards, and a list containing 1275 genes was downloaded [Supplementary Table S2].

Identification of co-expressed ammonia metabolism modules related to tumor staging

WGCNA can be applied to analyze gene co-expression networks. This method clusters and weights gene-level data by analyzing the expression patterns of multiple samples, constructs co-expression networks between genes, and identifies gene modules with related functions in biological processes or diseases [29]. We used WGCNA to identify genes co-expressed with ammonia metabolism-related genes associated with the progression of lung adenocarcinoma. By selecting a soft threshold power of 4 to construct the weighted network, we identified five modules, with the gray module representing the set of genes that were not co-expressed. Additionally, we assessed the relationship between module eigengenes and traits, with an emphasis on modules related to tumor staging. To clarify the potential biological pathways related to these module eigengenes, we performed GO and KEGG analyses using the R package “ClusterProfiler” [30].

Construction of ammonia metabolism-related risk score (AMs)

After identifying the ammonia metabolism-related lung cancer progression genes that were common to training and validation groups, we employed the MIME package to determine the prognostic signature. We conducted a comprehensive evaluation using the MIME package, which involved 10 machine learning algorithms [31]. These algorithms mainly included stepwise Cox regression, LASSO, ridge regression, plsRcox, CoxBoost, GBM, SuperPC, random survival forest (RSF), and survival-SVM. We selected the RSF algorithm based on its highest C-index and AUC values to construct the ammonia metabolism-related risk score. RSF can robustly handle high-dimensional data, adapt to nonlinear relationships, and incorporate overfitting control through bootstrap method and random feature selection. We utilized the ML.Dev.Prog.Sig function from the MIME package, which automatically performs 10-fold cross-validation and repeats it multiple times to assess the model’s robustness. Feature stability was evaluated by examining the consistency of variable importance across bootstrap repetitions.

Molecular features and immune phenotype of the AMs subgroup

The TIMER 2.0 database provided immune infiltration data for our analysis to establish an association between the AMs subgroup and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in TCGA-LUAD.

The comprehensive data incorporated various models, including TIMER, MCP-counter, xCell, EPIC, CIBERSORT, and quanTIseq. To assess the association between the AMs subgroup and the expression patterns of immune checkpoint (ICP) genes, we conducted Spearman correlation analysis. We used ssGSEA to investigate the relationship between immune infiltration levels and AMs, as well as AMs-associated genes.

To identify the hallmark pathways potentially linked to the AMs subgroup, we employed the GSVA package to calculate scores for 50 cancer hallmarks and several immune-related pathways for each patient [32]. Next, we applied the limma package to analyze the GSVA outcomes, aiming to identify pathways that exhibited significant differences between the subgroups.

Predicting the immune checkpoint inhibitor response of AMs subgroups

Spearman correlation analysis was applied to demonstrate the relationship between AMs/AMs-related genes and several immune checkpoints. We collected relevant pathological data from cancer cases that received anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, including LUAD cases from the GSE135222 dataset, kidney and bladder cancer patients from the IMvigor210 dataset, melanoma patients from the GSE35640 dataset, and breast cancer cases from the GSE173839 dataset. Subsequently, survival analysis was performed using R software. We also utilized the TIDE method to assess the likelihood of individual patients being sensitive to immunotherapy [33]. Additionally, the differences in TIDE-related scores across various AMs subgroups were compared via the Wilcoxon test.

Single‑cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‑seq) analysis

GSE189357, GSE123902 and GSE131907 offer sufficient sample sizes and detailed cell type annotations, and are widely used lung adenocarcinoma single-cell RNA sequencing datasets in the literature. The GSE189357 dataset contains relevant sequencing data from nine early-stage LUAD samples, including three at the Adenocarcinoma in Situ (AIS) stage, three at the Minimally Invasive Adenocarcinoma (MIA) stage, and three at the Invasive Adenocarcinoma (IAC) stage. The scRNA-seq expression matrix was analyzed using Seurat (v5.1.0). To address batch effects, Harmony (v1.2.1) was used for correction. Subsequently, dimensionality reduction was performed and visualized using PCA and UMAP. We used AddModuleScore to evaluate the AMs score in the three stages and the SCP package (v0.5.6) to illustrate the differences. We also calculated the AMs score for cells in different clusters in GSE123902 and GSE131907 datasets and used the CellChat package to explore interactions between CD8⁺ T cells and epithelial cells [34]. A CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion score was calculated using AddModuleScore with a curated gene set comprising PDCD1, LAG3, TIGIT, HAVCR2, CTLA4, TOX, and ENTPD1, based on published exhaustion signatures [35].

Genomic variations in different AMs subgroups

We employed the R package “maftools” (v2.22.0) to generate waterfall plots of mutations in different AMs subgroups to explore somatic mutations related to these subgroups. Additionally, we calculated tumor mutational burden (TMB) in these patients to further investigate the correlation between genetic heterogeneity and AMs. The differences between AMs subgroups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test. We used the R package “ComplexHeatmap” (v2.22.0) to demonstrate the correlation between mutations and AMs.

Identification of candidate drugs for high-AMs or high-ABCC2 subgroups

To identify potential therapeutic agents for distinct high-risk subgroups, we performed drug sensitivity analyses in cases with either a high Ammonia Metabolism Score (high-AMs) or high expression of the core gene ABCC2 (high-ABCC2). Using the oncoPredict (v1.2) R package and drug response data from the GDSC database, we assessed the association between the predicted sensitivity (IC50) to chemotherapeutic agents and two variables: (1) the continuous AMs, and (2) ABCC2 expression levels across the LUAD cohort. Candidate drugs were defined as those showing significantly enhanced predicted efficacy (lower IC50) in samples with high-AMs or high-ABCC2 compared to their low-scoring or low-expression counterparts. The R package “pheatmap” (v1.0.12) was used to visualize the sensitivity profiles of candidate drugs. The R package “ggplot2” (v3.5.1) was used to generate volcano plots (highlighting significant drugs), boxplots (comparing IC50 between subgroups), and correlation scatter plots to illustrate the relationships.

Molecular docking

We retrieved the molecular structure of ABCC2 from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). For our evaluation, we retrieved the molecular structures of the candidate drugs from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). AutoDock Tools (v4.26) and MGLTools (v1.5.6) were employed to conduct docking studies, with the optimized model used as the docking target. To evaluate the reliability and accuracy of ligand placement, we used binding energy (BE), a weighted average of docking scores, as a key metric. A visual representation of the three-dimensional docking outcomes was generated using PyMOL (v2.5.7).

Immunohistochemistry and scoring

Human tissue microarrays (AF-LucSur2202) were bought from Aifang Biological Enterprise (Changsha, China), and the corresponding ethical approval was obtained (HN20250401). The evaluation criteria for grading the proportion of positive cells of ABCC2 expression were as follows: 0, 0–5%; 1, < 25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; 4, 75–100% positive cells. Staining intensity was determined as follows: 0, no staining; 1, mild staining; 2, moderate staining; 3, strong staining. The final scores were derived by calculating the product of the extent and intensity scores (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, or 12). The tissue microarray for our lung adenocarcinoma multicolor immunohistochemistry (LucSur2202) was also purchased from Aifang Biological Enterprise (Changsha, China). The specific reagents are listed in Supplementary Table S3, and the detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Construction of LUAD cell lines with overexpression and interference of ABCC2

The A549 and PC-9 cell lines were purchased from Meisen CTCC (Zhejiang, China). Primers for ABCC2 and other genes were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (Supplementary Table S4). The ABCC2 overexpression plasmid was obtained from iGene Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). These two cell lines were cultured under standard conditions in basal medium containing 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin. Next, ABCC2 plasmid transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine. Transfected cells were collected 48 h after transfection for RT-qPCR analysis. ABCC2 knockdown lentivirus was purchased from Corues Biotechnology (Nanjing, China). A549 cells were treated with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 75. Stable cells were collected and used for RT-qPCR and western blotting (WB) analysis.

CCK-8 experiment

The CCK-8 method was used to assess cell proliferation. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates. Cells from both groups were treated according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the blank medium served as a blank control. After incubation under standard conditions for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 3 h. Afterward, the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (ELX800) at 450 nm. The cell proliferation rate was calculated using the formula: (experimental OD − blank OD) / (control OD − blank OD) × 100%.

Cell invasion assay

Transwell chambers were used to analyze cell invasion ability. Chambers with 8 μm pore size filters were precoated with Matrigel diluted 1:8 in basal medium. After adding 100 µL of the diluted Matrigel, the chambers were incubated for 60 min under standard conditions, and the excess solution was removed. A total of 200,000 A549 cells or 100,000 PC-9 cells suspended in basal medium were added to the upper chambers, which were then incubated for 48 h under the same conditions. Cells on the upper surface were removed, and the cells that invaded the lower chamber were fixed for 30 min and subsequently stained with crystal violet. Invasive cells were counted using an inverted microscope.

The cell apoptosis analysis

The Annexin V-fluor647 / PI detection kit (40304ES60) was used to detect cell apoptosis. 140,000 cells were seeded and treated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-fluor647 and 10 µL of PI reagent for 20 min in the dark. The level of apoptosis was determined using flow cytometry (FACSCanto, BD, USA).

qPCR

cDNA was synthesized under the following conditions: 42 °C for 5 min, 37 °C for 15 min, and 85 °C for 5 s, using a MyCycler (T100). qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 system with the following protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Three technical replicates were used for each sample. The transcript levels were normalized to GAPDH using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test, as appropriate.

Western blot

A549 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor and incubated on ice for 30 min. Subsequently, the lysates were boiled for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Afterward, the membranes were blocked for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ABCC2 (29261-1-AP, 1:1,000, Proteintech, China) and β-actin (20536-1-AP, 1:5,000, Proteintech, China), followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibody (anti-rabbit, 1:10,000) for 45 min at 25 °C. Membranes were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent. Detailed information on all reagents is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

RNA-seq

RNA-seq analysis was performed by RuiXing Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). For library preparation, 1 µg of total RNA was treated with RQ1 DNase using the VAHTS RNA-seq Prep Kit. VAHTS mRNA capture beads were used to capture mRNA. The fragmented RNAs were reverse transcribed into cDNA. Subsequently, the cDNA was ligated to VAHTS RNA Multiplex Oligos Set 1, and the ligated products were then amplified, purified, and quantified. The libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 system.

Subcutaneous xenograft model

All procedures involving mice were carried out in strict compliance with the animal-study guidelines authorized by the Army Medical University’s Ethics Committee. Five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice weighing approximately 20 g were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China) and maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. For in vivo assessment of the effect of ABCC2 on tumor growth, 1 × 10⁷ ABCC2-knockdown or control A549 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of SPF nude mice (n = 6 per group). Mice were observed daily, and after tumor formation, the tumor length and width were measured and recorded. At the 20-day endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were isolated, weighed, and photographed. Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (24 h), tumor tissues underwent sequential processing: rinsing in running water (2 h), dehydration through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, 100%; 1 h each), clearing in xylene (2 × 30 min), and paraffin embedding. Sections (4 μm) were then cut, baked at 65 °C for 2 h to melt paraffin and improve adhesion, and subjected to immunohistochemical staining (IHC). IHC was performed with rabbit anti-ABCC2 (29261-1-AP, Proteintech; 1:500) and rabbit anti-Ki67 (27309-1-AP, Proteintech; 1:200). For each tumor section, three non-overlapping 200× fields were randomly selected and captured under identical exposure and illumination settings. Quantitative image analysis was performed with Image-Pro Plus 6.0: regions of positive staining were delineated, and the positively stained area, integrated optical density (IOD), total field area, and percentage of positive area were automatically calculated.

Quantitative determination of ammonia inside and outside cells

The intracellular and supernatant ammonia content was detected using the Abcam Ammonia Assay Kit (ab83360, Abcam, UK). This kit is based on the catalytic principle of glutamate dehydrogenase: ammonia and α-ketoglutarate are converted into glutamate under the action of glutamate dehydrogenase, and NADH is reduced to NAD⁺ simultaneously. The decrease in NADH is directly proportional to the ammonia content in the sample. By measuring the change in absorbance at 570 nm, the ammonia concentration in the sample can be accurately quantified. The specific experimental procedures are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) were used for statistical analysis. For continuous variables, a t-test was used for comparisons between two groups if the data were normally distributed with homogeneous variances. For comparisons involving more than two groups or two factors with an interaction term, two-way ANOVA was used. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data. The prognostic significance of the AMs level was assessed using univariate Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Results were considered statistically significant if the p-value was below 0.05.

Results

Identification of ammonia metabolism-related genes associated with lung adenocarcinoma staging progression

After differential analysis of tumor and healthy tissue samples in the TCGA-LUAD dataset, 5,451 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the screening criteria (p < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1), with 3,536 upregulated and 1,915 downregulated genes in the tumors (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table S5). Several core genes associated with ammonia metabolism were also differentially expressed in normal and LUAD tumor tissues (Fig. 1B). To identify ammonia metabolism-related genes associated with the progression of LUAD, we conducted WGCNA analysis on 1275 ammonia metabolism-related genes (Supplementary Figure S1, Fig. 1C). Ultimately, the brown and turquoise modules were positively correlated with LUAD stage progression (Fig. 1D). The brown module contained 144 genes, and the turquoise module contained 422 genes. A total of 112 intersecting genes were identified between the DEGs and the two module genes (Fig. 1E). These genes represent ammonia metabolism-related genes that are differentially expressed in LUAD and associated with LUAD progression. Enrichment results also indicated that these genes were associated with ammonia metabolism (Fig. 1F).

Construction of a machine learning-based signature for lung adenocarcinoma prognosis and progression

Using previously identified ammonia metabolism-related genes associated with lung adenocarcinoma progression, we constructed a prognosis and progression-related signature using multiple machine learning methods. The TCGA-LUAD dataset served as the training set, and five additional lung adenocarcinoma GEO datasets were used to validate the results. The final model was determined based on the C-index of the training set, the mean C-index, and the AUC values at 1, 3, and 5 years across different datasets. Ultimately, the RSF algorithm was chosen as the optimal prognostic and progression-related model (PPM) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure S2). The PPM score effectively stratified patients by prognosis in the six lung adenocarcinoma cohorts (Fig. 2B-G). The PPM model achieved a C-index greater than 0.6 in six LUAD cohorts, indicating favorable prognostic prediction performance (Fig. 2H). The AUC values of the PPM model also indicated good prognostic predictive ability across the cohorts (Fig. 2I). Compared with 13 other lung adenocarcinoma prognostic models (Supplementary Table S6), our model exhibited a higher C-index in multiple datasets and a stronger ability to predict prognosis (Supplementary Figure S3) [36–48]. The PPM consists of 20 genes (Supplementary Table S7 and S8), including ABCC2 and CPS1, with ABCC2 being identified by multiple algorithms (Supplementary Figure S4). Moreover, a moderate correlation was observed between ABCC2 and AMs (r = 0.458, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S5B).

The comprehensive prognostic value of ammonia metabolism score in clinical and genomic contexts of lung adenocarcinoma

The ammonia metabolism score (AMs), defined as the PPM score, demonstrated significant associations with tumor progression and genomic alterations in lung adenocarcinoma. In the TCGA-LUAD cohort, AMs increased with advancing tumor stage (p < 0.001, Fig. 3A; Table 1, Supplementary Table S9) and was higher in male patients (p < 0.05, Fig. 3B), but showed no difference across age groups (Fig. 3C). Cox regression analyses confirmed that AMs, alongside tumor stage, served as an independent risk factor for prognosis (Fig. 3D and E). GSVA enrichment analysis further revealed that higher AMs were positively correlated with pro-tumorigenic pathways and immune microenvironment remodeling, such as E2F targets and the p53 signaling pathway in cancer (Fig. 3F and G).

Relationship between AMs level and clinicopathological features of TCGA-LUAD patients

Beyond clinical correlates, AMs was also linked to distinct genomic landscapes. The AMs-High and AMs-Low groups exhibited different patterns of chromosomal alterations, with specific regions highlighted in Supplementary Figure S6A. The AMs-High group was associated with a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB, p < 0.01, Supplementary Figure S6B) and specific key driver mutations in genes like EGFR and KRAS (Supplementary Figure S6C). Critically, survival analysis indicated that patients with both high AMs and low TMB had a significantly worse prognosis (Supplementary Figure S6D), underscoring the integrated prognostic value of AMs with genomic features.

ScRNA‑seq reveals a distinct role of AMs in LUAD

We performed clustering on the single-cell dataset GSE189357 of early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and calculated the AMs using AMs signatures (Fig. 4A, B). We found that in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, as the tumor progresses, epithelial cells in invasive adenocarcinoma exhibit relatively higher levels of AMs compared to other stages (p < 0.0001, Fig. 4C). We also performed clustering analysis on the LUAD single-cell datasets GSE123902 and GSE131907, and calculated AMs (Fig. 4D, E, Supplementary Figure S7A-C). In the GSE123902 dataset, we observed a progressive increase in epithelial AMs across the three groups (Normal tissue, Primary tumor and Metastatic tumor) (p < 0.0001, Fig. 4F). AMs of CD8⁺ T cells were higher in the primary tumor group compared to the other two groups (p < 0.0001, Fig. 4G). AMs in CD8⁺ T cells are positively correlated with T cell exhaustion scores (Fig. 4H). Through cell communication analysis, we found more interactions between epithelial cells and CD8⁺ T cells via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Fig. 4I). In the GSE131907 dataset, we also observed a significant increase in AMs of epithelial cells and CD8⁺ T cells in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (p < 0.0001, Supplementary Figure S7D, E). We also found that the AMs of CD8⁺ T cells showed a weak positive correlation with T cell exhaustion scores, and there were more interactions between epithelial cells and CD8⁺ T cells via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Supplementary Figure S7F, G).

Immune landscape and correlation with AMs

To clarify the immunological characteristics presented by AMs, this study analyzed the TCGA-LUAD data to determine the association between AMs and the degree of immune infiltration. Seven different analytical methods were used to determine the immune infiltration score. The heatmap showed that immune infiltration was greater in the cohort with lower AMs (Fig. 5A). Multiple analysis results indicated that the AMs-High group had lower CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (Fig. 5B and D). ssGSEA results also showed that AMs was inversely related to the immune infiltration level (Fig. 5E). GSVA results also showed that AMs was negatively correlated with several stages in the cancer-immunity cycle (Fig. 5F). These findings indicated that high AMs was associated with immune suppression.

The impact of AMs on immune therapy

Having observed that AMs influence immune infiltration in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) of lung adenocarcinoma, we analyzed their impact on immunotherapy. The negative association between genes in the signature/AMs and the expression of various immune checkpoints indicated that higher AMs was associated with a lower likelihood of response to immunotherapy (Fig. 6A, B). Upon validation in multiple immunotherapy-related datasets, patients with higher AMs had worse immunotherapy responses, as expected (Fig. 6C and I). The TIDE algorithm indicated that in the TCGA cohort, lower AMs scores were associated with better immune responses (p < 0.001, Fig. 6J).

Potential therapeutic agents for individuals with high AMs or high ABCC2 expression

Integrating previous analyses, we found that cases with high AMs were less sensitive to immunotherapy and had a higher risk of gene mutations. Therefore, it is essential to identify drugs suitable for this group of cases. We conducted a correlation analysis of 198 drugs (Supplementary Table S10). Supplementary Figure S8A shows drugs strongly associated with AMs, indicating drugs to which AMs-high cases were sensitive or resistant. We found that many drugs, including gefitinib, were more effective in the AMs-high group (Supplementary Figure S8B–I). Moreover, the predicted IC50 of some drugs was negatively correlated with AMs, indicating that these drugs might be more effective in patients with higher AMs (Supplementary Figure S8J–M).

Based on the parallel analysis of the high ABCC2 expression subgroup, we further screened potential therapeutic drugs that might be effective for this group (Supplementary Table S11). Correlation analysis showed that the sensitivity (IC50) of multiple drugs was significantly correlated with the expression level of ABCC2 (Fig. 7A). We focused on validating several drugs with the most significant associations, including AZD6738, WIKI4, VE821, and MK-1775, and found that these drugs had greater predicted efficacy in tumor models with high ABCC2 expression (Fig. 7B and F).

Candidate therapeutic agents for patients with elevated ABCC2 with molecular docking analysis. (A) Volcano plot showing the correlation between drug sensitivity and expression of ABCC2. (B-F) Box plots showing potential therapeutic drugs for patients with high ABCC2 expression. (G-J) Molecular docking simulations for (G) AZD6738, (H) WIKI4, (I) VE821, and (J) MK-1775 with ABCC2. ****: p < 0.0001, using the Mann-Whitney U test

To explore their potential mechanisms of action, we conducted molecular docking simulations of the above candidate drugs with the ABCC2 protein. The results showed that AZD6738, WIKI4, VE821, and MK-1775 could all stably dock in the active pocket of the ABCC2 protein with relatively low binding energies and form key hydrogen bonds (Fig. 7G and J). In the binding pocket between ABCC2 and AZD6738, AZD6738 interacted with ABCC2 through one typical hydrogen bond at residue Phe-1034 with a distance of 2.8 Å (Fig. 7G). The interaction between AZD6738 and ABCC2 exhibited the lowest binding energy (BE) of − 3.68 kcal/mol. Furthermore, the analysis showed that ABCC2 interacted with WIKI4 via a standard hydrogen bond at residue Phe-725, with a distance of 2.9 Å and a BE of − 3.11 kcal/mol (Fig. 7H). The ABCC2-VE821 complex was stabilized by three hydrogen bonds with residues Glu-727 and Phe-725, at distances of approximately 1.8, 3.2, and 3.2 Å, respectively, with an associated BE of − 3.49 kcal/mol (Fig. 7I). The ABCC2–MK-1775 complex was stabilized by a single hydrogen bond to Lys-344 (2.2 Å) with an associated binding energy of − 3.27 kcal/mol (Fig. 7J). From a structural perspective, this suggests that these compounds may be potential substrates or inhibitors of ABCC2 and may serve as potential therapeutic agents for chemotherapy-resistant lung adenocarcinoma.

The protein levels and evaluation of the important gene ABCC2 of AMs in lung adenocarcinoma tissues

Among all the signature genes, ABCC2 appeared in most algorithms, highlighting its importance in AMs (Supplementary Figure S4A, B). To better demonstrate the clinical value of our predictive model, we experimentally validated the expression of ABCC2. We performed immunohistochemical staining for ABCC2 in 79 pairs of LUAD and adjacent normal tissues (Table 2, Supplementary Table S9). The protein level of ABCC2 was significantly higher in LUAD samples (p < 0.001, Fig. 8A, B). Simultaneously, patients with higher ABCC2 expression had poorer prognosis (Fig. 8C). With advancing lung adenocarcinoma stage, the protein expression of ABCC2 also increased (p < 0.05, Fig. 8D, E). We also found that the important genes ABCC2 and CPS1 in AMs had higher levels in the tumor group compared to those in the normal group, and the high-expression group had poorer prognosis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort (Fig. 8F and I).

Disrupting ABCC2 affects the levels of core genes in ammonia metabolism and impacts related pathways

To explore the influence of ABCC2 on core ammonia metabolism genes and related pathways in LUAD, we used shRNA to knock down ABCC2 expression (Fig. 11A-C), quantified the expression of core ammonia metabolism genes, and performed transcriptome sequencing on LUAD cells. After ABCC2 knockdown, the mRNA expression of the core ammonia metabolism genes CPS1, GLUL, and HNF4α was significantly decreased (Fig. 11D-F). Similar positive correlations between ABCC2 and CPS1 were also observed in datasets such as TCGA-LUAD (Supplementary Figure S5A). Moreover, in the TCGA-LUAD dataset, GSEA of the high AMs group identified tumor progression pathways such as cell cycle and DNA replication, as well as ammonia metabolism-related pathways (Supplementary Figure S5C–D, Supplementary Table S12). After ABCC2 knockdown, differential expression analysis revealed that 2,048 genes were upregulated and 1,477 genes downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma cells (Fig. 11G, H, Supplementary Table S13). The downregulated differentially expressed genes were enriched in multiple signaling pathways that promote cancer progression and were associated with ammonia metabolism-related pathways such as amino acid transport (Fig. 11I, Supplementary Table S14). In contrast, upregulated DEGs were enriched in multiple pathways that suppressed cancer progression (Fig. 11J, Supplementary Table S15).

ABCC2 expression correlates with immune exclusion and impaired CD8⁺ T cell function in lung adenocarcinoma

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining revealed spatial distribution patterns of MRP2⁺ (ABCC2⁺) tumor cells relative to CD8⁺ T cells in normal and tumor tissues (Fig. 13A, B). Spatial analysis of 47,054 MRP2⁺ tumor cells showed that 83.5% were located in immune‑excluded regions (distance > 50 μm), whereas only 7.0% exhibited an infiltrated phenotype (≤ 30 μm) (Fig. 13C, Supplementary Table S16). MRP2 expression levels differed significantly across spatial phenotypes, with the highest expression observed in excluded cells and the lowest in synapse‑forming cells (Kruskal‑Wallis, p = 0.00053) (Fig. 13D). Stratification by MRP2 expression tertiles revealed a dose‑dependent increase in immune exclusion distance, as the high‑expression group showed significantly greater distances compared to the low‑ and medium‑expression groups (Kruskal‑Wallis, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 13E). Furthermore, CD8 expression in T cells negatively correlated with spatial distance from MRP2⁺ tumor cells (r = − 0.092, p < 0.0001), with infiltrating T cells (≤ 30 μm) exhibiting significantly higher CD8 expression than distal T cells (> 100 μm) (Fig. 13F).

Discussion

Over the past ten years, the advent of molecularly targeted therapies and immune checkpoint (ICP) inhibitors has significantly enhanced the survival rates of patients with advanced lung cancer. Despite these advancements, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally [49]. Most patients with advanced NSCLC eventually acquire resistance to existing treatments and continue to progress [50]. Therefore, comprehensive multi-omics analysis combined with machine learning is crucial for developing innovative personalized treatment strategies and discovering novel therapeutic targets, particularly for previously untreatable LUAD cases. Once regarded solely as a toxic nitrogenous by-product, ammonia is now recognized as a key participant in many physiological processes and is closely related to cancer [51]. Ammonia in the tumor microenvironment (TME) supports the biosynthesis of non-essential amino acids and activates mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1, which is closely involved in tumor growth [52]. In addition, ammonia serves as an important molecule that stimulates lipid synthesis via the activation of sterol regulatory element-binding proteins. This process not only meets the requirements of proliferating tumor cells but also suppresses the functions of various immune cells [53, 54]. To clarify the function of ammonia metabolism in LUAD progression, we screened for ammonia metabolism-related genes associated with lung cancer progression. We used lung adenocarcinoma transcriptomic data to establish our prognostic model. We used a comprehensive machine learning framework comprising 10 different models and their combinations, which significantly enhanced the model reliability compared with using a single algorithm. AMs demonstrated excellent and consistent prognostic ability across the six independent cohorts. Moreover, we explored the mechanistic link between AMs and tumor immune suppression and found that patients in the high-AMs subgroup were less sensitive to immunotherapy. We also found that AMs are associated with KRAS and EGFR mutations in LUAD, which are the most common mutations in this type of cancer [55].

ABCC2 is an important molecule in the AMs prognostic model. Beyond establishing the critical role of the AMs prognostic model in LUAD patient stratification and personalized treatment, we further demonstrated that ABCC2 represents a potential therapeutic target for this patient population. As a member of the multidrug resistance-associated protein (MRP) subfamily, ABCC2 is a key factor in substance transport and drug absorption and excretion [56]. ABCC2 is recognized as having significant clinical value in the chemotherapy of NSCLC [15, 57]. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that elevated ABCC2 expression constitutes a risk factor for LUAD. Furthermore, ABCC2 overexpression promoted invasion and proliferation while suppressing apoptosis in LUAD cells. Subsequent in vivo experiments revealed that ABCC2 knockdown significantly suppressed xenograft tumor growth in nude mice. ABCC2 also affects the levels of key genes associated with ammonia metabolism (CPS1, HNF4α, GLUL), which can significantly impact the prognosis of lung cancer [11, 58, 59]. We found that in lung adenocarcinoma cells with ABCC2 knockdown, downregulated genes were related to lung cancer progression and the ammonia metabolism pathway, such as the Wnt signaling pathway and amino acid transport [60].

ABCC2 (MRP2) exhibits a dual role in lung cancer cells, influencing cellular metabolism through ammonia distribution while contributing to immune evasion via spatial exclusion of CD8⁺ T cells [61]. In this study, knockdown of ABCC2 resulted in intracellular ammonia accumulation, reduced extracellular ammonia levels, and downregulation of c-MYC and GLUL expression, suggesting that ABCC2 may affect tumor cell proliferation and metabolic remodeling through regulation of ammonia metabolism and glutamine synthesis [62]. Spatial analysis further revealed that tumor cells with high MRP2 expression were more prone to exclusion from CD8⁺ T cells in a dose-dependent manner, and CD8 expression in adjacent T cells diminished with increasing distance, indicating that MRP2 may suppress antitumor immune responses by forming physical barriers or modifying the local microenvironment [63]. Collectively, these findings suggest that ABCC2/MRP2 functions not only as a classical drug efflux pump but also as a critical node linking tumor metabolism and immune evasion [64]. Targeting this molecule may simultaneously disrupt metabolic adaptation and immune exclusion, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for lung cancer.

While our study provides insights into ammonia metabolism-related genes in LUAD progression and prognosis, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, this study performed a limited analysis using two single-cell datasets. Future research should incorporate a broader range of datasets to further validate our findings and clarify the interactions between ammonia metabolism and various cellular components, including tumor cells, macrophages, and T cells. Secondly, this study did not investigate the potential synergistic effects between ABCC2 and other core genes (such as CPS1). Simultaneously knocking down ABCC2 and CPS1 or other signature genes might reveal enhanced phenotypic effects, which will be pursued in future studies. Thirdly, integration of our findings with a comprehensive characterization of other immune cell populations (macrophages, neutrophils, natural killer cells) and immune checkpoint molecules (PD-L1, CTLA-4) will further refine the mechanistic model, representing important directions for future research. Fourthly, the exact biochemical mechanism by which ABCC2 regulates GLUL expression—whether through direct transcriptional control, indirect signaling, or metabolic feedback—remains to be fully elucidated. Fifthly, the drug sensitivity correlations and docking simulations presented herein are exploratory and require experimental validation; further in vitro and in vivo studies are needed for validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes the Ammonia Metabolism Score (AMs) as a comprehensive prognostic evaluation model for LUAD, capable of predicting an immunosuppressive microenvironment and resistance to immunotherapy. Moreover, this study reveals a potential link between ABCC2 and tumor ammonia metabolic reprogramming, and further identifies it as a pivotal nexus connecting tumor progression and treatment resistance. Targeting ABCC2 may present a promising approach to address LUAD and its associated chemoresistance.
