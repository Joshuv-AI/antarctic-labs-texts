NMR-Based Metabolic Profiling of Biobank Derived Blood Samples for the Identification of Liver Disease Biomarkers
Authors: Munki Choo, Chaeyoung Lee, Sunghyouk Park, Hyuk Nam Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver disease remains a leading cause of global cancer mortality and predominantly originates from chronic liver cirrhosis. Current surveillance strategies often suffer from suboptimal sensitivity which necessitates the discovery of robust biomarkers for early detection. In this study we utilized blood resources from the National Biobank of Korea to evaluate the diagnostic potential of Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy-based metabolomics in patients with cirrhosis and liver cancer. We aimed to identify specific biomarkers and investigate their correlation with clinical blood parameters by comparing the metabolic profiles of disease and healthy control groups. Multivariate statistical analysis demonstrated significant metabolic distinctions between liver disease patients and healthy controls. While the models successfully differentiated disease states the global metabolic landscape exhibited an overlap between cirrhosis and cancer groups suggesting a shared pathological background driven by systemic metabolic shifts. Quantitative assessment identified specific metabolic alterations characterizing the disease progression. We observed a marked accumulation of lactate and phenylalanine reflecting the Warburg effect and impaired hepatic hydroxylation capacity. Conversely branched chain amino acids specifically valine and isoleucine were significantly depleted in the disease groups indicating systemic metabolic stress. Receiver operating characteristic analysis revealed that a combinatorial biomarker panel yielded superior diagnostic accuracy compared to single markers. Furthermore, the validity of our metabolic profiling was corroborated by a strong correlation between the values predicted from metabolic profiles and clinically measured physiological parameters. Overall, our findings confirm the feasibility of utilizing retrospective biobank resources for high resolution metabolic phenotyping.Keywords: NMR-based metabolomics, Biobank, Cirrhosis, Liver cancer, Diagnostic markers

INTRODUCTION

The incidence and mortality rates of most cancers are decreasing, however hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a type of liver cancer, exhibits a gradual increasing trend and remains among the cancers with the highest global incidence and mortality rates (Abboud et al., 2024; Amini et al., 2022; Benson et al., 2021; Ozakyol, 2017; Sedeta et al., 2023). The five-year relative survival rate for HCC (2015-2019) is 37.7%, a statistic that notably contrasts with the overall cancer survival rate of 70.7% and represents approximately half the average survival rate observed across all cancers (Kang et al., 2022). Cirrhosis constitutes the most critical risk factor for the development of HCC and serves as the primary physiological foundation for the malignancy in the most patients. Epidemiological evidence consistently demonstrates that the presence of cirrhosis dramatically elevates the susceptibility to liver cancer, leading to a markedly higher incidence rate compared to the non-cirrhotic population (Llovet et al., 2021). Because cirrhosis is the predominant precursor, the cohort requiring clinical surveillance for HCC is relatively well-defined. Consequently, early detection through rigorous surveillance of these high-risk individuals is paramount to improving dismal survival outcomes. Under the Korean National Health Insurance system, screening for liver cancer is targeted specifically at hepatitis virus carriers and patients diagnosed with liver cirrhosis, prioritizing abdominal ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) testing (Jang and Park, 2020; Korean Liver Cancer Association (KLCA) and National Cancer Center (NCC) Korea, 2023). However, the persistent low survival rates suggest limitations in the sensitivity of current screening modalities for early-stage detection. Therefore, there is a critical demand for novel and accessible diagnostic biomarkers capable of detecting HCC during its earliest onset within the cohort requiring clinical surveillance.

To address this challenge, we utilized resources from the National Biobank of Korea, established in 2012 (Cho et al., 2012). The South Korean government operates one of the most robust human resource banking systems globally (Park et al., 2013), supported by the National Health Insurance (NHI) system, which boasts an enrollment rate of 99.4% as of 2021 according to the Korean Statistical Information Service (KOSIS) (Kim et al., 2020; Korean Statistical Information Service, 2021; Shin, 2019). Biobank samples serve as an invaluable resource for disease research by providing access to clinically relevant patient specimens (Madssen et al., 2019; Paskal et al., 2018). In practice, direct access to patient-derived samples offers significant advantages over in vitro models in terms of translational research efficiency. Numerous studies have successfully utilized biobank resources to identify blood biomarkers for pancreatic cancer (Fest et al., 2019), as well as for dementia and multi-disease outcome research (Buergel et al., 2022; Carrasco-Zanini et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024). Building on this translational utility, we focused on the metabolic characteristics of the liver because it serves as the central organ of homeostasis and metabolism, where metabolic reprogramming is recognized as a hallmark of HCC pathogenesis (Park and Hall, 2025). Metabolites, the end products of these cellular regulatory processes, provide a direct readout of physiological and pathological states, offering a distinct advantage over upstream genomic or transcriptomic changes (Johnson et al., 2016; Patti et al., 2012; Rinschen et al., 2019). Since blood circulates through the liver, capturing systemic metabolic alterations, it represents the ideal biofluid for accessible and non-invasive monitoring of liver health (Beyoglu and Idle, 2013; von Felden et al., 2020).

To achieve high-throughput profiling with rigorous reproducibility, which is a critical requirement for validation in large-scale cohorts, we utilized Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Vignoli et al., 2019). While various platforms exist, NMR is particularly well-suited for biobank research as it allows for the quantitative analysis of biofluids with minimal sample preparation and no sample destruction, thereby preserving the integrity of precious clinical specimens (Nagana Gowda and Raftery, 2019).

By integrating the extensive resources of the National Biobank of Korea with this robust NMR-based metabolomics approach, we aim to comprehensively profile the blood metabolome to identify a panel of metabolic biomarkers capable of distinguishing liver cancer from cirrhosis, thereby addressing the unmet clinical need for improved differential diagnosis in patients with chronic liver disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection and storage

Human blood samples, including both liver disease patients and healthy controls, were procured from the Biobank of Ajou University Hospital. The cohort consisted of 80 total samples: 20 normal plasma, 20 cirrhosis plasma, 20 normal serum, and 20 liver cancer serum. The use of different blood matrices such as plasma for the cirrhosis group and serum for the liver cancer group was primarily dictated by the availability of high-quality retrospective specimens specifically curated for each disease cohort within the national repository. As this study aimed to validate the feasibility and robustness of utilizing existing biobank resources for high-resolution metabolic phenotyping, we utilized these diverse specimens to identify biomarkers that transcend pre-analytical variability. Collected specimens were transferred to cryovials and stored at -80°C or in liquid nitrogen (LN2) tanks within the biobank facility. For laboratory transport, samples were packed in dry ice to preserve metabolic stability. To ensure the integrity of the metabolome, freeze-thaw cycles were strictly minimized prior to NMR-based metabolomics measurements.

Sample extraction and NMR preparation

For metabolite extraction, serum and plasma samples were thawed completely on ice. A 300 µL aliquot of each sample was mixed with 600 µL of ice-cold methanol (sample:methanol=1:2, v/v) to facilitate protein precipitation. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly to ensure complete homogenization prior to centrifugation. Following the removal of the protein pellet, 400 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a SpeedVac concentrator for partial vacuum concentration. After 4 h, an additional 400 µL of the supernatant was added to the same tube to prevent bumping, and the samples were concentrated overnight. The dried extracts were stored at −20°C until NMR analysis.

Prior to NMR spectral acquisition, each dried sample was reconstituted in 500 µL of NMR buffer. The buffer solution consisted of D₂O containing 0.025% sodium 3-trimethylsilylpropionic acid (TSP, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) as an internal chemical shift reference, supplemented with 5 mM NaH₂PO₄ and 2 mM Na₂HPO₄ for pH stabilization. Finally, the reconstituted samples were transferred to 5-mm NMR tubes for spectral acquisition.

NMR data acquisition and processing

1H NMR spectra were acquired at 27°C using an 850 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker AVANCE III HD) equipped with a cryogenic probe at the National Center for Inter-University Research Facilities (Seoul National University). To suppress the water signal, a NOESY experiment with water presaturation (noesy1dpr pulse sequence) was utilized. Acquisition parameters included a spectral width (SW): 15.9804 ppm along the 1H dimensions; O1P=4.697 ppm; number of scan (NS)=128; relaxation delay time=2 s; and mixing time=0.01 s. Spectral processing was performed using Mnova software (Mestrelab Research), including phase correction and baseline subtraction, and all spectra were referenced to the TSP signal at 0 ppm. Processed spectra were exported as ASCII files, and water-related peaks (4.71-5.1 ppm) were removed. Binning, normalization, and conversion were performed using custom-developed code written in Perl-based script. To ensure the accuracy of the metabolic profiling and to minimize potential interference from different sample matrices, spectral regions containing anticoagulant signals in plasma samples were strictly excluded. Specifically, the regions corresponding to EDTA signals at 2.53 to 2.58 ppm, 3.06 to 3.22 ppm, and 3.58 to 3.64 ppm were removed from the bucketed data prior to multivariate statistical analysis. Metabolite identification and quantification were carried out using Chenomx (Edmonton, AB, Canada) in conjunction with an in-house NMR spectrum database.

Statistical analysis

Multivariate statistical analyses including PLS-DA and OPLS-DA were performed using MATLAB version R2021b with in-house codes. To identify significant metabolic variables contributing to the group separation, S-plots were generated from the OPLS-DA models using the same MATLAB environment. The diagnostic performance of identified markers was evaluated via ROC curve analysis using Prism (version 9.0.0, GraphPad Software), and the corresponding AUC values were calculated to determine predictive power. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were employed to determine significance (p<0.05). Pearson correlation analysis was utilized to assess relationships between NMR signals and clinical blood parameters.

The most prominent clinical feature was the significant elevation of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in both disease patients compared to healthy controls. In contrast, while gamma-glutamyl transferase (rGT) showed an upward trend, it did not reach statistical significance as the 95% confidence intervals (CI) overlapped with the zero reference line. Metabolic profiles and multivariate statistical analysis of liver disease

High-resolution 850 MHz 1H NMR spectra were acquired to identify differences in metabolic profiles between liver disease patients and healthy controls. These spectra provided high-resolution metabolic snapshots where characteristic signals of key metabolites were clearly identified, such as the doublet of lactate at 1.33 ppm and the multiplet of phenylalanine in the region of 7.32 to 7.44 ppm. Representative 1D proton NMR spectra were shown in Fig. 1B and 1C for the cirrhosis and liver cancer, respectively. The specific metabolites of the samples were identified according to their chemical shift values and summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

To further evaluate these metabolic variations, Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was performed for each disease state. The cirrhosis model demonstrated a clear multivariate separation from the control group with high reliability and predictive power, as indicated by an R2Y of 0.892 and a Q2Y of 0.562 (Fig. 1D). Similarly, the liver cancer group showed a distinct metabolic distribution compared to the normal group, with comparable statistical performance (R2Y=0.866 and Q2Y=0.588; Fig. 1E). While some overlap was observed between the groups, the overall distribution confirmed a significant shift in the global metabolic profile of disease patients.

Identification of metabolic markers via S-plot analysis

To identify the specific metabolites responsible for the discrimination observed in the PLS-DA models (Fig. 1D, 1E), Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to generate S-plots for each disease state (Fig. 2). These plots represent the modeled covariance (p[1]) and correlation (p(corr)[1]) simultaneously, providing a visual assessment of the reliability of potential biomarkers.

Visualization of metabolic markers in liver disease through S-plot based on OPLS-DA. S-plots were generated OPLS-DA model-based covariance, p[1], and correlation, p(corr)[1], for (A) Cirrhosis and (B) Liver cancer groups compared to their respective healthy controls.

The S-plot distribution revealed that the metabolic separation in both cirrhosis and liver cancer is characterized by subtle but systemic shifts across a broad range of metabolites, rather than being driven by a few isolated, high-impact markers. This “metabolic profile shift” implies that the pathological signature stems from the cumulative impact of multiple variables. Despite their distributed positions on the correlation axis, certain metabolites exhibited highly consistent concentration changes across samples. Consequently, these candidates were prioritized for further individual validation to assess their independent diagnostic potential.

Diagnostic performance and validation of metabolic markers via ROC analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic reliability of the candidate markers identified from the global metabolic profile, the relative amounts of the markers in the two groups were compared using univariate analysis. Consistent with their roles in the overall metabolic rewiring, all selected markers showed statistical significance with p-values of less than 0.05 obtained from the Student’s t-test. In the plasma samples, the normalized peak areas for lactate and phenylalanine were significantly higher in the cirrhosis group compared to the healthy controls, as shown in the bar graphs (Fig. 3A).

(A) Normalized peak areas for lactate and phenylalanine show a significant elevation in the cirrhosis group compared to the healthy controls, where statistical significance was determined using the Student’s t-test and is presented with standard deviations. *p<0.05, **p<0.01. (B) ROC curves evaluate the diagnostic performance of these markers, where phenylalanine and lactate yield AUC values of 0.798 and 0.708 respectively. The multiple ROC analysis combining both markers results in an increased AUC of 0.830, which indicates a more robust diagnostic signature for cirrhosis compared to the individual metabolites.

To determine the clinical utility of these increased intensities, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate how well these two metabolites predicted cirrhosis versus the normal group (Fig. 3B). In the plasma samples used for the cirrhosis group, phenylalanine exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.798, accompanied by a sensitivity of 0.65 and specificity of 0.85. Lactate followed with an AUC of 0.708, showing a sensitivity of 0.75 and a specificity of 0.70. Notably, the multiple ROC analysis combining both markers resulted in an increased AUC of 0.830, which outperformed the diagnostic value of either marker used alone and confirmed that the combined intensity of these markers provides a more robust diagnostic signature (Fig. 3B).

Similar diagnostic trends were observed in the serum samples for the liver cancer group, where the metabolic shifts identified in the multivariate statistical models were validated through both univariate and ROC analyses. The normalized peak areas for the four key metabolites exhibited significant alterations between the liver cancer and healthy control groups (Fig. 4A). Specifically, lactate and phenylalanine were significantly elevated in the cancer group with p-values of 0.0005 and 0.0023, respectively. In contrast, the intensities of isoleucine (p=0.0166) and valine (p=0.0148) were significantly higher in the normal group, further characterizing the metabolic divergence associated with malignancy.

To assess the diagnostic potential of these markers, an ROC analysis was performed for each metabolite to evaluate its ability to distinguish liver cancer from the normal state (Fig. 4B). Phenylalanine again demonstrated the highest individual diagnostic accuracy with an AUC value of 0.850, accompanied by a sensitivity of 0.85 and a specificity of 0.75. Lactate yielded an AUC value of 0.765 with a sensitivity of 0.75 and a specificity of 0.90, while isoleucine and valine showed AUC values of 0.763 (sensitivity=0.70, specificity=0.75) and 0.710 (sensitivity=0.60, specificity=0.85), respectively. To determine if a combined metabolic signature could enhance diagnostic performance, a multiple ROC analysis incorporating all four markers was conducted (Fig. 4B). This comprehensive approach resulted in an increased AUC of 0.885, which was higher than the value obtained for any single marker alone. These results suggest that the synergistic use of these four metabolites provides a robust diagnostic model that explains the majority of the metabolic differences between liver cancer and normal healthy states.

Correlation between clinical parameters and global metabolic profiles

To examine whether clinical blood parameters correlated with the total NMR signal in cirrhotic plasma and liver cancer serum, PLS regression analysis was performed. The results showed a significant correlation between the measured clinical levels (dependent variables) and the NMR metabolome profiles (independent variables), suggesting that the global metabolic signature can effectively reflect the secondary diagnostic indicators of liver disease (Fig. 5). In the case of cirrhosis, robust predictive models were established for four key indicators derived from the total NMR signals (Fig. 5A-5D). Specifically, glucose exhibited the strongest correlation with an R value of 0.850 and a Q2 of 0.400, while alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) also showed significant predictive accuracy with R values of 0.630 and 0.550 respectively. Total cholesterol followed a similar trend, yielding a correlation coefficient of 0.430. Similarly, when disease markers and predictive models were evaluated based on liver cancer NMR data, the predictive models for ALT and total cholesterol were identified (Fig. 5E, 5F). The model for ALT demonstrated a significant correlation with an R value of 0.510 and a Q2 of 0.060, whereas the total cholesterol model showed a relatively lower correlation with an R value of 0.260. These findings suggest that in addition to the primary diagnostic markers identified through multivariate analysis, the total NMR metabolic profile provides valuable information for predicting secondary clinical blood levels in liver disease patients.

DISCUSSION

Hepatocellular carcinoma remains a leading cause of cancer related mortality worldwide and typically arises from a background of chronic liver cirrhosis. The early detection of malignant transformation within this high-risk population is critical for improving survival outcomes but relies heavily on surveillance strategies that often suffer from suboptimal sensitivity. Metabolic profiling has emerged as a powerful tool to capture systemic physiological alterations and offers a holistic view of the biochemical changes accompanying disease progression. While national biobanks provide a valuable repository of biological specimens for such investigations the potential of retrospective serum resources to generate robust and clinically relevant metabolic models warrants thorough validation.

In this context, we employed an NMR-based metabolomics approach combined with multivariate statistical analysis to investigate the metabolic signatures associated with cirrhosis and liver cancer using blood resources from the National Biobank of Korea. This study demonstrated significant metabolic differences between these disease states and healthy controls and aimed to validate whether stored retrospective samples could provide robust metabolic profiling comparable to prospective cohorts.

The demographic characteristics of the study population provided the initial clinical context for our metabolomic findings. We observed markedly elevated levels of liver enzymes including ALT, AST, and rGT in the disease groups compared to the normal controls (Fig. 1A). These clinical parameters are indicative of ongoing hepatocellular damage and cholestasis which are hallmarks of chronic liver disease progression. The correlation between these traditional enzymatic markers and the metabolic shifts observed in our study suggests that the metabolic profiles capture the systemic physiological alterations associated with hepatic dysfunction and tumor development.

The PLS-DA models for both cirrhosis and liver cancer successfully differentiated the disease states from the control groups with reliable statistical performance (Fig. 1D, 1E). The distinct clustering observed in the score plots indicated that the metabolic phenotype of patients with cirrhosis and liver cancer diverges significantly from that of healthy individuals. However, we also observed a minor overlap in the global metabolic landscape between the cirrhosis and cancer groups. This overlapping pattern implies a shared pathological background and suggests that the pathological signature of liver disease manifests as a global metabolic shift rather than isolated molecular events. This finding reinforces the notion that the metabolic discrimination is driven by systemic and moderate level shifts across the entire metabolic network rather than solely by a few dominant markers.

In alignment with the global metabolic rewiring identified in our multivariate models the specific accumulation of lactate and phenylalanine provides a biochemical readout of compromised hepatic integrity (Fig. 3A, 4A). Lactate historically viewed merely as a metabolic waste product is now recognized as a critical fuel source and epigenetic modulator driven by the Warburg effect in tumorigenesis (Torrini et al., 2022). We propose that its systemic elevation in our cohort reflects a dual pathology arising from the combination of accelerated glycolytic flux in the tumor and impaired hepatic elimination. Since the clearance of lactate represents a fundamental physiological function of the liver the compromise of this capacity in the diseased state directly contributes to the accumulation of circulating lactate (Jeppesen et al., 2013).

Similarly, the accumulation of phenylalanine serves as a surrogate marker for hepatocellular dysfunction and hemodynamic alteration (Fig. 3A, 4A). Under normal physiological conditions, the liver regulates phenylalanine levels via hydroxylation, so its elevation in the disease groups suggests a significant decline in hepatic metabolic reserve. Furthermore, this accumulation is likely exacerbated by the presence of portosystemic shunting that allows blood to bypass hepatic filtration, thereby preventing the metabolic processing of aromatic amino acids (Holecek, 2010). This observation aligns with established evidence that circulating aromatic amino acids are markedly elevated in patients with advanced cirrhosis, validating phenylalanine as a robust indicator of disease severity (Holecek, 2010).

Conversely, the metabolic footprint of the disease was characterized by a distinct depletion of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), specifically valine and isoleucine (Fig. 4A). This inverse correlation aligns with prospective evidence suggesting that systemic BCAA deficiency acts as a metabolic stressor promoting hepatocellular carcinoma progression (Tajiri and Shimizu, 2018). Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that BCAA supplementation significantly reduces the risk of liver cancer occurrence and recurrence, supporting the hypothesis that the depletion observed in our cohort reflects a critical loss of chemopreventive metabolic capacity (Sideris et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2022). Regarding the use of different blood matrices, we addressed potential concerns about the interference of anticoagulant signals such as EDTA peaks with the identified metabolic markers. We confirmed that our key biomarkers including lactate at 1.33 and 4.09 ppm, phenylalanine at 7.32 to 7.44 ppm, and branched-chain amino acids are located in spectral regions that do not overlap with the characteristic EDTA signals at 2.51 and 3.13 ppm. The identification of these consistent metabolic signatures across both serum and plasma matrices further supports the biological robustness of our findings and suggests that the underlying metabolic shifts reflect the disease pathology rather than pre-analytical artifacts. To evaluate the clinical utility of these metabolic alterations, we performed ROC analysis and assessed the diagnostic performance of the identified biomarkers. The results indicated that combining multiple biomarkers yielded superior diagnostic accuracy with higher AUC values compared to the use of single markers alone (Fig. 3B, 4B). These findings highlight the potential clinical utility of our metabolite panel as a strategic complement to current diagnostic standards. While alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) remains the primary biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance, its clinical application is often limited by suboptimal sensitivity in detecting early-stage malignancies or distinguishing cancer from advanced cirrhosis. Our identified panel consisting of phenylalanine, lactate, isoleucine, and valine provides a comprehensive biochemical profile that captures systemic metabolic shifts often overlooked by protein-based markers. We envision that integrating this NMR-based metabolic framework into routine screening protocols could offer clinicians additional evidence for decision-making, particularly in cases where AFP levels are ambiguous or imaging results are inconclusive. Since the depletion of branched-chain amino acids and the accumulation of lactate reflect fundamental metabolic reprogramming during the early stages of pathogenesis, this multi-marker approach holds significant promise for improving early detection and intervention strategies in high-risk populations. Furthermore, the validity of our NMR-based metabolic profiling was corroborated by the correlation analysis between the predicted values and the clinically measured parameters. We observed a significant positive correlation between the predicted and measured concentrations of physiological indicators such as ALT, AST, and total cholesterol (Fig. 5). This strong association supports that our NMR quantification accurately reflects the systemic characteristics and physiological state of the human body.

The clinical relevance of our findings is reinforced by the intrinsic capacity of blood sample NMR profiling to capture systemic physiological perturbations. The choice of blood as a sample source is particularly advantageous because plasma and serum contain a diverse array of exogenous and endogenous metabolites that can be sampled through a simple liquid biopsy with a relatively low burden on the patient (Wilmanski et al., 2019). Although this study utilized retrospective biobank-derived resources, the high predictive accuracy (Q2>0.5) and robust separation observed in our multivariate models demonstrate that archival samples retain sufficient metabolic fidelity to resolve disease specific signatures. This validates the feasibility of utilizing stored biological repositories for high-resolution metabolic phenotyping. despite the limitations often associated with retrospective cohorts (Liu et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023).

Moreover, the operational scalability of NMR spectroscopy positions it as a strategic complement to current diagnostic modalities. Conventional markers such as AFP often face limitations in sensitivity and specificity, particularly when differentiating hepatocellular carcinoma from cirrhotic background (Hu et al., 2022). In this context, the NMR-based assessment of metabolic profile offers a rapid and high throughput screening mechanism that can capture the complex clinical phenotype. Such a holistic metabolomics approach provides a more comprehensive assessment compared to traditional single marker assays and holds promise for improving the early detection and monitoring of hepatocellular carcinoma in high risk populations. Rather than replacing imaging or histological validation, this metabolomics framework provides a comprehensive biochemical layer to the diagnostic algorithm, potentially enhancing decision-making in clinical settings where traditional methods yield ambiguous results.

While this study successfully underscores the diagnostic potential of NMR-based metabolomics, several limitations warrant consideration. The use of different blood matrices, specifically plasma for the cirrhosis group and serum for the liver cancer group, introduces potential pre-analytical variability regarding EDTA interference in specific spectral regions (Vignoli et al., 2022). However, the identification of consistent disease associated metabolic patterns across these differing matrices supports the biological robustness of our findings and suggests that the underlying metabolic shift is sufficiently potent to transcend methodological variances. Additionally, as our study was conducted exclusively within a Korean cohort utilizing retrospective biobank resources the influence of region-specific dietary habits and genetic backgrounds cannot be fully ruled out. Therefore, future longitudinal studies involving multiethnic populations are therefore necessary to validate the generalizability of these metabolic signatures.

In conclusion, this study validates the utility of biobank derived resources in uncovering the systemic metabolic rewiring associated with chronic liver disease. The concurrent elevation of lactate and phenylalanine coupled with the critical depletion of branched-chain amino acids delineates a distinct pathological signature reflecting the comprehensive failure of hepatic homeostasis and the loss of chemopreventive metabolic capacity. The integrated metabolic landscape, highlighting the diagnostic performance and key metabolite shifts observed in this study, is summarized in Fig. 6. By demonstrating that these holistic signatures can be reliably detected even in retrospective samples, we provide a foundation for integrating metabolic profiling into routine clinical surveillance to facilitate early intervention and improve patient outcomes.
