Care complexity and inequities in caregiving time for undiagnosed infants and toddlers with special health care needs: a cross-sectional study in Japan
Authors: Tomo Nonoyama, Koji Tamakoshi, Kazuteru Niinomi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfants and toddlers with complex medical needs often rely on consistent caregiving and health services. However, when such children remain undiagnosed, their needs may be overlooked by health and social support systems, leading to unequal access to care. This study aimed to explore how caregiving time differs according to the complexity of health care needs among children younger than 3 years of age in Japan, with particular attention to those without formal diagnoses.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional online survey of caregivers raising children younger than 3 years of age in Japan (N = 296). Participants were categorized into three groups based on responses to the Children with Special Health Care Needs (CSHCN) Screener: more complex needs, less complex needs, and non-CSHCN. We examined the distribution and duration of caregiving activities, including medical care, rehabilitation, administrative coordination, commuting, and daily care.ResultsTwenty children were classified as having more complex needs despite lacking formal diagnoses. This group exhibited significantly higher time burdens related to rehabilitation, institutional coordination, and hospital visits than non-CSHCN, with minimal time spent on typical childrearing. The caregiving pattern revealed disparities in support and highlighted a high degree of unmet needs in the undiagnosed population.ConclusionChildren with complex medical needs who lack a diagnosis experience substantial caregiving burden that are often unrecognized by existing support systems. Addressing diagnostic barriers and expanding need-based support frameworks is essential for ensuring equitable caregiving conditions and child well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02655-4.Keywords: Caregiving burden, Health equity, Undiagnosed children, Special health care needs, Early childhood, Japan, Complex medical needs

Background

Children with special health care needs (CSHCN) require more health care services than other children, and the impact of their special health care needs on their own well-being, family, and health care system is substantial [1, 2]. CSHCN are defined as “children who have or are at a high risk of chronic physical, developmental, behavioral, or emotional disabilities and who require health-related services of a type or amount beyond those typically needed by children” [3], and they have health care service needs related to their daily lives irrespective of the type of disease or disability [4]. Among them, more medically complex CSHCN (more complex CSHCN) require more medical care than do children otherwise, have more complex service needs, and are chronically under the care of multiple specialists [5, 6].

The needs and medical complexity of CSHCN vary [7], and it is intentionally defined broadly; a subset of CSHCN that has been highlighted as one comprising children with the most medically vulnerable and complex needs children are children with medical complexity (CMC) [8]. Families of CMC are responsible for a considerable amount of care, and they must be provided with a generous support system [9]. CMC are defined on the basis of the diagnosis of one or more chronic diseases and a high rate of use of medical resources. Only approximately 1% of children fall into this category, referring to children with extremely severe needs [8, 10, 11].

It is important to note that CSHCN and CMC are not synonymous. The CSHCN terminology is intentionally broad and includes children with any type of complex health-related needs, regardless of diagnosis or specific condition, whereas CMC refers to a smaller subset characterized by multiple chronic conditions, functional limitations, and high health care use [3, 8]. We use the term “more complex CSHCN” to describe children who screened positively for multiple functional consequences on the CSHCN Screener, as a proxy for higher complexity health care. This group may overlap with CMC group but is identified through caregiver-reported needs rather than clinical diagnosis. Thus, the terms are not synonymous and have different definitions. Additionally, although social factors, e.g., poverty or abuse, may contribute to health risks, they are not included in the formal definition of CSHCN [3, 4]. Previous studies focusing on more complex CSHCN have shown that these children require care that combines multiple prescription medications, regular rehabilitation services related to functional limitations, and coordination among multiple agencies. However, families often report insufficient access to care coordination and respite care services [12, 13]. Moreover, they frequently experience various health issues (physical problems, such as worsening physical conditions, and developmental issues, such as speech), and the impact of this condition on families includes increased psychological stress, increased financial burden, and reduced labor force participation of caregivers, which places a substantial burden on family functioning and quality of life [14].

Many individuals have intellectual disability, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD)-related needs [6]. However, the age at diagnosis of ADHD and ASD, which are included in the aforementioned diseases and disorders, is approximately 5 years [15] (see also Eiland & Brooke 2024). This diagnostic delay may obscure the complexity of needs in infants and toddlers, creating blind spots in research and policy. Consequently, young children with complex needs—but without formal diagnoses—are at an increased risk of exclusion from support systems designed for diagnosed populations.

The CSHCN Screener has been extensively validated and implemented in large-scale epidemiological studies. Bethell et al. first developed and validated it, reporting high reliability and practical feasibility in diverse populations [16]. Subsequently, the United States (U.S.) National Survey of Children with Special Health Care Needs employed the CSHCN Screener across all states between 2000 and 2002, estimating a national prevalence of 15–20% and identifying significant disparities in access to care, especially for low-income and uninsured populations [17]. More recently, Ghandour et al. analyzed 2016–2019 National Survey of Children’s Health data and reported that 19.4% of U.S. children younger than 18 years of age met CSHCN criteria; these children were nearly four times more likely to experience unmet healthcare needs, including those for mental health, dental, and specialty services [18]. These robust findings support two key hypotheses for our study: (1) a substantial proportion of undiagnosed infants and toddlers will screen as “more complex CSHCN”; and (2) their caregivers will spend disproportionately more time on medical and coordination-related care than caregivers of non-CSHCN peers.

In Japan, the concept of CSHCN has only recently gained attention in policy and research. A previous study reported that 12.5% of children in the Tokyo Teen Cohort met the CSHCN criteria, and their mothers showed significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression due to a lack of social support compared with their counterparts [19]. Meanwhile, children requiring daily medical procedures, e.g., suctioning, oxygen therapy, or enteral feeding are officially referred to as “children requiring medical care” (iryōteki keaji), a group overlapping with CMC. A nationwide study demonstrated a marked increase in such children over the past decade, particularly among those younger than 2 years of age [20], highlighting the increasing demand for long-term, complex care. Furthermore, another study reported that although care coordination places a significant burden on caregivers, only 22% reported receiving such support, underscoring a gap in service access [21]. While the Act on Support for Children Requiring Medical Care and their Families was enacted in 2021 to institutionalize care, eligibility is limited to those receiving specific procedures [22]. Thus, many children with high but undefined or undiagnosed needs remain excluded from formal support systems. This reinforces the importance of screening approaches that identify children on the basis of functional care needs, not just diagnostic status. Therefore, our study fills this policy and research gap by examining undiagnosed but high-need infants and toddlers in Japan using the CSHCN Screener as a functional tool to assess caregiving burden.

We conducted a population-based study of Japanese infants and toddlers without a confirmed diagnosis, aiming to identify children with medically complex needs and assess the caregiving burden experienced by their families. This study specifically sought to (1) estimate the prevalence of undiagnosed, more complex CSHCN; (2) explore the extent and type of their special health care needs; and (3) examine differences in caregiving time by need complexity.

Research questions

What is the prevalence of undiagnosed infants and toddlers with more complex special health care needs in typical Japanese households?

What are the characteristics and distribution of special health care needs among this population?

How does the time spent on caregiving differ on the basis of the level of need complexity in the absence of a diagnosis?

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was based on an online survey and explored how caregiving time differs according to the complexity of health care needs among children younger than 3 years of age in Japan. This study focused on children aged 0–3 years, a period during which many health-related needs emerge without formal diagnosis. Targeting this group enabled us to identify early signs of complexity and caregiving needs before such children enter systems designed around diagnosed conditions. This age definition also aligns with the conventional categorization of “infants and toddlers” used in research and policy contexts in Japan.

Study setting

Participants were sampled from the Tokai-Hokuriku region of central Japan, specifically the prefectures of Aichi, Gifu, Mie, Toyama, Ishikawa, and Fukui. The region includes the Nagoya metropolitan area and rural municipalities, providing a mix of urban and non-urban living environments. Pediatric services are delivered through community clinics and general hospitals with referral networks to tertiary centers under Japan’s universal health insurance. Focusing on this mixed urban–rural region enabled us to capture diversity in family contexts while keeping service systems broadly comparable.

Participants

In this study, responses were obtained regarding the youngest child in an online survey of parents raising children aged ≤ 3 years in the Tokai-Hokuriku region of Japan.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 [23]. Based on the assumption that the distribution of care time would be compared among three groups, the more complex CSHCN, less complex CSHCN, and non-CSHCN groups, which were classified according to the special health care needs of the child, the sample size was calculated to be 159 (F-tests, effect size f = 0.25, α error = 0.05, power = 0.8, number of groups = 3). We adopted this sample size, and considering the possibility of missing values and other factors, set a target size of 200 cases.

The participants were randomly selected from the web panel of Macromill, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), a major Japanese marketing research firm with a nationwide web panel of over 1 million registered users, stratified by demographic and regional characteristics. It was chosen owing to its capacity to provide a randomized and demographically representative sample within the Tokai-Hokuriku region, ensuring diversity in age, sex, and household composition. The survey was conducted from August to September 2024 and targeted parents who met the following criteria based on the registration information on the web panel.

Although participants were recruited from a web panel, we minimized potential biases by randomly sampling from a large pool of panelists in the Tokai-Hokuriku region, which includes urban and rural areas. The recruitment was stratified by the age of children to obtain a balanced sample across the 0–3-year range. While Internet panel surveys inherently carry risks of selection bias due to factors such as voluntary participation and differential digital access, a prior study in Japan reported that, for certain sociodemographic variables, the characteristics of Internet survey respondents did not substantially deviate from those of respondents to traditional paper-based surveys or national census data [24]. This suggests that when appropriately designed and interpreted with caution, web-based methods may provide useful insights even in health-related population studies.

Although the survey was conducted only in Japanese, all panel participants, including any foreign residents, had registered with the web survey platform in Japanese and were confirmed to be capable of understanding and responding in Japanese. Thus, the CSHCN Screener and all survey items were administered in Japanese only. Regarding medical expenses, the public health insurance system of Japan covers a large proportion of pediatric care costs, including those for foreign residents with appropriate residency and insurance status. However, information on participants’ insurance coverage or out-of-pocket expenditures was not collected in this study.

Data collection

In this study, data were collected using a parent and child sociodemographic data form, the CSHCN Screener, and a unique survey with questions about caregiving time spent.

Parent and child sociodemographic data form

The parent and child sociodemographic data form was developed by the researchers with reference to previous studies [25–28]. The form consisted of questions about the parents’ age, sex, marital status, and household income and the child’s age (in 1-month increments), sex, race, and frequency of changes in physical condition (often, occasionally, not much, little, or none) and whether the child had visited multiple departments of the hospital (in the past year). As the target children were infants and toddlers aged 0–3 years, their ages were collected in 1-month increments rather than in years.

CSHCN Screener

The CSHCN Screener was developed through a national collaborative process as part of the Child and Adolescent Health Measurement Initiative (CAHMI) [29, 30]. It is a standardized, caregiver-reported tool developed in the U.S. to identify children who have complex needs for health care services due to chronic physical, developmental, behavioral, or emotional conditions. It has been widely used in large-scale surveys, e.g., the National Survey of Children’s Health, and was developed in a high-income country with a well-established pediatric healthcare system and high access to care. The CSHCN Screener focuses on the functional consequences of health conditions rather than diagnostic labels, allowing the identification of medically vulnerable children who may not yet have formal diagnoses. The benefits of using it include its diagnostic neutrality, applicability across age groups, and ability to capture diverse health-related needs across populations. In Japan, where early diagnosis of developmental conditions may be delayed, this screener enables the identification of complex care needs in infants and toddlers based on observable caregiver-reported challenges. To enhance cultural appropriateness, the Japanese version of the CSHCN Screener was linguistically translated and contextually adapted through a pilot process with Japanese caregivers and healthcare professionals. The items retained their original structure but were adjusted for cultural clarity. Although no full-scale psychometric validation has yet been published for the Japanese version, previous pilot studies have demonstrated high feasibility and acceptance among Japanese parents [19]. Thus. the use of this screener serves as a culturally adaptable proxy to assess medically complex needs in populations lacking early diagnostic data.

The CSHCN Screener consists of five questions related to the following topics: (1) need/use of prescription medications; (2) need/use of more medical care than usual; (3) limited ability to do things (functional limitations); (4) need/use of occupational, physical, or speech therapy (specialized therapies); and (5) need/use of emotional, behavioral, or developmental treatment/counseling. Parents who answer “yes” to any of the five outcome questions then answer up to two follow-up questions to determine whether the result is “due to a medical, behavioral, or other health condition that has lasted or is expected to last at least 12 months.” If the parent answers “yes” and answers positively to one or more of the follow-up questions, the child is considered to be CSHCN.

More/Less Complex CSHCN

There are many ways to use the CSHCN Screener to classify CSHCN according to the combination of items that correspond to the study’s analysis objectives [14]. In this study, to identify CSHCN with more complex medical needs, we used the CSHCN Screener and referred to previous studies that focused on more complex needs [6, 30]. We distinguished CSHCN who met the following three conditions as more complex CSHCN. We then refer to other CSHCN as less complex CSHCN. (1) They had to have a positive response to the item on the CSHCN Screener indicating “need for more medical care” than usual. (2) They had to respond positively to at least three of the four remaining items on the CSHCN Screener. (3) They had to visit multiple departments in the past 12 months [5, 6].

The more items on the CSHCN Screener that apply to a person, the greater his/her medical needs [14].

Application in the Japanese context

Although no full-scale psychometric validation has yet been published for the Japanese version, previous pilot studies have demonstrated high feasibility and acceptance among Japanese parents [19]. In addition to the pilot study by Kaji et al. [19], the Japanese version of the CSHCN Screener has been applied in prior population-based studies, e.g., Nonoyama et al.’s study [31], which examined caregiving burden among families of infants and toddlers in Japan. Their findings demonstrated the practical feasibility of the screener and its utility in identifying caregiving disparities among undiagnosed children with complex needs. These applications support the cultural relevance and preliminary validity of the Japanese version in the context of early childhood care needs.

Furthermore, the classification of “more complex CSHCN” used is grounded in thresholds and criteria aligned with previous studies [5, 6, 14] and has demonstrated meaningful differentiation in caregiving burden across groups in U.S. and Japanese contexts. Specifically, the use of multiple positive screener items (e.g., functional limitations, therapy needs, and frequent health service use) reflects an empirically supported approach to approximating higher complexity. The significant care time differences observed in Japanese cohorts [31] suggest that these cutoffs effectively capture clinically and socially relevant gradations in need, supporting their validity within the Japanese healthcare and social service context.

Care Time

The survey asked caregivers to report the average time spent on each care item in hours per week. In addition to the routine items assumed to be involved in caring for infants and toddlers, the researchers created a survey form that included items related to caring for infants and toddlers with special health care needs based on previous studies [19, 32]. The final items included rehabilitation (including home-visit rehabilitation), therapy other than rehabilitation (including psychological and music therapy by specialists), administrative procedures with other organizations (including correspondence with home-visit facilities and government offices), conferences (including discussions and consultations with professionals regarding child care and treatment), commuting to the hospital (including travel time to and from the hospital), taking and giving medicines (including eye drops and inhalation), taking baths, lulling a child to sleep, and meals/breastfeeding. In the same way as in this study, we investigated the time required for care per week, following previous studies that investigated the care burden of CSHCN [6]. We also calculated the total hours, which is the sum of all responses for each item.

The care-related questions asked respondents to reflect on the most recent typical week at the time of survey participation from August to September 2024. This time frame was chosen to minimize recall bias and to capture current caregiving patterns. The aim of these questions was to quantify the actual caregiving workload experienced by families across diverse activities, including basic childrearing and medically related tasks. These items were designed to align with prior research on caregiving burden for CSHCN and were reviewed by experts in pediatric nursing to ensure their relevance and clarity. Regarding the CSHCN Screener, each of the five main items and their follow-up questions were administered using structured response formats identical to those used in the original instrument. Specifically, respondents were first asked whether the child had any of the five health-related needs, followed by questions about whether the need was caused by a condition expected to last ≥12 months. The questions were delivered through a branching logic format embedded in the online survey system. The complete list of caregiving time questions is provided in Additional File 1.

Analysis of data

The data obtained as a result of this study were subjected to statistical testing and are presented as numbers, percentages, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was used to determine whether each variable had a normal distribution, and nonparametric testing was performed in accordance with non-normal distributions. Caregiving time variables were not normally distributed, with many items showing skewness and a high proportion of zero values.

For all descriptive statistics for parents and children, frequencies and percentages are presented for the overall group and the more complex, less complex, and non-CSHCN groups. For the continuous variables collected, parent age, child age, median, and IQRs are presented.

Chi-squared or Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted to compare the distribution of variables among the three groups (more complex CSHCN, less complex CSHCN, and non-CSHCN). Additionally, for the two groups (more complex and less complex CSHCN), the frequencies and percentages of the number of applicable items for each Screener item and number of applicable children are shown. The distribution of the number of people who met each screening item was compared using the chi-squared test.

Subsequently, to compare the distribution of care time in the three groups, the frequency and percentage of each group with non-zero (i.e., requiring care) time for each care item are shown, and the median and IQR of each care item are calculated. The chi-squared and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare distributions among the three groups.

Furthermore, to compare the care time of the three groups while considering the covariates, Quade’s nonparametric analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with care time as the dependent variable and adjusted for sex, age (continuous variable), race, and parental marital status based on clinical perspectives and previous studies [33]. This method was selected because it is robust to violations of normality assumptions and allows for covariate adjustment in ranked data, making it suitable for skewed outcome variables, e.g., caregiving time. The Quade nonparametric ANCOVA was used because the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated non-normal distributions for caregiving time, and standard ANCOVA assumptions were not met. This method adjusts for covariates by ranking the dependent variable and analyzing residual differences between groups. The analysis was conducted using the built-in procedure available in SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Finally, a post-hoc test using the Bonferroni correction was performed to compare each pair of the three groups.

The dependent variable was the total number of caregiving hours per week as reported by parents. The independent variable was the classification of the child’s health care needs (categorized into more complex CSHCN, less complex CSHCN, and non-CSHCN). The covariates included the child’s sex, age (continuous, in months), race (Japanese/other), and parental marital status (married/unmarried or other). These covariates were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and prior literature on caregiving burden in families with CSHCN [6, 33]. For instance, previous studies have shown that caregiving demands may vary depending on the child’s age and sex, while parental marital status has been linked to variations in available support and stress levels [33]. Race was included to account for potential differences in access to care or cultural caregiving practices, although the population was homogeneous. Adjusting for these factors allows for a more accurate assessment of the association between need complexity and caregiving time. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0). Statistical significance was set at p <.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, which strengthen the reporting of epidemiological studies [34, 35]. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Review Committee of the Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (approval no. 2024-0136-2).

For parents who fully met the criteria for participation, the research explanation document and consent form were presented on the website, and a survey was conducted for those who provided consent via the consent form online. The research explanation document and consent form indicated that participation was voluntary, that consent could be withdrawn at any time, that personal information would not be disclosed to comply with the “principle of confidentiality,” and that data would be stored strictly.

Results

Of these, data from 16 participants who did not meet the criteria at the time of response (responded that their child was 4 years old or had a clear underlying disease) were excluded, and the final analysis included 296 responses.

In terms of the children, males accounted for 53.4% of the total, slightly more than females, with a median age of 20 months (1 year 8 months) and an IQR of 8–33 months. In terms of race, the proportion of non-Japanese patients was 15.0% in the complex CSHCN group, which was higher than that in the other groups. In terms of the frequency of changes in physical condition, the most common responses were “often” (35.0%) and “occasional” (35.0%) in the more complex CSHCN group, “occasional” (44.4%) in the less complex CSHCN group, and “little or none” (37.9%) in the non-CSHCN group.

The results of the comparison of the distribution among the three groups using the chi-squared or Kruskal–Wallis test showed a significant difference in terms of race and frequency of changes in physical condition. No significant differences were observed for other items.

In the more complex CSHCN group, 15 children (75.0%) met all five screening items. Conversely, in the less complex group, 15 children (41.7%) met only one item. Regarding the applicable items, in the more complex CSHCN group, all children responded that they applied to the three items of need/use of prescription medicines, above-average need/use of medical care, and functional limitations.

In the less complex CSHCN group, 26 (72.2%) individuals were eligible for the need/use of prescription medicines, which was the highest number, followed by 17 (47.2%) who were eligible for an above-average need/use of medical care, which was just under half. When the distribution of the two groups was compared using the chi-squared test, a significant difference was observed in the number of applicable items.

Table 2 shows the results of the comparison of the distribution of care time in the three groups. Regarding the distribution of care time, the proportion of children requiring care and the median care time were both highest in the more complex CSHCN group for rehabilitation, therapy other than rehabilitation, administrative procedures with other organizations, conferences, commuting to hospitals, and taking and giving medicine. However, for taking baths, lulling a child to sleep, and meals/breastfeeding, almost all or all children required care, and the median care time was the largest for the non-CSHCN group and smallest for the more complex CSHCN group.

Discussion

This study, which surveyed infants and toddlers without a clear diagnosis, revealed the proportion of more or less complex CSHCN in this cohort, the extent and content of their special health care needs, and the fact that more complex CSHCN require more time for medical and coordinating care, such as rehabilitation, administrative procedures, and medication, and relatively less time for daily care. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to measure the level of special health care needs and time spent on care in detail for infants and toddlers without a clear diagnosis.

Previous studies have emphasized the need for support systems that prioritize children’s actual health care needs rather than relying solely on formal diagnoses [8, 18]. Moreover, international literature has raised concerns about potential inequities for children who are “undiagnosed but affected,” who are often excluded from formal services due to the absence of a diagnosis. For example, Toomey et al. reported disparities in the unmet need for care coordination among children in the U.S., highlighting that diagnostic status can significantly influence access to essential support services [36]. In contrast to prior reports that primarily targeted school-aged or diagnosed children, our study highlights an underrecognized group of infants and toddlers who, despite lacking a formal diagnosis, exhibit considerable medical complexity and caregiving burden. By quantifying care time and identifying their multi-dimensional needs, this study empirically demonstrates the presence of a hidden population at risk of unmet support in early childhood. This study offers novel, Japan-based, age-specific data to the global discussion on equity in pediatric care and underscores the importance of proactive support mechanisms irrespective of diagnosis.

In this cohort of infants and toddlers, the overall prevalence of CSHCN was 18.9%, and the prevalence of more complex CSHCN was 6.8%. A large-scale U.S. survey by Ghandour et al. reported a prevalence of CSHCN of approximately 19% among children younger than 18 years of age [18], aligning with our findings. Another study by Yu et al., which used national survey data from 2016 to 2019, further classified CSHCN into subgroups—CMC (1.6%), CSHCN with significant disabilities (5.8%), and other CSHCN (13.1%)—providing a useful comparison point for our classification of more complex CSHCN [37].

The proportion of patients with CSHCN in this study was similar to that reported in previous studies. In addition, the proportion of more complex CSHCN was similar to the combined proportion of CSHCN with significant disabilities and CMC in previous studies (7.4%) [37]. In general, it can be predicted that the proportion of CSHCN will be smaller in studies on children without a diagnosis than in other studies; however, the results of this study contradict this prediction. These findings challenge the assumption that undiagnosed children have milder needs than diagnosed ones and highlight a hidden population at risk of unmet care and social support. In addition, the frequency of changes in physical condition is more frequent when needs are complex due to disabilities [38], and the trend in the frequency of changes in physical condition in the more complex CSHCN group in this study is consistent with this. This trend also supports the possibility that the more complex CSHCN in this cohort had considerably complex needs.

As mentioned above, the complexity of special health care needs may be overlooked in infants and toddlers because of their young age and because their needs are not yet known or clear. In fact, in previous studies, the proportions of CSHCN and CMC were lower, and the proportion of non-CSHCN was higher in younger children (< 5 years) than in older children, although the age distribution of the total population was similar [37]. Although this cohort included only infants and toddlers who had not yet been diagnosed, it included a certain number of more complex CSHCN, suggesting that there are many children with medically complex health care needs among infants and toddlers who do not have clear chronic diseases or significant disabilities. Based on the above, we emphasize that in infants and toddlers, the existence of CSHCN with more complex needs may be overlooked due to the lack of a clear diagnosis of diseases and disabilities.

Our findings support this concern. Consistent with previous research [6, 33], the current study demonstrated that children classified as more complex CSHCN, based on caregiver-reported responses to the CSHCN Screener, exhibited greater caregiving time and more functional needs than less complex CSHCN and non-CSHCN. These differences were statistically significant and observed across multiple care domains. This suggests that the classification criteria used in our study, which incorporated the number and types of unmet needs as well as multispecialty service use, may be appropriate for identifying variations in health care needs and caregiving burden in the Japanese context.

In addition, three-quarters (75%) of the more complex CSHCN identified in this study met al.l five screening items, and the percentage of items met was significantly higher than that for less complex CSHCN. In general, encountering multiple items on a CSHCN Screener indicates a complex situation [39]. Furthermore, caregivers of CSHCN who score high on many items are more likely to face work challenges (reduction in work hours or retirement) and financial challenges due to their child’s condition [14].

Therefore, for the more complex CSHCN in this study, although they did not have a clear diagnosis, they had medically complex needs, and their families may face mental and financial challenges. A previous study suggested that all forms of social support, including support from caregivers, contributed to the health of children with high health care needs and their families [40]. Based on these findings, several specific policy and institutional recommendations should be considered. First, municipalities and health professionals should implement early needs-based screening, e.g., the CSHCN Screener, during routine infant health checkups regardless of diagnostic status. Second, systems for provisional support (e.g., respite care, transportation aid, and care coordination) should be made accessible on the basis of functional care needs rather than on the presence of medical certification. Third, pediatric community health centers should expand their outreach to identify families whose children may not yet meet diagnostic criteria but exhibit high care coordination or medical support needs. These actionable recommendations would ensure early and equitable access to resources.

However, access to various forms of social support may not reach infants and toddlers without a diagnosis or their families. Therefore, it is necessary to provide support that focuses on the substantial challenges faced by families, regardless of whether they have a diagnosis.

In addition, regarding the time spent on care, it was found that the more complex CSHCN spent a large amount of time on medical and coordinating care, such as rehabilitation, therapy other than rehabilitation, administrative procedures with other organizations, conferences, commuting to the hospital, and taking and giving medicine, whereas the non-CSHCN spent a large amount of time on everyday care, such as taking baths, lulling a child to sleep, and meals/breastfeeding. This significant difference was supported by the analysis adjusted for covariates. Furthermore, the final multiple comparison results showed no significant difference in total time. These results are consistent with previous findings on caregiving demands for CMC in Japan. For example, Fuyuki et al. reported that informal caregivers of CMC, including those requiring medical devices, face substantial time burdens related to treatment, transportation, and coordination with care providers [41]. Similarly, Yamaguchi et al. reported that caregiving for such children frequently involves extensive administrative tasks, medical appointments, and other burdensome activities [42]. These prior findings support the plausibility of the caregiving time patterns observed in our study and suggest that the results reflect the actual caregiving challenges faced by families with undiagnosed CSHCN or CMC in Japan.

Previous studies on care time have reported that the more medically complex a child is, the more time it takes for medical care and health care coordination [43]. Considering the aforementioned information, when a child has complex needs, the time spent by the caregiver on medical and coordinating care may be relatively high, and the caregiver may not be able to spend significant time on daily child care.

Miller et al. pointed out that caregivers of children with chronic health conditions (i.e., CSHCN) face considerable burdens, particularly in arranging or coordinating and providing medical care for their children, and advocating for the importance of facilities that support access to medical care [27]. Similarly, Hofacer et al. emphasized that professional care coordination can reduce the financial and time burdens on caregivers [44]. The results of this study extend these findings by comparing details of care. When children have medically complex needs, it is important for them to have more intensive care coordination and professional representation, especially in administrative procedures with specialized agencies. Coordinator intervention is associated with CMC, family free time, and satisfaction [45]. In addition, it is important to provide daily care as needed and ensure more careful and relaxed childcare time, as it is possible that infant caregivers may not have the time to spend on feeding and bathing assistance, which should be prioritized. In the care of infants with more complex needs, contributing to family time through professional support contributes to the fulfillment of their needs.

These findings align with those in the literature documenting cumulative caregiving burdens among families with CSHCN, including reduced caregiver employment and elevated stress levels [6, 14, 44, 45]. However, our study adds novel insight by demonstrating that such burdens are present even among undiagnosed children younger than 3 years of age—highlighting a gap in current diagnostic and support systems. This omission represents a gap in equity, as current supports are largely contingent on diagnosis. Health equity demands the recognition and inclusion of such “invisible” populations in resource allocation and service design.

This study has some notable strengths. First, using a cohort of infants and toddlers without a clear diagnosis has helped promote discussions about the existence and care of children with medically complex special health care needs, although they do not have a clear diagnosis. Second, using outcomes that include not only time related to medical care and care coordination but also routine child care, such as feeding, breastfeeding, and putting children to sleep, has enabled detailed analysis and comparison of care time.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the target cohort comprised infants and toddlers from general households in Japan, and responses were obtained from caregivers who were able to participate in the online survey. Many previous studies on CSHCN analyzed national data from the U.S. Therefore, it is possible that there were some variations in responses. In the future, it will be necessary to accumulate and compare studies on CSHCN and CMC in more diverse cohorts. Second, the items for the care time outcome in this study were refined from previous studies and general care content. It is difficult to cover all the aspects of care for all infants, and there may be factors other than measured needs and adjustment factors. However, no evidence suggests that these potential factors have a significant impact on the analysis. It is important to verify the results of this study by conducting future large-scale surveys. Third, this study has limitations related to the use of a web-based panel. The participants were drawn from a commercial web panel, which may be subject to selection bias; individuals with higher digital literacy or health awareness may be more likely to register and respond. Although random sampling was applied within the panel, the geographical scope was limited to the Tokai-Hokuriku region, which may not capture regional variations across Japan. Our sample may also underrepresent low-income households without stable Internet access or non-Japanese-speaking caregivers, limiting the generalizability of the findings. To enhance external validity, future studies should consider complementary data sources, e.g., community-based surveys or national cohort data, and employ weighting strategies to better match national demographic distributions. Fourth, as this study relied entirely on caregiver-reported information without verification by medical records, there is a potential for misclassification, particularly regarding undiagnosed conditions or unrecognized needs. To mitigate this risk, we excluded children with any known diagnosis based on parental report and emphasized functional care needs rather than disease names. The use of the CSHCN Screener also helped reduce bias caused by underdiagnosis, especially in very young children. Nonetheless, it remains possible that some children with complex needs were not fully captured, or conversely, that others were misclassified due to over-reporting or under-reporting by caregivers. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, although adjusted analyses accounted for child age, sex, race, and parental marital status, unmeasured and residual confounding (e.g., number of siblings and local service availability) may persist; these variables were not included in the primary adjusted models. Finally, although this study provides a valuable snapshot of caregiving burden among infants and toddlers with special health care needs, it does not capture longitudinal trajectories or changes over time. Future research should explore how caregiving intensity evolves as children age, especially for those whose conditions may later be diagnosed or clarified. Moreover, qualitative investigations into caregivers’ subjective experiences and perceived unmet needs would complement the quantitative data presented here. These future directions are crucial for developing more responsive, family-centered care models that adapt to changing needs in early childhood.

Implications to practice

This study indicated that caregivers of undiagnosed infants and toddlers categorized as more complex CSHCN tend to spend greater amounts of time on medical and care coordination activities than caregivers of non-CSHCN, despite the absence of a formal diagnosis. Although the differences in total caregiving time across groups were not statistically significant after adjusting for covariates, the distinct patterns of caregiving activities identified here highlight a potential gap in current diagnostic-based support systems.

Given these findings, practical and cautious policy recommendations may include the following: integrating caregiver-reported caregiving demands (e.g., medical care and coordination tasks) into routine infant health assessments (such as Japan’s 18-month and 3-year health checkups); considering provisional or flexible eligibility pathways for family support services that do not solely depend on formal medical diagnoses; and exploring the utility of standardized caregiving burden assessments in pediatric care settings. These recommendations should be carefully validated in larger and more diverse populations before widespread implementation, given the exploratory nature and limited regional scope of this study.

Conclusions

This study, using a regional cohort of Japanese infants and toddlers without formal diagnoses, identified a subgroup of children who met criteria for more complex CSHCN based on the CSHCN Screener. These children exhibited multiple indicators of complex care needs, particularly in medical and care coordination domains. While there was no significant difference in total caregiving time across groups after Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons, the composition of caregiving tasks differed by level of complexity. These findings suggest that some undiagnosed children may still experience high caregiving demands and may not be adequately reached by diagnosis-based support systems. To promote health equity, it may be helpful to consider more flexible support strategies that recognize children’s needs irrespective of diagnostic status, especially during early childhood.
