Source‐dependent variability in gut barrier disruption, bacterial translocation, and immune activation after ischemic stroke in Wistar rats
Authors: Cristina Granados‐Martinez, Nuria Alfageme‐Lopez, Manuel Navarro‐Oviedo, Victor Mora‐Cuadrado, David Sevillano‐Fernandez, Luis Alou‐Cervera, Maria Encarnación Fernandez‐Valle, Olivia Hurtado, Maria A Moro, Ignacio Lizasoain, Jesus Miguel Pradillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStroke induces profound neuroinflammation and systemic immune dysregulation, including disturbances in gut homeostasis. Experimental evidence suggests that intestinal barrier permeability (IBP) and bacterial translocation (BT) critically influence stroke outcomes. However, biological variability among commonly used rodent substrains has received limited attention. In this pilot study, we compared poststroke immune responses in two Wistar rat substrains obtained from different suppliers: RccHan (Envigo) and RjHan (Janvier). Naive animals (n = 4) and rats subjected to permanent cerebral ischemia (n = 8 per substrain) were evaluated 72 h after middle cerebral artery occlusion and stratified according to the presence or absence of BT. Immune cell populations in blood and bone marrow were analyzed using flow cytometry, and leukocyte infiltration into ischemic brain tissue was quantified using immunohistochemistry. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Both substrains developed significant infarcts and neurological deficits. RccHan rats exhibited larger infarct volumes and more extensive BT across multiple organs. In contrast, RjHan rats exhibited BT mainly confined to mesenteric lymph nodes but exhibited greater IBP. Although dissemination was broader in RccHan rats, overall bacterial burden was slightly lower compared with RjHan, and extraintestinal bacterial composition differed between groups. Particularly, RjHan rats exhibited stronger systemic and central immune activation, with significant alterations in lymphocyte and monocyte populations and enhanced granulocyte and T‐cell infiltration within ischemic lesions. These findings demonstrate that substrain origin profoundly influences poststroke intestinal barrier integrity, bacterial dissemination, and immune responses considering substrain‐related variability is essential to improve reproducibility and translational relevance in preclinical stroke research.Keywords: bacterial translocation, intestinal barrier, neuroinflammation, stroke, vendor effects, Wistar rats

INTRODUCTION

Ischemic stroke (IS) is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide and represents a major public health challenge. 1 Beyond the initial brain injury induced by arterial occlusion, secondary processes such as excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammation contribute significantly to the progression of damage. 2 , 3 The poststroke inflammatory response is not confined to the central nervous system (CNS): systemic immune activation, secondary immunosuppression, and barrier dysfunction are now recognized as decisive factors in clinical outcome. 4

Within this context, the gut–brain axis has gained increasing attention, as the intestine emerges as a vulnerable target organ after acute brain injury. Cerebral ischemia induces alterations in gastrointestinal motility, microbiota composition, and intestinal barrier permeability (IBP). 5 , 6 , 7 Disruption of epithelial tight junctions facilitates bacterial translocation (BT) and the passage of microbial products into the bloodstream and peripheral organs such as the spleen and liver. 8 , 9 This process increases the risk of poststroke infections and amplifies systemic inflammation, exacerbating secondary brain damage. 10 , 11 Experimental studies have shown that modulation of the microbiota or preservation of intestinal integrity significantly influences infarct size, leukocyte infiltration, and neurological recovery. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15

Particularly, most experimental studies investigating IBP disruption and BT after stroke have utilized mouse models, predominantly using transient ischemia paradigms. In contrast, data in rats, particularly using permanent ischemia models and evaluating vendor‐dependent biological variability, are limited. Moreover, direct comparisons of IBP and BT responses between substrains of the same rat strain obtained from different commercial suppliers have not been reported.

Despite these advances, potential sources of biological variability affecting the gut–brain axis in experimental stroke remain insufficiently explored. The Wistar rat is one of the most widely used strains in preclinical stroke research, particularly in middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) models, due to its availability and reproducibility. 16 However, physiological responses and ischemic outcomes may differ between rat strains or substrains. 17 , 18 , 19 Moreover, animals with the same genetic designation but obtained from different commercial vendors can exhibit significant differences in immune phenotypes, behavior, and gut microbiota composition. 19 , 20 , 21 These so‐called vendor effects represent a significant source of experimental variability that may compromise reproducibility and translational validity. 22 , 23

Janvier Labs and Envigo (currently Inotiv) maintain independent colonies of Wistar rats with distinct breeding histories. Since the original establishment of the Wistar strain in 1906, long‐term separation of breeding nuclei and vendor‐specific husbandry practices have led to accumulated genetic and microbiological divergence. 24 , 25 Consequently, although both are designated as “Wistar,” animals from different vendors are not genetically identical and may differ in biologically relevant traits.

Due to this knowledge gap, the present study was designed to test the hypothesis that vendor‐dependent substrain differences influence poststroke intestinal barrier dysfunction, BT, and associated immune responses. Particularly, we compared IBP, BT patterns, and peripheral and central inflammatory responses after IS in Wistar rats obtained from Janvier and Envigo. By identifying such differences, our aim is to improve experimental reproducibility and to inform appropriate model selection in preclinical stroke research. 26

METHODS

Details of materials and procedures are available in Supplemental Material. Data supporting the findings are available from the corresponding authors upon reasonable request.

Animals

Wistar rats were obtained from two commercial suppliers: Janvier Labs (Wistar stock maintained by Janvier, RjHan:WIS) and Inotiv/Envigo (RccHan:WIST, commonly referred to as “Wistar Han,” maintained from the original Hannover nucleus). All experiments were conducted in male Wistar rats weighing 250–300 g and aged 8–12 weeks. To minimize potential vendor‐related biases, animals were acclimated in our facility for at least 14 days prior to the start of the studies 24 , 25 , 26 and maintained under identical housing conditions (light–dark cycle, diet, bedding type, and environmental enrichment).

All procedures complied with the European Communities Council Directive (86/609/EEC), were approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Welfare of the Complutense University (PROEX no. 305/19 and 219.3/24), and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Animal numbers were minimized using prior experience and statistical tools (http://www.biomath.info), with no observed mortality.

Experimental groups, focal cerebral ischemia, and neurological evaluation

All experiments were randomly assigned and performed under blinded conditions, with investigators unaware of animal substrain, experimental group, and BT status during magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), microbiological, flow cytometry, and histological analyses. The experimental design included naive rats (n = 4 per substrain) and animals subjected to MCAO (n = 8 per experimental group). Based on previous evidence indicating that BT occurs between 48 and 72 h after stroke 7 , 8 and on prior experience with this model, all animals were killed and all analyses were performed at 72 h after experimental IS. After microbiological assessment of peripheral organs, the animals were classified as BT‐positive or non‐BT (NBT).

Focal cerebral ischemia was induced by tandem permanent occlusion of the left common carotid artery (ligation) and the left middle cerebral artery (electrocoagulation). Neurological function was assessed both in naive animals and in MCAO rats at 72 h postsurgery using standardized motor and behavioral scales (see Supplemental Material for details).

Infarct size and IBP determination using MRI

Infarct volume was assessed 72 h post‐IS using T2‐weighted MRI (Bruker 1T). IBP was evaluated 72 h post‐IS in naive and untreated MCAO rats using a noninvasive T1‐weighted abdominal MRI–based protocol with orally administered contrast agents (mannitol and MnCl2·4H2O). This approach was selected for its high translational potential and its applicability to clinical stroke research. Rats were fasted, anesthetized, and monitored during imaging on a Bruker 4.7T system. Intestinal damage was quantified by measuring contrast leakage using ImageJ software.

BT analysis

BT was assessed using microbiological cultures from mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN), liver, spleen, and lungs 72 h postsurgery. Tissues were aseptically collected, homogenized, and plated on selective media under aerobic/anaerobic conditions. Colony‐forming units (CFU) were counted and reported as log10 CFU/g. Bacterial classification followed the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes.

Determination of peripheral and central inflammation

Peripheral inflammation was evaluated using flow cytometry analysis of immune cell populations in blood and bone marrow (BM) from naive and stroke animals at 72 h, with the aim of assessing changes in immune cell distribution, mobilization, and trafficking between these compartments after ischemic injury and BT. Cells were stained with specific antibody panels and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Central inflammation was subsequently assessed using immunofluorescence on brain sections from naive and 72‐h stroke animals to quantify granulocyte and T‐lymphocyte (CD3+) infiltration into the ischemic tissue, thereby completing the evaluation of stroke‐ and BT‐induced immune cell redistribution across peripheral and central compartments. Images were obtained using confocal microscopy and quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation for variables with normal distribution, or as median and interquartile range for nonnormally distributed variables. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups, Student's t‐test was applied for parametric data and Mann–Whitney for nonparametric data. When more than two groups were analyzed, for nonparametric data Kruskal–Wallis test was applied followed by Dunn's post hoc multiple comparisons test. Shannon and Simpson indices were used to evaluate TB diversity across organs. The Shannon index quantified the overall dispersion of TB events among organs, whereas the Simpson index assessed dominance.

All statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software and R (version 4.4.2), and a p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Strain‐dependent differences in infarct volume and BT after IS

Experimental IS induced significant brain infarction in both Wistar substrains analyzed. Quantitative MRI assessment at 72 h post‐ischemia revealed that RccHan rats developed significantly larger infarct volumes compared with RjHan rats (Figure 1A). Both substrains exhibited comparable motor and behavioral impairments at the same time point, with no statistically significant differences between RjHan and RccHan animals in neurological scores (Figure 1B). These findings indicate that, at 72 h after stroke, lesion size and functional outcome are not strictly correlated between substrains.

BT was detected exclusively in ischemic animals and was absent in naive controls. Analysis of microbiological cultures from sterile organs demonstrated clear substrain‐dependent differences in the frequency and anatomical distribution of BT. RccHan rats exhibited a higher incidence of BT and a broader dissemination across multiple organs, including MLN, spleen, liver, and lungs. In contrast, RjHan rats exhibited a more restricted pattern of bacterial dissemination, predominantly confined to the MLN (Figure 1C).

When infarct volume was analyzed according to BT status, no significant differences were observed between BT‐positive and BT‐negative animals within each substrain. However, direct comparison of BT‐positive animals revealed that RccHan rats exhibited significantly larger infarcts than their RjHan counterparts (Figure 1D). Importantly, infarct size was primarily influenced by substrain origin. BT did not further worsen neurological outcome in either substrain, as motor and behavioral scores were similarly impaired in BT‐positive and BT‐negative animals (Figure 1E).

Bacterial dissemination and intestinal barrier integrity

To further characterize BT, both the frequency of organ colonization and the bacterial burden were analyzed in ischemic animals that developed BT. Although the overall proportion of BT‐positive animals did not differ significantly between substrains, distinct patterns of organ‐specific dissemination were observed. In RjHan rats, bacterial growth was most frequently detected in the MLN, whereas colonization of the spleen and liver was relatively uncommon. In contrast, RccHan rats exhibited more frequent bacterial growth in the spleen and liver, indicating broader systemic dissemination. No significant substrain differences were observed in bacterial detection in the lungs (Figure 2A). Analysis of BT distribution across organs using Shannon and Simpson indices revealed a trend toward a more homogeneous dissemination pattern in RccHan rats, whereas RjHan rats displayed a more localized BT distribution (Figure 2B), consistent with organ‐specific bacterial burden analyses.

Quantification of total bacterial load across all analyzed organs showed a lower overall bacterial burden in RccHan rats compared with RjHan rats; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2C). In contrast, organ‐specific analysis demonstrated a significantly higher bacterial load in the MLN of RjHan rats relative to RccHan rats (Figure 2D), consistent with a more localized pattern of bacterial dissemination in this substrain.

Taxonomic analysis of bacteria isolated from extraintestinal organs revealed that both substrains harbored bacterial phyla commonly associated with the gut microbiota, and no significant differences were detected in the overall proportion of gram‐positive versus gram‐negative bacteria. However, significant substrain‐specific differences emerged at the phylum level. In RjHan rats, Bacteroidota represented the predominant phylum, followed by Pseudomonadota. In contrast, Bacteroidota was absent in RccHan rats, in which Pseudomonadota was the dominant phylum. Additionally, RccHan rats exhibited a pronounced reduction in Bacillota and Actinomycetota compared with RjHan animals (Figure 2E).

IBP was assessed using contrast‐enhanced abdominal MRI, focusing on the spleen and MLN to align imaging findings with the organs analyzed for BT. In RjHan rats, ischemic animals with BT exhibited a significant increase in contrast signal intensity in the spleen and a strong trend toward increased signal in the MLN, indicative of barrier disruption. These changes were not observed in RccHan rats, either in BT‐positive or BT‐negative animals (Figure 2F). Together, these findings suggest that RjHan rats are more susceptible to BT‐associated IBP after IS.

Peripheral and central inflammatory responses at 72 h after stroke

Peripheral and central immune responses differed between substrains at 72 h post‐ischemia and were further influenced by the presence of BT. Overall, immune alterations were more pronounced in RjHan rats.

In BM, overall changes were modest; however, RjHan‐BT animals exhibited clearer reductions in monocyte populations and increases in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells compared with RccHan rats, suggesting enhanced immune mobilization (Figure 3A). In peripheral blood, RjHan‐BT rats exhibited significant increases in circulating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells accompanied by a significant reduction in B cells and monocytes compared with naive and non‐BT groups. These alterations were less evident or absent in RccHan animals (Figure 3A,B). When animals were stratified according to BT status within each substrain, BT‐positive RjHan rats exhibited more pronounced alterations in circulating immune cell populations than their non‐BT counterparts, whereas BT‐associated changes were less evident in RccHan rats (Figure 3C).

BT amplified systemic inflammatory responses in both substrains, but this effect was more pronounced in RjHan rats. Because only a single time point was examined, it remains unclear whether RccHan rats develop a delayed inflammatory response or exhibit a generally attenuated immune profile.

Within the ischemic brain, granulocyte infiltration was significantly increased in both substrains because of stroke. However, BT further enhanced granulocyte accumulation only in RjHan rats. Similarly, T‐cell infiltration within the ischemic lesion was observed exclusively in BT‐positive animals of both substrains, with a significantly stronger effect in RjHan rats (Figure 3D,E). These findings indicate that BT modulates both peripheral and central inflammatory responses, with greater sensitivity in RjHan rats.

Overall, these results demonstrate that RjHan rats exhibit a more robust inflammatory phenotype at 72 h after IS, both systemically and within the brain, particularly in the presence of BT.

DISCUSSION

This pilot study was designed as an exploratory step to investigate how IS affects IBP, BT, and their relationship with peripheral and central inflammation, while assessing the impact of biological variability related to vendor‐dependent Wistar rat substrains. Stroke remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, and increasing evidence highlights the critical contribution of systemic immune mechanisms to its pathophysiology and recovery. 1 , 2 , 3 Beyond central inflammatory cascades, 2 , 3 IS induces profound alterations in peripheral immunity, including CNS injury–induced immune deficiency. 4 More recently, IBP, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and BT have emerged as important modulators of poststroke immune responses and outcomes. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 24

In this context, we compared Wistar rats obtained from two widely used commercial suppliers, Janvier (RjHan) and Envigo (RccHan), and identified consistent substrain‐dependent differences in IBP, BT patterns, systemic inflammation, and immune cell infiltration into the ischemic brain. Although both groups are classified as Wistar rats, they originate from independent breeding colonies that have been separated for decades. Long‐term isolation combined with vendor‐specific husbandry practices likely contributes to genetic drift, epigenetic divergence, and distinct microbial exposures. 20 , 21 , 22 , 25 , 26 These accumulated differences may underlie the variability observed in infarct size, 17 , 18 , 19 immune activation, 3 , 4 and bacterial dissemination, 5 , 6 , 7 reinforcing vendor origin as a relevant source of experimental variability even within a single nominal strain.

A key finding of this study is the identification of distinct BT patterns between the two substrains. RjHan rats exhibited bacterial dissemination predominantly restricted to the MLN, whereas RccHan rats exhibited broader dissemination involving the spleen and liver. Although total extraintestinal bacterial burden did not differ significantly between substrains, organ‐specific analysis revealed significantly higher bacterial loads in the MLN of RjHan rats. Importantly, taxonomic profiling demonstrated clear differences in the phyla translocated beyond the intestine: RjHan rats were mainly characterized by Bacteroidota and Pseudomonadota, whereas RccHan rats lacked Bacteroidota and were dominated by Pseudomonadota, with a significant reduction in Bacillota and Actinomycetota. These qualitative differences persisted despite standardized housing and acclimation.

When integrated with MRI data showing greater IBP disruption in RjHan rats, 7 , 24 these findings suggest that substrain‐specific differences may influence preferential routes of bacterial dissemination after stroke. RjHan rats appear to exhibit a BT pattern more closely associated with lymphatic involvement, whereas RccHan rats exhibit a distribution consistent with broader systemic dissemination. Although these observations are in line with previous reports describing distinct anatomical pathways of BT, 8 , 9 they should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis generating rather than evidence of definitive mechanistic differences.

Importantly, these distinct BT patterns were associated with divergent immune responses. At 72 h poststroke, RjHan rats exhibited a more pronounced systemic and central inflammatory response, particularly in the presence of BT, with significant alterations in circulating leukocyte populations and increased immune cell infiltration into ischemic brain tissue. In contrast, immune changes in RccHan rats were more modest. These findings support the notion that substrain origin modulates immune responsiveness after stroke independently of infarct size, rather than indicating a direct causal link between BT, IBP, and stroke outcome.

In summary, this study demonstrates that vendor‐dependent substrain differences among Wistar rats significantly shape poststroke IBP, BT patterns, and systemic and cerebral immune responses. Our findings highlight that animals sharing the same strain designation may exhibit significantly different biological responses depending on their origin, with important implications for experimental variability, reproducibility, and interpretation of preclinical stroke studies. Careful consideration of vendor origin is therefore essential when investigating gut–brain axis mechanisms and immune responses after stroke.

Finally, this study has several limitations. First, analyses were restricted to a single poststroke time point (72 h), precluding conclusions regarding the temporal dynamics of IBP disruption, BT, and immune responses. Second, the sample size was modest, reflecting the exploratory nature of this pilot study. Third, immune characterization focused on major leukocyte populations without assessment of functional activation markers or specific lymphocyte subsets. Finally, BT was assessed using culture‐based methods without molecular validation. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, multiple time points, and complementary molecular approaches will be required to further delineate the mechanisms underlying the substrain‐dependent differences observed here.
