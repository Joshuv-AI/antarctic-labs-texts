Cefiderocol resistance in a clinical ST11-KL64 hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae isolate mediated by a novel CirA truncation and SHV-12 β-lactamase
Authors: Peiyao Zhou, Jiana Fu, Xiaoxia He, Haojin Gao, Chunyang Wu, Fangyou Yu, Ying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) infections have emerged as a major global public health threat. Cefiderocol is a novel siderophore-conjugated cephalosporin with potent activity to treat severe infections caused by CRKP. In this study, we investigated the underlying mechanisms leading to cefiderocol resistance in an ST11-KL64 hypervirulent K. pneumoniae FK3023 isolated from a patient with infective fever and chronic kidney disease in China.MethodsWhole genome sequencing (WGS), amino acid sequences alignment, cirA gene complementation and lactamases genes cloning assays were performed to elucidate the molecular basis of cefiderocol resistance. Pathogenicity was evaluated by quantitative siderophore production, string test, capsule quantification, and a Galleria mellonella infection model.ResultsThis strain carried a hybrid plasmid that contained both virulence and resistance determinants, including key virulence factors (iutA-iucABCD operon and rmpA gene) and β-lactamases genes (blaKPC−2, blaTEM−1B, blaCTX−M−3, blaCTX−M−65). Compared to the wild-type siderophore receptor CirA, K. pneumoniae FK3023 carried 119 nucleotide deletion (c.1830_1948del), leading to a frameshift, followed by a premature stop codon (Val611TyrfsTer32), resulting in a novel mutation in CirA. K. pneumoniae FK3023 complemented with the wild-type cirA showed a substantial decrease in cefiderocol MIC, further confirming the function of CirA. Each of the six identified β-lactamases genes in K. pneumoniae FK3023 was cloned into a pUC vector, and then expressed in Escherichia coli cells. The E. coli strains carrying blaSHV−12 showed an increase in cefiderocol MIC compared to the E. coli strains carrying empty vectors.ConclusionsIn conclusion, we described a clinical ST11-KL64 hypervirulent K. pneumoniae isolate exhibiting cefiderocol resistance likely mediated by a novel CirA truncation and blaSHV−12. K. pneumoniae FK3023 also carried the hybrid plasmid and two additional resistance plasmids, highlighting the convergence of hypervirulence and multidrug resistance and underscoring the challenges for clinical management.Keywords: Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, hypervirulent, cefiderocol, CirA

Introduction

The Gram-negative bacterium Klebsiella pneumoniae is a major opportunistic human pathogen, increasingly emerging as a global healthcare challenge [1]. The emergence and dissemination of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) has posed a significant threat to public health, and CRKP has been identified by the World Health Organization as a crucial priority for the development of novel control strategies [2, 3].

In recent years, convergence of hypervirulence and carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae represents an urgent global health concern [4]. Mechanisms for the emergence of hypervirulent and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CR-hvKP) can be summarised by three evolutionary paths [5]: (i) CRKP acquiring a hypervirulent phenotype; (ii) hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKP) acquiring a carbapenem-resistant phenotype; and (iii) K. pneumoniae acquiring both a carbapenem resistance and hypervirulence plasmid. ST11 CR-hvKP is now becoming a “high-risk” dominant clone group, harboring both hypervirulence and carbapenem resistance via horizontal transfer and evolution of plasmids [6, 7].

Due to the multidrug resistance (MDR) of CRKP, the limited availability of drug options makes the clinical treatment of CRKP infections more challenging. The recently introduced novel cephalosporin, cefiderocol, represents a promising treatment option for infections caused by MDR-KP and CRKP [8]. Cefiderocol, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2020, is a novel siderophore-conjugated cephalosporin that exploits bacterial iron-uptake systems via a chlorocatechol moiety, functioning as a “Trojan horse” to facilitate periplasmic penetration through active transport [9]. However, cefiderocol-resistant K. pneumoniae has emerged in clinical settings. Resistance to cefiderocol in K. pneumoniae could be associated with β-lactamases, deficiency in iron transporters as well as other factors [10, 11].

In this study, we described an ST11-KL64 CR-hvKP isolate FK3023 exhibiting cefiderocol resistance. Comprehensive genomic, phenotypic, and functional analyses were conducted to explore the underlying mechanisms. We identified a novel truncation mutation in the siderophore receptor CirA and demonstrated that SHV-12 β-lactamase further contributed to decreased cefiderocol susceptibility. Additionally, we characterized a hybrid plasmid co-carrying both virulence and resistance determinants. Our findings highlight the molecular basis of cefiderocol resistance and the clinical threat posed by the ongoing convergence of hypervirulence and multidrug resistance in K. pneumoniae.

Materials and Methods

Clinical case

A 60-year-old male with uremia on maintenance hemodialysis for over two years was admitted due to a 9-day history of fever and anorexia. He was diagnosed with infective fever secondary to pulmonary infection. The patient had previously received piperacillin-tazobactam (2.5 g bid + qn) for a 4-day. On day 5 of hospitalization, K. pneumoniae FK3023 strain was isolated from the sputum. The antibiotic regimen was then switched from piperacillin-tazobactam to tigecycline (50 mg, q12h) on day 5. After five days of tigecycline treatment, the patient’s condition stabilized, and he was discharged.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the K. pneumoniae strain FK3023 was determined using broth microdilution, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Antimicrobial agents, including β-lactamases, β-lactamase/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, aminoglycosides, quinolones, sulfonamide and tetracyclines, were tested. According to the the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 33rd edition [12], the MIC of cefiderocol was determined using iron-depleted cation-adjusted Mueller Hinton broth (ID-CAMHB), employing the broth microdilution method. E. coli ATCC 25,922 was used as quality control strain, and interpretation of the results adhered to the CLSI breakpoints. The MIC of tigecycline was interpreted according to the breakpoints established by European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) [13].

WGS and bioinformatics analysis

Genomic DNA of K. pneumoniae FK3023 was extracted using a commercial kit (TIANGEN, DP302) and sequenced using both HiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and the PacBio Sequel II system (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA). Mutations in genes previously reported to be related in cefiderocol resistance were investigated manually using BLAST tool. Kleborate, and PlasmidFinder databases were used to determine antimicrobial resistance (AMR), virulence determinants, plasmid replicon types, serotype and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) [14, 15]. Analysis of conjugation modules was performed using oriTfinder, and construction of circular plasmid maps was carried out with Proksee software [16, 17]. The comparisons of the gene environments of resistance region and virulence region within pFK3023-2, hvKP319 plasmid unnamed1, XSLGL-1 plasmid pKPC_NDM_1 and pLVPK were performed using Easyfig software [18]. Alignment of the amino acid sequences of wild-type and mutated CirA were performed using ClustalW sequence alignment tool and ESPript 3.0 [19, 20]. The CirA structure was modeled using Alphafold2 and visualized using PYMOL (DeLano) [21].

Quantitative siderophore production assay

To evaluate the iron-chelating capacity of K. pneumoniae FK3023, a semi-quantitative siderophore detection assay was conducted in accordance with established protocols [14]. Specifically, 1 µL aliquots of iron-saturated log-phase cultures were inoculated onto chrome azurol S (CAS) agar plates. Following 24-hour incubation at 37℃, siderophore production was quantified by measuring orange halo diameters surrounding bacterial colonies.

String test

The mucoviscosity of K. pneumoniae FK3023 was assessed using the string test as described previously [23]. A positive string test was defined as the formation of a viscous string exceeding 5 mm in length when a single bacterial colony was stretched with an inoculation loop.

Capsule quantification

The mucoviscosity was measured by quantifying uronic acid extracted from K. pneumoniae FK3023, according to a previously described protocol [15]. Bacterial cultures were grown overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, diluted 1:100 in fresh medium, and incubated at 37℃ for 6 h. Capsular polysaccharide extraction was performed by adding 100 µL of Zwittergent 3–12 detergent (1% w/v) to 500 µL of the bacterial suspension. The mixture was incubated at 50℃ for 20 min and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was subjected to ethanol precipitation by mixing with 1.2 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 10 min. The pellet was dried and resuspended in 200 µL of sterile deionized water. Then, 1.2 mL of a tetraborate solution (12.5 mM sodium tetraborate in sulfuric acid) was added, and the sample was heated at 100℃ for 5 min, immediately cooled on ice for at least 10 min, and mixed with 20 µL of hydroxyphenyl reagent (0.15% w/v). After a 5-min incubation at room temperature, the absorbance at 520 nm (OD₅₂₀) was measured.

G. mellonella in vivo infection model

The pathogenicity of K. pneumoniae strains FK3023 was evaluated by applying G. mellonella infection assays. K. pneumoniae NTUH-K2044 (hypervirulent starins) and HS11286 (avirulent strains) were used as virulence control starins [16]. Treatment groups were inoculated with 10 µL of the bacterial suspension containing 1 × 106 CFU/mL bacteria using sterile microsyringe, while the control groups received equivalent volumes of normal saline. For each treatment group, at least 30 caterpillars were reared in triplicate Petri dishes and maintained in complete darkness at 37℃. Survival was monitored over 72 h at 12-hour intervals.

Conjugation assay

Based on oriTfinder analysis predicting that pFK3023-4 carries essential conjugative modules, conjugation assays were performed to validate this finding. The transferability of this plasmid from K. pneumoniae FK3023 to E. coli DH5α was assessed using a previously described method [17].

Briefly, donor and recipient strains were grown to the mid-logarithmic phase, mixed at a 1:1 ratio, and harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 1 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 20 µL of 10 mM MgSO4, spotted onto an LB agar plate, and incubated overnight at 37℃. Following incubation, the bacterial mixture was serially diluted and plated onto LB agar containing selective antibiotics (cefuroxime [10 mg/L] for blaSHV−12 selection and apramycin [100 mg/L] for the E. coli DH5α recipient). Putative transconjugants were phenotypically confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry and genotypically verified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using PrimeStar HS DNA Polymerase (Takara, R045A). Primers (blaSHV−12-F/R) were described in Table 1.

Construction of pUC plasmids

Following growth in LB medium overnight, the pUC plasmid was purified from the liquid culture using a commercial DNA extraction kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The wild-type cirA and β-lactamases genes (including blaKPC−2, blaTEM−1B, blaCTX−M−3, blaCTX−M−65, blaSHV−11, blaSHV−12), which contained the promoter region, were amplified using PrimeStar. The PCR-amplified gene and promoter fragments were cloned into the apramycin-resistant pUC vector using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs, E2621). The assembled construct was subsequently introduced into E. coli DH5α by chemical transformation. Positive transformants were selected on LB agar supplemented with apramycin (100 mg/L) and subsequently confirmed by PCR and Sanger sequencing. The primers were described in Table 1.

Complementation with the wild-type cirA

To confirm the function of CirA, genetic complementation assays were applied by cloning the intact cirA gene from wild-type K. pneumoniae 1630 (accession no. GCA_050379395.1 ) in a vector pUC, followed by transferring into the K. pneumoniae FK3023 by electroporation and selection with 100 mg/L apramycin. K. pneumoniae 1630 belonged to ST11-KL64 CRKP which carried a wild-type cirA according to the BLAST analysis with NCBI database. The transformants were further verified by PCR amplification and sanger sequencing. The primers were described in Table 1.

Nucleotide sequence accession numbers of K. pneumoniae FK3023

The complete genome sequence of K. pneumoniae FK3023 has been deposited in GenBank under the accession number PRJNA1313590.

Statistics

Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test and the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test in GraphPad Prism software (v9.0), with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

K. pneumoniae FK3023 exhibited multi-drug resistance and hypervirulent phenotypes

According to the results of antimicrobial susceptibility test, K. pneumoniae FK3023 showed high level resistance to the carbapenems (meropenem, ≥ 16 mg/L; impenmem, ≥ 16 mg/L; ertapenem, ≥8 mg/L), cephalosporins (cefuroxime, ≥ 64 mg/L; ceftazidime, ≥ 64 mg/L; ceftriaxone, ≥ 64 mg/L; cefepime, ≥ 32 mg/L), cephamycin (cefoxitin, ≥ 64 mg/L), β-lactamase/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (BLICs), (cefopetazone/Sulbactam, ≥ 64 mg/L; piperacillin/tazobactam, ≥ 128 mg/L; amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ≥ 32 mg/L), aztreonam (≥ 64 mg/L), fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, ≥ 8 mg/L; ciprofloxacin, ≥ 4 mg/L). K. pneumoniae FK3023 were susceptible to tigecycline (1 mg/L) and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (≤ 20 mg/L). Notably, FK3023 exhibited resistance to cefiderocol (16 mg/L).

To determine whether FK3023 also exhibited hypervirulent characteristics, we compared its virulence-associated phenotypes with those of reference strains: K. pneumoniae HS11286 (a classical ST11 strain, cKP, avirulent control) [18] and NTUH-K2044 (ST23, KL1, hypervirulent control) [19]. FK3023 formed mucoid, opaque colonies and tested positive in the string test, consistent with a hypermucoviscous phenotype. As shown in Fig. 1A, quantitative siderophore assays demonstrated that FK3023 produced robust siderophores (halo diameter: 20 mm), comparable to NTUH-K2044 (22 mm) and significantly greater than HS11286 (13 mm). Capsule quantification (Fig. 1B) revealed that FK3023 synthesized slightly less uronic acid than NTUH-K2044 but more than HS11286, further supporting its hypervirulent feature. Pathogenicity was further assessed using the G. mellonella infection model (Fig. 1C). Three days post-infection, larvae survival was markedly reduced in groups infected with FK3023 (30%) and NTUH-K2044 (10%), whereas the HS11286 group maintained a significantly higher survival rate. These results indicate that FK3023 possesses strong virulence potential comparable to the hypervirulent control strain.

Collectively, K. pneumoniae FK3023 demonstrated both multidrug resistance including resistance to cefiderocol and hypervirulence. This dual phenotype poses a substantial challenge to clinical management. Therefore, whole genome sequencing (WGS) was conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying cefiderocol resistance in FK3023.

Loss of cefiderocol susceptibility mediated by a novel CirA truncation mutation

To elucidate the genetic basis of cefiderocol resistance in FK3023, we analyzed non-synonymous single-nucleotide polymorphisms (nsSNPs) and insertion/deletion (indel) mutations across six genes encoding siderophore receptors previously associated with cefiderocol susceptibility in K. pneumoniae (cirA, fiu, fhuA, fepA, efeO, and exbD). A different cirA mutation was found in the chromosome of FK3023, with no mutations detected in other genes. As shown in Fig. 3A, FK3023 carried 119 nucleotide deletion (c.1830_1948del), leading to a frameshift, followed by a premature stop codon (Val611TyrfsTer32). This mutation has not been previously reported and is predicted to produce a truncated CirA protein.

The CirA outer membrane receptor typically adopts a 22-stranded β-barrel architecture with a central globular plug domain that undergoes conformational rearrangement to facilitate siderophore translocation through the β-barrel channel (Fig. 3B). Notably, the Val611TyrfsTer32 frameshift mutation in FK3023 strain leads to amino acid substitutions and deletions, disrupting the β-strand structure and destabilizing the β-barrel integrity, thereby impeding cefiderocol transport across the outer membrane.

To confirm the functional role of CirA in cefiderocol susceptibility, genetic complementation was performed. The wild-type cirA gene from K. pneumoniae 1630 (accession no. GCA_050379395.1) was cloned into the pUC19 vector and introduced into FK3023 via electroporation, followed by selection with 100 mg/L apramycin. Complementation with intact cirA restored cefiderocol susceptibility, reducing the MIC from 16 mg/L to 0.5 mg/L (Table 3), thereby confirming that loss-of-function mutation in CirA was primarily responsible for cefiderocol resistance in FK3023.

Serine β-lactamases, especially SHV-12, substantially decrease cefiderocol susceptibility

Whole-genome analysis of K. pneumoniae FK3023 (accession no. PRJNA1313590) identified six β-lactamase genes: blaSHV−11, blaTEM−1B, blaKPC−2, blaCTX−M−3, blaCTX−M−65, and blaSHV−12. To evaluate their individual effects on cefiderocol susceptibility, each gene, along with its native promoter region, was cloned into the pUC19 vector and expressed in E. coli DH5α.

The E. coli strains carrying blaSHV−12 showed an apparent increase in cefiderocol MIC compared with the control strain E. coli DH5α (Table 3), and E. coli cells carrying blaKPC−2 showed a modest increase in cefiderocol MIC, while blaCTX−M−15, blaCTX−M−65 and blaTEM−1B had negligible effects on cefiderocol susceptibility. The transconjugant of pFK3023-4 also exhibited an elavation in cefiderocol resistance. These findings indicate that the serine β-lactamase SHV-12 plays a notable role in decreasing cefiderocol susceptibility. Moreover, in K. pneumoniae FK3023, the combination of SHV-12 production and CirA inactivation likely acts collectively, further compromising the efficacy of cefiderocol.

Discussion

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has become a critical global public health concern, driving nosocomial outbreaks associated with high morbidity and mortality [20]. In Asia, the ST11 lineage represents the predominant epidemic clone responsible for a substantial proportion of CRKP infections and is closely associated with increased in-hospital fatality rates [5, 21]. In China, the sub-lineage ST11-KL64 CRKP exhibits a concerning convergence of multidrug resistance profiles and enhanced virulence determinants, facilitating its persistent transmission within clinical environments [22–24].

Cefiderocol, a novel siderophore catecholate cephalosporin, holds promise as an effective antimicrobial agent against MDR Gram-negative bacteria [25, 26]. In particular, cefiderocol activity against KPC-producing K. pneumoniae is comparable or higher than that of ceftazidime/avibactam [27]. Although cefiderocol has not yet been approved for clinical use in China, there are already reports of cefiderocol-resistant isolates from surveillance studies. These emerging reports highlight the rapid adaptive evolution of K. pneumoniae under antibiotic selection pressure.

Siderophore uptake receptors play a crucial role in the evolution of cefiderocol resistance, as siderophores are a key factor for the survival of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae under iron-depleted conditions [9, 26]. Cefiderocol demonstrates structural homology with catecholate siderophores (e.g. salmochelin and enterobactin), facilitating its active transport into the bacterial periplasmic space via high-affinity iron-uptake systems, particularly through the colicin I receptor CirA [26]. The emergence of isolates resistant to cefiderocol due to mutations in the cirA gene has been reported worldwide. In this study, we identified a novel mutaion in CirA (Val611TyrfsTer32). It leads to a frameshift, followed by a premature stop codon, and resulting in CirA deficiency. The structure of mutated CirA also demonstrated the disruption of the integrity of the β-barrel channel structure, thus impeding the transportation of cefiderocol. Complementation with the wild-type cirA gene restored cefiderocol susceptibility, confirming that CirA deficiency was a major contributor to resistance. These results provide direct functional evidence that CirA loss-of-function mutations can substantially reduce cefiderocol activity in K. pneumoniae. CirA serves as a crucial gateway for cefiderocol entry into bacterial cells [11]. Mutations in CirA may reduce the transportation of cefiderocol into K. pneumoniae FK3023, resulting in the increase of cefiderocol resistance. A previous study demonstrated that K. pneumoniae strains with an incomplete CirA were globally distributed and had existed long before the FDA approval of cefiderocol in 2019 [28]. The diversity of incomplete CirA suggests bacterial adaptation and evolution in response to changing environmental pressures. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that cefiderocol exposure and the presence of NDM can facilitate the generation of incomplete CirA [29].

The presence of various β-lactamases (such as KPC, SHV, and NDM types) has also been proved to influence bacterial resistance to cefiderocol [10, 30]. To determine the roles of six β-lactamases genes (blaSHV−11, blaTEM−1B, blaKPC−2, blaCTX−M−3, blaCTX−M−65 and blaSHV−12) in cefiderocol resistance of K. pneumoniae FK3023, gene cloning was conducted. Results revealed that SHV-12 was associated with an apparent elevation in cefiderocol resistance, whereas other enzymes, including KPC-2, TEM-1B, and CTX-M variants, had minimal effects. This finding suggests that SHV-12 may act collectively with outer membrane alterations, such as CirA loss, to confer higher levels of resistance. Inactivation of CirA likely reduces cefiderocol influx, while residual drug molecules that passively diffuse through porins are hydrolyzed by SHV-12, leading to overall resistance. This two-step mechanism highlights how the combination of permeability defects and β-lactamase activity can compromise cefiderocol efficacy.

Recently, K. pneumoniae isolates harboring hypervirulence and multidrug resistance have increasingly emerged, which have caused severe infection and even death [31]. The formation of hybrid plasmid is one of the evolutionary paths leading to the emergence of carbapenem-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (CR-hvKP) [32]. In this study, we identified a hybrid plasmid pFK3023-2, a merger of IncFII(pHN7A8)/IncR backbones leading to convergence of AMR and virulence on a single plasmid. To date, reports of hybrid plasmids are derived from clinical isolates, suggesting a strong link between clinical antimicrobial pressure and the selection, persistence, and dissemination of these plasmids [33]. The virulence module of pFK3023-2 was flanked by two copies of IS903B and there are several mobile elements such as IS5, IS110, IS630, ISKpn26 and Tn3 inserted in the module. The insertion of the virulence-encoding region into the IncFII(pHN7A8)/IncR plasmid is possibly through the insertion mediated by IS903 family proteins. In China, blaKPC−2 are predominant among CRE clinical isolates especially K. pneumoniae and this gene is often carried on the IncFII(pHN7A8) plasmid. Under pressure and for persistence, CR-HvKp can integrate segments of virulence plasmid into the endemic plasmid types to obtain the two-fold advantage of coding antimicrobial resistance and virulence. pFK3023-2 may be generated as a result of recombination between a typical pLVPK-like virulence plasmid and an MDR plasmid in a high-risk ST11-K64 CRKP host. Corresponding to the genomic feature, the ST11-KL64 K. pneumoniae FK3023 isolate exhibited both hypervirulence and antibiotic resistance, contributing to its successful persistence. The strain K. pneumoniae FK3023 tested positive in the string test, and produced more capsule than negative control K. pneumoniae HS11286. Furthermore, assessment of siderophore production and Galleria mellonella survival rates collectively demonstrated that the virulence of FK3023 was comparable to the hypervirulent control strain NTUH-K2044.

ST11 is a highly dominant CRKP clone in Asia, especially China, and carries blaKPC−2 and multiple ESBL genes. Over the past decade, hv-CRKP has rapidly disseminated, a phenomenon typically attributed to the acquisition of virulence plasmids containing prevalent virulence genes, such as iucA, iroN, rmpA, and rmpA2 [34]. The virulence plasmids in ST11 were often accompanied by the absence of iroBCDN and the presence of the non-catecholate siderophore aerobactin (iuc), resulting to the hindering of cefiderocol transport, as observed in our study. The observed reduction in in vitro susceptibility to cefiderocol among ST11 CRKP isolates may be attributed to a combination of factors, including diversity in ESBLs, mutations in the CirA siderophore receptor, and the acquisition of specific virulence plasmids. Such findings emphasize the need for routine genomic surveillance, early detection of hybrid plasmids, and prudent antibiotic stewardship to prevent further dissemination.

Although cefiderocol has not been approved for clinical use in China yet, isolated case reports documenting cefiderocol-resistant clinical K. pneumoniae isolates have already emerged. Lan et al. revealed that CirA impacts cefiderocol susceptibility in K. pneumoniae [35]. In another study, Yang et al. suggested that cefiderocol resistance in CRKP typically involves expression of carbapenemases especially NDM and other factors, such as mutations in siderophore uptake receptor genes cirA, two-component system genes envZ and baeS and the production of enterobactin [8]. Furthermore, Lan et al. demonstrated that SHV-12, KPC-2, and the inactivation of CirA with a premature stop codon at position 399 collectively contributed to cefiderocol resistance in CRKP [11]. According to Pu et al., high cefiderocol resistance in KPC-producing ST11-KL64 CR-hvKP results from the synergistic effect of two factors: downregulation of catecholate siderophore receptors (driven by envZ mutation) and stable dysregulation of the TonB-ExbB-ExbD complex [36]. Iron transporter proteins are crucial for actively importing cefiderocol into bacterial cells. Therefore, the altered siderophore receptor CirA is one of the common mechanisms leading to cefiderocol resistance. In our study, we demonstrated that cefiderocol resistance in K. pneumoniae FK3023 results from the combined effects of a novel CirA truncation and SHV-12 β-lactamase activity. The presence of a hybrid virulence-resistance plasmid further enhances the clinical risk associated with this strain. Our study expands current understanding of cefiderocol resistance mechanisms and highlights the alarming convergence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence in the globally dominant ST11-KL64 clone.
