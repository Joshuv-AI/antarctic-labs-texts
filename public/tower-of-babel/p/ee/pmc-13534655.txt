Compost and rootstock effects on fibrous root physiology and bacterial community composition in young citrus trees under endemic Huanglongbing
Authors: Gabriel Pugina, Caroline Tardivo, Brittney Monus, Emma Dawson, Sarah L. Strauss, Ute Albrecht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Citrus production in Florida has declined drastically due to Huanglongbing (HLB), a bacterial disease associated with Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus. This disease significantly reduces fruit yield and quality and shortens the tree’s lifespan, severely impacting Florida’s economy. As no resistant citrus cultivars are currently available, intensive management is essential to maintain tree health and productivity. We conducted a commercial-scale field study in southwest Florida to test whether biannual compost applications improve soil health and belowground functioning of young ‘Valencia’ sweet orange trees grafted on four different rootstocks under endemic HLB. Compost increased soil organic matter, pH, nutrient content, and cation exchange capacity, while also increasing soil moisture and electrical conductivity. This translated into higher fibrous root respiration and increases in first-order specific root length. Metabolomics analysis revealed strong compost-associated reshaping of the fibrous root metabolome, including enrichment of several nitrogen-related amino acids and central carbon intermediates. Endorhizosphere profiling showed that compost altered the active bacterial community composition with limited effects on alpha diversity. Despite these belowground gains, compost did not affect tree size and productivity – rather, rootstock was the primary determinant. This suggests that the belowground improvements from compost are insufficient to overcome the physiological constraints imposed by HLB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58183-w.

Introduction

Citrus production in Florida has declined drastically over the past two decades, primarily due to huanglongbing disease (HLB or citrus greening) associated with the phloem-limited bacterium Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), transmitted by the Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri)1–3. Since HLB was first detected in Florida in 2005, the disease has spread rapidly in the absence of an effective eradication program, and became endemic around 20134. During this period, annual citrus production in Florida declined by approximately 90%, from 8 to 9 million metric tons in the mid-2000s to less than 0.6 million metric tons in recent seasons, accompanied by concurrent losses in bearing acreage5,6. Although hurricanes and market shifts have contributed to this decline, HLB remains the primary driver of tree loss, yield reduction, and shortening of orchard lifespan3,5.

HLB symptoms typically begin subtly, with leaf chlorosis and asymmetric blotchy mottling that can be easily confused with nutrient deficiencies, and progress to canopy thinning, production of small and misshapen fruit with off-flavors, and extensive preharvest fruit drop2,3,7. However, one of the earliest symptoms of CLas infection occurs below ground. Fibrous roots have been reported to decline before foliar symptoms become noticeable8, and recent work has shown that CLas-associated root system reductions can exceed aboveground reductions9, indicating that root health is central to tree performance under HLB-endemic conditions. This loss of fibrous roots increases the difficulty of growing citrus trees in the sandy, nutrient-poor soils typical of Florida’s citrus production regions10.

In this context, rootstock selection has become one of the most important cultural strategies for enhancing tree health and maintaining productivity under HLB pressure11,12. Rootstock influences tree vigor, canopy architecture, precocity, fruit quality, and yield, as well as tolerance to abiotic and biotic stresses13. Although no commercial citrus rootstock has yet shown resistance, some cultivars can improve the grafted tree performance, resulting in higher canopy volume, better canopy health, and greater productivity under disease-endemic conditions14–17.

At the same time, soil-focused practices, particularly the application of organic amendments such as compost, have gained attention as complementary tools to support tree health in HLB-affected citrus production sites. Florida citrus is typically grown on Spodosol or Entisol-type sandy soils with very low organic matter content and limited water and nutrient retention capacity, which challenges tree establishment and long-term productivity10,18. Compost applications can increase soil organic matter (SOM), improve soil aggregation and water-holding capacity, enhance the cation exchange capacity (CEC), and increase nutrient availability19,20. Compost can also stimulate soil and rhizosphere microbial activity, introduce new microbes, and enhance microbial diversity, which promotes nutrient cycling and can suppress certain soilborne diseases21–23. In citrus and other perennial fruit systems, compost was previously shown to improve tree growth, alter the rhizosphere microbial community, and enhance resilience to biotic stress24–26. In addition, recent work in citrus has begun to connect rootstock genetics with compost-induced shifts in the rhizosphere microbiome. For example, Castellano-Hinojosa et al. (2023) showed that interactions between citrus rootstock cultivar and compost application altered the abundance, diversity, and composition of the active rhizosphere bacterial communities, and that these changes were linked to the concentration of specific root nutrients depending on the rootstock genetic background. However, that study focused primarily on the rhizosphere, leaving open questions about how compost and rootstock interact to shape root structure and physiology.

There is growing interest in using metabolomics and other “omics” tools to understand how organic amendments such as compost influence plant metabolism and soil microbial processes. Multi-omics approaches that integrate microbial community profiling with untargeted metabolomics have been used in maize and other plants to track the succession of bacterial communities and microbial metabolites during manure composting, identifying dominant taxa and key lipid and fatty acid pathways27–29. Despite this progress, metabolomics and microbiome analyses have rarely been combined in perennial tree crops to examine how soil amendments modulate root metabolism and root-associated bacterial communities under disease pressure in a commercial production environment. To address these gaps, we conducted an integrated, commercial-scale field study using ‘Valencia’ sweet orange (Citrus sinensis) trees grafted on four widely used rootstocks growing under HLB-endemic conditions in southwest Florida. By combining measurements of soil physicochemical properties and fibrous root morphology and physiology, and profiling of the active endorhizosphere microbiome, we evaluated how repeated compost applications influenced belowground functions and how these were modulated by rootstock genetics.

Our objectives were to: (i) determine how compost alters soil health, root physiological traits, and whole tree responses under endemic HLB, (ii) characterize the root metabolomic and active fibrous root bacterial community response to compost amendment, and (iii) identify how these responses interact with rootstock cultivar. We hypothesized that compost application alters the soil environment and root system functioning, and that the extent to which these belowground improvements translate into grafted tree performance under HLB pressure depends on rootstock cultivar.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experimental design

Four commercial rootstock cultivars were used: US-802 (Citrus maxima ‘Siamese’× Poncirus trifoliata ‘Gotha Road’), US-812 (C. reticulata ‘Sunki’ × P. trifoliata ‘Benecke’), US-897 (C. reticulata ‘Cleopatra’ × P. trifoliata ‘Flying Dragon’), and X-639 (C. reticulata ‘Cleopatra’ × P. trifoliata ‘Rubidoux’). During the most recent production seasons, these rootstocks were among Florida’s top 15 propagated rootstocks30. The grafted scion was the standard ‘Valencia’ (C. sinensis) clone SPB 1-14-19, and the trees were purchased from Rasnake Citrus Nursery (Winter Haven, FL).

The trees were planted in an 8-hectare block at a commercial citrus production site near Felda, Hendry County, FL (26°34’30.0"N 81°30’33.8” W) in August 2019. Trees were arranged in double rows on raised beds, separated by furrows, and irrigated through seepage irrigation. One hundred trees were planted per row, spaced at 3.7 m, and the distance between rows was 7.3 m. Three months after planting, plant-based organic compost (Kastco Farms, Naples, FL) was applied by broadcast spreading underneath the canopy twice a year at a rate of 12.3 tons/ha. The compost had a pH of 7.7 and an electrical conductivity of 3.1 mS/cm, with a total solids content of 51.1% and an organic matter content of 23.6%. It contained 0.52% total nitrogen (0.52% organic N, 0.001% NH4+-N, and < 0.01% NO3--N), 0.08% phosphorus (equivalent to 0.18% P2O5), 0.26% potassium (equivalent to 0.31% K2O), 3.28% calcium, 0.31% magnesium, 0.09% sulfur, 0.07% sodium, 2500 ppm iron, 67.5 ppm manganese, and < 100 ppm boron.

The trial was set up as a 2 × 4 split-plot experimental design with eight replications, where soil amendment (compost or no compost) was the main factor and rootstock was the subplot factor (Fig. 1). The main plot consisted of 200 trees, 100 treated with compost and 100 without compost, and the subplots consisted of the four different rootstocks (US-802, US-812, US-897, and X-639) arranged in linear plots of 50 trees each, which constituted the experimental unit. The field trial was concluded after four years of data collection, as the grower collaborator decided to exit citrus production due to the compounding economic pressures of endemic HLB and recent hurricanes.

Tree size

Tree height, canopy width, and trunk circumference (both scion and rootstock) were measured in November 2022. The measurements were taken on eight trees per experimental unit that were randomly selected and tagged at the beginning of the experiment. Canopy height was measured from the soil to the canopy top using a tape measurer. Canopy width was measured in two cardinal directions (N-S and E-W), averaged, and the canopy volume was calculated by multiplying the canopy width by the height and dividing the product by 431. Scion and rootstock trunk circumferences were measured 5 cm above and below the graft union, respectively, and converted to trunk diameters.

Disease status

Leaves and fibrous roots were collected in November 2022 for real-time PCR detection of Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas). Four mature leaves from the most recent flush were collected from each of the same eight tagged trees used for tree size measurements and pooled by plot. Fibrous roots were collected from four randomly selected trees within each plot at various locations in the soil under the tree canopy and then pooled. Leaf and root tissues were pulverized with liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle for DNA extraction. One hundred milligrams of ground tissue were extracted using the Plant DNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) for leaves and the Plant DNeasy® Pro-Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) for roots. Real-time PCR assays were performed using the primers HLBas and HLBr and the probe HLBp developed by Li et al. (2006). For normalization, additional assays were conducted with the COXf, COXr, and COXp primer/probe system32. Each assay contained 2 µL of extracted DNA in a 20 µL reaction volume. Forty amplification cycles were performed in a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA).

Nutrient analysis

Soil and leaf nutrient analysis was conducted every summer (July) from 2020 to 2023. For soil nutrients, eight random subsamples of 30 cm-deep soil cores were collected between trees in each experimental unit using a 21” T-style stainless steel soil probe (Varomorous, Fort Lauderdale, FL, USA). The subsamples were combined and mixed to ensure homogeneity. Soil macronutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, Na, and S) and micronutrients (B, Zn, Mn, Fe, and Cu), organic matter (OM), nitrate (NO3-), ammonium (NH4+), pH, electric conductivity, and CEC were analyzed by Waters Agricultural Laboratories, Inc. (Camilla, GA). For leaf nutrients, the four most recent, fully mature leaves from eight randomly selected trees in each experimental unit were collected and pooled, resulting in a total of 32 leaves per sample. Leaf samples were analyzed for N, P, K, Mg, Ca, S, B, Zn, Mn, Fe, and Cu by Waters Agricultural Laboratories, Inc.

Soil volumetric water content (VWC) and electric conductivity (EC)

Both measurements were taken using a Field Scout TDR 350 Digital Moisture Sensor with an 8-inch probe (Spectrum Technologies, Aurora, IL, USA). Measurements were conducted monthly from February 2021 to January 2023 along the edge of the canopy of the same two trees that had the minirhizotron tubes installed (see root growth paragraph) within each experimental unit.

Weed biomass

To evaluate the effect of compost applications on weed growth, the aboveground weed biomass was measured in two random quadrats (0.25 m²) near the edge of the canopy in each experimental unit in March and September 2022. These sampling times were selected to capture contrasting seasonal weed pressure, with March representing the end of the dry season when weed pressure is typically low, and September representing the end of the rainy season when weed pressure is typically high in Florida citrus orchards. Weeds were cut at the soil surface and oven-dried at 65 °C until they reached a constant weight.

Fibrous root respiration

Fibrous roots were collected seasonally (every three months) from June 2021 to March 2023, resulting in a total of eight sampling dates. Samples were taken from the soil underneath the canopy of two randomly selected trees per experimental unit using soil knives (A.M. Leonard, Piqua, OH, USA) and pooled. Roots were shaken to remove loose soil, triple-washed with tap water, and blotted dry. One subsample was used for root respiration, and the remaining roots were used to determine the specific root length. Only 1st, 2nd, and 3rd order roots33 were used, but roots were not separated by order for the fibrous root respiration. Respiration was measured immediately after collection, in a 50 mL loop respiration chamber using an LI-850 CO2/H2O gas analyzer (LI-COR, Inc, Lincoln, NE, USA). The accumulation of CO2 in the chamber was measured every 0.5 s over a 3-minute interval. The root tissue was then dried at 60 °C until it reached a constant weight to determine the dry weight. Respiration rates were expressed as milligrams of CO2 per gram of dry weight per hour. Data were averaged for each season (spring, summer, fall, winter).

Fibrous root specific root length (SRL)

A subset of fibrous roots (< 1.5 mm in diameter) was collected as described in the root respiration paragraph. Roots were kept at 4 °C until scanning. Fibrous roots were separated by order33 and scanned using an EPSON Perfection V850 flatbed scanner (EPSON, Nagano, Japan) at 300 dpi resolution and a threshold of 110 in black-and-white quality. The images were analyzed by RhizoVision Explorer v2.0.334 to determine the total root length for each order. The specific root length was calculated as the root length divided by the root dry weight and expressed in meters per gram (m/g).

Fibrous root metabolomics

Fibrous roots were collected, as described in the root respiration paragraph, from two randomly selected trees within each experimental unit in July 2022 and pooled. Roots were washed three times with tap water, blotted dry, and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen in the field. In the laboratory, tissues were ground under liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. Twenty milligrams of powdered tissue was extracted twice with 1 mL of a methanol: chloroform: water (5:2:2, v/v) mixture at 4 °C for 20 min using a RotoShaker agitator (Scientific Industries, Bohemia, New York). Extracts were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were pooled. Aliquots (1 mL) were dried using a Vacufuge Plus concentrator (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) and stored at − 80 °C until analysis35,36.

Metabolite profiling was performed at the West Coast Metabolomics Center (University of California Genome Center, Davis, CA) using gas chromatography-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS) according to established protocols37,38. Internal retention index (RI) markers were added, and samples were derivatized by methoximation and trimethylsilylation prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was conducted on an Agilent 6890 gas chromatograph coupled to a Gerstel automatic liner exchange system, and detection was carried out using a LECO Pegasus IV TOF-MS. Raw data were processed with the BinBase metabolome database39, and normalization was performed using the mTIC sum approach. Each metabolite peak was expressed as a fraction of the total identified metabolite signal.

Endorhizosphere active bacterial community analysis (16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing)

Sample collection and RNA extraction

Fibrous roots were collected simultaneously with those used for metabolite analysis, from two randomly selected trees within each experimental unit and pooled. Roots were washed three times with tap water, blotted dry, and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. In the laboratory, root tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of ground tissue using the RNA Powerkit® Total RNA Isolation kit (Qiagen). RNA concentrations were quantified using the Qubit™ RNA High Sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Co-extracted DNA was removed using DNase I (RNase-free, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed with the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System with ezDNase (Thermo Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA concentrations were quantified using the Qubit™ DNA High Sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Library preparation and sequencing

The extracted cDNA was submitted to the Genomics and Microbiome Core Facility at Rush University (Chicago, IL) for sequencing. The V4-V5 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the 515Fa (GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA)40 and 926R (CCGYCAATTYMTTTRAGTTT)41 primers following the Earth Microbiome Project protocol. The sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate paired-end reads. After sequencing, raw reads were processed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) version 242. Reads were denoised, merged, and chimera-checked using DADA243 to generate amplicon sequencing variants (ASVs). ASVs were assigned taxonomies using the SILVA 138.1 database and a naïve Bayes classifier within QIIME244–46. Non-bacterial sequences (mitochondria and chloroplasts) were removed, and samples with fewer than 1,000 reads were excluded from the analysis.

Preharvest fruit drop, yield, and juice quality

Dropped fruits and fruits retained on the tree were counted for the eight tagged trees in each experimental unit one week prior to the commercial harvest in March 2023. Random fruits from the same eight trees were collected to fill one half-bushel fruit bag (approximately 50–60 fruits) for each experimental unit. Measurements were conducted at the Juice Processing Pilot Plant, Citrus Research and Education Center, University of Florida, Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences (Lake Alfred, FL). Fruit weight was measured using a digital scale, and juice was extracted using a pinpoint extractor (JBT, Chalfont, PA, USA). The juice percentage was calculated based on the weight of the extracted juice. Juice color was assessed using a spectrophotometer (Macbeth Color-EYE 3100). The total soluble solids (TSS) content was measured in degrees Brix (°Brix) following standard commercial procedures (John Bean Technologies Corporation, 2018). Titratable acid (TA) was determined by titrating juice samples with NaOH, using phenolphthalein as an indicator, and the TSS/TA ratio was determined. The average fruit weight was multiplied by the number of fruits on the tree to estimate yield per tree in kilograms.

Statistical analysis

Tree biometric analysis

A linear mixed model in R (version 4.4.3)47 was used for each dependent variable. Mixed-effects models were constructed with compost application, rootstock, and their interaction as fixed factors, while block and the soil amendment-within-block interaction were included as nested random effects to account for the split-plot experimental design. A repeated measures design was employed for the root growth analysis. The model included compost, rootstock, and sampling dates as factors. The Shapiro-Wilk tests and Q-Q plots were used to assess assumptions of normality and homogeneity of distribution of the data. When assumptions were not met, data were log-transformed. Where effects were significant (P < 0.05), a post-hoc comparison of means was conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test (emmeans). Visual ratings of canopy health were analyzed non-parametrically using aligned ranks transformation (ART) ANOVA48.

Multivariate nutrient analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to evaluate the overall variation in soil and leaf nutrient profiles between compost-amended and no compost treatments and among rootstock cultivars. Data were auto-scaled to unit variance to normalize differences in measurement units. PCA biplots were generated using the ‘factoextra’ package49 to visualize sample distribution and nutrient vector contributions. Ellipses representing 95% confidence intervals were overlaid to delineate clustering patterns between treatment groups. To statistically evaluate the multidimensional differences between the soil amendment and rootstock cultivars, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed. The PERMANOVA was conducted on Euclidean distance matrices generated from the scaled data using the ‘vegan’ package50.

Root metabolomics analysis

All analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.051. Prior to statistical analysis, raw peak intensities were processed. Features with a relative standard deviation greater than 10% across pooled quality-control samples were removed, and the remaining dataset was normalized by sum, log-transformed, and auto-scaled to unit variance.

To evaluate the overall metabolic profile, multivariate analyses were performed. Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was used to assess differences between compost-amended and no compost trees, and among the four rootstock cultivars. PLS-DA model robustness was assessed by 5-fold cross-validation, reporting R² and Q² values across up to five components. The optimal number of components was selected based on the highest Q² value. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were constructed using the 25 most discriminating metabolites (ranked by p-values). Heatmaps were generated using Euclidean distance and complete-linkage clustering on normalized auto-scaled intensities.

Univariate statistical analyses were conducted using two-way ANOVA with compost, rootstock, and their interaction as fixed factors. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), and metabolites with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Assumptions of normality were automatically evaluated within MetaboAnalyst, and log-transformed values met the variance-stabilizing requirements.

Fold-change analysis (compost vs. no compost) was performed to identify highly responsive metabolites with an absolute fold change ≥ 2.0.

Fibrous root endorhizosphere bacterial community analysis

Alpha diversity metrics, including observed richness, Shannon diversity index, and Simpson diversity index, were calculated using the phyloseq package52. Differences in alpha diversity metrics between factors (compost-amended vs. no compost, and rootstock cultivars) were assessed using non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was conducted to visualize differences in bacterial communities, and the first two principal components were reported. Group differences in beta diversity were tested using PERMANOVA in the vegan package with 999 permutations to estimate the variance explained (R²) and statistical significance (p < 0.05). Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons were conducted as necessary to evaluate specific group interactions.

Differential abundance analyses were conducted using a two-step approach. First, a global analysis was performed across all rootstocks at the class level to identify broad, compost-associated shifts in the bacterial community. Subsequently, rootstock-specific differential abundance analyses were conducted at the family level to capture cultivar-dependent responses to compost application. All analyses were performed using the ‘DESeq2’ package within the ‘phyloseq’ framework, using raw count data. Compost treatment was included as the main factor in the models. Differential abundance was quantified using log2 fold change values, with positive and negative values indicating enrichment in compost-amended and no compost samples, respectively.

Results

Tree size

At the end of the study, there were no differences in size between trees grown in compost-amended and no compost plots. On average, the trees were 1.67 m tall with a canopy volume of 1.7 m3, a rootstock trunk diameter of 8.6 cm, and a scion trunk diameter of 6.7 cm (Table S1). In contrast, there were significant rootstock effects on canopy volume, rootstock trunk diameter, and the scion/rootstock trunk diameter ratio. Trees grafted on X-639 (1.94 m³) had a significantly larger canopy volume than those on US-802 (1.55 m³) and US-897 (1.62 m³). Similarly, the rootstock trunk diameter was significantly larger for US-802 (9.7 cm) than for US-812 (7.8 cm). The smallest scion/rootstock trunk diameter ratio was found for trees grafted on US-802 (0.67). Trees on the other rootstocks had ratios of 0.80 to 0.86, with significant differences among all rootstocks. There were no significant rootstock cultivar effects for tree height and scion trunk diameter. There was no significant interaction between soil amendment and rootstock cultivar.

Disease status

CLas titers are expressed in cycle threshold (Ct) values. A higher Ct-value indicates lower CLas titers and vice versa. Leaf CLas titers were 20.6 on average, and all trees were infected. There were no significant differences between trees from compost-amended and no compost plots for both leaf and root Ct-values (Table S2). Rootstock had no significant effect on leaf Ct-values but did impact root Ct-values. X-639 roots had the highest Ct-value (36.1), indicating the lowest bacterial titers, while US-812 roots had the lowest Ct-value (33.2). There were no significant interaction effects.

Soil and leaf nutrients and other soil properties

Soil nutrients

The soil nutrient analysis revealed significant differences between compost-amended and no compost plots for several nutrients after four years of treatment (Table S3). Compost-amended plots contained more K (131.0 kg/ha), Mg (173.5 kg/ha), Ca (1912 kg/ha), and B (0.717 kg/ha) compared to plots without compost, which contained 72.2 kg/ha of K, 104.0 kg/ha of Mg, 1230 kg/ha of Ca, and 0.437 kg/ha of B. Compost-amended plots also had a higher CEC (5.89 meq/100 g) and contained more organic matter (OM) (0.97%) compared to no compost plots (4.43 meq/100 g and 0.80%, respectively). In contrast, no compost plots contained more Mn (28.8 kg/ha), Fe (201.2 kg/ha), and Cu (112.9 kg/ha) compared to compost-amended plots (17.2 kg/ha, 179.9 kg/ha, and 77.5 kg/ha, respectively). Compost-amended plots had a higher pH (6.67) than no compost plots (5.81) and contained higher nitrate (NO₃⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) levels (0.84 ppm and 0.37 ppm, respectively) than plots without compost (0.16 ppm and 0.14 ppm, respectively).

Multivariate principal component analyses (PCA) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) were conducted to explore differences between the compost and no compost treatments across the four years of application (Fig. 2). In 2020, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 59.0% of the total variance. Although the 95% confidence ellipses overlapped visually along these primary axes, PERMANOVA indicated a significant difference in the overall nutrient profiles between the treatment groups. By 2021, this difference became visually more apparent, as treatment groups began to diverge along PC1 (39.4% variance) and PC2 (17.9% variance). In 2022, treatments continued to separate, mostly along PC1, accounting for 30.3% of the variance. In 2023, a more distinct separation between treatments was observed along PC1 (41.9% variance). This separation was driven by specific nutrient groups. While the no compost treatment was consistently associated with higher levels of the micronutrients Mn and Cu and H+ saturation, the compost treatment was associated with higher levels of macronutrients, specifically K, Mg, and Ca, as well as B, Na, and NO3–, and elevated base saturation of K, Mg, Na, and Ca. Another driver for distinguishing between compost treatment and no compost treatment was the CEC and the amount of organic matter.

Leaf nutrients

There were significant differences in the leaf nutrient content between compost-amended and no compost plots, and among rootstock cultivars after four years of compost application (Table S4). Leaves from trees amended with compost contained more K (2.21%) compared to no compost trees (1.87%). However, no compost trees contained more leaf Mg (0.34%) than compost-treated trees (0.31%). Leaf Zn and Mn content were lower in compost-amended trees (33.3 ppm and 31.0 ppm, respectively) compared to the no compost trees (38.2 ppm and 37.0 ppm). There were no significant differences between the treatments for the other nutrients. Notably, the increase in availability of soil nutrients (e.g., Mg and Ca) did not translate into increases in leaf nutrient concentrations. Significant differences were observed among rootstocks for all leaf nutrients except N and Cu. Trees grafted on US-802 and X-639 had a higher leaf P content (0.21%) than trees grafted on US-812 (0.20%). Trees grafted on US-802 had a higher leaf K content (2.26%) than those grafted on the other rootstocks (1.87–2.02%). Trees grafted on X-639 had a higher leaf Mg content (0.37%) than trees grafted on the other rootstocks (0.30–0.32%). Trees grafted on US-802, US-812, and US-897 had a higher leaf Ca content (3.36–3.46%) than trees grafted on X-639 (3.14%), while the leaf S content was highest in trees on US-812 (0.35%) compared to trees on the other rootstocks (0.32%). Trees grafted on US-802 and US-812 had a higher leaf B content (77.9 ppm and 81.6 ppm, respectively) than those grafted on X-639 (61.6 ppm). Trees grafted on US-802 had a higher leaf Zn content (39.1 ppm) than those on US-812 and US-897 (32.8 ppm and 35.3 ppm, respectively). Trees grafted on US-897 had a higher leaf Mn content (40.9 ppm) than trees on the other rootstocks (30.4–32.8 ppm). Trees grafted on US-812 had a higher leaf Fe content (68.4 ppm) than those on US-802 (60.1 ppm).

Multivariate principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to further explore the differences between compost and no compost treatments and among rootstocks over the four years of treatment. The 95% confidence ellipses for compost and no compost trees overlapped across all four years, with no clear separation between treatments, though PERMANOVA was significant for all years (Figure S1). A similar pattern was observed for rootstock differences, with overlapping confidence ellipses but significant PERMANOVA (Figure S2).

Soil moisture

Compost-amended plots maintained a significantly higher soil moisture content than no compost plots throughout most months during the 23-month monitoring period (Figure S3A) except for a few isolated dates late in the experiment (August 2022 to January 2023). The soil moisture in compost-amended plots ranged from 8% to 18% during the first year and peaked at 22–24% between April and June 2022, coinciding with the onset of the rainy season. No compost plots exhibited lower values throughout the entire period, ranging from 4% to 12% and peaking at approximately 19% during May and June 2022. Both treatments exhibited clear seasonal patterns, with higher soil moisture contents during the wet season (April to July) and lower moisture levels during the drier winter months (September to March).

Electric conductivity (EC)

Compost-amended plots also exhibited a significantly higher soil electrical conductivity (EC) compared to no compost plots across most sampling dates (Figure S3B), except for brief periods during late summer and early winter (August–December 2022). The EC in compost-treated plots ranged from 0.03 mS cm⁻¹ to 0.12 mS cm⁻¹ during the first year and peaked at 0.24 mS cm⁻¹ in April 2022 following a period of heavy rainfall. Plots without compost exhibited lower EC values overall, ranging from 0.01 mS cm⁻¹ to 0.07 mS cm⁻¹, with a peak of 0.18 mS cm⁻¹ in April 2022. After the 2022 main rainfall period, EC declined in both treatments, stabilizing below 0.05 mS cm⁻¹ until late 2022, before rising slightly again in early 2023.

Weed biomass

In March 2022, 28 months after the first compost application, a significant effect of the soil amendment on weed dry biomass was observed (Fig. 3). Weed biomass was significantly higher in compost-amended plots (39.0 g) compared to no compost plots (11.5 g) (Table 1). Rootstock cultivar also had a significant effect on weed biomass; plots with X-639 and US-802 had a significantly higher biomass (30.5 g and 29.6 g, respectively) than those with US-897 and US-812 (22.4 g and 18.7 g, respectively). There were no significant interaction effects. In contrast, in September 2022, soil amendment did not significantly affect weed biomass, with an average of 75.6 g in the compost plots and 70.1 g in the no compost plots. There was no significant rootstock effect nor interaction.

Fibrous root specific root length (SRL)

In 2021-22, soil amendment significantly affected the SRL of 1st order roots, with compost-amended trees exhibiting a slightly higher SRL (33.2 m g⁻¹) than no compost trees (32.3 m g⁻¹) (Table 2). The rootstock had a significant effect on SRL across all root orders. For the 1st order roots, US-897 exhibited the highest SRL (36.6 m g⁻¹), followed by X-639 and US-812 (33.7 m g⁻1 and 34.2 m g⁻¹, respectively), whereas US-802 had the lowest (26.8 m g⁻¹). Similarly, the 2nd order SRL differed significantly among rootstocks, with higher values observed in US-897, US-812, and X-639 (22.2–23.7 m g⁻¹) compared to US-802 (18.5 m g⁻¹). The 3rd order SRL also varied among rootstocks, with US-812 having the highest (11.0 m g⁻¹) and US-897 the lowest (9.8 m g⁻¹). Seasonal effects were significant across all root orders. The highest SRL for 1st, 2nd, and 3rd order roots were observed in summer (34.4, 24.0, and 11.5 m g⁻¹, respectively), while the lowest were observed in spring (30.7, 20.7, and 9.3 m g⁻¹, respectively). There were no significant interaction effects.

In 2022-23, both soil amendment and rootstock significantly influenced the specific root length (SRL) of 1st order roots (Table 3). Trees in compost-amended plots had a higher 1st -order SRL (29.0 m g⁻¹) than no compost trees (26.8 m g⁻¹). Among the rootstocks, US-897 exhibited the highest 1st -order SRL (31.0 m g⁻¹), followed by X-639 and US-812 (28.3 and 27.9 m g⁻¹, respectively), while US-802 had the lowest (24.3 m g⁻¹). The 2nd -order SRL differed significantly among rootstocks but not between soil amendment. US-897, X-639, and US-812 displayed a higher 2nd -order SRL (18.5–20.3 m g⁻¹) than US-802 (16.4 m g⁻¹). The 3rd -order SRL was neither affected by soil amendment nor rootstock. All root orders were significantly affected by season, with spring and summer generally showing a higher SRL. No significant interactions were detected among soil amendment, rootstock, or season for any variable.

Fibrous root metabolomics

Untargeted gas chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS) profiling of fibrous roots revealed extensive metabolic restructuring driven by both soil amendment and rootstock cultivar. Across all samples, 373 metabolites were detected, of which 104 (27.9%) were chemically identified and 269 were unknown.

Compost applications strongly altered the fibrous root metabolome. The PLS-DA scores plot (Fig. 4A) showed a clear separation between compost-amended and no compost trees along component 1, which accounted for 21.5% of the total variation, with component 2 explaining an additional 7.9%. The biplot shows the 25 most significant metabolites that contributed to the grouping between treatments. Among these were various amino acids, organic acids, and energy-related intermediates, such as glucose-6-phosphate, valine, phenylalanine, oxoproline, shikimic acid, and several unknown metabolites, which were enriched in response to compost application. In contrast, the no compost treatment was characterized by higher levels of putrescine, maleic acid, and several other unknown compounds. These patterns were reflected in the hierarchical clustering heatmap (Fig. 4B).

Preharvest fruit drop, juice quality, and yield

Preharvest fruit drop and yield were not significantly affected by compost amendment (Table S7). In contrast, the rootstock cultivar had a significant effect on both variables. Trees grafted on US-897 produced the most fruit (6.81 kg tree⁻¹) compared to the other rootstocks (3.86–4.53 kg tree⁻¹) but also exhibited a significantly higher fruit drop rate (19.7%) compared to US-802 and X-639 (8.5% and 8.4%, respectively). Compost amendment had a significant influence on juice quality. Juice content was slightly but significantly higher in no compost trees (49.8%) than in compost-amended trees (48.4%). Fruit from compost-amended trees had a lower total soluble solids (TSS) content (6.97 °Brix) than fruit from no compost trees (7.39 °Brix) and a lower TSS/TA ratio (9.4 vs. 10.3). Titratable acid (TA) did not differ between soil amendment treatments. Rootstock cultivar significantly affected juice percentage, TSS, and TSS/TA, but not TA. US-897 produced fruit with the highest juice content (52.8%), the highest TSS (7.46 °Brix), and the highest TSS/TA ratio (10.6) of all cultivars. The lowest TSS was produced by US-812 (6.97). No significant interactions between soil amendment and rootstocks were observed for most variables, except for TSS. Trees grafted on US-897 produced the highest TSS content when grown without compost (data not shown).

Discussion

This study provides an integrated assessment of how compost amendment and rootstock influence soil properties, fibrous root physiology, metabolite profiles, and endorhizosphere active bacterial communities in young citrus trees growing under endemic HLB conditions in southwest Florida. Our findings demonstrated that compost application primarily enhanced soil properties and fibrous root physiological functioning, while the rootstock cultivar was the dominant factor determining fibrous root morphology, tree size, and productivity.

Under the specific conditions of this trial, biannual compost amendment did not translate into significant gains in vegetative growth during the first three years after planting. This lack of aboveground response may be at least partially associated with the physiological constraints imposed by HLB, including phloem dysfunction and fine-root loss8,9, which limit carbohydrate transport and restrict the tree’s ability to use additional soil resources. In contrast, the rootstock’s genetic background strongly influenced tree vigor, i.e., canopy volume, rootstock trunk diameter, and the scion/rootstock ratio, consistent with previous reports that rootstock selection drives tree vigor under HLB pressure13,14,17,53. Compost amendment also did not affect the CLas titers in the leaves or fibrous roots. In contrast, fibrous root CLas titers differed among rootstocks, though this did not result in concomitant differences in the leaves. This aligns with previous research indicating that the rootstock cultivar has little influence on CLas colonization in the grafted scion54,55.

Soil properties were substantially affected by soil amendment. Compost increased the SOM and the CEC as well as the nitrate and ammonium content, and the content of several macro- and micronutrients, while also increasing cation saturation. Similar effects were previously documented when organic amendments were applied to citrus in other production sites in Florida56–58. However, improvements and increases in soil properties and nutrients, respectively, did not translate into concomitant increases in the leaves. In fact, leaf nutrient responses were sometimes inverse to those of the soil, possibly reflecting HLB-induced disruption of nutrient uptake and redistribution59,60. For example, despite an increased soil Mg content in response to compost application, the leaf Mg content remained unchanged, and compost-treated trees exhibited lower leaf Zn and Mn levels than untreated trees. The lower leaf nutrient levels observed here likely reflect the elevated pH and high sorption capacity associated with compost-amended soils. Furthermore, while an increased CEC is beneficial for nutrient retention and for preventing leaching, it also facilitates the sequestration of Zn and Mn into more stable manganese and iron oxide fractions. As demonstrated by Shuman et al.61, compost can redistribute metals from highly mobile, water-soluble pools into less mobile and less soluble forms. Therefore, while the total soil nutrient content increases, the immediate availability of Zn and Mn may be reduced due to the strong chemical binding supported by the increased organic carbon, CEC, and pH.

Compost applications improved soil moisture retention and increased soil electrical conductivity, with effects persisting across seasons, a response that is particularly relevant for HLB-affected trees that are sensitive to transient water deficits59. Consistent with these changes in soil physical conditions, compost-amended plots exhibited greater weed pressure than plots without compost early in the season (spring). However, this effect was not sustained into late summer, the rainy season, when weed pressure is high in Florida. This seasonal pattern aligns with the findings of Pokhrel et al.58, who found a lower weed biomass in early spring compared to summer. However, in that study, the weed biomass was unaffected by the soil amendment, regardless of the season. These results suggest that compost amendment may enhance early-season weed establishment under limiting conditions, while seasonal climatic drivers ultimately override the effects of the amendment as weed growth becomes less constrained.

The significant difference in weed biomass among rootstock cultivars during the spring season may be attributed to competition with the trees’ fibrous roots. In the poorly drained Spodosols of Southwest Florida, citrus roots are notably shallow, often restricted to the upper 30 to 40 cm of the soil10,62. Previous characterization of different citrus rootstock cultivars in this environment confirmed that roots occupy a limited depth of approximately 33 cm62. Vigorous rootstocks, such as US-802 and X-639, may prioritize biomass allocation to structural lateral roots for anchorage over fibrous roots, therefore, generating less competition for water and nutrients in the topsoil, and allowing weeds to grow better than less vigorous rootstocks.

Compost increased the fibrous root respiration and first-order SRL, indicating greater fine-root metabolic activity. This is consistent with other studies on citrus and other trees where increased soil moisture and nitrogen availability, both enhanced by compost in this study, lead to elevated root metabolism and respiration rates58,63,64. Rootstock effects were more pronounced for fine-rooted cultivars, such as US-897, which consistently exhibited a higher SRL and root metabolic activity, reflecting the genetic control of fine-root traits previously documented in citrus62,65.

The untargeted metabolomic analysis revealed that compost increased several nitrogen assimilation and stress associated amino acids, including aspartate, glutamine, and proline, suggesting improved nutrient assimilation, in line with the higher nutrient supply measured in this study. This aligns with Neugart et al.66 who found that compost and other amendments enhanced the concentration of several amino acids involved in nitrogen assimilation and stress in pak choi. Proline is known for its various beneficial functions in plants exposed to stress67, suggesting a potential stress-moderating effect of compost. The elevated glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) levels in compost-treated roots found in this study suggest an increase in carbohydrate flow towards primary metabolism. As a critical metabolic intermediate, G6P fuels both energy production and the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway. This pathway is the primary source of NADPH, a vital reductant for nitrogen assimilation and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS)68. In contrast, no compost roots accumulated higher levels of putrescine, a stress-related polyamine commonly associated with nutrient and oxidative stress responses69–71. Taken together, these findings suggest that compost may have reduced fibrous root stress in our study – however, this did not translate into any measurable effects on the grafted scion, likely because of the overriding physiological constraints imposed by HLB.

The rootstock cultivar also affected the fibrous root metabolic composition. US-897 and X-639 had the most similar metabolic profiles, which is consistent with their shared genetic background as hybrids of ‘Cleopatra’ mandarin (C. reticulata) and trifoliate orange (P. trifoliata). The metabolic profile of US-802, a hybrid of pummelo (C. maxima) and trifoliate orange, was most distinct from the other rootstocks, reflecting the different genetic background of this cultivar.

Compost altered the fibrous root endosphere bacterial community composition, producing significant shifts despite limited changes in alpha diversity. Similar responses were found in other trees where organic amendment reshaped microbial composition more than microbial richness24,72. A previous study conducted under similar production conditions as this study reported that compost primarily modified rhizosphere bacterial community composition and the relative abundance of key taxa, with comparatively minor effects on diversity indices73. These changes likely reflect compost-driven alterations in SOM, soil moisture, and root exudation patterns. Rootstock effects on the fibrous root endosphere communities were not significant, possibly due to the dominant influence of the soil environment.

Differential abundance analysis also demonstrated a targeted shift in community composition rather than a broad increase in the endorhizosphere diversity due to compost. Roots from compost-amended trees exhibited a higher relative abundance of taxa potentially involved in carbon cycling (e.g., Anaerolineae, Desulfuromonadia, Blastocatellia, and Vicinamibacteria). This enrichment aligns with ecological frameworks that suggest organic inputs increase carbon availability, favoring responsive, copiotrophic taxa74,75. In contrast, bacterial classes typically associated with low-nutrient or stress-prone environments, such as certain classes of Actinobacteria, Chloroflexia, Planctomycetes, and Cyanobacteria, were significantly more abundant in roots from trees that did not receive any compost. This finding is consistent with reports that oligotrophic bacteria tend to persist in soils with limited availability of labile carbon76.

Differential abundance analysis at the family level revealed that compost application induced selective and rootstock-dependent shifts in the bacterial community of the fibrous root endorhizosphere. Compost-amended trees showed enrichment of a limited number of families, most consistently Comamonadaceae, in US-802 and US-812. This pattern suggests a targeted response to increased organic inputs, likely favoring copiotrophic taxa within the Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria) phylum that thrive in nutrient-rich environments77.

In contrast, families enriched under no compost conditions were present across all rootstocks, particularly Coleofasciculaceae and Acidothermaceae. These patterns indicate that compost applications may reduce the relative abundance of taxa adapted to low-nutrient or stress-prone environments while maintaining overall microbial stability. The enrichment of Acidothermaceae aligns with previous findings that identify this family as a biomarker for high-stress conditions78. The prevalence of Coleofasciculaceae (Cyanobacteria) in non-amended soils is consistent with their known capacity to colonize infertile substrates and tolerate extreme environmental stresses79. The variable magnitude of responses among rootstocks, with US-812 showing greater sensitivity and X-639 minimal change, highlights the critical role of host genotype in mediating microbiome responses to soil amendment.

Despite the notable improvements in soil properties, fibrous root structure, and physiology, compost applications did not affect yield or fruit drop, similar to the findings of Pokhrel et al.58, suggesting a limited capacity of HLB-affected trees to translate improved soil conditions into fruiting responses. Rootstocks with higher fine-root activity, such as US-897, produced more fruit despite experiencing relatively more fruit drop. Compost produced subtle changes in juice quality, including a lower TSS content and a smaller TSS/TA ratio. This pattern is consistent with irrigation studies in citrus showing that greater soil moisture availability leads to dilution of soluble solids and lower TSS in citrus fruit80. One reason for a lack of yield response to compost, despite notable belowground improvements, may be the increased weed pressure observed in compost-treated plots during the early-season spring flush. Previous studies have shown that organic amendment can stimulate weed biomass and competitiveness due to enhanced nutrient supply, sometimes offsetting positive crop responses. For example, soybean and corn yield was reduced when the weed biomass increased in response to applications of composted swine manure81,82. In our study, the enhanced nutrient availability and moisture retention in the compost-amended topsoil likely stimulated aggressive early-season weed growth, creating resource competition. This competition could have limited the young citrus trees’ ability to capitalize on the compost-induced benefits and explain why the belowground gains did not translate into measurable aboveground gains under endemic HLB.

Conclusion

Overall, compost improved soil nutrient availability, moisture retention, and root metabolic function, while driving significant shifts in endorhizosphere microbial community composition; yet these belowground gains did not translate into improved tree growth or productivity. The rootstock cultivar was the primary determinant of most tree morphological and physiological traits, including canopy size, fine-root traits, nutrient uptake efficiency, metabolic capacity, and yield, reflecting inherent genetic strategies for resource acquisition and carbon allocation. These findings underscore that rootstock selection remains an important aspect of management under endemic HLB, and that soil amendment strategies alone are insufficient to overcome the limiting effects of the disease.
