Virtual reality versus conventional exercise for knee osteoarthritis: protocol for a randomised controlled trial on functionality and fall risk
Authors: Isabel Tunas-Maceiras, Javier Pereira, Sonia Pértega Díaz, José Ángel López Campos, Beatriz Rodríguez-Romero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionKnee osteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of disability in older adults, with health and economic impacts. Despite pharmacological advances, exercise continues to be a fundamental pillar in the management of OA, with lower limb strength training showing significant benefits. Virtual reality (VR)-based interventions have emerged as innovative tools, providing immersive environments to facilitate functional movement exercises. VR offers pain relief, improved functionality and reduced fall risks, although its efficacy in OA management requires further exploration. The main aim of the study is to assess whether a VR-based intervention provides superior improvements in pain, stiffness, physical function and movement biomechanics compared with conventional therapeutic exercise in adults aged 60 years and older with knee OA.Methods and analysisThis is a protocol for a randomised controlled trial comparing the effects of immersive VR interventions with conventional therapeutic exercises in individuals aged 60 years and older with knee OA. Participants are allocated 1:1 to experimental (VR) and control groups. The VR intervention involves 18 supervised sessions over 8 weeks, using Meta Quest 3 goggles to perform functional movements in virtual environments. The control group follows standard therapeutic exercise protocols per Osteoarthritis Research Society International guidelines. Outcomes include OA-related symptoms, kinematic performance, pain intensity, kinesiophobia and fall risk. Secondary measures assess cybersickness, depressive symptoms, medication use and comorbidities. Assessments occur at baseline, ninth week, sixth and 12th months. Data analysis employs intention-to-treat principles, leveraging descriptive statistics, t-tests and multiple imputations for missing data.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the A Coruña-Ferrol Research Ethics Committee (reference: 2023/557), under the Galician Health Service. All participants will be required to provide written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study. Participant data will be pseudonymised and securely stored. Additionally, anonymised datasets will be deposited in open-access repositories (Zenodo).Trial registration numberNCT06362785.Keywords: Knee, Virtual Reality, Physical Therapy Modalities, Aged, Musculoskeletal disorders, RHEUMATOLOGY

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the leading cause of disability among older adults,1 with a global prevalence of 22.9% in people aged 40 years and older.2 According to the OA Research Society International (OARSI), OA is defined as a ‘disorder involving movable joints characterised by cell stress and extracellular matrix degradation initiated by micro- and macro-injury that activates maladaptive repair responses including pro-inflammatory pathways of innate immunity’.3 Specifically, knee OA is the most common joint disease and is associated with significant pain, disability and premature mortality.4

Knee OA significantly impacts quality of life and poses a major economic burden, with lifetime costs averaging $140 3005 and OA-related healthcare expenses accounting for 1–2.5% of gross domestic product in developed nations.6 Likewise, knee OA in older adults has significant consequences for their quality of life and functionality, increasing fall risk and reducing independence.7

It is essential to develop non-pharmacological approaches that are safe and effective for the treatment of knee OA in older adults. VR-based interventions have emerged as a promising alternative, as they have demonstrated their potential to improve health outcomes in older adults.8 Furthermore, VR has demonstrated both statistically and clinically significant effects on outcomes such as pain management, muscle strength and functional mobility in individuals with knee OA. Moreover, in this population, VR-based interventions have been shown to enhance rehabilitation adherence and patient engagement compared with conventional therapies.9 Immersive VR has been identified as a viable and effective option for improving balance and reducing the risk of falls in older adults due to its high tolerance.10

Although VR has been more extensively studied in the context of knee arthroplasty,11 few studies have investigated its effects on individuals with OA, with a lack of solid evidence regarding strategies for treating knee OA.12 In this context, a recent study examining immersive VR-assisted lower limb strength training in individuals over 60 years old with knee OA found that participants expressed a strong desire to incorporate advanced technology into managing their health conditions, despite encountering some challenges with its use. They reported that VR helped them manage knee OA symptoms and improved adherence to exercise programmes.13

Among therapeutic options, therapeutic exercise is widely recognised as a fundamental pillar in knee OA treatment, as it contributes to pain relief and functional improvement.1 14

In previous studies comparing conventional treatments based on therapeutic exercise with VR-based intervention in individuals with knee OA, therapeutic exercise has been found to significantly improve pain intensity, physical capacity and balance, whereas the VR interventions have primarily shown improvements in biomechanical parameters.13 15

Although the results of these studies are inconsistent, the discrepancy may be attributed to the type of VR technology used, as they primarily relied on non-immersive systems, such as the Xbox 36015 or smartphones adapted to a head-mounted display,13 which may not provide the same level of engagement and sensory feedback as fully immersive VR headsets.

Immersive headsets, which incorporate motion-tracked stereoscopic head-mounted display (HMDs) and, in some cases, motion controllers, have been associated with an enhanced sense of presence, realism, embodiment, memory and spatial awareness compared with non-immersive devices.16

Additionally, regarding pain relief with VR, studies indicate that more immersive forms of VR are more likely to achieve musculoskeletal pain relief compared with less immersive forms.17

In some cases, VR headsets were used not for therapeutic exercise but rather for virtual environment visualisation or meditation practices.18 19 Additionally, some studies applied VR in home settings13 or implemented it without providing feedback on exercise execution,18 which could have affected treatment effectiveness.

As an emerging treatment modality, VR requires further clinical trials, and future directions in this field include more research to standardise its application and thus understand the underlying mechanisms and the impact of VR on physiological, psychological and functional factors.20

On the other hand, OARSI has recommended guidelines for assessing physical function in individuals with knee OA.21 While visual assessments of functional task execution by trained healthcare professionals provide valuable insights, the gold standard for movement analysis remains marker-based 3D motion capture systems.22 This shift in evaluation methodology holds the potential to revolutionise the understanding and application of rehabilitation strategies, moving beyond isolated task success to focus on real-world functionality.23 The incorporation of kinematic analysis allows for a more precise evaluation of movement patterns. As previous studies suggest, future research should focus on identifying subgroups within the affected population based on specific movement profiles.12

Additionally, evidence suggests that certain biomechanical gait parameters are linked to a higher risk of knee OA onset and progression. In particular, the presence of varus thrust has been associated with knee OA progression.24 In scientific literature, the majority of studies primarily focus on gait, rather than addressing other activities.25

In addition, VR’s non-invasive nature and ability to reduce reliance on pharmacological treatments also offer a sustainable and cost-effective solution for healthcare systems.26 Although this type of therapy impacts pain perception, disability and treatment satisfaction, there is still insufficient scientific literature to conclude that VR is superior to other treatments.20

It is hypothesised that old adults with knee OA who undergo VR intervention will demonstrate better symptom management, functionality and fall risk reduction compared with a control group. The main aim of the present study is to assess whether a virtual reality-based intervention in individuals aged 60 years and older with knee OA demonstrates superior effects compared with therapeutic exercise in improving symptom management (pain, stiffness and physical function) and movement biomechanics.

In addition, the following secondary objectives are established: (1) to identify the characteristics of patients with OA associated with kinematics differences during functional tasks; (2) to observe how the use of VR impacts kinesiophobia, fall risk, cybersickness, general health status and depressive symptoms and (3) to assess whether kinesiophobia, falls, cybersickness, general health status, depressive symptoms and demographic factors influence the effectiveness of VR.

Methods: patient and public involvement, trial design

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Trial design

This interventional study is designed as a unicentre, research-blinded, randomised, controlled study with two parallel groups. Randomisation will be performed as a block randomisation with a 1:1 allocation.

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Trial setting

The study will be carried out in the area of influence of A Coruña (Spain).

Assessments will take place at the Research Center for Information and Communication Technologies (CITIC), while the intervention will be carried out at the CITIC, Faculty of Physiotherapy of the University of A Coruña and primary health centres in A Coruña city.

Interventions and comparator

Eligible patients will be randomised in equal proportions between an experimental and a control group. The intervention group will engage in immersive virtual reality sessions, participating in interactive activities that involve functional body movements within a virtual environment, using Meta Quest 3 goggles. This custom-designed virtual environment will be complemented by a 3D motion capture system to provide real-time feedback to the participants. It has been demonstrated that therapeutic exercise programmes, particularly those focused on strengthening, are effective in managing knee OA over a 6-month period.27 Moreover, recent evidence suggests that therapeutic exercise for knee OA indicates that interventions of 8 weeks, with a frequency of three sessions per week, are required to achieve clinically meaningful improvements in pain and physical function.28 29 Therefore, the planned intervention will last for 8 weeks, comprising a total of 24 sessions, with a maximum duration of 30 min of virtual reality and 15 min of rest (45 min in total per session). Individual sessions will be conducted, as studies have demonstrated that supervision by a healthcare professional achieves a greater effect size compared with group supervision.27 Furthermore, due to the use of VR, individual oversight is essential to ensure patient safety, given the potential risks associated with unsupervised VR-based exercises.

On the other hand, the control group will receive conventional therapeutic exercise treatment following the recommendations of the OARSI, similar to the exercises performed with VR.

The details of the exercises performed are provided in the online supplemental material.

The virtual reality intervention may be interrupted or modified at any time if any of the following situations arise: (1) if the participant reports discomfort, dizziness or significant distress that prevents them from continuing the activity; (2) if any changes in the participant’s physical or mental state are detected that may compromise their safety or well-being and (3) if unforeseen situations arise that could affect the validity of the results or the integrity of the study. In any of these cases, the need to adapt the intervention or suspend it temporarily will be assessed, always prioritising the participant’s health and safety. Adherence and compliance will be collected, and additionally, an open-ended individual question on participants’ opinions will be included.

To enhance adherence to intervention protocols, several strategies will be implemented: (1) educational pills: participants will receive comprehensive training on the virtual reality intervention, including its purpose, procedures and expected outcomes, to foster understanding and motivation; (2) clear communication: regular communication will be maintained with participants to address any concerns, provide reminders and ensure that they feel supported throughout the process; (3) flexible scheduling: offering flexible scheduling options for intervention sessions will accommodate participants’ individual needs and increase their likelihood of attending. Participants will be allowed to continue their usual analgesic medication at stable doses. No additional physiotherapy or structured exercise programmes outside the assigned intervention will be permitted during the trial.

Outcomes

The measurement procedures are detailed below. All measurements are taken at baseline, with follow-up assessments at the ninth week, at the end of the intervention (at the sixth month) and 12th month from the start of the intervention to evaluate changes from the initial baseline values, except for anthropometric data, which are collected only at baseline.

The primary variables studied are symptomatology related to knee OA, kinematics of the spine and extremities and pain intensity, while secondary variables include kinesiophobia, falls, cybersickness, general health status and depressive symptoms. Additionally, demographic and clinical variables will be analysed.

Knee OA variables will be assessed using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities OA Index (WOMAC), a self-administered questionnaire with 24 items across three subscales: pain, stiffness and physical function. Items are scored from 0 (none) to 4 (extreme). Subscale scores range from 0 to 20 (pain), 0–8 (stiffness) and 0–68 (physical function), with a total WOMAC score indicating the severity of pain, stiffness and functional limitations.30

Sample size

The G-Power programme was used to estimate the minimum number of participants per group required to detect medium effect sizes with a type I error probability of 0.05 and a power of 0.80. Using these parameters, a total of 128 participants (64 per group) are required for an effect size of 0.5, considering analysis using the independent two-sample Student’s t-test. This sample size is also sufficient for the planned study to detect changes between groups in the WOMAC index using a repeated-measures Analysis Of Variance (ANOVA), with an expected effect size f=0.22 (α=0.05 and power=0.80). The design considered two groups with four repeated measurements and an assumed correlation of 0.7 among repeated measures. Considering the findings of Lin et al,40 an effect size of about 0.22 seems justified, as detecting smaller effects would not provide sufficient rationale for preferring virtual reality over conventional exercise.

Recruitment

The recruitment of participants will be carried out through the dissemination of the study via informative posters distributed in the facilities of University of A Coruña; in the network of community centres in the city of A Coruña; through the musculoskeletal disease clinic at University Hospital of A Coruña and primary care consultations in the A Coruña health area; as well as electronically through the researchers’ social media networks (X, Facebook and Instagram). Anyone interested in participating will contact the research team, which will verify the eligibility criteria before signing the informed consent. The participant information process, as well as the request for informed consent, will be conducted in the CITIC, after having answered the preselection questionnaires.

Methods: assignment of interventions

Randomisation

For the generation of the allocation sequence in this study, computer-generated random numbers will be used through Research Randomizer. Stratification will be conducted based on relevant demographic and clinical factors, depending on the sample size. To generate the allocation sequence in this study, a computer-generated random number sequence will be obtained using Research Randomizer. Randomisation will be stratified according to gender and age (considering two groups, 60–80 vs ≥80 years). Within each of the four strata, randomisation will occur in blocks of two in a 1:1 ratio to ensure even allocation.

To reduce the predictability of the random sequence, allocation blocks will be implemented.

To ensure the integrity of the study, an allocation concealment mechanism will be employed using sealed, opaque and sequentially numbered envelopes. These envelopes will be securely stored until the moment of intervention assignment. This measure will ensure that the allocation sequence remains concealed until all corresponding assignments have been made, minimising the risk of bias.

Blinding

The allocation sequence will be generated by an independent investigator with experience in clinical trial methodology. The recruitment of participants will be handled by physiotherapists.

Due to the nature of the study, full blinding is not feasible, as both the participants and the researchers will know who is receiving the virtual reality intervention and who is in the control group. Due to the fact that the evaluator will perform the intervention and data analysis, a clear protocol will be followed to maintain the study’s integrity and ethical standards.

Methods: data collection, management and analysis

Data collection methods

To promote participant retention and ensure complete follow-up, various strategies will be implemented, such as regular reminders and continuous support throughout the trial. These include sending an email reminder for each session and a message on Fridays to gather feedback on how symptoms have been throughout the week. Data will be collected from participants who withdraw from the study or deviate from the protocol, including reasons for withdrawal, time spent in the study and any changes in their health status. The outcomes to be studied, as previously described in the Outcomes section, will include relevant parameters to measure the impact of the intervention.

The intervention will be conducted by two physiotherapists: one with extensive clinical experience working with individuals with knee OA and the other with experience in knee OA management coupled with strong expertise in the use of technological systems, including OptiTrack and VR. Both physiotherapists will receive specific training on the study procedures and the use of the VR and OptiTrack systems prior to the start of the intervention to ensure consistency.

Data management

The necessary data to carry out this study will be collected and stored pseudonymised (coded). This is the processing of personal data in such a way that it cannot be attributed to an individual without the use of additional information. In this study, only the research team will have access to the code that links to the identity of the participant. Similarly, it cannot be guaranteed that the participant will not be identified in the recordings made. However, to protect their identity, all captured images will be coded by assigning a unique number, unlinking them from any direct personal data, and the images will be temporarily stored on a secure server at the University of A Coruña. Additionally, once the study is completed, all images will be permanently destroyed.

Statistical methods

A descriptive analysis will be performed to characterise the subjects in detail, globally and by experimental and control groups. Qualitative variables will be presented as absolute figures with their respective percentages, and quantitative variables as measures of central tendency (mean and median) and dispersion (SD, minimum, maximum and IQR). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test will be used to assess the normality of data distribution.

To explore significant differences between groups, a bivariate statistical analysis will be conducted, both for primary and secondary outcomes at different time points. The Student’s t-test for independent samples will be used for comparing means if the variables under consideration are normal; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test will be employed for non-normal variables. The χ2 will be applied for the comparison of percentages. Effect measures and their 95% CIs will be estimated to compare outcome data between the two intervention groups. For dichotomous outcomes, the risk ratio, risk difference and the number needed to treat will be calculated. For continuous outcomes, the standardised mean difference will be determined. The correlation between quantitative variables will be assessed using the Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficient. Changes over time in primary and secondary outcomes will also be analysed in both the intervention and control groups using statistical tests for paired data. Paired Student’s t-test or signed Wilcoxon rank test will be used for continuous outcomes and McNemar’s test for qualitative outcomes. Improvement in results will be compared between groups and according to different sociodemographic clinical characteristics, using a bivariate statistical strategy similar to that described above. Finally, repeated-measures logistic and linear regression analysis will be used to summarise the effect of the intervention on outcomes over 12 months, while also identifying characteristics associated with changes in outcomes, controlling for the intervention received.

A significance level of p value<0.05 will be considered. Data processing and analysis will be carried out using the statistical package SPSS, V.24.0 for Windows (Armonk, New York: IBM, USA).

The analysis population is defined considering the level of protocol adherence by the participants. An intention-to-treat approach will be used, where all subjects will be analysed according to the group to which they were randomised, regardless of their adherence to treatment or full protocol compliance. This approach is chosen to preserve the effects of randomisation and avoid biases arising from dropouts or protocol deviations.

Methods: monitoring

Data monitoring committee

A data monitoring committee is not necessary because the trial involves non-invasive, well-established exercise protocols with minimal risk, and its straightforward, low-risk design allows the research team to manage participant safety and data monitoring directly, following established protocols.

Trial monitoring

The trial will be monitored directly by the research team throughout its duration. Given the non-invasive nature of the intervention and its low-risk profile, formal external monitoring is not deemed necessary. The research team will conduct regular checks on protocol adherence, data quality and participant safety during each session. In the event that significant risks are identified, such as an increase in the incidence of adverse effects related to the virtual reality experience (dizziness, nausea, cybersickness), or if it is determined that the intervention is clearly harmful, the researchers may decide to discontinue the participant’s involvement.

Ethics

Research ethics approval

A favourable report was obtained for the study with registration code 2023/557 from the A Coruña-Ferrol Research Ethics Committee under the Galician Health Service (SERGAS).

Protocol amendments

Any important modifications to the protocol that may impact the study’s conduct, participant safety or scientific validity, such as changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes, sample size or data analysis methods, will be promptly communicated to all relevant parties. These include the investigators, the A Coruña-Ferrol Research Ethics Committee, ClinicalTrials.gov registry and study participants. All amendments will be documented with dates and version numbers in a protocol revision chronology.

Consent or assent

All participants will receive a participant information sheet that includes the study’s title, the researchers involved, the institution where it is being conducted, the study’s purpose, the reason they are being invited to participate, what their participation will involve, any potential discomforts or inconveniences, whether they will benefit from participating, the type of information they will receive from the study, whether the results will be published, how their data will be collected (in this case, pseudonymised or coded), if there are any financial interests in the study and how to contact the research team. After receiving the participant information sheet, they will be provided with a consent form to voluntarily agree to participate in the study (See online supplemental file 1).

Patients must provide written, informed consent before any study procedures occur.

Confidentiality

The data required to conduct this study will be collected and stored in a pseudonymised (coded) manner. Pseudonymisation involves processing personal data in such a way that it cannot be attributed to a specific individual without the use of additional information. In this study, only the research team will have access to the code that allows the identification of individuals.

Likewise, it cannot be guaranteed that participants will not be identifiable in the recordings made. However, to protect their identity, all captured images will be coded by assigning a unique number, disconnecting any direct personal data. These images will be temporarily stored on a secure server at the University of A Coruña. Furthermore, once the study is completed, all images will be permanently destroyed.

Ancillary and post-trial care

Given the non-invasive nature of this trial, no specific ancillary or post-trial care is planned. However, any adverse events that arise during the study will be documented and managed appropriately by the research team, and participants will be referred to their healthcare providers if needed. Participants will not receive financial compensation for their participation.
