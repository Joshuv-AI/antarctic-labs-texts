Impact of the clonal architecture and probable biallelic inactivation of TET2 mutations in myelodysplastic neoplasms
Authors: Yudi Zhang, Wei Wang, Qiaoli Li, Li Ye, Xinping Zhou, Lingxu Jiang, Liya Ma, Yingwan Luo, Hongyan Tong
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

TET2 mutations are among the most frequent genetic alterations in myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS), but their prognostic significance remains controversial [1–3]. Previous studies suggest TET2 acquisition timing may shape clinical phenotypes and clonal evolution [4, 5], whereas biallelic TET2 inactivation has been associated with monocytosis, chronic myelomonocytic leukemia-like biology [6, 7], and possibly a distinct MDS subgroup enriched for splicing factor co-mutations [8]. However, whether the inferred TET2 clonal hierarchy adds prognostic value beyond TET2 mutation status alone in MDS remains unclear. Here, using two independent MDS-restricted cohorts, we investigated the clinical and prognostic relevance of TET2 clonal hierarchy and probable biallelic TET2 inactivation.

The study cohort comprised 701 de novo MDS patients according to the WHO 2016 criteria [9] from our institution and an external validation cohort of 2,165 patients from the International Working Group for the Prognosis of MDS (IWG-PM; https://www.cbioportal.org/). Baseline characteristics (Table S1) and methods are provided in the Supplementary Information. Consistent with published methodology, clonal hierarchy was inferred by ranking variant allele frequencies (VAFs) [10]. TET2 was classified as ancestral when its VAF was the highest in the sample (dominant) or within 5% of the highest-VAF mutation (co-dominant), and as secondary otherwise. Probable biallelic TET2 inactivation was defined as a cumulative TET2 VAF ≥ 50% (derived from single or multiple mutations) [6].

Within our institutional cohort, TET2 was the third most common mutation; 184 variants were identified, and 38/133 (29%) cases harbored multiple TET2 lesions (Figs. 1A; S1). TET2 mutation prevalence increased with age. The median VAF was 42%. Missense, frameshift, and nonsense variants were the major classes. C > T transition was the predominant base-pair change, consistent with an aging-associated mutational signature [11] (Fig. S2). In the IWG-PM cohort, TET2 was the most frequently mutated gene (n = 619; 29%; Fig. S3), and all 619 TET2-mutated cases included in downstream analyses are listed in Table S2. We identified 976 TET2 variant records for site-level mapping. Sixty-eight (51.1%) cases exhibited ancestral (dominant/co-dominant) TET2MT, and 65 (48.9%) had secondary TET2MT in our cohort. Ancestral TET2MT was associated with younger age (63 versus 68 years, P < 0.001), higher monocyte counts (0.27 versus 0.19 × 109/L, P = 0.02) and lower IPSS-M risks (P = 0.008), while secondary TET2MT was enriched for MDS-EB (63% versus 39.7%, P = 0.007) and higher-risk WHO 2022 criteria subtypes (MDS-biTP53, MDS-f and MDS-IB; 64.6% versus 42.6%; P = 0.008; Table S3). Of 619 TET2-mutated IWG-PM cases, 371 (60%) harbored ancestral and 248 (40%) secondary mutations. Similarly, ancestral TET2MT was associated with higher hemoglobin (10 g/dL versus 9.5 g/dL), higher monocyte counts (0.42 versus 0.32 × 109/L), and lower IPSS-M (P < 0.001) and lower IPSS-R risk (P = 0.004; Table S3).

Patients with ancestral TET2MT had fewer co-mutations on average than those with secondary TET2MT (mean 2.98 versus 4.83, our cohort; 5.41 versus 6.77, IWG-PM cohort; all P < 0.001). When TET2 was the first hit, the most common second mutation was another TET2, followed by ASXL1 and U2AF1. When TET2 was the secondary hit, the dominant clone most frequently involved ASXL1 and RUNX1. Ancestral TET2MT MDS showed higher U2AF1 prevalence (18% versus 6%, P = 0.04). Secondary TET2MT cases were enriched for ASXL1 (42% versus 22%, P = 0.02), TP53 (22% versus 9%, P = 0.04), RUNX1 (28% versus 13%, P = 0.04), EZH2 (22% versus 2%, P < 0.001), ZRSR2 (22% versus 2%, P < 0.001), and STAG2 (22% versus 5%, P = 0.004) (Fig. 1B, C). In the IWG-PM cohort, secondary TET2MT cases likewise showed higher co-mutation rates than ancestral TET2MT cases for ASXL1 (40% versus 20%), RUNX1 (22% versus 13%), ZRSR2 (21% versus 6%), STAG2 (20% versus 5%), and EZH2 (15% versus 6%) (all P < 0.001; Fig. S5).

We next explored whether common co-mutations modified phenotype in ancestral TET2MT MDS within our cohort. Subclonal TET2 was associated with higher monocytes (0.39 versus 0.24×109/L, P = 0.009); subclonal ASXL1 with less leukopenia (3.91 versus 2.78 × 109/L; P = 0.04), more complex karyotypes (27% vs 4%, P = 0.02), and higher IPSS-R/IPSS-M risks (P = 0.01 and P = 0.002); subclonal RUNX1 with lower platelet counts (18 versus 67 × 109/L; P = 0.02); and subclonal SF3B1 mutation with higher platelet counts (182 versus 49 × 109/L, P < 0.001) and higher mean corpuscular volume (MCV, 116 versus 95 fL, P = 0.004).

Overall, TET2 mutation status alone was not associated with overall survival (OS; Fig. 1D, E). However, secondary TET2MT MDS was associated with shorter survival than ancestral TET2MT and TET2MT-negative cases in our cohort (19 versus 61 versus 42 months, P = 0.03 and P = 0.02, respectively; Fig. 1F). Similarly, ancestral TET2MT MDS showed longer survival than secondary TET2MT and TET2MT-negative cases in the IWG-PM cohort (56 versus 40 versus 42 months, P = 0.02 and P = 0.01, respectively; Fig. 1G). Given the high prevalence of TET2 mutations in clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) and clonal cytopenias of undetermined significance (CCUS) [12], our findings are compatible with a CHIP/CCUS-to-MDS evolutionary continuum in a subset of patients, possibly contributing to a relatively indolent course.

In our cohort, TET2 clonal hierarchy retained prognostic significance (HR = 1.21; 95%CI,1.01-1.45, P = 0.04) when analyzed with IPSS-R, but not with IPSS-M in multivariate analysis (Table S4). In a gene-level Cox model adjusting for cytogenetic risk and recurrent co-mutations, secondary TET2 hierarchy remained independently associated with inferior OS (HR = 1.20; 95%CI,1.01-1.42, P = 0.04; Table S5). Because hypomethylating agents (HMAs) are commonly used in MDS and TET2 mutations have been reported to associate with HMA response [13], we performed exploratory OS analyses stratified by HMA exposure; OS patterns differed by HMA exposure (Fig. S6). We additionally evaluated leukemic transformation (LT) and acute myeloid leukemia free survival (AMLFS) by clonal hierarchy. Secondary TET2MT showed shorter AMLFS compared to ancestral TET2MT (P = 0.01) and TET2WT (P = 0.003) in our cohort, while ancestral TET2MT showed superior AMLFS versus TET2WT cases (P = 0.005) in the IWG-PM cohort (Table S6; Fig. S7).

Furthermore, allelic-state effects were evaluated. Forty-seven cases met criteria for probable biallelic TET2MT (29 with ≥2 TET2 mutations and cumulative VAF ≥ 50%, 18 defined solely by a single TET2 mutation with VAF ≥ 50%). The remaining 86 cases had monoallelic TET2MT (77 single mutations with VAF < 50% and 9 multiple mutations with cumulative VAFs <50%; Fig. 2A–C). Splicing factor mutations occurred in 57% of probable biallelic TET2MT MDS, most frequently involving U2AF1 (19%), SRSF2 (19%), and ZRSR2 (11%). Comparative genomic analysis revealed higher mutation frequencies of ASXL1 (34% versus 20%; OR = 2.06, 95% CI, 1.09–3.89, P = 0.02), RUNX1 (28% versus 12%, OR = 2.81, 95% CI, 1.41–5.59, P = 0.002), SRSF2 (19% versus 6%, OR = 3.72, 95% CI, 1.66–8.32, P < 0.001), ZRSR2 (11% versus 2%, OR = 6.03, 95% CI, 2.00–18.16, P < 0.001) and KRAS (9% versus 3%, OR = 3.02, 95% CI, 1.11–9.36, P = 0.03) in probable biallelic TET2MT cases compared with cases without TET2MT. KRAS (9% versus 0%, P = 0.01) was more frequent in probable biallelic TET2MT compared to monoallelic TET2MT cases (Fig. 2D, E).

Similar to the IWG-PM cohort [8], OS did not differ between probable biallelic TET2MT and other MDS in our cohort (Fig. 2F). HMA-stratified analyses likewise showed no within-stratum OS difference (Fig. S8), although residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded. LT rates were comparable across inferred TET2 allelic-status groups (Table S8). Given the small probable biallelic subgroup, these comparisons were likely underpowered.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, clonal hierarchy was inferred from bulk-sequencing VAFs, which can be influenced by sampling variability, tumor heterogeneity, specimen purity, and copy-number alterations, potentially distorting apparent clonal dominance. Moreover, without single-cell sequencing, co-occurring TET2 mutations cannot definitively be assigned to the same or distinct clones. These limitations and nuances of VAF-based clonal ordering were discussed previously [14, 15]. Second, lacking a uniform copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity assessment, probable biallelic TET2 was inferred from multiple mutations and/or cumulative VAF ≥ 50%. This may over- or underestimate true biallelic loss, particularly as a single high-VAF mutation might merely reflect heterozygous clonal dominance. In a sensitivity analysis excluding these specific cases, the strictly ‘multihit’ subgroup showed inferior survival (Fig. S10). Despite the small sample size, this suggests unequivocal multi-hit TET2 loss may capture a biologically distinct subset, requiring validation in larger cohorts with allelic-state confirmation. In summary, our data indicate that both clonal hierarchy and allelic status contribute to molecular and clinical heterogeneity in TET2-mutated MDS.
