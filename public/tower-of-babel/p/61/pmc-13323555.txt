Therapeutic potential of ketamine after severe traumatic brain injury
Authors: Vanessa M. Mazandi, Todd J. Kilbaugh, Jimmy W. Huh, Kumaran Senthil
Published: 2026

Introduction

Severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide with a significant financial burden.[1] However, there are no FDA-approved therapies to improve survival or neurologic outcomes following severe TBI. Ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist that was once dogmatically avoided after TBI, may now be a potential therapeutic to mitigate secondary injury following TBI. Small case series from the 1970s associated ketamine with elevation of intracranial pressure (ICP), but more current studies have found it to be non-inferior to alternative sedatives to decrease ICP and increase cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) in adult and pediatric patients with TBI.[[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]

Recently, ketamine has been found to effectively treat status epilepticus in pediatric and adult populations and treat cortical spreading depolarizations in adult populations, both of which may occur in severe TBI and are associated with worse clinical outcomes.[[10], [11], [12]] In this Perspective, we discuss recent studies examining ketamine’s therapeutic role against these pathophysiologic processes and advocate for more studies of ketamine as a sedative and therapeutic after severe TBI.

Evolution of Ketamine’s Effect on ICP

Shortly after ketamine’s introduction as a procedural sedative that could maintain airway tone and respiratory drive, studies in the 1970s cautioned against ketamine’s use in pediatric and adult patients at risk for elevated ICP due to concern for increased cerebral blood flow (CBF) and associated increase in intracranial blood volume. A closer inspection revealed limitations in extrapolating findings into clinical practice. Many had small sample sizes, including case reports with two to eleven patients as well as inclusion of patients with abnormal flow of cerebrospinal fluid (i.e., obstructive hydrocephalus), limiting generalizability of conclusions.[2,[7], [8], [9]] In most studies, ketamine was delivered to spontaneously breathing patients and observed increases in ICP may have been attributable to hypoventilation and subsequent increase in CBF.[2,[7], [8], [9]] Despite these limitations, the inclination to avoid ketamine in TBI became dogma. This persisted for decades, influencing guidelines and clinical practice worldwide. However, recent studies have found it to not increase ICP but potentially decrease ICP and maintain CPP when used as a sedative during intubation in adequately ventilated adult and pediatric patients with severe TBI.[[3], [4], [5], [6]]

Early Post-Traumatic Seizures (EPS)

EPS, defined as seizures occurring within the first seven days following TBI, are associated with higher rate of morbidity, posttraumatic epilepsy, and mortality. While prevalence of status epilepticus is relatively low in TBI patients, the morbidity and mortality in those who develop status epilepticus following injury are especially high.[13]

In a retrospective cohort study comparing intravenous (IV) ketamine to midazolam as second line rescue medications for out-of-hospital status epilepticus patients (predominantly adults), Zitek, et al.[10] found that ketamine resulted in higher cessation of seizures. Jacobwitz, et al.[12] retrospectively examined IV ketamine vs. midazolam infusion for cessation of refractory status epilepticus (RSE) in pediatric patients, and found that ketamine successfully terminated more seizures than midazolam. Furthermore, there were more adverse events in the midazolam cohort compared to ketamine, with hypotension being the predominant complication. The maintenance of favorable hemodynamics is of particular importance after TBI, as numerous studies have demonstrated maintenance of CPP as a clinically significant hemodynamic target.[14]

Ketamine’s demonstrated efficacy in treating seizures (status epilepticus and RSE) prompts the question of whether it will be one of the first line anti-epileptic medications in future iterations of adult and pediatric TBI guidelines. Additionally, many EPS are non-convulsive (only detected on EEG), so ketamine’s role as a sedative following TBI may provide additional benefit in cessation of non-clinical seizure activity.[13] Ketamine’s hemodynamic and anti-epileptogenic profile warrants further investigation to determine its efficacy in prevention and treatment of EPS.

Cortical Spreading Depolarization

Cortical spreading depolarizations (SD) are sustained, glutamate-mediated activations of neuronal and glial cells that cause dysregulation of transmembrane ion gradients, mitochondrial injury and neurovascular uncoupling, which eventually lead to electrical silencing of the brain and cerebral oligemia. Restoration of these transmembrane ion gradients can persistently increase cerebral metabolic oxygen consumption for hours after the SD, which in conjunction with restricted blood flow can result in cerebral ischemia.[15]

Ketamine extinguishes SDs in adult patients with neurologic injury, which suggests that NMDA-receptor activation is necessary for SD initiation or propagation.[11] Clustered SDs (more than three within two hours) and isoelectric SDs that occur in electrically silent areas of the brain are both independently associated with worse neurologic outcomes in adults after severe TBI.[15] The relationship between SDs and neurologic outcomes is observational, but SDs are hypothesized to be part of the causal pathway of secondary injury; this has prompted prospective randomized trials studying SD-directed therapy with ketamine in adults with TBI.

The Future of Ketamine

Ketamine’s well-earned safety profile and potential for diverse mechanisms to mitigate secondary brain injury make it a compelling pharmacotherapy for preclinical and clinical trial design. Additionally, ketamine is unique among antiepileptics in its ability to preserve CPP.[4,5] Its demonstrated ability to preserve CPP and lower ICP, together with its sedative, anti-epileptic and anti-SD properties, should make it a cornerstone of therapy in patients who require sedation following severe TBI. With further studies, these neuroprotective properties may potentially allow for the acute administration of ketamine in a wider range of non-TBI critically-ill intubated patients in the NeuroICU, such as patients with malignant cerebral edema from stroke, refractory status epilepticus and patients with brain tumors experiencing obstructive hydrocephalus.[5]

Ketamine has the potential for a remarkable transformation in severe TBI management; from dogmatically avoided to demonstrated safety with emerging evidence of therapeutic potential across multiple mechanisms relevant to secondary brain injury. While ongoing clinical trials will define its precise role, the convergence of mechanistic rationale, safety data and preliminary efficacy signals suggests severe TBI management may evolve to include ketamine as a cornerstone of acute therapy. The field must now move beyond simply asking whether ketamine is safe, to a framework that rigorously determines when, how and in whom ketamine therapy can improve neurologic and overall outcomes after severe TBI.
