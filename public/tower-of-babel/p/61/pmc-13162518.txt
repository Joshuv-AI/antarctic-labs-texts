Antibiotic consumption and prescription patterns in a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nosheen Nasir, Beenish Khalid Mehmood, Safia Awan, Alyna Khan, Saadia Sattar, Salma Tajuddin, Sonia Qureshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPakistan ranks third among low-middle-income countries (LMICs) in human antibiotic consumption. Moreover, it has alarmingly high antimicrobial resistance rates. Surveillance of antibiotic consumption rates can guide towards more responsible use. Our study aims to describe the antibiotic consumption and prescribing patterns in a tertiary care center in Karachi, Pakistan between 2016 and 2023.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Karachi between 2016 and 2023. Data on antibiotic use was extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records and pharmacy database. Antibiotic consumption was reported using the defined daily dose (DDD) per 1000 patient-days. Data was also extracted on types of antibiotics consumed and prescription patterns were assessed using WHO AWaRe classification.Watch antibiotics included fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, piperacillin-tazobactam, carbapenems, and vancomycin, which require cautious use and early review. Reserve antibiotics included colistin, ceftazidime-avibactam, and tigecycline, which are considered to be last resort agents for confirmed or suspected multidrug-resistant infections. The Chi-Square trend analysis was used to compare the trend in consumption of the WHO AWaRe antibiotic groups. P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsDuring the study period, there were 413,596 total admissions, with 67.3% receiving antibiotics during hospitalization. Over eight years, the average antibiotic consumption was 1128.7 DDD/1,000 patient-days, with the greatest in 2016 (1145 DDD/1000 patient-days) and the lowest in 2021 (1009 DDD/1000 patient-days). The top 5 antibiotics utilized were ceftriaxone, meropenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, vancomycin, and cefazolin. According to the WHO AWaRe Classification, Watch antibiotics were the most prescribed (68.8–73.9%), followed by Access antibiotics (58-63.9%), while Reserve antibiotics increased from 3.8% to 5.3% during the study period (p < 0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, this study reveals that a high proportion of patients receive antibiotics during hospitalization, and the majority of these were from the Watch category. These findings highlight the need to complement AWaRe- and DDD-based surveillance with evaluations of prescribing appropriateness and expanded monitoring to better inform antimicrobial stewardship efforts.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Antibiotic stewardship, Antibiotic consumption, AWaRe classification

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a public health challenge with the potential of reversing the gains we have achieved in the medical field [1]. It arises when pathogenic micro-organisms develop mechanisms that render them resistant to their therapeutic drugs [2]. These resistant genetic traits can then be transferred to the community posing a great threat to our environment [3]. Drug-resistant bacterial infections have been linked with 4.95 million fatalities in 2019, according to the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluations (IHME) Global Burden of Disease project. Of those deaths, 1,270,000 were directly related to AMR [4]. Furthermore, It is estimated that by 2050, 10 million people will die every year due to AMR and its impact will be greater in low-middle-income-countries (LMICs) [5]. This is Not only due to the increasing morbidity and mortality [6] but also costs associated with extended hospital stays and the need for more advanced medical equipment to treat patients [7].

Asia-Pacific is considered an epicenter of AMR due to higher resistance rates and antibiotic consumption compared to other regions [8]. Majority of these countries have poor infrastructure to combat AMR [8]. Pakistan is among these developing countries in South Asia, with high rates of AMR [9]. In recent years, microorganisms that are extensively drug resistant (XDR) and multi-drug resistant (MDR) have both been found in Pakistan [10]. Enterobacterales (formerly Enterobactereaceae) resistance to quinolones has grown throughout the past ten years in Pakistan [11]. Furthermore, XDR Salmonella typhi is a rapidly escalating problem almost unique to Pakistan. A study done in Hyderabad, Pakistan outlined the history of antimicrobial overuse as one of the factors contributing to the rampant spread of ceftriaxone-resistant S. typhi [12].

Antibiotics have been instrumental in saving numerous lives and revolutionizing the course of modern medicine [13]. They are the most frequently prescribed medications, with an increasing trend in usage seen, especially in developing countries [14, 15]. AMR is primarily caused by the inappropriate and excessive use of antimicrobial drugs in both the human and agricultural sectors, which has escalated over the previous two decades [16]. Global antibiotic consumption in the healthcare sector increased by 65–70% between 2000 and 2015 [17, 18]. Every 10 min, almost two tons of antibiotics are used around the world, that too without any prescription control [19]. Analysing consumption trends and patterns can help policymakers create solutions to the antimicrobial resistance problem and worldwide inequities in antibiotic access. The optimum method for quantifying antibiotic usage has yet to be determined. To draw comparative assessments, the standard way for the measurement of antibiotic consumption is to calculate aggregated ratios on the amount of antibiotic used. The defined daily dosage (DDD) or the number of days of therapy (DOT) are the two standard units used in these ratios [20]. Hospitals can implement these units to compare and measure their antibiotic consumption rates with other hospitals by estimating their antibiotic use in the form of DDD per 100 beds (DDD/100-BD) [21]. The misuse and abuse of antimicrobials has been associated with an increase in AMR. The use of antibiotics must be managed more carefully. The World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted the need to optimize antimicrobial use as one of the strategic objectives in its Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2015) [18]. To support this goal, WHO introduced the AWaRe classification of antibiotics, grouping them into four categories: Access (first- or second-line agents with a narrow spectrum and lower resistance potential), Watch (broad-spectrum agents with higher resistance potential), Reserve (last-resort antibiotics for multidrug-resistant infections), and Discouraged/Not recommended combinations that should generally be avoided [19, 20]. Antimicrobials from the “Access” group should make up 60% of the total antibiotic consumption in order to prevent resistance [22–24]. Following this, initiatives to enhance antimicrobial prescribing have been created and are commonly referred to as antimicrobial stewardship programs (AMS/ASP). This has proven to not only reduce prescription rates but also a decline in the usage of “Reserve” antimicrobials that ultimately has resulted in a cutback of related costs as well [22, 25].

Our study aims to bridge that gap and bring to light the antibiotic consumption and prescription patterns in a major tertiary care hospital, in Karachi- one of the most populated cities of Pakistan.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study, analyzing in-patient antibiotic consumption through electronic medical records over eight years at a major tertiary care center in Karachi Pakistan.

Study setting

The Aga Khan University Hospital is a 750-bed academic medical center and among three Joint Commission International Accreditation (JCIA) accredited hospitals in the country with an antimicrobial stewardship program. The accreditation is conferred to hospitals with high standards for quality and patient safety.

Study population and study duration

The study included all patients admitted to the Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH) from January 2016 to December 2023 who were receiving antibiotics during hospitalization. All age groups and both genders were included. Patients admitted for day-care procedures or with incomplete medical or pharmacy records were excluded.

Data collection

The study utilized inpatient data obtained from electronic medical records and pharmacy databases. Patient demographics, antibiotic types, in-patient service (such as admission under medicine, surgery, oncology etc.), and number of antibiotics prescribed was extracted [26]. All systemic antibiotics classified under the J01 category of the World Health Organization (WHO) Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system [26] were included. Antibiotics were selected based on availability of the prescription data, and representation across major therapeutic subclasses. Other routes of administration, such as topical and other antimicrobials, such as antivirals, were excluded.

Antibiotic consumption was expressed as defined daily dose (DDD) per 1000 patient-days [27] This methodology provides an assumed average maintenance dose for each antibiotic for comparison. The total DDD was determined by dividing the total amount of active pharmaceutical ingredients (antibiotic) consumed by the WHO-assigned DDD for each antibiotic. It is further divided by the total patientdays provided by the hospital’s health management information system (HIMS).Patient-days were derived from the hospital electronic admission–discharge records as the sum of daily inpatient census across the study period.

Antibiotics were also classified according to the Access, Watch, Reserve (AWaRe) classification by the World Health Organization (WHO) [28] which allows monitoring of appropriate use of antibiotics in the healthcare setup, according to their category.

Formula for calculating DDD/1000 patient-days.

Some of the key definitions are as follows.

Antibiotic Consumption: Refers to the total quantity of an antibiotic utilized, based on the strength of its active pharmaceutical ingredient, and expressed in DDD per 1000 patient days according to the WHO (ATC)/DDD methodology. This provides a standardized metric allowing comparison across different drugs, settings, and time periods.

Antibiotic Prescription: Refers to any antibiotic drug prescribed to a patient during a single clinical encounter, regardless of its dosage form, strength, or duration.All antibiotics included in the analysis were systemic antibacterial agents (ATC J01 category) available in the hospital formulary during the study period. No further selection based on frequency or class was applied. The formulary encompassed the complete list of approved therapeutic antibiotics used in inpatient care, as endorsed by the hospital’s Antimicrobial Stewardship Committee. AWaRe Classification: According to WHO, Access group refers to first or second choice antibiotics, while Watch group are also first and second choice of antibiotics like Access but indicated for limited infections. On the other hand, Reserve group contains antibiotics, that are used only when other treatments have failed.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics and visualized through multiple figures. The study cohort was described across four time periods (2016–2017, 2018–2019, 2020–2021, and 2022–2023). Differences in categorical variables were evaluated using the linear-by-linear chi-square test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Temporal trends in antibiotic consumption, the distribution of antibiotic prescriptions by class and specific drug, consumption patterns categorized according to the WHO AWaRe framework, and department-level differences in prescribing stratified by admitting department were presented in separate figures. All figures were generated using Microsoft Excel or Power BI. The p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Data management and security

Data were obtained from the Aga Khan University Hospital Information Management System (HIMS) and the hospital pharmacy database. The transformation process was designed such that raw data from the source files were imported directly for analysis. Initial data mapping was conducted using a pilot dataset covering one year. The finalized transformation procedures were subsequently applied to the complete dataset spanning 2016 to 2023.

Data were reviewed for completeness and consistency. Duplicate records were identified and removed, and missing records were verified against source records and corrected when possible. Dates were formatted to ensure consistency across the study period, and antibiotic consumption values were aggregated by defined time periods (2016–2017, 2018–2019, 2020–2021, and 2022–2023). WHO AWaRe classification categories and department-level prescription summaries were generated for comparative analysis.

The data was saved on a password-protected computer. It was synced daily and stored on a secure central server. Only the principal investigator (PI), co-investigators (Co-Is) and research team had access to the data.

Ethical considerations: This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013) and approved by the Aga Khan University Ethics Review Committee (AKU ERC) (Ref. No.: 2017). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of de-identified data. All the information was anonymous and kept confidential. The data was used for research purposes only.

Results

During the study period, a total of 413,596 patients were admitted. Of these, 278,510 (67.3%) received antibiotics during hospitalization. Median age of patients who received antibiotics was 40 years [interquartile range (IQR):23–61], with 144,937(51.9%) female patients. A total of 35 antibiotics were analyzed. Of these, 12 antibiotics were in Access Category, 17 antibiotics in Watch category and 3 in Reserve category. A total of 650,672 antibiotics were prescribed, and the mean antibiotic consumption was 1128.7 DDD/1,000 patient-days over 8 years. The annual antibiotic consumption was highest in year 2016 with 1145 DDD/1000 patient days and lowest in year 2021 with 1009 DDD/1000 patient days (p-value < 0.001) The antibiotic consumption showed a declining trend till year 2021 followed by steady increase till year 2023. (Fig. 1). Out of 650,672 antibiotic prescriptions, 92,733(33.3%) were prescribed at least one antibiotic per hospital encounter, followed by 88,008(31.6%) who were prescribed at least two antibiotics per hospital encounter. Majority (77%) of antibiotics prescribed were parenteral antibiotics.

Types of antibiotics consumed

Overall, the most consumed antibiotic was ceftriaxone (1453 DDDs/1000 patient days), followed by meropenem (1008 DDDs/1000 patient days), piperacillin-tazobactam (873 DDDs/1000 patient days) vancomycin (781 DDDs/1000 patient days), and cefazolin (724 DDDs/1000 patient days). Among the classes, the highest number of prescriptions were for cephalosporins followed by beta lactam/beta lactamase inhibitors. Figure 2 shows the distribution of antibiotic prescriptions by different classes and specific drugs during the study period. The trends of antibiotic prescriptions were evaluated and a significant increase was observed in prescriptions of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, azithromycin, and meropenem (p < 0.001) while significant reductions were found in prescriptions of metronidazole and imipenem (p < 0.001). Furthermore, while ceftriaxone remained the most commonly prescribed antibiotic, its use declined from 43.1% to 37.4% between 2016 and 2023 (p < 0.001). (Table 1).

Discussion

Our eight-year analysis of antibiotic consumption and prescribing trends in a large tertiary care hospital in Karachi provides important insights into inpatient antibiotic use patterns in Pakistan. We found an initial decline followed by an increase in overall annual antibiotic consumption. Furthermore, there was a trend towards increased consumption of Watch antibiotics and more alarmingly of Reserve antibiotics. Amongst the antibiotics, the most consumed was ceftriaxone, followed by meropenem and the most frequent route of administration was parenteral. This data from one of the country’s major academic tertiary care centers fills an important gap in the existing knowledge of hospital prescribing practices and can serve to guide antibiotic stewardship efforts in tertiary care centers of LMICs.

Antibiotic consumption was highest between 2016 and 2017 and dropped precipitously in 2018 which is likely attributable to the development of antimicrobial stewardship committee in the hospital in January 2018 heralding the beginning of formal stewardship efforts. The consumption of antibiotics continued to decline reaching a nadir in 2021 reflecting changes due to the COVID-19 pandemic with an overall reduction in hospital admissions. Multiple studies have documented substantial disruptions to healthcare utilisation during the pandemic, including significant reductions in non-COVID and elective hospital admissions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [29]. In Pakistan, tertiary-care hospitals reported marked declines in overall admissions during lockdown periods due to movement restrictions, suspension of elective services, and public avoidance of healthcare facilities [30–32]. Similar reductions in hospital utilisation were observed globally, providing a plausible explanation for the reduced aggregate antibiotic consumption observed in our setting [33].

The impact of COVID-19 on overall antibiotic utilization has been variable across settings. The empiric use of antibiotics in COVID-19 has been reported in several studies from both LMICs and high-income countries despite low rates of bacterial co-infection [34]. A multicentre study from Pakistan demonstrated increased use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, including azithromycin and third-generation cephalosporins, during COVID-19 waves, likely reflecting diagnostic uncertainty and variable stewardship implementation [35]. However, global trends of human antibiotic consumption across 67 countries reportedly declined during the pandemic period, followed by a subsequent rebound which was most pronounced in middle-income countries [36]. Similarly, a steady increase in antibiotic consumption has been noted subsequently in our study and requires vigilant monitoring and perhaps newer and more innovative antibiotic stewardship interventions. A study on global antibiotic consumption reported disparities with high rates of antibiotic consumption in high-income and upper-middle-income countries and comparatively very low rates of consumption in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Southeast Asia though greater inappropriate use was observed in Southeast Asia [37]. Studies from LMICs have shown variability in trends such Ethiopia found a consistent decline in antibiotic prescriptions between 2016 and 2022 [38] whereas a study from Ghana’ secondary hospital has shown similar trends with initial decline though they could not find a plausible explanation for it [39]. Another study from Pakistan has shown fluctuations in trends of prescribing with peaking during COVID-19 pandemic which is in contrast to ours and could be potentially due to lack of appropriate stewardship interventions [21].

Our study found an increased consumption of antibiotics from the Watch category as opposed to WHO recommendation of at least 60% consumption from Access category. This is largely attributable to the increasing drug resistance patterns and an increased prevalence of extended spectrum beta lactamase-producing organisms in the community [40]. This may also explain the increased consumption of carbapenems and BL/BLI combinations. Moreover, there is an alarming increase in consumption of Reserve antibiotics suggesting increased AMR and highlighting need for strengthening antibiotic stewardship efforts in our country. This pattern mirrors findings from other South Asian hospitals, including studies from India and Bangladesh, which have similarly documented increased use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [41, 42]. A recent multi-country study captured antimicrobial consumption data from 6 countries including Pakistan and found that only 4 out of 6 countries were able to achieve the WHO set goal of consumption of Access antibiotics (60% of total antibiotic usage). However the study does not provide country level details [43]. This contrasts with data from other LMICs particularly from Africa where majority of utilized antibiotics are from Access category [38, 39].

The high use of ceftriaxone and meropenem in our study likely reflects empirical prescribing in the absence of timely microbiological confirmation, a challenge widely reported in South Asian hospital settings [17, 44]. Studies conducted in Pakistan have also reported ceftriaxone, piperacillin–tazobactam, and meropenem as the most commonly prescribed agents in tertiary care hospitals [21, 45]. While these antibiotics remain crucial for severe infections, their extensive use in empiric therapy contributes to escalating resistance rates, particularly against third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems [46].

Our findings have several practical implications. First, the disproportionate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics calls for targeted stewardship interventions, including prescriber training, feedback mechanisms, and improved diagnostic capacity. Second, establishing standardised DDD and AWaRe monitoring frameworks will enable continuous evaluation of antibiotic use and resistance trends. Finally, integrating hospital-level antibiotic consumption data into Pakistan’s national AMR surveillance programme will help guide resource allocation, support antimicrobial policy development, and strengthen the country’s progress toward WHO’s Global Action Plan on AMR objectives However, the study has several limitations. Our center is one of 3 centers in the country with a robust antimicrobial stewardship program and electronic pharmacy records which is unfortunately not available in most other centers. While this limits the generalizability, it provides valuable insights from a country perspective highlighting the need for surveillance and scaling up of antimicrobial stewardship. Furthermore, the absence of indication-level data was a key limitation as it precluded the assessment of prescribing appropriateness against clinical guidelines. Consequently, antibiotic consumption patterns based on DDD and AWaRe classification may reflect case mix, disease severity, and institutional referral practices rather than suboptimal prescribing.

Conclusion

Our study identified persistently high antibiotic consumption, with predominance of Watch category agents and a gradual increase in Reserve antibiotics. The observed patterns likely reflect the clinical complexity and case mix of a tertiary-care hospital with a robust antimicrobial stewardship program, whereby the use of antibiotics from the watch and reserve category may be clinically justified. These results underscore the need to complement aggregate consumption metrics such as DDD and AWaRe classification with qualitative evaluations of guideline adherence, de-escalation practices, and indication-specific prescribing. Expanding standardized antibiotic consumption surveillance beyond accredited hospitals remains essential to provide a more representative national picture and to inform antimicrobial resistance containment efforts in Pakistan.
