Driving after vagus nerve stimulation therapy: Is it possible?
Authors: Javier Peña‐Ceballos, Tenzin Choekyi, Niamh Colleran, Breege Staunton‐Grufferty, Nicola Dempsey, David Lewis‐Smith, Hany El Naggar, Kieron J. Sweeney, Donncha O'Brien, Patrick B. Moloney, Norman Delanty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDriving is consistently reported as an important goal for people with epilepsy. We sought to determine the proportion of people with drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) who became medically fit to drive after treatment with vagus nerve stimulation (VNS). We conducted a retrospective observational study of consecutive patients treated with VNS attending our neuromodulation clinic. We classified patients as either “VNS responders without additional intervention” or “VNS responders with additional intervention” and determined if patients were eligible to drive Group 1 vehicles (cars, motorcycles, and light work vehicles) under Irish Medical Fitness to Drive regulations. Among 87 patients treated with VNS for ≥12 months, 15 became medically eligible to drive: five were current drivers, three were learner drivers, and seven were eligible to drive based on their seizure control but were not driving for various reasons. Eleven were seizure‐free, three had nondisabling focal seizures with preserved consciousness, and one had exclusive sleep‐related seizures. Eleven were “VNS responders without additional intervention,” while four achieved eligibility after adding ASM or following subsequent epilepsy surgery, while VNS was still active. No patients reported driving‐related accidents. ASM burden remained unchanged post‐VNS. VNS was well tolerated, and no patients discontinued treatment. In this cohort, 17.2% of patients treated with VNS became eligible to drive, despite it being considered a palliative treatment. These findings highlight the potential role of neuromodulation in improving seizure control and promoting patient autonomy and independence.Plain Language SummaryThis study investigated whether people with refractory epilepsy in Ireland could become eligible to drive after VNS treatment. INTRODUCTION

Driving remains a complex and often debated issue in the management of epilepsy. Many studies have shown that people with epilepsy (PWE) have a higher risk of road traffic accidents compared with the general population, 1 , 2 although fatal crashes directly attributed to seizures are extremely rare. 3 As a result, PWE are subject to specific driving restrictions that vary across jurisdictions, with seizure control serving as the primary determining factor. 4 In the Republic of Ireland, PWE must refrain from driving Group 1 vehicles (cars, motorcycles, or light work vehicles) for at least 1 year following a seizure. However, those who demonstrate a stable pattern of exclusively sleep‐related seizures or nondisabling focal seizures with preserved consciousness (FPC) for a minimum of 1 year may be permitted to resume driving, subject to evaluation and approval by their neurologist. 5

Among PWE, concerns about driving have been identified as the most significant factor negatively impacting quality of life (QoL), surpassing worries related to employment or seizure unpredictability. 6 PWE who are permitted to drive report significantly higher scores in emotional well‐being, energy, cognition, and social function. 7 Achieving fitness to drive is particularly challenging in drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) due to the combined burden of active seizures and side effects associated with anti‐seizure medication (ASM) polytherapy. Despite this, the rates of driving eligibility among PWE following treatment with newer ASMs or neuromodulation techniques remain underreported in the literature. Attaining legal driving clearance following vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) treatment would generally be considered improbable, given its widely perceived role as a palliative intervention for DRE. Here, we report the characteristics and percentage of PWE who were able to start or resume driving after VNS treatment at a large neuromodulation clinic.

METHODS

Participants and study design

We conducted a retrospective observational study of consecutive patients with DRE receiving active VNS treatment for at least 1 year at our neuromodulation clinic in Beaumont Hospital, Dublin, Ireland between January 2024 and March 2025. All patients actively treated with VNS were seen in the clinic during this period at least once. In our neuromodulation clinic, VNS therapy is initiated 2 weeks after implantation, with initial settings of 0.25 mA of output current (OC), 250 ms pulse width, 20 Hz stimulation frequency, 30 s ON time and 5 min OFF time (10% duty cycle). In newer devices, automatic titration is programmed every 2 weeks, increasing the OC by 0.25 mA increments until a target range of 1–1.75 mA is reached, based on the clinician's assessment and the patient's response and tolerability. This protocol is subject to individualization based on patient‐specific factors.

Data collection and outcome measures

Patients were identified through our VNS database. Baseline clinical information was obtained from our epilepsy‐specific electronic patient record and paper‐based medical notes. We established the driving status of patients prior to VNS implantation. We determined if patients were “drivers,” “driver learners,” “eligible to drive but not driving,” or “not eligible to drive,” either due to active seizures or other medical or cognitive reasons.

After determining each patient's driving status before VNS implantation, we assessed their legal eligibility to drive at the last follow‐up following VNS treatment. Eligibility was defined as being seizure‐free for at least 1 year at the last follow‐up, or, after 1 year of a consistent seizure pattern, experiencing only sleep‐related seizures or nondisabling FPC. We subdivided patients who became eligible to drive into two groups: “VNS responders without additional intervention,” defined as those who became eligible to drive without ASM additions or surgical intervention after VNS implantation, and “VNS responders with additional interventions,” defined as those who achieved eligibility with new adjunctive treatments (ASM or epilepsy surgery) while continuing VNS. All patients underwent video‐electroencephalography (video‐EEG) before VNS implantation, which meant seizure types were well characterized at baseline. However, video‐EEG was not routinely repeated after VNS implantation, and posttreatment seizure frequency relied on reports from patients and their next of kin.

We excluded patients whose VNS was previously switched off and who were not actively seen in the neuromodulation clinic during the study period. Patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) were also excluded, as they are highly unlikely to achieve driving eligibility. Clinical data was extracted and analyzed in April 2025.

At last follow‐up, 15 patients (17.2%) were either driving or eligible to drive (Figure 1). Of these, five patients (5.7%) were “drivers,” three patients (3.4%) were “learner drivers,” and seven (8%) were “eligible to drive but not driving.” All five active drivers held Group 1 vehicle licenses, and none reported involvement in road traffic accidents after obtaining their license. The sole driver at the time of VNS implantation had a breakthrough seizure from wakefulness during the study period and lost driving privileges. Another patient with temporal lobe epilepsy achieved seizure freedom and started driving during the study period but experienced a breakthrough seizure and had to cease driving. Three patients (3.4%) with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy were free of generalized tonic–clonic seizures but experienced frequent myoclonic jerks throughout the day, rather than being restricted to the early morning and often presenting in clusters. These patients were deemed “not eligible to drive” by their neurologist.

At last clinical review, 11 patients were seizure‐free, three had nondisabling FPC, and the other had exclusive sleep‐related seizures. Among seizure‐free patients, seven were “VNS responders without additional intervention” and four were “VNS responders with additional intervention.” Among those who required further interventions, one patient initially experienced a 75%–99% reduction in seizures with VNS therapy and later achieved seizure freedom following optimization of brivaracetam 26 months after VNS implantation. Two patients who had an initial 50%–74% seizure reduction after VNS treatment subsequently became seizure‐free, one after resection of an FCD type IIB, and the other following the addition of lamotrigine 32 months after the VNS implantation. The remaining patient had a 25%–49% seizure reduction with VNS and later achieved seizure freedom after starting cenobamate 28 months after VNS implantation. No ASM changes were made in the three patients who only experienced nondisabling FPC. In the patient eligible to drive due to exclusively sleep‐related seizures after VNS initiation, cenobamate was subsequently added 44 months after VNS implantation, achieving a ≥50% seizure reduction of sleep‐related seizures. The median number of ASM at the last clinical review was 3 (IQR = 1.5), unchanged from the baseline median. Lacosamide (n = 7), levetiracetam (n = 5), clobazam (n = 5), and cenobamate (n = 4) were the most prescribed ASMs at final review.

There were no perioperative or postoperative complications. Six patients reported voice changes or hoarseness, one reported mild throat discomfort, and one had left‐sided tinnitus which resolved after reducing the duty cycle from 44% to 25%. One patient developed lancinating left‐sided lower jaw pain, following the distribution of the mandibular branch of the trigeminal nerve, restricted to ON time, which began 3 months after VNS was initiated. No patients discontinued VNS therapy at any point during the follow‐up period.

DISCUSSION

Our study offers a unique perspective on the potential benefits of VNS therapy. At our neuromodulation clinic, 17.2% of the patients became eligible to drive following treatment with VNS. Most achieved this landmark without changing their ASMs. While VNS has traditionally been viewed as a palliative treatment option, our findings highlight that it can provide seizure freedom in some patients, thereby enhancing patient autonomy, particularly in relation to driving. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published report quantifying the percentage of patients who become eligible to drive after VNS implantation.

Our VNS cohort represents a highly refractory population, with use of a median of three ASMs at implantation and treatment with six previously failed ASMs. Over two‐thirds experienced convulsive seizures in the 12 months prior to VNS implantation. Despite this clinical severity, a substantial proportion of patients achieved seizure freedom or experienced control of seizures from wakefulness or seizures associated with impaired consciousness. Our findings underscore the potential of VNS for more robust seizure control, even in highly refractory cases. Evidence suggests that earlier VNS implantation is associated with improved outcomes, particularly in children with DRE. 8 , 9 , 10 VNS has demonstrated particular efficacy for convulsive seizures, with seizure freedom rates reported between 37% and 55% 11 , 12 and it might be associated with a reduced risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy. 13 , 14

Driving consistently ranks as the most important concern for PWE, with those who are unable to drive reporting lower QoL scores. 6 , 7 Although we did not objectively measure QoL, our study adds real‐world evidence that adjunctive VNS therapy might enhance QoL and autonomy and independence in people with DRE, as previously reported by other studies. 15 , 16 Overall, there was no reduction of ASM burden pre‐ and post‐VNS implantation in those who became eligible to drive. Previous observational studies have shown that while VNS may be effective, it has not been linked to a reduced burden of ASM. 17 , 18 Notably, almost half of the patients who were eligible to drive chose not to do so. Although we did not explore their reasons due to the retrospective nature of the study, this observation has previously been reported in PWE who are legally eligible to drive but choose to abstain. 19 However, this may not be a surprising decision for some patients with a history of DRE and may be related to an overall general nervousness and lack of confidence around driving given the prior long‐term difficulty around robust past seizure control.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective, single‐center design and relatively small sample size may affect the generalizability of our findings. Patients who previously discontinued VNS due to lack of efficacy and were no longer under active follow‐up in the neuromodulation clinic were not included in this study, which may have resulted in an overestimation of VNS treatment's impact on driving eligibility. We acknowledge that including patients with a minimum of 12 months of VNS treatment may bias driving eligibility rates toward early responders, while overlooking ‘fluctuating responders.’ Although Kostov et al. reported better outcomes after more than 24 months of VNS therapy and a fluctuating response in 15%, most VNS studies use 12‐month response rates as their primary outcome. 20 In our cohort, the mean follow‐up duration was 93.7 months (range: 13–262 months), with the majority (74/87, 85%) treated for more than 24 months. Among those eligible to drive, only 26.7% (4/15) had received VNS for 12–24 months. Posttreatment seizure frequency was assessed based on reports from patients and next of kin, which may be subject to underreporting, particularly if patients sought to avoid driving restrictions. Previous studies have documented noncompliance with driving regulations among individuals with uncontrolled epilepsy. 21 , 22 However, we believe that this is highly unlikely in this group of patients, given the prior severity of their epilepsy and the likely careful monitoring by patients' family members. The absence of reported accidents or near‐accidents provides some reassurance that unrecognized seizures are not impacting driving in our VNS patients. Lastly, the absence of QoL data limited our ability to fully explore the impact of driving on patient well‐being.

In conclusion, 17.2% of DRE patients treated with VNS became eligible to drive, highlighting the potential role of neuromodulation in seizure reduction and enhancing patient autonomy. Although VNS is widely regarded as a palliative treatment, many patients can achieve a meaningful therapeutic response, with the possibility of driving as a long‐term goal for some.
