Diagnosis of traumatic scleral perforation in a dog using ocular ultrasound and ultrasound biomicroscopy
Authors: Dajeong Jeong, Eunji Lee, Lina Susanti, Hyelin Kim, Kangmoon Seo, Seonmi Kang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 10‐year‐old spayed female Maltese dog was presented for hypotony of the right eye (OD) with decreased vision. The dorsomedial region of conjunctiva was partially swollen but the globe remained intact. Slit‐lamp biomicroscopy revealed intraocular haemorrhage, uveitis, and a limbal, corneoconjunctival ulcer near the swollen conjunctiva. During ocular ultrasonography (OUS), the sclera became indented and hypotony occurred, implicating a full‐thickness scleral wound. Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) confirmed a full‐thickness scleral defect, which was surgically apposed. The intraocular pressure normalised and the uveitis subsided post‐operatively. OUS and UBM were helpful in suggesting and definitively diagnosing scleral perforation, leading to accurate treatment and good prognosis.Keywords: ocular ultrasonography, scleral perforation, ultrasound biomicroscopy

INTRODUCTION

Scleral perforation can be caused by blunt or sharp trauma, and the former is more common (Maggs, 2017). It frequently accompanies intraocular haemorrhage, lens capsular rupture, lens luxation, retinal detachment or hypotony (Mitchell, 2014; Maggs, 2017). Scleral perforation can be presumptively diagnosed based on medical history and clinical features consistent with these lesions; alternatively, the wound can be directly visualised through clinical ophthalmic examinations, ocular ultrasonography (OUS) or histopathology post‐enucleation. However, the diagnosis is tentative because it is difficult to visualise scleral perforations by traditional examination techniques such as OUS or slit‐lamp biomicroscopy. Scleral perforation can be treated by several surgical techniques, and the choice of treatment depends on the location and size of the defect. However, when perforation is caused by trauma, the prognosis is guarded. In a retrospective study of scleral rupture cases in 30 small animals caused by blunt trauma (Rampazzo et al., 2006), most animals underwent enucleation due to severe intraocular damage.

This case report describes scleral perforation in a dog, in which imaging findings were diagnostic and thus allowed for precise surgical planning. Additionally, clinical and imaging aspects to be considered for maintaining the globe and vision in the perforated sclera will be discussed.

Case history

A 10‐year‐old female spayed Maltese dog was presented with a chief complaint of hypotony of the right eye (OD) and an intraocular pressure (IOP) of 8 mmHg at the referring veterinary hospital. The left eye was previously enucleated because of a dog fight. A complete ophthalmic examination was performed OD. The Schirmer tear test (Schirmer tear test; MSD) result was 18 mm/min and rebound tonometry (Tonovet; Icare Finland Oy) showed an IOP of 14 mmHg. Menace response was positive but decreased with the reduced frequency of responses to the threatening motions of hand, and dazzle reflex was positive. It was difficult to evaluate the pupillary light reflex due to a fixed miotic pupil (Figure 1A). Slit‐lamp biomicroscopy (SL‐D7; Topcon) revealed focal bulging of the dorsomedial region of conjunctiva (Figure 1B), conjunctival haemorrhage and mild chemosis in the dorsal region (Figure 1C). Severe aqueous flare, hyphema and fibrin within the anterior chamber were also observed (Figure 1D). When two drops of 1% tropicamide (Mydriacyl; Alcon) were instilled to evaluate the lens and posterior segment of the eye with an indirect ophthalmoscope (Keeler Vantage Plus; Keeler Ltd), intravitreal haemorrhage and fibrin (Figure 1E) were observed, which blurred the ocular fundus. A fluorescein staining test was positive, extending from the dorsomedial limbal cornea to the adjacent conjunctiva, close to the protruded lesion, with suspected scleral staphyloma. Blood pressure was 110 mmHg, and no remarkable findings related to intraocular haemorrhage were detected in the blood analysis, including complete blood count, serum chemistry and electrolytes (Table 1).

OUS was performed to detect any structural ocular abnormalities. Intraocular abnormalities were not detected, except for intravitreal hyperechogenicity (Figure 2A) suggesting intravitreal haemorrhage and inflammation. However, during OUS, gradual indentation of the sclera was observed (Figure 2B). The IOP after OUS decreased from 14 to 6 mmHg, suggesting full‐thickness scleral perforation. While considering the direct suture of the sclera, medical treatment was systemically and topically initiated to relieve hyphema, anterior uveitis and to address the corneoconjunctival ulcer. Systemic prednisolone (0.5 mg/kg per os [PO], twice a day [BID]; Yuhan), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (12.5 mg/kg PO, BID; Ilsung Pharm) and famotidine (0.5 mg/kg PO, BID; Nelson Pharm) were prescribed. Topical medications, including 0.5% levofloxacin (Cravit; Santen) four times a day [QID], 1% cyclopentolate (Ocucyclo; Samil Pharm) BID, 4% acetyl‐cysteine diluted with artificial tears (Mucomyst; Boryung Pharm) QID and 0.3% hyaluronic acid (Hyalein mini; Santen) QID were prescribed.

Images of ocular ultrasonography (OUS): (A) intact eye with intravitreal hyperechogenicity at OUS initiation; (B) indentation of the globe during OUS.

Five days later, the degree of conjunctival haemorrhage, hyphema and anterior uveitis decreased compared to that at the first presentation, but the IOP was 3 mmHg. Owing to the relatively clearer ocular media, an irregular appearance of the posterior lens capsule and vitreous haemorrhage were more clearly observed through slit‐lamp biomicroscopy (Figure 1F,G). These findings suggested lens capsular and choroidal involvement. As the corneoconjunctival ulcer had healed, ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) was performed to confirm the perforation of the sclera and its extent, and to determine the need for surgical treatment. The sclera corresponding to the suspected perforation wound appeared to be disconnected on UBM. Unlike the normal conjunctival and scleral tissue, a subconjunctival fluid space was identified, which was considered to be due to the leakage of intraocular fluid through the perforated sclera. The scleral perforation was occluded with a hyperechoic material thought to be composed of prolapsed uveal tissue and fibrinous material (Figure 3A).

Sutured sclera (red arrowhead) remains stable. Subconjunctival tissue is thickened, which is thought to be post‐operative fibrous tissue.

Surgical treatment was planned based on the imaging findings. The scleral wound was surgically repaired under general anaesthesia with the administration of midazolam (0.2 mg/kg; Bukwang Pharm) and propofol (6 mg/kg; Myungmoon Pharm), which was maintained with isoflurane (I‐FRAN; Hana Pharm). Under the operating microscope, a stay suture was placed at the limbus near the perforated sclera. After conjunctival incision, the perforated sclera was detected in the dorsomedial region, corresponding to the location observed on the UBM. Tenon's capsule along with fibrin and prolapsed choroidal tissue filling the scleral defect were debrided (Figure 4), and scleral margins of the defect were sutured with 6–0 polyglactin 910 (Vicryl, Ethicon). Conjunctiva was closed with 8‐0 polyglactin 910. After the oral prednisone had been discontinued to avoid impediment of wound healing, there were 2 days of withdrawal period. Post‐operatively, carprofen (2.2 mg/kg PO, BID; Pfizer), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (12.5 mg/kg PO, BID) and famotidine (0.5 mg/kg PO, BID) were prescribed for 6 days. Topical medications, including 0.5% levofloxacin QID, 1% prednisolone (Prednilone; Dae Woo Pharm) QID, 1% cyclopentolate once a day [SID] and 0.3% hyaluronic acid QID were prescribed. Among the eye drops, the former two were tapered and discontinued 22 days post‐surgery, and the latter two were applied for 6 days.

Intraoperative image. Perforation site post conjunctival incision. The arrow is used to indicate the staphyloma (which has been debrided and excised). The third eyelid margin is observed in black.

The first recheck was 6 days after the surgery. Sutured site looked stable, and aqueous flare disappeared. One month post‐surgery, the sutured site of the sclera and overlying conjunctiva was observed to be stable via UBM (Figure 3B). The IOP was normal (11 mmHg), and no aqueous flare was observed. An immature cataract was observed 6‐month post‐surgery, and the ocular fundus appeared normal, but the fundoscopy was slightly blurred due to the cataract. The sutured site looked stable, and vision was preserved, although decreased due to the cataract, until the last follow‐up (34 months). No phakitis, uveitis, retinal tear or detachment was observed in the follow‐up period, although visualisation was obscured during ophthalmoscopy due to cataract. Unfortunately, UBM was not performed after the 1‐month follow‐up due to systemic complications the patient experienced.

DISCUSSION

Traumatic scleral perforation in dogs is often attributed to car accidents or fights with other animals (Rampazzo et al., 2006). Damage to intraocular structures caused by sharp trauma is generally less severe than that caused by blunt or concussive trauma (Maggs, 2017). In this case, the owner did not know what exactly happened to the dog, but the dog had a history of an enucleated left eye because of a dog fight. Furthermore, the linear ulceration of the corneoconjunctiva with minimal intraocular damage indicated sharp trauma.

Definitive diagnosis of scleral perforation on clinical examination is difficult, because conjunctival healing is faster than scleral healing. This makes it difficult to identify the scleral perforation site (usually at the posterior eye regions) (Rampazzo et al., 2006). A retrospective study of scleral ruptures caused by blunt trauma in small animals reported that out of 30 cases, only 2 could be detected through clinical ophthalmic examinations, whereas the other 18 cases were only detected post‐enucleation by gross examination or histopathology (Rampazzo et al., 2006). Therefore, treatment for perforation is mostly administered based on a presumptive diagnosis. Furthermore, several cases have been reported in which enucleation was performed because the site of perforation could not be specified (Rampazzo et al., 2006; Welihozkiy et al., 2011). In the present case, the conjunctiva overlying the scleral wound appeared anatomically intact, and the scleral defect was not observed, as the conjunctiva had already healed, covering the scleral defect filled with fibrinous material. Moreover, OUS has limitations in identifying near‐field structures, making it difficult to identify structural damage to the sclera near the transducer. Findings, such as the ulcerated limbus with adjacent protruded conjunctiva, conjunctival and intraocular haemorrhage and OUS‐induced hypotony, led to the suspicion of scleral perforation. A significant reduction in IOP after OUS could be important when perforation is suspected, as it can be a critical clue for definitive diagnosis. However, the diagnosis was finally confirmed through UBM using a high‐resolution ultrasound transducer. Because the scleral lesion was close to the limbus, OUS may have low resolution and distortion. UBM may be of particular importance in the diagnostic process of scleral laceration, as UBM is more appropriate for visualising the anterior segment compared to OUS. However, when traumatic scleral perforation occurs near the posterior pole, UBM cannot visualise the posterior segment; hence, the diagnosis should be made considering both the OUS and UBM examination results (Rampazzo et al., 2006).

Several surgical techniques, including scleral suture and grafting, are being used to treat scleral perforation (Barros & Safatle, 2000; Grundon et al., 2011; Lewin, 1999; Maggs, 2017; Mitchell, 2014; Plummer et al., 2008; Skorobohach & Hendrix, 2003). However, in most cases of traumatic scleral perforation, enucleation might be the only treatment option, as perforation usually results in blindness and phthisis bulbi (Rampazzo et al., 2006). Even in some cases where vision is intact, enucleation may still be the only option because the perforation site is too posterior to allow surgical intervention (Welihozkiy et al., 2011). In this case, early diagnosis, assisted by appropriate diagnostic imaging, helped determine whether surgical intervention was necessary and feasible.

In conclusion, this report suggests the diagnostic importance of performing both UBM and OUS for diagnosing scleral perforation in dogs. After thorough planning based on the imaging findings, the dog was surgically treated to maintain its globe and vision. OUS and UBM were helpful in identifying scleral perforation and in the definitive diagnosis of scleral perforation, respectively, leading to prompt treatment and a good prognosis.
