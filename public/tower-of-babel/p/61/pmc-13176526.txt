Simvastatin Restores Uteroplacental Hemodynamics and Trophoblast Function in Obstetric Antiphospholipid Syndrome in a Placenta‐on‐a‐Chip Model
Authors: Hongli Liu, Yuan Wang, Yanjun Cai, Ruixin Guo, Shunhua Liu, Tingting Chen, Tingting Sun, Ruiheng Huo, Tengwei Liu, Qingfeng Lv, Xietong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObstetric antiphospholipid syndrome (OAPS) presents substantial clinical challenges owing to limited therapeutic options. The hemodynamic mechanisms underlying statin protection in OAPS remain unclear. In this study, the therapeutic potential of simvastatin in OAPS was investigated. Herein, an OAPS mouse model was used to evaluate the effects of simvastatin on pregnancy outcomes and uteromaternal hemodynamics. A placenta‐on‐a‐chip platform was engineered to assess trophoblast invasion under high shear stress. Transcriptome sequencing and molecular assays validated the mechanisms by which simvastatin mitigates endothelial injury induced by antiphospholipid antibodies. Simvastatin attenuated maternal vascular inflammation and rectified uteromaternal hemodynamic aberrations in mice. Furthermore, treatment indirectly promoted trophoblast invasion under high shear stress, facilitating spiral artery remodeling and improving placental perfusion. Mechanistically, these effects were mediated by activation of the endothelial Kruppel‐like factor 2 (KLF2)/endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) pathway. In summary, simvastatin ameliorates placental hypoperfusion and restores trophoblast function disrupted by aberrant hemodynamics via regulation of endothelial KLF2/eNOS signaling. These findings highlight simvastatin as a potential therapeutic option for refractory OAPS.Keywords: eNOS, hemodynamics, KLF2, obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome, placenta‐on‐a‐chip, simvastatin

Introduction

Obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome (OAPS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder driven by antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) and characterized by adverse pregnancy outcomes. Clinically, OAPS manifests as recurrent pregnancy loss, intrauterine fetal demise, fetal growth restriction (FGR), and severe preeclampsia (PE) [1]. The primary pathogenic mechanism involves autoantibodies targeting phospholipids or phospholipid‐binding proteins on trophoblast surfaces, resulting in placental dysfunction and inflammation. Impaired spiral artery remodeling and consequent placental infarction and hypoperfusion are primary contributors to obstetric morbidity in OAPS [2, 3].

Standard therapy consists of low‐dose aspirin (LDA) and low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH). Recent recommendations from the International Antiphospholipid Antibody Conference highlight hydroxychloroquine and statins as emerging treatments [4]. Low‐dose prednisone and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are also utilized for refractory OAPS, while novel agents—such as eculizumab and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α inhibitors—have demonstrated promising efficacy [5, 6]. Despite these options, 20%–30% of patients still experience severe complications, often linked to irreversible placental damage [7, 8], underscoring the imperative for more effective therapeutic strategies [9].

Statins (3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl coenzyme A [HMG‐CoA] reductase inhibitors) reduce total cholesterol and low‐density lipoproteins and are widely used to prevent cardiovascular disease [10]. In patients with APS and hypercholesterolemia, statins prevent and treat OAPS‐related thrombosis through anti‐inflammatory, anticoagulant, and immunomodulatory effects [11, 12]. Although previously classified as contraindicated in pregnancy, accumulating evidence supports the safety of statins in this context. A European multicenter study and a large observational cohort (n = 1,152) found no increased risk of teratogenicity or major birth defects following first‐trimester statin exposure [13, 14]. In 2021, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) removed its strongest warning against statin use during pregnancy [15], allowing individualized treatment based on maternal disease severity. Meta‐analyses further confirm their safety [16, 17, 18].

Mechanistically, pravastatin demonstrates the potential to prevent or treat preeclampsia by lowering lipid levels, improving endothelial function, and reducing oxidative stress [19, 20]. Simvastatin reduces maternal blood pressure in experimental PE and FGR via extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK)/mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling without impairing fetal weight, while fluvastatin inhibits APS‐associated thrombosis by suppressing monocyte tissue factor, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and Flt1 [21, 22]. Simvastatin also attenuates uterine smooth muscle contractility during inflammation, preventing preterm birth [23]. Our previous work demonstrated that simvastatin attenuates neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)‐mediated inflammation in OAPS and prevents adverse pregnancy outcomes without affecting placental development [24]. Collectively, these findings highlight statins as promising candidates for treating placenta‐derived pregnancy disorders. However, mechanistic studies elucidating how statins counteract aPL‐induced placental dysfunction remain limited, and current clinical use in refractory OAPS is largely empirical [2], emphasizing the necessity for further investigation.

During early gestation, trophoblasts migrate into maternal spiral arteries, infiltrate the vascular wall, and remodel these vessels into large‐capacity, low‐resistance channels [25]. This transformation is initiated by the invasion of extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) into the decidual stroma and spiral arteries of the proximal decidua and myometrium [26]. Early trophoblast‐induced occlusion of spiral arteries produces high‐resistance, low‐velocity flow that generates low shear stress, a mechanical cue essential for trophoblast migration and successful spiral artery remodeling [27]. Trophoblast invasion is driven by the differentiation and phenotypic maturation of EVTs, whose intrinsic capacity declines with gestation, whereas key molecules, including HLA‐G and MMPs, coordinately drive extracellular matrix degradation and cellular migration, and this process is further modulated by a spectrum of decidual‐derived secretory factors [28].

Advances in in vitro modeling have unveiled novel therapeutic targets for OAPS. Organoid and organ‐on‐chip platforms provide novel avenues to delineate OAPS pathogenesis and refine treatment strategies [29]. The placenta‐on‐a‐chip system offers a physiologically relevant framework for evaluating the efficacy of simvastatin in restoring trophoblast invasion competency disrupted by aberrant hemodynamic shear stress at the maternal–fetal interface [30]. In 2021, Pu et al. [31] established a 3D microfluidic placenta‐on‐a‐chip that mimics trophoblast–endothelial interactions, facilitating assessments of invasive behavior and toxicology. Richardson et al. [32] developed two maternal–fetal interface chip models mimicking the trophoblast–endothelial bilayer to characterize statin diffusion, metabolism, and pharmacodynamics. Current innovations increasingly incorporate physiologic mechanical cues, such as shear stress, to promote trophoblast syncytialization and enhance model fidelity [33].

In this study, we combine an OAPS mouse model with a placenta‐on‐a‐chip platform to replicate aPL‐induced hemodynamic disturbances at the maternal–fetal interface. We comprehensively evaluate the protective effects of simvastatin and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms, offering novel perspectives for optimizing therapeutic strategies for OAPS.

Results

Simvastatin Attenuates Decidual Vessel Pathology and Enhances Placental Perfusion in OAPS Mice

To evaluate the impact of simvastatin on pregnancy outcomes, we established an OAPS mouse model via tail vein injection of anti‐β2GPI antibodies (Figure 1A). Mice were sacrificed at E14.5 to assess the fetal resorption frequency (FRF) and fetal weight (Figure 1B). Compared with the OAPS group, simvastatin administration significantly decreased the FRF (p = 0.0007; Figure 1D) and increased fetal weight (p = 0.0002; Figure 1E). Histopathological examination of placental and decidual tissues via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed pronounced pathological alterations in the OAPS group, characterized by thinning of the basal decidual zone, inflammatory vascular lesions within the junctional zone, and a contracted labyrinthine area. Furthermore, decidual tissues displayed marked vascular hyperplasia, inflammatory infiltration, vessel wall thickening, stenosis, and luminal fibrinoid necrosis. Simvastatin treatment markedly attenuated these pathological features (Figure 1C). Immunofluorescence staining for IL‐1β and IL‐6 indicated heightened inflammatory responses in the decidual vasculature of mice with OAPS; these responses were significantly mitigated following simvastatin treatment (Figure 1F,G). Similarly, levels of VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1 were markedly upregulated in the OAPS group and were substantially suppressed by simvastatin (Figure 1H,I), indicative of diminished endothelial inflammation and adhesion signaling. Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated that eNOS expression in the vascular endothelium was significantly downregulated in the OAPS group but was restored by simvastatin (p = 0.0018; Figure 1J,L), suggesting ameliorated endothelial vasodilatory function. Finally, FITC‐dextran imaging exposed a marked reduction in placental perfusion in mice with OAPS (p < 0.001; Figure 1K); however, simvastatin significantly recuperated perfusion intensity (Figure 1M). Collectively, these findings indicate that simvastatin mitigates aPL‐induced decidual vascular injury, attenuates inflammation, restores endothelial function, and augments placental blood flow in the OAPS model.

Simvastatin Improves Uterine Artery Hemodynamics and Promotes Placental Perfusion in OAPS Mice

Given that the uterine artery constitutes the primary source of placental blood supply, we interrogated uteroplacental circulation using the Vevo 3100 system at E14.5. Doppler evaluation of the uterine artery (Figure 2A) revealed that mice with OAPS exhibited elevated pulsatility index (PI) and resistance index (RI), a sharp systolic peak, and a persistent early‐diastolic notch compared with controls. Simvastatin administration significantly lowered the PI and RI (Figure 2B,C), indicative of reduced downstream vascular resistance.

Color Doppler imaging of branch‐level blood flow of the uterine artery (Figure 2D) demonstrated that aPL exposure markedly compromised mean blood flow velocity. Simvastatin treatment increased both the velocity‐time integral (VTI) and flow velocity, although these differences did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.0535 and p = 0.1533, respectively; Figure 2E,F). Moreover, the VTI, a critical parameter reflecting placental perfusion, remained significantly lower in the OAPS group relative to the other cohorts. Together, these data demonstrate that simvastatin ameliorates the high‐resistance uterine artery flow induced by aPLs and partially restores placental perfusion.

Simvastatin Rescues Impaired Trophoblast Invasion Under Pathological Shear Stress Using a Placenta‐on‐a‐Chip Model

Design and Establishment of the Placenta‐on‐a‐Chip System

To recapitulate the microenvironment of early pregnancy in which EVTs invade the microvascular endothelium and remodel spiral arteries (6–9 weeks of gestation), we utilized a commercial microfluidic organ‐on‐a‐chip platform (Emulate CHIP‐S1) (Figure 3D). The system comprises two PDMS microchannels separated by a 3D microfluidic scaffold engineered to approximate human physiological conditions (Figure S3A–D).

A thin ECM membrane separated the compartments seeded with primary human EVTs and HUVECs, thereby establishing an EVT channel and a maternal microvascular endothelial channel (Figure 3A). Primary EVTs isolated from chorionic villi of healthy pregnancies (6–9 weeks of gestation) were verified via immunofluorescence (Figure 3B) and labeled with Green CMFDA for seeding in the upper channel. HUVECs isolated from fresh umbilical cords were confirmed by immunofluorescence (Figure 3C), labeled with CellTracker Red CMTPX, and seeded in the lower channel. At 12 h post‐seeding, both cell types adhered robustly, and by 24 h, they formed confluent monolayers (Figure 3E). To assess EVT invasion, we applied the optimal shear stress specified by the manufacturer (30 µL/h; 0.01 dyn/cm2) [34] and conducted confocal imaging (Figure 3G). GFP‐labeled EVTs were observed invading the endothelial layer in the lower channel (Figure 3H). Z‐stack reconstruction following DAPI staining further confirmed EVT penetration across the endothelial interface, demonstrating the successful establishment of a 3D EVT–endothelium invasion model (Figure 3F). This chip platform thus faithfully recapitulates early trophoblast invasion and offers a physiologically relevant system to evaluate the impact of pathological shear stress and simvastatin intervention.

Simvastatin Enhances EVT Invasion under High Shear Stress through Endothelial Cell Modulation

After 24 h of culture in the placenta chip, green fluorescence from EVTs first appeared along the endothelial interface and intensified progressively over 72 h, evidencing directional migration and invasion into the lower channel (Figure 4A). To evaluate the impact of shear stress on EVT invasion, we established low (0.01 dyn/cm2), middle (0.1 dyn/cm2), and high (0.3 dyn/cm2) shear stress conditions, alongside an additional high shear stress group treated with simvastatin. Confocal imaging quantified EVT invasion based on fluorescence intensity and cell counts. As shown in Figure 4B,C, EVT fluorescence did not differ between the low and middle shear stress conditions (p = 0.1589). In contrast, high shear stress significantly attenuated fluorescence intensity relative to both low and middle shear stress groups (p = 0.0012 and p = 0.0208, respectively). Simvastatin significantly rescued the invasive phenotype under high shear stress (p = 0.0482). Cell counts displayed a consistent pattern (Figure 4D–E): the middle shear stress group exhibited a non‐significant trend toward reduction compared with low shear stress (p = 0.0737), whereas high shear stress significantly reduced cell numbers relative to both groups (p < 0.001). Together, these results demonstrate that EVT invasion declines progressively with increasing shear stress and that simvastatin effectively ameliorates this deficit under high shear stress.

Considering that EVTs migrate along spiral arteries during early pregnancy, interacting with maternal endothelial cells to transform vascular terminals into funnel‐shaped conduits that reduce flow velocity and prevent turbulence‐induced villous injury (Figure 4F), we further examined EVT–endothelial interactions under shear flow. Because low and middle shear stress outcomes did not differ significantly, subsequent experiments included only low shear stress (0.01 dyn/cm2), high shear stress (0.3 dyn/cm2), and high shear stress with simvastatin. To determine whether simvastatin augments EVT invasion by modulating endothelial function, we evaluated eNOS and vascular endothelial cadherin (VE‐cadherin) expression in HUVECs using immunofluorescence and Z‐stack reconstruction (Figure 4G). To assess endothelial barrier integrity during EVT invasion, we quantified VE‐cadherin levels. VE‐cadherin expression was significantly lower under low shear stress than under high shear stress (p < 0.001; Figure 4H), suggesting that EVT invasion perturbs endothelial junctions. To further delineate how simvastatin enhances invasion under high shear stress, we analyzed eNOS expression. High shear stress markedly reduced eNOS levels, whereas simvastatin significantly upregulated eNOS (p < 0.001; Figure 4I). High shear stress also increased IL‐1β and IL‐6 concentrations in the lower channel (p < 0.01 and p = 0.0298, respectively; Figure 4J,K) and decreased matrix metalloproteinase‐2 (MMP2) in the upper channel (p < 0.001; Figure 4L). Simvastatin substantially reversed these perturbations. Collectively, these findings indicate that simvastatin indirectly promotes EVT invasion by enhancing endothelial vasodilatory function via eNOS elevation, suppressing inflammation, and restoring MMP2 expression.

High‐Throughput Sequencing Reveals Molecular Mechanisms by Which Simvastatin Protects Against aPL‐Induced Vascular Endothelial Injury

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing simvastatin‐mediated protection against aPL‐induced endothelial injury, we conducted high‐throughput mRNA sequencing on endothelial cells exposed to aPLs alone or aPLs combined with simvastatin. Differential expression analysis of the 34,458 detected genes yielded 806 significantly dysregulated transcripts, comprising 217 upregulated and 589 downregulated genes (Figure 5A,B). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed for both gene sets. Among the upregulated genes, the top 20 enriched pathways included signal transduction and nuclear factor‐kappa B (NF‐κB) signaling (Figure 5C,D). Conversely, the downregulated genes were enriched in pathways involving phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)–Akt, Ca2 + signaling, and cytokine–receptor interactions (Figure 5E–H). These data suggest that simvastatin modulates endothelial function via regulation of these signal transduction pathways. Specifically, co‐treatment with aPLs and simvastatin (A+S) inhibited NF‐κB signaling while activating the PI3K–Akt and Ca2+ signaling pathways, which function as canonical routes for eNOS activation and nitric oxide (NO) production. Notably, expression of the transcription factor Kruppel‐like factor 2 (KLF2) was significantly upregulated in the A+S group. Given that KLF2 serves as a key transcription factor for endothelial protection, we hypothesize that simvastatin mitigates endothelial injury by modulating the KLF2/eNOS signaling axis.

In Vitro Study Demonstrates that Simvastatin Attenuates aPL‐Induced Functional Injury in HUVECs via the KLF2/eNOS Pathway

Initially, we assessed HUVEC viability to establish a safe and effective simvastatin concentration. Simvastatin at 50 µm significantly compromised cell viability compared with the control; however, lower concentrations (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 µm) elicited no detectable cytotoxicity (Figure 6A). Accordingly, 10 µM simvastatin was selected for subsequent experiments. Western blotting and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‐PCR) analyses confirmed that aPL exposure markedly upregulated VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, and E‐selectin expression in HUVECs (Figure 6B), an effect significantly reversed by simvastatin treatment (Figure 6D–I). Flow cytometry revealed that aPLs induced a robust elevation in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), whereas simvastatin significantly attenuated ROS levels (p < 0.0001 vs. aPL; Figure 6C,L). Imaging with DAF‐FM DA demonstrated that simvastatin restored NO bioavailability suppressed by aPL stimulation (p < 0.0001 vs. aPL; Figure 6J,M). Furthermore, enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results indicated that simvastatin attenuated aPL‐induced secretion of IL‐6 and IL‐1β (p < 0.0001 vs. aPL; Figure 6O,P).

To investigate whether endothelial modulation mediates the restoration of EVT invasion, we established a Transwell coculture system comprising HUVECs and HTR8/SVneo trophoblasts. As shown in Figure 6K,N, aPLs significantly inhibited HTR8/SVneo invasion compared with the Control group (p = 0.001). Crucially, co‐treatment of HUVECs with simvastatin markedly attenuated this inhibitory effect, resulting in a significant increase in invading cells (p = 0.0025 vs. Control). These findings suggest that simvastatin indirectly promotes trophoblast invasion by modulating endothelial signaling pathways.

To delineate the regulatory role of simvastatin on the KLF2/eNOS axis, we employed small interfering RNA (siRNA) to silence KLF2 in HUVECs. Western blot analysis demonstrated that simvastatin upregulated KLF2 and eNOS protein expression in aPL‐treated HUVECs (Figure 6Q). As shown in Figure 6R, aPLs significantly suppressed KLF2 protein levels (p = 0.0055), whereas simvastatin restored KLF2 expression (p = 0.0180). This restorative effect was abrogated by KLF2 knockdown (p = 0.0422). Similarly, aPLs markedly reduced eNOS protein expression (p < 0.001); simvastatin significantly restored eNOS levels (p = 0.049), an effect eliminated by siKLF2 (p = 0.0200; Figure 6S). Transcriptional analyses corroborated this pattern: aPLs downregulated KLF2 and eNOS mRNA, simvastatin reversed this reduction, and siKLF2 completely blocked the rescue effect (Figure 6T,U). Collectively, these results demonstrate that simvastatin counteracts aPL‐mediated suppression of KLF2, and that KLF2 is essential for the simvastatin‐mediated recovery of eNOS expression. These findings support a model in which simvastatin preserves endothelial function by activating the KLF2–eNOS signaling pathway.

Discussion

The development of an optimal therapeutic strategy for obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome (OAPS) remains a formidable clinical challenge [1, 9, 35]. In recent years, statins have garnered increasing attention for their utility in managing pregnancy complications, owing to their pleiotropic anti‐inflammatory, antithrombotic, and immunomodulatory properties [6, 19, 22, 23, 36]. However, the mechanistic basis by which simvastatin ameliorates OAPS pathology has not been definitively elucidated. Here, employing a placenta‐on‐a‐chip platform, we systematically delineate, for the first time, the molecular mechanisms through which simvastatin mitigates aPL‐mediated placental hypoperfusion. Our findings demonstrate that simvastatin attenuates vascular inflammation and restores hemodynamic function by activating the KLF2/eNOS signaling pathway in endothelial cells. These results position simvastatin as a promising therapeutic candidate for refractory OAPS, given its capacity to improve uteroplacental hemodynamics.

Vascular endothelial cells (ECs) serve as primary cellular targets of aPLs, and aPL–EC interactions initiate multiple pathological cascades [2]. Viall CA et al. [3] analyzed placental pathology in 580 aPL‐positive cases and reported decidual inflammation and defective spiral artery remodeling as hallmark lesions of OAPS. Canaud et al. [37] later demonstrated that aPL‐β2GPI binding activates PI3K–AKT–mTORC signaling, triggering aberrant endothelial proliferation, vascular wall thickening, lumen narrowing, and tissue ischemia. Consistent with these observations, decidual tissues from our OAPS mouse model exhibited impaired spiral artery remodeling, luminal stenosis, and fibrinoid necrosis—lesions that were markedly ameliorated by simvastatin treatment. Previous studies have established that aPLs engage TLR4/2 and ApoER2 on ECs, activating NF‐κB signaling via the TLR4/MyD88 axis and driving the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, adhesion molecules, and tissue factor [38, 39, 40]. Concurrently, this interaction suppresses eNOS and NO production, thereby promoting vasoconstriction, platelet aggregation, and defective spiral artery remodeling [41, 42]. Consistently, in our study, simvastatin significantly upregulated eNOS expression in the decidual tissue of mice with OAPS. Correspondingly, we observed elevated IL‐6 and IL‐1β levels, along with increased ICAM‐1 and VCAM‐1 expression in the OAPS decidual vasculature, all of which were attenuated by simvastatin treatment. Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that simvastatin mitigates decidual vascular inflammation and preserves placental structural integrity in OAPS, thereby reducing adverse pregnancy outcomes.

We subsequently investigated the impact of simvastatin on uteroplacental hemodynamics, as uterine artery flow patterns are critical determinants of placental perfusion [43, 44, 45]. Recent analyses of aPL‐positive placentas revealed markedly increased risks of maternal and fetal vascular malperfusion (MVM/FVM) compared with healthy pregnancies [46]. Consistent with this pathology, our study also identified histopathological features of impaired MVM/FVM in OAPS placentas relative to normal controls (Figure S1). Jurisic et al. [47, 48] reported that statins ameliorate uteroplacental vascular injury and normalize hemodynamic parameters via NO‐dependent pathways. Similarly, Lefkou et al. [42, 49] showed that pravastatin, when combined with standard therapy, corrects aPL‐induced hemodynamic abnormalities and prevents adverse pregnancy outcomes in refractory OAPS. In agreement with these findings, our OAPS mice displayed significantly elevated uterine artery pulsatility index (PI) and resistance index (RI), reflecting increased vascular resistance and compromised placental perfusion. Simvastatin reversed these abnormalities, lowering uterine artery resistance and enhancing perfusion, consistent with prior animal studies demonstrating statin‐mediated restoration of placental blood flow in preeclampsia [50, 51]. Nonetheless, branch‐level blood flow velocity and VTI remained only partially corrected, likely due to the absence of remodeling in proximal muscular arteries (arcuate and radial arteries), whose flow responses depend on endocrine and NO‐mediated dilation [52]. Overall, our data demonstrate that simvastatin enhances uteroplacental hemodynamics and improves placental perfusion by mitigating maternal vascular inflammation, reducing adhesion molecule expression, and restoring aPL‐suppressed eNOS expression.

During early pregnancy, EVTs migrate into the decidua to facilitate the physiological conversion of maternal spiral arteries, with maternal–fetal exchange beginning around day 35 and continuing through the first trimester [25]. Aberrations in EVT‐mediated vascular remodeling contribute to the pathogenesis of disorders such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction. Mathematical modeling indicates that physiological remodeling produces a 4–10‐fold luminal dilation (2–3 mm), which generates a funnel‐shaped orifice and reduces inflow velocities from 1–2 m/s to ≈10 cm/s; this optimizes oxygen exchange while mitigating turbulent jet‐induced villous injury [52]. Conversely, pathological remodeling results in inadequate dilation and the formation of high‐velocity inflow jets at arterial openings. Roth et al. [53]. demonstrated that undilated spiral arteries generate high‐velocity inflow jets that increase local shear stress, elevate intervillous pressure, and create recirculating vortices—factors that collectively contribute to intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). Although ultrasound resolution limits the direct measurement of spiral artery jets in the OAPS model, multiple reviews [2, 54, 55] suggest that defective spiral artery remodeling produces turbulent high‐velocity inflow and elevated wall shear stress at the maternal–fetal interface.

To determine whether simvastatin mitigates the adverse effects of high‐velocity flow on EVT invasion, a placenta‐on‐a‐chip platform was utilized to model first‐trimester (6–9 weeks) EVT invasion under controlled shear stress. Physiologically low shear stress promotes trophoblast invasion, syncytiotrophoblast differentiation, and metabolic regulation [56, 57, 58], whereas abnormally high shear stress disrupts villous architecture, induces oxidative stress, and increases the risk of pregnancy complications [33, 53, 59, 60]. Simvastatin is also documented to counteract vascular inflammation and pathological proliferation induced by disturbed shear stress in atherosclerotic models [61, 62, 63, 64]. Consistent with these reports, results from the current platform demonstrated EVT invasion under low and moderate shear stress, whereas high shear stress markedly suppressed invasion—a pathological effect reversed by simvastatin treatment.

Endothelial cell modulation was further explored as a potential mechanism for simvastatin‐enhanced EVT invasion. Park et al. [65] showed that EVT invasive capacity is regulated by endothelial cells within placenta‐on‐a‐chip models, suggesting a role for endothelium‐derived extracellular matrix cues. EVT invasion is further modulated by chemokines and adhesion molecules secreted by ECs under low shear stress [66, 67, 68]. Orsenigo et al. [69] demonstrated that shear stress modulates vascular permeability through Src‐dependent VE‐cadherin phosphorylation: low shear stress increases VE‐cadherin phosphorylation to loosen junctions, whereas high shear stress stabilizes these junctions. Consistently, elevated VE‐cadherin expression was observed under high shear stress in this study, which decreased following simvastatin treatment. Simvastatin also increased MMP‐2 levels in the effluent, which facilitates extracellular matrix degradation and enhances EVT invasion. Moreover, simvastatin upregulates endothelial eNOS expression, suggesting that the vasodilatory factor NO may support EVT invasion under pathological shear stress. Results from the transwell coculture system further indicate that simvastatin indirectly promotes EVT invasion by ameliorating aPL‐induced endothelial damage. While the precise molecular pathways by which endothelial eNOS signaling modulates EVT behavior remain to be fully elucidated, future rescue experiments under flow conditions designed to isolate endothelial or EVT responses to aPLs would be valuable.

Spiral artery remodeling requires EVTs to migrate toward maternal vessels, degrade the extracellular matrix, invade the microvasculature, and replace endothelial cells [70]. Park et al. [65] also found that EVTs induce endothelial cell apoptosis via a caspase‐dependent pathway, thereby replacing maternal endothelial cells and promoting spiral artery remodeling. James et al. [27] further reported that low shear stress promotes trophoblast invasion via NO signaling and facilitates trophoblast‐induced endothelial apoptosis through the Fas/FasL and TRAIL pathway. Notably, this mechanism is characterized as contact‐dependent, wherein endothelial apoptosis occurs primarily upon direct interaction with trophoblasts [26]. Interestingly, our in vitro coculture experiments of endothelial cells with EVTs demonstrated that EVTs adhere to endothelial cells and jointly form tubular structures, and the concomitant decrease in endothelial cell number indicates that EVTs can replace endothelial cells (Figure S4); these observations align with the findings reported by Xu et al. [71]. Moreover, during EVT migration, increased MMP‐2 levels in the effluent degrade elastin and the extracellular matrix and disrupt the vascular smooth muscle layer structure, thereby facilitating trophoblast invasion into the endothelium. Although it is unlikely that direct cell‐contact pathways are the sole factors of importance in human uterine artery remodeling in vivo, our study indicates that the interaction between trophoblasts and endothelial cells establishes essential conditions that are permissive for spiral artery remodeling from a hemodynamic perspective. Taken together, by modulating endothelial function, simvastatin indirectly enhances EVT invasion under pathological shear stress, which in turn facilitates spiral artery remodeling.

At the mechanistic level, mRNA transcriptome sequencing was utilized to delineate how simvastatin reverses aPL‐mediated endothelial injury. KLF2 emerged as a significantly upregulated transcription factor among the differentially expressed genes, a finding validated by qRT‐PCR and Western blot analyses. As a canonical mechanosensitive regulator, KLF2 responds dynamically to blood‐flow‐derived shear stress and maintains vascular homeostasis by modulating endothelial genes involved in anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and antithrombotic processes [72]. Previous studies have shown that statins correct aPL‐induced dysregulation of KLF2/KLF4 expression and suppress endothelial inflammation and thrombosis by inhibiting NF‐κB activation [73]. Moreover, Nayak et al. [74] demonstrated that shear stress induces KLF2 expression through dual MEKK2/3–MEK5–ERK5 and mitochondrial metabolic pathways, thereby promoting eNOS expression, enhancing NO production, and reducing inflammatory signaling and adhesion molecule expression. Consistently, simvastatin treatment in the current study upregulated KLF2 while suppressing inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules in aPL‐exposed endothelial cells. These results align with previous reports [75, 76, 77] showing that simvastatin enhances KLF2 and the downstream eNOS pathway to restore endothelial homeostasis.

These findings further define a critical KLF2–eNOS regulatory axis governing endothelial responses under aPL challenge. Results show that aPL exposure markedly downregulates KLF2 transcription, whereas simvastatin restores its expression. This rescue effect is abolished by KLF2 knockdown, confirming that the protective actions of simvastatin depend largely on KLF2 upregulation. eNOS mRNA exhibited a parallel transcriptional pattern to KLF2; specifically, simvastatin restored eNOS expression in a KLF2‐dependent manner, as siRNA‐mediated KLF2 silencing eliminated the simvastatin‐induced increase in eNOS. Recent research [78] also demonstrated that statins activate eNOS through LRP8/ApoER2 signaling in a β2GPI‐lipid‐raft‐dependent manner, offering an additional mechanistic target for APS therapy. Beyond the KLF2 axis, transcriptomic analysis revealed that simvastatin suppresses multiple aPL‐driven proinflammatory and injury‐associated pathways, consistent with recent findings [38]. These results indicate that simvastatin not only protects endothelial cells in OAPS but also broadly regulates cellular homeostasis, thereby supporting the stabilization of the maternal–fetal interface and potentially improving pregnancy outcomes.

Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. First, while the animal model replicates the core pathological features of OAPS, it may not fully capture the complexities of treatment‐resistant disease. Future studies should incorporate standard‐of‐care therapy to induce a refractory phenotype in mice, enabling the evaluation of simvastatin as an adjunct or salvage treatment. Second, the absence of validation using pathological placental tissues from patients with OAPS limits the clinical translatability of these findings, partly due to the current low utilization of simvastatin in this patient population. Incorporating patient‐derived placental samples in future work would enhance both disease specificity and clinical relevance. Additionally, because the current model lacks immune cells and decidual stromal components, it is not fully representative of the in vivo disease environment. Future investigations should integrate immune cell populations, such as macrophages [79] and NK cells [80], to more comprehensively characterize the relationship between hemodynamics, endothelial cells, and trophoblasts in OAPS. Lastly, the safety and clinical applicability of simvastatin during pregnancy warrant confirmation through large‐scale clinical trials.

Conclusion

A multimodal experimental platform was established by integrating a mouse model of OAPS, organ‐on‐a‐chip technology, cellular assays, and transcriptomic analyses to systematically evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of simvastatin in ameliorating placental hypoperfusion secondary to abnormal uteroplacental hemodynamics. These findings elucidate a fundamental mechanism through which simvastatin restores trophoblast invasion impaired by aPLs at the maternal–fetal interface via activation of the endothelial KLF2/eNOS signaling axis, which indirectly facilitates spiral artery remodeling and enhances placental perfusion. Furthermore, this custom‐engineered maternal–fetal interface chip system provides a sophisticated in vitro framework for future mechanistic investigations and toxicological assessments.

Experimental Section

Establishment of the OAPS Mouse Model and Simvastatin Intervention

Healthy SPF‐grade C57BL/6J wild‐type mice (Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.), aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 18 ± 2 g, were maintained at 26°C under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, males and females were paired at a 2:1 ratio at 18:00. Examination for vaginal plugs occurred at 8:00 the following morning; plug‐positive mice were designated embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). Mice were identified via ear studs and housed individually.

The OAPS model was established via tail vein injection of aPL‐IgG [81] (Changzhou Xiangtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Pregnant mice in the OAPS and OAPS+Sim cohorts received aPL‐IgG (5 mg/kg) on E0.5 and E7.5; conversely, control animals, and all other experimental groups—excluding the OAPS+Sim cohort—received equal‐volume saline. Simvastatin (5 mg/kg/day; MSD China Co., Ltd.) was administered via gavage from E0.5 to E14.5 in the OAPS+Sim group. On E14.5, uterine artery blood flow and placental perfusion were evaluated by ultrasound. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with the 2020 AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Uteroplacental tissues were harvested, the decidua was dissected, and fetal/placental weights were recorded. The embryo resorption rate was calculated as follows: (number of resorbed embryos / total pregnancies) × 100%. Tissues were either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or stored at −80°C.

Hematoxylin‐Eosin (H&E) Staining and Immunohistochemistry

For H&E staining, placental and decidual tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 µm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Images were acquired using an Olympus VS200 scanner (Olympus, Japan).

For immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was neutralized with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 min, followed by non‐specific site blocking with 3% BSA for 30 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by treatment with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min. Visualization was facilitated by DAB, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting. Images were captured using an Olympus VS200 and analyzed via OlyVIA software.

Primary antibodies: CD31 (GB15063, 1:1200, Servicebio); eNOS (AF0096, 1:100, Affinity).

Secondary antibody: HRP‐goat anti‐mouse IgG (GB23301, 1:200, Servicebio).

Immunofluorescent Staining of Decidual Tissues

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, antigen‐retrieved, and blocked with BSA (GC305010, Servicebio) for 30 min. Slides were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio). Autofluorescence was neutralized using a quenching agent (G1221, Servicebio) prior to mounting. Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse C1 microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified via ImageJ.

Primary antibodies: CD31 (GB15063, 1:500, Servicebio); ICAM (GB14040, 1:1000, Servicebio); VCAM‐1 (TA502391S, 1:100, ORIGENE); IL‐6 (GB11117+G, 1:1600, Servicebio); IL‐1β (B11113, 1:1600, Servicebio).

Secondary antibodies: CY3‐goat anti‐mouse IgG (GB21301, 1:300, Servicebio); HRP‐goat anti‐rabbit IgG (GB23303, 1:500, Servicebio).

Assessment of Placental Vascular Perfusion

At E14.5, 100 µL of FITC‐dextran (F70109, SAITONC) was injected via the tail vein. Ten minutes later, mice were euthanized to prevent fluorescence quenching; placentas were rapidly excised, embedded in freezing medium, and sectioned at ‐25°C. Fluorescence was imaged using an Olympus VS200 scanner (excitation 488 nm), and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified via ImageJ.

Placental Ultrasound and Uterine Artery Doppler Parameters

Pregnant mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (maintained at 1.2–1.5%) and positioned supine on a 40°C platform. Following abdominal hair removal, imaging was performed using a Vevo 3100 system (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, USA) with an MX400 linear probe (30 MHz). In pulsed wave Doppler mode, the uterine artery was identified posterior to the bladder, and the insonation angle was maintained at ≤ 30°. Spectra from ≥ 3 stable cardiac cycles were acquired. Vevo LAB 5.8.2 software was utilized to quantify PSV and EDV and calculate PI = (PSV—EDV) / mean velocity, RI = (PSV—EDV) / PSV, and VTI. Triplicate measurements were performed by a single operator to ensure technical consistency, and mean values were calculated. Continuous ECG monitoring ensured heart rate variation remained < 10%. Spectral envelope clarity served as the primary criterion for data validity.

Isolation and Identification of Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs)

Fresh umbilical cords were transported on ice in PBS supplemented with 10% P/S (Gibco). Following three PBS washes to remove residual blood, vessels were perfused with 10–15 mL of 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (140642, Beyotime) and digested for 10 min at room temperature. The reaction was neutralized with ECM complete medium (1001, Sciencell), and the cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in ECM medium and seeded into T25 flasks. HUVECs were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2, with medium replacement every 24 h. Upon reaching 90% confluence, cells were passaged at a 1:3 ratio. Cells from passages 2–4 were utilized for all subsequent experiments.

For immunofluorescent identification, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X‐100, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Following incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies in the dark for 60 min, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Zeiss Cell Discoverer 7 confocal microscope. Antibodies included CD31 (Proteintech, 11265‐1‐AP, 1:200), CD34 (Proteintech, 14486‐1‐AP, 1:200), and vWF (Proteintech, 27186‐1‐AP, 1:200), with Alexa Fluor 488/594–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG secondaries.

Isolation and Identification of Primary EVTs

Chorionic villi were harvested from healthy pregnant donors at 6–9 weeks of gestation following spontaneous pregnancy loss. Villi were thoroughly rinsed with sterile PBS (PS0295, SAITONG) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) to eliminate residual blood. Tissue fragments (2–3 mm) were dissected using sterile ophthalmic scissors, resuspended in complete medium (8123426, Gibco), and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. EVT outgrowth was microscopically monitored, and upon reaching 80%–90% confluence, cells were detached utilizing trypsin for purification. Passage 2–4 cells were utilized for all subsequent experiments.

For identification, cells were fixed, permeabilized, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by treatment with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 60 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence imaging was conducted utilizing a Zeiss Cell Discoverer 7 confocal system. Antibodies utilized included CK7 (15539‐1‐AP, 1:100, Proteintech) and HLA‐G (ab283260, 1:100, Abcam), visualized with Alexa Fluor 488/594–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG secondaries.

Seeding and Culture of the Placenta‐on‐a‐Chip Model

Microfluidic organ chips (Emulate Inc.) composed of transparent PDMS were utilized. Each platform contained two parallel channels: an upper channel (1000 µm × 1000 µm) and a lower channel (1000 µm × 200 µm), separated by a 50‐µm porous membrane (7‐µm pores). Chips were activated by perfusing with 0.5 mg/mL ER1 buffer (Emulate Inc.) and exposing the platforms to UV light (Nailstar NS‐01‐US) for 15 min. Channels were rinsed with ER2 buffer and ice‐cold PBS to render the PDMS surfaces hydrophilic, with strict measures taken to prevent bubble formation.

Before seeding, channels were coated with extracellular matrix components: the upper chamber with Matrigel (356234, Corning) diluted 1:9 in medium, and the lower chamber with fibronectin (40 µg/mL; F8180, Solarbio). EVTs (6 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded into the upper channel, and HUVECs (1 × 106 cells/mL) into the lower channel. Following a 12 h incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, HUVECs were reseeded into the lower channel at the same density. The chip was then inverted to facilitate endothelial adhesion to the membrane, thereby reconstructing the maternal microvascular interface.

Chips were mounted onto Pod modules and placed into automated Zoe and Orb culture systems (Emulate Inc.) for precise regulation of flow rates and mechanical forces. Based on manufacturer guidelines, the optimal flow rate was 30 µL/h, corresponding to a shear stress of 0.01 dyn/cm2. Chips were assigned to four groups based on lower‐channel flow conditions: control (Low Shear, 30 µL/h, 0.01 dyn/cm2), Low Shear (300 µL/h, 0.1 dyn/cm2), High Shear (1000 µL/h, 0.33 dyn/cm2), and High Shear + Simvastatin (1000 µL/h, 0.33 dyn/cm2) [31, 33, 34, 82].

Immunofluorescent Staining of the Placenta‐on‐a‐Chip

Following removal from the culture system, both channels were rinsed with PBS and fixed with 100 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 15–20 min at room temperature. Channels were washed three times with PBS, and the lower channel was permeabilized by injecting 0.3% Triton X‐100 while the upper channel was filled with PBS. Following a 30 min incubation and subsequent washes, QuickBlock blocking solution (P0220, Beyotime) was applied to the lower channel for 2 h at room temperature.

Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by three PBS washes and a 2 h incubation with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and imaging was conducted utilizing a Zeiss Cell Discoverer 7 laser confocal microscope; subsequent analysis was performed using Zen Blue 3.7. Antibodies utilized included VE‐cadherin (1:50, 10433‐MM01, Sino‐Biological) and eNOS (1:100, AF0096, Affinity). Secondary antibodies included Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG (SA0013‐4, 1:500, Proteintech), Alexa Fluor 555–conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (A0453, 1:500, Beyotime), and Alexa Fluor 555–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (RGAR003, 1:500, Proteintech).

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

ELISA was utilized to quantify MMP‐2, IL‐6, and IL‐1β in effluents harvested separately from the upper and lower channels. Effluents were drawn from the medium storage tanks, transferred into 1.5‐mL EP tubes, and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to eliminate debris. Supernatants were aliquoted into fresh EP tubes and stored at −80°C. Commercial kits were utilized for IL‐6 (RE3186HG, Reed Biotech), IL‐1β (RE1074H, Reed Biotech), and MMP‐2 (RE1838H, Reed Biotech) according to manufacturer protocols. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm utilizing a SpectraMax Absorbance Reader (for Galectin‐3 detection, a reference wavelength of 540 nm was recorded simultaneously).

Cell Culture and Grouping (In Vitro Activation of Simvastatin)

Simvastatin, an inactive lactone prodrug, was hydrolyzed into its active β‐hydroxy acid form. Briefly, 43.245 mg of simvastatin was dissolved in 1 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by the addition of 1.5 mL of NaOH and incubation at 50°C for 2 h. The pH was adjusted to 7.0–7.4 utilizing 0.1 mmol/L HCl, and the solution was diluted to 10 mmol/L, sterile‐filtered (0.22 µm), and stored at 4°C. Passage 2–4 HUVECs were allocated into three groups: Control (equal volume of saline), aPL (60 µg/mL), and aPL+Sim (60 µg/mL aPL + 10 µmol/L simvastatin).

CCK‐8 Cell Viability Assay

HUVECs were treated with simvastatin at concentrations of 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, or 50 µmol/L for 24 h. Cell viability was evaluated utilizing a CCK‐8 kit (C0038, Beyotime) according to manufacturer protocols. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured utilizing a SpectraMax microplate reader to determine relative metabolic activity.

Flow Cytometry for ROS Detection

Cells were harvested and resuspended in serum‐free medium, followed by incubation with 10 µm DCFH‐DA (S0033S, Beyotime) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. Following centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in pre‐cooled PBS. ROS‐associated fluorescence was quantified utilizing a BD flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) within 30 min of preparation.

Nitric Oxide Assay

Intracellular NO was quantified utilizing DAF‐FM DA (S0020S, Beyotime) in accordance with manufacturer instructions. Following experimental treatment, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 5 µm DAF‐FM DA in serum‐free medium for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. After three PBS washes, fluorescence images were acquired within 30 min utilizing a Zeiss Cell Discoverer 7 confocal system and analyzed with Zen Blue 3.7.

Trophoblast Invasion Assay (Transwell Coculture)

A Transwell coculture system was established utilizing HUVECs seeded in the lower chamber of a 24‐well plate and permitted to adhere overnight. Following 24 h of pretreatment, the upper Transwell insert (Corning, NY, USA) was coated with 60 µL Matrigel (Sigma–Aldrich). Serum‐starved trophoblasts were resuspended and seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in 200 µL of serum‐free medium. Inserts were subsequently placed into the lower compartment containing pretreated HUVECs to facilitate soluble‐factor communication in the absence of direct cellular contact. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, invaded cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed, stained, and quantified across five random fields per membrane. Experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated independently for a minimum of three iterations.

Direct Coculture

HUVECs were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL in confocal imaging dishes and permitted to adhere for 12 h in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Adherent cells were subsequently stained with 5 µmol/L CellTracker Red CMTPX (HY‐D1727, MCE) for 30 min. Following incubation, the staining medium was aspirated, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Separately, EVTs were stained with 7.5 µmol/L Green CMFDA (HY‐126561, MCE) for 45 min. After PBS washes, labeled EVTs were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended to a final density of 2 × 105 cells/mL. This suspension was applied to the HUVEC monolayers for a 48 h coculture period. Control cohorts received an equal volume of PBS in lieu of the EVT suspension. Imaging was conducted utilizing a Zeiss Cell Discoverer 7 laser confocal microscope, and HUVEC density was quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

Experimental groups included a minimum of three biological replicates (n = 3). Results are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 10.0. For comparisons involving ≥ 3 cohorts, one‐way or two‐way ANOVA was utilized, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Two‐group comparisons were performed utilizing two‐tailed t‐tests to calculate p‐values and assess statistical significance.
