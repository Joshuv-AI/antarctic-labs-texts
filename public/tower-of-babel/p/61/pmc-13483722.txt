Mechanism study of RUNX1/NSUN2 upregulating fatty acid-binding protein 5 through 5-methylcytosine methylation to mediate lipid metabolic reprogramming to promote immunotherapy resistance in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Changjun He, Yingbin Li, Haowen He, Fucheng Zhou, Bicheng Fu, Jianlong Bu, Mingyu Wang, Xianglong Kong, Pengju Li, Boxiong Ni, Shidong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study explored the mechanism of RUNX1/NSUN2/FABP5 promoting immunotherapy resistance in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsNSCLC patients received anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy. In vitro A549 and NCI-H520 cells and in vivo xenograft mouse models were established. RUNX1, NSUN2, FABP5, PD-L1, and lipid metabolic profiles were analyzed using RT-qPCR, Western blot, and biochemical assays. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed to assess the roles of RUNX1, FABP5, and NSUN2 in NSCLC. CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues were assessed through flow cytometry. A NSCLC cell-CD8⁺ T cell co-culture system was established, with T cell activation and effector cytokine secretion determined. The mechanism of the RUNX1/NSUN2/FABP5 axis in NSCLC was elucidated by dual-luciferase, ChIP, MeRIP, RIP, and Actinomycin D assays.ResultsRUNX1 was highly expressed in immunotherapy-resistant NSCLC. RUNX1 was positively correlated with elevated serum lipids and negatively associated with CD8⁺ T cell activation in tumor tissues. RUNX1 promoted PD-L1 expression and resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo via FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming. RUNX1 regulated FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming, enhanced PD-L1 expression, and induced CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction. FABP5 knockdown inhibited lipid metabolism and partially reversed the effects of RUNX1 in NSCLC. RUNX1 promoted NSUN2 transcription, which in turn increased m⁵C methylation of FABP5 and enhanced FABP5 mRNA stability. NSUN2 knockdown partially averted RUNX1’s effect in NSCLC.ConclusionRUNX1 alters m⁵C methylation levels by regulating NSUN2 transcription to promote FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming, which in turn enhances PD-L1 expression and consequently induces immune resistance in NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03728-w.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) denotes a primary subtype of lung cancers that stands as the primary cause of cancer-associated mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Recently, immunotherapy, especially immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, has reshaped the scenery of NSCLC treatment [3]. Unfortunately, a significant portion of patients either fail to respond or develop acquired resistance, ultimately leading to disease progression [4]. Thus, continued investigation into new drugs and therapies is crucial to expanding clinical benefits to a broader affected population and to improving clinical outcomes in NSCLC. The mechanisms underlying ICB resistance are mainly associated with the tumor microenvironment, defects in antigen presentation and interferon signaling, exclusion of T cells, and immune inhibitory molecules [5]. Therapies targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) represent a crucial component of ICB treatment [6]. The PD-1/PD-L1 axis is a key negative regulator of T cells. When tumor cells exhibit high PD-L1 expression, the binding of PD-L1 to PD-1 on T cells inhibits T cell function, leading to immune escape [6, 7].

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer [8]. Metabolic reprogramming indicates that the metabolic characteristics and preferences of tumors alter during progression and may represent pathways leading to drug resistance [9]. This phenomenon has been documented in various drug-resistant tumors, including lung cancer, acute myeloid leukemia, mantle cell lymphoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma [10–14]. Lipids, components of cells, are critical to tumor biology by supporting cell growth and facilitating signal transduction processes [15]. Cancer cells exhibit a heightened requirement for lipids to meet the immediate needs for energy production and membrane synthesis during division and to sustain signaling synthesis and bioactive molecules that maintain the cellular processes implicated in cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [16]. Lipid metabolic reprogramming, involved in cancer initiation, invasiveness, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and immunity, has garnered widespread research attention [17–19]. Specifically, lipid metabolic rewiring is a key driver of lung carcinogenesis, progression, and therapy resistance [20]. As lipid building blocks, fatty acids (FA) are pivotal players across all phases of cancer development and progression [21]. Fatty acid binding proteins (FABPs), a family of evolutionarily conserved proteins, are responsible for intracellular transport, metabolism, and signaling responses inside cells [22]. In lung adenocarcinoma, FABP5 is robustly induced and represents a regulatory hub of lipid metabolism and tumor progression [23, 24]. Of note, FABP5 controls lung tumor metastasis by virtue of its role in regulating maturation of natural killer cells in the lung [25]. Moreover, it has been documented that the reprogramming of FA metabolism in NSCLC contributes to immune escape by upregulating PD-L1 expression and impairing the activation of CD8+ T cells [26]. A parallel mechanism mediating immune suppression has been further shown in pancreatic cancer [27]. Supporting this therapeutic axis, studies in lung adenocarcinoma demonstrate that targeting lipid metabolic reprogramming can counteract immune escape by reducing PD-L1 and thereby mitigate resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy [28]. Despite these lines of evidence, the functional roles and underlying mechanisms of FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming in NSCLC remain unexplored.

RNA 5-methylcytosine (m5C) denotes a prevalent RNA modification in multiple RNA types, conserved among prokaryotes, archaea, and eukaryotes [29]. m5C modification can have a substantial influence on the tumor microenvironment by targeting immune cells, such as T cells, macrophages, B cells, and dendritic cells [30]. Due to these effects, m5C modification is a critical player in the pathogenesis of various tumors [31, 32]. Notably, NSUN2 is a critical RNA methyltransferase to initiate m5C modification [33]. A recent study suggests that NSUN2 induces m5C modification, improves the stability of FABP5 mRNA, and upregulates the expression of FABP5, thereby promoting FA metabolism and accelerating the progression of osteosarcoma [34]. NSUN2-mediated m5C methylation can facilitate the growth, glutamine metabolism, metastasis, and immune escape of NSCLC [35, 36]. The JASPAR database predicted the potential binding sites of runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) in the NSUN2 promoter region. RUNX1 is a member of the RUNX family of metazoan transcription factors implicated in several biological processes, including cancer metastasis, proliferation, angiogenesis, and drug resistance, demonstrating its potential utility as an effective biomarker for cancers, particularly for NSCLC [37, 38]. RUNX1 can facilitate the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype and promote angiogenesis in colorectal cancer, thus potentiating cancer cell invasion and migration [39]. In addition to that, RUNX1 overexpression enhances cell migratory and proliferative functions and influences downstream functional proteins by modulating the mTOR pathway activity [37]. These results collectively support the supposition that RUNX1 may promote FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming by regulating NSUN2 transcription to alter m5C methylation levels, which in turn enhances PD-L1 expression and leads to immune resistance in NSCLC. The objective of this study was to provide a novel mechanistic insight into understanding immune resistance and combination strategies for overcoming immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (KY2022-05) and performed in compliance with the Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and the guidelines for Animal Experimentation issued by the Chinese Association for Laboratory Animal Science. This study was reviewed and ratified by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical samples

A total of 90 patients with NSCLC who received PD-1 therapy at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital between August 2023 and August 2024 were selected. Among them, 32 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and ten were excluded as per the exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 48 NSCLC patients were included as the study subjects. According to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1, patients with complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or stable disease (SD) were classified into the Sensitive group, while those with progressive disease (PD) were assigned to the Resistant group [40]. Clinical data such as serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were attained from patients prior to anti-PD-1 therapy. Patient serum samples acquired before anti-PD-1 treatment were adopted to measure free fatty acid (FFA) levels employing an Indiko™ Plus clinical biochemistry analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). Tumor tissue samples obtained from patients before anti-PD-1 therapy were applied for reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and flow cytometry analyses.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of NSCLC with measurable solid tumor lesions via pathological examination; (2) expected survival time ≥ 3 months; (3) age ≥ 18 years; (4) receipt of anti-PD-1 therapy; (5) availability of complete clinical data.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) complication of other malignancies; (2) presence of sensitivity or contraindications to chemotherapeutic agents; (3) accompanied by infection or immune diseases; (4) pregnant or lactating patients.

Cell culture

Human lung adenocarcinoma cell line A549 (CCL-185) and human lung squamous cell carcinoma cell line NCI-H520 (HTB-182) were acquired from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The medium contained 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco). Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell grouping and treatment

NSCLC cells were subjected to the following treatments and grouped accordingly: (1) the Blank group: cultured under normal conditions; (2) the oeRUNX1 and oeNC groups: transfected with the RUNX1 overexpression (oeRUNX1) plasmid or its negative control (oeNC) plasmid; (3) the shRUNX1 and shNC groups: transfected with the short hairpin (sh)RUNX1 plasmid or the negative control shRNA plasmid (shNC); (4) the oeRUNX1 + Fatostatin and oeRUNX1 + Vehicle groups: transfected with oeRUNX1 and then treated with 5 µM Fatostatin (the lipid metabolism inhibitor, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide or an equal volume of the solvent vehicle for 48 h [41]; (5) the oeRUNX1 + shFABP5 and oeRUNX1 + shNC groups: co-transfected with oeRUNX1 and shFABP5 or shNC; (6) the oeRUNX1 + shNSUN2 group: co-transfected with oeRUNX1 and shNSUN2; (7) the shRUNX1 + FA and shRUNX1 + Vehicle groups: transfected with shRUNX1 and then reacted with 20 µM FA (MCE, dissolved in ethanol) or an equivalent volume of solvent for 48 h [42].

NSCLC cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates. Cells at 80% confluence were transfected with 2 µg of oeRUNX1, shRUNX1, shNSUN2, shFABP5, or their respective NC plasmids (all from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequent experiments were implemented 48 h post-transfection.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Harvested NSCLC cells were incubated with 25 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) for 2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured employing a Bio-Rad 680 microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The experiment was carried out in triplicate. Calculation of cell viability was achieved using the following formula: cell viability (%) = [(OD450 of experimental well -OD450 of blank well) / (OD450 of control well - OD450 of blank well)] × 100%.

Establishment of xenograft mouse models

The logarithmically growing NSCLC cells were harvested and resuspended in Hanks’ balanced salt solution to a concentration of 3 × 106 cells (200 µL). The suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right side of C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old; 18–22 g; acquired from Heilongjiang Ailemu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) to generate xenograft mouse models.

Animal grouping and treatment

Seventy-two 6-8-week-old C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6 per cell line): (1) the Model group: subcutaneously injected with 200 µL of NSCLC cells (A549/NCI-H520) to develop a xenograft mouse model; (2) the PD-1 mAb group: subcutaneously injected with 200 µL of NSCLC cells, followed by intraperitoneal administration of 150 µg PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) (MCE) one week later, repeated every three days [27]; (3) the Lv-oeNC + PD-1 mAb, Lv-oeRUNX1 + PD-1 mAb, Lv-shNC + PD-1 mAb, and Lv-shRUNX1 + PD-1 mAb groups: subcutaneously injected with 200 µL of NSCLC cells previously transfected with the NC overexpression lentiviral vector (Lv-oeNC), Lv-oeRUNX1, Lv-shNC, or Lv-shRUNX1, respectively. One week later, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 150 µg PD-1 mAb every three days. The Lv-oeRUNX1, Lv-shRUNX1 lentiviruses and their NCs (Lv-oeNC, Lv-shNC) were procured from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Twenty-one days later, blood was collected from the mouse heart to obtain serum. Mice were then euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg). Tumor tissues were harvested. The positive expression levels of RUNX1, NSUN2, FABP5, and PD-L1 in tumor tissues were determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC). The levels of CD8⁺ T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Determination of serum lipid levels

Differently treated NSCLC cells and mouse serum samples were harvested. Following the manufacturers’ guidelines, TC, TG, and FFA levels in both NSCLC cells and mouse serum were assessed using the TC Assay Kit (A111-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), TG Assay Kit (A110-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), and FFA Assay Kit (A042-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute).

IHC

Paraffin Section. (5 µm thickness) of tumor tissues were dewaxed, hydrated, and antigen retrieved in a microwave. Endogenous peroxidase inactivation was implemented with H2O2, and blocking was performed at ambient temperature for 1 h by means of 3% bovine serum albumin (Gibco). Afterwards, the sections were immunostained overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies to RUNX1 (1:500, ab92336, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), PD-L1 (1:250, ab213524, Abcam), and FABP5 (2 µg/mL, ab151772, Abcam). Following phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody IgG (1:100, ab6721, Abcam) at ambient temperature for 2 h. The final signals were developed by means of 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (ab64238, Abcam). The sections were analyzed by optical microscopy (Olympus Optical Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Image J software (acquired from National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) was used to determine the positive expression rate (%) of RUNX1 and NSUN2 in tumor tissues.

BODIPY 493/503 staining

NSCLC cells were plated in a 96-well plate. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were fixed by means of 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed by PBS. Subsequently, cells were stained by means of 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and BODIPY 493/503 lipid probe (M9850, Abmole Bioscience Inc., USA) for visualization of nucleus and lipids in cells, respectively. Intracellular lipid distribution was monitored by fluorescence microscopy (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Co-culture of NSCLC cells with CD8+ T cells

C57BL/6 mouse primary CD3+CD8+ T cells purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. CD8+ T cells were activated for 72 h using Dynabeads Mouse T-Activator CD3/CD28 (11452D, Gibco). The activated cells were then co-cultured with NSCLC cells subjected to different treatments in the Transwell co-culture system (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) for 72 h [43].

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues and CD8⁺ T cells from each group were harvested. As previously described [43], tissues were minced into small pieces, detached at 37 °C for 60 min using a solution containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.25 mg/mL), and filtered through a 70-µm sterile cell strainer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). After that, cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, resuspended in 100 µL PBS, and incubated with 1 µL of Fc receptor blocking solution (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) at ambient temperature for 10 min. Thereafter, cells were introduced with fluorescently labeled antibodies (1–2 µL per 106 cells): FITC-conjugated anti-CD3 plus phycoerythrin-conjugated anti-CD8 antibody (ab34266, Abcam), and allophycocyanin-conjugated anti-interferon-gamma antibody (17-7311-82, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) and gently mixed and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Following, cells were washed twice with PBS and then resuspended. CD8⁺ T cells (CD3⁺CD8⁺IFN-γ⁺) were identified and analyzed from the mouse tumor tissues using a BD FACSAria™ II cell sorter (BD Biosciences). The percentage of CD8⁺ T cells within tumor tissue cells was further analyzed using FlowJo 10.10 (BD Biosciences).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The cell culture supernatant was obtained after co-culture. The levels of CD8⁺ T cell effector cytokines, IFN-γ and granzyme B (GZMB), were evaluated separately using specific ELISA kits (IFN-γ: KMC4022, Invitrogen; GZMB: BMS6029, Invitrogen). All procedures were implemented strictly as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) staining

Harvested NSCLC cells underwent fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and later treatment with a proteinase K solution at 37 °C for 22 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Subsequently, they were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After another PBS wash, the buffer was applied dropwise and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Following the protocol of the TUNEL assay kit (T2196, Solarbio, Beijing, China), terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase, dUTP, and reaction buffer were mixed in a 1:5:50 ratio. This mixture was applied to the samples, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h and washed with PBS. Nuclei were counterstained by applying a DAPI solution dropwise and incubating for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, samples were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching sealer. Stained samples were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative analysis of the images was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from NSCLC cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine RNA concentration. Then 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit (Invitrogen). RT-qPCR was conducted using an ABI ViiA7DX System (Foster City, CA, USA). Fold changes were calculated by relative quantification (2-△△Ct method) and the results were normalized to GAPDH gene. The primers are shown in Table 1.

Western blot

NSCLC cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (P0013F, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride protease inhibitor for 30 min on ice. The cell extracts were then centrifuged at 13,000 g and 4 °C for 10 min, with the supernatant being harvested. After determining the protein concentration by means of a BCA Kit (PH0326, Phygene, Fujian, China), equal amounts of protein were separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, electrically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and blocked by means of 5% skim milk for 1 h. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against RUNX1 (1:5000, ab92336, Abcam), FABP5 (0.5 µg/mL, ab151772, Abcam), PD-L1 (1:1000, ab213480, Abcam), NSUN2 (1:1000, ab259941, Abcam) and GAPDH (1:2500, ab9485, Abcam). After washing, the membranes underwent incubation with secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, ab97051, Abcam) at ambient temperature for 1 h. Finally, the membranes were visualized by means of enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (XH057, Beijing Biolab, Beijing, China). Band intensities were quantified by means of Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, USA), normalized to GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was carried out to observe the nuclear translocation of RUNX1. Briefly, NSCLC cells were fixed by means of 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min, and blocked by means of 10% normal goat serum at ambient temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies to RUNX1 (ab229482, Abcam) and NSUN2 (ab259941, Abcam) were added and incubated with cells at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 488 (green fluorescence) IgG H&L secondary antibody (ab150077, Abcam) for 30 min at ambient temperature in the dark, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI (ab104139, Abcam). Finally, the nuclear translocation of RUNX1 was observed under fluorescence microscopy (Leica).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FISH was performed to assess the cellular co-localization of FABP5 mRNA and NSUN2 protein. Harvested NSCLC cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min, and blocked with 10% normal goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. FABP5 mRNA was labeled with orange-red fluorescence by Cyanine 3 probe (Invitrogen). The cellular co-localization of FABP5 mRNA and NSUN2 was then assessed by fluorescence microscopy.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Potential binding sites of RUNX1 in the NSUN2 promoter region were predicted by means of the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net/). The two binding sequences with the highest scores were selected and used to construct wild-type (WT1-NSUN2 and WT2-NSUN2) and mutant (MUT1-NSUN2 and MUT2-NSUN2) luciferase reporter plasmids [44] using the pMIR-REPORT Luciferase vector (Fenghui Biotech Co., Ltd., Hunan, China). These plasmids were co-transfected into NSCLC cells with 0.5 µg oe-RUNX1 and oe-NC (Fenghui Biotech). After culture for 48 h, the dual-luciferase assay kit (Yuanpinghao Biotechnology, Beijing, China) was used to detect the luciferase activity in cells.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assay was performed by means of the EZ-Magna ChIP Assay Kit (Merck Millipore; Burlington, MA, USA, 17-10086) using the previously described methods [45]. Briefly, 1 × 107 cells were cross-linked with 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS at ambient temperature for 10 min, with the unreacted paraformaldehyde quenched using 10× Glycine. Afterwards, samples were lysed by means of lysis buffer, and sonicated to shear chromatin into 200-1,000 bp fragments. Next, chromatin was immunoprecipitated with 5 µg of anti-RUNX1 (1:20, ab92336, Abcam) or rabbit IgG (2729, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology) antibodies. qRT-PCR was used to analyze the relative expression of NSUN2 mRNA. PCR primers for NSUN2 used in the ChIP assay are Forward: 5’-TCCCCAAGGGGGAAACAGTA-3’ and Reverse: 5’-TGTGTAGCGCTCCAAGATCG-3’.

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed using the Magna RIP Kit (Merck Millipore). In brief, cells were lysed by means of RIP lysis buffer, and the RNA-protein complexes were incubated with antibodies against NSUN2 (ab259941, Abcam) or IgG (ab6721, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. Next, the complexes were washed by means of RIP-wash buffer for 10 min at 4 °C and RIP-lysis buffer for 5 min at 4 °C. The co-precipitated RNAs were purified with phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol and subjected to a qRT-PCR analysis to determine FABP5 mRNA expression.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay

Sequences for FABP5 mRNA were downloaded from the NCBI database available at (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The m5C modification sites on FABP5 mRNA were predicted by the iRNA m5C database (http://lin-group.cn/server/iRNA-m5C/service.html) and RNAm5Cfinder database (http://www.rnanut.net/rnam5cfinder/). Total RNA was extracted from NSCLC cells, and m5C RNA was enriched after immunoprecipitation using the GenSeq m5C RNA methylation immunoprecipitation kit (GS-ET-003, Yunxu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Finally, m5C-enriched FABP5 mRNA expression was detected by means of qRT-PCR to evaluate the m5C modification level of FABP5.

mRNA stability analysis

Cells were treated by means of 5 µg/mL actinomycin D (MCE) to inhibit mRNA transcription, and then collected at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h to analyze FABP5 mRNA expression and its degradation rate. Total RNA was extracted for qRT-PCR detection.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significance between the two groups was analyzed by an independent sample t test. The comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test. Data analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). p < 0.05 denotes statistical significance.

Results

RUNX1 is highly expressed in immunotherapy-resistant NSCLC and correlates with serum lipid levels and CD8⁺ T cell activation in tumor tissues

Analysis of RUNX1 levels in patient tumor tissues by qRT-PCR revealed substantially higher expression in the Resistant group compared to the Sensitive group (p < 0.01, Fig. 1A). Flow cytometry assessment of CD8⁺ T cell activation within the tumor tissues showed markedly lower levels in the Resistant group versus the Sensitive group (p < 0.01, Fig. 1B). Furthermore, serum levels of TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and FFA were measured prior to anti-PD-1 therapy. The Resistant group exhibited higher serum levels of TC, HDL-C, and FFA than the Sensitive group (all p < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed in LDL-C and TG levels between the two groups (both p > 0.05, Fig. 1C-G). Subsequent Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated that RUNX1 expression in NSCLC patients positively correlated with serum TC levels (r = 0.6417, p < 0.0001) and FFA levels (r = 0.8128, p < 0.0001), and negatively with CD8⁺ T cell activation in tumor tissues (r = -0.6787, p < 0.0001). A statistically significant correlation was found between RUNX1 expression and serum HDL-C levels (r = 0.0927, p = 0.0354), yet it was relatively weak (Fig. 1H-K). The aforesaid results demonstrated that RUNX1 expression is elevated in immunotherapy-resistant NSCLC and is associated with serum lipid profiles and CD8⁺ T cell activation.

RUNX1 promoted PD-L1 expression and immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC by mediating lipid metabolic reprogramming via FABP5

NSCLC cells (A549 and NCI-H520) were cultured in vitro, and an NSCLC xenograft mouse model was established by subcutaneous injection of A549 and NCI-H520 cells, followed by anti-PD-1 treatment. It was found that anti-PD-1 treatment led to a reduction in both tumor weight and volume (all p < 0.01, Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1A) and an increase in the proportion of CD3⁺CD8⁺IFN-γ⁺ T cells within the tumor tissues (p < 0.01, Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 1B). Subsequently, A549 and NCI-H520 cells were treated with Lv-oeRUNX1 or Lv-shRUNX1 to overexpress or knock down RUNX1, respectively (both p < 0.01, Fig. 2C-D, Supplementary Fig. 1C-D), before being utilized to establish the NSCLC mouse model, which then received anti-PD-1 therapy. Relative to the Lv-oeNC + PD-1 mAb group, the Lv-oeRUNX1 + PD-1 mAb group displayed raised RUNX1 expression in tumor tissues (p < 0.01, Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 1E), greater tumor weight and volume (both p < 0.01, Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1A), elevated FABP5 and PD-L1 expression (both p < 0.01, Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 1E), lower proportion of CD3⁺CD8⁺IFN-γ⁺ T cells (p < 0.01, Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 1B), and higher serum TC, TG, and FFA levels (all p < 0.05, Fig. 2F-H, Supplementary Fig. 1F-H). Discussion

This study revealed that elevated RUNX1 expression in NSCLC is associated with resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. Mechanistically, RUNX1 promotes lipid metabolic reprogramming by transcriptionally upregulating NSUN2, which in turn enhances the stability of FABP5 mRNA via m5C modification. Consequently, this RUNX1/NSUN2/FABP5 axis increases lipid accumulation and PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, impairing CD8+ T cell function and fostering an immune-resistant tumor microenvironment.

Recent evidence has established RUNX1 as an oncogenic driver in NSCLC, where its upregulation exacerbates disease progression [37]. Our findings extend this role by linking RUNX1 overexpression to immunotherapy resistance. Patients with resistant tumors exhibited higher RUNX1 levels, which correlated positively with serum lipid levels (TC, FFA) and negatively with intratumoral CD8+ T cell activation. This aligns with the growing recognition that metabolic reprogramming, particularly lipid metabolism, is a pivotal regulator of antitumor immunity and immunotherapy response, primarily through its control over T cell differentiation, survival, and effector functions [46]. In this context, raised circulating FFA levels serve as precursors for both cancer cell biomass and oncogenic lipid signaling molecules [47]. Dysregulations of TC and TG have also been identified as two main blood lipid indicators and are intimately linked to cancer-associated lipid metabolism [48]. Functional experiments confirmed that reinforced RUNX1 expression in tumor cells augmented lipid uptake and synthesis (increased TC, TG, FFA), upregulated PD-L1, and suppressed CD8+ T cell activity in vitro and in vivo. Conversely, RUNX1 knockdown reversed these effects. These results position RUNX1 as a central coordinator linking lipid metabolism to immune escape in NSCLC. As validated by prior work, RUNX1 is amplified in NSCLC tissues and exacerbates the progression of NSCLC [37]. RUNX1 overexpression in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes leads to the transformation of adipocytes, enhancing the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells [49]. Conversely, knockdown of RUNX1 is associated with increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration and upregulated lipid metabolism genes [50]. These observations raise the possibility that RUNX1 may regulate lipid metabolic reprogramming and thus play a role in the resistance of NSCLC to immunotherapy.

To explore the downstream mechanisms underlying RUNX1-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming in NSCLC, this study next focused on FABP5 based on several considerations. FABP5 exerts oncogenic roles in NSCLC [23–25]. Additionally, FABP5 has shown a critical regulatory role as an upstream node in lipid metabolism [51]. Importantly, an FABP5-targeted nanocarrier system has been reported to enhance PD-L1 expression, while FABP5 deficiency promotes infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and effector memory T cells, leading to a pronounced systemic anti-tumor effect [52]. Activation of FABP5 suppresses the PPARα signaling pathway (which is implicated in FA oxidation) to drive monocytes to secrete substantial IL‑10. This reaction, in turn, activates the JNK–STAT3 pathway and upregulates PD-L1 on regulatory T cells, which binds to PD‑1 on the surface of T cells and effectively turns off the cytotoxic activity of T cells, thereby establishing a key mechanism (FABP5-IL-10-PD-L1+Tregs) for an immune-privileged microenvironment [53]. This highlights FABP5 not only as a regulatory hub of lipid metabolism but also as a key messenger linking lipid signals to immune suppression. Furthermore, a recent study has reported that NSUN2 enhances FABP5 mRNA stability through m5C methylation [34], and our in silico analyses showed multiple m5C modification sites within FABP5 mRNA, aligning with the mechanistic focus of our study. Notably, it has been reported that FABP5 is highly expressed in human NSCLC cells [54]. FABP5 plays a crucial role in shaping antitumor immunity. In tumor-associated macrophages, FABP5-mediated unsaturated FA metabolism is key to modulating T-cell responses and impacting immunotherapy efficacy [55]. Furthermore, in activated CD8+ T cells, FABP5 interacts with TAGLN2 to facilitate its cell surface function and localization [56]. Depletion of FABP5 in tumour-associated macrophages can enhance the anti-tumor immunity of T cells and inhibit the tumor growth of hepatocellular carcinoma [55]. In contrast, FABP5 inhibition decreases the expression of immunosuppressive molecules (PD-1 and PD-L1) in tumor-associated macrophages and enhances the efficacy of ICB therapy in lung cancer [43]. The present study data revealed that RUNX1 overexpression up-regulated FABP5 levels in NSCLC tissues. Innovatively, our study experimentally testified that RUNX1 promoted PD-L1 expression and immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC by affecting FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming. PD-L1 is often abundantly expressed in human cancers, and its binding to the receptor PD-1 on activated T cells demonstrates its ability to repress anti-tumor immunity through eliminating T cell-activating signals [57]. Consequently, antibody-based PD-L1 inhibitors can promote T cell-mediated tumor immunosurveillance and induce durable tumor remissions in cancer patients [58]. PD-1 is a well-known marker of T cell exhaustion, and its upregulation has been associated with T cell exhaustion [59, 60]. Besides, our study revealed that RUNX1 upregulated PD-L1 expression and promoted CD8+ T cell dysfunction via FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming. This adds a new layer of regulation, suggesting that metabolic interventions could modulate PD-L1 expression. Supporting this, the lipid metabolism inhibitor Fatostatin mimicked the effects of FABP5 knockdown, attenuating PD-L1 upregulation and restoring CD8+ T cell function against RUNX1-high tumor cells. Furthermore, FA supplementation was applied to lipid metabolic reprogramming, leading to the reversion of PD-L1 downregulation and CD8+ T cell dysfunction induced by RUNX1 knockdown. This highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting lipid metabolism to overcome RUNX1-driven immune resistance. Taken together, these findings establish that depleting FABP5 may become a promising strategy to improve immunotherapy efficiency for NSCLC.

Mechanistically, we unraveled how RUNX1 modulates lipid metabolic reprogramming through FABP5. Through the JASPAR database and dual-luciferase reporter assay, we found RUNX1 binding sites in the promoter region of NSUN2. This is the first evidence of a relationship between RUNX1 and NSUN2, which may be of significance to help understand the biology of NSCLC, and to identify novel therapeutic targets and strategies. Prior work has shown that NSUN2 is a major RNA methyltransferase responsible for induction of m5C modification, which is a widely distributed posttranscriptional modification in mRNA; in keeping with our findings, recent work has suggested that NSUN2 can improve the stability of FABP5 mRNA by enhancing the m5C modification of FABP5 mRNA and increase FABP5 expression, further promoting FA metabolism in osteosarcoma cells [34, 61, 62]. NSUN2 stabilizes FABP5 mRNA by stimulating m5C modification and further facilitates FA metabolism in osteosarcoma cells [34]. Meanwhile, evidence has highlighted the augmented expression of NSUN2 in NSCLC tissues, and its elevation has been closely associated with tumor grade and size, as well as poor outcomes for NSCLC patients [63]. One of the possible mechanisms underlying the tumor-promoting effect of NSUN2 can be attributed to its involvement in immune-related biological processes, such as the humoral immune response, T cell activation, and B cell activation [64, 65]. Specifically, NSUN2 activation maintains TREX2 expression via m5C RNA methylation and restricts cGAS/STING activation; this effect arrests apoptosis and CD8+ T cell infiltration, ultimately promoting tumorigenesis and resistance to anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy [66]. Consistently, we observed that RUNX1 regulated m5C modification and enhanced FABP5 mRNA stability by promoting the transcriptional expression of NSUN2. The functional importance of this axis was underscored by rescue experiments: knockdown of either FABP5 or NSUN2 partially reversed the pro-tumorigenic and immunosuppressive effects of RUNX1 overexpression. Additionally, downregulation of NSUN2 partially reverses the promotion of lipid metabolic reprogramming mediated by FABP5 and immune escape in NSCLC facilitated by RUNX1, supporting the significance of this axis in tumorigenesis and resistance to immunotherapy.

In summary, our work first reveals the mechanism that RUNX1 alters m⁵C methylation levels by regulating NSUN2 transcription to promote FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming, which in turn enhances PD-L1 expression and consequently induces immune resistance in NSCLC. This study has several limitations. First, clinical validation is absent in this study but solely present in A549 and NCI-H520 cells. Second, we are currently unable to provide key evidence directly demonstrating that PD‑L1 regulation is a direct transcriptional outcome of lipid metabolic reprogramming, such as the detection of FABP5 enrichment at the PD‑L1 promoter region. At present, our cross-sectional clinical data have only verified the correlations of elevated RUNX1 expression with serum lipid levels and immune resistance in NSCLC patients, while direct causal relationships have yet to be confirmed through longitudinal or interventional studies. Future research will therefore incorporate longitudinal cohorts to dynamically monitor the changes in RUNX1 expression, lipid levels, and CD8+ T cell activation throughout the course of immunotherapy. Also, attention may be paid to the impact of RUNX1-targeted interventions on the treatment outcomes. In future studies, we will also collect clinical NSCLC tumor tissues to isolate FABP5+/FABP- tumor-associated macrophages for validation experiments, further verify the mechanisms in multiple human lung cancer cell lines, and perform complete mechanistic validation in vivo.

Clinical translation challenges

FABP5 exhibits specifically high expression in tumor tissues but low expression in normal tissues, thereby providing inherent tissue selectivity for targeted intervention. The FABP4/5 dual-target inhibitor Y18, developed by the team of Professor Yingxia Li at Fudan University, demonstrates superior selectivity over Roche’s RO6806051 [67]. Moreover, FABP5 inhibitors act locally in psoriasis models without affecting systemic lipid metabolism [68, 69], suggesting a relatively low toxicity profile. In contrast, NSUN2 participates in essential physiological processes in normal tissues, and its systemic inhibition may result in significant adverse effects [70, 71]. To address this, a research team at Wenzhou Medical University developed ROS-responsive ferrocene nanoparticles (PRPFc@siNSUN2) to achieve targeted silencing of NSUN2. In gastric cancer models, these nanoparticles accumulate specifically at the tumor location and substantially suppress tumorigenesis, exhibiting low cytotoxicity and favorable biocompatibility [72]. Myofibroblast-specific knockdown of NSUN2 effectively alleviates myocardial fibrosis [73], suggesting that tissue-specific delivery may represent a critical strategy to mitigate the toxicity of NSUN2-targeted therapies. Beyond this, RUNX1 is a pivotal hematopoietic transcription factor [74] that plays an indispensable role in normal hematopoiesis; consequently, its systemic inhibition may cause severe adverse events such as myelosuppression [75, 76]. However, given the essential physiological functions in normal tissues, directly targeting RUNX1 and NSUN2 may lead to substantial off-target effects. By contrast, as a metabolic hub, FABP5 exhibits more pronounced tumor-specific expression and functional relevance. Thus, targeting FABP5 not only effectively disrupts the pro-tumorigenic effects of the RUNX1-NSUN2 axis but also minimizes interference with normal physiological processes, thereby markedly enhancing both the safety and translational potential of therapeutic strategies. In future studies, we will further optimize the selectivity and pharmacokinetic properties of FABP5 inhibitors, develop novel inhibitors with enhanced tumor tissue affinity, and explore the combination strategies with chemotherapy or immunotherapy to augment anti-tumor efficacy. We will also pursue the development of tissue-specific delivery and cell-type-specific intervention systems to specifically reduce NSUN2 expression, enabling more precise targeted treatment and minimizing cytotoxicity.
