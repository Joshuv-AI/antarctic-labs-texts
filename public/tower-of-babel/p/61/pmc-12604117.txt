The GLASS tool: a new lens for evaluating generalist leadership competencies for medical education
Authors: Youngjin Cho, Stefanie Carter, Charles Gullo, Kelly Quesnelle, John Szarek, Gabi N. Waite, Amy J. Prunuske
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent shifts in medical education have moved curricula away from siloed, discipline-based courses toward competency-focused, integrated models. Leading within a longitudinal, integrated curriculum requires a distinct set of leadership competencies. To address this need, medical education leaders across five institutions collaborated to develop a generalist leadership framework. Using a modified Delphi process, the team conducted literature reviews reviewing both published and gray literature and held monthly meetings over six months. This process resulted in the identification of core competencies, organized into three domains: Humility and Life-long Learning, Supporting Diverse Collaborations, and Holistic Stewardship of the Curriculum. A competency based self-assessment tool, Generalist Leadership Assessment for Skills and Strengths (GLASS) Tool, was then developed around this framework. The GLASS tool was piloted and validated in workshops at three national medical education conferences. Feedback from these sessions demonstrated strong support for the tool, with an average of 94% agreement on the relevance and clarity of the competencies. Participants used the tool to self-assess their leadership competencies, with higher self-ratings observed in the domain of humility and life-long learning over holistic stewardship of the curriculum. The tool was refined based on expert input clarifying the milestones and consolidating the competencies. The GLASS tool is designed to support reflective leadership development across four levels of educational engagement: individual, departmental, institutional, and national. It offers a transparent approach for faculty development, leadership readiness, and strategic planning in evolving medical education environments.

Introduction

The landscape of curricula in United States medical schools has dramatically changed over the past two decades, characterized by a significant shift towards active learning pedagogies and the integration of previously siloed, science-based courses into an integrated foundational science curriculum oriented toward patient outcomes. This transformation places new demands on undergraduate medical education (UME) leaders, who often possess deep disciplinary expertise cultivated through research training but limited educational leadership training. These faculty are increasingly tasked with the administration of integrated courses and supporting vertical and horizontal integration of content [1].

While substantial professional development efforts have focused on equipping educators with active learning strategies, a critical gap exists in supporting the development of administrative competencies necessary to effectively lead integrated courses and threads. Traditional professional development frequently neglects essential skill sets for managing diverse student and faculty populations, recognizing and mitigating bias, fostering professionalism, and preventing burnout among both learners and educators [2]. Developing these skills is paramount for the advancement of educators now entrusted with significant administrative responsibilities. Existing models for educator advancement, such as those emphasizing educational administration, leadership, and management [3,4], and expanded definitions of medical teachers as curriculum developers and leaders [5] underscore this need.

Formative milestones, like those from the ACGME (Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education [6], are intended to drive continuous quality improvement for educators and programs. While frameworks such as the Clinician Educator Milestones [7] capture important teaching skills and attitudes, they do not fully address the leadership skills required for navigating the rapidly evolving medical education landscape, particularly in managing faculty with varied content expertise and assessing patient-oriented outcomes. Building upon our prior work [8], we posit that leaders in longitudinal integrated curricula benefit from a generalist competency framework encompassing leading with humility, facilitating collaboration in complex disciplinary environments, and fostering a broad, holistic vision of curriculum. Such a framework, grounded in constructivist learning theories [9], would facilitate the integration of new, generalist knowledge with existing scientific expertise, ultimately enhancing the relatedness of concepts and their application to patient outcomes. To further develop this framework, a consensus-building process is essential to establish a shared model of relevant competencies and milestones within the medical educator community.

To this end, we employed a modified Delphi technique, a structured method for achieving expert consensus through sequential, anonymous feedback [10]. This approach was chosen to effectively address the complexities of defining leadership in longitudinal integrated curricula. First, the Delphi process leverages the collective wisdom of diverse experts across institutions, offering a broader perspective. Second, its iterative nature allows for continuous refinement based on expert input, ensuring validity through consensus rather than predetermination [11]. Third, the systematic collection of quantitative agreement and qualitative feedback provides a robust method for identifying essential competencies. This methodology aligns with established approaches used in developing other educational competency frameworks [12,13] and was utilized to build the Generalist Leadership Assessment for Skills and Strengths (GLASS) Tool.

Materials and methods

Tool development

The authors identified significance statements as to the relevancy of the work and critically analyzed the literature to create a generalist framework identifying relevant attributes, leadership skills, and management skills [8]. The group converted the generalist framework into a self-assessment tool and identified associated developmental milestones. The generalist leadership competency self-assessment tool utilized a 6-point Likert-type scale with clearly defined behavioral anchors: 0 (No experience), 1 (Novice), 2 (Beginner), 3 (Competent), 4 (Proficient), and 5 (Expert). The initial framework contained 12 competencies organized under three principal domains: (1) Humility and Life-Long Learning, (2) Supporting Diverse Collaboration, and (3) Holistic Stewardship of Curriculum. For each competency, detailed behavioral descriptions were provided for the associated milestones to guide participants in accurately assessing their abilities.

Expert validation

From March–June 2024, team members conducted workshops at 3 medical education conferences: Central Group on Education Affairs, Northeast Group on Education Affairs, and the International Association of Medical Science Educators with the goal of having 30−50 additional experts validate the tool using a modified Delphi process. The objectives of the workshops included: 1) Identify generalist roles and responsibilities needed to lead longitudinal, integrated curriculum; 2) Refine a set of proposed generalist medical education leader competencies that could be used to develop generalist leaders at participants' home institutions; 3) Collaborate across institutions to identify strategies that can be used to facilitate a generalist approach toward longitudinal, integrated curriculum among faculty; and 4) Recognize opportunities to implement approaches to build institutional leadership and capacity for change toward a generalist mindset.

At the end of each workshop, participants were invited to complete an anonymous RedCap survey regarding the GLASS tool. On the voluntary agreement survey, participants were asked to provide basic demographic information to ensure that all participants held roles in medical education, and they were asked to respond whether they ‘Agree’ or ‘Disagree’ with each of the competencies. Free text boxes were included to share feedback about the competencies and to indicate if there were any missing competencies. Workshop participants completed the GLASS Tool on paper. Participants were encouraged to take a photo of their responses and then return the paper copy, which contained no identifying information, to the workshop facilitators.

During the workshop, participants discussed the tool and the facilitators took notes on the participants' feedback. A revised version of the tool was generated based on common themes identified in the feedback to improve its usability and clarity. The research protocol was deemed exempt from human research by the IRB of the University of South Carolina.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to determine the level of agreement for each domain and competency. For each competency, the percentage of participants who selected ‘Agree’ was calculated. Domain-level agreement rates were determined by averaging the agreement percentages of all competencies within that domain. As part of the Delphi process, the group set a goal to achieve at least 75% consensus [14].

To examine patterns of disagreement, co-occurrence analysis was conducted using Excel to identify competencies that were frequently disagreed with by the same participants. This was done by calculating the frequency with which pairs of competencies were simultaneously disagreed with across all participants. Once a competency pair with simultaneous disagreement was identified, a 2-by-2 contingency table was constructed and a Chi-Square test was performed to calculate statistical significance.

To examine relationships between competencies, Pearson correlation coefficients (rho) were calculated for all possible pairs of competencies and statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed T-distribution test in Excel. Only paired observations with complete data for both variables were included in correlation calculations. Correlation strengths were ranked from strongest to weakest with correlations close to or exceeding 0.60 indicating strong relationships between competency domains. For all analyses, missing data were handled using pairwise deletion to maximize available information.

The mean and standard deviation of each competency were calculated. Several participants self-rated themselves between integer values (e.g., 3.5), indicating they perceived their competency level to be between two anchored descriptions (e.g., between ‘Competent = 3’ and ‘Proficient = 4’). These non-integer ratings were included in the analysis as reported. Missing data points were excluded. Domain-level scores were calculated as the average of completed competency ratings within that domain.

Results

Literature search and expert consolidation

The goal of the authors was to develop the Generalist Leadership Assessment for Skills and Strengths (GLASS) tool for leaders in longitudinal, integrated curriculum (Appendix). The literature search yielded the identification of the generalist framework including three core domains, Humility and Life-long learning, Supporting Diverse Collaborations, and Holistic Stewardship of Curriculum, and was described previously [8].

Modified Delphi process to develop GLASS tool.

The multi-institutional group then partnered to identify how the generalist framework could be used to encourage self-reflection and professional development training. Over several months, the group identified areas of agreement and disagreement for the relevant competencies and milestones for each of the three domains. The feedback was iteratively categorized and refined to create an initial draft of the GLASS tool to facilitate self-assessment and to gather additional expert feedback.

Domain I: Humility and Life-long Learning emphasizes that knowledge is alive and in flux requiring individuals to develop the following competencies: continuous learning across fields, credibility through an evidence-based mindset, and humble leadership. The milestones for continuous learning across fields break down learning processes similarly to the Master Adaptive Learner framework [15] including goal setting, gathering new ideas, applying, and refining mental models based on lived experiences. The competency cultivating credibility through an evidence-based approach is needed to ensure rigor and generalizability across contexts. Finally, humble leadership, which is closely linked to servant leadership, necessitates recognizing one's own shortcomings and proactively providing support to others [16].

Domain II: Supporting Diverse Collaborations recognizes the importance of interpersonal relationships to support active and inclusive engagement of individuals from various backgrounds, professions, and perspectives. The competencies within this domain include communication across disciplines and professions, conflict resolution, fostering collaboration, and holistic feedback of performance. The milestones incorporate building trust, creating shared ownership and understanding, and empowering others to reflect on their biases and adjust their behaviors.

Domain III: Holistic Stewardship of the Curriculum recognizes that curriculum must be implemented within a complex system. The big picture visioning competency is necessary to inspire others and facilitate change [17]. Integration of curriculum across the continuum requires leaders to understand global competencies and how topics are interrelated. Designing effective assessments and evaluating faculty are necessary for quality improvement and programmatic assessment. Finally, leaders should understand how topics are relevant to comprehensive knowledge of social determinants of health in health care landscape.

Modified Delphi process

To validate the GLASS tool, workshop participants at three medical education conferences were asked to self-assess and provide feedback on the tool. Workshop participants represented a diverse array of educational backgrounds, experience levels, and leadership roles within medical education. The majority of participants held doctoral degrees, with 20 (58.8%) having PhD degrees and 9 (26.5%) having MD degrees. One participant (2.9%) held a Doctor of Education degree, and 4 (11.8%) reported other qualifications including Masters in Science Education, Masters of Public Policy and Management, Education Masters, and Masters of Medicine (Table 1). The sample predominantly consisted of experienced educators, with half of participants (17, 50.0%) reporting 11−20 years of involvement in medical education, followed by more than a quarter over 1/4th (9, 26.5%) having had 6−10 years of experience (Table 2).

The diverse backgrounds and leadership experiences of participants provided a rich expert group to evaluate the validity and utility of the tool. While institutional affiliation was not collected, the broad geographical distribution of attendees at these conferences likely contributed to institutional diversity within our sample. We met our goal of getting feedback on the tool from at least 30 experts and the heterogeneity in the group supports the generalizability of the results. The predominance of individuals with substantial experience in medical education and in active leadership positions suggests the participants were well-positioned to offer informed perspectives on the relevance of the proposed competencies in leading integrated contemporary curricula.

The analysis of the agreement survey by 34 participants revealed a high overall agreement rate of 94.1% across all competencies (Table 4). Nearly two-thirds of participants (64.7%) agreed with all 12 of the initial competencies, indicating substantial consensus regarding the proposed competency framework. Three competencies achieved unanimous agreement (100%): ‘Communicate effectively across disciplines and professions,’ ‘Foster a collaborative environment,’ and ‘Integration across curriculum.’ Four additional competencies demonstrated near-universal agreement (97.1%): ‘Continuous learning,’ ‘Lead with humility,’ ‘Conflict resolution,’ and ‘Big picture visioning.’ The remaining five competencies showed slightly lower, though still substantial, agreement rates ranging from 85.3% to 91.2%. The lowest agreement rates were observed for ‘Evaluate teaching faculty’ and ‘Comprehensive knowledge of the healthcare landscape’ (both at 85.29%), followed by ‘Design effective assessment strategy’ (88.2%). Given the high level of agreement and that all of the competencies reach the 75% threshold we decided to not engage in additional rounds of voting.

Analysis of disagreement patterns revealed that these three competencies frequently co-occurred in participants' responses, with three participants simultaneously disagreeing with both ‘Comprehensive knowledge of the healthcare landscape’ and either ‘Design effective assessment strategy’ or ‘Evaluate teaching faculty.’ While it is observed with only three participants, co-occurrence was statistically significant (Chi-Square, p < 0.05). These significant results suggest that there might be underlying conceptual or structural reasons why participants who disagreed with one of these competencies also tended to disagree with the others, possibly indicating that these competencies may be viewed as less essential or more specialized than others in the framework.

Self-assessment results

Analysis of cross-sectional self-assessment data from 33 of the 34 participants revealed distinct patterns in self-perceived competency levels across the three principal domains and their associated competencies (Figure 2). Individual differences in self-ratings were substantial, with overall mean scores ranging from 4.50 (participant p13) to 1.67 (participant p32). The top five highest self-rated participants consistently scored above 3.75 across domains, typically rating themselves at the ‘Proficient’ level, while the five lowest self-rated participants averaged below 2.25, generally placing themselves at the ‘Novice’ to ‘Beginner’ levels across most competencies.

Among the 12 competencies, participants rated themselves highest on ‘Continuous learning’ (M = 3.73, SD = 1.10) and ‘Lead with humility’ (M = 3.52, SD = 0.93), both from Domain 1. This suggests participants felt most confident in their abilities to seek innovative ideas, apply new knowledge in different settings, and recognize limitations while acknowledging others' contributions. The lowest self-ratings were observed for ‘Lead an evidence-based evaluation of teaching faculty’ (M = 2.32, SD = 1.51) and ‘Design effective assessment strategy’ (M = 2.45, SD = 1.13), both from Domain III. These areas focus on specific tasks around faculty evaluation and assessment, skills perceived as more specialized than broadly required for faculty.

The variability in ratings was particularly pronounced for competencies in Domain III, with standard deviations exceeding 1.50 for ‘Comprehensive knowledge of social determinants of health in health care landscape’ and ‘Lead an evidence-based evaluation of teaching faculty,’ suggesting considerable heterogeneity in participants' self-perceived abilities in these curriculum stewardship areas.

Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between competencies in Domain III, particularly between ‘Big-picture visioning’ and ‘Integration across continuum’ (r = 0.62, p < 0.001), and ‘Big picture visioning’ and ‘Designing effective assessment’ (r = 0.55, p < 0.001). These correlations between competencies in Domain III indicate that they measure related constructs and that competencies are appropriately grouped together. However, we also saw moderate but significant cross-domain correlation, such as ‘Holistic feedback’ (Domain II) correlations with ‘Big picture visioning’ (Domain III) (r = 0.58, p < 0.001), and ‘Continuous learning’ (Domain I) and ‘Foster a collaborative environment’ (Domain II), (r = 0.57, p < 0.001) suggests that certain competencies, despite being in different domains, draw on related skills around big picture and learning from others.

Feedback integration

Feedback from participants provided suggestions for tool refinement. One change implemented was the consistent use of nouns to describe the competencies and verbs to articulate the behavioral milestones. For example, ‘Communicate effectively across disciplines and professions’ became ‘Communication across disciplines and professions.’ An example of a change that was made to the milestones based on the participants feedback was replacing the milestone 5 verb 'coach' with 'mentor' for several competencies, as mentoring was viewed as a key element of holistic development. Additional feedback was incorporated into each of the domains and the final tool can be found in the Appendix.

The Humility and Life-long Learning domain had the highest agreement and minimal changes were made to this domain. The discussion supported that reflection in this area needs to occur in the individual leader, but at the highest level of the milestones, leaders need to mentor compassion in others and be attentive to their wellbeing.

The participants agreed that being in an educational leadership role often requires skills linked to Domain II, collaborating with individuals from diverse backgrounds, professions, and perspectives. One competency that was suggested to be added to Domain II by participants was delegation and we made this more explicit under the communication milestone 3 to say, ‘Delegate activities recognizing the value of diverse contributions of expertise and resources.’

Under Domain III Holistic Review of the Curriculum, we changed milestone 1 of big picture visioning from creating a vision to explain curricular vision including overall curricular objectives since it was pointed out that many education leaders inherit a vision. Based on the feedback and rating, we decided to combine competencies around assessment and evaluation into an overall competency of continuous quality improvement bringing down our total number of competencies to 11. Assessment requires one to be able to gather information about the curriculum from multiple sources and evaluation involves making judgements based on that information, which occurs at higher levels of expertise, and this is now reflected in the milestones.

Based on feedback regarding the competency, ‘Comprehensive knowledge of social determinants of health in health care landscape,’ we revised the competency in the final tool to ‘Community health engagement.’ This competency requires medical education leaders to consider local health needs and how these can best be incorporated into the curriculum through the inclusion of local patient perspectives. It also includes principles of collective impact [18] which focuses on principles that support successful community partnership like having bidirectional communication, structural support, and shared measurable outcomes. Feedback from workshop experts led to a stronger overall tool and fostered the sharing of strategies for milestone acquisition.

Discussion

This innovation study presents the development and expert validation of the GLASS tool, specifically designed for UME faculty leading longitudinal, integrated medical curricula. As the academic medicine landscape evolves, this framework can be used to understand how the medical educator role is changing, and it provides a ready-to-use resource for institutions to apply across multiple areas of the faculty career progression. Using a modified Delphi approach, a panel of faculty experts from multiple institutions collaboratively designed the framework, which was iteratively refined through surveys and self-assessment activities conducted at three national medical education conferences. At these conferences, the tool received strong endorsement, with an average agreement exceeding 94% on the validity of all components. Additionally, self-assessment results revealed meaningful variation across competencies, underscoring the framework's value in identifying individual strengths and areas for targeted growth. These findings support the framework's utility as a formative mechanism for guiding faculty development at both individual and institutional levels across diverse educational settings, offering insights into previously overlooked areas of faculty growth.

While other competency-based frameworks have guided educator development, they often overlook the complex leadership demands faced by faculty in integrated curricula. Existing tools rarely address the systems-level thinking, cross-disciplinary integration, and faculty support strategies essential for leading longitudinal, interdisciplinary courses [1,5]. The GLASS tool fills this gap by emphasizing generalist leadership competencies such as ‘Big Picture Visioning’ and ‘Integration Across the Curriculum,’ alongside relational and servant leadership principles like ‘Fostering Collaboration’ and ‘Leading with Humility.’ Such novel competencies go beyond traditional management tasks, focusing instead on collective impact and systems thinking skills increasingly vital in modern medical education. Workshop participants emphasized another growing hallmark of effective educational leadership: evaluating educational success through its impact on the community. This perspective is now reflected in the revised competency, ‘Community health engagement,’ which highlights the importance of community-responsive stewardship. These examples demonstrate that the GLASS tool complements and significantly expands upon existing frameworks, offering a more comprehensive approach to faculty identity in modern medical education.

The GLASS tool offers versatile applications across two key domains: faculty development and program evaluation. From a faculty development perspective, it serves as a formative self-assessment mechanism, supported by behavioral anchors that facilitate reflective practice. Its adaptability across the 4 M framework [19] (micro to mega levels of educational development) enables its use in self-directed learning, departmental coaching, institutional workshops, and inter-institutional faculty development programs (Table 5). This approach highlights areas of faculty growth that are otherwise often overlooked. It can also be integrated into annual review cycles, promotion dossiers, and onboarding processes for educators involved in integrated course delivery.

Beyond individual development, the GLASS tool can support program evaluation, recruitment, and retention efforts. Institutions may use it to assess leadership potential in new hires and monitor faculty engagement and development. Its structured format lends itself to diagnosing departmental culture and collaboration. Future applications could include mapping tool usage to changes in learner experiences, team dynamics, or curricular coherence, thereby offering a data-informed approach to continuous improvement in medical education.

Several limitations merit consideration. Although the participant pool included individuals with diverse experiences and institutional roles, its modest size may limit generalizability across geographic regions and institutional types. Feedback was primarily self-reported, introducing potential bias, and the brevity of the survey may have constrained deeper reflection or contextual nuance. To help mitigate the self-assessment bias, users could triangulate their data incorporating additional data sources. Additionally, interpretations of certain items may vary depending on the respondent's role (e.g., course director vs. department chair), highlighting the challenge of creating a universally applicable tool. We recommend that, similar to other competency frameworks, the competencies within GLASS may need to be reviewed and updated periodically (e.g., every 5−10 years) considering the dynamic nature of medical education [20].

Future directions for the GLASS tool include evaluating its inter-rater reliability, assessing its predictive validity in faculty development outcomes, and exploring its utility for longitudinal tracking of leadership growth and how this may vary across groups. Expanding its application to other health professions, such as nursing or interprofessional education could enhance its generalizability and relevance across disciplines. Additionally, comparative studies examining self-assessment versus peer or supervisor assessments would provide further evidence of the tool's accuracy and practical value in diverse educational settings.

In conclusion, this study presents a novel competency framework designed to meet the generalist leadership needs of faculty in longitudinal integrated curricula. By emphasizing relational, strategic, integrative, and community-responsive skills, the GLASS tool addresses critical gaps in existing educator development resources. With continued refinement and broader adoption, it holds significant potential to support individual faculty growth, inform institutional planning, and elevate educational excellence across diverse academic settings.
