Patellar Osteochondral Allograft Transplantation Yields Clinically Meaningful Improvement in 2-Year Patient-Reported Outcomes
Authors: Nathaniel M Tchangou, Christopher A Schneble, Randal Timothy Kreulen, Andres Perez, Rioke M Diejomaoh, Fotios P Tjoumakaris, Kevin B Freedman, Bradford S Tucker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess the outcomes of patellar osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation and access the effect of preoperative indications and patient characteristics on outcomes.MethodsPrimary patellar OCA transplantation cases were retrospectively identified from a single surgeon from August 2017 and August 2021, all using grafts preserved with the Missouri Osteochondral Allograft Preservation technique. Patients with less than 2 years of follow-up were excluded. Variables collected included demographics, previous surgical and concomitant procedures, primary pathology and indications, graft size and location, range of motion, Magnetic Resonance Observation of Cartilage Repair Tissue 2.0 scores, patient-reported outcome measures, and reoperations. Primary pathology and unipolar versus bipolar grafting subgroups were analyzed. Preoperative and postoperative comparison was analyzed via paired sample t tests. Subgroups were compared using independent t tests for nonpaired continuous variables, with χ2 and Fisher exact tests used for categorical data. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.ResultsTwenty-eight cases (16 unipolar patellar OCA transplantation, 12 bipolar transplantation) met both inclusion and exclusion criteria, with a mean age of 31.0 ± 10.3 years, a mean body mass index of 30.0 ± 5.6, and a mean follow-up of 3.6 ± 1.4 years (range 2.3-5.8 years). Eighteen knees presented with lateral patellar instability, 9 with patellofemoral (PF) chondrosis, and 1 with juvenile osteochondritis dissecans. Three knees (10.7%) in total were converted to total knee arthroplasty, all of which occurred in the setting of PF chondrosis. OCA transplantation improved International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC; 36.6-71.9; P < .001), Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (49.8-77.5; P = .001), Short-Form 12 Physical Health Survey (33.5-45.3; P = .002), and Veterans RAND 12 Physical Health Survey (34.5-49.6; P < .001) scores at 2-years. The IKDC, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement, Short-Form 12 Physical Health Survey, and Veterans RAND 12 Physical Health Survey thresholds (0-100) and percentages for achieving minimal clinically important difference were 18.75 (70.6%), 18.76 (73.5%), 11.90 (73.5%), and 13.56 (61.8%), respectively. Age and body mass index had strong negative correlations with IKDC scores in knees with PF chondrosis (r = −0.903; P < .01 and r = −0.875; P = .010 respectively). Bipolar transplantation for “kissing” lesions had larger improvements in IKDC scores than unipolar cases (+54.6 vs +24.1; P = .007).ConclusionsPatellar OCA transplantation resulted in overall improvement in PROs with an 11% rate of conversion to total knee arthroplasty at 2-years follow-up.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, therapeutic retrospective case series.

Osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation is a joint-preserving procedure that restores damaged articular cartilage and subchondral bone using a composite graft of fresh articular cartilage and underlying bone from a cadaver transplant. OCA can treat most cartilage defects from a variety of different causes and shows good patient satisfaction and clinical outcomes, with long-term survival rates from 77.2% to 91%.1, 2, 3, 4

Because of its ability to treat a large defect area along with any subchondral bone changes, OCA is often particularly useful for large or uncontained defects, or salvage situations.5 Within the patellofemoral compartment, the results of OCA thus far have been promising; however, data throughout the literature involve patients with a mixed collection of surgical procedures, deformities, defect locations, and many other features.6, 7, 8 There are many factors that vary among patients that plausibly impact joint kinematics and healing potential, ultimately impacting patient outcomes. Potential factors include concomitant surgical procedures, patellar alignment, patellar tracking, and cartilage defect size.9, 10, 11, 12, 13

There is evidence to suggest that allograft preservation technique might influence results, with reports suggesting the use of the Missouri Osteochondral Preservation System (MOPS) could lead to improved outcomes.14,15 The MOPS technique is unique in that it affords a prolonged period of essential chondrocyte viability levels during allograft storage by preservation in a nutrient-rich, room-temperature (∼25 solution °C), exhibiting viability to at least 56 days compared with the viability limit of 28 days observed with standard preservation techniques.16 Because of the impact prolonged storage time may have on graft availability, a more detailed understanding of outcomes after OCA transplants using the MOPS technique is of particular interest. Unfortunately, the heterogeneity often present among patellofemoral cartilage defect populations has made it difficult to control confounding variables to truly assess the influence of preservation technique on outcomes.

The purpose of the current study was to assess the outcomes of patellar OCA transplantation and access the effect of preoperative indications and patient characteristics on outcomes. The authors hypothesized that OCA in the patellofemoral joint would result in satisfactory clinical and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).

Methods

Study Design and Participants

For the time period of August 2017 to August 2021, patients between 14 and 50 years of age were identified via Current Procedural Terminology codes specific to OCA procedures (code 27415) and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes for chondromalacia patellae (M22.41 and M22.42), then filtered for a single, high-volume patellofemoral practice surgeon’s cases. All allografts were preserved with the MOPS technique.17 Patients with concurrent OCA transplantation (trochlea or tibiofemoral compartments) were included. Patients with concomitant tibiofemoral disease also were included in the study and were treated at the time of surgery to eliminate any source of pain. However, the concomitant tibiofemoral disease was not considered the primary issue for these patients. Exclusion criteria consisted of open physes, previous OCA transplantation, or less than 2 years of follow-up. Any postoperative PROs acquired sooner than 2 years from the date of the OCA transplantation surgery were excluded. The indications for osteochondral allograft to the patella were patients with grade 3 to 4 articular cartilage injuries to the patella of at least 2.25 square centimeters in diameter. Patients with or without bone defects were included. All patients failed at least 6 months of nonoperative treatment. Retrospective in design, this study received institutional review board approval (Thomas Jefferson University; study #21E.989) approval.

Demographic and Baseline Variables

Demographic variables were collected from the electronic medical record along with previous ipsilateral surgical procedures. The primary pathology, i.e., the diagnosis that contributed to the cartilage defect, was also collected and categorized as follows: lateral patellar instability (LPI), as defined by lateral dislocations and/or subluxations, juvenile osteochondritis dissecans (OCD), as defined by an atraumatic unstable osteochondral fragment, or patellofemoral (PF) chondrosis, defined by nontraumatic PF chondral defects in the absence of reported subluxations or dislocations. Preoperative cartilage assessments included Outerbridge Scores as determined at the time of surgery and the intraoperative dimensions of cartilage defects. Donor allograft characteristics also were recorded, including donor age, graft storage time, graft diameter, and graft area.

Surgical Procedure

Operative notes, datasheets, and images were all reviewed to confirm procedure details. All patients underwent diagnostic arthroscopy to confirm the severity and size of the cartilage defect before the ordering of a size-matched allograft. The cartilage status of all knee compartments was assessed routinely using a marked probe to measure lesions. Indications for proceeding with the second stage was the presence of focal cartilage defects >2 cm2 in active individuals who lacked advanced osteoarthritis.

OCA transplantation was performed with any additional indicated procedures such as tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) or medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFLR), often through a medial parapatellar arthrotomy except in instances in which an isolated lateral retinacular lengthening was performed with the OCA, for which a lateral parapatellar arthrotomy was used. Cartilage defects were measured (Fig 1) then removed en bloc with the subchondral bone using the hollow core reamer. The desired depth of bone to be removed is approximately 4 mm, with strict attention to ensure no less than 4 mm of bone was present at any point along the perimeter of the plug. The allograft plug was then harvested from the donor and shaped to match the depth of the first plug. The bony undersurface of the allograft plug was perforated with a 2.0-mm drill bit without violating the subchondral bone (Fig 2). The allograft plug was then aligned in the patient’s harvest site and impacted in place (Fig 3).

The postoperative protocol for isolated OCA of the PF joint (patella and or trochlea) included protected weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches in a hinged knee brace locked in extension for 6 weeks. Full range of motion (ROM) was encouraged immediately at postoperative day 1. If a TTO was performed, the patient was non−weight-bearing for the first 6 weeks. If medial femoral condyle or lateral femoral condyle OCA was performed, the patient was toe-touch weight-bearing over the first 6 weeks. At the 6-week time point, all patients were permitted to weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches and instructed to wean off the crutches over the next 6 weeks. There were no ROM restrictions imparted at any point. In all patients, impact exercises and activities were permitted after 6 months.

Imaging Measures

Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, when available, were reviewed by 2 blinded raters (C.A.S. and R.T.K.) to determine Magnetic Resonance Observation of Cartilage Repair Tissue (MOCART) 2.0 scores for patellar lesions in accordance with previously established methods.18 MRIs were not performed in response to the patient’s symptoms but were routinely performed from 9 months to 1 year postoperatively to evaluate graft incorporation for research purposes. Tibial tubercle-trochlear groove distances were measured on all patients using preoperative MRIs, when available.

PRO Measures

PRO measures were collected using the Outcomes Based Electronic Research Database software (Universal Research Solutions; www.oberd.com). PROs assessed included International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Knee Form (IKDC), Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (KOOS JR), Short-Form 12 Physical Health Survey (SF-12 PH), and Veterans RAND 12 Physical Health Survey (VR-12 Physical). The IKDC, KOOS JR, SF-12 PH, and VR-12 Physical thresholds for achieving MCID was defined as an increase of 18.75, 18.76, 11.90, and 13.56, respectively (0-100).

Data Analysis

When appropriate, paired sample t tests were used to assess for changes in preoperative and postoperative PRO scores. To assess the normality of the distribution, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. Independent t tests were used to assess differences amongst nonpaired continuous variables. Cohen d was calculated to determine effect sizes along with 95% confidence intervals. χ2 and Fisher exact tests were used to analyze categorical data. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) was used for correlations. Subanalyses were conducted for primary pathology and bipolar osteochondral allografting (kissing lesions). For all statistical testing, P values <.05 were deemed to be significant. Analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29.0.1.0.

Results

Study Population Descriptive Data

In total, 37 knees among 34 different patients were identified. Nine knees were excluded for incomplete records or inadequate follow-up, leaving 28 knees in 26 patients. Twenty-two knees belonged to female patients (78.6% of all knees). The mean follow-up was 3.6 ± 1.4 years (range 2.3-5.8 years) after OCA implantation. The mean age was 31.0 ± 10.3 years and the mean BMI was 30.0 ± 5.6. Eighteen knees (64.3%) presented with lateral patellar instability, 9 (32.1%) with PF chondrosis, and 1 (3.4%) with patellar OCD. Six (21.4%) patients had concomitant tibiofemoral disease.

Baseline Cartilage Defects

All knees had patellar lesions with Outerbridge Scores of either 3 (n = 5) or 4 (n = 23) at the time of the staged diagnostic arthroscopy, and 19 (67.9%) had 1 or more additional femoral cartilage defects with an Outerbridge score of 3 or greater. Patellar and femoral defects with Outerbridge scores of 3 or greater are mapped in Figure 4 A and B. The area of patellar chondral defects was 4.5 ± 1.9 cm2 (range 2.3-7.5 cm2). Twelve knees (42.9%) had a bipolar “kissing” defects within the trochlea, with the average trochlear defect area in these patients being 4.1 ± 3.7 cm2 (range 1.5-15 cm2).

Allograft Characteristics

All allografts (100%) were preserved using the MOPS technique. The average graft storage time was 51.2 ± 5.6 days, with an average donor age of 24.3 ± 5.3 years. The mean diameter of the allografts harvested for patellar implantation was 21.3 ± 4.3 mm.

Previous and Concurrent Procedures

A total of 18 (64.3%) knees underwent previous surgeries distinct from staging arthroscopy. No knees had previous osteochondral allografting, autologous chondrocyte implantation, matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation, or particulated juvenile articular cartilage transplantation. However, 4 (14.3%) had received previous microfracture of the patella. For the index study procedure, 16 (57.1%) knees underwent unipolar patellar OCA transplantation, whereas 12 (42.9%) bipolar transplantations for “kissing” lesions. Eleven knees underwent a TTO (concomitant or previous), comprising 61.1% (11/18) of the knees with LPI knees. No knees with PF chondrosis or OCD had a previous or concomitant TTO. Range of Motion

The mean follow-up time after OCA transplantation for ROM assessment was 1.5 ± 1.2 years. Knee ROM for all patients was 2.1° (±7.9°) to 135.0° (±6.7°). Permanent extension loss of 5° or greater was seen in 2 knees (7.1%), both of which occurred in the same patient, with both knees having received unipolar OCA transplantation for PF chondrosis.

Repeat Interventions

In total, 8 knees (28.6%) had a repeat intervention. Five repeat interventions were isolated manipulations under anesthesia (MUA), one was a MUA followed by a subsequent total knee arthroplasty (TKA), and the remaining 2 were isolated TKAs. No knees (0%) underwent a revision cartilage or salvage procedure within the first 2 years after surgery.

Conversion to Arthroplasty

Three knees (10.7%) in total were converted to TKA, all of which occurred in the setting of PF chondrosis. The age at the time of arthroplasty was 40.4 ± 1.7 years, which occurred on average 3.8 ± 0.9 years (range 2.8-4.7 years) after the index procedure. Two of these cases were the result failure of OCA plug incorporation with loosening of the graft, both of which occurred in the same patient but in separate knees. The third arthroplasty was performed at 4.7 years after the initial OCA procedure and was done for progressive osteoarthritis.

Radiographic Outcomes

Of the 28 knees in the study, 16 (57.1%) had follow-up MRIs available for review. The mean time from OCA transplantation to follow-up MRI was 2.0 ± 1.5 years (range 9 months to 6.7 years). MOCART 2.0 scores were 70.9 ± 19.1 (range 45-95). Twelve of the 16 patients (75%) had signal changes present in the subchondral bone, including 3 knees (18.8%) with subchondral cysts. Eleven of the 16 knees (68.8%) had signal changes in the cartilage of the OCA plug, with most cases being only minor signal change (10/11; 91%) and only 1 occurrence of hyperintense signal change. Age did not correlate with MOCART 2.0 scores (r = 0.311; P = .120), nor did time to MRI after surgery (r = −0.018; P = .950)

Discussion

This study demonstrates improvements in patient-reported functional outcomes including IKDC, KOOS Jr, SF-12 PH, and VR-12 physical at a mean follow-up of 3.6 ± 1.4 years after OCA transplantation. Subgroup assessments were suggestive of differences between patients on the basis of whether their primary pathology was LPI or PF chondrosis.

The present study only assessed patients who received allografts preserved via the Missouri Osteochondral Preservation System, which has been previously reported in Stoker et al.16 They studied 194 patients undergoing OCA in the knee and found that patients with MOPS were more likely to experience successful outcomes (84% vs 60%, P = .028) and less likely to undergo revision (5% vs 21%, P = .001) when compared with standard preservation techniques. Despite these findings, the population in their study exhibited a great deal of heterogeneity, including a composite assessment of OCA transplantations in the tibiofemoral and PF compartments, in addition to substantial variability regarding age and concomitant procedures. The current study provides a more refined view of what short-term outcomes may be expected in a PF population.

This study demonstrated PROs were improved at 2 years after OCA transplantation in the patella. In particular, IKDC scores have been previously shown to be a good measure of knee function after articular cartilage surgery,19 having markedly increased from the preoperative to postoperative state (mean change: +35.4 points) in this study. This is similar to the findings of Melugin et al20 findings of a 29.7-point improvement after cryopreserved osteochondral allografting in the PF joint, and the 26.1-point improvement reported in the systematic review from Chahla et al21 on PF OCA transplantation. An improvement of 35.4 points is well above both the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 9.8 points and the substantial clinical benefit (SCB) of 26.9 points. Patient outcomes also improved regardless of age, BMI, differing primary pathology, or whether bipolar OCA was performed, suggesting that patellar OCA has the capacity to improve patient quality of life despite subtle differences amongst patients who are indicated.

The current study did find manipulation under anesthesia occurred in 21.4% (6/28) of the cases, which was twice that reported by Melugin et al22 (10.5%). This increased rate could reflect differences in patient pathologies, patient sex, concomitant surgical procedures, varying rehabilitation protocols, and patient engagement and active commitment to their rehabilitation.22, 23, 24, 25 Two MUAs occurred in patients with LPI who had TTO, MPFLR, and lateral retinacular lengthening procedures performed concurrently with OCA transplantation, whereas the other 4 MUAs occurred in patients between 35 and 47 years old with PF chondrosis who had bipolar OCA transplants. Although this does not confer causality, it sheds light on the context in which these reinterventions occurred. Only 1 of the 6 patients that underwent MUA had IKDC scores below the MCID, with all 6 patients achieving full symmetric range of motion back by 6 months postoperatively. This suggests that despite undergoing a MUA, most patients still acquired meaningful improvement in their knee function at 2 years.

There were no subsequent revision and salvage cartilage restoration procedures performed within the first 2-years of follow-up; however, 3 knees (10.7%) subsequently received an arthroplasty. This was similar to the rate of 12.5% reported by Melugin et al.20 All 3 cases in our study occurred in older patients with PF chondrosis, with a mean time to arthroplasty of 3.8 ± 0.9 years (range: 2.8-4.7 years) after transplantation. The lack of subsequent arthroplasty in patients with LPI is not surprising, given the mean age of patients with LPI was nearly 12 years younger than that of the patients with PF chondrosis. This age discrepancy likely played a role in whether arthroplasty was even offered as a subsequent intervention to younger patients, making it difficult to meaningfully interpret this finding. Two of the 3 TKA cases were the result failure of OCA plug incorporation with loosening of the graft, both of which occurred in the same male patient but in separate knees.

Subgroup analysis revealed bipolar OCA transplantations for “kissing lesions” showed greater improvements in their IKDC scores than those who had unipolar OCA transplantations (P = .007; 54.6 vs 24.1, respectively). This finding is in opposition to previously published findings; however, the follow-up duration in this study is shorter than the comparison, and the graft-preservation technique was standardized to only include allografts preserved via MOPS.1,8 Another thing to consider is the improvement in IKDC scores in bipolar OCA knees could potentially influenced by sampling bias in a low powered, heterogeneous population. To understand why this finding may have occurred, the authors created Supplemental Table 1 to tabulate patient characteristics for the 4 patients who did not reach improvements in IKDC above the minimum clinically important difference, all 4 of which occurred in patients who received unipolar transplantations. It is possible that the factors listed in Appendix Table 1, available at www.arthroscopyjournal.org, could have influenced PROs, particularly given the presence of additional injuries and states of impaired psychological well-being, which have been shown to influence outcomes reported by patients and is worth consideration.26, 27, 28 Thus, the authors are unable to draw a meaningful conclusion from the increased improvement seen in the bipolar transplantation subgroup within this study.

The mean MOCARTS 2.0 score in this study was 70.9, which was slightly greater than the MOCARTS score of 62.5 reported by Melugin et al.20 One possible explanation for this finding is that the present study included a larger collection of MRIs that were assessed (n = 16) compared with the 6 MRI assessments performed in the study by Melugin et al.20 It is also possible that this difference was from the use of the MOCARTS 2.0 score, which includes updated and expanded assessments, instead of the original MOCARTS score.18 Although the updated assessment criteria of the MOCARTS 2.0 scale are an expanded version of the parent scale, caution should be exercised when trying to directly interchange and compare these scores given this sort of interchangeability has not yet been validated.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, the present study is underpowered to detect granular differences that could occur if smaller effect sizes were in fact present amongst any comparisons that did not reach statistical significance, thus any “negative findings” should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, this study includes a heterogeneous population of patients, so there are many confounding variables that may have affected our results. In addition, limiting the analysis to cases using the MOPS method establishes expectations for outcomes with the use of this technique, but may not be applicable to other osteochondral allograft preservation systems. Finally, selection bias and surgeon decision making likely introduced bias. Despite the limitations in sample size and population heterogeneity, extensive effort was undertaken to understand the results a priori (via planned subgroup comparisons) and post-hoc (via retroactive reviews of the records) in an effort to optimize the validity in which the authors interpreted and reported the results.

Conclusions

Patellar OCA transplantation resulted in overall improvement in PROs with an 11% rate of conversion to TKA at 2-years follow-up.
