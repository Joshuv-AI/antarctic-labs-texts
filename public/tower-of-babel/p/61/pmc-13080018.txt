Toward the simultaneous detection of multiple diseases with a highly cost-effective cell-free DNA methylome test

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCell-free DNA (cfDNA) in blood carries molecular signals from multiple organs, offering a powerful, noninvasive way to detect disease and monitor health. Current cfDNA methylation tests are costly and usually focus on a single condition. We developed MethylScan, a low-cost assay that sequences cfDNA methylome from blood. In over 1,000 individuals, MethylScan shows robust performance across a range of clinical applications, including multicancer detection in the general population, liver cancer surveillance in high-risk individuals, liver disease classification, organ injury detection, and ancestry prediction, all from one blood sample. This versatile approach enables affordable, wide-ranging cfDNA tests that can identify various health conditions simultaneously, with the potential to transform early disease detection and health monitoring across diverse clinical settings.

When cells die, they do not simply vanish; they leave behind molecular traces, including cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in the blood stream. cfDNA is a mixture of DNA fragments released from various organs, offering valuable insights into the health of these organs. Among the features of cfDNA, methylation has demonstrated significant potential for noninvasive diagnostic and prognostic applications (1–4). Unlike an individual’s genome, which remains largely stable across tissues and over time (except for rare somatic mutations), the DNA methylome is tissue-specific (3, 5) and dynamically changes with the tissue’s disease status (6). Nearly all diseases can leave a signature in cfDNA methylation in two key ways: 1) Aberrant DNA methylation patterns often arise early in the development of many diseases, including cancer (7), immunodeficiencies (8), and psychiatric or neurodegenerative disorders (9, 10). Affected tissues release cfDNA bearing disease-specific methylation signatures. 2) Many diseases (e.g., cancer, myocardial infarction, stroke, and autoimmune conditions) increase the rate of cell death, resulting in elevated levels of cfDNA from the impacted tissues in the blood. Because each tissue type has a distinct DNA methylation pattern, it is possible to trace the tissue of origin of cfDNA and infer the increased death of specific cell types (3, 11, 12).

Current applications of cfDNA methylation are primarily limited to detecting single clinical indications, such as cancer (1, 2, 13–19), fetal abnormalities (20, 21), and organ transplantation (22), typically by targeting a specific panel of genomic loci at a time. However, profiling methylation across the entire genome from cfDNA could provide a more comprehensive approach, enabling the simultaneous detection of multiple diseases by capturing all potential methylation markers of cfDNA originating from all organs. Thus, the cfDNA methylome has the potential to provide a holistic view of the health status of organs throughout the body. This approach is particularly valuable when the underlying disease is unclear or when specific biochemical diagnostic tests are unavailable.

A major challenge in using the cfDNA methylome for disease detection is the high cost of sequencing. The majority of cfDNA in circulation originates from white blood cells, while abnormally methylated cfDNA from diseased tissues usually constitutes only a small fraction, particularly in the early stages of disease. For example, in early-stage cancer patients, tumor-derived cfDNA may comprise less than 0.1% of the total cfDNA. Detecting such low-abundance signals requires extremely deep sequencing; to obtain even a single read from tumor-derived cfDNA at a given locus may require coverage exceeding 1,000×, rendering genome-wide approaches financially impractical. To reduce the cost of cfDNA methylome sequencing, we previously developed the cell-free DNA Methylome Sequencing (cfMethyl-Seq) technology (1), which predominantly sequences cfDNA fragments located in CpG islands across the genome, providing over 12-fold enrichment in these regions compared to WGBS. While cfMethyl-Seq significantly lowers the cost of cfDNA methylome sequencing, the expense remains too high for the assay to be widely adopted as a clinical test for the general population.

Here we propose the MethylScan method to further significantly reduce the cost of cfDNA methylome sequencing while demonstrating its broad utility in detecting a wide range of diseases. Like our previous cost-effective method (1), cfMethyl-Seq, this assay also enriches cfDNA from CpG islands. However, MethylScan goes further by depleting the abundant background cfDNA, primarily derived from blood cells, thereby significantly reducing sequencing costs. In healthy individuals, about 85% of cfDNA originates from blood cells (23), creating substantial background noise that can obscure cfDNA from tumors or diseased organs. By removing this background DNA and specifically capturing the remaining cfDNA, MethylScan enhances the enrichment of disease-associated cfDNA, such as tumor DNA.

Using the tumor DNA dilution series, we demonstrate that MethylScan can effectively enrich tumor cfDNA fragments, leading to sensitive and accurate detection of tumor signals at a low cost. We further demonstrated the broad clinical utility of the MethylScan assay across a range of applications, including multicancer detection from general populations, liver cancer detection in high-risk populations, classification of various liver diseases, organ-specific abnormalities via cfDNA tissue deconvolution, and race prediction of blood donors.

Results

The MethylScan Method.

The MethylScan method is a targeted methylation assay that combines Methylation-Sensitive Restriction Enzymes (MSRE) digestion with a custom panel to enrich hypermethylated cfDNA from tissues beyond blood, enabling cost-effective detection of multiple diseases. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the rationale of the MethylScan method is to use MSRE to cleave the hypomethylated cfDNA, and subsequently, we target-capture cfDNA only from those genomic regions where the background cfDNA (e.g., cfDNA of blood cells) naturally exhibits consistent hypomethylation and therefore has been cleared by the enzymatic cleavage. These regions serve as a superset of potential hypermethylation markers for any diseases, including cancer. Note that other genomic regions, where the background DNA is not consistently hypomethylated, cannot serve as ideal hypermethylation markers, because even if disease-associated DNA is abnormally hypermethylated, it is challenging to distinguish the disease signals from the background.

Illustration of the principle of the MethylScan assay and the criteria of choosing target regions of the MethylScan panel. Shown are four genomic regions, of which the middle two regions have consistent unmethylated MSRE cutting sites in the background cfDNA and are therefore included in the MethylScan panel. In Step 1, upon MSRE digestion, the background cfDNA in the two panel regions are removed. In Step 2, the target panel specifically captures cfDNA from the panel regions, thereby enriching tumor cfDNA in the final sequencing pool.

The MethylScan method contains a targeted panel and an experimental protocol. The target panel exhaustively contains genome regions consistently hypomethylated in the background cfDNA, mostly cfDNA of White Blood Cells (WBC) or more generally, cfDNA from healthy individuals. To define target regions of the MethylScan panel, we search for 120-bp regions that satisfy the following criteria: 1) contains at least one recognition site for the MSREs HpaII (CCGG) or HhaI (GCGC), 2) contains at least 4 CpG sites, 3) shows consistent hypomethylation at CpG sites within background cfDNA, defined as cfDNA from WBC or from plasma of healthy individuals. Since we cleave the background DNA, we can no longer directly derive the fraction of nonbackground DNA falling onto a genome region. To compute such fractions, we additionally select 300 control regions without CpG or MSRE sites. The final panel contains 154,028 regions of 120-bp, covering 1,600,725 CpG sites. 87.4% of target regions fall in CpG islands, and 61.3% fall in gene promoter regions. The genomic breakdown of the panel regions and their enrichment fold changes compared to genome-wide distributions are shown in Fig. 2A. Specifically, gene promoters and CpG islands showed enrichment fold changes of 44.4 and 27.1, respectively, in the panel compared with the genome-wide background, indicating that these features are highly enriched in the capture panel.

The MethylScan assay. (A) The enrichment analysis result with the capture panel for gene promoters, CpG islands, CpG island shores, CpG island shelf, and open sea regions. Note that percentages do not sum to one because some regions overlap. (B) Diagram of the MethylScan assay protocol.

The MethylScan experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. 2B. Given a cfDNA sample, we cleave hypomethylated DNA with MSRE, followed by target capture using the designed panel. Specifically, the input cfDNA or sheared genomic DNA (gDNA) is ligated to the standard barcoded adapter, followed by digestion with the MSREs HpaI and HhaI. Next, the cfDNA will be converted using the enzymatic EM-seq conversion module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). The first PCR amplification is performed on the enzymatically converted cfDNA to construct the full-length Illumina libraries with index sequences. Note that only undigested cfDNA fragments still have adapters on both ends and can be amplified. The amplification products are pooled and undergo hybrid capture using the MethylScan panel, followed by a second run of PCR amplification. Subsequently, the cfDNA will be sequenced to provide the counts of nucleic acid molecules with methylation patterns of interest in the targeted regions. Detailed sequencing setting and data processing see SI Appendix, section S3.

Analytical Evaluation of the MethylScan Panel for Cancer Signal Detection.

We performed the spike-in experiments by generating the dilution series of tumor DNA, in which the genomic DNA (gDNA) of liver tumor tissue is diluted by the gDNA of WBC at different ratios (0%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 5.0% for liver tumor gDNA). Four spike-in technical replicates at each nonzero dilution ratio (six replicates at 0% dilution ratio) and their MethylScan data were generated. Based on the design of the MethylScan panel, we define the cancer signals as the number of reads whose α-value5 (calculated as the percent of methylated CpG sites in a read) is higher than 0.8. The hypermethylated read count is then normalized by the average coverage of all control regions. Using dilution series, we benchmarked the Limit Of Detection (LOD) and linearity of the MethylScan panel in detecting cancer signals: 1) The LOD of the panel was evaluated by comparing the cancer signal (i.e., normalized hypermethylated read count) at every dilution ratio against that in the control samples. As shown in Fig. 3A, the cancer signal can be clearly detected at tumor dilution ratios as low 0.05% (Student t test P-value = 0.000058). 2) The linearity was evaluated by the correspondence between the observed and the expected tumor fractions (i.e., dilution ratio), where the observed tumor fraction of each sample was estimated from the MethylScan data by using the method of nonnegative least squares (12, 23). As shown in Fig. 3B, a strong linear relationship (R2=0.983) was observed between the expected and estimated tumor fractions.

(A) The limit of detection (LOD) and (B) the accuracy of the MethylScan assay, where a z-score was used to calculate the tumor signals of a replicate in comparison to the replicates with dilution ratio 0%, and the accuracy is evaluated by the correlation between the expected and estimated tumor fractions. (C) The limit of detection (LOD) and (D) the accuracy of the MethylScan assay without the digestion step. The statistical significance in the figures indicated the P-values of the Student’s t tests between the z-scores of each nonzero dilution ratio and those of the zero dilution ratio: “ns” means not statistically significant (P-value > 0.05); *P-value < 0.05; **P-value < 0.01; ***P-value < 0.001; ****P-value < 0.0001.

To evaluate the benefit of the MSRE cleavage in the MethylScan experimental protocol, we generated the same dilution series without the MSRE digestion. We generated a similar amount of sequencing data for the undigested dilution series (median 735 K paired-end reads of 150 bp) compared to the digested dilution series (median 653 K paired-end reads of 150 bp). The digested dilution series samples reached coverage of 109.1 in control regions per million paired-end reads, while the undigested dilution series samples reached coverage of 21.3 in control regions per million paired-end reads. Thus, the digested dilution series achieved 5.1 times more coverage in the control regions than the undigested dilution series. As shown in Fig. 3 C and D, without MSRE digestion, cancer signals can only be detected at 0.5% and the quantitative accuracy also decreased (R2=0.858). This comparison showed that the MethylScan panel and experimental protocol effectively enriched tumor cfDNA fragments, leading to sensitive and accurate detection of tumor signals at a low cost. More analysis of the MethylScan assay, including capture efficiency, assay reproducibility, and the retention of tumor-like reads after digestion, can be found in SI Appendix, section S4.

Sample Cohorts and Their Usage.

To demonstrate the clinical applications of MethylScan, we used the tissue samples of 355 patients and plasma samples of 1,061 patients. The tissue samples (n = 899) have two groups (Fig. 4G): 378 tissue samples (tumors and their adjacent normal tissues) from 189 cancer patients were used to discover markers for multicancer detection; and the remaining 512 normal tissue samples from 166 noncancer individuals covered 29 major types of human tissues and were used exclusively for marker discovery in the tissue deconvolution study. The plasma samples (n = 1,061) were from 460 cancer patients (covering 4 major cancers: liver, lung, ovarian, and stomach cancer) and 601 noncancer individuals. The noncancer individuals (n = 601) can be further categorized into three groups: noncancer patients at high-risk of liver cancer who have chronic liver diseases (n = 157), noncancer patients at high-risk of lung cancer who have benign lung nodules (n = 43), and general noncancer hospital visitors (n = 401). Across all samples, with an average of 5.3G data per sample, we achieved an average sequencing coverage is 347× in control regions, 8.3× in target regions (9.7× for cancer samples and 6.7× for noncancer samples), and 0.17× in nontarget regions. The sample characteristics are shown in Fig. 5 and details are listed in Dataset S1. As shown in Fig. 4 A–F, we used the plasma samples to design five subcohorts for five applications of the MethylScan assay: multicancer detection, liver-cancer surveillance, liver disease classification, disease detection by tissue deconvolution and race prediction. Summary statistics of the MethylScan sequencing data are available in Dataset S2.

Multicancer Detection With MethylScan Data.

Cancer-specific methylation marker discovery using MethylScan data of tissue samples.

We applied the MethylScan protocol to 189 pairs of tumor and adjacent normal tissues of 4 cancer types (78, 58, 29, 24 from liver, lung, ovary, and stomach cancer patients, respectively, Fig. 4G). Because there is no heavy background from blood cells in the tissue, we omitted the MSRE digestion step. Using the normalized hypermethylation read count of each tissue sample, the MethylScan multicancer marker was identified as the top 5,000 target regions exhibiting the most frequent tumor-specific signals when comparing tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues across each cancer type. The final marker set combines these identified regions from all cancer types, totaling 15,155 markers (Dataset S3). The heatmap visualization of the MethylScan data on the identified markers clearly demonstrated that these markers carry strong cancer signals in both the tissue and plasma cfDNA samples (Fig. 6 A and B). Furthermore, we used the 450 K methylation array data of The Cancer Genome Atlas (24) (TCGA) as an independent data source to confirm the differential power of these markers, between tumors and normal tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). These hypermethylation markers are enriched within regions corresponding to CpG islands, promoters, 5’-UTR, Exons, promoter regions, simple repeat, and histone modifications such as H3K4me2, H3K4me3, and H3K9ac (SI Appendix, Figs. S2–S4). Further inspection of the MSigDB (25) showed that 44% of genes adjacent to or inside the top 2000 cancer-specific markers are involved in known cancer modules, and 53% of those are part of oncogenic signatures. Moreover, we selected the top 100 cancer-specific methylation markers for each cancer type, resulting in a total of 374 distinct markers. We then identified 83 genes whose promoter regions overlap with these markers. Of these 83 genes, 61 (73%) have been reported in cancer-related publications (see SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2 and Dataset S6 for details). For tissue-of-origin (TOO) predictions, we identified markers that significantly differ between any two cancer types and between any cancer type and all other cancer types using MethylScan data from 189 solid tumors, comprising liver (n = 78), lung (n = 58), ovary (n = 29), and stomach (n = 24) samples. The final marker set, totaling 6,430 regions (Dataset S4), was derived from these four major cancer types. Consequently, the TOO predictions in this study are specifically focused on distinguishing among these four cancer types.

Performance of the MethylScan panel for multicancer detection.

Our subcohort for multicancer detection consists of 861 individuals, including 460 cancer patients representing four major cancer types (liver, lung, ovarian, and stomach cancers), and 401 noncancer patients (Fig. 4B). Among all cancer patients, 51.5% are at early stages. Detailed characteristics of these individuals refer to Fig. 5. Note that 1) the cancer patients providing plasma samples are distinct from those contributing tissue samples, and 2) the noncancer group comprises general hospital visitors, predominantly older adults, with a median age of 60. This reflects realistic clinical scenarios for using multicancer detection.

For validating the MethylScan panel for multicancer detection, we performed the stratified fivefold cross-validation, ensuring an even distribution of age, gender, and cancer types and stages across the folds. To ensure robustness, this cross-validation procedure was randomly repeated 50 times, and the average and 95% CI of the performance metrics across the 50 runs were reported. We achieved an average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.938 (95% CI: 0.920 to 0.954) with a sensitivity of 63.3% (95% CI: 58.9 to 67.9%) at 98.0% specificity in the fivefold cross validations (Fig. 6C). Breaking down this performance into cancer stages, for early-stage (stage I and II) cancers, we achieved an average AUROC of 0.916 (95% CI: 0.890 to 0.940) with a sensitivity of 55.3% (95% CI: 49.1 to 62.1%) at 98.0% specificity, and particularly for stage-I cancers an average AUROC of 0.906 (95% CI: 0.873 to 0.937) with a sensitivity of 54.4% (95% CI: 46.3 to 62.8%) at 98.0% specificity. When breaking down these results to each cancer type and cancer stage (Fig. 6D), we achieved sensitivities at or above 40% at the specificity of 98.0% in all cases.

We used the same fivefold cross-validations to evaluate the performance of TOO prediction of four cancer types, i.e., liver, lung, ovary, and stomach cancers. Our method achieved an average accuracy of 91.7% (95% CI: 87.9 to 94.6%) across all stages (Fig. 7A) and 89.8% (95% CI: 83.3 to 94.5%) across early stages (Fig. 7B). All four cancer types exhibit accuracies exceeding 84% for patients across all stages. However, among these, stomach cancer shows the lowest accuracy, especially for early-stage patients, compared to the other cancer types, possibly due to the much smaller number of stomach tumor tissue samples available for marker discovery. Note that this accuracy was calculated after excluding cancer patients with a multicancer detection predictive score below the cutoff corresponding to a specificity of 98.0%.

Liver-Cancer Detection With MethylScan in the High-Risk Population.

Liver cancer is the fastest-rising cause of cancer-related death in the United States (26). Early detection is critical to improving patient survival, and as such, regular surveillance is recommended for individuals at high risk. However, the current standard of care—biannual abdominal ultrasound—suffers from low patient compliance and limited sensitivity (27). In this study, we evaluated the performance of MethylScan for the detection of liver cancer. In particular, we aimed to distinguish liver cancer patients from individuals at high risk for liver cancer, specifically, those with one of four major liver diseases: hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, alcoholic liver disease (ALD), and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). To this end, we established a subcohort (Fig. 4C) comprising 91 liver cancer patients and 157 high-risk, noncancer individuals (65 with HBV, 40 with HCV, 31 with ALD, and 21 with MASLD).

Using the same marker discovery approach applied in our multicancer detection work, we analyzed 78 matched pairs of liver tumor and adjacent normal liver tissue samples to identify the top 5,000 liver cancer methylation markers. Among these markers, we selected the top 100 and analyzed the genes whose promoter regions overlapped with these markers for functional characterization. Of the 24 genes with liver cancer markers in their promoter regions (Dataset S7), 19 (79%) have been previously reported in the literature. Notably, the top-ranking genes—LRRC4, OTX1, and TBX15—have been reported as hypermethylated at their promoter regions in hepatocellular carcinoma (28, 29).

We performed the stratified fivefold cross-validation, ensuring balanced distributions of cancer stages, liver disease types, age, and gender across the folds. To ensure robustness, this cross-validation procedure was randomly repeated 50 times, and the average and 95% CI of the performance metrics across runs were reported. For the surveillance purpose, our liver cancer detection model achieved an average AUROC of 0.927 (95% CI: 0.889 to 0.959), with a sensitivity of 79.6% (95% CI: 70.6 to 87.8%) at a specificity of 90.4% in cross-validation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). For early-stage liver cancer (stages I and II), which accounted for 79.1% of all liver cancer cases, the model achieved an average AUROC of 0.915 (95% CI: 0.870 to 0.953) and a sensitivity of 76.3% (95% CI: 64.9 to 85.6%) at the same specificity level. While high specificity is desirable for multicancer detection in the general population to minimize unnecessary follow-up costs, high sensitivity is crucial for surveillance in high-risk populations to detect as many cancer cases as possible.

Using MethylScan For Noninvasively Classifying Multiple Liver Diseases.

Early detection and accurate differentiation of various liver diseases (e.g., ALD, HBV, HCV, MASLD) enable tailored treatment strategies based on the specific type and severity, thereby improving patient outcomes. cfDNA methylome assays offer a promising approach for classifying liver diseases while reducing the need for invasive procedures, thus enhancing patient comfort and safety.

We conducted the stratified fivefold cross-validation on 157 patients at high risk for liver cancer (Fig. 5: 65 HBV, 40 HCV, 31 ALD, and 21 MASLD), ensuring balanced distributions of age and liver disease types across folds. Unlike cancer detection that can utilize tissue samples for biomarker discovery, obtaining tissue biopsies for chronic liver diseases is challenging. Therefore, we used plasma samples from the training set to identify differential markers among liver disease types. Specifically, we applied the limma method (30), which uses an empirical Bayes framework, to identify target regions capable of distinguishing one liver disease from others (details in Methods). The final marker set comprised all selected regions and was used in the liver disease classification model. To ensure robustness, this cross-validation procedure was randomly repeated 50 times, and the average and 95% CI of the performance metrics across runs were reported.

Our liver disease classification model classified 62.4% of patients, requiring the ratio of the two highest class probabilities to exceed 2.0 for a reliable classification. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 84.7% (95% CI: 76.0 to 91.2%). When assessing performance by disease type (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), HBV and HCV achieved good performance in both accuracy (>79%) and precision (>92%). In contrast, ALD and MASLD showed lower performance: For ALD, both accuracy and precision were 66.7%, whereas MASLD achieved an accuracy of 71.4% but with a lower precision, likely due to their limited sample sizes for training.

Detecting Organ-Specific Abnormalities By Tissue Deconvolution of MethylScan Data.

In our recent study (3), we demonstrated that analyzing tissue composition from plasma cfDNA samples can reveal increased cell death in diseased tissues. In this study, we applied tissue deconvolution to MethylScan data to evaluate the assay’s potential for general disease detection. As shown in Fig. 4E, we analyzed 834 plasma cfDNA samples, including those from 401 noncancer general hospital visitors, 157 noncancer patients with liver diseases, 43 noncancer patients with benign lung nodules, 91 patients with liver cancer, and 142 patients with lung cancer.

Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, we found that patients with liver disease or liver cancer have a significantly higher fraction of cfDNA originating from the liver compared to noncancer general hospital visitors (Fig. 8; P-values: 5.6e−3 for liver disease and 4.1e−16 for liver cancer). Similarly, patients with benign lung nodules or lung cancer show a significantly higher fraction of cfDNA originating from the lung than noncancer general hospital visitors (Fig. 8; P-values: 0.062 for lung nodules and <2.22e−16 for lung cancer). Furthermore, cancer patients exhibited significantly more tissue damage (Fig. 8; P = 6.3e−09 for liver disease vs. liver cancer; P = 2.2e−4 for benign lung nodules vs. lung cancer). These findings demonstrate that the MethylScan assay effectively detects tissue damage and may be useful for diagnosing cancer and other diseases. Moreover, the extent of tissue damage is correlated with disease severity.

Using MethylScan to Predict Race.

We evaluate the performance of using MethylScan to predict race, using all 326 noncancer general hospital visitors—comprising 248 White and 78 Asian individuals (Fig. 4F). Other racial groups were excluded due to insufficient sample sizes for reliable cross-validation. We performed stratified fivefold cross-validation to ensure an even distribution of race and age across the folds. We applied the limma method (30), which uses an empirical Bayes framework, to identify markers associated with races. To ensure robustness, this cross-validation procedure was randomly repeated 50 times, and the average and 95% CI of the performance metrics across runs were reported. Our race prediction model achieved an average overall accuracy of 97.1% (95% CI: 94.6 to 98.7%). Breaking down performance by race, the model achieved average accuracies of 99.2% for White individuals and 90.5% for Asian individuals. The difference of accuracies of these two race groups most likely attributes to the difference in terms of training sample sizes. Although this study involved only two racial groups, we can easily expand the model to classify additional races in the future.

Discussion

We developed the MethylScan method to enrich for disease-informative, hypermethylated cfDNA, thereby significantly increasing the assay sensitivity and reducing the cost of cfDNA methylome sequencing for disease detection. This approach targets genomic regions that are consistently hypomethylated in cfDNA derived from blood cells, namely, regions that can serve as effective hypermethylation markers in blood-based assays. In these regions, even trace amounts of hypermethylated cfDNA can stand out. By using methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes (MSREs) to deplete the hypomethylated background from blood-derived cfDNA, we substantially enrich for disease-associated signals. Although MSREs combined with qPCR have previously been used to target differentially methylated regions, multiplex PCR is not scalable to the hundreds of thousands of loci needed, and MethylScan overcomes this scalability limitation.

Although MSRE digestion efficiency is nearly complete (99.5 to 99.8% based on our experiments), we observed that 26% of uncleaved cfDNA fragments within the target regions retain uncut MSRE sites. These results indicate that, while MSRE digestion effectively enriches hypermethylated fragments, it can also inadvertently enrich partially hypomethylated fragments that escape digestion, potentially compromising cancer detection sensitivity and TOO analysis. Therefore, we retained the deamination conversion step in MethylScan to maximize the accuracy of methylation quantification and ensure robust detection of aberrant methylation signals.

Because cfDNA in blood originates from multiple organs, and MethylScan captures a broad spectrum of robust hypermethylation markers, this assay has the potential to detect a variety of diseases, provided that appropriate training cohorts are available. In this study, we demonstrated its applicability in multiple contexts: multicancer early detection, liver cancer surveillance, liver disease classification, general health monitoring, and even race prediction. Since DNA methylation patterns can accurately reflect race, race-specific methylation may act as a confounding factor in methylation-based diagnostics. Thus, it is essential to ensure that disease markers are not race-associated and that racial distribution is balanced between cases and controls when assembling training cohorts.

Although our panel design eliminates most WBC-derived signals, it can still detect deviations when WBC methylation patterns shift from the normal baseline. During systemic inflammation, both WBC methylation and cellular composition can change substantially, resulting in profiles that deviate from the noninflammatory WBC baseline. Such deviations can generate detectable signals in our assay, allowing inflammatory responses to be reflected, even though the primary design goal was to suppress WBC background noise.

The rapid advancement of omics-based diagnostics and AI is challenging the traditional “one assay, one disease” paradigm. Multicancer early detection exemplifies a shift toward holistic diagnostics, and the prospect of pan-disease detection is increasingly within reach. One major challenge in AI-based diagnostics is the need for large-scale training datasets. This requirement cannot be met through conventional “one disease, one panel” approaches, which yield limited data per test. In contrast, cfDNA methylome profiling generates rich, genome-wide information for each patient, making it ideal for continuous stacking up of data and thus well suited for machine learning and AI applications.

Traditionally, biomarker discovery has involved stepwise filtering to reduce assay complexity and cost. However, the cost-efficiency of MethylScan enables retention of comprehensive biomarker information. This shifts the paradigm from binary biomarker selection to assigning variable weights to each marker, representing a move from discrete to continuous modeling. Each marker may contribute differently across diseases, with importance weights derived from disease-specific training cohorts.

For future work, we may develop an additional panel, targeting those consistently hypermethylated regions in background DNA, and capture hypomethylated cfDNA, which may arise from tumors or diseased tissues. Analogously, hypermethylated DNA can be cleaved with Methylation-Dependent Restriction Enzymes, followed by targeted captures. However, CpG sites hypermethylated in the background (and hypomethylated in cancer and other diseases) are more sparsely distributed and widely spread, requiring a much larger panel to exhaustively cover them (31, 32). In addition, hypomethylated regions in tumors are often located in repetitive regions (33), challenging the current paradigm of panel design. These challenges remain to be addressed to expand MethylScan to capture the hypomethylation signals of cancer.

Methods

Blood and Solid Tissue Samples.

1,061 blood samples were processed for the MethylScan assay. Blood samples were collected at UCLA hospitals, collected in collaboration with George Washington University and University of Maryland, or purchased from commercial vendors. In addition, 189 pairs of tissue samples were processed using the MethylScan assay. The solid tumor and adjacent normal tissue were collected from the UCLA hospital, UCLA Translational Pathology Core Laboratory, or purchased from commercial vendors. The details of patient enrollment are in SI Appendix, section S5, and the characteristics of the patients who provided plasma and tissue samples are in Datasets S1 and S5.

MethylScan Panel Design.

We used the WGBS data of 48 white blood cell (WBC) samples and 30 plasma samples from healthy individuals to identify hypomethylated HpaII/HhaI cutting sites. In the hg19 genome, we selected 568,995 cutting sites (CCGG or GCGC) that are consistently hypomethylated (β-value < 0.2) in ≥90% of 48 WGBS WBC gDNA samples or ≥90% of 30 WGBS cfDNA samples. From these hypomethylated cutting sites, we identify the candidate 120-bp target regions that satisfy the following criteria: 1) contains at least 4 CpG sites, 2) contains at least 1 selected cutting site in the middle 80 bp regions (position 21 to 100), and 3) 24mer methylome mappability score >0.8. We also selected 300 control regions that have 24mer methylome mappability score >0.8 without CpG or MSRE sites. In the computation of the methylation profile, we used the average coverage of all 300 control regions as the denominator to normalize the read counts in the target regions.

MethylScan Library Construction of Plasma and Tissue Samples.

10 ng from each cfDNA sample was used as the input material. For gDNA samples, 1.5 to 2 µg of DNA was first sonicated by Covaris R230 system (Woburn, MA). A two-step size selection using 0.8× to 1.5× Ampure XP beads was then performed to enrich the 150 to 200 bp fragments. A total of 200 ng of the size-selected gDNA was used as input material. The precapture library construction was performed with NEB UltraII DNA library prep kit for Illumina (Ipswich, MA). Before the first PCR amplification, the gDNA samples were subjected to bisulfite conversion by QIAGEN Epitect kit, and cfDNA samples were subjected to enzymatic conversion by NEBNext enzymatic methyl-seq kit. Twist panel hybrid capture was performed with their standard protocol (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA). The concentration of the postcapture library after the second PCR amplification was measured by Qubit 1xdsDNA HS kit (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). Their quality was examined by TBE-UREA PAGE and Bioanalyzer before sequencing.

Enrichment of Genomic Features in the Capture Panel.

To quantify the overrepresentation of specific genomic features within the capture panel, we performed an enrichment analysis for gene promoters, CpG islands, CpG island shores, CpG island shelves, and open sea regions. The enrichment fold was calculated by comparing the proportion of genomic context observed in the panel to their expected proportion in the entire genome. Specifically, we defined: background ratio=total length of genomic context in genometotal length of genome and panel ratio=length of genomic context overlapping the paneltotal length of panel. The enrichment fold change was then calculated as enrichment fold change=panel ratiobackground ratio.

Profile Generation for the MethylScan Data.

For MethylScan data, we extracted each hypermethylated read in each target region that satisfies the following criteria: 1) it overlaps with at least 30% of the 120 bp target regions, 2) its MSRE cutting sites are hypermethylated, and 3) its α value ≥0.8. For any target region, we computed its normalized hypermethylated read count as RCnorm=Number of the hypermethylated reads in the target regionAverage coverage of control regions and use its logarithm transformation ln (1 + 1000 × RCnorm) to serve as the signal value in the profile of a MethylScan sample.

Panel Validation by the Spike-In Dilution Series Experiment.

In order to analytically evaluate the tumor signal detection of the MethylScan assay, we conducted the experimental dilution series by using the sonicated and AmpureXP beads size-selected healthy control WBC gDNA spiked-in with 0%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 5% of liver tumor tissue gDNA as input material. Four spike-in technical replicates at each nonzero dilution ratio (six replicates at 0% dilution ratio) and their MethylScan data were generated. In order to detect tumor signals, we also generated four additional replicates at 0% dilution ratio, serving as the four original WBC samples (denoted as controlsWBC) together with the original liver tumor sample (denoted as tumorliver) for selecting the 5,000 differential target regions that had no tumor signals in controlsWBC and the highest tumor signals in tumorliver. Here, the tumor signal in a target region is the normalized hypermethylated read count in the region as aforementioned. These selected target regions were used as the differential markers to quantify tumor signals in the dilution series. Using the dilution series, the tumor signal detection was assessed in two quantitative ways: 1) The limit of detection (LOD) of the assay was evaluated by comparing the Tumor Signal (abbreviated as TS) i.e., the number of reads accumulated over 5,000 differential target regions, at every dilution series sample against in the six control samples (i.e., controlsWBC). Specifically, for each sample in the dilution series, we calculated a z-score with respect to controlsWBC, i.e., z=TS-mean(TScontrolsWBC)standard deviation(TScontrolsWBC). The LOD was then assessed by the Student’s t-tests between the z-scores at every nonzero dilution ratio and the z-scores at 0% dilution ratio. 2) The quantitative accuracy of the assay was evaluated by the correspondence between the observed and the expected tumor fractions (SI Appendix, section S6).

Marker Selection and Predictive Model for Cancer Detection.

We used 189 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples that passed quality control criteria (control region coverage >20 and CHH methylation <10%) and did not overlap with donors of plasma samples used in this study. To identify the tumor markers of the MethylScan data, we calculated differences between the tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples using the normalized hypermethylation read counts (denoted as RC) for each paired sample at the i-th region, i.e., Δ(regioni)=RCtumor(regioni)-RCadjacentnormal(regioni) and counted the number of differential tissue pairs whose Δregioni is significantly high (i.e., ≥0.1), which is denoted as freqΔ≥0.1(regioni). For each cancer type, we sorted all target regions by freqΔ≥0.1(regioni) in descending order and then selected the top N target regions with the largest number of differential tissue pairs as the differential regions. In the final result, we combined the top N = 5,000 differential target regions of each cancer type as the final marker set of MethylScan data for cancer detection, resulting in 15,155 markers. To mitigate overfitting, we applied singular value decomposition (SVD), a widely used dimensionality reduction technique (34, 35), to the training data, and reduced the feature set from 15,155 to 500 to enhance model generalizability. We used plasma samples in the training set to train the Linear Support Vector Machine (LSVM) classifier with the L2 penalty. For the study of liver cancer detection for the high-risk population, we used the same marker discovery strategy in the 78 pairs of liver tumors and adjacent normal liver tissues and identified the top N = 5,000 differential target regions as the liver-cancer-specific markers. We then applied the SVD to reduce the feature set from 5,000 to 100 and employed the LSVM with the L2 penalty. Performance evaluations of this study and other studies refer to SI Appendix, section S7.

Marker Selection and Predictive Model for TOO Prediction.

We used 189 tumor samples of four cancer types (liver, lung, ovarian, and stomach cancers) that passed quality control criteria (control region coverage >20 and CHH methylation <10%) in this study. The popular empirical Bayes method, which moderates the SE of the estimated log-fold changes (30) (R package limma) was used to identify those target regions that can differentiate between one cancer type and all others (one-vs-rest) and between any two pairs of cancer types (one-vs-one). The final marker set combines all these identified regions, totaling 6,430 markers. To mitigate overfitting, we applied SVD to the training data, and reduced the feature set from 6,430 to 300 to enhance model generalizability. We used the plasma samples in the training set to train the one-vs-rest multiclass LSVM classifier with the L2 penalty.

Marker Discovery and Predictive Model for Classifying Multiple Liver Diseases.

Unlike cancer detection, which can utilize tissue biopsies for marker discovery, obtaining tissue biopsies for chronic liver diseases is challenging. Therefore, we used plasma samples in the training set to identify differential markers between liver diseases. Specifically, the empirical Bayes method, which moderates the SE of the estimated log-fold changes (30) (R package limma) was used to identify those target regions that can differentiate between one liver disease and all other diseases. The final marker set combines all these identified regions. Conducting 50 runs of the stratified fivefold cross validations, 709 markers on average across 50 runs were obtained. To mitigate overfitting, we applied SVD to the training data, and reduced the feature set from to 100 to enhance model generalizability. We then train the multiclass one-vs-rest Linear Support Vector Machine with the L2 penalty.

Marker Discovery and Tissue Deconvolution for Disease Detection.

We utilized our previously generated reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) data for 521 tissue samples of noncancer participants from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) and UCLA hospitals (EGAS00001007213) to identify tissue-specific methylation markers (3). Note that the tissue samples in our previous study and the plasma samples in this study were profiled using two different assays which resulted in the data difference. To accommodate the MethylScan assay, we harmonized the fragment-level methylation information from RRBS assay as a MethylScan like format. Specifically, for each target region in the MethylScan assay, we counted the overlapping fragments with fully methylated MSRE cutting sites and an α-value of ≥0.8. Then we normalized the fragment counts in each target region as the count per million reads in the RRBS data. Then we selected tissue-specific methylation markers using 1) one-tissue-vs-another-tissue strategy and 2) one-tissue-vs-the-rest strategy. For each strategy, we compared the methylation signals between two groups (namely positive and negative groups) of tissue samples. We regarded a region as a candidate marker if the normalized fragment counts in the negative group was zero. Then we ranked the candidate markers by the percentage of samples in the positive group which have positive normalized fragment counts. We selected the top 200 markers from every comparison in the one-tissue-vs-another-tissue strategy and the one-tissue-vs-the-rest strategy. In total, 18,317 target regions were selected as tissue markers for tissue deconvolution. For each plasma sample, we deconvoluted its cfDNA tissue fractions using nonnegative least square estimation (SI Appendix, section S8).

Marker Discovery and Predictive Model for Race Prediction.

We performed stratified fivefold cross-validation to ensure an even distribution of race and age across the folds. Within each training set, differential markers between races were identified using the empirical Bayes method, which moderates the SE of the estimated log-fold changes (30) (R package limma). Specifically, target regions that distinguished one race from all others were selected. The final marker set combined these identified regions. Conducting 50 runs of the stratified fivefold cross validations, 336 markers on average across 50 runs were obtained. We then train the multiclass one-vs-rest Linear Support Vector Machine with the L2 penalty.
