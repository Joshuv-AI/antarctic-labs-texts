Functional analysis of FH variants of uncertain significance using Caenorhabditis elegans model
Authors: Suzanne Ferris, Ivette M Menendez Perdomo, Maja Tarailo-Graovac
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVariants of uncertain significance (VUS) in the essential metabolic gene fumarase (FH) have hampered diagnosis for patients with FH-associated diseases. Yet, early intervention is crucial for disease management, making the accurate and timely interpretation of VUS a clinical priority. Utilizing the multicellular model Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), with a highly conserved fumarase ortholog (FUM-1), we aimed to facilitate functional analysis and interpretation of clinical FH variants.MethodsWe used CRISPR-Cas9 techniques to generate C. elegans strains homozygous for FH variants reported in ClinVar and then assessed the effects on the animal’s development.ResultsC. elegans strains homozygous for a known benign variant did not show any visible phenotypes, whereas strains containing edited pathogenic variants could not survive. Assessment of 20 FH missense VUS revealed that majority (75%) did not display a visible phenotype under different conditions, whereas the rest affected viability and could therefore be reclassified as pathogenic.ConclusionWe showed that C. elegans could be reliably used for the reclassification of VUS in FH. Importantly, our proposed methodology based on the nematode’s quick generation cycle, ease of strain creation, and phenotypic readouts opens the door for future studies on FH using C. elegans, including genotype-phenotype correlation and background effects.Keywords: C.elegans, Clinical variant classification, CRISPR, FH/fum-1, VUS

Introduction

Dysfunctional fumarase, an essential metabolic enzyme responsible for energy production and genome maintenance, results in severe, rare metabolic disease and cancer. In humans, it is encoded by the gene FH (HGNC:3700), which produces mitochondrial and cytosolic isoforms.1 Mitochondrial fumarase metabolizes malate to progress the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.2 Cytosolic fumarase is translocated into the nucleus to resolve DNA damage by triggering a repair pathway through fumarate generation.1 Carriers of heterozygous FH variants are predisposed to develop the autosomal dominant disease hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer (HLRCC; OMIM 150800).3 HLRCC is characterized by benign tumors on the uterus and skin and a highly aggressive renal cancer. Furthermore, homozygous or compound heterozygous FH variants result in the autosomal recessive disease, fumarase deficiency (FD; OMIM 606812), characterized by brain abnormalities, developmental delays, early-onset hypotonia, seizures, and early childhood death.4 Currently, there are no treatments for either of the diseases, but management plans are available. For example, individuals with HLRCC undergo regular scans and surgeries to regulate tumor metastasis and prevent disease worsening.5 Typical tests performed to indicate FH deficiency are measurements of FH activity throughout different cell types, quantification of fumarate levels in urine, and detection of stable FH with immunohistochemistry.6,7 However, patients have displayed variable results not always concordant with typical fumarase dysfunction; thus, genetic testing is needed to confirm diagnosis.6,8,9 Because each disease is highly aggressive, timely diagnosis is needed to provide patients with their needed care.

Genetic testing as the primary evaluation method for FH disease diagnosis has uncovered many ambiguous variants. Clinical variant databases, such as ClinVar,10 document variant information such as significance (pathogenic/likely pathogenic, benign/likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance [VUS]) and associated phenotype to provide clinicians’ insight for patient diagnosis.11 VUS are the variants that can neither be classified as pathogenic nor benign because of lack of evidence.11 In this regard, FH VUS make up ∼73% of documented missense variants in ClinVar10 (Figure 1). Therefore, VUS pose a major issue on the clinical front, delaying or preventing diagnosis and treatment.

Proportion of FH missense variants of each clinical significance documented in ClinVar10 [accessed December 2024].

The exponential rise in VUS,10 attributed by growing genetic data collection, created a pressing need for their rapid interpretation. Multiplex assays of variant effect (MAVEs) are a high-throughput variant interpretation technique that utilizes single-cell models to assess pathogenicity through simple functional readouts.12 This technique assesses thousands of variants in parallel but the limitation of cellular and physiological context for complex interpretation and the lack of standardized clinical guidelines has prevented their integration into clinical databases and practice.13 Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) is a hermaphroditic multicellular animal model with a generation time of 3 days that has previously been used to interpret human variants through genetic induction and phenotypic observation.14,15 The orthologous gene fum-1 in worms encodes fumarase, an enzyme functionally conserved to FH taking part in the TCA cycle, fumarate metabolism, and malate metabolism. Knockdown of fum-1 using RNAi results in maternal sterility, larval arrest, and embryonic lethality, indicating gene essentiality required for the development and survival of C. elegans.16,17

We aimed to use C. elegans as a high-throughput multicellular model for FH disease modeling to determine the pathogenicity of missense FH VUS obtained from ClinVar.10 CRISPR-Cas9 generated homozygous strains were assessed with different phenotypic assays at restrictive temperatures. Model robustness was verified because known pathogenic and benign variants had the expected phenotypic outcomes in worms. A total of 20 VUS were analyzed: 15 displayed no significant phenotypic difference when compared with wild-type (WT) worms, whereas 5 caused various severe phenotypes, some leading to lethality, confirming pathogenicity. This study shows that C. elegans is an excellent model for the assessment of variants related to fumarase-associated diseases.

Materials and Methods

Worm maintenance

Worms were kept in standard laboratory conditions on nematode growth media agar plates spread with a lawn of OP50 Escherichia coli (E. coli) and maintained at 15 °C.18 Nematode growth media plates were prepared by adding 10.5 g of NaCl, 8.75 g of peptone, and 59.5 g of agar into 3.5 L of distilled water. Solution was autoclaved and 3.5 mL 1 M CaCl2, 3.5 mL of 5 mg/mL cholesterol, 3.5 mL of 1 M MgSO4, and 87.5 mL of 1 M K2PO4 were then added. Worms were replated weekly to avoid starvation and frozen stocks were thawed every 4 to 6 months to avoid de novo variant accumulation. Strains were genotyped using mutant-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or crude celery juice extract (CJE) digestion on flanking PCR products on genomic DNA. In celery, a single-strand-specific (sss) nuclease, CEL1, targets and cleaves mismatch sites, allowing for variant analysis.19 CJE preparation and digestion were performed similarly to Till et al.19 Briefly, in 10 μL of hybridized PCR product, 1 μL of CJE and 1.3 μL of 10× digestion buffer (37.5 mL water, 5 μL 20mg mL−1 BSA, 100 μL 10% Triton X-100 (vol/vol), 2.5 mL 2 M KCL, 5 mL 1 M HEPES pH 7.5, and 5 mL 1 M MgSO4) were added directly into the PCR tube. After incubation for 30 minutes at 45 °C, digestion is stopped by adding 2 μL of 0.225M EDTA. Homozygotes were identified by mixing 5 μL of WT and mutant PCR products together to create the heteroduplex during hybridization. Cleavage was visualized through gel electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel. Primer information is found in Supplemental Table 1.

CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing and microinjection

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing methodologies were used to knock in all missense variants.20 The guide RNAs (gRNAs) were generated using the databases UCSC (University of California, Santa Cruz) genome browser21 and CHOPCHOP22 and chosen based on location relative to the target site and efficiency score (Supplemental Table 2). Single stranded oligo donor nucleotides (ssODNs) containing the knockin variant were designed using the 100 base pairs of DNA sequence surrounding the variant (Supplemental Table 2). Constructs were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (https://www.idtdna.com/pages).

A microinjecting technique was used to transfect the variants into the worm’s genome. Using the WT N2 strain, young adults were injected with a microinjection mix into the rachis, a structure that contains immature germ cells, to ensure incorporation of the mutated DNA into the nuclei of oocytes.23 The microinjection mix contained 3.5 μL of 10 μM preannealed gRNA and tracrRNA, 0.5 μL of 10 μg/μL Cas9 (Integrated DNA Technologies; ID: 1081058), and 1 μL of 100 μM ssODN repair template for the variant of interest, as well as a coinjection marker construct for dpy-10(cn64) for which 1 μL of 10 μM preannealed gRNA and tracrRNA and 1 μL of 100 μM ssODN repair template for this variant were added. dpy-10(cn64) is heterozygous dominant and causes a visual roller phenotype24 that allows for the confirmation that gene editing had taken place.

Two microinjected worms (P0) were plated per plate. Rollers (F1) found were plated individually and allowed to propagate (F2). Rollers (F1) were genotyped using either standard PCR with mutant-specific primers or specific flanking primers for CJE digestion to identify variant presence. At least 8 progenies (F2) at early larval stages from heterozygous lines (F1) were plated individually, grown at 20 °C, and genotyped once again using PCR with WT-specific primers or specific flanking primers for CJE digestion to isolate homozygotes (Supplemental Table 1). Homozygotes were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Information on all strains used in this study is found in Supplemental Table 3.

Balancing and assessing lethal alleles

All variants that had a fully penetrant lethal phenotype (fum-1(Ser149Ile), fum-1(Ser356Gly), fum-1(Thr225Ala), fum-1(Gly388Arg), and fum-1(Ala466Glu)) were balanced against an hT2 balancer chromosome (hT2 [bli-4(e937) let-?(q782) qIs48]) that is linked to a GFP marker for maintenance in a stable heterozygous state. To then assess the lethal alleles, 1 L4 VC1497 (fum-1(ok1998) III/hT2 [bli-4(e937) let-?(q782) qIs48] (I;III)) control and 3 balanced L4 heterozygotes (GFP tagged) were plated individually (plate A) and were replated after 24 hours (plate B). Afterward, roughly every 10 to 15 hours, the parental worm was replated until it no longer laid eggs (plate H). Every time the parental worm was removed from the plate, the number of eggs laid was counted. 12 to 24 hours after removal, unhatched eggs were counted to document embryonic viability. Three days after removal, viable heterozygotes (GFP tagged) and homozygotes (no GFP signal) were counted, and homozygotes were plated separately and individually to ensure phenotypes. Four days after removal, when all worms should be fully grown, heterozygotes and homozygotes were imaged, and their length was measured. Seven days after removal, the homozygotes were observed to determine if all worms displayed the same phenotype to indicate complete penetrance. Plates were kept at 20 °C at all times.

The length of the worms was quantified using the tool ImageJ.25 Using the straight-line tool, a millimeter on a ruler is measured 3 times to get the average conversion rate of pixels to millimeters. The segmented tool is drawn along the middle of the worm’s body from head to tail to get its length in pixels, and the conversion rate is used to calculate its length in millimeters. Magnification for all images taken is the same.

Maturation assay

Rate at which weaker strains reached full maturity relative to N2 were assessed to accommodate strain growth in further experiments to ensure accuracy. One young adult was plated individually and was left to lay eggs for 15 hours. Plates were checked every 24 hours. The time at which all possible eggs had hatched, when the majority of worms were L4s, and when all possible worms were adults were marked down. Plates were kept at 20 °C at all times.

Temperature sensitivity assay (TSA)

To assess if the allele causes temperature sensitivity, progeny of variant homozygotes were incubated at 20 °C and 25 °C alongside N2. Strains were grown at 25 °C because this is on the higher end of the worm’s temperature tolerance. One L4 larva plated individually was incubated at 20 °C and 25 °C. After 1 week, 5 L4 larvae from the incubated plate were plated onto a new plate to combat starvation. After 1 more week, plates were observed, and temperature sensitivity was scored by strain propagability. No propagation was determined by complete sterility or complete F1 larval arrest. Mild propagation was observed on plates with <20 adult worms. High propagation was observed by plates featuring worms of multiple generations. (Replicates n = 5)

Brood size and viability assay (BSVA)

On day 1, the L4s were plated individually (plate A) and incubated at 20 °C and 25 °C to monitor their fertility and progeny viability. Every 24 hours the parental worm is transferred to the next plate (B to E) until the worm no longer lays eggs. At 24, 48, and 72 or 96 hours after parental worm placements, the number of eggs laid, unhatched eggs, and adults, respectively, present were counted to calculate the total brood size, embryonic, and larval viability. (Replicates n = 6 to 17). However, we waited an extra 24 hours for fum-1(Tyr59Cys) and fum-1(Leu123Ser) to reach adulthood because of developmental delay.

Results

Known pathogenic FH variants cause lethality in C. elegans

To test whether C. elegans model could be used to assess FH variant effects, we first assessed variants classified as either pathogenic or benign in ClinVar.10 Multiple sequence alignment of the fumarase protein sequence created with TCoffee26 had shown fumarase is highly conserved through evolution (Supplemental Figure 1). We selected the variants that were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic with disease association to FD and/or HLRCC10 (Table 1). The selected variants [fum-1(Ser149Ile), fum-1(Ser356Gly), fum-1(Thr225Ala), and fum-1(Gly388Arg)] were in highly conserved regions of the protein sequence located in or near the active site of FH (Supplemental Figures 1 and 2). Beyond the 4 pathogenic variants, we also modeled 1 missense variant that alters an invariable amino acid that has been classified as benign, fum-1(Asp56Asn) (Supplemental Figure 1). A single benign variant was analyzed because of the limited selection of conserved benign variants listed on ClinVar.10

Next, we tested the 5 knockin alleles. We noticed immediately that fum-1(Asp56Asn) can be propagated as a homozygote, whereas the fum-1(Ser149Ile), fum-1(Ser356Gly), fum-1(Thr225Ala), and fum-1(Gly388Arg) cannot and needed to be balanced over the hT2 balancer. Analysis of the balanced alleles allowed us to determine that worms homozygous for the pathogenic genotypes corresponding to 3 alleles (fum-1(Ser149Ile), fum-1(Ser356Gly), and fum-1(Gly388Arg)) arrested at around the L3 stage with complete penetrance, similar to the KO allele (Figure 2A-C). On the other hand, the fum-1(Thr225Ala) displayed more variable lethal phenotypes. Namely, fum-1(Thr225Ala) also resulted in fully penetrant lethality, but the stages varied from embryonic arrest to occasionally reaching adult stage and laying some eggs but could not be propagated beyond the second generation (Figure 2A and D-H). Beyond these 4 pathogenic alleles, we further assessed the benign allele (fum-1(Asp56Asn)) which could be normally propagate as a homozygote at 20 °C. To further confirm its benign effect, propagation and viability were observed through a temperature sensitivity assay (TSA) at 25 °C incubation for 2 weeks, to ensure that the variant did not cause a temperature-sensitive (Ts) phenotype. Worms can be cultivated at a range of temperatures from 15 °C to 25 °C; however, a gene variant can result in a rare heat-sensitive allele, thus creating a strain that displays a detrimental phenotype when grown at a restrictive temperature.27 The strain was observed to have normal levels of propagation after 2 weeks of incubation. Next, we performed BSVAs at 20 °C and 25 °C, scoring total progeny and embryonic and larval viability. The benign strain was found to behave normally with no differences to WT N2 worms (Figure 3A-C). Therefore, we conclude that C. elegans can be used to discern between the alleles with no obvious phenotypic effect and pathogenic (lethal) alleles in FH.

The majority of the VUS have no phenotypic effects despite evolutionary conservation

There were 2 main protocols applied to select a pool of 20 missense FH VUS from ClinVar.10 First, a set of 9 VUS (clinvar_20230416.vcf.gz) was chosen based on (1) association to the HLRCC phenotype in both ClinVar10 and the somatic cancer variant database COSMIC,28 (2) variant localization in or near the active or monomeric interaction sites of homotetrameric FH (3E04)29 based on PyMOL30 visualization, and (3) prediction of disruptive effect based on AlphaFold.31 This resulted in the modeling of variants fum-1(Tyr59Cys), fum-1(Ala95Thr), fum-1(Ala185Thr), fum-1(Gly158Cys), fum-1(Gln228Arg), fum-1(Ala264Thr), fum-1(Ala285Val), fum-1(Ser325Gly), and fum-1(Pro500Leu). The second set of 9 VUS (clinvar_20240107.vcf.gz) was chosen from ClinVar10 based on the (1) presence of an FH-associated condition classification, (2) at least 1 logged case with an affected status of “yes,” and (3) amino acid conservation. This resulted in modeling of variants fum-1(Ile84Met), fum-1(Leu123Ser), fum-1(Gly236Arg), fum-1(Gly293Asp), fum-1(Asn320Ser), fum-1(Gly345Glu), fum-1(Ser390Gly), fum-1(Ala463Thr), and fum-1(Ala466Glu). Two conserved variants modeled were selected at random: fum-1(Ile153Thr) and fum-1(Pro494Arg). A total of 20 FH VUS were induced into the C. elegans fum-1 ortholog using CRISPR-Cas9 to get homozygous strains and observe their phenotypic effect (Table 1, Supplemental Figure 1).

Similar to the above assessment of the benign and pathogenic variants, we were able to immediately notice that 3 variants [fum-1(Tyr59Cys), fum-1(Leu123Ser), and fum-1(Ala466Glu)] were difficult to maintain in a homozygous state due to lethality. The majority of the variants, however, could be propagated at 20 °C. To be certain that the 17 homozygous strains, indeed, have no effect on viability, we next tested development using several phenotypic assays. First, to observe if any of the 17 alleles result in conditional lethality, TSAs were performed. The method applied is as previously described, observing strain propagation at 20 °C and 25 °C after a 2-week incubation period. All of the 17 VUS strains had normal propagation rates, such as N2 and the benign alleles, across both permissive and restrictive temperatures (Supplemental Table 4). Next, BSVAs were done to quantify the variant’s effects on strain fertility and viability. It was observed that 15 of the 17 strains were comparable to N2 (Supplemental Table 4), supporting no developmental effect at tested conditions. Therefore, we concluded that 15 of the 20 tested VUS had no visible phenotypes and developed similar to WT worms, suggesting that these are either benign or have mild effects not captured using our developmental and viability assays.

C. elegans models reveal 5 new pathogenic alleles

As noted above, 3 of the variants fum-1(Tyr59Cys), fum-1(Leu123Ser), and fum-1(Ala466Glu) immediately showed difficulty being maintained in the homozygous state and were therefore balanced over the hT2 balancer to prevent the loss of the knockin alleles. Detailed analysis of the balanced strains revealed that fum-1(Tyr59Cys) and fum-1(Leu123Ser) were sluggish and sick, getting sicker with every generation in a homozygous state, developing slower than WT but still capable of propagating. Both strains displayed developmental delays because these were found to reach the L4 and adult stage 24 hours later than N2 (Figure 4A). On the other hand, the homozygotes for the fum-1(Ala466Glu) arrested as larvae, never reaching adult stage (Figure 4B), demonstrating complete lethality comparable to the KO allele. Furthermore, analyses at different temperatures revealed that the brood size of strains fum-1(Gln228Arg) and fum-1(Ala264Thr) was reduced at 1 or both temperatures, 20 °C and 25 °C (Figure 4C), respectively, whereas overall strain viability of both fum-1(Tyr59Cys) and fum-1(Leu123Ser) was reduced significantly at 1 or both temperatures (Figure 4D-I). However, the variant effects were exacerbated for the fum-1(Leu123Ser) at the 25 °C, leading to inability to propagate long-term at 25 °C (Figure 4I).

Alongside functional analysis, further forms of evidence reinforce their pathogenic significance. Most of the variants, except FH(Gln237Arg), have been recently reported in HLRCC and/or paraganglioma-pheochromocytomas tumors32,33 and were shown to decrease or completely lose FH activity (Supplemental Table 5).33, 34, 35, 36, 37 The low population frequency (<1%)38 and likely pathogenic effect prediction from the in silico tool AlphaMissense39 (Supplemental Table 5) further point toward each of these 5 alleles having deleterious effects.

Overall, our results show that 5 of the VUS lead to quantifiable visible pathogenic phenotypes in C. elegans of varying severity. Although fum-1(Ala466Glu) shows similar phenotype to the KO allele, fum-1(Tyr59Cys), fum-1(Leu123Ser), fum-1(Gln228Arg), and fum-1(Ala264Thr) are hypomorphs resulting in milder, yet significant developmental defects, which, in the case of fum-1(Leu123Ser), are exacerbated by the temperature, resulting in failure of the strain to propagate.

Discussion

Dysfunctional fumarase causes rare metabolic disease and cancer that requires immediate care. Genetic testing for FH-associated diseases has become the primary form of diagnosis with VUS creating uncertainty for therapy administration. With the majority of ClinVar10 FH missense variants classified as VUS (Figure 1), it highlights an unmet need for a clinically reliable, rapid variant classification technique.

In this study, we selected C. elegans as a multicellular model to test FH variant interpretation due to enzyme conservation and organism characteristics. FH variants known to be benign or pathogenic in humans showed similar effects in C. elegans in which a benign variant did not noticeably affect the nematodes’ development, but the pathogenic ones resulted in lethality. This finding encouraged us to test a total of 20 VUS to assess their effect on C. elegans development. Although the majority (75%) of the C. elegans strains containing the same amino acid changes as the VUS identified in humans had no visible effects on development, 5 variants significantly affected viability and/or development. However, assessment of variant effect on enzyme function was limited to a temperature range suitable for C. elegans cultivation (20 °C to 25 °C). Thus, some alleles that appeared functional in worms may be in fact inactive at 37 °C. Namely, fum-1(Tyr59Cys), fum-1(Leu123Ser), fum-1(Gln228Arg), fum-1(Ala264Thr), and fum-1(Ala466Glu) resulted in varying degrees of pathogenicity, from fully penetrant arrest for fum-1(Ala466Glu), suggesting complete loss of function akin to the KO allele, to reduced brood size and viability, worsened by higher temperature in fum-1(Leu123Ser), suggesting hypomorphic effects of milder alleles.

It is important to note that most of the pathogenic VUS are around the FH active site (Table 1), potentially causing adverse effects due to enzymatic activity disruption. FH deficiency has been linked to infertility in women.40 Interestingly, beyond the overall development and viability, most of the pathogenic variants affected C. elegans fertility (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4). The disruption of the TCA cycle may be affecting the maturation of worm oocytes because low oocyte ATP levels have been associated with a reduction of their quality and competence.41,42 Rahman, Rosu, Joseph-Strauss, and Cohen-Fix43 found that the downregulation of the TCA cycle enzymes prevented the first mitotic cell-cycle division, which may be causing the partial embryonic arrest seen in viable strains fum-1(Tyr59Cys) and fum-1(Leu123Ser) (Figure 4E and H). In addition, impaired FUM-1 may have failed to repair DNA damage due to fumarase’s involvement in double-stranded break repair, accumulating variants over generations, resulting in the heat-sensitive progressive sterility and fitness decline of fum-1(Leu123Ser).

VUS classification hinders the clinical decision-making process, exposing patients to more uncertainty and limited access to care.44,45 In our hands, most of the VUS we analyzed did not visibly affect C. elegans development and may be very mild alleles that have no effects on worm development under the tested conditions or may be benign alleles, aligning with previous VUS reclassification data,44 suggesting a significant fraction of VUS may not be harmful. However, caution needs to be exercised when considering these in patients as further tests are needed before the variants that had no effects on worm development under the conditions presented here could be deemed benign. Importantly, the new knowledge on 5 pathogenic variants is likely to help eliminate the complication associated with their current VUS status in diagnosis and treatment of patients, thus further enforcing the importance of exploring new, reliable avenues for variant interpretation methods.

The short generational turnover, editability of the C. elegans genome, and easy to assess phenotypes provide an ideal system for the high-throughput interpretation of VUS, overcoming some bottlenecks currently faced with single-cell and transient methods, such as MAVEs.13 The single-cell aspect of MAVEs limits interpretation to a single cell type and exploration of affected cellular interactions, which is resolved by the multicellularity of worms that provides the overall effect on development of the organism, and hence multiple types of cells. The lack of physiological context weakens result confidence and also misses any complex, long-term effects, such as on development,46 which we observed in worms at different developmental stages. Furthermore, MAVEs’ short-term experimental nature limit the assessment of variant effects during extended periods, through multiple generations or different genetic backgrounds to assess for genetic modifier effects and hence penetrance and expressivity. Yet, phenotypic variability seen in FD and HLRCC patients, even in individuals carrying the same variant, suggests a potential genetic modifier, second site variant, which suppresses/enhances the disease phenotype.47,48 The heritability of created C. elegans strains allows for further analysis unlike single-cell models. Strains with complex phenotypes, such as the weakened phenotype of fum-1(Leu123Ser) at a restrictive temperature, can be utilized for suppressor identification with a high-throughput forward genetic screening process to rapidly identify genetic modifiers.49

Conclusion

This study aids in the advancement of fumarase-associated research and VUS reclassification. Establishing C. elegans as a robust model organism for FH allows for future larger-scale studies to probe additional VUS (Figure 1) and gain an understanding and insight into their impact. The evaluation of fum-1 VUS has expanded our understanding of patient relevant variants that will help improve the process of clinical diagnosis and medical care planning while also examining a new high-throughput tool for future variant classification. Furthermore, our work shows the advantages of the strains that we created in exploring genotype-phenotype correlations and variant effects in diverse genetic backgrounds, an opportunity not available in other model systems.
