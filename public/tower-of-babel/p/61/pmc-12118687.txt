Putting metabolism centre stage in health and disease
Authors: M K Montgomery
Published: 2024

Metabolism is essential for the optimal function of every cell in our body. Metabolic impairments impact the normal function of all body tissues and underpin some of the leading causes of death in our society. These include obesity, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and many cancers. The past two decades have seen a renewed interest in metabolism research which is reshaping our understanding of human physiology and pathophysiology. Advances in technologies have provided new insights into cellular metabolism and led to the development of new therapeutic strategies, including new or ‘refreshed’ approaches for cancer and cardiometabolic diseases. Combined capabilities across molecular, analytical and clinical sciences, together with targeted strategies for drug development, have underpinned both the pre-clinical discovery stage and clinical validation of many metabolism-targeting drugs.

Obesity is a major driving force for many cardiometabolic diseases, exacerbating the risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease, adversely impacting bone health, reproduction, increasing the risk of many cancers, and overall reducing an individuals’ quality of life. Adult obesity has more than doubled world-wide since 1990, and, even more worryingly, adolescent obesity has quadrupled1. Currently, more than 40% of adults are overweight or obese, with an estimated economic impact of ~2.2% of the global gross domestic product (GDP)2. Even more alarmingly, in countries such as the US, Mexico and Australia, more than 64% of adults are considered overweight or obese3, with no easing of this trend in sight. While lifestyle interventions and behaviour modification strategies are recommended by primary care clinicians, they frequently show low adherence.

The last decades have increased our understanding of the mechanisms underlying body weight regulation and the role of the gut-brain axis in appetite control, with hormone-based therapies such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists having shown promise in the clinic4. Semaglutide results in 15–17% mean body weight loss, and current strategies focus on combinations of GLP-1 and other entero-pancreatic hormones, including glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon4. In this respect, Tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1 and GIP receptor agonist, that results in up to 22% of weight loss5, has been recently approved (November 2023) for chronic weight management by the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency. The last years have shown that we are living in the era of rapidly evolving pharmacotherapy for weight management, with current trials achieving weight loss similar to what is observed after bariatric surgery, completely shifting our view of what is achievable in obesity treatment without the need for surgical intervention. Globally accessible and affordable weight loss pharmacotherapy would undoubtedly improve the lives of billions of people, alleviate a multitude of accompanying cardiometabolic health problems, and save the global economy billions of dollars.

The rapid increase in obesity prevalence is a primary contributor to the exponential growth of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Currently, more than 500 million people have diabetes (of those, 96% have T2D), reflecting 6.1% of the global adult population, with ~50% of T2D incidence being attributable to a high BMI6. Type 2 diabetes is a complex multifactorial disease with the molecular mechanisms often being cell/tissue specific and dependent on both environmental and genetic factors. A recent study published in Nature integrated multi-ancestry genome-wide association study (GWAS) data with single-cell epigenomics, and highlighted the heterogeneity that drives T2D pathogenesis, identifying more than 100 novel T2D loci and providing cluster-specific polygenic scores for cardiometabolic outcomes in T2D7. This study, among many others, re-defines the complexity of T2D and associated cardiometabolic health issues. Recently, a large-scale population-based study investigated the burden of diabetes across the life course in 46 million English adults, with some eye-opening insights8. An individual with diabetes will (likely) develop other cardiometabolic health problems 15-20 years earlier in life than the general population. In addition, by 50 years of age, one-third of individuals with diabetes have at least three other metabolic conditions, spend >20 years with them and die 11 years earlier than the general population8. These statistics highlight the urgent need for us to better understand diabetes pathogenesis and develop new therapies for diabetes, and even more important, multi-faceted glucose-plus therapies; that is, therapies that improve glycaemic control and concomitantly treat diabetes comorbidities.

While obesity and diabetes are global health issues that require a better understanding and improved treatment strategies, we cannot overlook the intricate inter-connects with other systemic or tissue-specific metabolic defects. These include, for example, MASLD and the more severe form MASH (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis), with MASLD prevalence reaching 70% among overweight individuals, and 55% among individuals with T2D9. These are alarming statistics given that MASH and liver fibrosis increase an individual’s risk in developing late-stage liver cirrhosis, liver cancer and contribute to all-cause mortality. Despite this increasing clinical epidemic, the first drug for MASH has only been provisionally FDA-approved in 2024 (resmetirom, Madrigal Pharmaceuticals10), and new strategies for advanced liver disease are urgently needed. Defective lipid metabolism in the liver is a major contributor to MASLD progression, and a better understanding of the fundamental mechanisms underlying dysfunctional lipid metabolism is likely to lead to the development of new treatment strategies for MASLD. Similarly, defective lipid metabolism has been shown as a contributing factor to peripheral neuropathy11, cancer and cancer drug resistance12,13, Alzheimer’s disease14, and cardiovascular disease15, among many other metabolism-associated pathologies.

In addition to the important role of metabolism in the above discussed health problems, its role in general physiology should not be overlooked. Metabolism plays a role in all aspects of life, with recent high-impact studies highlighting a role for metabolism all the way from understanding the evolution of endothermy16, to the role of bacterial metabolism in the health of honey bees17 and strategies to utilize new knowledge on plant-specific metabolic networks in the development of plant-derived pharmaceuticals18. It is evident that we are just scratching the surface in understanding metabolism and that the coming years will lead to ground-breaking developments in metabolic health and disease.

Here are npj Metabolic Health and Disease we aim to bring together researchers with expertise in breakthrough discovery and translational research in cellular metabolism, to ultimately generate fresh insight into how to tackle metabolic disorders, and to create an international community of research excellence in metabolism. npj Metabolic Health and Disease invites submissions on all aspects of metabolism, including the basic mechanisms of metabolism and metabolic diseases, as well as pre-clinical and clinical studies focussing on diagnostic tools, effects of interventions for the prevention and treatment of metabolic pathophysiology, epidemiology, public health, and global health.
