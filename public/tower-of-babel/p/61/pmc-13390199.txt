Mechanisms and reversal strategies of liver fibrosis: from regulation of cell fate to clinical translation
Authors: Chuhan Zeng, Qiuxia Lu, Qi Zhao, Jian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver fibrosis is a dynamic and reversible pathological process underlying chronic liver diseases, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix deposition and progressive hepatic architectural distortion. It acts as a critical precursor to cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma, imposing a substantial global disease burden.Main bodyAccumulating evidence indicates that liver fibrosis is a highly plastic process governed by multicellular crosstalk, immune microenvironment remodeling, epigenetic–metabolic coupling, and mechanotransduction. This review outlines core cellular effectors and their heterogeneity revealed by single-cell omics, and highlights key regulatory layers including circadian rhythm, epigenetic imprinting, metabolic reprogramming, and the gut–liver axis, as well as etiology-specific differences in fibrosis progression, reversibility, and therapeutic response. We also summarize advances in non-invasive diagnosis and clinical translation of anti-fibrotic therapies, and discuss key bottlenecks leading to clinical trial failures. ConclusionA deeper understanding of cell fate regulation and multicellular ecosystem remodeling will facilitate the development of precise strategies to achieve meaningful fibrosis regression and improve long-term clinical outcomes in chronic liver diseases.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, resulting in approximately 2 million deaths annually [1]. Liver fibrosis is one of the most decisive pathological processes in chronic liver diseases. Epidemiological evidence shows that nearly 20% of the global adult population has liver fibrotic changes at different stages, constituting a huge disease burden [2]. Moreover, liver fibrosis can gradually progress to liver cirrhosis and lead to serious complications such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver failure [3].

Pathologically, liver fibrosis is a maladaptive repair response to sustained hepatic injury from etiologies including viral infection, metabolic disorder, alcohol abuse, and bile stasis, characterized by abnormal extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation [4, 5].

Notably, extensive clinical and animal experimental studies have demonstrated that liver fibrosis is reversible under certain conditions [6–8]. With the rapid development of translational research, therapeutic strategies have evolved from etiological management to targeted interventions, including metabolic nuclear receptor modulation, anti-inflammatory approaches, inhibition of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) activation, as well as emerging epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming strategies [9]. This review summarizes the updated cellular and molecular mechanisms, immune microenvironment remodeling, and clinical translation advances in liver fibrosis. We aim to provide an integrated framework for developing multi-target and precision strategies to promote fibrosis regression and improve clinical outcomes.

Cellular mechanisms of liver fibrosis

Liver fibrosis is not driven by a single cellular event but results from coordinated interactions among multiple hepatic cell types under conditions of persistent injury and dysregulated repair. Hepatocyte injury serves as the initiating signal, triggering inflammatory cascades and the release of pro-fibrogenic mediators. Sustained activation of HSCs represents the central source of excessive ECM deposition. Meanwhile, macrophages, inflammatory cells, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells amplify inflammation, modulate immune responses, and promote pathological angiogenesis, collectively shaping a pro-fibrotic microenvironment that drives fibrous septa formation and progressive architectural remodeling of the liver.

Hepatocytes

In HSCs and Kupffer cells, accumulation of free fatty acids (FFAs) can activate c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and NF-κB pathways, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory and chemotactic factors, ultimately leading to hepatocyte necrosis or apoptosis [10]. Additionally, during bile stasis, accumulation of bile salts within the liver can induce oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and mitochondrial damage, exacerbating cell apoptosis [11]. Damaged hepatocytes produce substances that stimulate HSCs and inflammatory cells, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to activate Kupffer cells and HSCs, inducing inflammatory and fibrotic responses [12, 13].

Recent studies have also found that hepatocyte-specific CEBPA inhibits the transactivation of Spp1, reducing the secretion of osteopontin, a key factor in promoting HSCs fibrosis. This suggests that CEBPA plays a critical role in the progression of liver fibrosis [14].

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs)

In healthy livers, HSCs are in a quiescent state, distributed within the space of Disse in liver sinusoids. They exhibit a stellate morphology, with cytoplasm rich in lipid droplets and no obvious proliferative activity [15]. When the liver is stimulated by injury signals such as TGF-β, PDGF, ROS, or LPS, quiescent HSCs are induced into an activated state. During this process, HSCs gradually lose their typical stellate morphology and cytoplasmic lipid droplets, transitioning to a myofibroblast-like phenotype accompanied by significant upregulation of α-SMA expression. Simultaneously, they synthesize and secrete large amounts of type I and III collagen fibers and produce tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), thereby inhibiting ECM degradation and aggravating fibrous deposition [16]. Abnormal ECM deposition disrupts normal tissue remodeling mechanisms, keeping the wound healing process in a prolonged activated state, continuously stimulating fibrous tissue generation, and ultimately driving the sustained development of liver fibrosis [17, 18].

In general, activated HSCs are the primary cell type synthesizing ECM in the liver, accounting for approximately 90% of all ECM-producing myofibroblasts, playing a dominant role in fibrous tissue formation [19]. However, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have revealed significant functional heterogeneity within activated HSCs, implying that distinct subpopulations exhibit divergent responses to therapeutic intervention. Unsupervised clustering based on transcriptomic profiles has categorized HSCs into multiple functional subsets, with some primarily characterized by the high expression of collagen and pro-fibrotic genes, while others are associated with ferroptosis, inflammation, or metabolic reprogramming [20]. Furthermore, these subpopulations demonstrate marked differences in their pathogenic potency and driving pathways [20]. Such specificity in response suggests that single-target interventions typically address only specific functional modules, making it difficult to comprehensively reverse the complex fibrotic network. Consequently, the future paradigm of precision medicine should shift from “broad-spectrum activation inhibition” toward the identification and selective targeting of high-pathogenic subpopulations that drive disease progression.

Portal fibroblasts (PFs) and cholangiocytes

Although HSCs are the primary source of ECM, portal fibroblasts (PFs) exert an indispensable priming role, particularly in cholestatic fibrosis [21]. As resident stromal components of the portal tract, PFs exhibit heightened sensitivity to biliary injury, initiating proliferation and activation prior to HSCs during the early stages of disease. By constructing the initial periportal fibrous scaffold, PFs establish the pathological foundation for subsequent parenchymal damage. Concurrently, the role of cholangiocytes in the fibrotic microenvironment has evolved from passive targets of injury to pivotal signaling regulators. Under chronic biliary pressure or inflammatory stimuli, damaged cholangiocytes undergo a “ductular reaction” and secrete an array of pro-fibrotic cytokines, such as TGF-β2, PDGF, and various chemokines. These molecules act via paracrine signaling to directly recruit and activate adjacent PFs and HSCs [22]. Furthermore, the potential of cholangiocytes to undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) under specific conditions further diversifies the sources of matrix-producing cells. This multicellular communication network is profoundly regulated by matrix stiffness-mediated mechanical transduction [23]. Local stiffening resulting from ECM deposition activates intracellular signaling via integrin receptors, which not only reinforces the profibrogenic phenotype of HSCs but also induces pro-inflammatory feedback from PFs and cholangiocytes, creating a reciprocal “mechanobiological” loop [22]. At a systemic level, this intrahepatic network interacts with the host’s immunometabolic status through the gut-liver axis and immune cell recruitment, collectively driving the persistent evolution of fibrosis [24].

Macrophages

During the early stages of liver injury, resident macrophages (Kupffer cells) are the first to be activated, releasing chemokines such as CCL2 and CCL5 to recruit monocyte-derived macrophages (MoMFs) into the damaged site [25]. Traditionally, macrophages were simplistically categorized into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the pro-fibrotic M2 phenotype [26, 27]. However, within the complex hepatic pathological environment, their phenotypes actually represent a highly dynamic continuum.

As the inflammatory milieu evolves, macrophage function extends beyond the mere release of TNF-α or TGF-β, exhibiting pronounced context-dependency. During the progressive phase of fibrosis, specific subpopulations induce the activation of HSCs via paracrine signaling. Conversely, during the resolution phase following the removal of the primary etiology, macrophages facilitate critical matrix degradation and tissue repair by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and clearing cellular debris [28]. This functional transition—from triggering inflammatory cascades to reconstructing tissue homeostasis—highlights the complexity of macrophages as central regulatory hubs in the progression of liver fibrosis. Their refined classification under high-resolution omics (such as scar-associated macrophages, SAMs) and their spatial distribution characteristics will be discussed in depth in Sect. Remodeling of the Immune Microenvironment and Multicellular Heterogeneity of this review.

Myofibroblasts (MFs)

In healthy livers, typical myofibroblasts (MFs) do not exist [29]. MFs are an acquired phenotype that emerges during chronic liver injury repair, serving as the ultimate effector cells leading to massive collagen synthesis and pathological ECM accumulation [30].

As core effector cells in the fibrotic microenvironment, MFs not only produce matrix but also actively participate in inflammation maintenance and vascular remodeling. MFs can secrete various pro-inflammatory factors (such as CCL2, CCL21, and IL-1β), regulating the functions of innate and adaptive immune cells, maintaining a chronic inflammatory microenvironment [31–33]. Additionally, liver MFs are key regulators of pathological angiogenesis, secreting mediators such as VEGF-A, EGF-BB, and angiopoietin-1/2, and expressing corresponding receptors to directly respond to hypoxic signals [34–36]. This hypoxia-dependent angiogenesis often synchronizes with fibrosis progression.

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs)

In normal livers, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) are in a highly differentiated state, exhibiting significant anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and tissue regenerative functions [37]. Unlike endothelial cells in other tissues, LSECs possess high permeability while retaining unique “sieve” pores. It retains the unique “fence” pores, featuring typical porous structures and the absence of a basement membrane. It can inhibit the activation of HSCs by secreting paracrine factors such as nitric oxide (NO), maintain their quiescent state, and thereby prevent the occurrence of fibrosis [38, 39].

In fibrotic livers of humans and rats, upon liver damage, LSECs undergo capillarization, manifested by reduced fenestrae, basement membrane formation, and phenotypic changes. This process weakens their ability to inhibit HSCs, instead promoting their transition to myofibroblast-like phenotypes, enhancing ECM synthesis and deposition [40].

Inflammatory cells

In the early stages of liver tissue injury, chemotactic factors and fibrosis-related cytokines released by damaged cells attract HSCs and neutrophils to the injury site. Neutrophils, as frontline defense cells in the body’s stress response, first participate in clearing apoptotic hepatocytes and releasing inflammatory signals. Persistent liver tissue injury leads to damaged hepatocytes releasing large amounts of ROS and various danger signal molecules, thereby inducing immune system responses and activating inflammatory effector cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes.

Among them, hepatic macrophages (including resident Kupffer cells and peripheral blood-derived monocytes) are activated upon recognition of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and then secrete various proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, as well as CCL2 and CXCL10. These factors further facilitate the infiltration of neutrophils and monocytes. Meanwhile, reactive oxygen species (ROS) released by neutrophils amplify local inflammatory responses, which together with the cytokine network form a self-amplifying inflammatory loop, ultimately driving the initiation and progression of liver fibrosis [41].

Molecular signaling pathways in liver fibrosis

The initiation and progression of liver fibrosis are governed by coordinated activation of multiple signaling pathways that intersect across distinct hepatic cell types. These pathways collectively drive HSCs activation, amplify inflammatory responses, and promote excessive ECM deposition. Among them, the TGF-β pathway represents the central pro-fibrotic signaling axis, PDGF signaling primarily regulates HSCs proliferation and migration, and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway contributes to cell fate determination and maintenance of the fibrogenic phenotype. TGF-β signaling pathway

In liver fibrosis progression, TGF-β exerts its effects mainly through two signaling axes: the classic Smad-dependent pathway and non-classic, non-Smad pathways such as MAPK and PI3K/Akt. TGF-β stored in ECM exists mainly in latent complex form, with its activation depending primarily on protease cleavage or integrin-mediated mechanical signals [42, 43].Upon liver injury, damaged hepatocytes, macrophages, and platelets secrete large amounts of TGF-β. Meanwhile, local hepatic proteases (e.g. plasmin and matrix metalloproteinases) cleave TGF-β latent complexes in the ECM, releasing bioactive TGF-β.The activated TGF-β binds to TGF-β type Ⅱ receptors (TGF-βRⅡ) on the surface of HSCs, which further recruits and phosphorylates TGF-β type Ⅰ receptors (TGF-βRⅠ), thereby activating downstream Smad2/3 molecules. Phosphorylated Smad2/3 forms complexes with Smad4, translocates into the nucleus, binds to the promoter regions of target genes, and upregulates the expression of pro-fibrotic genes such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and type Ⅰ/Ⅲ collagens, ultimately driving the transdifferentiation of quiescent HSCs into myofibroblast-like phenotypes.As a complementary pathway to the canonical Smad-dependent cascade, the non-Smad pathway (including the MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling axes) amplifies pro-fibrotic effects. Specifically, the MAPK pathway enhances the transcriptional activity of Smad complexes by phosphorylating Smad co-proteins, while the PI3K/Akt pathway inhibits HSCs apoptosis, prolongs cell survival, and thus sustains ECM synthesis and deposition.

Recent systems biology perspectives have revealed key crosstalk between liver circadian clocks (CC) and the TGF-β signaling pathway. In healthy livers, CC oscillators finely regulate rhythmic expression of TGF-β target genes; in metabolic injury states like Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH), CC rhythm disruptions significantly enhance TGF-β signaling activity, promoting fibrosis progression. In-depth studies confirm that CC components (e.g. REV-ERBα) negatively regulate the TGF-β pathway, with its specific agonist SR9009 demonstrating significant anti-fibrotic efficacy in multiple models. These findings not only elucidate the new role of circadian rhythms as an intrinsic regulatory network but also establish its core components as highly promising therapeutic targets, providing a theoretical basis for chronotherapy in liver fibrosis [44]. It should be noted that current findings are confined to preclinical stages and have not yet been validated in human clinical trials [44]. Therefore, the future development of novel circadian regulators with superior pharmacodynamic profiles and tissue specificity remains critical for bridging the gap between fundamental research and clinical translation of chronotherapy in liver diseases.

PDGF signaling pathway

Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) plays a central role in liver fibrosis by regulating cell proliferation, migration, and ECM synthesis [45, 46]. Its four subunits (PDGF-A, -B, -C, -D) form five dimers, with PDGF-B being a key factor initiating early HSCs activation, while PDGF-C/-D dominate later fibrosis formation [47–50]. In liver injury environments, PDGF is provided not only by platelets and recruited Kupffer cells but also by activated HSCs themselves through autocrine mechanisms via surface-expressed PDGF receptors (PDGFR), amplifying activation signals [51, 52].

Recent studies reveal that hepatocyte-expressed Ninjurin2 plays a central role in regulating HSCs activation, and Ninjurin2 has been identified as an important upstream factor promoting the activation of PDGF signaling. Its mechanism involves Ninjurin2 interacting with IGF1R to initiate the PI3K-AKT-EGR1 signaling cascade within hepatocytes, significantly promoting expression and secretion of the mitogen PDGF-BB. Released extracellular PDGF-BB acts in a paracrine manner on adjacent HSCs, activating surface PDGFRβ to strongly drive HSCs activation and proliferation, ultimately leading to excessive ECM deposition. This pathway elucidates Ninjurin2 as a molecular bridge linking hepatocyte injury and HSCs activation, providing a theoretical basis for its targeted therapy [53].

Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway primarily depends on the stability control of β-catenin protein. In the absence of Wnt signaling, a “degradation complex” composed of GSK-3β, Axin, and other proteins guides β-catenin to proteasomal degradation through sequential phosphorylation and ubiquitination; when Wnt ligands bind to membrane receptors Frizzled/LRP, this degradation process is effectively inhibited, allowing β-catenin to stably accumulate in the cytoplasm and translocate to the nucleus. Nuclear β-catenin further assembles into a transcriptional complex with TCF/LEF family transcription factors and co-activators such as histone acetyltransferases p300/CBP, driving expression programs of multiple pro-fibrotic genes including α-SMA [54].

In recent years, research on the regulatory mechanisms of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in liver fibrosis has achieved new breakthroughs. Studies indicate that Aurora kinase A (AURKA) promotes HSCs activation and exacerbates liver fibrosis development by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, suggesting AURKA as a potential therapeutic target for liver fibrosis [55]. On the other hand, cell therapy studies show that human amniotic mesenchymal stem cells (hAMSCs) derived from amniotic membranes can secrete active factors such as IGFBP3, DKK-3, and DKK-1, which effectively block the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and significantly inhibit HSCs activation. In animal models, hAMSCs significantly alleviate mouse liver fibrosis and improve liver function indicators through this mechanism, providing new ideas and experimental evidence for cell therapy in liver fibrosis [56].

Pathological stratification and reversibility of liver fibrosis based on etiological heterogeneity

Although the progression of liver fibrosis shares common cellular pathways, its initiation, spatial distribution, immune microenvironment, and reversibility trajectories are profoundly driven by primary etiologies, exhibiting significant heterogeneity.

Viral hepatitis

Fibrosis driven by viral hepatitis (particularly HBV and HCV) primarily originates in the portal area (Zone 1), resulting from the interplay between host immune responses and direct viral interference. In addition to the release of DAMPs from virus-induced hepatocyte apoptosis, sustained antiviral immune responses are considered the core driver of ECM production. In chronic infection, Kupffer cells and lymphocytes continuously secrete pro-fibrotic cytokines and chemokines under immune pressure [57–59]. HCV exhibits clear direct pathogenicity: its core protein induces HSCs proliferation via the Ras/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways; NS3/NS5 proteins activate the NF-κB axis to amplify inflammation; and the envelope protein E2 binds to CD81 on the HSCs surface to activate MMP2, exacerbating parenchymal damage by remodeling the matrix structure [60, 61]. In contrast, while the direct effect of HBV on HSCs remains debated, the encoded HBx protein is a key mediator that induces hepatocyte overexpression of SATB1, promoting HSCs activation through paracrine CTGF and PDGF signaling [62]. Furthermore, HBV possesses unique immunomodulatory features, such as the ability of HBx to directly activate regulatory T cells (Tregs), maintaining chronic inflammation by remodeling the intrahepatic immune microenvironment [63, 64].

From the perspective of reversibility, viral hepatitis demonstrates the highest clinical potential for regression. Following the eradication of HCV by direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) or the long-term suppression of HBV by nucleoside analogs, the rapid resolution of inflammation often leads to significant histological improvement [65, 66]. However, late-stage patients face an “epigenetic bottleneck”: long-term infection-induced epigenetic remodeling (pathogenic imprinting) may persist even after viral clearance, leading to incomplete fibrosis reversal and a sustained high risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [67, 68].

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)

In the progression of MASH, liver fibrosis typically manifests as a “chicken-wire” pattern centered in Zone 3 of the hepatic lobule, a process deeply driven by systemic metabolic disorders. Mechanistically, lipotoxicity induced by insulin resistance is the initiating factor. The accumulation of saturated fatty acid metabolites triggers endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and hepatocyte injury. Subsequently, DAMPs released by damaged hepatocytes activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in Kupffer cells and myeloid cells via Toll-like receptors (TLRs). The resulting burst of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18, constructs a persistent pro-fibrotic microenvironment and induces HSCs activation [69–72]. Additionally, PAMP translocation mediated by gut dysbiosis further exacerbates the intrahepatic inflammatory cascade [73]. Notably, chronic oxidative stress in MASH impairs the regenerative capacity of mature hepatocytes, forcing the expansion of hepatic progenitor cells and inducing a ductular reaction. This process significantly accelerates matrix deposition in the portal area by releasing TGF-β or through the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway [74–76].

This complex metabolic-immune crosstalk causes MASH-related fibrosis reversal to exhibit a distinct “metabolic lag.” Unlike the rapid recovery seen in viral hepatitis after pathogen clearance, histological improvement in MASH depends heavily on the deep remodeling of systemic metabolic homeostasis [77]. Persistent ER stress and sphingolipid metabolism disorders (e.g., S1P/S1PR pathway abnormalities) induced by long-term lipotoxicity often form a metabolic “memory.” Even after the resolution of primary injury, this memory may sustain an active inflammatory microenvironment, thereby limiting ECM degradation efficiency [78, 79]. At the translational level, current intervention strategies are shifting toward multi-target combination therapies, such as comprehensive PPAR activation or S1PR4 inhibition, to overcome the efficacy bottlenecks of single-drug therapies in complex metabolic settings [80, 81].

Alcoholic liver disease (ALD)

Fibrosis in ALD typically begins in Zone 3 and possesses unique features driven by metabolites and immune evasion. Mechanistically, acetaldehyde, a metabolite of ethanol, is the core driver of fibrosis; it not only induces TGF-β secretion but also directly binds to HSCs response elements to initiate Type I collagen expression [82, 83]. Simultaneously, ROS generated during metabolism further reinforce HSCs activation via paracrine pathways [84]. Immunologically, long-term alcohol consumption causes microbial translocation via the gut-liver axis, activating the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [85]. Furthermore, the phagocytosis of alcohol-induced hepatocyte apoptotic bodies by Kupffer cells (KCs) activates the NLRP3 inflammasome (involving SYK and TXNIP regulation), leading to a massive release of IL-1β and amplification of the inflammatory cascade [86–88].

These complex mechanisms determine the unique therapeutic response and reversibility trajectory of ALD. While early ALD shows significant potential for reversal upon strict abstinence, long-term alcohol exposure significantly impairs the immune clearance of activated HSCs by natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages [89, 90]. This “immune surveillance failure” leads to an imbalance between pro- and anti-fibrotic factors, which is key to the difficulty of reversing late-stage ALD. Regarding treatment, although traditional anti-TNF-α therapies are limited by infection risks, targeting the SIRT1 pathway, SYK inhibitors, or IL-1 receptor antagonists has shown potential clinical promise in ameliorating alcoholic liver injury [88, 91, 92].

Cholestatic and drug-induced liver injury (DILI)

The progression of fibrosis in cholestatic liver diseases (e.g., PBC, PSC) and DILI is characterized by unique physical and chemical stress profiles. The pathological distribution is primarily centered in the portal area, accompanied by a prominent ductular reaction, presenting a characteristic “onion-skin” pattern surrounding the bile ducts. Mechanistically, bile salt accumulation or drug metabolites directly drive HSCs activation by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [93, 94]. Moreover, these injuries are associated with significant neutrophil infiltration, which sustains the pro-fibrotic cascade through NLRP3 inflammasome activation [95].

This physical damage-based pathological foundation results in extremely low reversibility potential for this type of fibrosis. Unlike other etiologies, the fibrous septa formed around bile ducts are typically accompanied by strong expression of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). This leads to high biochemical stability and cross-linking of the ECM, making it exceptionally difficult for endogenous enzymes to degrade [96]. From a clinical intervention perspective, although targeting the SIRT1 pathway has shown potential in mitigating DILI in experimental models, effective means to induce complete matrix regression in progressive cholestatic fibrosis remain elusive, and the disease often follows a progressive course [97].

The driving role of the gut-liver axis and dysbiosis

Gut dysbiosis and resultant barrier dysfunction are core drivers of MASH-related liver fibrosis. In pathological states, gut-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocates into the liver via the portal vein. As a typical pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), LPS primarily activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on the surface of quiescent HSCs [98]. TLR4 signaling through the MyD88–NF-κB pathway downregulates the TGF-β pseudo-receptor Bambi, rendering HSCs hypersensitive to TGF-β secreted by KCs. Concurrently, activated HSCs release chemokines to recruit and aggregate KCs, together amplifying intrahepatic inflammation and driving excessive HSCs activation and collagen deposition [98].

Additionally, gut microbiota directly interfere with bile acid metabolism through metabolites, regulating the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) signaling pathway. FXR agonists not only strengthen the intestinal barrier but also inhibit the expression of intrahepatic inflammatory and fibrotic genes, representing a major translational direction for treating liver fibrosis via the gut-liver axis [99].

Mechanisms of liver fibrosis regression

For a long time, liver fibrosis was considered an irreversible progressive disease [100]. However, with deepening research in recent years, it has gradually been recognized that liver fibrosis can be reversed under certain conditions. Liver fibrosis reversal is characterized by a significant reduction in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, as well as TGF-β following etiological clearance. This process involves a rapid decline in the population of activated HSCs and myofibroblasts, the cessation of ECM synthesis, and a marked proteolytic shift where the expression of collagen-degrading MMPs is significantly upregulated relative to TIMPs [101]. In CCl₄-induced experimental liver fibrosis models, approximately 50% of activated HSCs undergo apoptosis after injury cessation, with the remaining cells entering a senescent state [102, 103]. This repair process is accompanied by weakened inflammation levels, downregulated pro-fibrotic factors, significantly reduced numbers of activated myofibroblasts, and ECM degradation [104].

Current studies generally agree that the core mechanisms of fibrosis regression primarily include: senescence, apoptosis, and inactivation of activated HSCs and myofibroblasts; macrophage polarization and ECM degradation; and removal of etiological factors in chronic liver injury.

Connotation and standardized definition of liver fibrosis reversal

With the advancement of clinical research, the academic definition of “liver fibrosis reversal” has evolved from mere histological alterations into a multidimensional and comprehensive evaluation system. Based on recent consensus in the Journal of Hepatology and related translational medicine, this review defines its connotation across four interrelated yet distinct levels:

Histopathological Level: The core manifestation is a substantial decrease in the histological stage of fibrosis (e.g., Metavir or Ishak scores), characterized by the thinning, fragmentation, resorption, or complete disappearance of fibrous septa.Non-invasive Assessment Level: This is indicated by clinically significant improvements in liver stiffness measurements via vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) or magnetic resonance elastography (MRE), accompanied by a reduction in serological biomarkers such as the ELF score, Pro-C3, and M2BPGi.Pathophysiological Level: This level involves the alleviation of portal hypertension (e.g., improvement in the threshold for clinically significant portal hypertension, CSPH) and the re-conversion of cirrhosis from a decompensated to a compensated state, a process termed “recompensation.“Clinical Outcome Level: Ultimately, reversal is reflected in a reduced incidence of hepatic decompensation events (e.g., ascites, variceal bleeding) and a substantial decrease in the long-term risk of HCC [105].

Fate regulation and epimetabolic imprinting of hepatic stellate cells and myofibroblasts

When pro-fibrotic factors such as TGF-β,PDGF-β, and IL-17 A are absent, myofibroblasts can initiate diverse cell fate conversion programs, primarily manifesting as three core forms: inactivation, senescence, and apoptosis [104]. Among them, myofibroblast senescence can be triggered by various stress signals, including telomere shortening, DNA damage, chromatin remodeling, or oncogene activation, and senescent myofibroblasts can further be cleared through death receptor pathway-mediated programmed death [106]. Notably, the Notch signaling pathway, as an evolutionarily highly conserved cellular regulatory pathway, has been confirmed to widely participate in cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis regulation in various tissues [107]. Studies confirm that specific blockade of the Notch signaling pathway in myofibroblasts can significantly alleviate liver fibrosis by regulating its occurrence, development, and regression processes, providing new potential strategies and research directions for targeted therapy of liver fibrosis.

However, liver fibrosis regression is not entirely dependent on myofibroblast apoptosis but is also related to HSCs senescence and apoptosis.Some studies find that a portion of activated HSCs, upon injury signal removal, are not cleared but instead return to a resting state [7]. Using lineage tracing models (such as Col1a1-Cre and Vimentin-CreER), approximately half of activated HSCs can migrate back to the space of Disse and regain lipid droplet storage and anti-fibrotic characteristics. These re-inactivated cells (iHSCs) exhibit significant downregulation of fibrosis-related genes (Col1a1, α-SMA, TIMP1) at the transcriptional level, while reactivating genes related to lipid metabolism and quiescent phenotype maintenance such as PPARγ and LRAT [8].In contrast, the remaining fraction of activated HSCs undergoes apoptosis or cellular senescence, thereby attenuating the persistent deposition of extracellular matrix. The senescence machinery of HSCs is primarily governed by the p53 and p16 signaling pathways: in mice with genetic deletion of Trp53 and Cdkn2a (the genes encoding p53 and p16, respectively), activated HSCs fail to undergo senescence, leading to sustained fibrogenesis. Subsequent studies have further elucidated that telomere shortening, DNA damage response and chromatin remodeling are also involved in this process, whereas telomerase overexpression can delay HSCs senescence [108]. On the other hand, the apoptotic mechanisms of activated HSCs are pleiotropic, encompassing death receptor pathway activation, pro-apoptotic protein upregulation, and immune-mediated clearance by intrahepatic natural killer (NK) /natural killer T (NKT) cells [89, 109, 110].

Emerging evidence suggests that HSCs undergo profound epigenetic remodeling during activation, including alterations in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA-mediated regulation. These changes stabilize the activated phenotype, which may persist even after the initial injurious stimuli have subsided [111]. Concurrently, HSCs activation is accompanied by metabolic reprogramming, characterized by enhanced glycolysis, dysregulated lipid metabolism, and the depletion of retinol stores. Notably, metabolic intermediates can further reshape the epigenetic landscape, creating a mutually reinforcing feed-forward loop between metabolism and gene regulation [112]. These findings support the concept that HSCs may enter a “pre-activated” or persistent pro-fibrotic state, which contributes to fibrotic relapse and therapeutic resistance despite apparent lesion regression [8].

Remodeling of the immune microenvironment and multicellular heterogeneity

The traditional perspective posits that the diversity of macrophages allows them to flexibly switch between pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory/restorative (M2) states based on microenvironmental cues [113, 114]. M1 macrophages drive inflammatory responses and promote HSCs activation by secreting TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, while M2 macrophages secrete IL-10 and TGF-β to inhibit inflammation and mediate tissue regeneration and matrix remodeling [115]. With advancements in single-cell and spatial omics, researchers have confirmed that the hepatic immune microenvironment constitutes a highly structured “fibrotic niche” [28]. Within this unit, immune cell subpopulations mediate multicellular communication through dense ligand-receptor interaction networks, shifting their functional paradigm from triggering inflammatory cascades in early injury to facilitating matrix degradation and homeostatic reconstruction following etiological clearance.

Immune landscapes defined by high-resolution omics and “hot/cold” fibrosis

The paradigm of liver fibrosis research has shifted from simple inflammatory models to the concept of a “fibrotic niche” composed of multicellular heterogeneity. These technologies have not only precisely defined pathogenic subpopulations but also provided new spatial perspectives on fibrosis reversal.

Analysis of MASLD/MASH samples has identified scar-associated macrophages (SAMs) characterized by TREM2 and CD9 expression. These cells are highly enriched within scar centers during the progressive phase, driving matrix deposition via the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A axis, yet they exhibit restorative plasticity during the resolution phase [28]. Furthermore, the quantification of cell-cell communication has led to the conceptualization of “hot” and “cold” fibrosis. “Hot fibrosis” is characterized by active immune infiltration and dense ligand-receptor crosstalk, indicating high remodeling potential. In contrast, “cold fibrosis” regions are difficult to reverse due to sparse cellular components, highly cross-linked matrix, and deficient communication [116]. scRNA-seq has further confirmed that activated HSCs are not a homogenous population but include pathogenic subsets with high collagen expression and marginal subsets with immunomodulatory functions. The identification of the SAMs-HSC interaction loop using models like CellChat is driving a shift from “broad-spectrum anti-inflammation” toward “precision elimination” of specific pathogenic subpopulations [117].

Scar-associated macrophages (SAMs) and the TREM2 Axis

Within the heterogenous subpopulations defined by omics, SAMs and the TREM2 axis play a central role in spatial remodeling. Ramachandran et al. first defined SAMs as a specific subpopulation enriched in fibrotic regions [28]. The pro-fibrotic properties of these TREM2 + CD9+ cells are highly dependent on their spatial localization. During progression, SAMs cooperate with endothelial cells and activated HSCs via signals such as TWEAK to consolidate the pathological microenvironment [28]. During resolution, SAMs exhibit significant functional plasticity. By modulating TREM2-mediated pathways, they can downregulate pro-fibrotic factors and polarize into a restorative phenotype that secretes MMP9 and MMP12, initiating effective ECM deconstruction.

Chemokine axis-mediated spatial recruitment and equilibrium

The immune transition during fibrosis reversal depends largely on the precise recruitment of lymphocyte subsets by chemokines and their receptors, exhibiting complex context-dependency [118].

The CXCR3 axis is a core channel for lymphocyte infiltration. Recent evidence suggests that CXCR3 recruits both IFN-γ-secreting Th1 cells and immunosuppressive Treg cells [119, 120]. Thus, translational research should focus on balancing the ratio of pro-inflammatory to regulatory T cells rather than simple pathway blockade.

The CXCR6-CXCL16 axis governs the spatial distribution of intrahepatic lymphocytes. While blocking this axis can alleviate inflammation and fibrosis, CXCR6 + NKT cells also perform critical anti-tumor immune surveillance [121, 122]. In MASH, CXCR6 + hepatic resident CD8 + T cells may exacerbate injury through auto-aggressive behavior due to metabolic stress [123, 124]. This etiology-dependent functional heterogeneity poses a major challenge for precise immune intervention.

Functional polarization and immune surveillance of hepatic lymphocytes

In the hepatic sinusoids, CXCR6 + NKT cells act as “spatial surveyors.” During injury, they secrete Th2-type cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) to initiate pro-fibrotic cascades [121], whereas during resolution, they switch to an IFN-γ-secreting phenotype to mediate the immune clearance of activated HSCs [125]. Complementing this, NK cells utilize the NKG2D receptor to recognize stress signals on activated HSCs, triggering apoptosis via granzyme B and FAS/TRAIL pathways [89]. Since TRAIL receptors are specifically upregulated on activated HSCs, this provides a therapeutic window for anti-fibrotic drugs. Additionally, NK-derived IFN-γ amplifies these pro-apoptotic effects via the STAT1 axis [126].

However, chronic injury and metabolic stress can drive the immune microenvironment into an exhausted state. MASH progression is associated with the enrichment of Tregs and mMDSCs, which induce immune exhaustion in effector lymphocytes via checkpoint molecules like PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIGIT. Reversing this exhaustion using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to restart the clearance of pathogenic myofibroblasts has become a frontier for achieving cirrhosis “recompensation” [124]. Furthermore, mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells play a critical role in the phenotypic remodeling of the immune microenvironment. These cells exhibit a distinct functional evolution throughout the progression of fibrosis: during the injury phase, they secrete IL-17 to synergistically amplify inflammation and drive fibrogenesis; however, during the resolution phase, inhibiting their activation can promote the reprogramming of monocytes and macrophages toward a restorative phenotype, thereby accelerating matrix remodeling [127]. This dynamic plasticity identifies MAIT cells as a key therapeutic target for reversing chronic liver injury and initiating tissue repair.

ECM degradation

The reversal of liver fibrosis fundamentally entails the effective degradation of the ECM and the subsequent restoration of tissue architecture. Following the cessation of primary injury, the attenuated pro-inflammatory milieu prompts a phenotypic shift in macrophages toward a pro-restorative state. These cells facilitate collagen cleavage by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while a concomitant decline in tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) further unleashes endogenous proteolytic activity [128]. However, the efficiency of this degradation is severely constrained by the degree of collagen cross-linking. Covalent cross-linking mediated by lysyl oxidase (LOX) and LOXL2 enhances the biochemical stability of fibrous bundles, creating a physical barrier against MMP-mediated proteolysis—a key factor underlying the recalcitrance of “cold fibrosis” in advanced stages [129].

Furthermore, increased hepatic stiffness resulting from ECM accumulation is transduced into intracellular chemical signals via mechanosensitive pathways, such as integrins and the YAP/TAZ axis. This process establishes a mechanical feedback loop that sustains the activation of HSCs [130]. Under such biomechanical cues, activated HSCs maintain their profibrogenic phenotype and continue to produce ECM even after the removal of the initial insult, leading to further matrix stiffening. Consequently, achieving complete fibrosis reversal depends not only on biochemical proteolysis but also on the improvement of the mechanical microenvironment. Matrix softening disrupts these mechanotransduction signals, depriving HSCs of the physical support required for sustained activation and leading to their subsequent apoptosis or inactivation. This transition breaks the self-perpetuating cycle of fibrogenesis, fostering a virtuous cycle that progresses from physical environment remodeling to the reversal of pathological cellular behaviors [130].

Removal of etiological factors in chronic liver injury

Numerous clinical studies have confirmed the critical role of etiology-targeted interventions in promoting the reversal of liver fibrosis. In the field of viral hepatitis, the clinical application of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has enabled patients with hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection to achieve a high sustained virological response (SVR) rate at different disease stages, accompanied by significant improvements in liver function. With the complete clearance of the virus, patients’ fibrosis-related serological markers (such as FIB-4 score and AST/PLT ratio) show a marked downward trend, indirectly reflecting the gradual repair of liver tissue structure and the alleviation of fibrosis severity [131–133]. For metabolism-related liver diseases (e.g., MASH), bariatric surgery or acetyl-CoA carboxylase inhibitors can effectively reduce hepatic lipid accumulation and improve metabolic disorders, thereby delaying or even reversing the fibrotic process. Furthermore, novel fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) receptor agonists, as candidate drugs targeting metabolic regulation, have demonstrated potential in improving hepatic steatosis and insulin sensitivity in clinical trials, providing a new research direction for the treatment of MASH-related liver fibrosis [134, 135].

Non-invasive assessment system for liver fibrosis

As the clinical research paradigm shifts from histological dependence toward non-invasive evaluation, the construction of a multidimensional non-invasive test (NITs) framework has become central to assessing fibrosis reversal.

Serological evaluation and direct matrix biomarkers

Beyond traditional indices like FIB-4 and APRI, serological panels with superior diagnostic performance have gained mainstream acceptance. The Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) score and M2BPGi have been validated across multiple clinical trials for their efficacy in identifying advanced fibrosis. Notably, Pro-C3 (type III collagen propeptide), a direct matrix biomarker reflecting the dynamic equilibrium of collagen synthesis, is regarded as a critical metric for predicting fibrotic progression and evaluating therapeutic efficacy due to its ability to precisely reflect the rate of fibrogenesis [136].

Imaging-based elastography

Vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE/FibroScan) is widely utilized for large-scale screening and the assessment of hepatic steatosis via the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) due to its procedural simplicity [137]. Conversely, magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is considered the current non-invasive “gold standard” for assessing liver stiffness. Its ability to provide whole-liver coverage without being compromised by obesity makes it a core endpoint in Phase II/III clinical trials [138].

Emerging liquid biopsy approaches

Frontier research is increasingly focusing on liquid biopsy technologies, which offer higher sensitivity and non-invasive advantages for capturing subtle fluctuations in the hepatic microenvironment. The methylation profiling of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) has demonstrated exceptional diagnostic potential; by analyzing differential methylation patterns in specific gene promoter regions, it is possible to precisely trace the origin of injury and identify early fibrotic evolution [139].Furthermore, exosomal miRNAs (e.g., miR-122, miR-21, and miR-192) are emerging as potent biomarkers. Because exosomes protect their encapsulated RNA from degradation by circulating nucleases, specific miRNA profiles can accurately reflect early perturbations within the hepatic microenvironment [140].

Pharmacological treatment of liver fibrosis

Current drug development strategies for liver fibrosis can be primarily categorized into four dimensions: etiological control and metabolic regulation, anti-inflammation and immune modulation, inhibition of HSCs activation and matrix remodeling, as well as exploration of emerging targets related to epigenetic and transcriptional regulation. Treatment strategies for liver fibrosis

Etiological control

This content has been explained in “Removal of etiological factors in chronic liver injury” and will not be repeated here.

Targeting metabolic nuclear receptors and signaling pathways

Obeticholic acid (OCA), as a potent farnicol X receptor (FXR), agonist, has shown anti-fibrotic potential in clinical trials for MASH [141]. Studies show that OCA treatment can improve liver histological features, including reducing fibrosis extent [142]. Recent phase III clinical trials further confirm that 25 mg OCA can significantly improve fibrosis and multiple disease activity indicators [99]. However, its clinical application still needs to balance efficacy with adverse reactions such as pruritus and elevated low-density lipoprotein. The FDA’s decision to ultimately deny approval for OCA in the treatment of MASH serves as a critical reminder that even when a candidate drug achieves histological anti-fibrotic endpoints, safety risks stemming from systemic metabolic side effects remain a primary bottleneck hindering clinical translation.

Increasing evidence indicates that activating thyroid hormone receptors (THRs) can alleviate inflammation and fibrous deposition, exhibiting certain anti-fibrotic potential. Currently, a variety of thyroid hormone analogues mainly used to improve dyslipidemia, such as Resmetirom, are being used in clinical exploration for MASH [143]. However, key differences exist between these drugs’ targets and fibrosis-related mechanisms. Latest studies show that anti-fibrotic effects in HSCs are more likely mediated by THRα rather than THRβ, which is primarily located in hepatocytes and involved in lipid metabolism regulation [144]. This means that although THRβ-selective agonists have clear advantages in reducing liver fat content, their direct improvement on fibrosis itself may be limited.

Inhibiting the liver inflammatory microenvironment

Existing evidence suggests incorporating drugs with anti-inflammatory effects into fibrosis management systems may further enhance treatment responses and bring better prognoses [145]. However, since they simultaneously maintain immune homeostasis and tissue repair, long-term direct blockade often carries significant safety risks, such as increased infections or impaired wound healing [146]. This also prompts researchers to focus more on precise, limited immunomodulatory means.

In this context, pentoxifylline (PTX) has drawn attention as a phosphodiesterase inhibitor that can inhibit the production of tumor necrosis factor - α (TNFα) and exert anti-inflammatory effects by reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [147]. Based on its regulatory capacity on inflammatory pathways, PTX has been used in clinical explorations for MASH patients. Early studies show PTX treatment is associated with liver fibrosis improvement, suggesting it may intervene in inflammation-driven fibrosis progression [148]. Notably, although these studies found no obvious adverse reactions, PTX’s clinical benefits remain controversial. Current evidence is insufficient to prove that TNFα downregulation is its primary anti-fibrotic mechanism, nor can other anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory effects be excluded. Therefore, PTX is often regarded as a candidate treatment option with potential but incompletely clarified mechanisms, with its efficacy still requiring larger sample sizes and clearer mechanistic clinical studies for further validation. The inconsistent efficacy of PTX observed in clinical trials underscores the inherent challenges of using broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory agents. Without the guidance of precise molecular phenotyping, achieving consistent clinical benefits within a highly heterogeneous fibrotic population remains difficult.

In addition, interventions targeting IL-1β and its upstream inflammasomes (especially NLRP3) have gradually become research hotspots. The NLRP3 inflammasome promotes IL-1β maturation through caspase-1 and amplifies inflammatory responses, with its activity inhibitable by various small molecules, including methylsulfonylmethane and glyburide [149, 150]. In early MASH clinical studies, pan-caspase inhibitors showed potential in improving apoptosis-related markers [151, 152]. Given that IL-1β can further induce expression of inflammatory factors such as TNF and CCL2, chemokine axes like CCR2/CCR5 are considered more operable anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic targets [153, 154].

From the upstream nodes of cellular stress-inflammation-fibrosis, apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1), due to linking oxidative stress, cellular injury, and inflammation propagation, becomes a potential key target, with its downstream MAPK pathway particularly important in advancing pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic reactions. The ASK1 inhibitor selonsertib (GS-4977) showed certain fibrosis improvement trends in phase II trials, suggesting potential clinical value for earlier-stage MASH patients. However, subsequent phase III studies failed to meet primary endpoints in more advanced (F3–F4) populations, indicating the drug’s therapeutic effects may be mainly limited to relatively reversible early stages (F1–F3) of fibrosis [155]. Furthermore, strategies using genetically engineered immune cells to precisely clear pathological cells have begun gaining attention in fibrosis treatment. For senescence-associated hepatocytes upregulating urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR), designed chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) have shown ability in preclinical models to effectively alleviate inflammation and reduce fibrous deposition [156].

Directly targeting HSCs activation and matrix remodeling

With deepening insights into signaling pathway regulatory mechanisms, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway-targeted inhibitor ICG-001 (PRI-724) has become an important candidate drug for liver fibrosis treatment [157]. This drug effectively blocks abnormal HSCs proliferation and induces differentiation toward quiescence by selectively inhibiting cyclic AMP response element-binding protein-binding protein (CBP)-dependent β-catenin transcriptional activity. Early clinical trial data indicate that ICG-001 has good safety and tolerability in HCV-related cirrhosis patients, with common adverse reactions mainly mild nausea and fatigue, and no serious adverse events observed [158]. Growth factor-mediated signaling pathways are also key regulatory nodes in liver fibrosis progression, with abnormal activation of the platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) pathway significantly accelerating fibrosis by promoting fibroblast proliferation and activation. Targeted inhibitors of this pathway (such as imatinib) have shown significant anti-fibrotic effects in multiple liver fibrosis animal models, providing important experimental basis for subsequent clinical translation [159, 160].

Excessive ECM crosslinking makes it difficult to degrade. Lysyl oxidase-like protein 2 (LOXL2), as an important enzyme in collagen fiber crosslinking, is considered a key target for inhibiting ECM stabilization. However, although simtuzumab had a good biological foundation in preclinical studies, its phase II trial was terminated early due to failure to significantly improve liver fibrosis. This failure not only underscores the limitations of isolated LOXL2 inhibition but also reflects significant pharmacodynamic hurdles. Specifically, large-molecule agents face severe impediments when penetrating highly cross-linked matrix barriers, preventing them from achieving effective target engagement concentrations within the core of the fibrotic lesions. Notably, LOXL2’s core role in fibrosis formation is still widely recognized, and its targeting potential has not been negated [161]. Additionally, galectin-3 (Gal-3), as a hub molecule regulating inflammation and fibrosis networks, is another class of targets receiving attention. The Gal-3 inhibitor belapectin (GR-MD-02) showed good safety in early clinical trials, but its anti-fibrotic efficacy still requires larger samples and long-term follow-up studies for further confirmations [162, 163]. Notably, the development focus of Belapectin has shifted from isolated histological improvement toward the intervention of hard endpoints, such as portal hypertension. This trend toward targeting clinically significant outcomes establishes a new paradigm for the evaluation of anti-fibrotic therapies [163].

In addition, Cilostazol elevates liver cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and regulates inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, promoting thioacetamide (TAA)-induced fibrosis reversal; in alcoholic liver injury, it downregulates α-SMA, collagen I/III, TGF-β1, and CTGF, thereby alleviating fiber formation [164]. The drug similarly inhibits HSCs activation and reduces fibrosis extent in NAFLD and CCl₄ models [165, 166]. Overall, cilostazol possesses anti-fibrotic potential, but its mechanisms require further research.

Emerging targets: metabolic reprogramming, epigenetics, and transcriptional regulation

m6A modification-driven metabolic-epigenetic feedback loops

Epigenetic modifications play a pivotal role in the phenotypic transition of HSCs. Traditional intervention strategies have primarily focused on histone acetylation, utilizing BET inhibitors (e.g., JQ1) or HDAC inhibitors (e.g., TSA) to potently suppress the transcriptional programs of profibrogenic genes by targeting broad-spectrum acetylation “readers” or “erasers” [167, 168]. However, because these enzymes and proteins exert pleiotropic regulatory effects across diverse cell types, their clinical translation is frequently hindered by severe systemic side effects, such as myelosuppression and gastrointestinal toxicity [169].

Recent research by Zhou et al. reveals the “master switch” role of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification in this process [170]. In their study, a new m6A-binding protein, insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2), which enhances mRNA stability and translation, was observed. IGF2BP2 is highly expressed in liver fibrosis and activated HSCs, and inhibiting IGF2BP2 suppresses HSCs activation and liver fibrosis occurrence. Its primary mechanism is that IGF2BP2 specifically enhances expression of the key glycolysis pathway enzyme ALDOA, thereby promoting glycolytic flux and lactate production in LX-2 cells. The accumulated lactate serves not only as a metabolic product but also as a substrate for histone lactylation, significantly increasing overall lactylation levels and specific histone site H3K18la modifications, confirmed in TGF-β1-stimulated LX-2 cells and CCl₄-induced mouse liver fibrosis models. Importantly, this study reveals a positive feedback loop driven by lactylation modifications: elevated H3K18la can further enrich in glycolysis gene promoter regions, enhancing their transcription and thereby continuously amplifying HSCs glycolytic activity and activated phenotypes [171, 172]. Additionally, inhibiting IGF2BP2 effectively blocks this metabolic-epigenetic axis and suppresses HSCs activation, an effect reversible by overexpressing ALDOA or exogenous lactate supplementation, confirming the central position of the IGF2BP2-ALDOA-lactate-histone lactylation pathway in HSCs activation. Although IGF2BP2 knockout or inhibition can alleviate fibrosis, its specific domains and binding mechanisms with m6A sites on ALDOA, as well as lactylation functions on non-histones, require further analysis [173–176].

Anti-fibrotic mechanisms of hepatocyte CEBPA

CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein α (CEBPA) is a multifunctional non-ligand-dependent transcription factor, first discovered to participate in myeloid cell differentiation regulation and tumorigenesis [177]. In recent years, CEBPA-based therapeutic strategies (such as MTL-CEBPA) have entered cancer clinical research stages and shown positive effects in improving hepatocellular carcinoma patient prognoses [178–180]. Studies indicate that CEBPA is widely expressed in hepatocytes, participating in glycolipid metabolism homeostasis regulation and closely related to liver energy metabolism remodeling [181, 182]. However, the role of hepatocyte-specific CEBPA in liver fibrosis was previously unclear. Yan et al., using hepatocyte-specific Cebpa gene knockout CebpaΔHep mice, tamoxifen-inducible CebpaΔHep, ERT2 mice, and adeno-associated virus serotype 8-forced (AAV8-forced) CEBPA overexpression mice, confirmed the anti-fibrotic role of hepatocyte CEBPA: in human and mouse MASH progression, liver CEBPA expression decreases and negatively correlates with fibrosis; CEBPA-deficient mice exhibit enhanced liver fibrosis, while forced expression in hepatocytes alleviates fibrosis. The core mechanism by which hepatocyte CEBPA regulates liver fibrosis is: by binding to CEBPRE1 to competitively reduce H3K27ac binding in the Spp1 promoter region, inhibiting Spp1 transactivation, thereby reducing synthesis and release of hepatocyte-derived osteopontin (OPN), ultimately suppressing HSCs activation. Studies confirm that elevated Spp1 mRNA in MASH-state livers mainly originates from hepatocytes, and this source of OPN has pro-fibrotic effects, with serum OPN serving as a non-invasive biomarker for liver fibrosis, while CEBPA, as a strong negative regulator of hepatocyte OPN release, has its regulatory role verified through in vitro interventions and in vivo rescue experiments [183–186]. Notably, macrophage-derived OPN may have anti-fibrotic effects, suggesting functional differences in OPN from different cellular sources, thus targeting hepatocyte CEBPA to restrict Spp1 transcription may be a potential strategy for liver fibrosis treatment [187]. However, current research still has multiple key questions unresolved, such as mechanisms of hepatocyte CEBPA interactions with immune cells and cell source-specific functions of OPN and its pleiotropic regulatory networks remain unclear. Core driving factors for hepatocyte CEBPA downregulation in MASH progression require exploration, and causal relationships between liver glycogen storage and liver fibrosis and SPP1 regulation also await further elucidation.

Transcription factor-mediated ferroptosis defense and sex differences

Transcription factors occupy central positions in maintaining human physiological homeostasis and driving various disease processes, with their constructed gene regulatory networks providing foundational frameworks for analyzing complex biological processes including cell death [188]. Although ferroptosis’s role in liver fibrosis is gradually gaining attention, its upstream transcriptional regulatory networks remain incompletely elucidated. Existing evidence shows that transcription factor MafG is highly expressed in early liver fibrosis lesions and late-stage liver cancer tissues [189, 190]. Deng et al.’s research further clarifies that MafG is significantly upregulated in fibrotic liver tissues, promoting HSCs ECM secretion, suggesting its driving role in fibrosis processes. MafG is also confirmed to participate in ferroptosis processes by regulating iron metabolism balance and oxidative stress [191–193]. Notably, the traditionally cytoplasm-localized myosin MYH9 has recently been found to enter the nucleus and participate in transcriptional regulation, possessing DNA-binding capacity [194–196]. Based on this discovery, Deng et al. first reveal that MYH9 can act as a co-factor of MafG in regulating HSCs ferroptosis, but whether they co-bind target gene promoters or regulate transcription in auxiliary ways requires further verification. Meanwhile, one known function of the lipocalin LCN2 is interfering with iron ion acquisition, with its deficiency inducing iron ion accumulation and ferroptosis in various tumor cells [197–199]. Deng et al.’s study ultimately constructs a “MafG-LCN2” regulatory axis, confirming that blocking this axis can promote HSCs iron overload and lipid peroxidation, revealing a novel MafG-dependent ferroptosis regulatory pathway. Future research still needs to further analyze relationships between MafG and antioxidant factors, as well as complete transcriptional regulatory modes of MYH9-MafG, to enrich the foundation for MafG-targeted anti-fibrotic strategies.

Beyond ferroptosis-related pathways, transcription factors also mediate significant sex differences in liver fibrosis. Males exhibit significantly higher liver fibrosis and related mortality risks than females, with HCC incidence reaching 5–6 times that of females [200, 201]. Although sex differences have clear epidemiological evidence, their molecular basis remains insufficient. Wu et al.‘s research first reveals that the Y chromosome gene SRY has strong pro-fibrotic functions in liver fibrosis. SRY is primarily localized in hepatocytes and considered one of the pro-oncogenic genes in liver cancer [202–204]. It is continuously significantly upregulated in patient liver fibrosis/cirrhosis tissues and multiple mouse models, positively correlating with type I collagen deposition. Using specific transgenic mice combined with fibrosis induction experiments further confirms that SRY’s fibrosis-exacerbating effects are independent of sex hormone regulation [201]. Mechanistically, SRY can directly promote transcription of its target gene PDGFRα, thereby enhancing HMGB1 secretion and activating HSCs, driving fibrosis processes. Multi-omics analyses also show that SRY can upregulate HMGB1 expression by activating the p-JNK-ELK1-cFos signaling pathway in HSCs [205–207]. PDGFRα is its clear direct target gene, with its inhibition or knockout blocking the pro-fibrotic effects of the SRY-HMGB1-HSC axis [208]. Additionally, the SRY inhibitor DAX1 can effectively downregulate key molecules in this pathway and alleviate fibrosis extent [209]. However, since no direct interaction between SRY and HMGB1 has been detected, the specific molecular mechanisms by which SRY regulates HMGB1 secretion require further elucidation. Future focus can be on screening possible intermediary factors or related signaling pathways to refine the overall regulatory network of SRY-mediated liver fibrosis [210].

Beyond genetic factors mediated by the SRY pathway, differences in sex hormone signaling and the gut microbiome collectively shape the sexual dimorphism of liver fibrosis. Research has confirmed that 17β-estradiol exerts significant anti-fibrotic effects by activating estrogen receptors ERα or ERβ. This signaling axis not only directly inhibits the activation and proliferation of HSCs but also attenuates ECM deposition by downregulating the TGF-β pathway and oxidative stress, providing a robust explanation for the lower risk of fibrosis in premenopausal women [211]. Furthermore, host sex influences hepatic immune homeostasis by modulating the gut microbiota structure and bile acid metabolic profiles; these sex-based differences in the gut-liver axis further amplify the susceptibility of male individuals to fibrotic injury [201]. Consequently, sexual dimorphism in liver fibrosis arises from a complex network mediated by genetic background, endocrine environment, and symbiotic microbial communities. Future precision interventions should comprehensively account for the interplay of these multidimensional factors.

Precision drug delivery strategies for liver fibrosis

Despite the identification of numerous promising therapeutic targets, a major challenge in the clinical translation of anti-fibrotic drugs is achieving effective concentrations in target cells (particularly activated HSCs) without eliciting systemic toxicity. Currently, precision delivery systems are providing translational solutions by enhancing the selective distribution of drugs. Active targeting of specific receptors overexpressed on the surface of activated HSCs is a central focus of research [212]. For example, leveraging the high expression of integrin αvβ3 on activated HSCs, nanocarriers modified with RGD-containing peptides can achieve precise drug delivery to damaged regions [213]. Additionally, drug-loaded nanoparticles or antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) targeting receptors such as the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptor and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β (PDGFR-β) have demonstrated significant selective accumulation and anti-fibrotic potency in experimental models [214]. These delivery systems not only address the limitations of small-molecule drugs, such as poor water solubility and rapid metabolism, but also minimize systemic adverse effects through spatially specific targeting mechanisms, serving as a vital technological bridge between target discovery and clinical feasibility.

Bottlenecks, breakthroughs, and paradigm shifts in clinical translation

Despite significant progress in fundamental research, the translational efficiency of liver fibrosis drugs from bench to bedside remains low. This bottleneck stems primarily from the intrinsic differences between experimental models and human pathology. Traditional models, such as CCl4 or bile duct ligation (BDL), fail to replicate the chronic metabolic disorders, lipotoxicity, and gut-liver axis signaling characteristic of human MASH, making it difficult for drug efficacy observed in animals to be reproduced in humans [215]. Furthermore, rodent models exhibit a rapid tendency for fibrosis reversal that is often limited in clinical practice, where patients face decades of heavy ECM cross-linking and vascular remodeling [216]. Additionally, regulatory endpoints still rely on liver biopsy scores, which suffer from sampling errors and invasiveness, hindering the sensitive capture of early dynamic reversal [215]. These limitations, combined with the heterogeneity of patient microenvironments and pharmacodynamic hurdles in penetrating cross-linked matrix barriers, lead to low success rates in Phase II/III trials [215, 217]. Safety concerns also remain significant; for instance, while TGF-β inhibitors show potent anti-fibrotic activity preclinically, their pleiotropic physiological roles often lead to unacceptable systemic side effects [218]. Such heterogeneity suggests that for advanced, established scars, the therapeutic goal may need to shift from complete reversal toward functional “recompensation.”

Reflections on target redundancy and clinical trial failures

Clinical failures over the past decade have highlighted the complexity of liver fibrosis, particularly regarding pathway redundancy and immune compensation. For example, the failure of the CCR2/5 antagonist Cenicriviroc in the Phase III AURORA trial suggests that blocking a single chemokine axis may induce compensatory activation of alternative inflammatory pathways [219]. Another key lesson involves the mismatch between intervention windows and pathological stages; the failure of the ASK1 inhibitor Selonsertib in the STELLAR-4 trial reflects its inability to reverse heavily cross-linked matrix in advanced cirrhosis [155]. Moreover, the failure of Simtuzumab underscores pharmacodynamic barriers, as large-molecule antibodies struggle to penetrate “cold fibrosis” regions to achieve effective target engagement [220]. These cases have prompted a shift in therapeutic goals, suggesting that converting “active fibrosis” into more stable, less angiogenic “quiescent scars” to improve microcirculation may be a more pragmatic clinical endpoint [217].

Breakthrough of resmetirom in MASH clinical translation

Resmetirom (Rezdiffra™) represents a milestone in anti-fibrotic drug development, receiving accelerated FDA approval in March 2024 for non-cirrhotic MASH patients with moderate to advanced fibrosis (Stages F2–F3) [221]. As an oral, selective THR-β partial agonist, it possesses approximately 28-fold higher selectivity for the β-receptor over endogenous T3, allowing it to restore hepatic metabolic homeostasis and reduce intrahepatic triglycerides while avoiding systemic thyroid-related toxicity [222]. Resmetirom’s success establishes a paradigm for multi-target synergy by simultaneously addressing lipotoxicity and indirectly downregulating HSCs activation [220]. In the MAESTRO-NASH trial, it achieved the dual endpoints of NASH resolution and fibrosis improvement. Its approval also signals a shift toward non-invasive assessment (NITs), utilizing metrics like MRI-PDFF and FibroScan to monitor therapeutic response [223].

Integrated evaluation systems and combination strategies

Future translational research will emphasize the multidimensional integration of NITs (e.g., combining VCTE/MRE with Pro-C3) to achieve continuous therapeutic monitoring [223]. While FIB-4 remains useful for risk stratification, its sensitivity in capturing early drug responses is limited [217]. Therefore, precision intervention will increasingly rely on the integration of multimodal data. Combination strategies are becoming mainstream, pairing drugs with systemic metabolic regulatory effects (e.g., GLP-1/GIP receptor agonists) or those targeting the gut-liver axis (e.g., FXR agonists) with direct anti-fibrotic agents to achieve synergistic effects [224]. This multi-pronged approach aims not only to reverse pathological staging but also to improve hepatic hemodynamics and reduce portal hypertension, ultimately achieving true hepatic recompensaton.

Conclusions

The progression and regression of liver fibrosis constitute a highly dynamic and plastic process, driven by cell fate transition, immune microenvironment remodeling, and ECM homeostasis imbalance. Single-cell and multi-omics studies have demonstrated that hepatocyte injury, immune activation, HSCs activation, and ECM remodeling do not act as linear cascades, but form an interactive regulatory network through metabolic coupling, signaling crosstalk, and spatial regulation. Fibrosis regression depends on the coordinated activation of multi-layered programs, including HSCs inactivation or clearance, macrophage phenotypic conversion, and the restoration of MMP/TIMP balance.

Emerging regulatory mechanisms have expanded the scope of anti-fibrotic interventions to epigenetic reprogramming, circadian rhythm modulation, and targeted cell fate control. Future research should be promoted synergistically at mechanistic, translational, and therapeutic levels.

Overall, the field of liver fibrosis is shifting from simple structural damage suppression toward a systematic therapeutic paradigm centered on cell state regulation and tissue ecosystem remodeling. With further clarification of pathogenic mechanisms and continuous innovation of therapeutic strategies, clinically effective strategies for fibrosis reversal will be gradually realized.
