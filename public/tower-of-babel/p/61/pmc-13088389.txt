A central role for fibrinogen in promoting microglial repair of the hypoxia-disrupted blood-brain barrier
Authors: Sebok K. Halder, Arjun Sapkota, Richard Milner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Building on our previous finding that a small fibrinogen peptide inhibits microglial repair of the hypoxia-disrupted blood-brain barrier (BBB), we directly evaluated the importance of fibrinogen in this process by studying these events in fibrinogen knockout (KO) mice. While young (4 months old) fibrinogen KO mice were still capable of mediating a vasculo-protective response to chronic mild hypoxia (CMH; 8% O2), the extent of vascular leak in these mice was much greater than wild-type (WT) mice. Consistent with this, fibrinogen KO mice displayed greatly reduced levels of hypoxia-induced microglial activation, proliferation, and aggregation around leaky blood vessels. Notably, defects in fibrinogen KO mice became more pronounced with age such that by 8-months old, a large fraction (3/8) of fibrinogen KO mice failed to survive 4 days CMH, and those that survived showed much greater loss of myelin and neurons compared to WT. Furthermore, under normoxic conditions, microglia in middle-aged (13 months old) fibrinogen KO mice were significantly less activated compared to WT, suggesting that slow fibrinogen leak across the age-weakened BBB might explain why microglia are more activated in aged mice. Finally, in vitro studies revealed that microglia readily attach to fibrinogen and that compared to other pro-adhesive ECM proteins such as fibronectin, fibrinogen promotes morphological transition into a polarized phenotype capable of greater levels of migration. Together, these observations demonstrate the importance of fibrinogen in promoting an effective microglial vasculo-protective response and suggest that pharmacological manipulation of fibrinogen-microglial interactions in the aged brain could hold therapeutic promise in the prevention or treatment of vascular dementia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03759-6.

Introduction

Tight electrical resistance and low permeability are two important properties of central nervous system (CNS) blood vessels that distinguish them from those in other organs. These properties, together with the ability to precisely regulate transport of many substrates while preventing entry of potentially damaging blood components and inflammatory immune cells are together termed the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [1, 2]. At the molecular level, the basis of the BBB lies in (i) the tight connection of adjacent endothelial cells via adherens and tight junction protein complexes, (ii) the strong adhesion of endothelial cells to the extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins of the vascular basement membrane, and (iii) the influence of other CNS cells, particularly astrocyte foot processes, pericytes, and microglia [3–6]. Disruption of the BBB is central to the pathogenesis of many neurological diseases, including multiple sclerosis (MS), ischemic stroke, meningitis, and vascular dementia [7–9]. Furthermore, a growing number of studies have demonstrated that BBB integrity also declines with age [10, 11] suggesting that this may be a critical contributory factor in the pathogenesis of vascular dementia and associated neurodegeneration [12, 13].

BBB integrity is also disrupted by hypoxia (low oxygen) which occurs at higher altitudes as well as in many diseases of the heart and lungs and infections. Over the last fifteen years we have characterized how hypoxia promotes the growth of new CNS blood vessels (angiogenesis) in addition to altering BBB integrity, both in healthy mice and in models of neurological disease [5, 14–16]. Notably, we found that chronic mild hypoxia (CMH; 8% O2) induces transient BBB disruption that is associated with a strong microglial protective response in which microglia become highly activated and aggregate around leaking blood vessels [5, 15]. Depletion of microglia results in exaggerated BBB disruption, demonstrating that microglia play an important vasculo-protective function in hypoxic conditions [15]. Building on the work of others [17], we found that this protective microglial response was inhibited by a fibrinogen peptide that prevents fibrinogen interacting with the microglial Mac-1 integrin receptor [5]. While our peptide-blocking studies suggest a role for the fibrinogen-Mac-1 interaction in mediating microglial vasculo-protective responses, we wanted to evaluate how important fibrinogen is in mediating vascular repair, primarily to determine if this mechanism is modulatory or absolutely essential. To address this question, we studied these events in fibrinogen knockout (KO) mice, that show increased mortality in the first few days of life, but of the mice that survive this vulnerable period, the majority are viable and fertile [18]. The goal of this study was to compare CMH-induced vascular remodeling and microglial vasculo-protective responses in fibrinogen KO and wild type (WT) mice. Specifically, we evaluated how absence of fibrinogen impacts (i) the extent of hypoxia-induced BBB disruption, (ii) microglial activation and proliferation responses following BBB disruption, (iii) microglial aggregation around leaky blood vessels, and (iv) ultimately determine how lack of fibrinogen impacts the repair of hypoxia-disrupted cerebral blood vessels.

Results

The extent of hypoxia-induced BBB disruption is greatly amplified in fibrinogen KO mice

As fibrinogen blocking peptide studies suggest that microglial vasculo-protection is mediated, in part via a fibrinogen-driven mechanism [5], here we directly evaluated the role of extravascular-leaked fibrinogen in mediating this protective function using fibrinogen KO mice. Before starting our analysis, we confirmed the total absence of fibrinogen in these KO mice both under normoxic and hypoxic conditions (supplementary Fig. 1A and B, respectively). Fibrinogen KO and WT 4-month-old male mice were exposed to CMH for 4 days before all blood was removed by perfusion, and brain tissue examined for BBB disruption by dual-immunofluorescence (IF) using CD31 to identify endothelial cells and hemoglobin to detect extravascular leak. We studied these events primarily in the olfactory bulb because this is the brain region showing the highest levels of BBB disruption at this young age [19]. As shown in Fig. 1A, though no BBB disruption was observed in either strain under normoxic conditions, 4 days CMH induced obvious BBB disruption, which in keeping with our previous findings, was most abundant in the olfactory bulb (OB; Fig. 1B). In seven different brain regions examined, the density of vascular leaks was not appreciably different in fibrinogen KO mice (Fig. 1B). However, when we compared the size of vascular leak in the two strains, we found that the cumulative area of vascular leak in fibrinogen KO mice was significantly greater than WT both in the OB and the midbrain (MB; Fig. 1A and C). Dual-IF with CD31 and an alternative marker of BBB breakdown, the red blood cell marker TER-119, revealed very similar findings, with no leak seen under normoxic conditions, no appreciable difference between fibrinogen KO and WT mice in the density of hypoxia-induced leaks in any brain region, but a significantly enhanced area of vascular leak in the OB and MB of fibrinogen KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C). An overview of the differences in vascular disruption between WT and fibrinogen KO mice is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, both at the level of whole brain (A) and low power magnification of IF staining in the olfactory bulb (B), which clearly demonstrate greater extent of vascular disruption in fibrinogen KO mice. As fibrinogen plays an important role in promoting blood coagulation after vascular disruption, it is likely that the enhanced hypoxia-induced vascular disruption observed in fibrinogen KO mice could be in part due to reduced blood coagulation. To examine the spatial relationship between vascular disruption and platelet recruitment, we performed dual-IF for hemoglobin and the well-established platelet marker GPIIb integrin (CD41) on brain sections taken from mice exposed to 4 days CMH (Supplementary Fig. 4). In the two brain areas showing the highest degree of BBB disruption (olfactory bulb and midbrain), this revealed that platelets were often observed at sites of vascular disruption, though the level of platelet aggregation was much smaller than the extravascular leak signal and platelets were not detected in all vascular leaks. No obvious differences in platelet aggregation were observed between WT and fibrinogen KO mice.

In previous time-course studies we demonstrated that the density and cumulative area of hypoxia-induced BBB disruptions are greatest after 4- and 7-days hypoxic exposure but then diminish sharply after 14 days hypoxia [15] consistent with the idea that vascular disruptions are gradually repaired over time, even when mice are maintained under hypoxic conditions. To examine how the absence of fibrinogen impairs this endogenous repair capacity, we next compared the time-course of vascular leaks in fibrinogen KO and WT mice after 4, 7 and 14 days of CMH. Using hemoglobin as a marker of vascular disruption, this showed that the density of vascular leaks was high after 4- and 7-days hypoxia but after 14 days hypoxia, this density had fallen to a much lower level in both fibrinogen KO and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 5A-B). In support of this, while fibrinogen KO mice displayed a greater area of vascular leak (as measured by hemoglobin) at all three timepoints, the overall time-course of vascular disruption was not noticeably affected by the absence of fibrinogen, such that the vascular leak area peaked at 4 and 7 days hypoxia but by 14 days hypoxia, had reduced to a much lower level (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Similar analysis using the red blood cell marker TER-119 as an alternative marker of vascular disruption, confirmed these results (Supplementary Fig. 6A-C).

Hypoxia-induced microglial activation, proliferation, and aggregation around leaky blood vessels are greatly reduced in fibrinogen KO mice

Previous studies have shown that hypoxia-induced BBB disruption promotes microglial activation, as measured by increased expression of Mac-1 integrin, a morphological switch from predominantly ramified (small cell body with long process extensions) to activated (larger cell body with short process extensions) and increased proliferation rate [19]. Comparing these responses in fibrinogen KO vs. WT mice under normoxic control conditions, there were no apparent differences in microglial morphology, Mac-1 expression, and no microglial proliferation in either strain (Fig. 2A-C). However, after 4 days CMH, obvious differences between fibrinogen KO and WT mice became apparent. First, while CMH induced a marked increase in microglial Mac-1 expression and obvious morphological activation in WT mice, these changes were noticeably attenuated in fibrinogen KO mice (Fig. 2A and B). Second, while CMH induced robust microglial proliferation in WT mice, this effect was markedly suppressed in fibrinogen KO mice as shown by a much lower number of Ki67+ microglial cells (Fig. 2A and C). As prior studies show that in young adult WT mice, most vascular leaks are surrounded by aggregates of CD68+ activated microglia [19], we next examined if lack of fibrinogen impacts the propensity of microglia to aggregate around leaky cerebral blood vessels. To evaluate this, we performed dual-IF in fibrinogen KO and WT mice with the extravascular leak marker hemoglobin, and CD68, a marker of microglial priming. This revealed that in WT mice exposed to 4 days CMH, almost all hemoglobin+ vascular leaks were surrounded by aggregates of CD68+ activated microglia (Fig. 3). By contrast, aggregation of CD68+ cells around vascular leaks was markedly deficient in fibrinogen KO mice, with many vascular leaks not surrounded by microglial aggregates (Fig. 3A and quantified in Fig. 3B). Taken together, these data show that in the absence of fibrinogen, microglial activation and aggregation around leaking blood vessels is markedly attenuated. Triple IF with CD31/hemoglobin/CD68 demonstrated hemoglobin deposition around leaky blood vessels and confirmed marked reduction of microglial aggregation around vascular leaks in fibrinogen KO mice (Fig. 3C). To examine if immune cell infiltration contributes to the aggregation of Mac-1+ cells at sites of vascular disruption, we performed dual-IF with antibodies for hemoglobin and the pan-leukocyte marker CD45. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, no CD45+ cells were detected at sites of vascular disruption, in either WT or fibrinogen KO mice. Furthermore, dual-IF with TER-119 and the microglia-specific marker TMEM-119 confirmed strong microglial accumulation at sites of vascular disruption.

Hypoxia-induced vascular defects in fibrinogen KO mice become worse with age, resulting in loss of myelin and neurons

As our previous data show that hypoxia-induced BBB disruption is significantly exacerbated with age [19], we next examined how loss of fibrinogen impacts microglial function and BBB disruption in older mice using 8-month-old fibrinogen KO and WT mice. Interestingly, while we observed no hypoxia-induced mortality in fibrinogen KO and WT mice in 4 months old young adult mice, in 8-month-old mice, 3 out of 8 fibrinogen KO mice in the hypoxic group died during 4 days of CMH exposure, while no WT mice died. This represents a 37.5% mortality rate of fibrinogen KO mice exposed to CMH, suggesting that under hypoxic conditions, absence of fibrinogen in older (8 months old) mice may lead to irreversible vascular leak and/or hemorrhage that cannot be rescued by other endogenous protective/repair mechanisms. Consistent with our findings in 4 months old mice, the 8 months old fibrinogen KO mice that survived showed much greater levels of BBB disruption than WT, as indicated by area of extravascular leak of both Hb and TER-119 (Fig. 4). At this later age, hypoxia-induced BBB disruptions also became more common in other brain areas such as the midbrain and the cerebral cortex with the extent of vascular leak much more extensive in fibrinogen KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 8A-D). It was also striking that unlike the 4-month-old mice, many brain areas in 8-month-old mice (midbrain, cerebral cortex, corpus striatum, and corpus callosum) also showed significant increases in the density of vascular leaks (Supplementary Fig. 8E-F). In summary and consistent with our previous findings of worsening hypoxia-induced vascular leak with age [19], the density and area of hypoxia-induced vascular leak was increased both by age and absence of fibrinogen (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Because extravascular leak was much greater in 8-month-old mice, we next examined the impact of this on myelin integrity and neuronal survival by performing TER-119/fluoromyelin (FM) or TER-119/NeuN, respectively. Analysis of myelin integrity in the corpus callosum, the largest white matter tract in the brain, revealed that exposure to CMH induced larger areas of vascular disruption in fibrinogen KO mice that correlated with significantly greater loss of myelin in these affected areas compared to WT mice (Fig. 5A and B). In a similar manner, when we analyzed neuronal density by performing TER-119/NeuN dual-IF in the olfactory bulb, this revealed that vascular leak co-localized directly with neuronal loss and that this loss was far greater in fibrinogen KO mice (Fig. 5C and D).

Under normoxic control conditions, middle-aged fibrinogen null mice show reduced levels of microglial activation

Previous studies demonstrate that microglial activation state increases with age [20, 21]. As it is also recognized that BBB integrity declines with age [10, 11], we wondered if these two events are directly connected, whereby minute levels of fibrinogen leak cross the deteriorating BBB to stimulate microglial activation in the aged brain. First, we examined potential hemoglobin or TER-119 extravascular leak in 13-month-old fibrinogen KO or WT mice maintained under normoxic conditions, but this revealed no detectable vascular leak (Supplementary Fig. 10). Next, to test the hypothesis that very low levels of fibrinogen trickle through the aged BBB to trigger enhancement of microglial activation we compared microglial activation state in middle-aged 13-month-old fibrinogen KO and WT mice maintained under normoxic conditions. This revealed that microglial activation, as evaluated by two independent markers, Mac-1 and CD68, was significantly lower in fibrinogen KO mice vs. WT (Fig. 6). In agreement with this, Mac-1 IF showed that microglia in fibrinogen KO mice displayed a less activated morphology as shown by a smaller cell body with less thick process extensions (see HP panels in Fig. 6A).

Fibrinogen promotes microglial adhesion, followed by transition into a polarized migratory phenotype

To investigate how fibrinogen regulates microglial function at a more reductionist level, we studied the behavior of primary mouse microglia in vitro. First, we compared the behavior of microglia on several physiologically relevant ECM substrates, including laminin which is abundantly expressed in the vascular basement membrane, and fibronectin and fibrinogen which are circulating serum proteins that leak into the CNS parenchyma following BBB disruption [17, 22]. After 4 h incubation, microglia had attached and spread well on fibronectin and fibrinogen but much less well to laminin, where many microglia were rounded up phase-bright cells (Fig. 7A). After 24 h, while microglia were still poorly attached to laminin, microglial morphology on fibronectin and fibrinogen had started to show some interesting differences. Specifically, while microglia cultured on fibronectin displayed a well spread ameboid phenotype, on fibrinogen, many microglia displayed a polarised bipolar phenotype typical of migratory cells (Fig. 7A, lower row). Based on this observation, we wondered if fibrinogen might be stimulating microglial transition into a pro-migratory phenotype. To test this idea, we conducted migration assays to compare microglial migration rates on fibronectin and fibrinogen. In this migration assay, microglia were plated at very high density in the middle of the well and then cell migration away from the edge of the high-density population was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 7B, differences in microglial behavior on fibronectin and fibrinogen became apparent as early as 4 h. Specifically, while microglia cultured on fibronectin displayed a predominantly amoeboid shape and tended to remain closely packed together at high density, those cultured on fibrinogen were fast to adopt a bipolar polarized phenotype and migrated away from the edge of the high-density population. At the later timepoints of 24 and 48 h these differences became more pronounced with microglia migrating much greater distances on fibrinogen compared to fibronectin. This enhanced migration rate on fibrinogen was associated with a more polarized bipolar or sometimes multipolar morphology compared with the predominantly amoeboid appearance of microglia on fibronectin. Quantification confirmed that microglial migration on fibrinogen was much greater than that on fibronectin (Fig. 7C).

Mac-1 integrin is localized at the leading edge of migratory microglia on a fibrinogen substrate

Microglia express high levels of the fibrinogen receptor Mac-1 (CD11b) [17, 23]. Our previous finding that an inhibitory peptide targeting the Mac-1-fibrinogen interaction suppressed microglial aggregation around hypoxia-induced BBB disruptions in vivo suggests that the Mac-1 integrin plays a critical role in mediating microglial responses to vascular breakdown [5]. To investigate this further we examined the cell surface distribution pattern of Mac-1 of microglial cells cultured on fibronectin or fibrinogen. This revealed that on the relatively non-migratory substrate fibronectin, where microglia displayed a well spread amoeboid morphology, Mac-1 was mostly distributed across the body of the cell in a non-polarized manner with some occasional higher accumulations at specific points at the cell border (Fig. 7D). By contrast, on the pro-migratory substrate fibrinogen, where most microglia displayed a highly polarized bipolar morphology, Mac-1 integrin was most often focused at high density at the leading edge (lamella) of the migratory cells (arrows). This localization is consistent with the idea that Mac-1 plays an important role in promoting microglial migration on fibrinogen.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that extravascular fibrinogen leaking out of disrupted cerebral blood vessels plays an important role in promoting microglial activation and aggregation around these vessels, thereby facilitating BBB repair. Importantly, absence of fibrinogen markedly suppressed the efficacy or speed of the microglial vasculo-protective response, as shown by greater area of individual vascular leaks. This correlated with attenuated microglial responses, including reduction in microglial activation, proliferation, and aggregation around the leaking vessels. Of note, while fibrinogen was not essential for mediating vascular repair responses in young 4-month-old mice, as shown by the marked reduction in density of vascular leaks after 14 days hypoxia in both fibrinogen KO and wild-type mice, a significant fraction (3/8) of 8-month-old fibrinogen KO mice failed to survive 4 days hypoxic exposure. This implies that the dependence on fibrinogen to promote microglial vascular repair becomes greater with increasing age, which could either be the result of age-dependent downregulation of other vasculo-protective mechanisms, or rather that all protective mechanisms are overwhelmed by the extent of BBB breakdown at the older age. It is also noteworthy that in the 8-month-old group, BBB disruption in the fibrinogen KO strain led to much greater loss of myelin and neuronal density, supporting the concept that hypoxia-induced BBB breakdown likely plays an important pathogenic role in contributing to the demyelination and neurodegeneration in the etiology of vascular dementia. It also reinforces the notion that fibrinogen-microglial interactions play a critical role in maintaining optimal BBB integrity [15].

Fibrinogen promotes blood coagulation, immune cell activation, and inflammatory responses

One important caveat to consider when interpreting these studies is that fibrinogen is an important component of the blood coagulation cascade [24], such that absence of fibrinogen would be expected to result in greater degree of vascular leak aside from the separate role of fibrinogen in promoting microglial vasculo-protection. Interestingly, with greater vascular leak of blood proteins, one would expect to see enhanced levels of microglial activation and aggregation around the disrupted blood vessels, but in fact we observed the exact opposite in fibrinogen knockout mice. This strongly supports the idea that fibrinogen plays dual roles in maintaining CNS vascular integrity, first by promoting blood coagulation, and second by stimulating the microglial vascular repair response. Interestingly, while fibrinogen plays a vital role in the clotting cascade, it is not essential because although fibrinogen deficient mice have extended bleeding times, they are still viable [18]. To be more precise, fibrinogen KO mice show increased mortality during the first few days of life because of the extended bleeding time, but the majority survive, though show elevated mortality throughout life, primarily due to abdominal hemorrhage. In addition to its beneficial role in promoting injury-induced blood coagulation, fibrinogen also exerts an important influence in promoting immune cell activation and inflammatory responses. Specifically, reports have shown that phagocyte accumulation on implanted materials can be blocked by targeting the fibrinogen-Mac-1 interaction [25] and leukocyte adherence to vascular endothelium is partly dependent on an interaction between fibrinogen and intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1 [26]. Other studies have highlighted the importance of the fibrinogen-Mac-1 interaction by showing that a mutated fibrinogen lacking the Mac-1 binding motif failed to support adhesion of neutrophils and macrophages. Furthermore, mice carrying this mutated fibrinogen demonstrated a reduced ability of peritoneal leukocytes to clear inoculated bacteria from the peritoneum [27]. As platelets have been shown to influence microglial function [28], it is possible that in addition to its direct effect on microglial activation, fibrinogen may also impact microglial activation indirectly by recruiting platelets to the site of vascular disruption. To shed light on this issue, in future studies, we plan to examine the interplay between fibrinogen, platelets, and microglia.

Extravascular fibrinogen’s effects in the central nervous system

A large body of work has demonstrated that fibrinogen leaked from blood vessels plays a strong pathogenic role in promoting disease progression. This has been extensively described in the CNS by the Akassoglou lab who study the pathogenesis of inflammatory demyelinating disease. Specifically, over several studies, they showed that extravascular leak of fibrinogen into the CNS parenchyma during inflammatory demyelinating disease stimulates a microglial activation response via the Mac-1 integrin that results in microglial-mediated destruction and phagocytosis of myelin. Notably, interruption of this molecular interaction through fibrinogen depletion, use of a function-blocking fibrinogen peptide, or transgenic mice lacking the Mac-1 binding motif in fibrinogen all led to attenuated disease severity in the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model of multiple sclerosis (MS) [17, 29, 30]. While these studies highlight the pathogenic destructive role of vascular-leaked fibrinogen in triggering death of oligodendrocytes and neurons and perpetuating an inflammatory response, our current study presents the other side of the coin by showing that extravascular fibrinogen also plays a protective role following BBB disruption by promoting microglial repair of the disrupted blood vessel. Therefore, just as microglia are often described as a double-edged sword, playing both protective and harmful functions [31], fibrinogen can be described likewise. So what determines if fibrinogen plays a positive or negative role? Likely, this is largely dependent on the nature of the primary insult, particularly the severity and the chronicity of the insult. For instance, in the EAE model, which presents a very robust pro-inflammatory insult similar to the pathology of MS, this stimulates a marked and sustained fibrinogen release through prolonged BBB breakdown. This in turn leads to a strong stimulation of microglia and infiltrated macrophages that culminates in death of oligodendrocytes, demyelination, and eventually axonal degeneration. By contrast, the relatively less severe stimulus of mild hypoxia described in the current study, leads to extravascular fibrinogen leak, but lacks the strong pro-inflammatory component of EAE, can be viewed as more of a sub-clinical physiological remodeling event, in which fibrinogen activates microglia enough to mediate vascular repair but not enough to perpetuate vascular leak and pathological sequalae.

One observation that has yet to be fully understood is why microglia in aged brain are more activated than the young [20, 21]. Together with others, we have postulated that the age-related decline in BBB integrity leads to residual leak of blood proteins into the parenchyma, which then stimulates microglial activation [19]. In the current study we provide evidence that supports this concept by demonstrating that microglia in middle age (13 months old) normoxic fibrinogen KO mice are significantly less activated than their WT counterparts while also implying that fibrinogen is a key molecule influencing this process. One caveat of this study is that the WT mice we used were generated by breeding heterozygous (Fbg+/−) mice together and selecting the Fbg+/+ (WT) progeny so while they had the same overall background strain as fibrinogen knockouts, they were not actual littermates.

Potential alternative mechanisms mediating microglial vasculo-protection

Our observation that effective, though somewhat delayed, vascular repair continues in the absence of fibrinogen, at least in young mice, suggests the presence of alternative molecular mechanisms. Two obvious possibilities include the ECM proteins fibronectin and vitronectin, which like fibrinogen, are also present at high levels in blood, and therefore also released into the parenchyma upon BBB disruption. We previously showed that fibronectin and vitronectin also stimulate microglial activation, as seen by morphological transformation into ameboid morphology, increased cell surface expression of Mac-1 and other “activation” integrins, α4 and α5, as well as MHC class II, and increased expression of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-9 [32]. Function-blocking studies revealed that fibronectin and vitronectin activate microglia not via the Mac-1 integrin, but via the α4 and α5 (fibronectin) and αvβ3 and αvβ5 (vitronectin) integrins.

Conclusions

To evaluate the importance of fibrinogen in mediating microglial repair of the hypoxia-disrupted BBB we studied these events in fibrinogen KO mice. We found that young (4 months old) fibrinogen KO mice were still able to mediate a vasculo-protective response though the extent of vascular leak in these mice was far greater than WT mice, though it should be borne in mind that this could be due to fibrinogen’s actions both in promoting blood coagulation and stimulating microglial vasculo-protection. In keeping with this, microglia in fibrinogen KO mice showed blunted levels of activation, proliferation, and aggregation around leaky blood vessels. Interestingly, defects in fibrinogen KO mice became more marked with age such that by 8-months old, almost half fibrinogen KO mice failed to survive 4 days CMH, and mice that survived had greater loss of myelin and neurons compared to WT. Finally, in vitro studies showed that fibrinogen promotes microglial morphological transition into a polarized migratory phenotype. These observations support the concept that fibrinogen plays an important role in promoting microglial vasculo-protection.

Experimental procedures

Animals

The studies described were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at San Diego Biomedical Research Institute (SDBRI). All mice were maintained under pathogen-free conditions in the closed breeding colony of SDBRI. Aα fibrinogen chain-deficient mice were obtained from Dr. Matthew Flick (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, NC, USA). Homozygous males (Fbg−/−) were bred with heterozygous females (Fbg+/−) to generate a mix of homozygous (fibrinogen knockout) and heterozygous offspring. Heterozygous mice were also bred together to generate wild-type (WT) control progeny that were used as controls. All offspring were genotyped as previously described [18].

Chronic hypoxia model

Male homozygous fibrinogen knockout (Fbg knockout) and wild-type (WT) mice were housed 5 to a cage, and placed into a hypoxic chamber (Biospherix, Redfield, NY) maintained at 8% O2 for periods up to 14 days. Littermate mice were kept in the same room under similar conditions except that they were kept at ambient sea-level oxygen levels (normoxia, approximately 21% O2 at sea-level) for the duration of the experiment. Every few days, the chamber was briefly opened for cage cleaning and food and water replacement as needed.

Immunohistochemistry and antibodies

Immunohistochemistry was performed on 10 μm frozen sections of cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS) perfused tissues as described previously [33]. Rat monoclonal antibodies reactive for CD31 (clone MEC13.3; 1:300) and Mac-1 (clone M1/70; 1: 50) were obtained from BD Biosciences (La Jolla, CA) and for CD41 (GPIIb, clone MWReg30; 1:100) and CD45 (clone 30-F11; 1: 500) were obtained from Biolegend (San Diego, CA). The hamster anti-CD31 (clone 2H8; 1:500) monoclonal was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, MA). Rabbit antibodies reactive for the following proteins were also used: Ki67 (1:4000 from Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO), fibrinogen (1:1500 from Millipore, Temecula, CA), hemoglobin (1:20,000 from Cloud Clone Corp, Katy, TX), TMEM119 (1:2000 from Genetex, Irvive, CA), and NeuN (clone EPR12763; 1:4000) from Abcam (Waltham, MA). Other rat antibodies used included anti-TER-119 (1:2000 from R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) and anti-CD68 (clone FA-11; 1:2000 from Bio-Rad laboratories, Irvine, CA). Fluoromyelin red (1:50) was obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA). Secondary antibodies used (all at 1:500) included Cy3-conjugated anti-rabbit and anti-rat and Cy5-conjugated anti-rabbit (from Jackson Immunoresearch (West Grove, PA), and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rat and anti-hamster from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA). For all IF staining, control staining of tissue sections was performed by switching primary antibodies for isotype control antibodies (for monoclonals) or normal rabbit serum (for rabbit polyclonals) to exclude the possibility of non-specific artefacts and these images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Image analysis

Images were taken using a 5X, 10X or 20X objective on an Axioskop2 plus microscope (Carl Zeiss, Dublin, CA, USA) equipped with an Infinity 3 S camera (Lumenera, Ottawa, ON, Canada) and Infinity Analyze imaging software (Lumenera). For each antigen, images of at least three randomly selected areas were taken at 5X, 10X or 20X magnification per tissue section and three sections per brain analyzed to calculate the mean for each animal (n = 3–10 mice per group). For each antigen in each experiment, exposure time was set to convey the maximum amount of information without saturating the image and was maintained constant for each antigen across the different experimental groups. The number of hemoglobin+ or TER-119+ vascular leaks or NeuN+ neurons per field of view (FOV) was quantified by capturing images and performing manual counts. Areas of vascular leak, Mac-1, CD68, or fluoromyelin were quantified by measuring the hemoglobin+ or TER-119+ area, Mac-1 or CD68 area, or fluoromyelin area/FOV using Image J. Microglial proliferation was quantified by counting the number of CD31/Ki67 dual-positive cells per FOV. The number of hemoglobin+ leaky blood vessels showing CD68+ microglial accumulation was quantified by capturing images and performing manual counts. Each experiment was performed with 3–10 different animals per condition, and the results expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post-hoc test or Student’s t test, in which p < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. RM was aware of the group allocation at all stages of the experiment.

Cell culture

Primary mouse microglia were obtained as described previously [34]. Briefly, mixed glial cultures (MGC) were prepared by culturing cell suspensions from postnatal day 0–2 mouse brains on poly-D-lysine coated T75 flasks cultured in high glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10 mM Glutamine (Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin/100 U/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen), and 10% FBS (Millipore). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2/95% air). Medium was changed every 3–4 days until cells reached confluence. After 7–10 days, flasks were shaken for 30 min and the supernatant (astrocyte conditioned medium (ACM)) containing detached microglia was collected. Microglia were then resuspended in N2 serum-free medium (DMEM supplemented with N2 (Sigma)) and applied to ECM substrates previously coated for 2 h at 37 C with a 10 µg/ml solution of fibronectin, fibrinogen, or laminin (all from Sigma).

Migration assay

Microglial migration was evaluated using a modification of the Varani assay previously described [35, 36]. In this assay microglia obtained from shaking a MGC were centrifuged and resuspended in a small volume of ACM medium. 7 µl drops of this cell suspension were then plated at the center of each well in a 24 well plate. One hour later, after all microglia had attached to the plate, the high-density microglial island was surrounded by 25 µl of ACM containing 10 µg/ml of fibronectin or fibrinogen to allow these ECM substrates to coat the surface outside the high-density drop. 2 h later, the well was filled with 0.5 ml of ACM. Over the next 48 h cells migrated away from the edge of the high-density population. The extent of migration in each condition was evaluated by measuring the distance between the edge of the drop and the leading edge of the migratory cell population on all four sides by light microscopy using a graticule within the microscope eyepiece. For each well, the mean cell migration on all four sides of the drop was calculated. Experiments were repeated three times, with n = 8 wells for each ECM substrate per experiment.

Immunofluorescence and imaging

Microglia were cultured on glass coverslips coated with fibronectin or fibrinogen (two hours with a 10 µg/ml solution) in ACM. After 24 h cells were fixed in methanol/acetone (1:1) for 20 min at -20 °C. All incubations were performed at 37º C. After three rinses in PBS, cells were blocked in the incubation buffer (5% normal goat serum in PBS) for 30 min, then labeled with a Mac-1 antibody (clone M1/70; 1: 50 from BD Pharmingen) for one hour. Following three rinses in PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h with goat anti-rat-Cy3, washed again, then mounted in DAPI-containing mounting medium (Sigma).
