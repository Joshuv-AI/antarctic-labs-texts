Management of CMV encephalitis with persistent CSF CMV DNA in a patient with HIV: a case report of successful treatment with ganciclovir–foscarnet combination therapy
Authors: Elif Tanyel, Cengizhan Tanyel, Server Sena Ersarı, Derya Seyman, Hande Berk Cam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a common opportunistic pathogen in immunocompromised individuals and can lead to significant mortality and morbidity via end-organ involvements. CMV related multiorgan involvement including encephalitis is rare and difficult to manage.We presented a case with multiorgan involvement of CMV including encephalitis, colitis, and pneumonia in a patient with HIV, in whom neurological symptoms worsened under ganciclovir monotherapy but responded to a combination therapy of ganciclovir and foscarnet.Case presentation A 30-year-old male was admitted to the hospital with complaints of cough and shortness of breath. Anti-HIV test was found to be positive. CD4+ T-cell count was 4 cells/mm³. A chest CT scan revealed extensive ground-glass opacities, and CMV PCR was positive in the bronchoalveolar lavage. During follow-up, the patient developed diarrhea, dysarthria, and seizures. CMV viral inclusion bodies were identified in colon biopsy samples, and CSF CMV DNA was positive. Brain MRI findings were consistent with CMV encephalitis. Pulmonary and gastrointestinal symptoms improved with ganciclovir, but neurological symptoms worsened. Despite no detected ganciclovir resistance, foscarnet was added on day 16 due to persistent high CSF CMV DNA levels and worsening neurological status. On the second day of the combination therapy, his seizures ceased, neurological symptoms improved. On the second month, CSF CMV DNA levels become undeceteable and he was able to return to his normal daily life. Maintenance therapy with valganciclovir continued nine months until the patient’s CD4+ T-cell count exceeded 100 cells/mm³.Conclusion This case underscores the potential benefits of ganciclovir–foscarnet combination therapy in patients with CMV encephalitis. It also highlights the role of serial CSF CMV DNA monitoring in guiding therapeutic decisions and assessing treatment response in severe cases. We suggest that ganciclovir-foscarnet combination therapy may offer a therapeutic advantage over monotherapy in treating CMV encephalitis, even in the absence of ganciclovir resistance.Keywords: Cytomegalovirus, Encephalitis, Foscarnet, Ganciclovir, HIV

Background

Opportunistic infections are a major cause of hospitalization in people living with HIV (PLWH), particularly those with advanced immunosuppression. Among AIDS-defining illnesses, CMV is one of the most clinically significant viral pathogens, resulting from reactivation of latent infection or reinfection, typically in patients with CD4 + T-cell count below 50 cells/mm³ [1, 2]. CMV retinitis and gastrointestinal involvement are the most common presentations, whereas involvement of other organs is uncommon [3]. Exceptionally rare cases of multiorgan involvement have also been reported [4]. CMV neurological disorders, may present as encephalitis, polyradiculomyelitis or peripheral neuropathies, are associated with high morbidity and mortality [5]. Especially CMV encephalitis has a limited clinical and microbiology response to antiviral therapy and remains a clinical management challenge [6].

Herein, we present a case with multiorgan involvement of CMV including encephalitis, colitis and pneumonia in a patient with AIDS. Neurological symptoms were unresponsive to ganciclovir monotherapy but resolved with the addition of foscarnet, suggesting potential benefit of combination therapy.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old Turkish male patient with no known medical history presented to the emergency department with complaints of progressive shortness of breath and dry cough lasting for one month. He had a cachectic appearance and a weight loss of 16 kg over the past eight months. Anti-HIV test was found to be positive. On initial clinical examination his temperature was 38.6 °C, pulse 112 beats/min, respiratory rate 24/min, and blood pressure 100/50 mmHg. His oxygen saturation was 95% while breathing ambient air. Examination of the respiratory system revealed rales in the basal regions of the lungs. Laboratory results were as follows: hemoglobin 9.3 g/dL, white blood count (WBC) 11,700/mm³ (neutrophils 10640/mm³ lymphocytes 370/mm³), platelets 350,000/mm³, LDH 532 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 55 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 113 U/L, CD4 + T-cell count 4 cells/mm³ and HIV RNA 677,851 copies/mL. Other biochemical tests were within normal levels. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed extensive ground-glass opacities. Empirical trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (15 mg/kg/day IV) was initiated due to high suspicion of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, considering the patient’s progressive dyspnea over the past month and elevated LDH (532 U/L). On the third day of treatment, the patient developed diarrhea and abdominal pain. No Cryptosporidium, Cyclospora or Isospora oocysts were seen in a modified acid-fast stained stool sample but Giardia intestinalis cysts and trophozoites were detected, leading to the initiation of metronidazole therapy. Abdominal ultrasonography (USG) showed diffuse wall thickening in the colonic segment, and a subsequent colonoscopy revealed fragile lesions with fibrin-coated surfaces and surrounding erythema. Biopsy samples were collected for pathological examination. Before initiating antiretroviral therapy (ART), bronchoscopy was performed. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) analysis showed no growth in bacterial, tuberculosis, or fungal cultures. However, CMV DNA was detected at 148,933 copies/mL in BAL and 313,526 copies/mL in plasma. Given the partial improvement in pulmonary symptoms, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole treatment was continued. The patient, who had a low CD4 + T-cell count, was started on dolutegravir, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) combination therapy. On the 15th day of follow-up, the patient developed cognitive and psychomotor slowing, facial fasciculations and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. He also had memory impairment and delayed response time and word-finding difficulty in speech.

Serological tests for anti-Toxoplasma IgM and IgG, VDRL, Treponema pallidum hemagglutination assay and QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus were negative. A lumbar puncture was performed, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings were as follows: non-xanthochrome, WBC 0/mm³, glucose 62 mg/dL, protein 140 mg/dL. CSF cultures (bacterial, fungal, and tuberculosis) showed no growth. While Cryptococcus neoformans antigen, JC virus PCR, and tuberculosis PCR were negative, CMV DNA was detected at 173,286 copies/mL and HIV RNA at 81,665 copies/mL in the CSF. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed signal enhancement in the lateral ventricles and bilateral frontoparietal regions, consistent with CMV encephalitis (Fig. 1). Meanwhile, CMV viral inclusion bodies were observed in the histopathological examination of the colon biopsy. Ophthalmological examination showed no indications of retinitis.

MRI findings in CMV encephalitis. Axial T2-FLAIR images show nonspecific hyperintense signals particularly surrounding the lateral ventricles. These findings, in the context of positive CSF CMV PCR, support the diagnosis of CMV encephalitis

Firstly, the patient was diagnosed with proven CMV colitis based on the detection of CMV viral inclusion bodies in colonic biopsy. Secondly, the presence of CMV DNA positivity in both BAL and CSF supported diagnosis of probable CMV pneumonia and encephalitis. Therefore, ganciclovir 5 mg/kg intravenously twice daily was initiated as induction therapy. Elevated AST and ALT levels were noted prior to treatment; however, the presence of CMV hepatitis could not be confirmed, as liver biopsy was not performed due to worsening mental status. On the third week of ganciclovir treatment, the patient’s symptoms of cough, shortness of breath, and diarrhea resolved completely, and AST and ALT levels returned to normal. However, due to worsening level of consciousness, increased seizure frequency, and newly developed right hemiparesis, a repeat lumbar puncture was performed. Ganciclovir treatment significantly reduced plasma CMV DNA levels to 2006 copies/mL, but no significant change was observed in CSF CMV DNA levels (161665 copies/mL), also CSF HIV RNA decreased to 47,412 copies/mL. Cervical and thoracolumbar spine MRI and electroencephalography showed no evidence of myelitis. IRIS was not considered due to persistently high CSF CMV DNA levels, and no evidence of significant cerebral edema or mass was observed; therefore, no corticosteroids or anti-edema therapy was given. Due to the patient’s worsening neurologic symptoms and uncontrolled CMV viral load in the CSF, foscarnet (60 mg/kg IV, three times daily) was added to the treatment regimen on the 16th day of ganciclovir therapy. On the second day of ganciclovir-foscarnet combination therapy, the patient’s fasciculations improved, and no further seizures were observed. CSF was sent to the reference center to investigate potential CMV drug resistance, ganciclovir resistance mutations were not found. By the 14th day of ganciclovir-foscarnet combination therapy, the patient’s mental status, memory, and speech improved completely, and CSF CMV DNA levels decreased to 1764 copies/mL. On the 21 st day of the combination therapy, he was able to mobilize independently, and CSF CMV DNA levels further declined to 335 copies/mL. In light of these notable clinical and virological improvements, the patient was switched to maintenance therapy with foscarnet (90 mg/kg once daily) and ganciclovir (5 mg/kg once daily). On the 28th day of combination therapy, cranial MRI showed resolution of findings consistent with CMV encephalitis. By day 62, CSF CMV DNA became undetectable (Table 1). Foscarnet and ganciclovir were discontinued, and the patient was discharged on maintenance therapy with valganciclovir (900 mg once daily), dolutegravir-emtricitabine-TDF, and a single tablet of trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole daily for PCP primary prophylaxis. No nephrotoxicity or any other adverse event occurred during the combination therapy with foscarnet and ganciclovir. He had no neurological sequelae and was able to return to his normal daily life in good health. Maintenance therapy continued nine months until the patient’s CD4 + T-cell count exceeded 100 cells/mm³. Discussion and conclusions

CMV is a significant opportunistic pathogen in patients with AIDS. In these patients, the most common clinical manifestation of CMV end-organ disease are retinitis and gastrointestinal involvement, while neurological involvement is rare [7]. Recently, In Brazil, at a referral center [8], prevalence of AIDS-related CMV encephalitis was found to be 2.4% among 248 cases with a CD4 + T-cell count ≤ 100/mm3, and 7.7% among 78 individuals with neurological opportunistic diseases in the late ART era. Additionally, multiorgan involvement of CMV has been reported quite infrequently in the literature [4]. In another study conducted by Caplan et al. in 2021 [7], while CMV gastrointestinal involvement was the most frequent with a rate of 68%, CMV retinitis was found to be 28%, pneumonia 8%, multi-organ involvement 4%, and no cases of CMV encephalitis were observed. Our case was difficult and instructive in terms of the diagnostic process and management of CMV treatment due to the presence of CMV multiorgan involvement including encephalitis.

CMV neurological involvement is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition in PLWH [9, 10]. The morbidity and mortality associated with CMV encephalitis are higher compared to other forms of CMV end-organ disease. The introduction of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) after 2000 significantly reduced the incidence of invasive CMV disease and improved long-term survival in PLWH [11–13]. However, over the past two decades, the number of patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy has been progressively increasing, thus CMV is becoming a clinically significant opportunistic pathogen among immunocompromised populations such as solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients, hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) recipients, and patients on biologic immunosuppressives [14]. Baghban et al. [15] analyzed 48 cases of CMV encephalitis between 2007 and 2017, only six of whom were PLWH, and reported a high mortality rate of 56.26% among the cases.

Ganciclovir, foscarnet, valganciclovir, and cidofovir are considered effective therapeutic options for the treatment of CMV infections. However the treatment of CMV neurological involvement is particularly challenging due to the poor bioavailability of antiviral drugs in CSF and possible drug resistance. Ganciclovir has been reported to have limited penetration into the CSF. In a study performed by Fletcher et al. [16], the pharmacokinetic effects of ganciclovir on human were observed in six patients with CMV pneumonia or retinitis in 1986, the CSF penetration rate of ganciclovir was found to range between 24% and 67%. Later, in 1999, Serabe et al. [17] investigated the plasma and CSF pharmacokinetics of ganciclovir in primates and determined a CSF penetration rate of 15.5 ± 7.1%. With a CSF penetration rate of 23–27% and concentrations that may reach up to 3.4-fold higher than plasma, foscarnet demonstrates superior distribution compared to ganciclovir [18, 19]. However, there is insufficient data in the current literature regarding the CSF pharmacokinetics of ganciclovir and foscarnet, and the available studies are relatively outdated. Further pharmacokinetic studies assessing the CNS penetration of ganciclovir and foscarnet are needed to better understand the mechanisms associated with the inability to control CSF CMV viral load. In our case, on the third week of ganciclovir monotherapy, gastrointestinal and pulmonary symptoms markedly resolved, liver enzymes returned to normal, and plasma CMV DNA levels decreased from 121,665 copies/mL to 2006 copies/mL. However, the patient’s worsening neurological symptoms and the persistent elevation of CSF CMV viral load suggest that inadequate penetration of ganciclovir into the CSF could be a possible explanation.

In the literature, there is limited information on clinical and microbiological efficacy of ganciclovir-foscarnet combination therapy. Only case series reports have demonstrated the superiority of combination therapy to ganciclovir monotherapy [20, 21]. In a prospective study conducted by Anduze-Faris et al. [22] before the HAART era, the efficacy of ganciclovir–foscarnet combination therapy was evaluated in 31 patients with CMV central nervous system (CNS) involvement, including 17 with encephalitis and 14 with myelitis. Clinical improvement or stabilization was observed in 74% of patients during induction therapy; however, relapse occurred in 43%, almost all of whom had encephalitis. In the HAART era, Silva et al. [9] reported outcomes of 13 AIDS patients with CMV-related neurological involvement, including nine with encephalitis and seven with polyradiculopathy (polyradiculopathy and encephalitis simultaneously in three). The mortality rate was lower among patients receiving ganciclovir–foscarnet combination therapy compared to those on monotherapy (20% & 50%). Although these rates are not statistically significant due to the small number of cases in the study, they may still provide guidance in treatment decisions. Notably, Palma et al. [5] reported in 2018 a case of a patient suffering from disseminated CMV disease with gastrointestinal and CNS involvement who showed limited clinical response to ganciclovir monotherapy. However, successful clinical improvement was achieved using combination therapy of foscarnet and ganciclovir followed by maintenance therapy with valganciclovir. Similarly, in our case, no neurogical improvement was achieved with ganciclovir monotherapy, the clinical and microbiological response were obtained with ganciclovir-foscarnet combination therapy. There are no randomized controlled trials or observational studies to support the efficacy and safety of combination therapy. Therefore, in the HIV Clinical Guidelines: Adult and Adolescent Opportunistic Infections, combination therapy is recommended with a CIII level of evidence. Likewise, the European AIDS Clinical Society Guidelines do not provide a clear recommendation for combination therapy, but state that ganciclovir may be used in combination with foscarnet, particularly in progressive or recurrent cases [1, 23].

CSF CMV DNA level has been reported to be useful in monitoring treatment response in CMV encephalitis [24]. In our case, the persistence of high CSF viral load led to the addition of foscarnet. Although a few case reports in the literature have described improvement with monotherapy, quantitative CSF CMV DNA levels during clinical follow-up have not been reported [25, 26]. Thus, we propose that the monitoring of CSF CMV DNA levels should be taken into consideration during clinical practice when switching to maintenance therapy and preventing potential relapse. In light of the fact that the factors influencing relapse and the duration of therapy are still not well defined for CMV CNS disease, we strongly recommend the use of ganciclovir-foscarnet combination therapy as the initial treatment, together with performing serial lumbar punctures to monitor CSF CMV DNA levels.

In conclusion, CMV diseases should be considered in PLWH and immunocompromised patients presenting with unexplained organ-specific symptoms. In CMV encephalitis, the duration of treatment and the risk factors associated with mortality and relapse are not clearly defined in guidelines, which significantly complicates the management of CMV CNS disease. Our case was particularly challenging to manage due to CMV-related multi-organ involvement including colitis, pneumonia, and encephalitis. This case is one of the few reports supporting the clinical and microbiology efficacy of ganciclovir–foscarnet combination therapy compared to ganciclovir monotherapy. We continued the combination therapy until symptoms resolved together with clearance of CMV-DNA in CSF for two months. Therefore we suggest that the combination therapy should be considered for an initial treatment in CMV encephalitis, especially PLWH with high CSF CMV DNA level. Moreover, serial CSF CMV DNA monitoring should be performed to assess treatment response and to guide decisions on when to escalate, swich to maintenance, or discontinue therapy. Further prospective studies are warranted to establish standardized management strategies for CMV encephalitis in immunocompromised populations.
