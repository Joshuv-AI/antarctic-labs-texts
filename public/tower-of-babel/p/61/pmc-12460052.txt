Assessment of prognostic value and development of predictive model for prolonged lymphopenia in patients with glioblastoma following chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Shuhei Takahashi, Kentaro Nishioka, Takashi Mori, Shigeru Yamaguchi, Yukitomo Ishi, Keiji Kobashi, Yoichi M Ito, Zen-ichi Tanei, Hiromi Kanno-Okada, Shinya Tanaka, Hidefumi Aoyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLymphopenia during chemoradiotherapy (CRT) for glioblastoma has been shown to be a poor prognostic factor. However, the relationship between prolonged lymphopenia (PL) after CRT and prognosis remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the relationship between PL and glioblastoma prognosis and develop a predictive model for PL risk. We analyzed 87 patients with primary glioblastoma who underwent postoperative CRT with 60 Gy in 30 fractions of radiotherapy and temozolomide. PL was defined as grade 2 or higher lymphopenia 1 month after the completion of CRT. We conducted survival analysis, identified risk factors for PL, and developed a predictive model for PL risk. Of the 87 patients, 41 developed PL, and progression-free survival (PFS) was significantly shorter in the PL group (median 8.0 months vs 15.4 months, P = 0.003). However, there was no significant difference in overall survival between the two groups. PL was also a significant factor for shorter PFS in multivariable analysis (P = 0.040). Brain V20Gy (percentage of brain volume receiving ≥20 Gy), gross total resection (GTR), and preoperative Karnofsky performance status (KPS) were identified as significant risk factors for PL. The predictive model showed that the risk of PL could be predicted by brain V20Gy, sex, age, GTR and preoperative KPS. PL was identified as a PFS shortening factor. Our model suggests that reducing irradiated brain volume may help prevent PL and could potentially improve glioblastoma prognosis by preserving cancer immunity.

INTRODUCTION

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults and has a very poor prognosis despite intensive treatment, including surgery, postoperative chemoradiotherapy (CRT), and adjuvant chemotherapy.

As novel approaches are sought to improve the prognosis of various malignancies, including GBM, there has been growing attention to incorporating cancer immunity in therapeutic strategies, particularly with the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Lymphocytes play a key role in cancer immunity and are known to be highly radiosensitive, with lymphopenia occurring even at low doses of radiation [1, 2]. Lymphopenia has been reported to correlate with the irradiated dose, field size, and dose-volume histogram (DVH) parameters of organs at risk, including the parenchymal organs and large blood vessels [1, 3–6]. Clinically, lymphopenia often occurs during radiotherapy (RT), and RT-induced lymphopenia is now recognized as a significant adverse event associated with shorter survival and higher recurrence rates in various malignancies, including GBM [3, 6–12].

Lymphopenia often persists for months or even years [12–14], so recovering from it after RT is crucial for long-term tumor control. While several studies have reported favorable outcomes for patients with pancreatic and esophageal cancer who recovered from radiation-induced lymphopenia [15, 16], few studies have focused on prolonged lymphopenia (PL) in GBM.

In this study, we focused on PL following postoperative CRT for primary GBM, analyzed its relationship with risk factors and prognosis, and developed a predictive model for its risk.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients cohort

Adult patients aged 20–70 years with newly diagnosed GBM who underwent initial surgery between July 2008 and May 2023 and CRT with 60 Gy in 30 fractions of RT and temozolomide (TMZ) at our institution were retrospectively reviewed. We excluded patients with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations, those who received systemic therapy before CRT, those censored before 2 months after CRT, and those without peripheral blood cell count results before and within 1 month after CRT. This study was approved by our institutional review board and registered under number 022-0179. All patient data were anonymized. The need for written informed consent was waived, but all patients in the study had the opportunity to opt out.

Treatment

Three-dimensional conformal RT (3D-CRT) or intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) was performed using the respective techniques. The gross tumor volume (GTV) included the residual tumor (defined as a lesion with gadolinium contrast enhancement or a very low signal in the T1-weighted image and surgical cavity). For clinical target volume (CTV) and the planning target volume (PTV), CTV1 was defined as a 15-mm expansion from the GTV and included a fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyper-intense lesion. CTV2 was a 15-mm expansion from the GTV for boost. Some CTVs were manually adjusted to accommodate anatomical structures. PTV1 and PTV2 were expanded by 3–5 mm from CTV1 and CTV2, respectively. In sequential RT, PTV1 received doses ranging from 40 to 50 Gy in 2 Gy fractions, while PTV2 was treated with 60 Gy in 2 Gy fractions. In simultaneous integrated boost RT, the prescribed dose was equivalent to that in sequential RT.

All the patients received concurrent TMZ (75 mg/m2 daily). After completion of CRT, patients received adjuvant TMZ (150–200 mg/m2 for 5 days during each 28-day cycle) unless adverse events prevented administration. Whether or not concurrent TMZ was discontinued and whether maintenance TMZ was administered for more than six cycles were recorded.

Lymphopenia

Absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) was assessed using peripheral blood counts. Lymphopenia was graded based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Each grade was defined as follows: 800 ≤ ALC < 1500/μl as grade 1, 500 ≤ ALC < 800/μl as grade 2, 200 ≤ ALC < 500/μl as grade 3, and ALC < 200/μl as grade 4. Based on a previous study [9, 17] and our preliminary analysis, we defined PL as grade 2 or higher lymphopenia occurring 1 month after the completion of CRT, regardless of the lymphopenia grade at the start of CRT. Blood test for the assessment of PL was performed in all patients prior to the initiation of maintenance TMZ. Identically, we defined baseline lymphopenia (BL) as grade 1 or higher lymphopenia at the start of CRT, and nadir lymphopenia (NL) as grade 1 or higher lymphopenia at the time of the lowest recorded ALC between the start of CRT and 3 months after its completion. Further information regarding lymphopenia at each time point is presented in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Tables S1–S3).

Statistical analysis

Age, gross total resection (GTR), preoperative Karnofsky performance status (KPS), sex, presence of methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) expression, and preoperative tumor size were assessed as baseline characteristics. The cutoffs were set as follows: 60 years or older for age, 95% or higher extent of resection for GTR, and 90% or higher for preoperative KPS. MGMT expression was evaluated by immunostaining. The method of evaluating immunostaining was performed using the scoring method described by a previous study [18]. Preoperative tumor size was defined as the product of the long and short diameters of the largest cross-section of the tumor on the axial slice, referring to the Response Assessment Neuro-Oncology (RANO) 2.0 criteria measurement method [19]. In the case of multiple lesions, the product of each was summed. For the RT parameter, only brain V20Gy (percentage of brain volume receiving ≥20 Gy relative to the whole brain) was used for analysis as the representative value of DVH parameters, as described by a previous study [3, 5]. In our study, the brain was defined as from the top of the skull to the foramen magnum.

Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank tests. Hazard ratios for the univariable and multivariable analyses were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Stratified Cox proportional hazards analysis was also performed to adjust for confounding factors. OS and PFS were calculated from the date of surgery. With respect to PFS, disease progression is determined according to the RANO criteria [19] and clinician-assessed progression. Baseline characteristics and brain V20Gy were compared using Fisher’s exact test or Welch’s t-test to investigate the risk factors for PL. Associations between PL and BL, as well as NL, were also investigated using Fisher’s exact test. For modeling to predict the risk of PL, age, GTR, preoperative KPS, sex, preoperative tumor volume, and brain V20Gy were selected as the variables. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed by regularization with an Elastic Net and internal validation using the leave-one-out method. All statistical analyses and modeling were performed using JMP Pro 17.0.0, except for stratified Cox proportional hazards analysis. Stratified Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed using R 4.5.0. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Risk factors of PL

The characteristics of the groups with and without PL are presented in Table 3. Median age was 61 years (range, 25–70). The numbers of patients with preoperative KPS ≥ 90%, 80%, 70%, 60% and ≤50% were 37, 26, 7, 10 and 7, respectively. The range of tumor size was 100–4480 mm2 and the range of brain V20Gy was 25.1–90.6%. TMZ was discontinued during CRT in 11 patients; however, none of the discontinuations were due to lymphopenia. Non-GTR (P = 0.042), worse preoperative KPS (P = 0.009), and higher brain V20Gy (P = 0.004) were identified as significant risk factors for PL. Furthermore, strong correlations with PL were found for BL (P < 0.001) and NL (P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in age, sex, MGMT expression, tumor size or concurrent TMZ discontinuation between the PL and non-PL groups.

DISCUSSION

This study indicated that PL was a significant PFS shortening factor and that brain dose reduction led to a decrease in the risk of PL, which is consistent with recent studies [12, 17]. Although the cutoff for radiation-induced lymphopenia in most previous studies has been regarded as ≥ grade 3, this study suggests that even ≥ grade 2 lymphopenia at 1 month after the completion of CRT shortens PFS and is an adverse event to be avoided.

To provide context for our focus on PL, it is important to note that lymphopenia following immunological injury, such as RT, chemotherapy or surgery [20], frequently lasts for months or even years. Recent studies have shown that RT for GBM induces myeloid-derived suppressor cells, resulting in long-term lymphopenia [14]. Although the Checkmate 498 [21] and Checkmate 548 trials [22] did not demonstrate an extension in OS with the addition of nivolumab to CRT for primary GBM, it has been suggested that ICIs may have a beneficial effect on the tumor microenvironment of GBM in a small study [23]. Thus, cancer immunity remains an important focus for developing novel therapeutic approaches for GBM. However, the mechanism by which PL is associated with recurrence remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

PL was a more dominant prognostic factor than was lymphopenia at the baseline and nadir in this study (Table 1). It should be noted that the P-value of BL for PFS is 0.06, which is close to the significance level and does not mean that BL is not correlated with PFS. The treatment approach depends on whether PL or BL is the true prognostic factor, but it is difficult to determine which is the true factor. However, previous studies have reported that recovery from lymphopenia is linked to prognosis [15, 16], and DVH parameter is linked to lymphopenia [3–5, 9–12, 17], which is consistent with our study. It suggests that PL may serve as a biomarker for the suppression of cancer immunity and that preserving lymphocytes in the mid- to long-term may be a promising therapeutic strategy.

Other issues to mention regarding the relationship between PL and prognosis are that age and GTR were significantly correlated to prognosis, in addition to PL, and the P-value of preoperative KPS for PFS (P = 0.058) was close to the significance level. Additionally, there may be other unknown prognostic factors. Furthermore, GTR and KPS were significantly correlated with PL, and the P-value of age for PL (P = 0.084) was also close to the significance level. These factors may confound the relationship between PL and prognosis. Although the results of stratified Cox proportional hazards analysis support that PL is a poor prognostic factor, PL should be regarded as just one of the prognostic factors.

Previous studies have suggested that brain dose and PTV volume are linked to lymphopenia [3–5, 9–12, 17], and that lymphopenia during CRT is caused by the irradiation of circulating blood [1]. In contrast, while brain V20Gy was identified as a significant risk factor for PL and an important variable in this study, preoperative tumor size did not significantly contribute to lymphopenia or the predictive model. Although brain dose often correlates positively with preoperative tumor size, it is also influenced by factors such as tumor location, extent of FLAIR hyper-intense lesion, planning technique, and RT modality. Therefore, if lymphopenia is caused by irradiation of circulating blood, then the DVH parameter should be more influential in predicting lymphopenia than the preoperative tumor size. Indeed, brain V20Gy was a more dominant variable than was the preoperative tumor size, supporting the hypothesis that lymphopenia is caused by the irradiation of circulating blood.

A reduction in the irradiated brain volume may lead to lymphocyte preservation, offering an effective approach to improve GBM prognosis. However, lymphopenia is related not only to the brain dose but also to patient characteristics [3–5, 9–12, 17]. Therefore, it is crucial to determine the extent to which the risk of lymphopenia can be reduced when the radiation dose and irradiated volume are reduced by a certain amount. To address this, we developed a predictive model for PL.

The significant variables identified in the survival analyses were primarily incorporated into this model; however, BL and NL were excluded. This is because BL and NL are so strongly correlated with PL (Table 3), leading to a problem of multicollinearity in modeling, which may make it impossible to assess the contribution of other background factors to the risk of PL. In addition, since surgery and lymphopenia may be related [20], baseline ALC is not a sufficiently reliable parameter as it may also vary with surgical invasion and time since surgery. For these reasons, we avoided including BL and NL in the model.

As an example of the advanced use of this model, we compared the predicted risk of PL between IMRT and 3D-CRT, assuming that other background factors were homogeneous. Table 5 presents the mean or median values of brain V20Gy and the difference in predicted risk (ΔRisk) of PL between the IMRT and 3D-CRT subgroups for several planning studies. In each study, plan simulations were performed using 3D-CRT and IMRT for the same patient, with a dose fraction of 60 Gy in 30 fractions. Despite variations in margin settings across studies, IMRT consistently resulted in lower brain V20Gy compared to 3D-CRT, which corresponded to a reduction in PL risk by approximately 5.90–10.53% according to the model. Although this was a narrative review, it allowed for a quantitative assessment of how dose reduction impacts the risk of PL. In this study, the mean brain V20Gy was 52.3% for the IMRT group (n = 19) and 63.0% for the 3D-CRT group (n = 68), with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.009). This represents a 9.7% lower brain V20Gy in the IMRT group, indicating that dose reduction with IMRT was expected. However, since plan comparisons were not performed for the same patients, we did not evaluate the differences in the predicted risk in this study. It should be noted that the definition of whole brain varies from study to study, such as brain minus GTV [24], brain minus PTV [25, 26] and excluding the brainstem but not the target [3, 4]. In this study, we assumed that irradiation of circulating blood leads to lymphopenia, and as there may be blood flow in the target and brainstem, we did not exclude them from the whole brain.

In addition to IMRT, there are several other approaches for reducing the dose to the brain. In a prospective randomized controlled trial comparing proton beam therapy (PBT) and IMRT [5], the mean brain V20Gy of PBT and IMRT were 37.4% and 53.6%, respectively. This suggests that proton therapy may reduce the risk of developing PL. In the case of limited-field RT with a smaller margin from the surgical cavity, the volume of circulating blood affected by irradiation is expected to decrease [1]. A previous retrospective study reported non-inferiority in OS and reduced lymphopenia with limited-field RT.[4] While hypofractionated RT may reduce the effective dose to circulating blood [1], a different retrospective study reported that lymphopenia was less severe in patients treated with hypofractionated RT (58.5 Gy in 25 fractions, 2.34 Gy per fraction) than in those treated with normal fractionated RT (60 Gy in 30 fractions, 2 Gy per fraction) [17]. However, it should be noted that limited-field RT may compromise the coverage of microscopic residual lesions, and hypofractionated RT may increase toxicity to normal tissues. Therefore, optimizing margin setting and RT dose prescription is necessary to preserve lymphocytes. A predictive model for PL may also be useful for evaluating different treatment modalities. To broaden the scope of the model, it is important to enhance it by incorporating cases with different treatments as training data.

The major limitations of this study include its retrospective observational nature and the small number of cases. Notably, age and GTR were poor prognostic factors for both PFS and OS, while no significant difference in OS was observed between the PL and the non-PL group. According to the survival curve for this OS, there were no mortality events in the PL group during the first 6 months from the start date, but the gradient of the curve was steeper in the PL group until approximately 2 years later, and the survival curves crossed. Due to insufficient observation of early mortality events, some bias in early events between groups had a marked effect on survival curves, which may have led to inconsistencies in PFS and OS results. This may be attributed to the limited sample size, which reduced statistical power. Additionally, patients who were transferred to hospice immediately after the completion of CRT were not included because of inadequate follow-up. Some of these patients were among the most severely ill and may have experienced the earliest recurrence or death. Therefore, it potentially leads to an overestimation of the survival time in the PL group. Moreover, since IMRT for GBM was recently introduced at our institution, the observation period was not long enough to evaluate OS and PFS in IMRT cases, which are expected to better preserve lymphocytes. Regarding other limitations, external validation of the model could not be performed owing to the small number of cases. Additionally, in evaluation of MGMT status, only a few patients were evaluated for methylation of the MGMT promoter region owing to cost and equipment limitations. Furthermore, all patients used in the analysis were treated with TMZ, and the treatment regimens were almost homogeneous. Therefore, the effect of TMZ on PL was considered equivalent in the PL and non-PL groups; however, it was not possible to distinguish the contribution of TMZ or RT to PL. Therefore, the results of this study should be considered hypothesis-generating. To establish lymphocyte preservation as an effective and novel therapeutic approach for GBM, it is necessary to develop a more reliable predictive model that includes a larger number of patients with diverse patient backgrounds and a long-term follow-up.

In conclusion, lymphopenia at 1 month after completion of CRT was associated with worse PFS. Brain V20Gy was a significant risk factor for PL and was the dominant variable in the predictive model for PL. Reduction of irradiated brain volume may lead to lymphocyte preservation and may be an approach to improve the prognosis of GBM.
