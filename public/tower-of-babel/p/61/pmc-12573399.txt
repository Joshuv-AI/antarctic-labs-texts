An evaluation of approaches to imaging for the pre-surgical assessment of intraocular foreign body size
Authors: Kujani Wanniarachchi, Hrishikesh Kaza, Antoine Safi, Tarini Ratneswaren, Arabella Poulson, Martin P Snead
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to assess the accuracy of CT imaging in estimating intraocular foreign body (IOFB) size by comparing pre-operative radiological measurements with post-surgical dimensions. A retrospective review of 12 cases over 9 years at a UK tertiary centre. IOFB size on CT imaging was compared with post-surgical measurements. Soft tissue window CT overestimated IOFB size (p=0.025). Bone window CT was more accurate but still varied, with one-third differing by>50% to definitive size. We conclude that caution is needed in CT-based IOFB size estimation. Plain radiographs may provide additional accuracy.Keywords: Trauma, Imaging, Orbit

Introduction

The incidence of globe injuries associated with an intraocular foreign body (IOFB) has been estimated to be 0.16 per 100 000 in the UK, primarily affecting the posterior segment.1 Workplace trauma, particularly hammering, is a leading cause,1 2 with 55–91% of IOFBs being metallic.2

Determining the presence, number, size and exact position of any IOFB(s) is an essential stage of the patient assessment. If the diagnosis is missed, IOFBs can lead to infection, retinal toxicity and vision loss. Iron and copper-containing IOFBs require urgent intervention due to siderosis bulbi and chalcosis.3 Imaging plays a critical role in confirming IOFB presence and guiding surgical planning, as the size of the exit sclerostomy will be functionally related to IOFB dimensions and any additional instrumentation deployed for removal.

Historically, plain radiographs were used but were inaccurate in differentiating intra- from peri-ocular IOFBs. B-scan ultrasonography poses risks for open globe injuries,2 and CT is now the preferred imaging modality due to its higher sensitivity, and the generally accepted detection threshold of IOFBs is approximately 0.06 mm.2 However, as metallic IOFBs are hyperdense, they are often associated with streak artefacts, which can distort their dimensions.2

CT accuracy depends on algorithm selection, particularly the kernel (reconstruction filter) and window (Hounsfield Unit range). Bone kernels enhance IOFB edge definition but introduce noise, whereas soft tissue kernels provide smoother images but reduce clarity.4 A window refers to the range of Hounsfield Units (HU) displayed and can be adjusted by the radiologist to enhance the visibility of desired tissues.

Previous studies have suggested that CT bone window and bone kernel may be the most accurate for the evaluation of metallic foreign bodies.5 To inform the surgical approach, it is useful for ophthalmologists to understand which CT imaging parameters yield the closest IOFB size estimate. To date, few studies have compared the definitive physical dimensions of IOFB with those predicted by radiography in patients.

Methods

Patient records from January 2014 – December 2022 with penetrating ocular trauma, confirmed IOFB and surgical management were reviewed. All patients had a non-contrast CT, and three patients also had plain radiography. IOFB removal was performed via globe exploration or pars plana vitrectomy, using either diamond dusted introcular foreign body forceps for small and medium sized IOFBs and an intraocular lasso for very large or awkward shaped IOFBs.6 Post-operative IOFB measurements (length and size) were obtained using a microscope and callipers. IOFB size was calculated by multiplying the two longest dimensions.

Twelve male patients (mean age: 38.1 years) were included. CT images were reconstructed using four algorithm combinations:

Results

Three patients had additional plain radiographs due to poor IOFB visibility on CT. The average ratio of plain radiograph IOFB length to post-operative length was 0.84 (range 0.45–1.04).

Discussion

This study investigated the discrepancy between pre-surgical radiological estimates of IOFB dimensions and post-surgical measurements. Briscoe and colleagues looked at a comparison between the actual size of IOFB and imaging (CT scan) in cows’ eyes, where they noted the magnification on CT scan for metallic IOFBs ranged from 1.17 to 2.3 times, with iron being the most overestimated and aluminium the least.7 We found BW/BK measurements to be the closest to true IOFB measurements (mean ratio: 1.06). This conforms with previous reports that BK/BW is superior to other algorithms for size estimation. Compared with Lin et al8 and Liu et al,9 our estimates showed less overestimation (mean ratios: 1.48 and 1.46, respectively).

Despite BK/BW’s accuracy, a third of cases showed over 50% discrepancy, and the range in ratio to definitive size was considerable (0.22–2.14). In one case, the BK/BW CT estimate was 1.8 mm, but the actual dimensions of the IOFB (which importantly was not visible because of media opacity pre-operatively) were more than four times that at 8 mm. Overestimation likely results from metallic blooming artefacts, while underestimation may occur when IOFBs are near bony structures or within soft tissue. Precise knowledge of IOFB size and dimensions is crucial for ophthalmologists in planning the surgical approach, particularly when they are not visible pre-operatively. A sclerostomy that is too small risks a failed extraction which could result in further displacement of the foreign body and collateral damage to structures such as macula.

The average ratio of plain radiograph IOFB length to definitive length was 0.84. Given the considerable variation in IOFB length even using the first line BK/BW CT, there may be a role for adjunctive plain radiography. Unlike CT, plain radiographs are less susceptible to blooming artefacts.10 Magnification correction via calibration markers or geometric calculations may improve plain radiograph accuracy. A study looking at the magnification of dental implants on panoramic radiographs found a range of magnification factors for the implant width of 111.09–126.02%.11

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that even with optimal CT parameters (BK/BW), IOFB size estimates vary. One case underestimated dimensions by a factor of four, thus, CT imaging should be interpreted with caution when planning the surgical approach, as the impact on incision size may be profound. Adjunctive plain radiography could help provide more reliable IOFB size estimates alongside CT imaging.
