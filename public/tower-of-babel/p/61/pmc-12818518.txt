Effects of Five-Element Music on Cancer-Related Fatigue and Negative Emotion in Patients with Lung Cancer
Authors: HuiFang Liu, GuangMei Xia, Qi Zhang, SiQi Liang, MianMian Mo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This research explored the potential benefits of five-element music in cancer-related fatigue (CRF) and mood disturbances among patients with lung cancer.Methods:This study included 460 hospitalised patients with lung cancer from July 2023 to February 2024. The participants were divided into two arms as follows: 217 received five-element music therapy and conventional nursing care (combined group), whilst 243 were assigned to conventional nursing care (routine group). Outcomes such as adverse reactions, Piper Fatigue Rating Scale (PFS-R) score and psychological status (Self-rating Anxiety Scale [SAS] and Self-rating Depression Scale [SDS] scores) were assessed before and after the care. The logistic regression model was used to explore the influence of five-element music on the above indicators.Results:Post-care data revealed declines in PFS-R scale scores for both groups, with the combined group exhibiting superior outcomes in cognition, behaviour, body and emotion (all P < 0.001). Psychological metrics (SAS/SDS) similarly favoured the combined group (all P < 0.001). The combined group demonstrated a significantly higher medication compliance rate than the routine group (92.63% vs. 81.48%, P < 0.001), underscoring the therapeutic advantage of the proposed approach. The results of logistic regression showed that significant associations were found between medication compliance and two key factors: anxiety scores (SAS, odds ratio [OR]: 1.055, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.013–1.100) and depression scores (SDS, OR: 1.246, 95% CI: 1.178–1.318).Conclusion:The application of five-element music therapy demonstrates potential benefits for patients with lung cancer, including alleviating CRF, mitigating negative emotions and improving medication compliance.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies in clinical practice.[1] Whilst recent advances in treatment have substantially extended patient survival,[2] many individuals continue to suffer from cancer-related fatigue (CRF). This condition is characterised by persistent exhaustion, emotional disturbances (such as anxiety, depression and irritability) and diminished treatment adherence, ultimately leading to immune dysfunction and reduced quality of life.[345] Consequently, identifying effective supportive care strategies to address these issues is a critical priority. Five-element music therapy, a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) intervention, offers a potential solution. Rooted in the theory of the five elements (metal, wood, fire, water and earth) and their correspondence with the five zang organs (lungs, liver, heart, kidneys and spleen), this approach utilises rhythmic sound waves to modulate qi (vital energy in TCM) flow. In TCM five-element theory (metal, wood, water, fire and earth), specific musical tones (e.g., Shang, Jiao, Zhi, Yu and Gong) are believed to correspond to specific zang organs (e.g., lung, liver, heart, kidney and spleen) and emotions (e.g., sorrow, anger, joy, fear and pensiveness). The rhythmic sound waves of targeted tones have been proposed to modulate qi flow, harmonise visceral functions and promote qi–blood balance (in TCM, ‘qi–blood balance’ is an important concept referring to the two basic substances and energy of ‘qi’ and ‘blood’ in the human body in a harmonious and balanced state. This concept is essential for maintaining health and preventing disease), thereby potentially enhancing psychological well-being, vitality and overall physical resilience.[67] Considering these mechanisms, this study investigated the effect of five-element music therapy on CRF and emotional distress in patients with lung cancer. The findings are presented in the following sections.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This work is a retrospective study. In accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 460 patients with lung cancer hospitalised in Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital from July 2023 to February 2024 were enrolled and allocated to groups according to actual treatment plans (July–December 2023 for routine care [routine group, n = 243] and November 2023–February 2024 for combined care [combined group, n = 217]. The study protocol received approval from the Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No. XYFY2024-KL081-01). All patients signed informed consent.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) pathologically diagnosed as stage I–III lung cancer and received chemotherapy, (3) complete medical records, (4) received routine care alone or in combination with five-element music. A total of 473 cases met the inclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria: (1) severe cardiac or renal dysfunction, (2) malignant tumours in other sites, (3) pregnancy or lactation, (4) psychiatric disorders and auditory impairments. Observation Indicators

The following indicators were measured 1 day before the start of treatment and on the 2nd day after the end of treatment.

CRF

Piper Fatigue Rating Scale (PFS-R)[10]: This scale consists of four dimensions: cognitive, behavioural, somatic and emotional, with a total of 22 items. The scale uses a visual analogue scale from 0 to 10 for scoring, with the total fatigue score ranging from 0 to 220. The higher the score, the more severe the fatigue. The average score is the total score divided by 22. The scoring methods for each dimension are the same as those for the total score; that is, the total score of this dimension is divided by the number of items in this dimension. Cronbach’s α is 0.867.

Psychological Status

Anxiety and depression levels were measured using the following:

Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)[1112]: This scale consists of 20 items, among which 5 items are scored in reverse, and 15 items are scored positively. All items are rated using the Likert 4-point scale. The raw score ranges from 20 to 80, and the standard score = raw score × 1.25. Scores ≥50 indicate clinical anxiety. Cronbach α is 0.782.

Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS)[1213]: This scale consists of 20 items, among which 10 items are scored in reverse, and 10 items are scored positively. All items are rated using the Likert 4-point scale. The raw score ranges from 20 to 80, and the standard score = raw score × 1.25. Scores ≥53 suggest depression. Cronbach α is 0.793. High scores on both scales reflect severe symptoms.

Medication Compliance

The compliance of the two groups of patients was observed, and the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale[14] was used to investigate their medication compliance. This scale includes eight questions. The total score is 0–8 points: full compliance: 8 points; partial compliance: 6–7 points and noncompliance: 6 points. Total compliance rate = (full compliance + partial compliance) number of cases / total number of cases × 100%. Cronbach α is 0.754.

Statistical Methods

All data were analysed with SPSS software (International Business Machines, version 23.0). The normality of quantitative data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, supplemented by visual inspection of Q–Q plots and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis (with absolute values <2 and <7 indicating approximate normality). For normally distributed quantitative data, means ± standard deviations were calculated, and group differences were assessed via independent samples t-tests. Paired sample t-test was used for intragroup differences. Qualitative variables were reported as proportions (%) and evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test. Variables with P < 0.05 were selected for multivariate logistic regression analysis using the likelihood ratio test method of stepwise forward maximum partial likelihood estimation. A P-value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance. The pictures in this article were created in PowerPoint (Microsoft, version 16.0.19029.20136) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, version 8.0.2).

DISCUSSION

Patients with lung cancer often experience pronounced symptoms such as chest pain, coughing and haemoptysis. When clinical radiotherapy and chemotherapy are administered, the adverse effects of these treatments can further physically burden the patients and diminish their quality of life.[15] When confronted with death, humans tend to exhibit extreme emotions. Some patients may even refuse treatment or engage in other avoidant behaviours because of their inability to accept the reality.[16] With the growing emphasis on humanistic care in clinical medicine, the personal feelings of patients are increasingly valued. Five-element music, a branch of TCM, has been used in music therapy in China for a long time. Listening to appropriate music can have a certain auxiliary therapeutic effect on the five zang organs.[1718] This study applied five-element music to improve the condition of patients with lung cancer, which has been proven to be effective in mitigating their negative emotions.

Music therapy has emerged as a cutting-edge, interdisciplinary science by integrating medicine, psychology, aesthetics, special education and physical biology. It has been widely applied in clinical practice. Specific melodies and rhythms can influence people’s emotions, thereby eliminate psychological disorders and restore mental health.[19] In ancient China, sages in texts such as the ‘Yellow Emperor’ and ‘Yue Ji’ discussed the use of music in treating diseases. Five-element music therapy integrates the principles of the five elements to provide targeted psychological interventions for patients. It enhances emotional states, cognitive and social functions and overall quality of life of patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy.[20]

CRF is a distressing and enduring sensation of weariness that is highly prevalent among patients with cancer. The exact mechanisms underlying CRF remain elusive domestically and internationally, and current treatments primarily rely on pharmacological interventions and health education. As a result, established clinical therapies remain scarce, with few being highly effective. According to TCM and as stated in ‘Suwen • Comment on the theory of deficiency and reality’: ‘Essence is virtual’. Hence, the treatment of CRF in TCM often focuses on replenishing deficiency. Lung cancer is categorised as ‘lung accumulation’ in TCM. As the disease progresses and worsens, it is inevitable that the vital qi will be depleted. Whilst targeting tumour cells, chemotherapy also impairs the body’s vital qi (a fundamental idea that refers to the body’s natural ability to maintain health and resist illness), thereby exacerbating fatigue symptoms.[21] Therefore, strengthening the spleen and protecting the stomach, boosting healthy qi, alleviating CRF symptoms and enhancing patients’ quality of life are crucial during treatment. The PFS-R scale scores of the combined group were lower after the implementation of the caring method compared with those of the routine group. This finding indicates that combining five-element music with positive psychological care can effectively improve CRF in patients with lung cancer. This effect may be attributed to the potential of five-element music to modulate activity in the brainstem reticular formation and the cerebral cortex, potentially reducing sympathetic nerve excitability.[22] It helps shift attention, regulate emotions and relax the spirit, thereby effectively improving CRF in patients with lung cancer.

TCM’s five-element music therapy is grounded in the musical theories of ‘Yueji’ and the five-tone concepts from ‘Huangdi Neijing’. Over time, it has developed into a comprehensive ideological framework for music therapy in TCM. This method focuses on the patient’s specific syndromes and conditions, adhering to the TCM principle of syndrome differentiation and treatment. As a result, five-element music can alleviate clinical symptoms and eliminate pathogenic factors.[2324] This therapy is a well-established intervention for managing mood disorders, including depression, anxiety and menopausal-related distress. In this study, the patients receiving this therapy demonstrated remarkably lower SAS and SDS scores post-care compared with baseline. The reduction in these scores was more pronounced in the combined group than in the routine group, suggesting the therapy’s efficacy in alleviating anxiety and depressive symptoms. Additionally, this therapeutic approach helps regulate emotional disturbances, such as depressive states, whilst stabilising physiological organ function. By fostering emotional and physical balance, this music therapy supports patient recovery and is generally well-tolerated, enhancing treatment adherence. However, this study has several limitations, including a relatively small sample size, limited follow-up duration and reliance on subjective patient-reported outcomes. The grouping is in different seasons, making it difficult to reflect the physiological state, disease distribution or behaviour pattern of the population in different seasons. Hence, the overall characteristics are not well represented. The five-element music therapy nursing protocol was only administered during the 6 days of hospitalisation; the continued implementation of care after the patient’s discharge was not monitored. The lack of objective measures may introduce bias, underscoring the need for future research with large cohorts, extended observation periods and robust evaluation criteria to validate these findings.

CONCLUSION

Five-element music therapy combined with conventional care demonstrated potential benefits in reducing CRF, mitigating negative emotions and improving medication compliance in hospitalised patients with lung cancer. As a culturally resonant, nonpharmacological intervention, it warrants further investigation in large, rigorously designed randomised controlled trials with long follow-up periods and objective outcome measures.
