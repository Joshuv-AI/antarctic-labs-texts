Bioengineered milk-derived extracellular vesicles implementing high drug loading and membrane integrity for efficient oral drug delivery
Authors: Mingjie Ni, Liyun Xing, Yating Wang, Xi Liu, Lie Zhang, Yuting Li, Lian Li, Yuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Milk-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) are promising for oral drug delivery, yet different loading methods exhibit distinct impacts on drug encapsulation and membrane integrity. This study demonstrated that sonication method achieved high drug encapsulation in commercial milk-derived EVs (S-CM EVs), but impaired EV structure, compromising transcytosis. Incubation method (I-CM EVs) preserved EVs delivery ability, but had low drug loading. Further proteomic and transmembrane studies showed that sonication greatly damaged membrane proteins involved in trans-epithelial transportation, especially endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi pathway. To overcome this dilemma, we generated a hybrid CM EVs (H-CM EVs) by fusing I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs. H-CM EVs demonstrated comparable drug encapsulation to S-CM EVs (56.14%), significantly higher than I-CM EVs (11.92%). Importantly, H-CM EVs could maintain efficient drug delivery capability by restoring membrane fluidity, repairing damaged proteins, and enhancing enzyme resistance of S-CM EVs. H-CM EVs exhibited excellent absorption characteristics with 1.85-fold higher of area under the curve and 2.50-fold higher of max plasma concentration than those of S-CM EVs. On type Ⅰ diabetic mice, orally delivery of insulin loaded H-CM EVs and I-CM EVs showed improved hypoglycemic effects with pharmacological availabilities of 5.15% and 5.31%, which was 1.7-fold higher than that of S-CM EVs (3.00%). This H-CM EVs platform not only achieved high drug loading and maintained functionality for effective oral delivery but also highlighted the significant translational potential for improved clinical outcomes.

Introduction

In recent years, therapeutic protein drugs are an essential class of pharmaceuticals for treating various diseases, from oncology to autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Although injection continues to be the dominant administration route for these protein-based therapeutics [[5], [6], [7]], oral administration remains the most common and preferred route for delivering small molecule drugs as well as traditional medicines, owing to its superior patient compliance compared with injectable formulations [8,9]. Despite progress in the oral delivery of certain peptide drugs in recent years, the oral bioavailability of larger therapeutic proteins, such as insulin, continues to pose a critical challenge. Currently, commonly used strategies for oral delivery of protein drugs include enzyme inhibitors and/or absorption enhancers [10], nanoparticle carriers [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]], intestinal targeting systems [16,17], and microencapsulation [18,19]. However, the primary barriers to effective oral delivery are the low drug loading efficiency and their limited ability to penetrate the intestinal mucosal barrier in most drug carriers [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Overcoming these obstacles is essential for realizing the full potential of therapeutic proteins through the oral route of administration.

EVs possess the inherent capability to carry and transport biomolecules such as DNA, RNA, and proteins [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Milk, a widely consumed beverage, is rich in EVs [28]. This complex secretory product is designed to regulate neonatal development, and its ongoing consumption may have significant effects on human health [29]. Both raw and commercial bovine milk contain EVs that are rich in RNAs and proteins [30]. Milk-derived EVs surfaces are enriched in numerous resistant glycoproteins such as ALIX, EPCAM, and FLOT-1, which confer the ability to resist digestion by gastrointestinal enzymes and stomach acid [31]. Therefore, milk-derived EVs can provide protection to luminal cargo, including proteins and RNA, from harsh degrading conditions. And recent studies indicate that these EVs can be taken up by human and murine gut epithelial cells in vitro [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]].

However, there are still many factors that limit the application of milk-derived EVs, among which drug loading efficiency and the integrity of membrane are two indispensable attributes that determine the final therapeutic outcome. In terms of drug loading efficiency, the drug loading methods for milk-derived EVs are incubation, freeze-thaw, sonication, saponin permeabilization, and electroporation. Among these methods, incubation and sonication are mostly commonly used. Incubation relies on spontaneous and passive material exchange, thus leading to low drug encapsulation but little effect on membrane integrity [37]. Sonication methods may rupture the membrane integrity of the EVs, ensuring sufficient drug infiltration into EVs [38]. But it would also significantly reduce oral absorption efficiency of milk-derived EVs as reported [28,39]. Therefore, different drug loading methods can differently impact the drug encapsulation efficiency and membrane integrity of EVs. It is essential to explore a suitable drug loading method that enables milk-derived EVs to achieve high drug encapsulation while preserving membrane integrity.

In this study, we isolated EVs from both raw and commercial bovine milk (RM EVs and CM EVs) and found no difference. Then, the hybrid milk EVs (H-CM EVs) platform was proposed by fusing S-CM EVs and I-CM EVs using polyethylene glycol (PEG) to combine the advantages of both methods. This H-CM EVs platform not only retained the high drug encapsulation efficiency comparable with S-CM EVs, but also facilitated transcellular transport with similar efficiency with I-CM EVs. When insulin was encapsulated, H-CM EVs demonstrated pharmacological availabilities of 5.15%, achieving approximately 1.70-fold enhancement in oral drug absorption compared to S-CM EVs. Compared to other carriers such as PLGA nanoparticles and liposomes, H-CM EVs, derived from milk, exhibited better biocompatibility thereby reducing the risk of adverse reactions [40,41]. Besides, it also had advantages such as greater stability in the gastrointestinal tract and higher absorption compared to other carriers [42,43]. As oral insulin delivery vehicles, H-CM EVs can enhance patient adherence compared to traditional subcutaneous insulin formulations. Importantly, H-CM EVs significantly improved the glucose-lowering efficacy of orally administered insulin on type I diabetic mice models. Materials and methods

Materials

Porcine insulin (28.2 IU/mg) was sourced from Jiangsu Wanbang Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Streptozotocin (STZ) and Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) were sourced from Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was purchased from J&K Scientific Ltd. (Beijing, China). Gly-Sar was obtained from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (DiI) was acquired from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, USA). 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO), 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), Lyso-Tracker Green, ER-Tracker Green and Golgi-Tracker Green were obtained from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM, ULTROL grade) was purchased from Merck. PEG 8000 (BioUltra grade) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. All other chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA) and Shanghai Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and used as received unless noted otherwise.

HT29-MTX cell were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen) with high glucose, 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma), 1% (v/v) nonessential amino acid, 1% (v/v) L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (100 IU/ml). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Human colon cancer cell line Caco-2 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of Typical Culture Collection Committee (CBTCCC) in China. And Caco-2 cells were cultured under the same conditions as HT29-MTX cells. ICR and C57 mice were purchased from Chengdu Dashuo Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Animal housing and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University.

Isolation and characterization of milk-derived EVs

Raw milk was collected from prairie cattle during the mid-lactation period. Five batches of commercial defatted bovine milk (from Inner Mongolia Yili Industrial Group Co., Ltd.) were purchased from a local grocery store. The milk was mixed with acetic acid (100:1, v/v), which changed the pH from 6.5 to 4.5, corresponding to the isoelectric point of the abundant casein in the milk [44]. And then incubated at room temperature for 5 mi, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane to obtain whey. As previously reported researches [45], the whey was then ultracentrifuged at 210,000 ×g for 70 min at 4 °C (Beckman Coulter, SW40Ti rotor, Brea, CA, USA) to collect milk-derived EVs (RM EVs and various batches of CM EVs) in the pellet. In this centrifugation conditions, we can get high purity of milk-derived EVs [44]. The EVs pellet was resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and subjected to ultracentrifugation again. The pellet was then resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove any remaining precipitates. Total protein concentration was measured using a BCA kit. Finally, EVs were stored at −80 °C until needed.

The size and zeta potential of RM EVs and various batches of CM EVs were measured using a Zeta Sizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). transmission electron microscope (TEM) was used to examine the morphology of RM and CM EVs. EV samples were negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 3 min prior to observation. Western blotting (WB) was used to detect the marker proteins CD63, TSG 101 and ALIX in the EVs. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane, which was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary rabbit polyclonal antibodies, followed by a 2 h incubation at 37 °C with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent substrate and imaged with a Bio-Rad Chemi-Doc MP imager. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was conducted using a NanoSight LM10 machine (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK), equipped with a 405 nm laser sample chamber. EVs (1 mg/ml) were disaggregated before injection into the NanoSight sample chamber, capturing 30 frames per second. Experimental parameters included a camera level of 6, detection threshold of 16, flow rate of 50, and temperature set at 25 °C. Data analysis was performed using NanoSight NTA 3.2 software.

To detect the membrane compactness of CM EVs, Laurdan dye (0.1 µM/l) was added to CM EVs (200 µg) suspended in 2 ml PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min in the dark. After the loading of the dye, Laurdan excitation spectra (340 nm at both 435 nm and 490 nm emission wavelengths were obtained at 37 °C. The generalized polarization (GP) values were calculated according to the Eq. 1:(1)GP=I435−I490I435+I490where I435 and I490 were the emission intensity of 435 and 490 nm, respectively.

Synthesis of FITC labeled insulin

Insulin (17.0 Mm) was initially dissolved in 6 ml hydrochloric acid (HCl, pH 2), followed by pH adjustment to 7–8 using sodium hydroxide. Separately, 17.0 mM FITC was dissolved in 1 ml acetone. This FITC solution was added dropwise to the insulin solution while stirring, and the mixture was reacted at 4 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, the pH was adjusted to 5.4 using 1 M HCl to precipitate the product. The precipitate was then dissolved in a 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) solution and placed in a 2000 Da dialysis bag, undergoing dialysis at 4 °C for 48 h with NaHCO3, followed by 72 h of dialysis with deionized water. Finally, the FITC-labeled insulin (FITC-INS) was obtained through lyophilization.

Preparation of drug loaded CM EVs

Preparation and characterization of the I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs

Insulin was loaded in CM EVs via incubation and sonication. For incubation, CM EVs (1 mg/ml) and insulin (INS) (8 mg/ml) were mixed and incubated in a shaker for 15 min (60 rpm, 37 °C) to obtained I-CM EVs@INS. For sonication, CM EVs (1 mg/ml) and INS (8 mg/ml) were mixed and sonicated (10% amplitude, 3 min, 2 s/2 s on/off, 3 cycles) to obtain S-CM EVs@INS. The enzymatic stability experiments were performed as reported [22].

Preparation and characterization of the H-CM EVs

I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs were mixed at a I-CM EVs /S-CM EVs ratio of 1/1 (2 × 108 objects) in PBS (pH 7.4) at 40 °C. Objects were counted with nanoparticle tracking analysis, as described above in 2.2. It was reported that PEG8000 could facilitate close contact and dehydration of lipid bilayers [46]. To investigate the best fusion concentration of PEG8000, PEG was added at a final concentration of 5%−20% (w/v). The resulting sample was then centrifuged at 2,500 g for 10 min. The fused CM EVs was named as H-CM EVs. The morphology of H-CM EVs was observed using TEM, and H-CM EVs were negatively stained with phosphotungstic acid (2%) for 3 min before observation.

Lipid mixing during the fusion of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs was monitored using förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), as previously described [46]. Briefly, donor fluorescent S-CM EVs were prepared with an equal molar ratio (1.5%) of the fluorescent dyes DiO and DiI. Lipid mixing was assessed by measuring the DiO fluorescence intensity (excitation at 460 nm, emission at 501 nm) over time at 40 °C. After 2 h, the fusion reaction was halted by adding DDM (2.5%, w/v) to solubilize all EVs, enabling the measurement of the maximum DiO fluorescence intensity at infinite dilution (MaxDiO). Preparation of insulin loaded H-CM EVs: CM EVs (1 mg/ml) and INS (8 mg/ml) were mixed followed by incubation (60 rpm, 3 h, 37 °C) or sonication (10% amplitude, 3 min, 2 s/2 s on/off, 3 cycles) to prepare I-CM EVs@INS and S-CM EVs@INS. Then, I-CM EVs@INS and S-CM EVs@INS were mixed with final concentration of 20% PEG for 2 h. PEG8000 was removed by ultrafiltration to obtain H-CM EVs@INS.

In vitro drug release experiment: CM EVs@INS were allowed to release insulin in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2) for 2 h, followed by 6 h in simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8). A 2 ml suspension of CM EVs@INS was placed in dialysis devices (MWCO: 100 kDa) immersed in the release medium. At specified time points, 100 µl samples were taken from the dialysis tube. To disrupt the CM EVs, 1% Triton-X was added, and the remaining insulin was analyzed using HPLC to calculate the release profile.

To assess colloidal stability, I-CM EVs@INS, S-CM EVs@INS and H-CM EVs@INS were incubated in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2) for 8 h, simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8) for 24 h, shaken at 60 rpm and 37 °C. The particle size and zeta potential of EVs before and after insulin loaded were measured using a Zeta Sizer Nano ZS90. And the sizes stability of insulin loaded EVs in SGF (pH 1.2) and SIF (pH 6.8) were monitored at specified time points. To assess the stability of CM EVs under acidic conditions similar to those in the human stomach. One milligram of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs was added to 2 ml SGF (pH 1.2) or PBS (pH 7.4). And incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The samples were then ultrafiltered for 15 min at 4,000 g. The protein content of the recovered CM EVs was measured using a BCA assay to determine the percentage of protein recovery.

Assessment of stability: insulin loaded I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs were suspended in PBS (pH 7.4, 25 °C). The protein level was measured by BCA kit. The size, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta-potential were measured at specific time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 24 h). After ultrafiltration, the insulin encapsulation efficiency of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs was also evaluated by HPLC over the same period. And storage stability studies of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs were performed over a period of 4 weeks with interval measurements performed at 2 weeks, for samples stored at −80 °C, −20 °C and 4 °C. Size, PDI, zeta-potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and protein concentration level was detected using BCA kit.

Cell study

Cell viability assay

Caco-2 cells were digested and seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After 4 d of cell growth, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with pre-warmed PBS (pH 7.4). Next, CM EVs were added and incubated for 4 h. After removing the CM EVs, the cells were washed with PBS (pH 7.4) again and incubated with Alamar Blue (0.01 mg/ml) for 1 h. The fluorescence was measured using a Microplate Reader, and the cell viability of each group was calculated.

Cellular uptake and transepithelial transport study

For cellular uptake, Caco-2 cells were digested and seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After 4 d of cell growth, the culture medium was removed and 0.01 mg/ml Alamar Blue was added for incubation for 1 h. The relative cell number per well was corrected by measuring fluorescence (excitation wavelength: 530 nm; emission wavelength: 590 nm). Next, the cells were incubated with CM-EVs@FITC-INS for 3 h. Then, the samples were removed and the uninternalized CM EVs were washed by with PBS (pH 7.4). Finally, DMSO was added (100 µl per well) and the fluorescence intensity of FITC was quantified by Microplate Reader. After correcting for cell number, the relative value of cell uptake was obtained. For endocytic mechanisms studies, Caco-2 cells were pre-treated with different inhibitors for 30 min before adding CM EVs. The cells were then incubated with the CM EVs and inhibitors for another 3 h. To investigate the cellular internalization of EVs and the influence of mucus, Caco-2 cells and HT29-MTX cells were co-cultured at a 9:1 ratio with a density of 3 × 104 cells per well on 96-well plate to establish a differentiated monolayer with mucus production [47]. The cells were divided into two parts. One part was pretreated with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) to remove secreted mucus layer prior to uptake determination. Another part was non-pretreated. Then both pretreated and non-pretreated cells were incubated with different samples for 3 h, which was followed by a thorough washing process to remove the remained mucus and the attached samples. Then the cell lysis buffer was added, and the cell-related fluorescence was determined. The total protein was detected using BCA kit. The intake of FITC-INS was expressed as the number of FITC-INS associated with 1 mg of cellular protein.

For transepithelial transport study, Caco-2 cells and HT29-MTX cells were co-cultured at a 9:1 ratio with a density of 3 × 104 cells per well on Transwell inserts to establish a differentiated monolayer with mucus production. Perform medium was changed every other day for the first 8 d, and the transmembrane electrical resistance (TEER) values were measured. After 15 to 20 d of cell growth, cells (TEER >300 Ω·cm2) were selected for the experiment. Prior to the experiment, the cells were incubated with HBSS for 30 min of equilibration. Then, CM EVs@FITC-INS dispersion (200 µl) was added to the A chamber and HBSS (800 µl) was added to the B chamber. At specific time points, 80 µl samples from the B chamber were collected. An equal volume of DMSO was mixed with samples and the fluorescence intensity of FITC was measured by Microplate Reader. The apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) was calculated using Eq. 6, where dQ/dt was the rate of FITC-INS diffusion from A chamber to B chamber, C0 was the initial concentration of FITC in the A chamber, and A was the area of the polyester membrane.(6)Papp=dQdt×A×C0

To further verify the effect of PEG, we added PEG to S-CM EVs or I-CM EVs (PEG concentration and preparation procedures were consistent with the preparation of H-CM EVs), and then incubated with cells to investigate the cellular uptake and transcytosis efficiency.

Bidirectional exocytosis study

Caco-2 cell monolayers were seeded in Transwells with polycarbonate membranes. After a 30 min equilibration, free FITC-INS and CM EVs@FITC-INS were added to the apical side for 3 h. The apical and basolateral sides were then washed with ice-cold HBSS and replaced with fresh HBSS medium. At specific time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4 h), samples were collected from both sides, and an equal volume of HBSS was added. The fluorescence intensity of the samples was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). To examine the distribution of intracellular CM EVs during exocytosis, the cell monolayers were collected after 3 h of administration. Following DAPI staining, the samples were observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM800, Carl Zeiss).

Proteomic analysis of different CM EVs

All analyses were performed by a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo, USA) equipped with a Nanospray Flex source (Thermo, USA). Samples were loaded and separated by a C18 column (15 cm × 75 µm) on an EASY-nLCTM 1200 system (Thermo, USA). The flow rate was 300 nl/min and total run was 75 min (0–40 min, 5%−28% B;40–60 min, 28%−42% B;60–65 min, 42%−90%B;65–75 min, 90%B; mobile phase A = 0.1% FA in water and B = 0.1% FA in ACN). Full MS scans were acquired in the mass range of 300−1600 m/z with a mass resolution of 70,000 and the AGC target value was set at 1e6. The ten most intense peaks in MS were fragmented with higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with NCE of 32. MS/MS spectra were obtained with a resolution of 17,500 with an AGC target of 2e5 and a max injection time of 80 ms. The Q-E dynamic exclusion was set for 30.0 s and run under positive mode. Proteome Discoverer (v.2.4) was used to search all of the Q-Exactive raw data thoroughly against the sample protein database. Database search was performed with Trypsin digestion specificity. Alkylation on cysteine was considered as fixed modifications in the database searching. For protein quantification method, labeling method (TMT, 18 labels) was selected. A global false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 0.01 and protein groups considered for quantification required at least 2 peptides (Table S1).

In situ intestinal absorption study

Healthy C57BL/6 J mice were fasted overnight but had access to water. The mice were anesthetized, and laparotomy was performed to expose the intestine. Intestinal loops of ∼3 cm were created in the duodenum, jejunum and ileum of both healthy and pathological mice, followed by the injection of fluorescent CM EVs. After 2 h, the mice were sacrificed, and the loops were excised and washed with Krebs Ringer (K-R) buffer. The samples were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated overnight in 30% sucrose, embedded in optimum cutting temperature compound (OCT), and sectioned to a thickness of 10 µm (Lecia, Germany). Following DAPI staining for nuclei visualization, the samples were imaged using CLSM.

Integrity of EVs after crossing the intestinal barrier

I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs were labeled with equimolar of FRET dye pairs (CY3 and CY5) in 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) at 25 °C for 10 min. 1 h after oral administration to C57BL/6 J mice, blood samples were collected, and plasma was isolated. And then the fluorescent spectra of EVs-Cy5/Cy3 in plasma were recorded at the Cy3 excitation wavelength of 550 nm.

In vivo pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies

Pharmacokinetic studies

ICR mice were used to investigate the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of drug-loaded CM EVs delivered orally. The mice received an oral dose of 5 mg/kg of milk-derived EVs loaded with CY5-INS, while the subcutaneous injection group was given 0.1 mg/kg of CY5-INS. Blood samples (20 µl) were collected from the orbital sinus at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 h post-administration and mixed with 80 µl RO water. The fluorescence intensity of CY5-INS in the blood was measured using a microplate reader. After 8 h, organs were quickly excised from euthanized animals for ex vivo imaging. The relative fluorescence intensities were compared to those of the free dye control, and the images were analyzed using Living Image software (Caliper Life Sciences).

ICR mice (male, 18–20 g) were used to investigate the pharmacokinetics of CM EVs delivered orally. The mice were fasted overnight with free access to water and randomly divided into six groups. Blood samples were collected from the orbital sinus before administration. The mice were then orally given CM EVs labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine, 4-chlorobenzene sulfonate salt (DiD) at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Blood samples (20 µl) were taken at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 h post-administration and mixed with 80 µl of RO water. The fluorescence intensity of DiD in the blood was measured using a microplate reader, and the area under the curve (AUC), MRT, Tmax and max plasma concentration (Cmax) were calculated using DAS software.

Blood glucose lowering effects in type Ⅰ diabetics

C57BL/6 J male mice (18–20 g) were made diabetic through an intraperitoneal injection of STZ at a dose of 150 mg/kg. Mice were considered diabetic 1-week post-STZ treatment if fasting blood glucose exceeded 16.0 mM/l. Before the experiments, the mice were fasted overnight but allowed free access to water. Insulin solution (5 IU/kg, s.c.) served as the positive control. The oral administration groups included saline solution, insulin solution, I-CM EVs@INS, S-CM EVs@INS and H-CM EVs@INS (insulin = 50 IU/kg). Blood glucose levels were measured using a glucose meter (JPS-6, Yicheng, Beijing, China), and the pharmacological activity percentage (PA) was calculated based on previous reports. All animals received care in compliance with relevant laws and guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All procedures were approved by Sichuan University Animal Care and Use Committee (Animal Ethics Review Number: K2024008).

Statistical analysis

Statistical data was analyzed using Graphpad Prism 9.0 software and presented as mean ± SD. For two-group comparison, statistical significance was determined using Student’s two-sided t-test. For multiple comparison, the data were conducted the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality, and the results indicated that all groups met the assumption of normality. and then statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons without adjustments. Significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

CM EVs exhibited no differences compared to RM EVs

Considering commercial milk undergone pasteurization for human consumption, the high temperatures involved may impact the functional integrity of milk EVs. Therefore, we investigated whether pasteurization affects the properties of milk EVs. EVs were isolated from both raw milk (RM EVs) and different batches of commercial milk (CM EVs) by differential centrifugation (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, both RM EVs and various batches of CM EVs contained the EV marker protein TSG 101, while effectively removed most of the casein from whole milk (WM). Additionally, TEM and NTA revealed no significant differences in spherical morphology and size distribution between RM EVs and the different batches of CM EVs (Fig. 1C&1D, Table 1). And the stability of RM EVs and different batches of CM EVs were examined (Fig. 1E). The milk-derived EVs showed good particle size stability in PBS (pH 7.4) for 24 h, SGF (pH 1.2) for 8 h and SIF (pH 6.8) for 24 h, indicated excellent colloidal stability of milk-derived EVs in gastrointestinal tract. These results indicated that pasteurization minimally affects the structural stability of milk EVs, and there is good reproducibility among different batches of CM EVs. This property ensures consistent quality and performance of EVs across different batches, making them more reliable for therapeutic use. Next, we further examined the resistance of EVs membrane integrity to high temperatures and mechanical forces. The EVs were boiled or sonicated, then ultracentrifuged and probed for cellular uptake study and EV-enriched protein TSG101 by WB (Fig. 1F). The uptake studies in Caco-2 cells showed no significant difference in cellular uptake between RM EVs and CM EVs, and boiling treatment did not affect the uptake efficiency of either type. However, it is important to note that the cellular uptake efficiency of both EV types significantly decreased following sonication treatment (Fig. 1G and S1). WB analysis of TSG101 revealed that both RM EVs and CM EVs remained stable under boiled conditions. In contrast, stability was compromised in both RM EVs and CM EVs when subjected to sonication (Fig. 1H). These results indicated that sonication for drug loading disrupted the membrane integrity of EVs, while pasteurization did not. Therefore, we would further investigate the effects of sonication on the membrane integrity of CM EVs.

Sonication enhanced drug encapsulation but impaired membrane integrity

Therefore, the CM EVs extracted here is in line with the EVs standard. WB analysis confirmed the presence of EVs marker proteins (TSG101, CD63 and ALIX), but not in the whey (Fig. 2B). Then, we investigated the impact of incubation and sonication method on the drug encapsulation efficiency of CM EVs. Insulin was chosen as model drugs. S-CM EVs achieved a drug encapsulation efficiency of 56.14% ± 0.38%, significantly higher than those of I-CM EVs (11.92% ± 1.23%, P < 0.001, Fig. 2C). As Elena V. Batrakova et al. reported, this was likely due to the mechanical shear forces of sonication, disrupting the integrity of the vesicle membranes, allowing drugs to enter. Furthermore, they discovered that with sonication loading method, drugs were not only encapsulated inside the vesicles, but also potentially attached to the outer membrane, leading to a biphasic drug release profile [50]. The initial rapid release was from the attached drugs, followed by the slower release of the encapsulated drugs, consistent with our findings (Fig. S2A).

Therefore, we further examined the impact of sonication method versus incubation on the membrane integrity of CM EVs. At first, the size distribution, zeta potential (Fig. S2B) and stability (Fig. S2C) of CM EVs were similar between the two methods. It should be noted that the morphology of S-CM EVs became more fragmented as the red arrows indicated compared with I-CM EVs under TEM (Fig. 2D-2E). The membrane fluidity of S-CM EVs was significantly lower than I-CM EVs (P < 0.01), while there was no significant difference between I-CM EVs and the naive EVs (Fig. 2F). WB analysis also revealed a significant downregulation in marker proteins (TSG101, CD63 and ALIX) in S-CM EVs (Fig. 2G and S2D).

Moreover, the protect ability of S-CM EVs for loaded insulin from pancreatic protease degradation was decreased (Fig. 2H). Compared with S-CM EVs, I-CM EVs exhibited stronger resistance to acid and enzyme degradation. Studies reported this was likely due to the sonication method altered the lipid composition of the CM EVs [[51], [52], [53]]. Next, we investigated the differences in cellular uptake and transepithelial efficiency of S-CM EVs and I-CM EVs on Caco-2 cells. Both I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs had no significant cellular cytotoxicity (Fig. S2E), but the cellular uptake of S-CM EVs was decreased compared with I-CM EVs (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2I). Monolayer Caco-2 cell transmembrane experiments (Fig. 2J and S2F) also showed that the transepithelial efficiency of S-CM EVs was notably lower than I-CM EVs. Similar result was also observed in mucus penetration study (Fig. 2K).

To further investigate the key factors required for the enhanced cellular uptake of I-CM EVs compared to S-CM EVs, we examined the internalization of CM EVs in the presence of different types of endocytosis inhibitor. Consistent with previous findings that the inhibitors of clathrin-mediated and energy dependent endocytosis, such as chlorpromazine hydrochloride and NaN3, have general inhibitory effects on CM EVs cellular uptake, they suppressed the uptake of both I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs. The micropinocytosis inhibitor (5-(N-ethyl-N-isopropyl) amiloride) and the lipid-raft-mediated uptake inhibitor (lovastatin) had no obvious inhibitory effects on S-CM EVs (Fig. 2L). For the thorough comprehension of the intracellular pathway, we further investigated the transport of CM-EVS in Caco-2 cells by using different transport inhibitors. The exocytosis of S-CM EVs were barely affected by Brefeldin A and monensin, the two inhibitors of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi transport, while the distinct suppression was observed in the exocytosis of I-CM EVs. Chloroquine and LY294002, the two inhibitors of endosomal acidification, were further applied to quantitatively assess the amount of CM EVs in endolysosomal pathway. Notably, the exocytosis of S-CM EVs was significantly more inhibited by chloroquine and LY294002 compared to I-CM EVs, distinguished that I-CM EVs bypassed the endolysosomal pathway, whereas most S-CM EVs were involved in classical endolysosomal trafficking (Fig. 2M).

In this part of our study, we observed significant trade-offs between enhanced drug loading and compromised integrity in S-CM EVs. These findings underscore the importance of balancing drug encapsulation efficiency with vesicle integrity, which is crucial for maintaining the therapeutic efficacy of drug delivery systems. So, we hypothesized that sonication method reduced drug delivery efficiency by affecting the membrane integrity and protein composition of milk-derived EVs.

Differentially proteins contents existed between I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, changes of protein content and composition of CM EVs were examined by proteomic analysis (Fig. 3A and S3). Protein identification was performed using LC-MS/MS. Both sonication and incubation method resulted in the detection of an equal number of protein species in CM EVs, with a total of 3264 proteins identified (Fig. 3B and S4A&B). As for principal components analysis (PCA) (Fig. 3C), S-CM EVs group exhibited distinct protein composition when compared with I-CM EVs. Sonication method reduced the level of marker proteins such as CD9, CD63, CD81 and TSG101 (Fig. 3D). The downregulation of these proteins in S-CM EVs may lead to impaired membrane integrity, which can compromise their ability to mediate intercellular communication and effectively deliver therapeutic agents [54]. Then we identified 1380 differentially expressed proteins between I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs (Fig. 3E), indicating that sonication does not cause the complete loss of EV proteins, but rather reduces the levels of certain proteins. The same protein types in both groups of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs may be due to their origin from the same type of cells, which leads to the expression of the same core proteins. However, although the protein types were consistent, different drug loading methods may result in changes in the abundance of these proteins within the EVs. This indicated that the different loading methods primarily affected the protein content rather than the types of proteins, reflecting the functional differences of EVs under different drug loading methods. By comparison with S-CM EVs, there were 742 proteins up-regulated, 638 proteins down-regulated for I-CM EVs, respectively (Fig. 3F). The gene ontology (GO) enrichment of differentially expressed proteins revealed pathways associated with the ER were decreased in S-CM EVs compared with I-CM EVs. Additionally, GO terms related to protein binding in the molecular function (MF) category were also affected in S-CM EVs group (Fig. 3G). Importantly, sonication resulted in a significant downregulation of GO terms related to trans-epithelial transport, including endocytosis, vesicle-mediated transport, ER-Golgi transport, recycling endosome membrane, and phagocytic vesicle. Heatmap analysis also revealed a lower abundance of proteins associated with endocytosis and exocytosis in S-CM EVs compared with I-CM EVs (Fig. 4A and S5A). Therefore, these downregulated proteins in S-CM EVs may contribute to the decrease in basolateral exocytosis of CM EVs, consistent with the observed reduction in transepithelial transport in Caco-2 cells (Fig. 2J). To investigate the underlying mechanism, the variation of protein content related to transporters in the GO analysis revealed that S-CM EVs exhibited a significant decrease in the proteins involved in amino acid transporters, peptide transporters, and glucose transporters. These findings were consistent with the results of specific uptake inhibition experiments. PEPT-1 antibody significantly decreased the uptake of I-CM EVs. Furthermore, glutamate competitively inhibited the cellular uptake of I-CM EVs, which suggested the mediation of acid amino transporters. Glucose also decreased the uptake of I-CM EVs, indicating the mediation of glucose transporters. On the contrary, these inhibitors had no inhibitory effect on the cellular uptake of S-CM EVs (P < 0.05, Fig. 4F). This may also be related to the adhesion ability of EVs to cells (Fig. S5B). Moreover, when examining proteins related to immunoglobulin transcytosis, which can be mediated by neonatal receptors (FcRn), we observed a significant reduction (P < 0.05) in the transcytosis of immunoglobulins mediated by FcRn receptors in S-CM EVs (Fig. 4G).

Generally, the disadvantages of S-CM EVs in basolateral exocytosis, transcytosis and transepithelial transport were all positively correlated with the downregulation of corresponding functional proteins compared with I-CM EVs. These findings suggested that the changes to the CM EVs surface caused by sonication treatment were a major reason why this loading method could influence the intracellular trafficking routes of the CM EVs.

PEG facilitated effective fusion of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs

To address the contradiction of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs in drug loading and membrane integrity, we designed a H-CM EVs by fusion I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs using PEG8000 as an inducer. I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs were mixed in a 1:1 ratio, and then PEG8000 was added. The mixture was then shaken and incubated for 2 h. Studies had indicated that the close contact and dehydration effects of the lipid bilayer structure mediated by chemical agents could induce membrane fusion [[55], [56], [57]]. PEG 8000 had been shown to mediate the fusion of EVs and liposomes [46,58]. This fusion strategy can improve the drug encapsulation efficiency of EVs, which is primarily derived from the liposomes. However, the drug encapsulation efficiency of liposomes for biomacromolecular drugs is not very high. Furthermore, the fusion of milk-derived EVs with liposomes cannot fully leverage the potential of milk-derived EVs to enhance oral drug delivery. Here, we utilized PEG to mediate the fusion of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs, to simultaneously ensure the advantages of both two drug loading methods for efficient oral drug delivery.

FRET-based lipid mixing assay was used to monitor the fusion process. In brief, the FRET donor S-CM EVs containing the fluorescent dyes DiO and DiI diluted the phospholipid bilayer of the non-fluorescent acceptor I-CM EVs during the fusion process, increasing the distance between the DiO and DiI dyes and thereby decreasing the FRET efficiency. As a result, the increase in DiO fluorescence intensity over time was measured to indicate the fusion process (Fig. 5A). Firstly, we examined the effect of PEG concentration on the fusion process. I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs were fused at a 1:1 ratio (particle number detected by NTA) under different PEG8000 concentrations (0, 5%, 10%, 20%). The degree of fusion showed a positive correlation with PEG concentration: higher PEG led to greater increases in DiO fluorescence. After 2 h, 20% (v/w) PEG 8000 achieved a 31.30% ± 1.91% increase in DiO fluorescence (Fig. 5B&5C). Higher PEG resulted in greater reductions in FRET peaks, indicating 20% PEG had the highest fusion efficiency (Fig. 5D). To quantify fusion efficiency, the DiO fluorescence percentage was converted to fusion rounds (Fig. S6A). For example, 20% DiO fluorescence corresponded to 0.85 fusion rounds per I-CM EVs, which means if each I-CM EVs fused with one S-CM EVs, about 46% (calculated as: 0.85/(0.85+1) × 100%) of the I-CM EVs would have fused with S-CM EVs. Next, we used the lipid mixing assay based on abovementioned FRET to examine the fusion process. The degree of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs fusion was 21.92% DiO fluorescence/0.94 rounds of fusion. Notably, it was important to note that the fused EVs were composed of an average of 48.45% S-CM EVs (each I-CM EVs fused with 0.94 S-CM EVs), assuming similar diameters of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs (Fig. 5E-5G). We also used a Co2+-Calcein-EDTA experiment to verify the fusion process (Fig. 5H-5I and S6B&C). In brief, I-CM EVs contained EDTA and S-CM EVs loaded with the Co2+-Calcein complex. The fluorescence intensity versus time curve showed that the Calcein signal increased by about two-fold with 20% PEG-mediated fusion compared with that without PEG, confirming the mixing of the payloads from I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs, as EDTA preferentially binds to Co2+ and causes the release of Calcein fluorescence. Furthermore, the membrane fusion process also resulted in an increase in vesicle size compared with I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs (Fig. 5J). No significant difference was observed in particle size, PDI and zeta potential of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs (Table S2). In addition, the protein concentration of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs after 2 weeks and 4 weeks in different storage conditions (4 °C, -20 °C and -80 °C) also remained unchanged (Fig. S6D, P > 0.05). And TEM images revealed morphological changes during the membrane fusion of I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs (Fig. 5K).

Overall, the series of experiments showed that PEG could be an effective trigger factor for EVs fusion. The effective fusion of high drug-encapsulation efficiency of S-CM EVs and membrane integrity of I-CM EVs would allow the complementary advantages between different drug-loading methods of CM EVs.

H-CM EVs concurrently maintained drug loading and membrane integrity

Then, we investigated the insulin encapsulation efficiency of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs. The protein level, particle size, PDI and zeta potential of I-CM EVs@INS, S-CM EVs@INS and H-CM EVs@INS were unchanged. In addition, the insulin encapsulation efficiency also remained stable (Fig. S6E, Table S4). The insulin encapsulation efficiency of I-CM EVs was the lowest, only 11.92% ± 1.23%, while S-CM EVs exhibited higher encapsulation efficiency, reaching 45.62% ± 1.50%. Compared with S-CM EVs, H-CM EVs further improved the drug encapsulation efficiency to 56.14% ± 0.38% (Fig. 6A). And the particle sizes of the these EVs were stable with incubation in SGF (pH 1.2) for 8 h, and SIF (pH 6.8) for 24 h after insulin loaded (Fig. S6F). In addition, the particle sizes of the three EVs were remained unchanged with incubation in SGF (pH 1.2) for 8 h, and SIF (pH 6.8) for 24 h after insulin loaded (Fig. S6F). Next, the drug release was examined. And the results showed that the release behavior of H-CM EVs was similar to that of I-CM EVs, and significantly slower than that of S-CM EVs (Fig. 6B). This could be attributed to the fact that during the fusion process, some free drugs were further encapsulated into the hybrid EVs, which means that the process of fusion was also a process of drug repackaging. Additionally, the increase in the size of the EVs vesicles after fusion (with 120 nm to 150 nm) may also contribute to the higher drug encapsulation efficiency of H-CM EVs. For example, Lin et al. demonstrated that by fusing HEK293FT cell-derived exosomes with liposomes (Exo-Lip), the increased particle size enabled the loading of larger bioactive molecules [59]. To investigate whether the fusion progress would lead to leakage of the preloaded drugs, we loaded FITC-INS using both incubation and sonication methods. After loading, the mixture was divided into two parts, one part was ultra filtrated to remove free FITC-INS before fusion, while another part was proceeded with fusion. After fusion, all samples were subjected to ultrafiltration to remove any remaining free FITC-INS (Fig. 6C). The results showed that the drug loading was higher in the samples without ultrafiltration before fusion, indicating that the fusion process could not lead to leakage of the preloaded drugs but help the free drug further loaded into CM EVs.

Due to the significant impact of sonication on the membrane integrity of CM EVs, we further investigated the membrane integrity of H-CM EVs. Firstly, the results showed the membrane fluidity of H-CM EVs was restored to a level as I-CM EVs (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, we explored the resistance of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs to acidic gastric conditions. After being exposed to SGF (pH 1.2) for 2 h, the protein recovery rate of S-CM EVs was significantly lower than that of I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs (Fig. 6E, P < 0.01). WB analysis also demonstrated the similar expression of marker proteins in H-CM EVs compared with I-CM EVs (Fig. 6F). In addition, the tolerance to digestion by pancreatic trypsin was similar to I-CM EVs, substantially better than S-CM EVs (Fig. 6G). Subsequently, the cellular uptake efficiency of H-CM EVs on Caco-2 cells was comparable to I-CM EVs, which was better than S-CM EVs (Fig. 6H). Similar results were also observed in mucus penetrating experiment (Fig. S6G). Interestingly, there was no significant difference between the groups with and without the pre-removal of mucus for I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs, while S-CM EVs exhibited less cellular uptake in the presence of mucus (Fig. S6H). This result suggested that mucus layer acted as an obstacle for S-CM EVs, but not for I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs, which was in alliance with mucus penetrating results. In addition, we found that PEG, as a fusion promoter, did not affect the cellular uptake of S-CM EVs and I-CM EVs, and the superior performance of H-CM EVs observed in our study was attributed to the PEG-mediated fusion between S-CM EVs and I-CM EVs (Fig. S6I-S6J). Moreover, the cellular uptake of H-CM EVs was inhibited by PEPT-1antibody, glutamate, and glucose (Fig. 6I). These results also indicated that H-CM EVs were still taken up by multiple pathways, and the fabrication process did not cause any deformation of the proteins on the EVs. Meanwhile, H-CM EVs showed a higher co-localization with the ER and Golgi apparatus, and lower co-localization with lysosomes (Fig. 6J). This suggested that H-CM EVs, similar with I-CM EVs, preferentially utilized the ER-Golgi pathway for transportation and could avoid degradation in intracellular lysosomes.

Overall, these results demonstrated that H-CM EVs improved the drug encapsulation efficiency of the incubation method while also addressed the issue of reduced cellular uptake and transmembrane transport ability caused by sonication method. Therefore, it combined the advantages of both drug loading methods and overcame the contradiction between drug loading efficiency and membrane integrity in CM EVs using the incubation and sonication methods.

After endocytosis, the basolateral exocytosis of EVs determined the efficiency of transepithelial transport. Here, the bidirectional exocytosis of the milk EVs was evaluated on Caco-2 cell monolayers using Transwell permeable supports. The results demonstrated that both I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs accelerated basolateral exocytosis and meanwhile reduced the apical exocytosis. The exocytosis rate of S-CM EVs from the basolateral side was only 118.04% of that from the apical side. Notably, I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs increased the ratio to 200.80% and 204.60% (P < 0.05). Conversely, the basolateral exocytosis of S-CM EVs was only 118.04% (Fig. 7C-7E). The potential mechanism may be that the sonication method disrupted the proteins associated with basolateral exocytosis in CM EVs, as indicated by the proteomic analysis (Fig. 4B). Three-dimensional scanning images of the Caco-2 cell monolayer (Fig. 7F) also showed that S-CM EVs were more distributed in the apical region, while I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs tended to be transported towards the basolateral side during exocytosis.

Next, the influence of CM EVs on their intestinal absorption was examined by orally administering fluorescently labeled CM EVs (DiI) to healthy mice. Intestinal absorption studies (Fig. 7G) revealed that the duodenum and jejunum were the main sites of CM EVs absorption. Importantly, we found that both I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs exhibited notably enhanced absorption by the intestinal villi compared with S-CM EVs. To further investigate the integrity of EVs after oral administration into the bloodstream, we co-labeled the EVs with FRET dye pairs (CY3 and CY5) (Fig. S7). 1-h post oral administration, we collected blood samples from mice, centrifuged them to isolate the plasma, and measured the FRET efficiency of EVs in the plasma. Notably, compared to blank plasma, significant FRET signals were still evident in the plasma of both I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs groups, and these signals were markedly stronger than S-CM EVs group (Fig. 7H&7I). These findings indicated that EVs can effectively traverse the intestinal barrier and enter the bloodstream in an intact form.

Pharmacokinetic assessment

To investigate the oral delivery capabilities of I-CM EVs, S-CM EVs and H-CM EVs, Cy5 labeled insulin loaded in CM EVs were administered to ICR mice via gavage (Fig. 8A). The blood samples were collected from the tail and pharmacokinetic parameters of Cy5-insulin were calculated. Compared to S-CM EVs, the AUC values of I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs were approximately 1.6-fold and 2.4-fold higher (Fig. 8B, Table 1). Notably, at 8 h, the mouse intestine, and organs were extracted for living imaging (Fig. 8C). Results showed that the H-CM EVs had negligible distribution in the major organs (gastrointestinal tract, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) compared to S-CM EVs (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, ICR mice were orally administered with CM EVs labeled with DiD for pharmacokinetic evaluation. The AUC for I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs were 32.595 ± 5.346 and 33.571 ± 3.475 mg/l·h, which were approximately 1.79-fold and 1.85-fold higher than that of S-CM EVs (18.163 ± 8.747 mg/l·h). Cmax for I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs were ∼2.50-fold higher than S-CM EVs (Fig. 8E, Table 2). Additionally, we also investigated the gastrointestinal distribution of the EVs. The results showed that S-CM EVs exhibited a relatively shorter gastrointestinal retention time compared with I-CM EVs and H-CM EVs (Fig. S8). This suggested that the reduced gastrointestinal residence time of S-CM EVs may be one of the contributing factors to its lower oral delivery efficiency. It was reported that hybrid exosomes or liposomes showed different drug delivery characteristics. For example, Lin et al. highlighted that engineered fusion of EVs enhanced drug encapsulation capacity and targeting specificity [59]. In this study, we found that H-CM EVs further improved the drug encapsulation efficiency while retaining the efficient delivery capability of I-CM EVs, which may be due to the fact that the increase in the size of the EVs vesicles after fusion (with 120 nm to 150 nm). Furthermore, at an equivalent administered dose of EVs, H-CM EVs exhibited significantly enhanced hypoglycemic efficacy compared to I-CM EVs, which was attributed to their higher drug encapsulation efficiency (Fig. 6A and S9A-S9B).

Future research could explore the efficacy of H-CM EVs in delivering a broader range of macromolecular drugs, such as peptides, proteins, and nucleic acids. Investigating the interactions between H-CM EVs and different types of drugs will help determine their versatility as drug delivery vehicles. Additionally, studies could focus on optimizing the loading and release mechanisms for these macromolecules, thereby enhancing the therapeutic potential of H-CM EVs in various clinical applications.

Conclusion

In summary, this work identified two essential EVs attributes drug-loading efficiency and the integrity of the membrane, which determined the ultimate therapeutic efficacy of orally administered biopharmaceuticals encapsulated in EVs. Firstly, we observed no differences in membrane integrity between RM EVs and CM EVs, suggesting that pasteurization had little effect on milk-derived EVs. And we generated a H-CM EVs by co-incubating I-CM EVs and S-CM EVs with PEG. The fusion process allowed for higher drug encapsulation than the individual loading methods. H-CM EVs also retained the intact membrane integrity by restoring membrane fluidity, repairing damaged proteins, and enhancing enzyme resistance compared with S-CM EVs. The H-CM EVs delivered insulin effectively across the intestinal-blood barrier, maintaining adequate blood insulin concentration and improving patient compliance and efficacy.

While H-CM EVs demonstrated promising applications in oral drug delivery, several potential scalability challenges should be considered. One significant challenge is the consistency of EV production across larger scales. As the production volume increases, variations in the isolation and purification processes can lead to batch-to-batch variability in EV properties, such as size, concentration and cargo composition. Additionally, ensure robust quality control and standardized protocols is essential for maintaining therapeutic efficacy and reliability in clinical applications. Addressing these scalability and variability issues is crucial for the successful translation of H-CM EVs into clinical applications.
