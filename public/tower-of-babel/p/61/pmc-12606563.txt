Fabrication of PCL Blended Highly Aligned Nanofiber Yarn from Dual-Nozzle Electrospinning System and Evaluation of the Influence on Introducing Collagen and Tropoelastin
Authors: Daxian Zha, Nasif Mahmood, Robert S. Kellar, Jessica M. Gluck, Martin W. King
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conventional electrospinning is a widely used method for creating nanofibrous structures that show promise in skin wound healing. However, the membranes generated through conventional electrospinning techniques lack reproducibility, scalability, and sufficient mechanical strength, ultimately limiting their potential for clinical applications. In this study, we present a new approach for fabricating biomimetic yarn using a dual-nozzle electrospinning system that collects nanofiber yarn from a rotating funnel. Polycaprolactone (PCL) was combined with collagen-I and tropoelastin in the electrospinning solution to provide relevant biochemical cues necessary for human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cells while maintaining structural integrity. The novel yarns exhibited a hierarchical structure akin to collagen fibers forming the dermal layer of the skin. Furthermore, these yarns demonstrated superior mechanical strength compared to conventional electrospun nanofiber webs. Live/dead fluorescence staining and alamarBlue assays revealed that yarns incorporating both collagen-I and tropoelastin supported significantly higher HDF attachment (18.55% at 24 h) and sustained proliferation, increasing 2.93-fold from Day 1 to Day 7, compared to the PCL-only control yarns (9.34% at 24 h; 2.68-fold increase). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations confirmed that cells on protein-containing yarns formed a continuous, smooth cell layer with elongated, spindle-shaped morphology which was aligned along the yarn axis. This highlights the critical role of yarn architecture in guiding cell orientation and organization. The combined biochemical cues from collagen-I and tropoelastin, together with the aligned, twisted yarn architecture, produced scaffolds that not only enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation but also promoted native-like cell alignment and confluence. This synergistic integration of biochemical signaling and structural guidance underscores the potential of these electrospun yarns as advanced dermal scaffolds, offering both mechanical robustness and functional bioactivity for future wound healing and regenerative medicine applications.

Introduction

As the largest organ in the human body, the skin’s primary function is to serve as a protective barrier against the invasion of harmful material and pathogens from the external environment. Additionally, it participates in metabolic processes, plays resorptive and thermoregulatory roles, defends against microorganisms, contributes to immunological responses, and prevents water loss from the organism. Burn injuries, characterized as dermal and epidermal tissue damage that results from heat, overexposure to the sun or other radiation, or chemical or electrical contact, are considered a global public health problem. According to a report from the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 11 million burn injuries of all types occur annually worldwide, approximately 180,000 of which are fatal. These dynamic injuries affect the local and systemic tissues, which causes the body to undergo a range of responses, including overproduction of chemical mediators, immunosuppression, and increased susceptibility to infection. Current clinical procedures for treating burn injuries involve transplanting allograft or autograft split thickness skin tissue, which can lead to ethical issues, potential disease transmission and graft immune rejection. Therefore, a critical need exists for alternative therapies that can enhance wound healing while avoiding these complications.

Tissue-engineered scaffolds offer a promising alternative for the treatment of burn injuries. These scaffolds provide a supportive structure for the growth of new tissue and can be customized to mimic the natural environment of the injured tissue. − The skin structure consists of three main layers: epidermis, dermis, with an underlying hypodermis. The third and fourth degrees of burn injuries are associated with damage to the full thickness of the dermis layer. This layer of skin tissue comprises a dense network of collagen and elastin fibers, which provides strength, flexibility and elasticity to the skin.

Collagen is a fibrous protein that comprises amino acids, which are crucial for providing structural support to the extracellular matrix of connective tissue. Collagen plays a vital role in maintaining the shape of the skin and preventing it from excessive stretching or deformation. This protein is synthesized by various cells in the body, mainly fibroblasts, and currently 28 different isoforms of collagen have been identified. Among the collagen family, type I collagen is the most abundant in the dermal layer. Collagen confers tensile strength, elasticity, and resilience to these tissues, making it is an indispensable component in preserving the structural integrity of cells.

Elastin is another structural protein that plays a key role in providing the skin with elasticity. This elasticity enables the skin to return to its normal configuration after being stretched or deformed. Tropoelastin is the precursor of elastin, which is composed of three regions: a central hydrophobic region that provides elasticity, flanked by two hydrophilic regions that stabilize the molecule and facilitate its assembly into elastic fibers. The hydrophobic region contains numerous repeats of the amino acid sequence “VPGVG” (peptide sequence), which creates a highly flexible and spring-like structure that allows elastin fibers to stretch and recover without breaking. Tropoelastin molecules can be assembled into elastic fibers through a complex process that involves cross-linking with other elastin molecules and other proteins such as fibrillin. This process creates a network of elastic fibers that provide tissues with the ability to stretch and recover in response to mechanical stress. Several studies have shown that the scaffolds containing tropoelastin support the growth of new skin tissue and promoted wound healing. − Additionally, it has been reported that the existence of elastin and collagen-based scaffolds results in a decrease in stiffness, modulation of collagen degradation, enhanced angiogenesis, and greater elastin fiber formation. −

However, natural materials often lack the mechanical performance required for tissue engineering. ,− By combining natural materials with synthetic polymers, the resulting scaffold can have improved mechanical properties while maintaining biocompatibility and allowing for controlled release of bioactive molecules. − Polycaprolactone (PCL) is widely used in the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared and approved devices with several desirable properties, including biodegradability, biocompatibility, and the ability to provide mechanical support to developing tissue. , By combining natural and synthetic materials, the resulting scaffold can be engineered to have specific physical properties, tunable degradation rate, and controlled release of bioactive molecules. Therefore, an ideal dermal substitute would be expected to incorporate the mechanical and cell signaling properties of natural and synthetic materials to closely mimic the native tissue environment, which is critical for tissue regeneration and integration.

Electrospinning has been considered a straightforward and multifunctional technique that can manufacture in vitro fibrous scaffolds for tissue engineering applications. , Electrospun fibers have fiber diameters ranging from nanometers to micrometers. This scale of nanofibers has received much attention due to their architectural resemblance to the fibrillar structure of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM). Electrospun fibers in the submicron range provide several benefits, including a high surface area to volume ratio, adjustable porosity, and the capacity to control the composition of nanofibers to achieve specific properties and tunable mechanical performance. , In addition, the advantages of a high specific surface area, aspect ratio, and microporosity can resemble the native ECM environment and ameliorate cellular behavior. For example, the high surface area of electrospun fibers can enhance cell-material interactions, promoting cell adhesion and allowing for better nutrient and oxygen exchange. Moreover, the porosity of an electrospun web can be fine-tuned to regulate cell infiltration and tissue ingrowth while also providing a platform for drug delivery, as drugs can be loaded onto or into the fibers. , As a result, electrospun nanofiber webs show great potential for applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

However, the conventional electrospinning setup generates a randomly deposited nanofiber web over the grounded collector. While for tissue engineering applications, the optimal scaffold design may involve considering the extent of alignment and the degree of orientation, which more closely mimics the ECM. A variety of tissues, including the dermal, ligaments, tendons, and vascular tissues, is constituted by the oriented fiber. Therefore, influencing the cell behaviors at the correct orientation is required to develop a supportive structure. Generally, the aligned nanofibers were collected on the rotating drum, creating a nanofiber sheet on the surface of the drum collector. However, the lack of reproducibility in the structure limits the application in the repair of bulk tissue defects. , The nanofiber firm has a relatively low tensile strength to guide the growth of a desired specific tissue. Scaffolds prepared by highly aligned electrospun yarn with various applications including different soft tissue regeneration have been studied. It has been demonstrated that the cell’s behavior on these lined-up yarns could guide the spreading and orientation of cells along the axis of the yarn. , In addition, the electrospun yarn has the ability to incorporate diverse types of fibers to integrate the structural attributes and performance benefits of conventional microfibers and electrospun nanofibers. However, these yarn formation methods, including wet electrospinning in a water bath and core–shell structure, lack a twist angle on the final yarn, which prevents the increased mechanical performance, uniformity of nanofiber alignment, and post-processing into a three-dimensional (3D) structure. ,−

An alternative process known as conjugated electrospinning has been developed to produce continuous nanofiber yarns with a well-controlled twist level, which involves the aggregation and twisting of nanofiber bundles by rotating an un-grounded metal disc or funnel placed between two nozzles of opposite polarity. , The present research delves into the application of conjugated electrospinning as an optimized method to produce continuous nanofiber yarns using synthetic and biological materials. The study aims to investigate the principle and process of this spinning method in detail, with the potential to be utilized to achieve optimized architectures for skin tissue scaffolds. The research findings have significant implications for the development of advanced techniques in the field of skin tissue engineering.

Materials and Methods

Electrospun Yarn Fabrication

Polymer Solution Preparation

Three different polymer solutions were prepared by dissolving the polymer and proteins into 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-isopropanol (HFIP) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to obtain a 20% w/v concentration. Polycaprolactone (PCL) (Mw 150,000) (Scientific Polymer Products., Ontario, NY, USA) was blended with type 1 collagen (COL) from calf skin (MP Biomedicals. Irvine, CA, USA) at a ratio of 90:10 (PCL/COL) for the first experimental group and for the second experimental group, in addition to the blended PCL/COL components, additional recombinant human dermal tropoelastin (TE) was supplied by Dr. Robert S. Kellar’s Tissue Engineering and Regenerative Medicine (TERM) laboratory at Northern Arizona University, Flagstaff, AZ, USA and combined using a ratio of 85:10:5 (PCL/COL/TE). Pure PCL solution served as the control group. All three solutions were stirred continuously at room temperature overnight to ensure homogeneous mixing.

Electrospinning Setup

A conventional electrospinning setup was prepared by connecting a single pump system to the positive electrode of an electric power generator. The three polymer solutions were loaded into syringes individually, and a grounded copper film placed 10 cm away from the needle tip to serve as the collector (Figure A). A dual-nozzle electrospinning system (Holmarc Opto-Mechatronics Pvt Ltd., Kochi, India) was set up in a fume hood, comprising two needles, a dual output high-voltage power supply (one for each needle), a grounded collector, and a distance control mechanism, as shown in Figure B. The same polymer solutions were loaded into each syringe, and the system was primed to ensure a continuous flow rate ranging from 1 to 1.3 mL/h, depending on the blend of materials. The positive and negative high-voltage (9 kV) power supplies were applied to two syringes. The electric field generated by the electrostatic forces caused the polymer solutions to form Taylor cones prior to being ejected from each needle. The two streams of polymer solutions were combined and formed a web on the surface of the rotating funnel as they were electrospun from different sides. As the fibers were collected, a plastic rod was used to draw the web out into a one-dimensional structure and insert twist into the yarn, which was then collected on a rotating yarn wind-up system. The fabrication parameters were listed in Tables and . The electrospun yarns (EYs) were cross-linked with the protein components by exposing them to 25% glutaraldehyde in the vapor phase for 48 h. The samples were then placed in a fume hood to aerate for 24 h (Figure C).

Materials Characterization

Characterization by Scanning Electron Microscopy

In order to observe the electrospun yarn morphology for both conventional and dual nozzle electrospinning processes, and in order to quantify the fiber diameter, the fiber pore size, and the fiber alignment within the yarn and the electrospun mat, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken at different magnifications. First, the electrospun mat and yarn samples were mounted on SEM aluminum stubs using a carbon adhesive, followed by sputter coating (SC7620 mini Sputter Coater, Quorum Technologies Ltd., Laughton, U.K.) to create a 10 nm conductive layer of gold and palladium. The SEM images were obtained using a Phenom G1 desktop scanning electron microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Yarn diameters were measured by ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The Diameter J algorithm, a plugin of ImageJ software, was used to calculate the fiber diameters and pore size areas of the prototype samples. The Analyze Particles function in ImageJ software was utilized to analyze the black pixels in the segmented images. Representative examples of the transitions from SEM images to segmented images and then to mesh pore analysis images is illustrated in Figure A. The orientation of the fibers in the yarns and mat samples, as illustrated in Figure B, was determined by calculating the angular difference from the center line of the yarn using ImageJ software. The resulting data was then analyzed using R Studio software, which provided a precise and comprehensive analysis of the fibers’ orientation.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to characterize the chemical composition of the samples and to identify the functional groups on their surface. The electrospun samples with different compositions (PCL only, PCL/COL, and PCL/COL/TE) were analyzed using the Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer with a resolution of 4 cm–1. The samples were measured in absorbance mode in the 400–4000 cm–1 range. The spectra were collected and compared with known standards to identify the presence of amino acid groups, and to distinguish the protein materials from the synthetic polycaprolactone.

Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF SIMS) is a high-resolution analytical technique that is widely used to analyze elemental and molecular information at surfaces. This testing was performed on a TOF SIMS V machine (IONTOF, Inc., Chestnut Ridge, NY, USA). A pulsed Bi+ primary ion beam at 25 kV impact energy combined with 10 kV Cs+ and C60+ beam energy was utilized with less than 1 ns bunched pulse width. An electron gun was used for charge neutralization. The total accumulated primary ion dose for data acquisition was less than 1 × 1013 ions/cm2, which is equivalent to the number of ions within the static SIMS regime. The mass resolution on the Si wafer was about 8000 m/Δm at 29 AMU.

Mechanical Characterization

The mechanical properties, including ultimate tensile strength and elongation at break, were assessed on an MTS QTest/5 universal testing machine equipped with a 5 lb load cell. In order to carry out the mechanical tests, the electrospun mat samples were cut into specimens (n = 5) measuring 1 in. long and 0.5 in. wide when mounted across an index card window frame. The sides of the frame were trimmed prior to tensile testing. Similarly, yarn samples were also prepared with a 1 in. gauge length when mounted across the same window frames. The thickness of the specimens was measured using a vernier caliper. The tensile tests were performed with a 1 in. gauge length and a 1 in./min crosshead speed. Additional tests were carried out on both aligned and randomly oriented constructions, on samples with and without cross-linking, as well as under both wet and dry conditions. As for the wet condition, the samples were submerged in the water for 1 h. Young’s modulus was computed by evaluating the stress/strain curve in the elastic region of the samples.

Evaluation of Rate of Yarn Degradation over 7-Week In Vitro Study

The electrospun samples (PCL, PCL/COL, and PCL/COL/TE) were weighed to obtain an initial dry weight. Next, these samples were immersed in 20 mL of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (dPBS) solution (Gibco Life Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) in individual 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The tubes were capped and placed in a Boekel Grant shaker bath operating at a continuous speed of 20 rpm and a temperature of 37 ± 1 °C, for a designated time period. The dPBS solution was changed every week to maintain a stable pH of 7. The degraded specimens were collected at five designated time points, namely 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 weeks. Upon removal from the centrifuge tubes, the samples were thoroughly washed with deionized water and air-dried in a chemical hood with air flow overnight. The weight of the samples was recorded, and the rate of degradation was calculated in terms of the percent weight loss at each time point.

Cell Viability Using Human Dermal Fibroblasts

Cell Culture and Seeding

Neonatal human dermal fibroblasts (HDF), obtained from the University of North Carolina Tissue Culture Facility, were used to evaluate the biocompatibility of the samples. The HDF cells with a passage number of 11 were maintained in a culture medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Atlas Biologicals, Fort Collins, CO, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL, Gibco) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed every other day and when the culture reached around 80% confluency it was subcultured using Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The three electrospun yarn samples, namely: PCL alone, PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE, were prepared prior to seeding by cutting them into 10 cm lengths. These specimens were then placed in a 48 well-plate for further processing.

The sterilization process was carried out by immersing the samples in 70% ethanol for 2 h followed by 5 washes with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (dPBS). The specimens were conditioned by soaking them in cell culture media for 8 h prior to seeding them with 5 × 105 HDF cells for the cell viability and cell proliferation assays. In comparison, the morphological analysis was undertaken by seeding 1 × 105 HDF cells on the electrospun samples for comparison against the control sample cultured on tissue culture plates using a similar cell density.

Cell Viability and Proliferation

Both cell viability and proliferation assays were performed on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 following seeding of HDF cells. A LIVE/DEADTM Cell Imaging Kits (488/570) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to observe cell viability following the manufacturer’s recommendation to use a 1:1 dilution ratio with dPBS. After incubation with the prepared solution for 15 min, a fluorescent microscope (EVOS FL Auto 2) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to image the samples. The rate of cell proliferation was quantified using an alamarBlueTM cell viability reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The specimens were washed with dPBS and incubated for 3 h in the dark with the alamarBlueTM reagent diluted 1:10 v/v in cell culture medium. The supernatant liquids were collected in a 96-well plate and the resulting fluorescence values were measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) at 550 nm excitation and 590 nm emission wavelengths. The positive control samples contained cells cultured on tissue culture plates, and the negative control samples contained no cells. This is a necessary strategy to confirm that the positive control ensures the efficacy of the staining process, and the negative control verifies the absence of any background staining.

Analysis of Cell Morphology

The morphology of the HDF cells seeded on the electrospun yarn samples was observed after 7 days of cell culture. The samples were submerged in dPBS for 5 min and aspirated. Initially the specimens were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for a duration of 20 min followed by washing with dPBS three times, each time for 5 min. To prepare the specimens for SEM viewing they were first rinsed in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) at pH 7.2 for 10 min, followed by fixation in a 2% para-formaldehyde/2.5% glutaraldehyde solution in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 30 min. A second rinse was then performed using the same 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for more than 15 min. Serial dehydration was then performed by immersing the specimens in increasing concentrations of ethanol from 35% to 50, 70, 80, and 95% for 10 min each, followed by three 10 min treatments in 100% ethanol and one final 40 min immersion in 100% ethanol. After dehydration, the samples were dried, which involved immersion in a mixture of 50% ethanol and 50% hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) for 20 min, followed by exposure to 100% HMDS for another 20 min. Each of these steps did not exceed 20 min. The SEM specimens were then mounted on stubs with carbon tape and sputter coated. The viewing and capturing of SEM images followed the same procedure as described above.

Statistical Analysis

For each experimental procedure, at least five replicate specimens were tested, and each experiment was undertaken twice. The data in the Results Section are presented in terms of the means ± standard error of the mean unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analysis was carried out using R studio software to perform a one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey post hoc test, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, stars are employed as indicators of statistical significance for three of the most common p-values. A single star (*) signifies a p-value lower than 0.05, two stars (**) signify a p-value lower than 0.01, and three stars (***) signify a p-value lower than 0.001.

Results

Morphology of Electrospun Yarns and Electrospun Mats

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to observe the microstructure of the electrospun yarns (EYs) and the electrospun mats (EMs). SEM imagery revealed that both the aligned and randomly oriented filaments exhibited a three-dimensional (3D) fibrous structure (Figure C). The dual-nozzle electrospinning method successfully produced highly aligned, twisted PCL-based nanofiber yarns incorporating collagen and tropoelastin, achieving a well-organized architecture that closely resembled native ECM. A slight variation in twist angle relative to the yarn axis was observed among the three sample types, with the angle of 17° for PCL, 20° for PCL/COL, and 25° for PCL/COL/TE. After optimizing the electrospun processing parameters and lowering the ambient humidity as indicated in Table , no visible beaded electrospun fibers or filaments were observed on the surface of the electrospun samples. The absence of beads indicates stable jet formation and appropriate polymer solution properties, confirming that successful electrospinning was achieved under these conditions. Table presents the experimentally measured diameters of the three different yarns. The PCL yarn had a diameter of 0.415 ± 0.101 mm, while the PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE yarns had diameters of 0.755 ± 0.285 and 0.576 ± 0.034 mm, respectively. The mean values and the distribution data for the yarn diameters and twist angle were calculated using ImageJ (Figure A). Upon analyzing the various samples, it was observed that the PCL/COL composite sample had the thickest filament diameter in both the yarn (1053 ± 769 nm) and the random mat (981 ± 561 nm). However, the standard deviation was found to be larger, which corresponds to the wider spread of data in the histogram. The electrospun PCL/COL/TE yarn had a similar filament diameter to that of the random electrospun mat (725 ± 413 nm versus 797 ± 434 nm). While the 100% PCL electrospun yarn had a higher filament diameter compared to the 100% PCL electrospun mat (708 ± 504 nm versus 482 ± 235 nm).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

This study involved measuring the FTIR spectra of the three electrospun yarn samples, namely: PCL alone, PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE. The FTIR spectrum of the PCL yarn sample displayed absorption bands at 2939 cm–1 (C–H functional group in CH2) and at 1729 cm–1 (carbonyl stretching) (Figure ). In addition, the peaks of C–O–C stretching at 1239 cm–1 and C–O and C–C stretching at 1294 cm–1, correspond to the characteristic features of 100% PCL. It is pertinent to observe that by combining PCL with collagen, and with both collagen and tropoelastin, the same characteristic peaks of PCL were retained. In addition, the amide bonds associated with the proteins collagen and tropoelastin were also observed by the FTIR analysis. Absorption bands at 1551 and 1652 cm–1 corresponding to the bending vibration of the amide II NH bond and the tensile vibration of the amide I CO bond were also observed. In the protein samples, the peaks assigned to amide I and amide II exhibited different absorbance intensities. Upon closer analysis, it has been inferred that the PCL blended with both collagen and tropoelastin generated the highest absorbance at both wavelengths, indicating that this sample contained the most amide I and amide II chemical bonds of all the samples. In comparison, the 100% PCL control sample did not exhibit any absorption peaks in these two regions.

Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (ToF-SIMS)

Analysis of the negative ion spectra showed that the samples exhibited different chemical fragments (Figure ). Specifically, the fragments containing oxygen were most likely derived from PCL on the surface, whereas the CN- and CNO- fragments originated from the collagen and tropoelastin components. The intensity of the C2H2O2 peak is similar across all the samples, and as a result, it was used as the reference peak. The ion intensity of all the other chemical species was then normalized with respect to the C2H2O2 peak (Figure A). This allowed a semiquantitative analysis of the chemical fragments after normalizing against the reference peak (Figure B). This analysis indicates that the peak intensities for C3H3O2-, C6H9O2- and C9H7O- are similar for all three samples, implying that these constituents were derived from the same chemical compound. As mentioned previously, the concentration of the CN- and CNO- fragments is higher in the samples containing collagen and tropoelastin. So, the relatively small concentration of CN- and CNO- in the 100% PCL sample is most likely due to contamination.

The results of the mechanical tests performed on the aligned electrospun yarns and the randomly oriented electrospun mat for both cross-linked and non-cross-linked samples under a dry condition are presented in Figure B–D and in Table . Rate of In Vitro Yarn Degradation over 7-Weeks

The rate of degradation was assessed by measuring the rate of weight loss over a period of 7 weeks since these two variables are directly correlated (Figure ). Specifically, a faster rate of weight loss indicates a reduced resistance to degradation. There was a consistent increase in weight loss for all the samples over the 7-week period. All the samples began to degrade during the first week, but no significant difference was noted between the cross-linked and non-cross-linked samples. In the second week, a noticeable difference appeared between the cross-linked and non-cross-linked PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE samples. The weight loss experienced by both non-cross-linked samples was significantly higher in comparison to the cross-linked ones. In other words, the process of cross-linking effectively slowed down the rate of degradation of the samples in a hydrated environment. During the seventh week of the in vitro study, the non-cross-linked PCL/COL/TE sample exhibited the fastest rate of degradation followed by the cross-linked PCL/COL/TE sample. This result indicates that samples containing collagen and tropoelastin degraded at a faster rate. Similarly, those samples that were blended with collagen alone degraded at a faster rate than the 100% PCL control sample, which degraded at the slowest rate.

Cellular Response

Cell Viability

Biocompatibility was assessed using the LIVE/DEADTM cell imaging kit which cultured human dermal fibroblasts on the yarn samples for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days (Figure A). This particular LIVE/DEADTM viability staining kit discriminated between live from dead cells by simultaneously staining intracellular esterase activity with a green, fluorescent calcein-AM stain, and a red-fluorescent ethidium homodimer-1 to indicate a loss of plasma membrane integrity. Over the course of 7 days, an increase in the number of live cells was observed (Figure A). Notably, the PCL/COL/TE combined sample demonstrated more densely packed cells compared to the other samples. The samples containing collagen and tropoelastin were observed to exhibit cells with a more spindle-shaped morphology. Furthermore, compared with the 100% PCL control yarn, the cells attached to the PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE samples were associated with an orientation along the direction of the yarn. This finding has important implications on how material structure and morphology can influence cell viability.

Cell Proliferation

Over the 7-day culture period, human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) exhibited material-dependent proliferation profiles on the electrospun yarn scaffolds (Figure B). Quantitative analysis of alamarBlue reduction revealed that all groups showed increased metabolic activity from Day 1 to Day 7, except the 100% PCL control yarn, which plateaued and displayed a decline beginning at Day 5. This suggests that there was limited long-term support for cell growth on hydrophobic PCL surfaces. The 24 h cellular attachment rates (Figure C) further underscored the influence of material composition: PCL exhibited the lowest initial cell attachment (9.34 ± 1.03%), while PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE yarns achieved significantly higher values of 21.01 ± 1.70 and 18.55 ± 1.23%, respectively. Proliferation calculations indicated that PCL/COL/TE increased 2.93-fold from Day 1 to Day 7 (final value: 54.36 ± 3.05%), followed by PCL at 2.68-fold (25.04 ± 2.58%) and PCL/COL at 1.98-fold (41.61 ± 2.47%). Notably, although PCL/COL showed the highest 24 h attachment, PCL/COL/TE sustained a higher proliferation rate after Day 3, reaching the greatest overall cell density by Day 7.

Cell Morphology

The SEM study investigated the cell morphology of the HDF after being cultured for 7 days on the 100% PCL, PCL/COL and PCL/COL/TE electrospun yarns. The SEM images confirmed the successful proliferation and adherence of the HDF cells to the substrates (Figure D). We observed cells displaying a characteristic spindle-shaped appearance, growing in the direction of the filament axis, encircling the cylindrical shape of the electrospun yarn, and forming a three-dimensional and multicellular network across the nanofibrous scaffold structure. Furthermore, a continuous HDF monolayer was observed to have almost entirely covered the three electrospun yarn samples. With the addition of collagen and tropoelastin, the PCL/COL/TE matrix caused the cells to spread uniformly, presenting a normal morphology and superior confluence on the scaffold’s surface. In addition, the cells demonstrated a tendency to elongate and connect with each other, resulting in longitudinal cell sheets aligned in the direction of the yarn axis and leading to a tissue-like architecture. At higher magnification, the spreading and stretching of individual cells was observed on the electrospun filaments, demonstrating relatively high actin stretching associated with a redistribution of α- and β-actin in the HDF.

Discussion

This study examined the fabrication and biological performance of electrospun yarns incorporating PCL, collagen, and tropoelastin, using an innovative dual-needle electrospinning technique. The results revealed marked improvements in filament alignment, mechanical properties and biocompatibility, demonstrating the potential for using these electrospun yarns for skin tissue engineering and other applications in regenerative medicine.

The dual-needle electrospinning system employed in this study represents a significant advancement over traditional single-needle setups. Conventional electrospinning methods often yield randomly oriented nanofiber mats, which lack the alignment necessary to replicate the hierarchical structure of native tissues. In contrast, the dual-needle system allowed for the fabrication of aligned, twisted nanofiber yarns. The use of a rotating funnel as a collector was critical in achieving a hierarchical architecture that closely resembled collagen bundles within the dermis. , Similar systems have been reported by Liu et al. and Pan et al., and have demonstrated the potential of a dual-needle system to produce uniform, functional, biocompatible yarns with superior mechanical properties. The integration of a rotating collector ensures consistent alignment and uniform tension during fiber deposition, reducing common electrospinning defects such as beading. These improvements are crucial for clinical applications, where scaffold reproducibility and mechanical reliability are paramount. Moreover, controlling fiber alignment and twist angle have been shown to significantly enhance the tensile properties of scaffolds, supporting applications in load-bearing tissues. ,

The aligned yarns were observed to exhibit higher ultimate tensile strength and Young’s modulus compared to their nontwisted counterparts when observed under both dry and hydrated conditions (Figure ). This agrees with the findings of Nitti et al. and Soliman et al., who highlighted the superior mechanical performance of aligned fibers and filaments due to their directional organization. In the dual-nozzle electrospinning system used in this study, twist angle was governed by several parameters listed in Table , and was maintained within a narrow range (17, 20, and 25°) across all yarn compositions. While these differences in twist angle could affect tensile strength, packing density, and potentially cell alignment, the relatively narrow range observed in this study likely minimized their influence on the experimental results. The twist angles were achieved under constant fabrication parameters. However, minor variations in angle observed between compositions suggest that material properties, such as viscosity, surface tension, and electrospinning jet stability, may subtly influence the fiber deposition and twisting behavior. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate these effects.

To further enhance their dimensional stability and durability, cross-linking with glutaraldehyde vapor was employed. This process not only slowed degradation rates but also preserved the structural integrity under physiological conditions, consistent with other studies that have emphasized the role of cross-linking to improve scaffold durability. ,

A key innovation of this study was the inclusion of tropoelastin in the yarn composition, providing both biochemical and mechanical benefits. Tropoelastin, the soluble precursor of elastin, plays a crucial role in imparting elasticity and structural integrity to native tissues. , Its incorporation enhanced scaffold elasticity, promoted angiogenesis, and supported wound healing; making it especially valuable for dermal regeneration. , Tropoelastin also contributed to controlled collagen degradation and the promotion of elastin fiber formation, both of which are critical in skin repair. The presence of tropoelastin significantly improved HDF proliferation and alignment, with cells orienting along the yarn axis in a manner that closely mimics native dermal cellular architecture. , Its biochemical cues facilitated better cell organization and integration. This level of cellular organization is critical for tissue integration and functional regeneration, as it replicates the alignment and orientation of the native extracellular matrix.

Moreover, these results are in agreement with the work of Sierra-Sanchez et al., who demonstrated the importance of generating a confluent cell monolayer on the yarns in order for the scaffold architecture to promote skin regeneration and wound closure. The addition of tropoelastin improved the mechanical performance of the scaffold, including an increase in Young’s modulus. This enhancement is vital for scaffolds intended to function in dynamic tissue environments, where mechanical resilience is required. The observed mechanical enhancements are likely due to molecular interactions between the natural proteins, that is collagen and tropoelastin, and the synthetic polymer matrix. Functional groups such as amines and carboxyl groups facilitate hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with polymer chains, improving interfacial adhesion and scaffold stiffness. Previous studies on collagen/elastin hybrids and tropoelastin/PCL blends consistently report increased Young’s modulus, confirming the synergistic role of protein integration in mechanical reinforcement. ,

One of the challenges of incorporating proteins like tropoelastin and collagen into scaffolds is their susceptibility to degradation. Proteins are often rapidly degraded by hydrolytic and enzymatic mechanisms, which can compromise the dimensional stability and durability of the scaffold.. To address this, the study employed 25% glutaraldehyde vapor cross-linking. The cross-linked filaments provided an improved mechanical performance, particularly under hydrated conditions, and exhibited a slower rate of degradation. These enhancements are consistent with previous research, which highlighted the role of cross-linking in prolonging scaffold functionality and maintaining structural integrity during tissue healing. , The nontoxic nature of this cross-linking technique was confirmed through cell culture experiments, when human dermal fibroblasts displayed healthy proliferation and attachment to the scaffold. These results suggest that glutaraldehyde vapor cross-linking is a viable strategy for stabilizing protein-containing scaffolds without compromising their biocompatibility.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain. The hydrophobic nature of PCL, for instance, can limit initial cell adhesion. Future studies could explore surface modifications, such as radio frequency surface plasma treatment or blending PCL with hydrophilic polymers to enhance scaffold wettability and cell attachment. Additionally, while the degradation profile of the cross-linked yarns was favorable in this study, further tailoring of the degradation rate to match the healing timeline of other tissues, such as neural, bone and dental tissues, remains an area for optimization.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the successful fabrication of electrospun yarns using a dual-needle system, incorporating polycaprolactone, collagen, and tropoelastin to create scaffolds with superior mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and a positive cellular response. The innovative design and integration of natural and synthetic materials addressed the critical challenges in scaffold fabrication and highlighted the potential for using these yarns in a range of different regenerative medicine applications. Future work should focus on optimizing the scaffold properties for specific clinical needs and validating their performance in vivo.
