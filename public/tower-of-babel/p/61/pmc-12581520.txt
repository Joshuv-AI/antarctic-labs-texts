Prevalence of depression in a large regionally representative population sample in Côte d’Ivoire
Authors: Lena Jäggi, Irene Falgas-Bague, Siaka Koné, Nicole Probst-Hensch, Günther Fink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression is one of the main contributors to the global burden of disease. Data on depression in low- and middle-income countries are still lacking, making optimal intervention planning and programming difficult.MethodsWe used regionally representative data from the Taabo Multiple Generations Cohort (Taabo MGC) to describe the prevalence of depression in rural Côte d’Ivoire. Moderate or severe depression was defined as a PHQ-9 score > = 10. We investigated overall prevalence rates as well as prevalence rates by sex, age, residence and wealth.ResultsData from 5,093 women and 3,600 men were analyzed. Overall, 7.8% of the individuals in our sample reported moderate to severe depression symptoms. The prevalence of moderate to severe depression was almost double in women (9.6% [8.83–10.45%]) than in men (5.2% [4.52–5.98%])). Adults over 60 years of age showed the highest rates of depression, with 13.4% (11.40–15.45%).ConclusionsThe results presented here suggest that the depression burden in rural Côte d’Ivoire is moderate, with a particularly high burden among women and elderly individuals. Mental health services are needed to specifically support this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24879-3.

Introduction

Depression is a leading cause of disability worldwide and one of the main contributors to the global burden of disease [1]. Despite its significant impact, data on depression in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remain limited, with particularly large gaps in Sub-Saharan Africa [2, 3]. This gap in knowledge poses challenges for appropriate resource allocation and the development of effective intervention strategies tailored to these settings.

In Côte d’Ivoire, the overall prevalence of depression has been estimated to be between 4.2% [4] and 4.7% [2]. However, these estimates largely rely on global modeling and extrapolation rather than original data collected in the country. Further, the scarce mental health, and specifically, depression data from Côte d’Ivoire are predominantly derived from urban areas, especially from the capital, Abidjan. This urban-centric focus may limit the generalizability of findings, as rural populations constitute a large part of the country’s inhabitants and remain underrepresented in available datasets [5]. Comprehensive, population-based epidemiological data on depression are lacking in Côte d’Ivoire [6]. The few existing studies evaluating the prevalence of depression within Côte d’Ivoire have focused predominantly on narrow subgroups of the population, which are often at greater risk for common mental disorders, such as women during postpartum, or used data from selected clinical trials. Recent studies with pregnant and postpartum urban women reported depression rates between 22.6% [7] and 32.9% [8], which are much higher than the national average. Among older adults living with HIV, the prevalence of severe depressive symptoms was 17.9% [9]. Taken together, these studies suggest that the current national estimates may substantially underestimate the true burden of depression in Côte d’Ivoire [3].

As of today, the country’s health system and health policy are not equipped to systematically identify and/or address its population’s mental health problems. This lack of programming leads to significant gaps in care and surveillance [6, 10]. Thus, understanding the prevalence of depression, one of the most common mental health problems, across diverse population subgroups is essential to informing policy and supporting decision-making in the country for intervention while optimizing the response to this pressing public health issue.

The present study aims to address this gap by describing the prevalence of moderate to severe depression symptoms, as measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), in a large, regionally representative community sample in Côte d’Ivoire. Specifically, we used data from the Taabo Health and Demographic Surveillance System (Taabo HDSS) to investigate the prevalence of depression across age, sex and wealth quantiles.

Methods

Study context and design

Data for this study come from the ongoing first wave of the Taabo Multigenerational Cohort (Taabo MGC), a large, regionally representative multigenerational cohort that studies intergenerational dynamics of disease over the life course in south-central Côte d’Ivoire [11]. The Taabo MCG was established in the Taabo Health and Demographic Surveillance System (Taabo HDSS), which is located approximately 180 km northwest of the economic capital of Côte d’Ivoire, Abidjan [12]. The Taabo HDSS comprises one small town (Taabo-Cité), 19 main villages and more than 150 small hamlets (see Fig. 1). In 2018, the site had a total population of 46,847. The leading causes of death in the Taabo HDSS are infectious diseases (IDs, 58.9%), which are mainly attributable to malaria, acute respiratory tract infections, HIV/AIDS, and pulmonary tuberculosis [13–15].

The study area is mainly rural, and most households depend on subsistence farming as well as cultivating cash crops of cocoa and coffee for their nutrition and income. There is also some minor livestock production (cattle, small ruminants, pigs and poultry) and fishery in the man-made Lake Taabo [16].

The Taabo MGC study is planning to recruit 3,000 pregnant women and their children, as well as the infants’ fathers, and all grandparents who are still alive and living in the study area, totaling a target sample of at least 15,000 adult participants. To be fully representative of the adult population, the study also included a sample of approximately 100 childless adults.

Study participants and procedures

As part of routine population surveillance, field staff regularly visit all households within the Taabo HDSS at least once per year to record vital events such as pregnancies, births, deaths and migration. All pregnant women identified through those routine visits are invited to participate in the study in a follow-up visit by field staff of the Taabo MGC. Additional pregnant women were identified through key informants in each village. Pregnant women are eligible to participate if they are planning to keep residing in the study area for at least a year. Pregnant women also need to provide written consent to participate themselves, for the postpartum enrollment of their living child, and for contacting biological relatives of the child living in the area, including the child’s father, own parents and own grandparents. After obtaining the woman’s written consent, information about biological fathers and other ancestors of the index child is obtained. All biological ancestors of the unborn child (maternal and paternal) are considered eligible to participate if they reside in the area and provide written informed consent. Fathers and other biological ancestors of the child to be born are then recruited and consented sequentially and separately. All participants under the age of 18 are eligible for the study if written consent for participation from their legal guardian is obtained.

Baseline study procedures included interviews and health examinations for all participants, with additional modules for pregnant women and adults over 50 years of age. The interview questionnaires included questions on sociodemographic characteristics, the GPS location of the household, lifestyle risk factors for chronic disease, and mental health assessments. Owing to low literacy rates, all interviews were administered by trained field staff, conducted in person, using French (76.2%) or local languages (14.7 Baoulé, 8.9% Malinke), on participants’ preferences. Translations were conducted during the interview, based on the adapted French version. Field workers were trained by project staff by the local research team for one week. After this initial training week, interviewers met with the local PI every other week to go over specific questions or feedback from the field.

All procedures were performed in accordance with the Helsinki declaration. The study protocol for the Taabo MGC was approved by the responsible ethics committees of North-Western Switzerland (EKNZ) and the Comité National d’Ethique des Sciences de la Vie et de la Santé (CNESVS) as IRB00011917.

The present study includes data from 8,693participants (5,093 females, 58.6%) of the ongoing Taabo MGC who had completed assessments between October 31, 2023, and May 10, 2025.

Measures

Outcome

The level of depressive symptoms was assessed using a minimally locally adapted version of the French 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [17].

The PHQ-9 is a self-report screening questionnaire, which assesses the frequency of symptoms of major depressive disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) within the last two weeks [18, 19]. The questionnaire uses a four-point response scale (0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, and 3 = nearly every day), with the sum score ranging from 0 to 27. Scores >= 10 are rated as moderate, scores >= 15 are rated as moderately severe, and scores >= 20 are considered severe symptoms of depression. A previous study reported adequate psychometric properties of the PHQ-9 in pregnant women in Côte d’Ivoire (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.65) [8]. In our study, we used a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10 to identify individuals with moderate to severe depressive symptoms, aiming to estimate the prevalence of depression. While this conservative threshold is not without limitations, it was chosen to minimize the risk of misclassification in mild cases—a concern previously noted in the literature and attributed to socio-cultural influences and the limited contextual validation of the tool in similar settings [8, 20]. For 23.8% of the interviewees who did not understand French, the questionnaire was translated by the interviewer to the local language on site. The internal consistency of the PHQ-9 in our sample was adequate, with a Cronbach’s alpha >0.68 in all subgroups.

Demographic variables

In accordance with local cultural norms, gender was observed by the interviewers and recorded as a binary variable. Age in years, literacy and country of origin were also assessed. The main occupation was classified as farming or other by the study team. The type of residence was assessed based on a classification performed by the HDSS, which divides the sample into small towns (the city of Taabo), main villages and encampments; this latter group captures the scattered settlements in the areas surrounding the main villages of the HDSS. Wealth quintiles were calculated using principle component analysis of a standardized list of household asset holdings, including ownership of radios, beds, electricity, television, fridge, fans, water pump, AC, iron, motorbike, car, watch, computer, and mobile phones.

Data analysis

First, we conducted a series of descriptive analyses to estimate the prevalence of depression (no depression versus moderate to severe depression) among participants across all subgroups. To evaluate the severity of depression among positive cases, we then calculated mean depression scores among participants with moderate to severe depression. In the second step, we repeated all previous analyses for men and women separately. We then plotted the prevalence of depression and confidence intervals by sex and age group. Finally, we conducted logistic regression analyses to predict moderate to severe depression in men and women, using all other subgroups as predictors. All analyses were completed via STATA18 software.

As expected, when depression was predicted by all demographic characteristics in the overall sample, women were 2.30 (95%CI [1.84–2.86]) times more likely to have depression than men were, and odds of depression increased for participants over 60 (OR 1.49 [95%CI 1.10–2.03]), while decreasing for farmers (OR 0.70 [95%CI 0.57–0.87] and participants in higher wealth quintiles (Table 4). When considering women and men separately, regression analyses revealed some variation in the likelihood of depression across different demographic subgroups. Only men show increased odds by residence in a village or small informal settlements (encampment), (OR 2.08 [95%CI 1.20–3.61]; OR 2.46 [95%CI 1.19–5.09], respectively), while women who live in encampments show decreased odds of having depression (OR 0.59 [95%CI 0.38–0.91]). Conversely, only women show increased odds of depression with age over 60 (OR 1.47 [95%CI 1.02–2.13] and decreased odds of depression with greater wealth.

Discussion

The study analyzed data from 8,693 participants living in Côte d’Ivoire and reported that 7.8% reported moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Compared with men, women nearly doubled in prevalence (9.6% versus 5.3%, respectively). We also find a rather pronounced age gradient, with substantial increases in depressive symptoms after age 60, with prevalences more than doubling for women over 60 compared to women 39 and younger (17.8% versus 7.7%, respectively).

The overall prevalence estimates are higher than the currently available national estimates [2, 4] but substantially lower than those reported in specific populations, such as HIV-positive patients [9] and pregnant women [7, 8]. The higher rates of depression among women appear consistent with a larger literature, even though gender differences in the expression of depression symptoms seem likely in this context; further studies on mental health perceptions in this specific setting are definitely needed.

The strong age gradient raises a range of concerns given the rapid growth of the older adult population in Cote d’Ivoire and overall Africa. As this segment of society continues to expand, health care systems must adapt and strengthen their services, ensuring that they are equipped to provide comprehensive, age-responsive care that supports both physical and mental well-being in older adults, including tailored interventions that combine disability prevention and psychotherapy to address their unique needs.

Gender differences were also observed in the likelihood of reporting depressive symptoms among individuals living in informal settlements (‘campements’) surrounding the main villages, with women in these settings exhibiting higher levels of depressive symptoms and men showing lower levels. Consistent with the overall findings and previous literature from similar settings [21], women in these contexts are disproportionately exposed to poverty-related stressors, driven by gender inequity, such as food insecurity, intimate partner violence and limited educational and economic opportunities [22]. These factors may contribute to the observed differences and underscore the importance of prioritizing women in preventive interventions. This gender disparity may be also partly attributed to men’s underreporting of depressive symptoms and the manifestation of externalizing behaviors—such as irritability, anger, and substance use—that are not adequately captured by standard depression assessment tools [23, 24].

While this study offers valuable results, it is subject to certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, data collection in rural Côte d’Ivoire poses significant challenges, including the necessity of engaging with participants in multiple local languages, logistical difficulties related to limited transportation, and generally low literacy levels, all of which may have affected the accuracy and consistency of responses. Second but related to these overall challenges, responses to the PHQ-9 scale might be influenced by varying cultural norms or linguistic differences within these subpopulations. The lack of validated study tools for this population overall, as well as for migrant populations within this region, was a challenge and critical limitation of this study. While the internal consistency of the PHQ-9 scale was good in our sample (and comparable or higher than reported in previous studies), the French PHQ-9 scale has not been validated clinically for the cultural context of rural Côte d’Ivoire. It is also important to highlight the substantial linguistic diversity in this setting, where many respondents are more comfortable with local languages than with French. In our study, 24% of the assessments were performed in local languages spoken by study staff (translated on the fly, without following a formal validated translation process as many of these languages are rarely used in written form); even with these translations, it seems likely that some of the questions were not fully understood by the respondents. The linguistic and assessment challenges faced here are a general issue for many settings in Sub-Saharan Africa, and are particularly critical among populations never attending formal schooling (and thus not or only superficially familiar with official languages) [25]. In our study, 53% of the respondents never went to school und 65% self-reported to be illiterate; even if basic conversation in French was possible for many of them, the extent to which questions based on mainly western mental health concepts were understood remains unclear. Thus, research and validation efforts for this population appears to be particularly important [26, 27].

Despite these limitations, we believe that this study provides critical insights into the so needed epidemiology of depression in Côte d’Ivoire. While treatments are currently largely unavailable in these settings, the overall depression prevalence in rural Côte d’Ivoire appears to be moderate, with overall rates of 8% [CI7.26–8.39%] affecting more women and older populations. Integrated programs and specific interventions are urgently needed and need to be carefully integrated into existing health systems to provide access to the most vulnerable populations.
