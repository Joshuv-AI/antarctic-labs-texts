Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation initiation timing and prognosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yanting Zhang, Zhaoyang Li, Qiong Song, Sen Li, Chen Chen, Jin Li, Xinbo Ding, Chao Tian, Jing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a critical life-support intervention for patients with severe respiratory and circulatory failure. Nevertheless, identifying the optimal timing of ECMO initiation and its correlation with survival, mortality, and complication rates continues to pose significant clinical challenges.PurposeThis study intends to conduct a systematic review of the existing medical literature to clarify ECMO initiation timing and its association with patient outcomes, aiming to furnish evidence-based recommendations for clinical decision-making.MethodsWe performed systematic searches across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, The Cochrane Library, and Web of Science for eligible cohort and case–control studies. Studies combining ECMO with other extracorporeal life-support modalities were excluded to eliminate survival confounding. Inclusion criteria encompassed adult ECMO recipients with reported initiation timing and at least one outcome measure. Study quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS).ResultsFrom 1583 identified records, 30 studies were finally included, comprising 2 prospective cohorts, 1 case–control, and 27 retrospective cohorts. ECMO initiation timing was heterogeneously defined across multiple time intervals and clinical scenarios. Of the full set of 30 included studies, 23 performed statistical analyses examining the link between ECMO initiation delay and mortality; 18 of these 23 studies (60% of all 30 included studies) reported that prolonged ECMO initiation time was significantly associated with higher in-hospital or long-term mortality. Five of the 23 mortality-analyzing studies detected no statistically significant timing-mortality correlation, while the remaining 7 studies only reported organ complication outcomes and did not conduct any statistical testing of mortality as an endpoint. Quality appraisal demonstrated only one low-quality study, with all others graded as medium to high quality.ConclusionFor patients with severe ARDS, cardiogenic shock, drug-induced shock, cardiac arrest, or post-cardiotomy shock, early ECMO initiation—such as within 7 days of mechanical ventilation—can effectively decrease mortality and reduce the incidence of neurological, hepatic, and renal complications.Trial registrationThis study has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD420250652365).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06256-z.Keywords: Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO), Initiation Timing, Outcomes, Systematic Review, Critical Care

Contributions to the paper

What is already known

Early ECMO initiation can reduce patient mortality and lower the incidence of complications such as neurological, hepatic, and renal dysfunction.

What this paper adds

For patients with severe ARDS, cardiogenic shock, drug-induced shock, cardiac arrest, or post-cardiotomy shock, ECMO initiation timing can be defined as follows to reduce mortality and improve outcomes: time from mechanical ventilation to ECMO cannulation (early ECMO initiation within 2–7 days), time from ICU admission to ECMO cannulation (mean 12.5 days), time from intubation to ECMO cannulation (1–4 days), time from hospital admission to ECMO initiation (0–5 days), time from CPR to ECMO cannulation (20 min or less to 40 min), time from cardiac arrest to ECMO initiation (23.2 ± 12.6 min), and intraoperative initiation during cardiac surgery.

Introduction

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO), as a form of extracorporeal circulatory support technology, is a crucial life-saving measure for patients with severe respiratory failure and circulatory failure. It can temporarily replace the functions of the heart and lungs, rapidly improve hypoxemia, enhance systemic oxygen supply, and simultaneously remove carbon dioxide. ECMO primarily includes two modes: veno-arterial ECMO (V-A ECMO) and veno-venous ECMO (V-V ECMO). ECMO has been widely applied in the treatment of patients with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock, myocarditis, and pulmonary embolism. Recent studies have shown that the timing of ECMO initiation is critically linked to patient outcomes. Data from the H1N1 registry between 2009 and 2011 indicate that when ECMO initiation is delayed until seven days after tracheal intubation, the overall survival rate decreases from 72 to 31% [1]. Moreover, early initiation of VA-ECMO can improve survival rates in other cardiac surgeries, but in-hospital mortality remains high [2–4]. Therefore, accurately determining the timing for ECMO initiation is essential for timely intervention in critically ill patients.

The guidelines from the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) suggest indications for use when the systolic blood pressure is below 90 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), urine output is less than 30 ml/h, lactate levels exceed 2 mmol/L, oxygen saturation of mixed venous blood (SVO2) is lower than 60%, and there is a change in consciousness. If traditional pharmacological and fluid optimization therapies show no significant improvement after 6 h, further consideration for V-A ECMO support is recommended [5]. Meanwhile, China's National Health Commission believes that under optimal mechanical ventilation conditions (FiO2 ≥ 0.8, tidal volume 6 ml/kg PBW, PEEP ≥ 10 cmH2O), if additional treatment measures such as protective lung ventilation strategies, lung recruitment maneuvers, and prone positioning are still ineffective, patients with hypoxemic respiratory failure (where the oxygenation index PaO2/FiO2 is < 80 mmHg) and those with respiratory failure exhibiting hypercapnia (pH < 7.25) despite optimized conventional mechanical ventilation (respiratory rate 35 breaths/min, airway plateau pressure ≤ 30 cmH2O) should be considered for further V-V ECMO support [6]. However, these criteria do not specify the optimal timing for initiating ECMO, leading to considerable uncertainty in clinical practice.

Determining the optimal timing for initiating ECMO in clinical practice is challenging. Different patient groups (such as those with ARDS, cardiogenic shock, drug poisoning, cardiac arrest, etc.) may require varying strategies for ECMO initiation. Additionally, delays in initiating ECMO can lead to worsened patient outcomes, causing them to miss the best therapeutic window, affect treatment efficacy, and even endanger their lives. Conversely, initiating ECMO too early could increase unnecessary risks (e.g., increased risk of bleeding due to anticoagulation required for ECMO, infection at the cannulation site, and financial burden from prolonged use of expensive medical resources) and resource consumption. Therefore, clarifying the relationship between the timing of ECMO initiation and patient outcomes is crucial for optimizing clinical decision-making. Consequently, this study aims to comprehensively assess the current research on the timing of ECMO initiation and its correlation with patient outcomes, with the goal of providing references and more robust guidance for the implementation of relevant clinical treatments.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420250652365). It was conducted following the 2020 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines for systematic reviews [7] (Supplementary File 1).

Study characteristics

As we anticipate no randomized controlled trials on this topic, we have chosen to include both interventional and observational studies, encompassing cohort and case–control research (involving ≥ 10 patients). We searched for full-text publications in all languages from the inception of the databases until February 28, 2025. Eligible studies must report clear, quantifiable ECMO initiation timing indicators and at least one prognostic endpoint (mortality/survival) for correlation analysis. We excluded studies that failed to provide extractable, analyzable ECMO initiation time data for subgroup comparison and quantitative synthesis. Abstracts, animal experiments, and review articles were also omitted.

Participants

The study population consists of adult patients who underwent ECMO treatment; studies that report the timing of ECMO initiation and at least one prognostic indicator (such as mortality/survival rate) were included. We excluded studies where ECMO was used in conjunction with other extracorporeal life support treatments (such as continuous renal replacement therapy), as these combination therapies could impact the survival rates associated with the prognosis. Additionally, we also excluded studies involving pediatric populations.

We excluded studies that combined ECMO with concurrent continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) or other extracorporeal support modalities. Concurrent multi-extracorporeal interventions act as strong confounding factors for mortality and organ complication endpoints: CRRT itself independently impacts renal function, fluid balance, inflammatory status and survival outcomes. Without stratified subgroup data separating ECMO-only patients from those receiving dual extracorporeal support, we could not reliably isolate the pure prognostic effect of ECMO initiation timing, which is the core exposure of our review. To eliminate irreparable confounding bias in quantitative synthesis, this exclusion rule was implemented uniformly during literature screening.

Search strategy

Supplementary File 2 provides detailed information on the search strategy. The databases we searched include PubMed, Embase, Scopus, The Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The main keywords used in the search were: VA-ECMO, VV-ECMO, ECMO, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, extracorporeal life support (ECLS), initiation timing, outcomes, mortality. Additionally, we performed citation tracking for the retrieved literature to supplement the inclusion of studies.

Study selection process

After removing duplicate studies, two researchers (YT.Z and ZY.L) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the studies based on the aforementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria to identify relevant research. Then, the same two reviewers (YT.Z and S.L) proceeded to screen the studies that met the criteria by reading the full texts. In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through discussion, and if necessary, a third evaluator (C.T) was consulted. One of the researchers then searched for potentially relevant references from all the studies with full texts and screened them again following this process.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction was performed by two researchers (YT.Z and ZY.L) using a standardized data extraction form and recorded in an established Excel collection sheet. The extracted data included detailed information on study authors, publication date, country, study location, study design, study population, sample size, ECMO type, definition and reported values of initiation timing, and prognostic outcomes (such as mortality/survival rate).

For comparative purposes, simple calculations were performed to standardize the results of the included articles: ① Values reported only as percentages were calculated as raw values when necessary; ② The proportion of male patients was calculated based on the total number of patients; ③ If outcomes were reported only for certain groups, we calculated the overall total and; ④ The median was reported with interquartile range (IQR) and the mean was reported with standard deviation (SD). Three authors (C.C, J.L, Q.S) independently performed all calculations.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [8], with a total possible score of 0 to 9 points across three domains: study selection, comparability of cohorts, and outcome evaluation. We clearly predefined three quality tiers with fixed score thresholds: studies with NOS scores ≥ 7 were categorized as high quality; those scoring 4 to 6 points were defined as moderate quality; studies with scores ≤ 3 were classified as low quality. This identical classification standard was strictly implemented in all subsequent analyses, result descriptions and table annotations to ensure full consistency throughout the manuscript. In cases of disagreement during the evaluation process between two reviewers (YT.Z and ZY.L), a consensus was reached through discussion or with the involvement of a third reviewer (S.L).

Data analysis

A qualitative descriptive approach was used to extract data after reading the entire text, describing the characteristics of the studies and summarizing the timing of ECMO initiation and prognostic outcomes for each study. Finally, a comprehensive synthesis of the results was conducted and interpreted.

The primary outcome was survival rate. A meta-analysis of the relationship between ECMO initiation timing and survival rate was conducted using RevMan 5.4 software, with OR values and 95% CI combined statistics. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. If P > 0.1 and I2 < 50%, it indicated acceptable heterogeneity among studies, and a fixed-effect model was used for analysis; if P ≤ 0.1 and I2 ≥ 50%, it suggested substantial heterogeneity among studies, and after excluding the influence of significant clinical heterogeneity, a random-effects model was employed. Funnel plots were used to assess publication bias, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To pool survival comparisons between early and delayed ECMO initiation, odds ratios (OR) and 95% CIs were retrieved following a fixed priority rule: Multivariable adjusted ORs directly reported in original articles were prioritized. These estimates were adjusted for baseline confounders such as age, disease type, pre-ECMO organ dysfunction and ventilation support parameters. Where adjusted effect sizes were unavailable, crude unadjusted ORs were manually calculated from raw binary event counts (survival/death numbers of early vs late ECMO groups) provided in tables or text.

Results

Literature screening process

The search strategy identified a total of 1583 articles, including 389 duplicates and 24 articles marked as unqualified. The remaining 1170 articles were screened based on title and abstract. We evaluated the full text of 675 articles for eligibility according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, and finally included 30 articles for systematic review. Basic features included in the study

Among the 30 included studies, only 2 were prospective cohort studies [10, 11], 1 was a case–control study [12], and the remaining 26 were retrospective cohort studies [11, 13–39]. Twelve of the studies were multicenter cohort studies [10, 15, 18–20, 24, 30–33, 35, 39], while the other 18 were single-center studies. The total sample size ranged from 17 to 9418 patients. The main types of ECMO were VV-ECMO (43.33%, 13/30) and VA-ECMO (33.33%, 10/30), with 4 studies including both VV-ECMO and VA-ECMO [20, 28, 32, 37], and 1 study did not specify the type of ECMO. The primary indications for ECMO were acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), COVID-19, cardiac arrest, receiving tricuspid valve repair or replacement, ischemic heart disease, tuberculosis, drug poisoning, and post-cardiotomy shock. The relationship between ECMO initiation timing and prognostic outcomes

From the included studies, we found several definitions from the included studies, we found several definition ECMO initiation: time from mechanical ventilation to ECMO cannulation (early ECMO initiation time concentrated between 2–7 days) [11–16, 18, 23], time from ICU admission to ECMO cannulation (average 12.5 days) [17], time from intubation to ECMO cannulation (1–4 days) [10, 19, 20, 35, 37, 38], length of time from hospital admission to ECMO initiation (0–5 days) [21, 26, 27, 32, 33, 36], time from CPR to ECMO cannulation start (20 min or less—40 min) [22, 29, 39], time from cardiac arrest to ECMO cannulation (23.2 ± 12.6 min) [25], and intraoperative or postoperative initiation during cardiac surgery [24, 28, 30, 31, 34].

Among the 30 included studies, we categorized articles according to whether they performed statistical testing of the association between ECMO initiation delay and all-cause mortality: 18 studies (60% of the total cohort) reported a statistically significant correlation (defined per each study’s original P < 0.05 threshold, or adjusted risk ratios with 95% CIs excluding 1.0) between prolonged pre-ECMO waiting time and higher mortality risk [10, 11, 15, 18, 21–26, 28, 30, 32–36, 38]; 5 studies analyzed timing-mortality associations but detected no statistically significant difference [12, 13, 16, 17, 27]. The remaining 7 studies only evaluated organ dysfunction complications and did not conduct any formal statistical test of timing against mortality endpoints, so they were not incorporated into the comparison of significant vs non-significant mortality associations. Meta-analysis

We conducted a meta-analysis of 10 studies [13, 14, 18, 21, 23, 24, 30, 31, 33, 34] that explicitly reported early and delayed initiation of ECMO. By categorizing and integrating the definitions of ECMO initiation timing from each study, we performed one overall and three subgroup meta-analyses using a random-effects model. The overall pooled meta-analysis combining all studies with variable early/late timing definitions demonstrated a statistically significant survival benefit for early ECMO initiation (OR = 1.30, 95%CI[1.07,1.58], P = 0.009). This global estimate must be interpreted with extreme caution, as included studies relied on divergent time reference points and inconsistent dichotomization thresholds for ECMO timing, leading to substantial clinical heterogeneity that could not be fully eliminated in a single pooled comparison (Fig. 2).

Overall pooled effect of early versus delayed ECMO initiation on patient survival

Subgroup analyses by standardized timing origin yielded more clinically reliable results, with significant between-subgroup heterogeneity observed across the three strata (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). For ventilation-based timing (Fig. 3), the pooled effect failed to reach statistical significance (OR = 3.38, P = 0.16, I2 = 73%) [13, 14, 18, 23], tentative signal of survival benefit, non-significant. For admission-based timing (Fig. 4), no significant survival difference was identified (OR = 2.10, P = 0.40, I2 = 71%) [21, 33], tentative survival trend without statistical significance. Intraoperative cardiac VA-ECMO (Fig. 5) showed a borderline survival advantage compared to postoperative implantation (OR = 1.83, P = 0.06, I2 = 60%) [22, 24, 30, 34], borderline tentative survival advantage for intraoperative implantation. High I2 values in all subgroups further confirmed persistent residual heterogeneity within each stratified group, driven by minor differences in exact cutoffs across individual studies.

Publication bias

We constructed a funnel plot to assess publication bias for the overall meta-analysis including 10 eligible studies. According to the widely accepted standards for large-sample meta-analysis, when the number of pooled studies is small, the statistical power of the funnel plot is low, thereby limiting the reliability of inferences based on asymmetry. Visually asymmetric distribution of effect sizes was observed on the funnel plot, which tentatively hints at potential publication bias; however, this suspected bias cannot be definitively confirmed given the small sample of included studies, and relevant inferences carry substantial uncertainty. For all three subgroup analyses containing fewer than 10 studies each, we did not perform funnel plot evaluation due to inadequate statistical power to detect bias (see Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our study investigated the relationship between the timing of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) initiation and patient prognosis through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Out of 1583 articles, 30 were finally included in the systematic evaluation, with 10 studies undergoing meta-analysis. These studies covered critically ill patients with various types and causes, including severe acute respiratory distress syndrome, cardiogenic shock, drug poisoning, cardiac arrest, etc. We have several findings: ① Although early ECMO initiation significantly reduces mortality, some studies point out that other factors such as the patient's underlying disease state, the type of ECMO (VV-ECMO or VA-ECMO), and the presence of complications may also affect the final prognosis. ② According to the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) score, most of the included studies were of medium to high quality, with only one being of low quality. This provides a certain guarantee for the reliability of the research results.

In our study, 9 studies involved patients with COVID-19. In most cases (67–85%), critically ill patients diagnosed with COVID-19 who were admitted to the ICU developed ARDS [40]. VV-ECMO, as the ultimate means of respiratory support, can maintain oxygenation and effectively implement a "lung-protective ventilation strategy" [40]. In this part of the study [10–16, 18–20, 23, 35, 37, 38], the timing of ECMO initiation was mostly within 1–4 days (77.78%, 7/9). The duration from mechanical ventilation/tracheal intubation to ECMO initiation was used to define the timing of ECMO initiation. The shorter the duration from mechanical ventilation to ECMO initiation, the higher the patient survival rate (OR = 3.38, 95%CI[0.61,18.72]). This part of the study accounted for about half (46.67%, 14/30). However, only one study clearly compared the timing of ECMO initiation between the "early ECMO" group and the "late ECMO" group in patients with COVID-19-related ARDS [18]. Compared with the early ECMO group, delayed ECMO initiation was associated with an increase in mortality at 60 days after ECMO (88 vs. 50%, P = 0.044), and the overall mortality in the ICU also increased in the delayed ECMO group (94 vs. 57%, P = 0.026). Since COVID-19 is still prevalent, it is essential to initiate ECMO in a timely manner for patients who develop severe ARDS.

Our Meta-analysis results show that the shorter the duration from hospital admission to ECMO initiation and the initiation during cardiac surgery, the higher the patient survival rate (OR = 2.10, 95%CI[0.37,12.02]) and (OR = 1.83, 95%CI[0.99,3.39]). Defining the timing of ECMO initiation as the duration from hospital admission to ECMO initiation, a study by Steimer et al. [21] showed that patients intubated within 48 h had an overall 30-day and 90-day survival rate of 80%; patients undergoing standard ECMO intubation within 3 to 6 days had a survival rate of 60%; and patients intubated after 7 days had a survival rate of 16.7% (p = 0.013). Additionally, a study by Jentzer et al. [33] also indicated that early ECMO initiation (within 24 h after admission) was associated with lower in-hospital mortality (51.6% vs 54.7%; P = 0.007). Their study revealed that the time from admission to ECMO initiation and each additional 12 h from admission to ECMO initiation were gradually associated with higher in-hospital mortality. Therefore, if a patient has indications for ECMO intubation, ECMO should be applied as early as possible within 24 h of admission, and the maximum recommended duration should not exceed 5 days.

For patients with refractory cardiogenic shock requiring cardiac surgery, initiating ECMO during the operation seems to be a better approach. In our study, four studies confirmed this point [24, 28, 30, 34]. Compared with postoperative application, patients receiving intraoperative ECMO implantation experienced shorter ECMO running time as well as fewer complications and reinterventions. Moreover, patients receiving ECMO during the operation had a higher in-hospital survival rate than those receiving ECMO postoperatively. However, this type of patient refers to those at high risk of developing cardiogenic shock after cardiotomy (e.g., patients with severe cardiac dysfunction, complex surgical procedures, or preoperative high-risk factors). For patients with lower preoperative risks, ECMO initiation can be delayed until ICU admission if clinical conditions permit [30]. This requires an accurate risk prediction model to identify the risk level of these patients.

Due to the lack of large prospective randomized clinical trials, the aforementioned findings should be interpreted with caution. In our study, only 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis, and subgroup analysis was conducted based on the definition of ECMO initiation time, which further confirmed the impact of initiation time on patient survival rate. However, the specific initiation time has not been validated. Additionally, there are still 5 studies [12, 13, 16, 17, 27] that did not indicate whether there was a statistical difference between the timing of ECMO initiation and survival/mortality rates. Furthermore, 14 studies did not report the correlation between the timing of ECMO initiation and related complications. Therefore, the role they play in clinical practice still needs to be cautiously interpreted and referenced.

Collectively, the confounding effects of divergent underlying illnesses, variable baseline disease severity, inconsistent institutional ECMO activation thresholds, and survivor bias must be considered when interpreting our tentative pooled trends linking early ECMO to improved survival. No stratified subgroup analysis in this review could fully isolate these interacting confounders, which further explains the high unresolvable heterogeneity detected across all quantitative syntheses. Future multi-center prospective research should standardize baseline severity scoring, stratify analyses by primary critical illness diagnosis, collect institutional ECMO protocol data, and account for pre-ECMO mortality to mitigate survivor bias.

Strengths and limitations

Although we identified a large number of studies, which are generally of medium to high quality, most of them were retrospective cohort studies that were not blinded, and the sample sizes were mostly small, with only 5 studies having sample sizes sourced from databases, resulting in relatively larger sample sizes for those parts but still retrospective. And a prominent limitation of our meta-analysis stems from the lack of a universally accepted definition for "early" ECMO initiation across primary studies. Investigators used distinct reference timepoints and inconsistent numerical cutoffs to separate early versus delayed support. Although we stratified analyses into three major subgroups by shared time origin and collapsed multi-tiered groups into binary early/late contrasts, residual heterogeneity within each stratum remained due to variable threshold values. The overall pooled OR combining all heterogeneous timing definitions carries limited clinical interpretability and should not be used to derive uniform clinical timing recommendations. Future prospective multicenter trials are urgently needed to establish standardized, consensus-based time thresholds for ECMO initiation stratified by critical illness phenotype.

Substantial clinical heterogeneity remained despite subgroup analyses, restricting the generalizability of our findings. First, patients received ECMO for varied critical illnesses with distinct disease progression and optimal treatment windows. Second, baseline illness severity differed across studies, introducing residual confounding to mortality outcomes. Third, ECMO strategies varied widely between centers, with some institutions adopting early proactive use and others employing late rescue-only approaches. Fourth, survivor bias existed in all retrospective studies: patients who died rapidly before cannulation were excluded, meaning our survival conclusions only apply to individuals who survived long enough to receive ECMO.

Additionally, some studies did not describe whether there was a statistical difference between ECMO initiation time and mortality, and most studies did not specifically focus on the relationship between ECMO initiation time and prognosis but reported it as a secondary outcome measure or among other outcome measures in studies with different main topics, resulting in incomplete reporting of complication rates due to organ dysfunction. Therefore, we did not draw a conclusion for this part of the report summary. These limitations emphasize the need for prospective high-quality research in this field, requiring more reliable reference standards and definitions for ECMO initiation time to provide practical basis for clinical implementation.

Another notable limitation originates from our exclusion of studies enrolling patients receiving ECMO combined with CRRT or other extracorporeal supports. In real-world clinical practice, CRRT is routinely administered to a substantial proportion of critically ill ECMO patients with acute kidney injury, fluid overload or severe inflammatory states. This screening rule inevitably excluded a considerable volume of real-world observational data and shrank the pool of analysable studies, which may reduce the generalizability of our findings to ECMO patients requiring concomitant renal replacement therapy. Future primary research should stratify outcomes by concurrent CRRT use to enable separate analysis of timing effects in dual extracorporeal support populations.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

First, prospective multicenter studies stratified by ECMO indication are urgently needed to establish etiology-specific consensus timing thresholds for VV-ECMO, VA-ECMO and ECPR, with unified definitions of early versus delayed ECMO initiation to reduce clinical heterogeneity. Second, future trials should adopt standardized diagnostic criteria for organ complications and complete outcome reporting, which will enable reliable quantitative synthesis to explore the relationship between ECMO timing and adverse events. Third, researchers need to collect detailed data on baseline disease severity, center-specific ECMO treatment strategies and pre-ECMO mortality to mitigate confounding effects and survivor bias inherent in retrospective observational studies. Additionally, subsequent analyses should stratify survival and complication outcomes by concurrent CRRT use to clarify the optimal ECMO timing among patients receiving multiple extracorporeal supports.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis tentatively suggested that delayed ECMO initiation may correlate with higher all-cause mortality in critically ill patients. Due to substantial unexplained heterogeneity from inconsistent timing definitions, diverse underlying illnesses, varying disease severity, inter-center practice differences and survivor bias, our pooled results are only exploratory rather than definitive clinical evidence. We have revised the previously proposed universal 5-day threshold from mechanical ventilation for ECMO initiation. Consistent with the reviewer’s suggestion, existing observational evidence indicates delays beyond approximately 5–7 days of mechanical ventilation in severe ARDS treated with VV-ECMO, and delays exceeding 24–48 h after admission in cardiogenic shock receiving VA-ECMO, are linked to worse survival, yet precise optimal thresholds remain uncertain. Separating time-interval analyses for VV-ECMO and VA-ECMO would deliver more targeted clinical insights, though we were unable to conduct this further stratified analysis due to the limited number of eligible studies in each subgroup. Individualized clinical decisions are therefore recommended rather than reliance on a single fixed timing cutoff. We failed to perform a quantitative synthesis of hepatic, renal and neurological complications, as nearly half of included studies lacked relevant data and complication criteria were inconsistent. Thus, there is no robust evidence confirming that early ECMO lowers these organ complication risks. Future prospective multicenter studies with indication-stratified timing criteria and standardized adverse event reporting are required to generate reliable clinical guidance on optimal ECMO initiation timing.
