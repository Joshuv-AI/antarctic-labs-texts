Anticoagulation associated intracerebral haemorrhage trends, treatment and outcomes in a metropolitan cohort: analysed by availability of specific versus non-specific reversal agents
Authors: Shaddy El-Masri, Rudy Goh, Stephen Bacchi, Lachlan Tamlin, Nicola Sulley-Beales, Siqi Chen, Sarah Howson, Brandon Stretton, Daniel Zweck, Matthew Willcourt, Andrew Moey, Jim Jannes, Timothy Kleinig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDirect oral anticoagulants (rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran) and warfarin treatment are associated with an increased risk of intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH). Specific reversal exists for warfarin (vitamin K/prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)) and dabigatran (idarucizumab). Data on protocol-guided non-specific 3-factor PCC for factor Xa inhibitor reversal are lacking.AimsOur retrospective cohort study aimed to assess anticoagulation-related ICH secular trends. We also aimed to compare administration of reversal and antihypertensive treatments, where specific reversal agents were (dabigatran and warfarin) and were not (apixaban and rivaroxaban) available.MethodsWe included patients with anticoagulation-related non-traumatic ICH of <24 hours duration from South Australian Stroke units (January 2017–December 2023). Outcomes analysed included 30-day mortality and discharge modified Rankin Scale. Secondary outcomes included time to administration of reversal agent, intravenous antihypertensives and time to systolic blood pressure (BP) lowering <140 mm Hg.ResultsOf 310 included patients (median age 83 (76, 87)), 208 (67%) were in the factor Xa inhibitor group and 102 (33%) in the warfarin/dabigatran group. The proportion of factor Xa inhibitor-associated ICH increased from 3.7% in 2017 to 19.8% in 2023 (p<0.0001). The warfarin/dabigatran group was more likely to receive reversal (57% warfarin/dabigatran vs factor Xa inhibitor 39%, p=0.005). Where administered, time from hospital arrival to reversal did not differ between groups (132 min (94, 231) warfarin/dabigatran vs factor Xa inhibitor 126 (60, 225); p=0.3), nor did time to first dose of intravenous antihypertensives, time to BP <140 mm Hg and 30-day mortality.ConclusionFactor Xa inhibitor-related ICH increased proportionally over the study period. These patients were less likely to receive reversal treatment than warfarin/dabigatran-related ICH. There was no difference between time to administration of PCC and time to antihypertensive metrics. The high mortality in our study underscores the need for effective optimised and timely treatments.

Introduction

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs: apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran) are key treatments for preventing ischaemic stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation and for treating venous thromboembolism.13 While these agents offer several advantages over warfarin, particularly a reduced risk of intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), the residual risk remains substantial, with annual rates reported between 1.4% and 3.6%.1 2 Furthermore, patients on DOACs with ICH have higher in-hospital mortality rates compared with ICH patients not previously taking anticoagulants.4

Effective anticoagulation reversal agents may reduce death and disability following ICH by preventing ICH expansion and allowing surgery when indicated.5 6 Andexanet alfa, a specific reversal agent for the factor Xa inhibitors, apixaban and rivaroxaban, was shown to reduce ICH expansion without any demonstrable benefits on morbidity/mortality.5 The study was noted to be underpowered to determine any clinical benefit; however, real-world studies indicate it may reduce mortality.7 High cost and possibly increased thrombotic risk have limited widespread adoption,7 and prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) is often used instead despite limited evidence.8 In contrast, idarucizumab, a monoclonal antibody fragment which specifically rapidly reverses dabigatran, is more readily available.9

Despite the availability of these agents, there remains a lack of robust data on the timeliness and outcomes of DOAC reversal in ICH, especially compared with the well-established protocols for warfarin reversal that are associated with improved patient outcomes.9 10

We therefore aimed to investigate the proportion of patients with anticoagulation-related ICH in a large Australian metropolitan stroke unit pre-andexanet cohort who received specific (warfarin, dabigatran) or non-specific (apixaban, rivaroxaban) reversal agents. We aimed to assess the frequency and efficacy of 3-factor PCC use in factor Xa inhibitor-related ICH. We hypothesised that the proportion of patients with factor Xa inhibitor-related ICH increased over time and that patients receiving apixaban or rivaroxaban received less frequent reversal. Additionally, we assessed ICH expansion, thrombotic events and 30-day mortality in patients receiving both specific (eg, dabigatran-related or warfarin-related PCC) and non-specific (eg, 3-F PCC for apixaban and rivaroxaban) reversal treatments.

Method

We performed a retrospective review of consecutive patients with acute ICH aged ≥18 years presenting to all metropolitan stroke centres in greater Adelaide (population 1.35 million), South Australia (Royal Adelaide Hospital, Flinders Hospital and Lyell McEwin Hospital), from stroke registry commencement (1 January 2017) until 31 December 2023.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

All patients presenting with acute stroke symptoms were assessed by neurologically trained staff. Initial assessment included National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score on presentation and brain imaging, routinely non-contrast CT, with CT angiography and perfusion imaging performed in some. Patients were included if they had a non-traumatic ICH and the last known well (LKW) time was less than 24 hours given this is likely the time whereby haematoma expansion is greatest. Exclusion criteria included patients transferred from other hospitals (other than the three specified), isolated intraventricular ICH, or ICH secondary to haemorrhagic transformation of ischaemic stroke, tumour, venous sinus thrombosis or ruptured intracranial aneurysm.

Data collection and endpoints

We collected the following baseline characteristics: age, gender, ethnicity, baseline modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, comorbidities (including anticoagulation indication), DOAC (dabigatran, apixaban or rivaroxaban), enoxaparin or warfarin use, and antiplatelet use. Presentation variables included the following: last known well (LKW) time, symptom discovery time, Glasgow Coma Scale, NIHSS; core baseline non-specific and specific coagulation studies; estimated glomerular filtration rate, ICH volume (ABC/2 method, whereby ABC are the three ICH dimensions), presence of intraventricular haemorrhage, presence of hydrocephalus.

Intervention variables included the following: timing and type of ICH reversal and blood pressure (BP) lowering, tranexamic acid use, protamine use, surgical interventions, intensive care unit admission. Outcome variables included the following: timing of demonstrable international normalised ratio (INR) reversal (in warfarin-related ICH) of INR (in DOAC use), in-hospital thrombotic PCC complications (thrombotic complications—deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, acute myocardial infarction, stroke, other), time to systolic BP ≤140 mm Hg, infusion reactions (days 0–5, 6–14, 15–30), ICH expansion (absolute and >6 mL and/or 33% increase from initial to follow-up imaging), discharge mRS, 30-day mortality and length of stay.

The primary outcome in the comparator groups (specific vs non-specific DOAC reversal) was the proportion of anticoagulation-related ICH patients with administration of ICH reversal and its association with 30-day mortality and discharge mRS. Secondary outcomes included the time to completion of reversal treatment, the time to administration of intravenous antihypertensive therapy and the time to achieving systolic BP ≤140/80 mm Hg.

Safety outcomes of interest included infusion reactions and in-hospital thrombotic complications. The International Classification of Diseases coding for these safety outcomes was also analysed and reviewed in order to ensure the accuracy of the reported safety outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (V.3.6.3; R Core Team, 2020). For descriptive statistics, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. For comparisons between groups, continuous variables were analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data or t-tests for normally distributed data, while categorical variables were analysed using Fisher’s exact test or the χ2 test, depending on the size of the observations. The proportion of anti-Xa-associated ICH was analysed using the Cochran-Armitage test for trend. Continuous variables were summarised using medians and ranges, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions.

Univariate regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with the outcomes, with a threshold of p<0.1 for inclusion in the multivariate model. Variables identified in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate regression model, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

As this study involved the retrospective analysis of actual patient data, no formal power calculation was conducted. The sample size reflects the number of eligible patients identified within the historical cohort who met the inclusion criteria.

Results

From 2017 to 2023, among 1840 total ICH admissions, we identified 310 patients with anticoagulant-associated ICH following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Total ICH admissions had a stable trend throughout the 7-year period, with the total amount of anticoagulation-associated ICH more than doubling (figure 1). The proportion of apixaban and rivaroxaban-associated haemorrhages increased from 3.7% in 2017 to 19.8% in 2023 (p<0.0001); warfarin-associated ICH declined (p=0.018).

Patient characteristics

There were no differences between age in the two cohorts. There was a greater proportion of females present in the warfarin/dabigatran cohort (53% vs 40%; p=0.037). Patients in the factor Xa inhibitor group were more likely to have atrial fibrillation (84% vs 69%; p<0.001). Most presented with a systolic BP greater than 140 mm Hg (81%). The median haematoma size for the total cohort was 14 mL (IQR 3–40) and did not differ between groups.

The total cohort median time to CT scan was 37 min (IQR 24–110). The overall median times for administration of PCC, intravenous antihypertensive (anti-HTN) treatment and time to achieve systolic BP less than 140 mm Hg were 126 min (IQR 65–228), 49 min (IQR 24–103), and 94 min (IQR 17–184), respectively. Process times did not differ between groups.

Prothrombinex (PCC) was administered more frequently in the warfarin/dabigatran group (57%) compared with the factor Xa inhibitor group (39%) (p=0.028). The dosage of Prothrombinex was also significantly higher in the warfarin/dabigatran group (p=0.028). Idarucizumab was administered to only 1/13 patients with dabigatran-associated ICH.

The in-hospital 30-day mortality rates did not differ (38% in the factor Xa inhibitor group and 33% in the warfarin/dabigatran group; p=0.7). There were no differences in the rates of venous or arterial thromboembolism, which were overall low (0.01% and 0.03%, respectively).

Prothrombinex administration factors

In the factor Xa inhibitor group, univariate analysis identified NIHSS (OR 0.97 (95% CI 0.94 to 0.99), p=0.014), first CT volume (OR 0.99 (95% CI 0.98 to 1), p=0.044) and admission mRS (OR 0.80 (95% CI 0.63 to 0.97), p=0.025) as factors meeting the threshold for inclusion in the multivariate analysis (p<0.1) (table 2). In the multivariate analysis, admission mRS remained a significant predictor of Prothrombinex administration (OR 0.78 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.96), adjusted p=0.024), suggesting that functional independence at admission was associated with a higher likelihood of receiving Prothrombinex (table 2).

Discussion

This is the first Australian study to examine the associations and outcomes of DOAC-associated ICH. In this retrospective analysis, we noted increasing proportions of factor Xa inhibitor-related ICH and identified key predictors of Prothrombinex administration and 30-day mortality. Prothrombinex use was significantly higher in the warfarin/dabigatran group compared with the factor Xa inhibitor group, with administration delays noted in both. Delays were also noted in the time to administration of intravenous anti-HTN agents and the time to achieve systolic BP less than 140 mm Hg.

Interpretation of trends

Over the 2017–2023 period, an increase in the proportion of DOAC-related ICHs with decreasing warfarin-related ICHs was observed. This change in frequency likely represents broader prescribing trends. Large population-based studies have demonstrated significant increases in DOAC prescription rates (4.7–47.9%) over a 10-year period, with declining prescription of warfarin (52.4–17.7%).11

Prothrombinex use

There was a significant difference in PCC administration between the two groups. The factor Xa inhibitor-associated ICH group had an administration rate of 39%, consistent with recent analyses.12 Although lower NIHSS scores, smaller CT haemorrhage volumes and lower mRS scores were univariate predictors of PCC administration in factor Xa inhibitor-related ICH, only lower baseline mRS remained a significant predictor in the multivariate analysis. These findings suggest that functionally independent patients were more likely to receive the reversal agents. This association was not found in warfarin/dabigatran-related ICH, suggesting that the threshold for administration in warfarinised patients may be lower, perhaps reflecting greater confidence in potential efficacy of reversal and haemostasis.

We did not find an independent association between PCC administration and 30-day mortality, aligning with previous studies.12 Evidence regarding the effect of Prothrombinex on mortality and functional outcomes remains limited, and even less data which exist to support the timing of administration alter these outcomes. Some studies have suggested that a door-to-treatment time of less than 60 min is associated with decreased mortality, although functional outcomes do not seem to benefit from faster reversal.13

Mortality outcomes

The overall 30-day mortality rate in our cohort was 39%, which is comparable to those reported in recent 90-day mortality outcomes (up to 47%) and hospital mortality (up to 33%) in European cohorts,14 and 90-day mortality outcomes in Asian cohorts (up to 39%).12 The high mortality in our study was likely related to advanced age (median 83) rather than ICH volumes (median 14 mL). In addition to age, an elevated NIHSS was also independently associated with mortality in both univariate and multivariate analyses, echoing findings from previous retrospective reviews.12 This reinforces the idea that early clinical severity and patient age remain key predictors of outcome, regardless of anticoagulation status or reversal therapy.

Additionally, we demonstrated that a shorter time to CT was associated with an elevated mortality. This is likely due to confounding by indication—severe ICH likely treated more aggressively in the acute stages as demonstrated by our cohort with a timely presentation. However, once the diagnosis was made, those with severe deficits appeared less likely to receive a reversal agent.

Antihypertensive administration

We observed a median time from ICH diagnosis to intravenous anti-HTN administration of 48 min, with a median time to systolic BP <140 mm Hg of 94 min. We lacked power to test associations with outcomes. The intensive blood pressure reduction in acute cerebral haemorrhage trial-3 (INTERACT-3) and INTERACT-4 studies provide supportive evidence of early BP lowering, targeting a BP of 130–140 mm Hg within 60 and 30 min of treatment initiation respectively,15 16 which is in line with recent guideline suggestions.17 18

Limitations

This retrospective registry study is susceptible to selection and information bias, with non-standardised reversal and BP management decisions. The grouping framework was prespecified by the availability of specific reversal agents; however, the actual uptake (ie, low idarucizumab use) reduces the clinical distinction between these categories. Redefining exposure groups post analysis would introduce bias into our analysis, and hence, we have retained our prespecified framework and acknowledge its limitation. The dabigatran sample size was small, with only a single patient receiving the specific reversal agent, and hence, our analysis primarily consisted of reversal outcomes for warfarin-associated ICH. Additionally, our registry represents real-world data and is dependent on the accuracy and completeness of the data acquisition.

The sample size, while robust for certain analyses, was insufficient to examine the impact of antihypertensive timing on haematoma expansion or clinical outcomes. Future studies should aim to prospectively examine these associations in larger cohorts. Finally, while we included data on ICH volume, follow-up assessment for haematoma expansion was not standardised.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated increasing proportions of factor Xa inhibitor-related ICH and demonstrated that these patients were less likely to receive PCC than warfarin-related ICH, possibly reflecting ambivalence regarding treatment benefit. However, treatment metrics between groups did not differ if PCC was prescribed, nor did intravenous antihypertensive treatment metrics. The high mortality in our study underscores the need for effective optimised treatments in this patient group.
