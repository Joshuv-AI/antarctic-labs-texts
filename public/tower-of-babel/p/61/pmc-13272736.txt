Research progress and prospects of nanomaterials in imaging of central nervous system diseases
Authors: Houfan Li, Lihua Deng, Zongqian Wu, Chen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanomaterials have garnered significant attention in recent years for their application in the imaging of central nervous system diseases (CNSDs). To comprehensively understand the research progress and trends in this field, this paper conducted a bibliometric analysis of relevant literature over the past fourteen years, systematically reviewing the research hotspots and development trends in the application of nanomaterials for CNSDs imaging. Using CiteSpace for visual data analysis, relationship networks among countries, institutions, journals, research categories, authors, and keywords were presented through specified mapping. The findings reveal that China is the country with the highest number of publications, the Chinese Academy of Sciences is the institution with the most published articles, and “Biomaterials” is the most frequently cited journal. Keywords such as fluorescence imaging, release, proteins, and targeted delivery represent current research hotspots, indicating a shift in focus toward precision imaging and intelligent delivery. Cluster analysis reveals that current research has progressed from single-performance optimization to the development of intelligent responsive nano-systems—integrating targeted delivery, environmentally responsive release, and multimodal imaging. However, nanomaterials still face multiple challenges and limitations spanning from basic research to clinical translation, and their application in CNSDs remains experimental. Therefore, further interdisciplinary research across materials science, chemistry, biology, and medicine is necessary to break down disciplinary barriers and promote clinical translation, ultimately enabling revolutionary breakthroughs in nano-theranostic technologies for central nervous system diseases.

Introduction

With the aging of the world population and the influence of viruses and other factors, the incidence rate of central nervous system diseases (CNSDs) is increasing, which seriously threatens human health and greatly reduces the quality of life for patients [1, 2]. However, there are many causes to induce CNSDs, how to differentiate and diagnose CNSDs is a difficult problem. The appearance of imaging technology enables CNSDs to be diagnosed quickly and accurately [3]. In imaging examination, contrast agents can be used to enhance image contrast, which is more conducive to the diagnosis and treatment guidance for CNSDs [4]. Therefore, it is very important to design targeted and efficient contrast agents for CNSDs imaging.

Nanomaterials, as a novel contrast agent, have been extensively utilized in CNSDs research due to their distinctive physical and chemical properties, including small size, large specific surface area, and high stability [5–7]. Once the safety concerns have been addressed, these nanomaterials are poised to illuminate clinical applications associated with the nervous system, exhibiting immense potential in the future. In CNSDs imaging diagnosis, nanomaterials can be used to monitor and identify changes in neurodegenerative diseases or central nervous system tumors [7, 8]. In addition, the combination of nanomaterials and therapeutic diagnostic agents can also be used for the treatment of CNSDs [9]. Therefore, nanomaterials as contrast agents in the future will break through the limitations of traditional contrast agents that can only function in diagnosis, providing a one-stop solution for diagnosing and treating diseases. Although various nanomaterials have been developed for imaging of CNSDs, few studies systematically summarize the application of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging through bibliometric methods.

In this study, we collected relevant literatures of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging from 2012 to 2025 in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC). Based on the current bibliometrics methods, 1472 articles were visually analyzed through CiteSpace and the corresponding knowledge spectrum was made. We aimed to visualize the scientific achievements of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging through knowledge maps, and explore the research hot spots of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging.

Methods

Data collection and search strategy

The Science Citation Index-Expanded and Science Citation Index of WoSCC was selected as the data source. The following terms were used to collect scientific citations from 2012 to 2025:

TS= (nanoparticle* or nanomaterial* or nanodot* or “quantum dot*” or nanosphere* or nanorod* or nanofiber* or nanotube* or nanosheet* or nanocomposite* or nanodevice* or nanocluster* or nanotechn* or nanocarrier* or nanowire* or nanoliposome* or nanoemulsion* or nanocrystal* or nanogels* or nanoconjugate* or nanodiamond* or “metal organic framework” or MOF* or nanozymes or metallic nanomaterials).

And

TS= (“multiple sclerosis” or “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis” or “Parkinson’s” or “Parkinson Disease” or “Alzheimer’s” or “Alzheimer’s disease” or “Huntington’s” or “Huntington disease” or neurodegenerative or “neurodegenerative disorder” or “neurodegenerative disease” or neurodegeneration or “Parkinson’s disease” or “Alzheimer disease” or “Huntington’s disease” or “brain tumor*” or “brain neoplasm*” or encephalitis or neuroinflammation or glioma or glioblastoma or “neurological disease*” or “neurological disorder” or “brain disorder” or “brain injury” or “central nervous system disease” or “CNS disease” or “central nervous system disorder” or “CNS disorder” or “clinical neurology” or neuroscience or epilepsy or stroke or hereditary ataxia or cerebrovascular).

The publications only include papers and reviews in English. Finally, 2030 publications were retrieved from WoSCC. We downloaded the raw data of the retrieved publications from WoSCC as plain text files, including full records and cited references. In addition, an independent examination was conducted by two radiologists (Zongqian Wu and Lihua Deng), excluding articles unrelated to the application of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging, resulting in a final selection of 1472 articles. In cases of disagreement, a third experienced imaging specialist (Chen Liu) was consulted to determine whether to exclude the article. The whole research idea and flow chart are shown in Fig. 1.

Data analysis

The collected data were then systematically analyzed using OriginPro 8, Excel 2019 and CiteSpace (Version 6.4. R1). OriginPro 8 and Excel 2019 were used to describe the number trend of articles published each year and the distribution of article journals, while CiteSpace was used to build a knowledge network map of keywords/authors/institutions/countries. In addition, CiteSpace can also be used for co-citation analysis of literature/authors/journals and keyword burst detection. In this study, we set the time slice as one year and created different types of knowledge network maps. Finally, the content of each cluster is also analyzed using LLR weighting, according to Chen Chaomei’s suggestion [10].

Analysis of cited references

Table 3 also listed the first 10 co-cited references and the number of co-citations, in which Tang W (2019) ranked first in the number of citations [11]. This article highlights nanotechnology for brain cancer theranostics across the blood-brain barrier. It covers design strategies (size, shape, ligand modification) for enhanced delivery, various imaging techniques (MRI, PET, fluorescence, etc.), and therapeutic approaches (chemotherapy, gene therapy, PDT/PTT). Challenges like safety and clinical translation are also discussed. According to the Reference Co-citation Analysis (RCA), the keywords of the co-cited literatures were divided into 14 categories, and the keywords of clustering map shown in Fig. 6 were obtained. CiteSpace provided module value (Q value, Modularity Q) and average contour value (S value, Mean Silhouette) according to the network structure and the clarity of clustering. When Q value > 0.3, the clustering structure is significant, and when the S value reaches 0.5, it can be considered convincing. The Q value of Fig. 6 is 0.7496, and the S value is 0.6773, so the cluster structure of this map was very significant, and the results were convincing.

Besides that, we also used CiteSpace to explore the timeline of keyword clustering and the strong citation explosion of co-cited references. By combining the information from Figs. 6 and 7, we can conclude that the size and occurrence time of each category vary. We found that “#1 magnetic resonance imaging” and “#8 magnetic nanoparticles” were relatively early hotspots, while “#2 quantum dots,” “#3 NIR-II fluorescence,” and “#0 photothermal therapy” are currently popular research topics. The ascendancy of these latter topics reflects the field’s ongoing technological evolution. Near-infrared II imaging, for instance, leverages probes with minimal scattering and absorption to achieve deeper tissue penetration and superior signal-to-noise ratio for in vivo visualization [14, 15]. Quantum dots offer exceptional photostability and tunable emission spectra, enabling multiplexed labeling and longitudinal tracking of dynamic biological processes [16, 17]. On the therapeutic front, photothermal therapy converts light energy into localized heat for precise, minimally invasive ablation of pathological lesions [18, 19].

From the timeline visualization of keyword co-citation clusters (Fig. 7), it is evident that research on nanomaterials in central nervous system diseases (CNSDs) imaging has undergone distinct phase-wise evolution. Prior to 2010, studies predominantly focused on well-established technologies such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic nanoparticles, with the primary goal of applying them to the diagnosis and therapeutic evaluation of major diseases like brain tumors and Alzheimer’s disease, along with initial explorations of in vivo tracking for cell-based therapies (Clusters #1, #6, #8, #9, #10). The central question at this stage was whether nanomaterials could serve as effective tools for basic imaging and therapeutic intervention in the central nervous system. This period laid the essential technological foundation and application-oriented perspective for the field.

As research progressed, the period between 2010 and 2015 witnessed a notable paradigm shift, with optically mediated active intervention emerging as a new frontier. The combination of photoacoustic imaging and photothermal therapy marked a transition from passive diagnosis toward integrated diagnosis and therapy, while the focus on intraoperative imaging and stem cell tracking reflected a pursuit of precision in clinical translation (Clusters #0, #4, #12, #13). On the other hand, the blood-brain barrier became a focal point as a major delivery bottleneck, and research in this area, often coupled with physical-assisted delivery technologies such as focused ultrasound (Clusters #5, #7), aimed to establish efficient delivery channels for the aforementioned advanced diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. The research question has been elevated to how to design a multifunctional nano platform that can intelligently cross physiological barriers and achieve precise imaging and minimally invasive treatment simultaneously.

After 2015, the field entered a phase of deepening development, characterized by the pursuit of technological refinement and strategic optimization within the established paradigm. The evolution during this period is prominently reflected in two aspects. First, the refinement of imaging technologies, exemplified by quantum dots and NIR-II imaging (Clusters #2, #3), aimed to develop a new generation of optical probes with higher signal-to-noise ratios and deeper tissue penetration, seeking to surpass the physiological limitations of conventional imaging. Second, the advancement toward intelligent delivery strategies transformed delivery (Clusters #11) from a barrier to be overcome into a smart system requiring precise programming. Research emphasis shifted toward developing intelligent nanocarriers capable of responding to the lesion microenvironment and achieving spatiotemporally precise regulation. This evolutionary trajectory reveals that the field has progressed from early-stage technological application, through the innovation of active therapeutic paradigms, to the current phase of synergistic efforts aimed at overcoming both microscopic imaging limits and macroscopic delivery challenges.

Through keyword cluster analysis (Fig. 8B), the current intellectual structure of this field is clearly centered around two major clinical focuses: brain tumors (#0) and Alzheimer’s disease (#3). This indicates that research has progressed from early-stage broad exploration to a deepened phase targeting the pathological characteristics of specific diseases. In the direction of brain tumors, research has formed a paradigm of “physically energy-mediated theranostics”, where the knowledge base closely integrates magnetic resonance imaging (#1), iron oxide nanoparticles (#6), photothermal therapy (#4), and focused ultrasound (#7) assisted delivery. This reflects a closed-loop clinical approach, where MRI localization is used to open blood-brain barrier channels via focused ultrasound, followed by imaging-guided photothermal ablation assisted by nanocarriers. In neurodegenerative diseases, a deep understanding of disease mechanisms is pursued, focusing on neuroinflammation (#2) and oxidative stress (#5), while actively exploring biomimetic delivery systems with higher biocompatibility, such as extracellular vesicles (#8). Notably, magnetic resonance imaging (#1) serves as a pivotal hub connecting these two major directions, highlighting its irreplaceable role as a core imaging and evaluation tool across diverse disease models. Meanwhile, delivery strategies exhibit a “disease-specific” specialization trend, with brain tumors favoring physically assisted potent delivery, whereas neurodegenerative disease research prefers biology-inspired intelligent delivery. This structured knowledge graph provides clear direction for designing next-generation nanotherapeutic platforms tailored to disease specificity.

Through the analysis of the emergence of keywords, this study clearly reveals the dynamic evolution path of the forefront of research in this field (Fig. 9). In the early stage (2012–2017), the forefront focused on establishing animal models, verifying the feasibility of in vivo applications of basic materials, and exploring gene therapy and cell tracking around classic models such as malignant gliomas, which belonged to the technological foundation period. The forefront of mid-term (2018–2022) has significantly expanded, manifested in the diversification of treatment modalities (such as photodynamic therapy, the rise of radiotherapy) and the expansion of disease models (such as ischemic stroke). At the same time, in-depth research on biomimetic carriers such as extracellular vesicles and disease mechanisms marks a step towards therapeutic innovation and deepening biological understanding in the field. Recently (2022 present), the forefront is clearly pointing towards precision medicine and intelligent nanotechnology. The strong emergence of keywords such as targeted delivery, controllable release, and precise regulation, as well as the emergence of new functional materials such as graphene quantum dots, jointly outline the core direction of future development: to build a next-generation nanoplatform that can intelligently sense the microenvironment of lesions and achieve precise diagnosis and treatment interventions in time and space. This sudden evolution trajectory from “basic verification” to “diversified exploration”, and then to ‘precise intelligence’ is mutually confirmed with the clustering analysis results of this study, fully depicting the dynamic landscape of the field from infancy to maturity and continuously transforming into clinical core challenges.

Discussion

Research hotspots

With the help of bibliometrics, this study summarized the nanomaterials application in the imaging of CNSDs. Through co-citation references, keywords co-occurrence analysis and keywords burst (Figs. 6, 7 and 8), the current research focus on nanomaterials in the field of CNSDs imaging was determined. These cluster nodes can be divided into four major research directions: (1) Research on the nanomaterials themselves included the preparations, functions and properties of “iron oxide nanoparticles”, “gold nanoparticles”, “quantum dots”, “magnetic nanoparticles” and “graphene quantum dots”; (2) The application of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging reflected in “blood brain barrier”, “Alzheimer’s disease”, “brain tumor”, “Parkinson disease” and other common diseases; (3) The imaging methods of nanomaterials in CNSDs imaging contained “photoacoustic imaging”, “MRI” and “near-infrared imaging”; (4) The integration of diagnosis and treatment using nanomaterials in CNSDs primarily includes “photothermal therapy”, “sonodynamic therapy”, and “photodynamic therapy”. Chronologically, recent research has primarily focused on “photoacoustic imaging” [12, 13], “near-infrared II imaging” [14, 15], “quantum dots [16, 17]”, “photothermal therapy” [18, 19], and “focused ultrasound” [20, 21]. This indicates that the current research direction has shifted from fundamental imaging and treatment toward a technology-oriented approach centered on multimodality, high precision, and the integration of diagnosis and therapy. The integrated development of these technologies has jointly promoted the continuous evolution of central nervous system diseases towards precision, intelligence, and minimally invasive diagnosis and treatment.

Beyond this descriptive landscape, deeper structural analysis reveals that these four directions are not isolated but are orchestrated by an underlying “material-disease-technology” tripartite framework. The first dimension pertains to the intergenerational evolution of material families. The developmental trajectory from early-stage magnetic nanoparticles (#8) and iron oxide nanoparticles (#6), to mid-stage quantum dots (#2) and gold nanoparticles, and further to the recently emerging graphene quantum dots (Fig. 9) clearly illustrates a paradigm shift from “single-function carriers” toward “multifunctional intelligent platforms”. This progression is fundamentally driven by the field’s sustained pursuit of biocompatibility, targeting efficiency, and theranostic integration, thereby laying the material groundwork for the subsequent dimensions. The second dimension involves the focused deepening of disease models. Cluster analysis distinctly identifies two core pathologies-brain tumors (#0) and Alzheimer’s disease (#3) a divergence that profoundly reflects the decisive influence of disease pathology on research paradigms. Brain tumor research emphasizes physical energy-mediated precision ablation (closely associated with #4 photothermal therapy and #7 focused ultrasound), driven by the focal nature of lesions requiring robust intervention. In contrast, Alzheimer’s disease research focuses on modulating the pathological microenvironment (closely linked to #2 neuroinflammation, #5 reactive oxygen species, and #8 extracellular vesicles), consistent with the diffuse nature of the disease that demands sustained and mild intervention. This disease-specific divergence suggests that future research should move beyond the pursuit of universal platforms and instead design nanoplatforms tailored to distinct pathological characteristics. The third dimension encompasses the convergent innovation of technological pathways. Drug delivery and controlled release reflect the deepening focus of research on constructing responsive and targeted delivery systems. The rise of regulation and mechanism research indicates that this field is no longer limited to verification at the application level, but rather delves into the molecular mechanisms and regulatory laws of the interaction between nanomaterials and living systems, in order to achieve more accurate diagnosis and therapeutic interventions. These emerging hotspots collectively point to a clear trend, and future research will focus more on building “intelligent nano-systems” that not only achieve high-precision imaging, but also have the ability to intelligently perceive the disease microenvironment, release drugs on demand, and regulate physiological processes, promoting the dynamic, accurate, and systematic diagnosis and treatment of central nervous system diseases.

Current challenges and limitations of nanomaterials in imaging of CNDSs

This study summarizes the research progress of nanomaterials in the field of central nervous system disease imaging through bibliometric analysis, but there may still be some limitations in this study. Firstly, literature retrieval relies on specific databases and topic strategies, which may lead to retrieval bias or omission of certain related studies. Secondly, the literature included in the analysis is limited to English publications, which may serve as sufficient information for research progress in other languages. In addition, although bibliometric methods can reveal macro trends, they are still insufficient in terms of specific technical details and deep-rooted obstacles to clinical translation. Although nanomaterials have shown great potential in the field of central nervous system disease imaging, they still face multiple key challenges and limitations from basic research to clinical translation.

Conclusions and perspectives

This study revealed the complete development trajectory of nanomaterials in the field of central nervous system disease imaging from infancy to foresight through a bibliometric analysis system: the early focus was on the validation stage of magnetic resonance imaging technology based on magnetic nanoparticles (before 2010); Subsequently, it entered a breakthrough period of integrated diagnosis and treatment paradigms such as photoacoustic imaging and photothermal therapy (2010–2015), accompanied by technologies such as focused ultrasound to overcome the bottleneck of blood-brain barrier delivery; Recently, it has moved towards the deep integration stage of imaging precision and intelligent delivery (after 2015). This evolutionary path clearly demonstrates the scientific development logic from “technological verification” to “paradigm innovation” and then to “system optimization”. The current research focus has shifted from single performance optimization to the construction of intelligent responsive nano-systems-integrating targeted delivery, environmental response release, and multimodal imaging functions, reflecting the clear direction of precision medicine and intelligent diagnosis and treatment. This evolutionary trajectory, as elucidated through the “material—disease—technology” tripartite framework, underscores the field’s coherent progression toward systematic and intelligent paradigms. However, clinical translation still faces enormous challenges, and most studies are still at the stage of experimental animal models and lack a standardized evaluation system for clinical grade materials.

Facing the future, this field urgently needs to build an interdisciplinary integrated innovation ecosystem: through deep collaboration between materials chemists, biomedical engineers, neuroscientists, and clinical physicians, establish a full chain transformation path from molecular design, animal validation to preclinical research. The key development directions should include: (1) developing multifunctional nanoprobes with clinical compatibility; (2) Developing precise and controllable delivery systems and barrier regulation technologies; (3) Build an intelligent diagnosis and treatment system that can provide real-time feedback on treatment response; (4) Establish a standardized biosafety and efficacy evaluation system. Only by breaking down disciplinary barriers and promoting bidirectional knowledge flow between laboratory and clinical settings can we ultimately achieve revolutionary breakthroughs in nano-diagnosis and treatment technology in the field of central nervous system diseases, providing truly accurate diagnosis and treatment solutions for major brain diseases such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease.
