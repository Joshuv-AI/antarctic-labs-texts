Male Sex Is Associated With Severe Pulmonary Hypertension and Worse Outcome in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Patients Referred for Right Heart Catheterization
Authors: Katarina Zeder, Klemens Orasche, Teresa John, Philipp Douschan, Vasile Foris, Shelsey Johnson, Bradley A. Maron, Nikolaus Kneidinger, Gabor Kovacs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSex differences in COPD patients with concomitant pulmonary hypertension remain unexplored. Analyzing 171 patients from the GRAPHIC registry in Graz, Austria, we found that male sex was independently associated with increased mortality risk, despite comparable comorbidities and airflow obstruction, which may be explained by the increased rate of severe PH.

To the Editor,

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is common in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and when present, it is independently associated with decreased exercise capacity [1], increased risk of exacerbations, hospitalizations, and mortality [2]. Whether sex‐based differences play a clinical and prognostic role in COPD patients with confirmed PH have not been systematically analyzed.

All patients diagnosed with COPD based on GOLD criteria, who underwent right heart catheterization (RHC) and full clinical evaluation due to suspected PH at the Medical University of Graz were entered into the prospective GRAPHIC (GRAz PH in COPD) registry13. RHC was performed in a supine position, zero‐referenced at the mid‐thoracic level, and pressure recordings were averaged over 3–5 respiratory cycles. Cardiac output was determined by thermodilution. For this analysis, we included all patients who had confirmed PH, as defined by a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg at RHC [3] between March 24, 2005 and June 30, 2023. Severe PH was defined as pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 5 wood unit (WU) [4]. We stratified patients based on their assigned gender at birth (male or female) and compared groups using the t‐test, Mann–Whitney U, or Chi‐square, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier and multivariate Cox‐regression analyses were used to analyze survival, after testing for proportionality and multicollinearity. The primary endpoint was all‐cause mortality. Vital status was inquired by the Austrian Social Security Death Index through December 12, 2023. Patients who survived the follow‐up period were censored at date of lung transplantation or June 30, 2023, allowing a 6 months follow‐up period. Statistical analysis was done in SPSS v29.0 and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant. This study was approved by the institutional ethics board (ID:1262/2024) of the Medical University Graz.

We included 171 patients with a median age of 68 [IQR 61–73] years; 100 (58%) were male, median packyears 30 [IQR 15–46], hemoglobin 13.7 ± 2.3 pg/mL, GOLD stage I/II/III/IV in 10.5%/49.1%/25.7%/11.1%, FEV1 55 ± 20%pred, FEV1%FVC 60 [IQR 50–66], DLCOcSB 53 ± 21%pred, world health organization functional class 1/2/3/4 in 2%/28%/62%/8%, 6‐minute walking test 297 ± 114 m, and median tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion on echocardiography 19 [IQR 16–23] mm (Table 1). In RHC evaluation across the cohort, mPAP of 35 [IQR 27–43] mmHg, PVR 4.7 [IQR 3.2–7.9] wood units, right atrial pressure 7 [IQR 5–11] mmHg, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) 10 [IQR 8–13] mmHg, cardiac index (CI) 2.5 [IQR 2.1–2.9] L/min/m2. There were 112 deaths during a median follow‐up time of 4.0 [2.2–8.2] years and two patients (both male) underwent lung transplantation during follow‐up. Compared to females, men had a stronger smoking history (37 [IQR 23–58] vs. 15 [IQR 8–30] packyears, p < 0.001), decreased CI (2.4 [IQR 2–2.8] vs. 2.6 [IQR 2.2–3.3] L/min/m2, p = 0.024), and increased prevalence of severe PH at index presentation (53% vs. 34%, p = 0.010). On the other hand, there were no significant differences between the groups regarding their airflow obstruction (FEV1:p = 0.954), cardiac comorbidities, history of pulmonary embolism (p = 0.182), mPAP (p = 0.075), PAWP (p = 0.412), or receiving PH therapy during follow‐up (men: 31% vs. 20%, p = 0.099).

Male sex was significantly associated with increased mortality risk (p = 0.004; Figure 1a), even after adjusting for age, FEV1, hemoglobin, smoking pack‐years, and PH therapy (HR 1.81 [95%CI 1.04–3.17], p= 0.037) compared to women. The median survival for women was 8.6 (IQR 6.1–11.1) years, while it was only 3.8 (IQR 2.8–4.8) years for men. In addition, severe PH was significantly associated with increased adjusted mortality risk in both sex groups (p < 0.001; Figure 1b). Of note, implementation of PH therapy during follow‐up was not significantly associated with survival in the whole cohort (log‐rank: p = 0.437), but when restricting the analysis to patients with severe PH only, we observed an age‐adjusted survival advantage in patients who received off‐label treatment as compared to those who did not (HR 0.51 [95% CI 0.29–0.89], p = 0.020).

In our study, we demonstrated that in COPD patients with confirmed PH, male sex was independently associated with increased mortality risk, despite comparable airflow obstruction to females, which may be explained by the increased prevalence of severe PH. This observation is clinically highly relevant considering the fact that median lifespan from diagnostic RHC was almost 5 years shorter in men compared to women.

In a previous analysis of the COMPERA registry, male sex was not significantly associated with mortality in PH associated with COPD patients in a univariate analysis, but turned significant in the multivariate analysis [5]. Our data support this observation and expand the existing data by the increased prevalence of severe PH as probable explanation, which has implications for clinical PH detection and management.

In COPD, PH is usually mild to moderate but may be severe in a small subset of patients [2]. The presence of severe PH, defined by a strong increase in PVR, is mainly attributed to underlying pulmonary vascular disease (PVD). In histological evaluation of COPD patients undergoing lung transplantation, severe PH was frequently associated with pronounced pulmonary arterial remodeling in combination with less severe emphysema [6], supporting this hypothesis. In our study, men had significantly increased PVRI and decreased CI, while parameters for COPD severity and prevalence of comorbidities were not different compared to women, further suggesting underlying PVD as a driver of adverse outcome.

The reason for the increased prevalence of severe PH in male patients remains unclear. A potential mechanism may include cigarette smoking and its associated inflammation and hypoxic vasoconstriction [7, 8], as men had significantly more pack‐years and lower pO2 values compared to women, although there was no difference in airflow obstruction between groups. While we did not observe significant correlations between packyears and pulmonary hemodynamics at rest (data not shown), we observed a significant negative correlation between packyears and diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide, which might re‐enforce the potential effect of smoking on the small pulmonary vasculature and gas exchange or the development of severe emphysema [9] that may be associated with severe PH and impaired prognosis. A potential alternative explanation is a lower clinical suspicion of PH in males as compared to females, resulting in a delay in RHC referral and PH diagnosis. However, data that show a transition of non‐severe PH to severe PH in COPD over time, if left unrecognized, are scarce [10]. In addition, noninvasive clinical markers that raise suspicion of PH [3], including World Health Organization‐Functional Class and N‐terminal pro brain natriuretic peptide were not different between groups, and other parameters, such as a lower pCO2 and decreased 6‐minute walking test would clinically enhance and not decrease PH suspicion in men.

In pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), the so called “sex paradox” describes female sex as a risk factor for PH, but at the same time, female PAH patients present with better right ventricular function and survival as compared to males [11]. Despite a similar mortality association in our COPD cohort, we did not observe significant differences in echocardiographic parameters of the right heart, including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), systolic pulmonary arterial pressure (sPAP), and TAPSE/sPAP ratio that would explain the sex‐based survival difference. This is in contrast to a previous study investigating a mixed group 3 PH cohort (COPD, lung fibrosis, obstructive sleep apnea) that observed that men had decreased right ventricular systolic, but not diastolic, function compared to females, which was associated with poor outcome [12].

Based on these collective observations, enhanced PH awareness and assessment of pulmonary hemodynamics in COPD, particularly in male patients is warranted. For example, applying noninvasive scores to detect severe PH [13] or advanced imaging tools [14] may promote detection of severe PH in COPD and facilitate inclusion into novel drug trials.

Limitations of our study include a retrospective, single‐center study design and the lack of chest CT data to analyze the contribution of parenchymal abnormalities. These may be compensated by the relatively large sample size and detailed characterization of the patients and systematical enrollment into a prospective registry.

In conclusion, male sex is independently associated with increased mortality risk in COPD patients with confirmed PH, which may be primarily due to the increased rate of severe PH. In ongoing and planned prospective trials special attention should be paid to sex‐based differences in treatment response and outcome.
