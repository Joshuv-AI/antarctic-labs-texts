Development and Feasibility Assessment of Mobile Application-Based Digital Therapeutics for Postoperative Supportive Care in Gastric Cancer Patients Following Gastrectomy
Authors: Ji-Hyeon Park, Hyuk-Joon Lee, JeeSun Kim, Yo-Seok Cho, Sunjoo Lee, Seongmin Park, Hwinyeong Choe, Eunhwa Song, Youngran Kim, Seong-Ho Kong, Do Joong Park, Byung-Ho Nam, Han-Kwang Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to develop and assess the feasibility and effectiveness of digital therapeutics for supportive care after gastrectomy.Materials and MethodThe study included 39 patients with gastric cancer who underwent minimally invasive gastrectomy and were able to use a mobile application (app) on their smartphones. The developed research app automatically calculates and provides daily targets for calorie and protein intake based on the patient’s body mass index (BMI). Patients recorded their daily diets, weights, and symptoms in the app and completed special questionnaires to assess the feasibility of the app in real-world clinical practice.ResultsAt the 10-week follow-up, the mean questionnaire scores for ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness of the app (primary endpoint) were 2.32±0.41, 2.35±0.43, and 2.4±0.39 (range: 0–3), respectively. Patients were classified as underweight (<18.5, n=4), normal (18.5–24.9, n=24), or overweight (≥25.0, n=11) according to predischarge BMI. Underweight patients showed higher compliance with app usage and a higher rate of achieving the target calorie and protein intake than normal weight and overweight patients (98% vs. 77% vs. 81%, p=0.0313; 102% vs. 75% vs. 61%, P=0.0111; 106% vs. 79% vs. 64%, P=0.0429). Two patients transitioned from underweight to normal weight (50.0%), one patient (4.3%) transitioned from normal weight to underweight, and two patients (22.2%) transitioned from overweight to normal weight.ConclusionsThe mobile app is feasible and useful for postoperative supportive care in terms of ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness. Digital therapeutics may be an effective way to provide supportive care for postgastrectomy patients, particularly in terms of nutrition.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04800991Keywords: Gastric cancer, Gastrectomy, Mobile application, Digital therapeutics, Nutrition

INTRODUCTION

Although curative gastrectomy is the most effective treatment for gastric cancer, most patients experience weight loss of approximately 10%–20% of their preoperative weight after gastric cancer surgery [1]. In our previous study, we found that weight change after gastrectomy was most noticeable within 6 months of gastrectomy, and weight did not recover afterward [2,3,4]. Therefore, effective methods of nutritional management and supportive care are required immediately after gastrectomy.

In previous studies, active nutritional education in patients with gastric cancer who underwent gastrectomy resulted in improved eating habits and quality of life (QoL) [5]. Even after discharge from the hospital, there is a need for a method to evaluate the degree of malnutrition in patients undergoing early gastrectomy and to provide continuous information and effective monitoring to educate patients.

A randomized controlled study of patients with esophageal cancer who underwent esophagectomy revealed that patients who received mobile phone- and Internet-based continuous postoperative nutritional management had higher nutritional value, QoL, and management satisfaction than patients in the control group [6]. However, this method requires considerable manpower and is expensive. Therefore, there is a need for effective tools that enable additional educational programs to help patients understand their disease and manage their health independently to prevent complications.

Digital therapeutics, a subset of digital health, is an evidence-based therapeutic intervention driven by high-quality software programs that prevent, manage, or treat medical disorders or diseases [7]. With recent developments in information and communication technology, mobile applications (apps) for healthcare have gained attention as close aids in patient management of numerous cancers and diseases, with related research actively underway [8,9,10,11,12]. Moreover, the use of high-quality apps in the postoperative management of patients with cancer is expected to contribute to reducing medical costs and improving QoL by providing useful information and promoting communication with medical staff [13]. Our research team questioned whether a mobile application that could provide effective nutritional management or supportive care after gastrectomy could be developed.

To evaluate the feasibility of postoperative management using “digital therapeutics” for patients who undergo gastrectomy, our research team developed a “mobile app for subjects-web portal for researchers.” This study aimed to assess the feasibility of using the application as a postoperative management tool for patients with gastric cancer who have undergone gastrectomy in real-world clinical practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted in two phases: development and feasibility assessment.

Development of a mobile app for patients and a web portal for researchers (phase 1)

To develop mobile app-based digital therapeutics for postgastrectomy patients, gastrointestinal (GI) surgical teams at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH), including GI surgeons, clinical dieticians, nurses, and coordinators, provided clinical knowledge and input. HERINGS, a personalized digital healthcare service company, developed an app system. Six months of continuous feedback and collaboration between the SNUH GI surgical team and HERINGS resulted in the development of the software HDT-202 (HERINGS Digital Therapeutic-202, Pipeline Serial Number), also known as Rhexium Onco Nutrition. This software was specifically designed to manage the diet and symptoms of patients who underwent gastrectomy for gastric cancer. HDT-202 comprises a “subject mobile app (version: 1.2.0, operation environment: Android operating system [app])” and an “investigator web portal (version 1.2.0, operation environment: Chrome browser [Web])” (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Functions of the mobile app for postgastrectomy patients

1) Daily meal recording

Patients can record meals immediately on the home screen, including three main meals and three mini-meals according to specific meal management plans following gastrectomy and up to three additional mini-meals can be added by selecting the “+” button. The time at which the participant recorded the meal was automatically indicated as the meal-intake time. Patients can select the food they eat through a food name search, recommended food lists, and recently searched food lists and select the amount of intake. The intake can be adjusted in units of 0.25, and up to three servings can be provided. When the patient clicked the save button after completing the meal record, the meal intake time, menu, calorie intake, and protein intake were automatically calculated and provided according to the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety Food Nutrients Database (https://various.foodsafetykorea.go.kr/nutrient/). If the patient has not eaten breakfast, lunch, or dinner, “did not eat” can be clicked, and this information is not provided (Supplementary Fig. 2).

2) Daily weight recording

Patients were able to record their weight daily (Fig. 1A) and monitor any changes. Once patients enter their weight up to the first decimal place and click the “Save” button, the weight is automatically transferred to the weight statistics screen. The weight statistics screen displays a graph and list of weights recorded thus far by day, week, and month. The body mass index (BMI) and the standard weight range (height[m]*height[m]*18.5–height[m]*height[m]*25, 18.5≤BMI<25 kg/m2) are automatically calculated and displayed. This helps to determine whether one’s current weight is classified as underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5 ≤BMI <25 kg/m2), or overweight (BMI ≥25 kg/m2). Moreover, the target calorie and protein intakes were automatically calculated to reach normal weight, accounting for the difference between the preoperative and current weights (according to the formula described in Fig. 1B). For example, patients were initially categorized into underweight, normal weight, and overweight groups based on their preoperative BMI. Subsequently, their current weight was used to calculate BMI again, and the patients were categorized as underweight, normal weight, and overweight. Based on these categories, recommended calorie and protein intakes to reach a normal weight were calculated using appropriate formulas and provided to the patients. The actual intake compared to the recommended amount is demonstrated as a graph, making it easy to understand (Fig. 1A).

3) Daily symptom recording

Patients can enter symptoms from among 11 provided every day: abdominal pain, diarrhea, cold sweat, dizziness, severe hunger, lethargy/hand tremors, early satiety, bloating/burp, reflux, constipation, and “no symptoms.” Other symptoms can be manually entered into the notepad and duplicate selection is possible. When a symptom was selected, detailed questions appeared according to the symptoms and a symptom relief guide was provided based on the answers to the questions.

4) Weekly mission

The weekly mission is provided with a “meal guide” and a “practice list” that are suitable for the patient’s recovery time, and the mission performance rate is shown by clicking the “Save” button after checking the patient’s practice list. The practice list could be manually added or deleted from the manager’s web portal at the discretion of the clinical dietitians. If the practice list was changed, the patient could evaluate the added practice list the following day.

5) Report

The main page of the report displays the accumulated weight and caloric intake from the time the patient signed the application. At the bottom of the main page, patients can access detailed pages for meal statistics, weight statistics, symptom statistics, mission statistics, and review statistics for the previous 7 days including the previous week and month (Supplementary Fig. 3).

6) Community

Patients can post their own thoughts by clicking on “Write.” All posts written by the patients can be evaluated in the form of a list, and comments and “likes” can be added to any post. A push notification is provided when a comment is added to a post. Patients can modify or delete their posts. The administrator has the right to delete posts deemed inappropriate for the community (such as slandering others and advertisements) without the consent of the posters.

Functions of the web portal for researchers

1) Patient management

In the “Patient Management” menu, patient information can be registered and modified, and the registered contents can be assessed. Registered patients can be viewed on a list, and when a registration number is clicked, the view moves to the patient information page, where patient information can be modified. The software and systematic management of the web portal for researchers were handled by HERINGS, whereas patient management was conducted by research coordinators at SNUH.

2) Statistics

In the “Statistics” menu, detailed records of app usage and summary statistics for each patient can be found. Under the “Compliance of last week” section, the compliance of each patient’s app usage from the previous week can be evaluated. The “Whether to end or not” section allows checking whether the research study should be continued or discontinued for each patient and enables the processing of a patient’s research termination. Clicking on a registration number from the patient list will direct the researcher to the “Inquiry by patient” page. The first tab, “Comprehensive,” on the “Inquiry by patient” page displays the overall accumulated history of meals, weight, symptoms, and mission performance rate at once (Supplementary Fig. 4). The second tab, “Meal statistics,” provides a detailed history of meals. The third tab, “Weight statistics,” displays the weight records in detail (Supplementary Fig. 5). The fourth tab, “Symptom statistics,” allows checking the symptom history in detail. The fifth tab, “Mission statistics,” displays the mission performance rate and mission contents in detail.

Feasibility assessment of the mobile app and investigator web portal (phase 2)

Study design and ethical statement

To evaluate the feasibility of the application and web portal, a prospective single-arm pilot study was conducted. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of SNUH (No. H-2011-020-1169) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (No. NCT04800991). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study.

Participants

This study was conducted with participants who satisfied the following criteria: 1) age 19 or older; 2) gastrectomy for curative intent after a diagnosis of gastric cancer; 3) capable of consuming an oral diet after gastrectomy; 4) owned an Android operating system-based smartphone; and 5) capable of using the research app.

The following patients were excluded from this study: 1) individuals with a history of diagnosis of other malignancies or metastases within 5 years from the time of screening; 2) individuals who were undergoing or planning to undergo enteral tube feeding or total parenteral nutrition; 3) individuals who were determined by the researchers to be ineligible for this study because of clinically significant medical or psychiatric problems; 4) individuals who owned a mobile device that was unable to install or run the app because of technical problems; and 5) individuals for whom the researchers determined that they were getting accustomed to using the research app or complying with other research procedures.

Outcomes

1) Primary endpoint

To evaluate the feasibility of the mobile app for subjects–web portal for researchers in terms of ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness, two different questionnaires named the “HDT-202 UX questionnaire” were developed: one for patients and another for researchers. The HDT-202 UX questionnaire for patients consisted of 22 questions (four questions for ease of learning, eight questions for usability, and 10 questions for effectiveness) to evaluate the feasibility of the mobile app (Supplementary Table 1), and the questionnaire for researchers consisted of nine questions (two questions for ease of learning, three questions for usability, and four questions for effectiveness) to assess the feasibility of the web portal, including 1–2 infinitive questions in each section (Supplementary Table 2). Points are assigned to each item as follows: strongly agree (3), agree (2), disagree (1), and strongly disagree (0).

The primary endpoints of this study were the scores for each section of the HDT-202 UX questionnaire for the patients at 2 weeks (visit 2), 6 weeks (visit 3), and 10 weeks (visit 4) after discharge; the HDT-202 UX questionnaire for researchers was assessed only once at 6 weeks (visit 3) after patient discharge. The patients and researchers were asked to provide a numerical value for each question from 0 to 3.

2) Secondary endpoints

The secondary endpoints of this study were as follows: 1) compliance with the research app according to the patients’ underlying characteristics (age, sex, operation name, medical history, cancer stage, baseline BMI, etc.); 2) changes in weight and achievement rate of target calorie and protein intake at 10 weeks (visit 4) compared to the baseline (visit 1) according to compliance with the research app; 3) frequency of postgastrectomy syndrome symptoms; and 4) changes in score for QoL questionnaires—European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire - Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) [14], EORTC QLQ – Gastric cancer module 22 (EORTC QLQ-STO22) [15], and Korean Quality of life in Stomach cancer patient Study group (KOQUSS) [16]— at 10 weeks (visit 4) compared to baseline (visit 1).

Study protocol and measurements

The patients enrolled in the study visited the hospital four times, including a baseline visit (visit 1). A 10-week follow-up period, starting immediately after hospital discharge, was selected to encompass the critical postoperative phase, which is marked by significant dietary changes and post-gastrectomy symptoms. During hospital visits, patients were required to carry a mobile device with a research app installed. The researchers assessed the data entered by the patients in the research app, conducted nutritional evaluations, and consulted with them. The parameters investigated and measured in patients at each visit are shown (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of the study.

1) Visit 1: discharge date after gastrectomy

During the screening period (hospitalization period after gastrectomy), patients’ demographic information, medical and medication histories, operation records, and pathology reports were explored. Among the patients qualified for this study, the following procedures were performed by those who agreed to participate and provided written informed consent. Preoperative weight and BMI were recorded. Height was measured only at the baseline. A research scale (model name: CAS, BF-1041-A) was placed in the hospital outpatient clinic and the same scale was distributed to patients on the discharge date (visit 1). Therefore, patients were able to weigh themselves under the same conditions throughout the study: every morning and upon waking up, their weight was measured on the research scale after the first urination with an empty stomach and in consistent clothing. The research app was installed on the patients’ mobile devices and guidance was provided on how to use it. Quality-of-life evaluation questionnaires (EORTC QLQ-C30, EORTC QLQ-STO22, and KOQUSS) were administered.

2) Visit 2: 2 weeks ± 3 days after discharge

A feasibility evaluation for the use of the research app was conducted through the “HDT-202 UX questionnaire for patients.”

3) Visit 3: 6 weeks ± 3 days after discharge

Feasibility evaluations of the use of the research app were conducted separately for both patients and researchers.

a. Patients completed the “HDT-202 UX questionnaire for patients” in the same way as at visit 3.

b. The “HDT-202 UX questionnaire for researchers” was completed by the researchers (surgeons and clinical dietitians). Surgeons who enrolled the study participants completed the questionnaire once during the 6-week visit (visit 3) for the first or second patient. Clinical dietitians who provided nutritional counseling completed the questionnaire once during the 6-week visit (visit 3) of the 20th patient (50% of the target study subjects were enrolled) or of patients registered thereafter.

4) Visit 4: 10 weeks ± 3 days after discharge

The HDT-202 UX questionnaire and the EORTC QLQ-C30, EORTC QLQ-STO22, and KOQUSS questionnaires were completed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Analysis was performed using data from all recruited patients. For continuous variables, descriptive statistics including mean and standard deviation (SD) are summarized as the means ± SDs, while categorical data are presented as proportions/percentages (%). The participants’ characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. For all statistical tests, statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Descriptive statistics (average, SD, median, minimum, and maximum) were used for the primary endpoint. Cronbach's α was used to evaluate the internal consistency of each section of the questionnaire.

For the secondary endpoints, the paired t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, two-sample t-test, Mann-Whitney test, analysis of variance, and Kruskal-Wallis test were used appropriately. The chi-square test was used to investigate the differences in app usage outcomes between groups defined according to baseline BMI. A two-sample t-test was used to examine differences in the results based on app usage compliance. Descriptive statistics were used to examine the number of postgastrectomy-related symptoms. The change in the QoL score for each QoL questionnaire was examined using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and paired t-test. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to describe the correlation between weight change and the protein intake achievement rate.

RESULTS

Patient enrollment and baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 2, 40 patients who satisfied the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Among them, three patients dropped out due to withdrawal of consent, one before visit 2 and two before visit 3. One patient was withdrawn based on the operator’s decision prior to visit 3 because of an additional remnant total gastrectomy performed due to a familial history of gastric cancer with multiple poorly cohesive carcinomas in the final histologic report. The final analysis was conducted on participants who had completed at least visit 2 (n=39).

The mean age and baseline BMI (visit 1) were 52.49±9.16 years and 23.11±3.12 kg/m2, respectively. All patients underwent minimally invasive gastrectomy (76.92% laparoscopic, 23.08% robotic). Most patients had stage I gastric cancer (97.44%). Only one patient (2.56%) had stage II gastric cancer and received adjuvant chemotherapy (Table 1).

The average scores (range: 0–3) of the “HDT-202 UX questionnaire for researchers and dieticians” in the ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness section at 6 weeks (visit 3) after patient discharge were 2.21±0.57, 2.05±0.23, and 2.46±0.44, respectively (Table 2).

Secondary end points

The average scores (range: 0–3) of the “HDT-202 UX questionnaire for patients” in the ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness sections were analyzed according to age, sex, medical history, operation name, and approach. The results demonstrated that the average score for ease of learning at 10 weeks after discharge (4 visit) was significantly higher in patients aged <50 years than in those aged 50–60 years and those aged >60 years (2.56±0.36 vs. 2.27±0.36 vs. 2.06±0.39, P=0.0167) (Supplementary Table 3). The average score for usability at 10 weeks (4 visit) after discharge was significantly lower in patients with a medical history than in those without (2.17±0.37 vs. 2.46±0.44, P=0.0382). Patients who were underweight (BMI <18.5) at baseline showed higher scores for ease of learning at 6 weeks (visit 3) after discharge than patients with normal weight (18.5≤BMI<25) or overweight (BMI≥25): 2.94±0.13, 2.29±0.42, and 2.22±0.42 (P=0.0123).

In the subgroup analysis based on baseline BMI, the body weight changes at 10 weeks (visit 4) after discharge were 1.6±1.93, −0.70±1.91, and −2.11±3.44 for the underweight, normal, and obese groups, respectively (Table 3). The usage compliance of the research app during the 2-, 6-, and 10-week study periods was highest in the underweight group: 95.00±10.00 vs. 80.42±25.89 vs. 76.07±30.31 for 2 weeks (P=0.2727); 98.03±3.95 vs. 75.38±24.24 vs. 79.40±15.46 for 6 weeks (P=0.0301); 98.46±4.89 vs. 77.32±22.47 vs. 80.91±14.68 for 10 weeks (P=0.0313) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 4). The target calorie and protein intake achievement rates at 10 weeks after discharge were also highest in the underweight group: 101.9±26.35 vs. 75.16±18.61 vs. 60.83±27.85 (P=0.0111); 105.58±33.27 vs. 79.43±22.29 vs. 64.21±35.17 (P=0.0429) (Table 3). Over the course of the study, two patients in the underweight group were reclassified into the normal group after 10 weeks: one was reclassified from normal to underweight, and two were reclassified from obese to normal (Table 3).

Symptoms related to postgastrectomy frequently occurred until 6 weeks after surgery but subsequently decreased: 239 cases in 89.74% of patients within 1–2 weeks after gastrectomy, 302 cases in 94.44% of patients within 3–6 weeks, and 167 cases in 69.44% of patients within 7–10 weeks. Bloating/burping, diarrhea, and abdominal pain were the most common symptoms reported immediately after surgery. However, as time passed, bloating/burping and abdominal pain decreased, while the prevalence of diarrhea increased to 83 cases at 10 weeks (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

As the rate of early gastric cancer detection increases and minimally invasive surgery continues to improve, recovery after gastrectomy in gastric cancer patients is gradually accelerating [17,18,19,20,21]. However, regardless of the extent of gastrectomy, gastrectomy still causes a lack of calorie intake owing to a decreased diet relative to that before surgery, resulting in weight loss and nutritional imbalance [22]. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of “digital therapeutics” as a postoperative supportive treatment in gastric cancer patients after gastrectomy.

Post-gastrectomy patients often face significant challenges related to dietary adjustments and management of unexpected symptoms following discharge. Despite receiving general nutrition counseling prior to discharge [23], continuity in care is frequently lacking, as healthcare providers are typically unable to monitor patients’ dietary difficulties, weight fluctuations, or symptom progression until subsequent outpatient visits.

In response to the increasing development of mobile applications across various fields [24,25,26,27], our research team developed a mobile app to address this critical gap in postoperative care, highlighting on three key areas: nutritional management, post-gastrectomy syndrome, and patient-provider communication. Research nurses and coordinators monitor app adherence weekly and reach patients with <70% adherence to encourage use. Dietitians can respond to dietary inquiries within 24 hours (excluding weekends) after reviewing patient records. During the outpatient visit, the physician will access the web portal to review the patient’s nutritional intake and post-gastrectomy symptoms recorded over a 2–4-week period following hospital discharge. The app supports the sharing of information and experiences among post-gastrectomy patients through a community window for peer communication. This comprehensive approach enables proactive management and support of patients after gastrectomy.

The primary endpoint of this study was to evaluate the average scores on the HDT-202 UX questionnaire, which measures the ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness from both patients’ and researchers’ perspectives. As shown in Table 2, both the patients and researchers scored the app highly, with scores ranging from 2 to 3. A score of 0 indicates an unusable or unacceptable state, whereas a score of 3 indicates a completely seamless or easy-to-use state. Over the course of 10 weeks, more than 97% of patients showed positive responses of 2 points or more to questions such as “I think most people will easily learn how to use the app,” “Overall, it was convenient for me to use the app,” and “I recommend using the app to other gastric cancer patients” (Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, the high scores obtained in all three categories demonstrated that the research app for postoperative supportive care achieved a high level of satisfaction in terms of ease of learning, usability, and effectiveness.

One problematic aspect was that Cronbach's α for the ease of learning section of the patient questionnaire tended to decrease over the 10-week period following discharge (2 weeks=0.7748; 6 weeks=0.5535; 10 weeks=0.4164, Table 2), while that for the usability section of the researcher questionnaire was negative (6 weeks=−1.9500, Table 2). Upon retrospectively analyzing the questionnaire items and response patterns, we determined that questions with negative statements (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), which were designed to increase the questionnaire's discrimination power, were responsible for decreasing Cronbach's alpha. While patients initially read the questionnaire carefully and answered it accurately, over time they may have tended to answer the questions without reading them as negative statements. The researchers filled out the questionnaire only once, 6 weeks after patient discharge, and many of the researchers stated that they did not realize that the questionnaire items were negative statements when they were asked to fill out the questionnaire.

As demonstrated in Table 3, app usage compliance, target calorie achievement rate, and protein intake achievement rate were significantly higher in the underweight group. This is probably because patients in the underweight group were more seriously considered to maintain their diet and nutrition after gastrectomy. In the high usage compliance group, target calorie and protein intake achievement rates were significantly higher. Two of four patients who were underweight before surgery gained weight and reached normal weight after 10 weeks, while two of seven patients who were overweight achieved normal weight after surgery. One of the 23 patients with normal preoperative weight lost weight and became underweight.

Therefore, we investigated whether effective dietary management could be achieved in patients who frequently used the app. According to the results of Table 4, patients with a usage compliance of 80.5% or higher demonstrated higher rates of target calorie and protein intake achievement, both exceeding 80%. Conversely, patients with compliance rates of 80.5% or lower had lower rates of calorie and protein intake achievement, which is approximately 60%. App utilization was lower in older patients (≥60 years old) than in younger patients, as we expected (Supplementary Table 3). According to our results, higher usage compliance with the app showed more positive results. Thus, it may be helpful for coordinators and nurses to actively educate and encourage the usage of the app, especially for older patients.

Postgastrectomy syndromes are a consequence of the alterations in form and function of the stomach that occur as a result of gastrectomy. This surgical procedure disrupts the stomach’s ability to act as a reservoir, and its mechanical digestion and gastric emptying functions. It is of the utmost importance to promptly recognize the symptoms of such syndromes and undertake a thorough evaluation, followed by appropriate treatment. Many of these syndromes can be resolved with minimal intervention or dietary modifications [28]. It is assumed that patients who have undergone gastrectomy will experience various uncomfortable symptoms while alone at home until their next outpatient visit. However, it is difficult for medical staff to know how many patients suffer from symptoms daily if they visit outpatient clinics in good condition and have trouble remembering the exact number and severity of symptoms. This application includes a function to record symptoms each time they occur and provides solutions to alleviate them. As shown in Table 5, 89.74% of the patients recorded 239 uncomfortable symptoms during the first 1–2 weeks after discharge, with gradual improvement over time. We found that the patients experienced far more uncomfortable symptoms at home alone after discharge than when they visited the outpatient clinic. More than 80% of patients responded negatively to the questionnaire question, “I practiced the relief guide for uncomfortable symptoms after eating, but it was not helpful (Supplementary Table 1),” indicating that the app may help with home care after discharge.

As a supplementary analysis (Supplementary Tables 5, 6, and 7), the change in QoL after gastrectomy was examined. As demonstrated in other research findings [29,30,31,32], our study found that functional parameters generally improved and symptoms decreased 10 weeks after gastric cancer surgery relative to baseline when measured using the EORTC QLQ-C30, QLQ-STO22, and KOQUSS questionnaires. While other studies typically measured preoperative baseline QoL, our study measured postoperative (before discharge) baseline QoL, resulting in a generally lower baseline QoL results than other studies. Similar to other studies that found that QoL generally recovered to preoperative levels 3 months after surgery, our study found that QoL, which dropped immediately after surgery, almost recovered after 10 weeks. Although our study demonstrated favorable outcomes in terms of weight change and QoL, since it was a single-arm pilot study without a direct comparison group, whether these positive outcomes can be directly attributed to the use of the app remains unclear. Thus, future clinical trials with appropriate control groups are necessary to assess the effectiveness of the app rigorously.

Active encouragement from patients’ medical teams may increase compliance with app usage. Thus, this application could be a feasible tool for use in real-world clinical practice for postoperative rehabilitation. In conclusion, digital therapeutics may be a feasible way to provide supportive care for postgastrectomy patients, especially in terms of nutritional intake and postoperative monitoring.
