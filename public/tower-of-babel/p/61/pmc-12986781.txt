Tumor characteristics impact prognosis in deficient mismatch repair/microsatellite instability-high localized colorectal cancer—a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ida Kolukisa Saqi, Amalie Thomsen Nielsen, Michael Tvilling Madsen, Ismail Gögenur, Adile Orhan, Tobias Freyberg Justesen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeficient mismatch repair (dMMR) and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors constitute ∼15% of localized colorectal cancers (CRCs). Prognostic biomarkers such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and BRAF and KRAS mutations may guide personalized treatment for these patients, and this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate their impact on survival outcomes.MethodsLiterature searches were conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, including studies published between 2004 and 2023. The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and cancer-specific survival. The risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and the certainty of evidence using the GRADE approach.ResultsThe literature search yielded 5636 articles. Fifty-four studies were included in the systematic review and 31 studies in the meta-analysis, totaling 4551 patients. High TIL density was significantly associated with improved OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.17 to 0.89) and DFS (HR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.29 to 0.71). BRAF and KRAS mutations were seen in 52% and 34% of patients, respectively, and were associated with poorer OS (HR = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.13 to 1.80 and HR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.09 to 1.54, respectively). Quality of evidence was moderate to high across all exposures and outcomes.ConclusionHigh infiltration of TILs correlated with improved OS and DFS, whereas BRAF and KRAS mutations were associated with worse OS in patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC. These findings highlight the potential utility of biomarkers for improving prognostic assessment and personalizing management in dMMR CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide with nearly 2 million new patient cases reported in 2022,1 and the number is projected to rise to 3.2 million by 2040.2 With the rising incidence of CRC and increased focus on immunotherapy, it is important to further understand the underlying factors influencing the prognosis in patients with CRC. Deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status are present in ∼10%-15% of patients diagnosed with localized CRC.3,4 The dMMR pathway results from the inactivation of DNA mismatch repair genes, which normally maintain genomic stability by correcting DNA mismatch mutations.5,6 Sporadic dMMR tumors are most often caused by MLH1 promoter hypermethylation, resulting in epigenetic silencing of the gene. In contrast, hereditary dMMR cases arise predominantly from germline MLH1 or MSH2 mutations, which represent the main cause of Lynch syndrome, accounting for ∼15%-20% of all dMMR CRCs.7-9 Compared with proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) or microsatellite stable tumors, localized dMMR/MSI-H tumors show marginal differences in prognosis,4 reduced response to chemotherapy,10-12 and superior response to immunotherapy.13,14 Deficient mismatch repair/microsatellite instability-high tumors exhibit a high mutational burden, resulting in high neoantigen expression and an increased density of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs).15 TILs represent the host immune response and consist of several subtypes with distinct roles. CD3+ cells reflect the total T-cell population, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells mediate direct tumor cell killing, and FOXP3+ regulatory T cells modulate immune suppression.16,17 Among these, CD3+ and CD8+ T cells are particularly elevated in dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancers, reflecting pronounced immune activation within the tumor microenvironment and high densities of subsets have consistently been associated with favorable prognosis, whereas elevated FOXP3+ T-cell infiltration may indicate an immunosuppressive milieu and poorer clinical outcome.18-20

Several genetic factors are known to influence CRC prognosis. v-Raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1 V600E (BRAFV600E) mutations, present in ∼40% of dMMR/MSI-H cases, and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations, found in 6%-30% of MSI-H cases, highlight key genetic markers in CRC.21 Mutations in the BRAF proto-oncogene, particularly the V600E mutation, are associated with poor prognosis in both pMMR and dMMR CRC.22 Similarly, KRAS mutations are implicated in the pathogenesis of several cancers, including CRC, and are correlated with less favorable outcomes.23

Investigating the impact of TILs and tumor mutations could provide deeper insights into the prognosis of patients with dMMR/MSI-H CRC, potentially leading to more tailored treatment and follow-up strategies for this subgroup of patients. Whereas MMR/MSI status is recognized as a prognostic marker, its combined value with other prognostic markers warrants further exploration. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the impact of TILs and tumor mutations on survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC.

Methods

Protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the EQUATOR Reporting Guidelines and the PRISMA-P guidelines24 (Table S1). The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024547582) and the Open Science Framework (ID: be8wa) before data extraction.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search using 4 electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library. Additionally, free-text keywords controlled vocabulary terms were used to ensure a broad and systematic retrieval of studies. The search strategy adhered to the Population-Exposure-Comparison-Outcome (PECO) framework. The detailed search string, visualized according to the PECO framework, is available in Table S2.

Our population of interest was patients with Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) stages I-III dMMR/MSI-H CRC. The exposures of interest included TILs, BRAFV600, and KRAS. Comparators were defined based on the density of the markers (high vs low cell density) or the presence of tumor mutations (mutation vs wildtype). The outcomes of interest were OS, DFS, and CSS. The literature search was performed across all 4 databases on September 14, 2023, and studies published after this date were not included. No filters were applied to the search string during the initial search, and studies that did not meet the predefined criteria were manually excluded during the screening phase.

Study selection and data management

Following the searches, all identified articles were imported into Covidence for duplicate removal. Covidence was used to support and organize the screening process, ensuring a transparent and consistent workflow between reviewers. Two independent reviewers, I.K.S. and A.T.N., screened titles and abstracts for potential inclusion. Full-text screening was then done in the same manner. Discrepancies in inclusion decisions were resolved by a third reviewer (T.F.J. or A.O.). Data extraction was carried out by 1 reviewer (I.K.S.) and continuously double-checked by another (A.T.N.) to ensure accuracy and consistency. In cases of uncertainty or disagreement regarding the extracted data, consultation with a third reviewer (T.F.J. or A.O.) was undertaken to reach consensus. Data such as publication year, exposure groups, outcomes, population number, and UICC tumor stage were extracted and recorded in a standardized data extraction table. In case of missing data, the corresponding authors were contacted.

Eligibility criteria

Peer-reviewed cohort studies involving adult patients with stages I-III dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer that investigated the relationship between TILs, BRAF, and KRAS mutations, and OS, DFS, and CSS were included. Studies published in untranslatable languages and those involving patients who had received neoadjuvant treatment were excluded. No restrictions were applied with regard to publication date.

Prognostic outcomes

The primary outcomes were OS, DFS, and CSS. Overall survival was defined as the time from the diagnosis of CRC to death from any cause. Disease-free survival was defined as the time from completion of primary treatment of CRC to the first relapse or death. Relapse-free survival and disease-specific survival were assessed as DFS due to the similar definitions.25 Cancer-specific survival was defined as the time from diagnosis of CRC to death due to cancer. Definitions of all outcomes were based on the National Cancer Institute’s Dictionary of Cancer Terms.26

Subgroups

Subgroup analyses were prespecified in the review protocol and planned to be conducted where sufficient relevant data were available, focusing on TIL subtypes, UICC cancer stages, and cancer types.

Data extraction

Hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted from the included studies. For studies that only provided Kaplan-Meier plots without hazard ratios, hazard ratios were estimated by applying the methods described by Tierney et al. and using the WebPlotDigitizer software for extraction of data from the Kaplan-Meier plots when individual patient data were not available.27,28 Data extraction from Kaplan-Meier plots was conducted by 1 reviewer and subsequently verified by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy. The process was further overseen by a supervising reviewer to confirm consistency.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were conducted using R-studio using the Meta29 and Metafor30 packages. Dichotomous data, hazard ratios, and their corresponding 95% confidence interval were computed using a random-effects model.31 The Sidik-Jonkman estimator, with Hartung-Knapp adjustment,32 was employed to calculate tau2 to achieve robust analysis in case of large heterogeneity and/or small-study numbers. Prediction intervals were also computed to illustrate the anticipated range of effects in similar studies.33,34 Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using I2 statistics. Funnel plots were used to assess asymmetry, alongside Egger’s test, for each exposure and outcome.

Study quality and risk of bias assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for cohort studies was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies.35 Each study can achieve a maximum score of 9 points, indicating the highest quality and lowest risk of bias. Few studies were of poor quality and studies of poor quality were not excluded or down-weighted in the final analyses.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The evidence level was assessed using a modified GRADE approach tailored for observational studies, specifically designed for systematic reviews of prognostic markers.36 Within this framework, evidence derived from meta-analyses of cohort studies initially starts at a high level of quality and may be downgraded because of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, or publication bias, or upgraded in the presence of a large effect size or a clear dose-response relationship. The overall quality of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

Search results

The literature search initially yielded 8877 articles. Following screening, 54 studies met the criteria for inclusion in the systematic review.15,21,22,37-87 Thirty-one of the 54 studies were eligible and ultimately included in the meta-analysis.15,21,39,40,43,46-52,54-56,58,60,62,63,65,71-73,76,79,81-83,85-87 Several studies were excluded due to overlapping databases. To address this, each cohort was cross-checked individually. In cases where 2 or more articles reported on study populations that were partially or fully derived from the same underlying data sources, or where there was overlap in the time periods from which patient data were extracted, only 1 article was retained. The included article was the one reporting the largest number of patients. This approach was applied to avoid duplicate inclusion of the same study population. Other studies were excluded due to insufficient information about the study population or statistical methods, which limited the ability to assess study quality and extract data reliably. A detailed overview of the selection process can be viewed in the PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The studies included in the systematic review were conducted across the following regions: Europe (n = 29), North America (n = 12), Asia (n = 10), and Australia (n = 3). All included studies were retrospective cohort studies published between 2004 and September 14, 2023. Only patients with UICC stages I-III were included, and none had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. All studies reported on resected specimens. Deficient mismatch repair/microsatellite instability testing was performed using one of the following methods: immunohistochemistry (IHC) (n = 30),15,37,40,41,46,47,50,51,53-59,63-66,69,72-75,80-85 polymerase chain reaction (n = 17),21,22,38,39,42-45,49,52,60-62, 67,68,71,87 or polymerase chain reaction when IHC showed inconclusive results (n = 7).48,70,76-79,86 The majority of the studies determined TIL cutoff values based on the mean or median value of the examined cohorts, by either IHC (CD3/CD4/CD8/Fox P3) or by hematoxylin and eosin staining. The follow-up periods for the studies ranged from 2 to 10 years.

Among all studies identified, those eligible for inclusion in the systematic review comprised 18 studies assessing TILs densities,15,37,40,44,46,47,50,51,55,58,62-64,71,72,80-83 31 studies evaluating BRAF mutations BRAF mutations,21,22,39,41-43,45,48,49,52,54,56,57,59-61,65-68,70,73-79,82,84,87 and 15 examining KRAS mutations.21,48,49,59-61,66,70,74,76-79,85,86 Of those studies reporting on relevant exposures but not included in the quantitative synthesis, 2 studies investigated Immunoscore,38,69 1 study reported on HER2 mutation,53 and 1 study assessed macrophages (CD68+)46 (see Table S3 for results). No studies included data on POLE, POLD1, NTRK fusions, dendritic cells, MDSC, or natural killer cells. The studies eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis comprised a combined cohort of 4551 patients. Baseline characteristics of all included studies are detailed in Table 1.

Eight studies (n = 1472) assessed BRAF mutation (n = 763) vs BRAF wildtype (n = 709) in relation to OS21,39,48,56,60,65,76,79 (Table 2). The meta-analysis revealed that BRAF mutation was significantly associated with an unfavorable OS, showing no heterogeneity across studies (Figure 3).

KRAS

Six studies (n = 904) reported on KRAS mutation (n = 303) vs KRAS wildtype (n = 601) in relation to OS21,48,60,76,79,85 (Table 2). The meta-analysis indicated that KRAS mutation was significantly associated with worse OS, demonstrating no evidence of heterogeneity across the included studies (Figure 3).

Furthermore, the prediction interval (1.13-1.69) indicates that the effect size in a future comparable study is expected to lie within this range.

Disease-free survival

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

Ten studies (n = 1128) investigated high (n = 721) vs low (n = 407) TILs infiltration and DFS15,46,47,50,51,58,62,63,81 (Table 2). The meta-analysis revealed that high infiltration of TILs was significantly associated with better DFS, with I2 = 32% indicating moderate heterogeneity among studies (Figure 2, B).

BRAF

Eight studies (n = 1470) explored BRAF mutation (n = 770) vs BRAF wildtype (n = 700) and DFS39,49,54,60,65,76,79,82 (Table 2). The meta-analysis suggested a trend toward worse DFS for patients with BRAF mutations; however, it was not significant (Figure S1).

KRAS

Six studies (n = 1033) reported on DFS in KRAS-mutated (n = 332) vs KRAS wildtype (n = 701)49,60,76,79,85,86 dMMR CRC (Table 2). However, the meta-analysis did not show a significant association between KRAS mutations and DFS (Figure S1).

Cancer-specific survival

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

Three studies (n = 355) evaluated CSS in CRCs with high (n = 153) vs low (n = 202) infiltration of TILs40,55,71 (Table 2). The meta-analysis did not show CSS to be significantly associated with higher TIL infiltration (Figure 2, C).

BRAF

Five studies (n = 421) reported on BRAF mutation (n = 217) vs BRAF wildtype (n = 204) and CSS21,43,52,73,87 (Table 2). The meta-analysis revealed a nonsignificant trend for poorer CSS among CRCs harboring BRAF mutation (Figure S2).

KRAS

Only 1 study (n = 65) reported on KRAS mutation (n = 23) vs KRAS wildtype (n = 42) and CSS, precluding the meta-analysis.21 This study suggested that KRAS-mutated CRCs had worse CSS, though not statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses

Only subgroup analyses according to the UICC cancer stages were done, due to limited data. No significant associations between TILs and OS in the different cancer stages were found (Figure S3). A significant association between TILs and DFS was found in patients with cancer stage III disease (HR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.31 to 0.80) (Figure S4). These subgroup analyses were prespecified in the protocol but should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample sizes within each stage. There were no significant associations between BRAF and OS, DFS, and CSS (Figures S5-S7) and KRAS and OS and DFS (Figures S8 and S9) in the cancer stage subgroups, which may partly reflect limited statistical power.

Study quality and risk of bias

All studies underwent assessment for risk of bias using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (see Table S4 for overview of scores for all studies). All but 2 studies received a good rating, corresponding to a score of 6 or higher out of a possible 9 points. The remaining 2 studies scored 5 points, indicating a poor rating. The most common reasons for downgrading were insufficient information on cohort follow-up and lack of control for confounding variables.

Publication bias

None of the funnel plots indicated potential publication bias for any exposure or outcome, and all analyses showed P >.05, suggesting no statistically significant evidence of asymmetry. Overall, the findings indicate an absence of publication bias or small-study effects across the included analyses. However, these methods have limited reliability when based on a small number of studies, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Funnel plots can be viewed in Table S5.

Certainty in evidence

The cumulative evidence was assessed for quality using the modified GRADE approach across all studies (Table S5). High-quality assessment was observed for TILs in relation to OS and DFS, whereas the evidence was moderate for TILs and CSS. Similarly, high quality was determined for BRAF and OS, and moderate quality for BRAF in relation to DFS and CSS. Moderate quality rating was assigned for KRAS and OS and DFS. None of the evidence was rated as low or very low quality. However, subgroups rated with moderate quality were downgraded for imprecision due to insufficient cumulative patient populations. High-quality evidence can be interpreted with a high degree of confidence, whereas associations rated as moderate quality should be interpreted with some caution until further validation through additional research. However, as the GRADE assessment is normally conducted on meta-analyses on randomized controlled trials, a modified version of the GRADE approach was used in this meta-analysis on observational studies. The modified GRADE assessment should be interpreted with caution in comparison to the standard GRADE assessment on meta-analyses conducted on randomized controlled trials only.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, high TIL infiltration was significantly associated with ∼60% improvement in OS and ∼55% improvement in DFS, whereas BRAF and KRAS mutations were significantly associated with ∼40% and ∼30% reduction in OS, respectively. These findings highlight their potential as valuable prognostic biomarkers for patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer. These findings align with the existing literature indicating an adverse impact on survival outcomes in patients with BRAF or KRAS-mutated dMMR/MSI-H CRC,88,89 whereas high levels of TILs is associated with improved prognosis.90 To our knowledge, no previous studies focusing specifically on localized dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer have reported results that contradict the present findings. However, several studies have not found significant associations between the investigated prognostic biomarkers and survival outcomes within this patient group or have reported nonsignificant trends consistent with our results.15,19,39,51,54,65,79,85–87 Most prior research has been based on pooled cohorts that included both dMMR and pMMR tumors or did not stratify by mismatch repair status or tumor stage. By restricting the analysis exclusively to dMMR/MSI-H tumors, the present study provides a more refined assessment of the prognostic relevance of these biomarkers in this biologically and clinically distinct subset of colorectal cancer. Although we intended to investigate several biomarkers in relation to dMMR/MSI-H CRCs, our meta-analysis was limited to studies on TILs, KRAS, and BRAF for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The introduction of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in the treatment of dMMR/MSI-H CRC caused a paradigm shift in clinical oncology, dividing CRC into 2 distinctive subtypes based on MMR status. A meta-analysis examining the prognostic value of biomarkers such as TILs, BRAF, and KRAS solely in patients with dMMR/MSI-H CRC supports a more personalized treatment approach for this subgroup of patients.

Currently, dMMR/MSI-H serves as the best-established biomarker predicting response to immunotherapy in patients with CRC.91,92 Growing evidence shows that patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC achieve high rates of complete pathological response when treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy.13,93-95

Predicting the response to immunotherapy in localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC is crucial, as response rates range from ∼50% to 100%, which may inform the optimal duration of treatment.95,96 Studies on malignant melanoma and non-small cell lung carcinoma have shown that high densities of TILs are associated with response to immunotherapy.97,98 In pMMR localized colon cancer, tumor-infiltrating T cells co-expressing CD8 and PD-1 have been shown to predict response to immunotherapy.14 Our study showed that high levels of TILs correlated with better OS and DFS within a cohort comprising only patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC. Although TILs are not yet established as predictive biomarkers in CRC, accumulating evidence supports their potential role in guiding immunotherapy. The Immunoscore, which quantifies CD3+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration, has already been validated as a strong prognostic tool in localized CRC and may, with further prospective validation, help identify patients who are most likely to benefit from adjuvant immunotherapy.69 Thus, further research examining the correlation between TILs density and immunotherapy response could be of high clinical relevance. Future studies should aim to validate TILs as a prognostic biomarker through large, prospective studies that integrate standardized quantification methods and evaluate their added value in refining existing prognostic frameworks. BRAF mutations serve as an important biomarker, and BRAF-targeted therapies are approved for the treatment of malignant melanoma.99 In metastatic CRC, the prognostic and therapeutic implications of BRAF mutations are well established, and targeted therapy with encorafenib, binimetinib, and cetuximab has demonstrated significant clinical benefit.100 Translating these findings into localized disease will require prospective trials that evaluate survival outcomes and validate the clinical utility of BRAF status. Routine testing in localized CRC is currently not recommended,101,102 although our findings indicate that BRAF mutations are associated with poor prognosis and may predict inferior response to immunotherapy in localized dMMR/MSI-H colon cancer.93 Likewise, our findings show that KRAS mutations are associated with worse OS, but KRAS mutations could be associated with a superior response to immunotherapy in localized dMMR/MSI-H colon cancer.93 However, further research into the impact of KRAS mutations on response to immunotherapy is warranted before any conclusion can be drawn.

Although our study provides meaningful insights, it has limitations that should be addressed. A notable limitation is the relatively small-study populations included in some of the subgroup analyses, which may have constrained the statistical power. Further, TILs were determined using varying methods, which may have introduced variability in the data. Another limitation is that some included studies only provided Kaplan-Meier plots, meaning that only unadjusted results were available and that they had to be extracted using the methods described by Tierney et al,27 which are susceptible to imprecision. Furthermore, all included studies were retrospective, which inherently limits causal inference. Additionally, most studies originated from Europe and North America, which reduces the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations and health-care contexts. Finally, potential confounding cannot be excluded, as many studies did not adjust for clinicopathologic factors such as tumor stage, age, gender, comorbidities, or oncological treatment. Despite the limitations, our study has strengths that enhance the validity of our findings. Adherence to the PRISMA-P guidelines and the PECO framework, together with a broad and sensitive search strategy, minimized the risk of bias and improved reproducibility, thereby strengthening confidence in the results. Moreover, no exposure groups and outcomes achieved less than moderate quality ratings when evaluating the cumulative evidence and heterogeneity was low in the results on TILs and OS. Finally, the absence of publication bias across all exposure-outcome combinations, as demonstrated by funnel plot symmetry and non-significant Egger’s tests, reinforces the overall certainty ratings assigned in the GRADE assessment. This lack of bias enhances the reliability of the synthesized evidence and supports the robustness of the conclusions drawn in this review. These methodological safeguards minimize risk of bias and ensure reproducibility, strengthening confidence in the findings.

The distinct immunological and genetic landscape of dMMR/MSI-H CRC, including high densities of TILs and BRAF and KRAS mutations, has significant prognostic and therapeutic implications. When these tumor intrinsic features are identified at an early disease stage and interpreted alongside established clinical factors such as UICC TNM staging, age, and patient performance status, they guide individualized treatment strategies. For example, our data suggest that patients with a high level of TILs may have a lower indication for adjuvant chemotherapy. This integrative approach not only supports improved risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making but also highlights the relevance of precision oncology, where molecular and clinical markers are combined to tailor interventions, follow-up regimens, and optimize outcomes for patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC. Thus, further research in randomized clinical settings are needed to investigate the prospective use of TILs, BRAF, and KRAS in guiding personalized treatments and follow-up regimens.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that high infiltration of TILs was associated with improved OS and DFS, whereas BRAF and KRAS mutations correlated with poorer OS in patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H CRC.

These findings suggest that immune and molecular biomarkers hold promise for refining risk stratification and guiding more individualized management approaches. In particular, such markers could help identify patients who may derive greater benefit from immunotherapy or intensified follow-up, and those for whom adjuvant chemotherapy may be reconsidered.

However, interpretation should take into account the methodological variability in TIL assessment, the relatively small-study populations and retrospective design of the included studies. Moving forward, validation through prospective trials will be essential to confirm these associations and to integrate these biomarkers into precision oncology frameworks aimed at optimizing outcomes for patients with dMMR/MSI-H CRC in the future.
