Deep learning-based in silico labeling for analyzing morphological features of MSCs to predict immunomodulatory capacity
Authors: Zhiyu Liu, Gang An, Xiao Liang, Xumin Wu, Junyuan Hu, Haijun Wang, Jingfeng Ou, Xiuping Zeng, Zhiliang Xia, Kaixiang Hou, Wanglong Chu, Jianbin Ye, Cui Liao, Zhengmian Zhang, Muyun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCellular morphology, a critical manifestation of biological characteristics, is linked to functions. In traditional cell detection, invasive labeling and detection methods not only compromise cellular viability but also entail labor-intensive workflows. Here we presented a non-invasive artificial intelligence framework that integrated deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) to predict the immunomodulatory capacity of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) through morphological profiling. The improved PreAct-ResNet50 encoder-decoder architecture was used to achieve high-accuracy instance segmentation of cells and nuclei, enabling quantification of morphological features. A LightGBM-based predictive model was subsequently employed to predict MSCs immunomodulatory biomarkers through morphological features. This dual-model system demonstrated satisfactory cell segmentation and biological characteristics prediction capabilities through performance testing. Our method provided an efficient, non- invasive tool for real-time MSCs potency assessment, which could enhance quality controls in cell therapy manufacturing.Subject terms: Machine learning, Mesenchymal stem cells, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Image processing

Introduction

Conventional brightfield microscopy, while extensively utilized in cellular observation, exhibits inherent limitations in resolving subcellular components, such as nuclei, due to constraints in contrast and resolution. Fluorescence labeling techniques offer enhanced capabilities for visualizing macromolecular structures, metabolites, and other subcellular elements. However, this invasive approach imposes significant limitations, typically requiring cell fixation that compromises physiological viability, and can be time-consuming. Furthermore, practical image acquisition is substantially restricted by photobleaching effects and progressive cell lysis. Additionally, for cell images analysis, manual interpretation remains operator-dependent with significant subjectivity and poor reproducibility. Prior to the widespread adoption of DL, cellular image analysis predominantly relied on traditional computer vision techniques. Although can be computationally efficient, these methods critically depend on manual feature engineering and demonstrate limited adaptability to complex cellular architectures, particularly in scenarios involving overlapping cells or low-contrast imaging1. The breakthrough performance of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in the 2012 ImageNet competition heralded the beginning of DL applications2. Subsequently, CNN-based architectures such as U-Net and Mask R-CNN have demonstrated exceptional advantages in medical image segmentation tasks.

MSCs exhibits significant therapeutic potential for immune-related disorders due to its unique immunomodulatory capacity, such as graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)3, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)4, and multiple sclerosis (MS)5. In 2006, the International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT) established standardized biological criteria for MSCs based on “invasive” techniques6. However, MSCs heterogeneity remains as a major challenge in clinical translation7. Therefore, to identify the functionally distinct MSCs subpopulations and establish classification systems are critical for optimizing therapeutic outcomes. Current strategies to address MSCs heterogeneity extend beyond conventional biotechnology approaches, such as RNA-seq-based prediction of differentiation potential8. Morphological characteristic is emerging as a complementary avenue for subpopulation identification and functional analysis. Morphological alterations in cells or organelles can reflect the regulatory effects of gene expression9, metabolic abnormalities10, or external stimuli (e.g., pharmacological agents11). Previous studies examined the morphology of MSCs through staining methods and qualitatively predicted the immunomodulatory capacity through cluster analysis12,13. But this is based on invasive image acquisition method and traditional computer vision technology. Kong et al. employed ML algorithms to predict adipogenic or osteogenic differentiation levels through MSCs morphological features obtained by a non-invasive method14. But it cannot quantitatively predict the differentiation process. He et al. developed a morphology-based DL framework for accurate detection of senescent cells in MSCs cultures15. DL-based cell segmentation methods have become relatively well-established16,17. However, most methods fall under semantic segmentation, which is not conducive to the morphological analysis of single-cells. Moreover, no study has utilized quantitative morphological features as input data to quantitatively predict the MSCs biological characteristics and immunomodulatory indicators. Notably, the implementation of innovative process monitoring and quality control methodologies within automated cell production systems has become imperative. To address the gap in non-invasive detection methods for the automated production of cell therapy drugs, we initiated this research.

In this study, to explore the relationship between the morphology and function of MSCs, we stimulated human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) with IFN-γ to induce changes in both morphology and function. Meanwhile, a comprehensive training database was established, which included brightfield and staining images and biological characteristic datasets of MSCs. DL and ML architectures were concurrently developed and optimized. For instance segmentation of MSCs brightfield images, a specially designed deep neural network-based encoder-decoder architecture enables the extraction of morphological features for each cell and nucleus. And the LightGBM algorithm, grounded in Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), was implemented to predict MSCs immunomodulatory capacity and related biological characteristics directly from brightfield image inputs. This integrated AI framework avoids analysis of stained cells and establishes a quantitative correlation between cellular morphology and biological functionality, providing valuable insights for cell therapy manufacturers and clinical institutions in therapeutic product optimization.

Results

MSCs image dataset collection and DL model training

To enable prediction of cell and nucleus contours from brightfield optical microscopy images, a training dataset was initially constructed for neural network optimization. This dataset primarily comprised paired brightfield and fluorescence images acquired from identical fields of view. The training set incorporated umbilical cord-derived MSCs from three donors, varying objective magnifications, diverse cell confluency, and phase contrast/non-phase contrast imaging modes (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Enhanced data diversity improved network compatibility for robust prediction across heterogeneous image parameters. After processing, the stained images serve as annotated training data. Pre-screening and standardization of image parameters were implemented to optimize network accuracy, though comprehensive data inclusion was maintained despite suboptimal predictive performance under certain parameter combinations. During acquisition, the microscope stage remained fixed in x-y coordinates while simultaneously capturing image sets containing brightfield, nucleus fluorescence, cytoplasmic fluorescence, and composite fluorescence channels (Fig. 1a), ensuring precise pixel registration between brightfield and fluorescence modalities. The out-of-focus cellular features were excluded from consideration, as z-stack analyses demonstrated minimal training value for defocused cell images in prior studies16.

Example images of unlabeled and labeled cells used for training and the DL workflow for network development.

a Training dataset: training images under different magnification objectives and in phase contrast/non-phase contrast mode, including images of brightfield, cell, nucleus, and a combination of the two. Ph means phase contrast. b Deep neural network-based encoding-decoding architecture with example segmentation process for brightfield cell images.

A total of 37,540 images were acquired using microscopy for model training. The DL model was iteratively trained to establish predictive correlations between brightfield and corresponding fluorescent images captured within identical fields of view. The model architecture incorporated a deep neural network-based encoder-decoder framework. The encoder component employed a modified PreAct-ResNet50 backbone, implementing convolutional neural networks with residual connections to perform hierarchical downsampling while extracting robust representative features. The decoder utilized a unified upsampling architecture comprising two specialized branches: the Nuclear Pixel (NP) branch and the Horizontal-Vertical Distance (HoVer) branch. The NP branch generated binary masks to discriminate cellular regions from background, providing preliminary segmentation information. The HoVer branch concurrently predicted horizontal and vertical displacement vectors relative to cellular centroids, enabling precise separation of adherent or overlapping cells. This dual-branch framework executed a two-phase process: initial region delineation followed by boundary refinement, thereby achieving precise instance segmentation (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1b).

DL model testing and image quality optimization

A novel test dataset was collected to evaluate the performance of the model (Supplementary Figs. 2-3). Gound truth was generated through CellProfiler assisted segmentation of fluorescently stained images followed by Python-based processing. The trained neural network was implemented to predict cellular and nuclear boundaries in brightfield images. Error maps were generated by comparing ground truth and model predictions. Different colors were used to visually encode distinct error categories through chromatic differentiation in error map. Magnified regions revealed significant segmentation challenges posed by adherent or mitotic cells (Fig. 2a). To quantitatively evaluate the segmentation model’s performance, three segmentation metrics were employed: Dice for semantic segmentation, aggregated jaccard index (AJI) for instance segmentation, and panoptic quality (PQ) for holistic assessment. Additionally, Cell confluency might influence prediction error rates due to cell contact and mitotic during MSCs growth. Even higher confluency substantially elevated segmentation complexity. Therefore, we quantitatively assessed four types of errors16: 1) Split: single ground-truth cell/nucleus erroneously divided into multiple predictions. 2) Merge: multiple true cell/nucleus incorrectly merged into one instance. 3) Add: spurious detection of non-existent cellular structures. 4) Miss: failure to detect valid cell/ nucleus regions (Fig. 2b).

The significance of differences between confluency groups (n = 10 independent measurements for each group) were assessed using two-way ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons.

The analysis revealed distinct error patterns between cell and nucleus predictions (Fig. 2c-d): cell segmentation errors predominantly manifested as add and split errors, while nucleus predictions primarily exhibited as add errors. Split errors occurred frequently at intercellular contact regions, explaining their scarcity in nucleus segmentation. Contrary to initial expectations, error ratios did not demonstrate positive correlation with the increased confluency. On the contrary, the add ratio and miss ratio of the nucleus showed a decreasing trend. Quantitative evaluation demonstrated superior performance in semantic segmentation, with cell dice metrics achieving 90% of accuracy (92.28% ± 0.65%), and nucleus dice metrics achieving 80% of accuracy (81.47% ± 0.61%), indicating robust cell-background discrimination suitable for confluency estimation. Cell instance segmentation metrics (AJI and PQ) could achieve over 67% of accuracy (AJI: 68.88% ± 3.32%, PQ: 67.84% ± 3.01%), confirming competent single-cell delineation. Notably, cell segmentation accuracy decreased with rising confluency, whereas nucleus prediction accuracy exhibited positive correlation. This divergence suggested that while increased cell contacts challenged cell instance segmentation, concurrent reductions in nucleus error ratios enhanced nucleus segmentation precision. In addition, the statistical results of the test data of different objective magnification and the phase contrast mode showed that the error ratio of the cell segmentation in the 10× objective and phase contrast mode was lower than the other groups (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The low resolution of the image in the 4× objective and the low contrast of the image in the non-phase contrast mode showed little information about the details of the cells, therefore, the nucleus prediction results were not good, and the predicted cell add ratio and miss ratio were relatively high, and nucleus were also difficult to predict, this result is expected. The 20× objective images used for training are less, so the error categories mainly lie in split, add and miss. On the other hand, the accuracy of predicting cells for images in 10× objective and phase contrast mode are higher than other groups, paralleling its error ratio advantages (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The accuracy of nucleus prediction was also difficult to achieve at 4× objective and in non-phase contrast mode (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Additionally, we observed an interesting phenomenon: under phase contrast mode, the cell segmentation performance for 10× images are superior to that for 20× images (Supplementary Fig. 4a, c), but the nucleus segmentation performance is worse than that of 20× images (Supplementary Fig. 4b, d). We analyzed that the significant size difference between cells and cell nuclei may have influenced the analysis of the segmentation model. The cell segmentation accuracy decreased as confluency increased (Fig. 2e), while the nucleus segmentation accuracy at 10% confluency were lower than other confluency (Fig. 2f). To balance the accuracy of cell and nucleus segmentation while minimizing the impact of error ratios, the image parameters of 10× objective, phase contrast mode, and 25% ± 10% cell confluency were used in subsequent experiments.

MSCs morphological features extraction

Standardized imaging parameters (10× objective magnification, phase contrast, 25 ± 10% confluency) were collected for systematic morphological features of each cell and nucleus across all acquired images. Brightfield images were analyzed using the segmentation model to delineate cell and nucleus boundaries, followed by application of python math module for quantitative feature extraction. Fluorescence-derived ground truth annotations, generated through standardized staining protocols and image pre-processing pipelines (Fig. 3a), underwent identical morphometric analysis. Comparative evaluations of 23 cell and nucleus morphological features between predicted and ground truth datasets demonstrated minimal quantitative discrepancies (Fig. 3b-m). While systematic variations were observed across all measured features, the model outputs exhibited minimal deviations, confirming robust segmentation performance. Notably, ground truth reliability was subject to technical constraints including staining process variability and suboptimal exposure conditions. In particular, insufficient exposure duration resulted in attenuated edge fluorescence, introducing measurable discrepancies between fluorescence-based and brightfield-derived morphometrics. While stringent protocol standardization minimized operator-induced variance, methodological limitations persisted, reflecting inherent challenges in biological image analysis.

The changes of MSCs morphology with IFN-γ exposure

To investigate IFN-γ-induced morphological changes in MSCs, three MSC lines were treated with recombinant human IFN-γ at concentrations of 0, 10, 25, and 50 ng/mL for 24 hours, followed by paired brightfield and fluorescence imaging. MSCs morphology in the treatment group became elongated compared to the control group (Fig. 4a). The DL segmentation pipeline generated 23 morphometric parameters for cell and nucleus features (Fig. 4b-m). IFN-γ treatment significantly increased cellular major axis length, mean radius, and perimeter, while decreasing cellular compactness and solidity. Nucleus morphometrics exhibited increased area, compactness, major axis length, minor axis length, mean radius, perimeter and solidity, alongside decreased form factor, eccentricity, perimeter/area ratio, and aspect ratio. To eliminate donor effects, two additional MSC lines were analyzed (Supplementary Figs. 5-6), confirming similar dose-responsive changes: cellular major axis length and perimeter increased; nucleus area, major axis length, minor axis length, mean radius, perimeter and solidity increased, while perimeter/area ratio decreased. To determine whether there is a linear relationship between morphological features and IFN-γ concentration, we performed a linear regression analysis of 23 morphological features and IFN-γ concentration of three MSC lines, and used the F-test to test whether the regression equation was statistically significant in general. The linear regression results further proved the above morphological and IFN-γ concentration correlation (Supplementary Table 1). We observed a significant increase in cell major axis length following IFN-γ stimulation through both qualitative and quantitative analyses. To ensure the reliability of DL model measurements, we measured cell major axis length using ImageJ and found no significant deviation from the DL model results (Supplementary Fig. 6m-o).

The changes of MSCs biological characteristics with IFN-γ exposure

The above results established that IFN-γ caused morphological changes in MSCs, and to find the relationship between morphological features and biological characteristics, biological tests of IFN-γ treated MSCs were performed. The MSCs were isolated and obtained. The characteristic results demonstrated that both IFN-γ untreated and treated MSCs possessed normal trilineage differentiation potentials (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 7a). Flow cytometric analysis showed over 95% expression of CD90, CD73, CD105, and CD29, with <2% positivity for CD34, CD19, CD45, and CD14 in control groups (Fig. 5b). Notably, IFN-γ stimulation significantly upregulated HLA-DR expression, with maximal positivity reaching 14.5% in treated groups (Fig. 5c), consistent with prior reports of MSCs immunomodulation under inflammatory conditions18–20.

Data were presented as means ± SD, indicated by error bars. The significance of differences between groups were assessed using one-way ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.

As a proinflammatory cytokine, IFN-γ is widely employed to augment the immunomodulatory capacity of MSCs21,22. MSCs were treated with IFN-γ (0, 10, 25, 50 ng/mL) for 24 hours followed by co-culture with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Functional assays demonstrated dose-dependent suppression of lymphocyte proliferation by IFN-γ treated MSCs (Fig. 5d). MSCs-mediated immunomodulation primarily involves regulation of T-cell subsets. Experimental findings confirmed that Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells were suppressed to varying degrees (Fig. 5e-f, Supplementary Fig. 7b), similar with previous studies23–25. Furthermore, it was found that MSCs treated with different concentrations of IFN-γ had different degrees of promotion effects on Treg cells (Supplementary Fig. 7c), similar with previous studies26,27. The immunomodulatory effects of MSCs were also reflected in the inhibition of TNF-α secretion by immune cells. A trend of increasing inhibition of TNF-α secretion by MSCs with increasing IFN-γ concentration was found by ELISA assay (Supplementary Fig. 7d).

Additionally, existing studies have identified soluble factor secretion such as human indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)28,29 and membrane protein CD5430,31 as critical mediators of hMSCs immunomodulation. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of IDO1 gene expression in IFN-γ treated MSCs across all concentrations (Fig. 5g). Flow cytometric quantification demonstrated concentration-dependent increases in IDO1⁺ MSCs populations post-treatment (Fig. 5h). ELISA assay further confirmed dose-responsive elevation of IDO1 protein levels (Fig. 5i), consistent with established IFN-γ-IDO1 regulatory mechanisms21,32,33. CD54 surface expression exhibited similar upregulation in IFN-γ treated groups, though inter-group comparisons showed no statistically significant differences (Fig. 5j). This CD54 induction pattern aligns with prior reports of IFN-γ-mediated immunomodulation in MSCs30,34.

Construction of LightGBM models and visual analysis of morphological-to-biological characteristics

In this investigative phase, a LightGBM model constructed on GBDT framework was utilized to analyze multivariate correlations between high-dimensional morphometric parameters and biological characteristics. To train the LightGBM model, three MSC lines were selected, and the morphological features, biological characteristics and immunomodulatory capacity indicators were collected through measurements as described above. ML models often function as black box, rendering their interpretation challenging. To identify the principal features influencing model outputs, SHAP was employed, a cooperative game theory-based approach that enhances model transparency by decomposing predictions into individual feature contributions. Using IDO1 analysis as an exemplar, the SHAP beeswarm plot visually demonstrated local feature contributions through scattered points, where red coloration indicated features elevating predictions and blue denoted those reducing them (Fig. 6a). For example, a high NC ratio value has a negative impact on prediction, while a high cell eccentricity value has a positive impact on prediction. Subsequent ranking of features by the mean absolute SHAP values across all instances revealed prediction impact magnitudes without indicating directional influence (Fig. 6b). Among the top 20 morphological features displayed, NC ratio (9.02), cell eccentricity (6.46), and nucleus eccentricity (2.46) emerged as the three most influential determinants for IDO1 prediction. To elucidate directional relationships between morphological features and biological outcomes, heatmaps were generated using mean SHAP values (Fig. 6c) to visualize positive/negative associations, while corresponding heatmaps of mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 6d) highlighted feature impact magnitudes. This dual visualization strategy enabled comprehensive interpretation of both the direction and relative importance of morphological feature contributions.

Performance evaluation of LightGBM model

To evaluate the practical predictive capability of the LightGBM model, three additional MSC lines were selected and treated with the above-mentioned IFN-γ concentrations. Twenty brightfield images per group were collected, and morphological features were extracted, which were subsequently used for LightGBM-based prediction of biological characteristics. In parallel, the actual biological features and immunomodulatory indicators were experimentally measured. MSCs surface markers (excluding HLA-DR) were excluded from final analyses due to their minimal response to IFN-γ treatment, as no morphological feature variations correlated with these markers. The mean absolute error (MAE) loss function was implemented to quantify discrepancies between predicted and experimentally measured values. Unlike conventional classification or regression evaluation metrics, MAE calculated the average of absolute differences between predictions and ground truth values, where lower MAE indicated superior predictive accuracy. Initial analysis of overall prediction errors across cell lines and IFN-γ concentrations revealed average MAE values of 5.21%, 5.34%, and 5.93% for the three MSC lines, respectively (Fig. 7a). Corresponding average MAE values across four IFN-γ concentrations were 4.06%, 5.96%, 5.23%, and 6.73% (Fig. 7b). Statistical analysis demonstrated no significant differences in overall prediction errors attributable to these two factors (p > 0.05). The results show that the third quartile for each group is below 10%, indicating minimal model sensitivity to cellular origin or cytokine concentration. Prediction errors across individual biological characteristics (Fig. 7c) identified CD54 + , IDO1 + , and PBMC inhibition rate as having higher average MAE values (11.08%, 8.61%, and 6.91%, respectively), while TNF-α inhibition rate and HLA-DR+ cells rate exhibited superior prediction accuracy with MAE values of 1.30% and 1.17%. A comprehensive heatmap visualization (Fig. 7d) illustrated MAE distributions across cell lines and concentrations for each biological characteristic. The heatmap confirmed consistent low MAE values for TNF-α inhibition rate and HLA-DR+ cells rate across all conditions, though no discernible patterns emerged regarding cell line-specific or concentration-dependent error variations.

a Prediction error of overall biological characteristics in different cell lines (n = 20). b Prediction error of overall biological characteristics in different IFN-γ concentrations (n = 20). c Prediction errors between different biological characteristics (n = 20). d Prediction errors of each biological characteristic with different cell line and different concentration, darker colors indicating greater errors (n = 20).

Validation of therapeutic efficacy in SLE mice

To validate the therapeutic efficacy of model-predicted high-quality MSCs in SLE mice, one MSC line was selected. IFN-γ pre-stimulated MSCs and untreated MSCs were analyzed by the dual-model to predict biological features associated with their immunomodulatory capacity. Prediction results indicated that the immunomodulatory capacity parameters of IFN-γ pre-stimulated MSCs were significantly enhanced, such as PBMCs inhibition rate (P = 0.0002), Th1 inhibition rate (P = 0.0004), and IDO1+ cells (P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 8). SLE mice subsequently received intravenous injections of either saline (control) or MSCs, with cell groups comprising untreated MSCs (MSC-0) and 50 ng/mL IFN-γ-treated MSCs (MSC-50). Following four treatments at weekly intervals, some results demonstrated disease improvement between the MSC-0 and MSC-50 groups. Compared with the MSC-0 group, the MSC-50 group significantly reduced the spleen coefficient by 30.1% (P = 0.0038) (Fig. 8b), anti-nuclear antibodies by 34.42% (P = 0.0292) (Fig. 8d), creatinine by 40.38% (P = 0.0030) (Fig. 8e), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) by 25.29% (P = 0.0060) (Fig. 8g), serum TNF-α levels by 64.51% (P = 0.0003) (Fig. 8h), and significantly increased blood Treg cell proportions by 74.01% (P = 0.0056) (Fig. 8j). Although some results did not show differences between the MSC-0 and MSC-50 groups, such as kidney coefficient (Fig. 8a), anti-dsDNA antibodies (Fig. 8c), urine total protein (TP) (Fig. 8f), serum IL-6 (Fig. 8h), histopathological scoring of kidney and spleen (Fig. 8k-m). Compared to the saline group both of two MSC groups demonstrated significant improvement in symptoms through inflammation levels reduction and immunomodulation. Collectively, these findings substantiate that MSCs with model-predicted superior immunomodulatory capacity effectively ameliorate SLE progression in mice.

Discussion

In this study, we presented a combined DL and ML strategy comprising a cell segmentation model and a biological characteristic prediction model. The cell segmentation model localizes and delineates cell/nucleus boundaries in unstained cell images, subsequently segmenting individual cells/nuclei and quantifying their morphological features. This non-invasive in silico labeling approach represented a significant advancement for studying cell morphology. Our study concentrated on cell and nucleus morphology, because subcellular organelles (e.g., mitochondria, exosomes, and endoplasmic reticulum) require super-resolution or electron microscopy for imaging at nanoscale resolution35–37, that conflicts with our non-invasive image acquisition method. For cell therapy manufacturers and clinical research institutions in the cell therapy field, current quality control protocols have employed qualitative morphological assessment, as morphology serves only as an indicator for evaluating cell growth and differentiation status. However, our model predicts immunomodulatory capacity by correlating MSCs morphological features and immunomodulatory indicators. The IFN-γ pre-stimulation approach enabled the model to distinguish high-score MSCs from low-score MSCs, meanwhile high-score MSCs demonstrated improved therapeutic effects in SLE mice.

We selected PreAct-ResNet50 as the backbone network due to its robust feature extraction capabilities and optimization for image features. Our task involves extracting multi-level features (from edges to overall structures) from complex microscopic images. PreAct-ResNet50 deep residual architecture effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks, making it highly suitable for this task. Compared to standard ResNet, PreAct-ResNet50 exhibits smoother gradient flow and improved training stability38. Prior to this, we attempted to use existing open-source image segmentation tools such as CellProfiler39 and Cellpose to accomplish our task. While CellProfiler excels at segmenting cells and nuclei in stained images and can also quantify morphological features of each cell, it cannot segment cells and nuclei in brightfield images. Additionally, Cellpose has a similar principle with our segmentation model, both employing the “HoVer-Net” algorithm. However, Cellpose is primarily designed for semantic segmentation scenarios. Functionally, it cannot segment nuclei in brightfield images, nor can it individually number cells and extract morphological features. It requires integration with ImageJ to analyze segmentation results. Therefore, our DL segmentation model integrates the core function of CellProfiler and Cellpose, enabling both cell and nucleus segmentation as well as morphological analysis in brightfield images. In ML models, we selected the LightGBM architecture for its high training efficiency and accuracy. The high-dimensional features extracted from DL models require establishing regression relationships with biological characteristics. When processing such data, LightGBM not only offers fast training speeds and low memory consumption, but its leaf-wise growth strategy also enhances accuracy and enables the evaluation of which morphological features contribute most significantly to the final decision40.

Certain limitations exist in the research, such as our exclusive focus on the effects of IFN-γ. Other cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β) have also been demonstrated to influence MSCs function. However, we did not investigate the morphological alterations induced by these additional cytokines. Future research comparing the individual and synergistic effects of these cytokines will be crucial for deepening understanding of the relationship between morphology and function. In addition, an imbalanced training dataset may lead to poor model generalization, in which 4× images, 20× images and non-phase contrast images were accounted for 0.98%, 3.87%, 7.94% respectively of the total training data (Supplementary Data 4). During cell culture, low-power microscopy is typically used to assess confluency, while high-power microscopy is employed to observe cell morphology. In non-phase contrast images, cells exhibit low contrast against the background. AI models struggle to extract key features from low-power and non-phase contrast images, whereas effective training can be achieved using 10× and 20× phase contrast images. Additionally, the BioTek Lionheart FX lacks a phase contrast mode at 20× magnification, presenting another limitation. Consequently, phase contrast images at 20× must be acquired manually using the IX73, which is an extremely time-consuming process. However, segmentation models demonstrate high performance under specific image parameters. In future research, we will prioritize expanding the low-proportion dataset and further optimize data balance through continuous data collection. Additionally, potential biases may exist in the annotation method for training images, as our image annotation approach utilizes segmentation results from CellProfiler. While this saves significant time compared to manual annotation and offers greater objectivity, its instance segmentation accuracy is influenced by multiple factors, such as exposure time of stained images and cell confluency.

From a manufacturing perspective, ensuring consistent high quality across every batch of MSCs products is critical to successful clinical translation. The image analysis pipeline we developed not only quantifies MSCs morphological features but, more importantly, holds the potential to be integrated into the manufacturing process as an objective, non-invasive “quality gate” for real-time identification and rejection of substandard MSCs. We will explore and establish threshold criteria distinguishing low-quality from high-quality MSCs. When analysis results exceed preset thresholds, the AI model indicates potential risks in the batch, prompting recommendations for further testing (e.g., flow cytometry, ELISA) or direct termination of the culture. This approach saves time and resources.

We are currently deploying the computational model into an online cell quality analysis software module, which will be seamlessly integrated into our proprietary Intelligent Manufacturing Platform of Fully-Enclosed Pipeline. This integration, combined with the platform’s automated microscopy capabilities, enables non-invasive real-time cellular monitoring and analytical processes throughout the manufacturing workflow. Moving forward, we will enhance model performance by expanding both size and diversity of the training datasets. Additionally, we plan to develop more comprehensive prediction models tailored for different cell types and cellular functions, aiming to provide more intelligent and non-invasive auxiliary detection strategies for the field of cell therapy.

Methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs were provided by Shenzhen Beike Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Ethical approval and informed consent for this study were obtained from Safety and Ethics Committee at Shenzhen Beike Biotechnology Co., Ltd (approval number: BK-SL-20240314-01). According to the manufacturer, the MSCs were isolated and cultured from the Wharton’s jelly of umbilical cords of maternal volunteers. Six MSC lines were used in this study, which indicates the heterogeneity of MSCs. Three MSC lines (1481, 1482, 1483) were used for collecting training data, including images and biological characteristics, while the other three MSC lines (1484, 1485, 1486) were employed for model performance testing.

MSCs were cultured in OrbioX hMSCs Basal Medium (Harbin Beike) supplemented with OrbioX Supplement (Harbin Beike). The cells were seeded at a density of 3000 cells/cm2 in 6-well plates (Corning) and incubated with 5% CO2 at 37°C for 24 hours. Then MSCs were stimulated with IFN-γ (10 ng/mL, 25 ng/mL, 50 ng/mL) for 24 hours and subsequently stained to collect images. On the other hand, 4.5 × 105 cells were seeded into T75 flasks (Corning) containing 10 mL of MSCs growth medium and incubated with 5% CO2 at 37°C for 2 days. Then cells were collected for biological characteristics testing after 24 hours. of stimulation with different concentrations of IFN-γ.

DL segmentation model architecture and training strategy

The segmentation model adopts an encoder-decoder architecture based on a deep neural network. The encoder, inspired by Preact-ResNet50, modifies the standard ResNet50 by reducing the downsampling factor from 32× to 8×. This is achieved by setting the stride to 1 in the first convolutional layer and removing the subsequent max pooling layer, thereby preserving fine spatial details critical for segmentation tasks. The encoder consists of an initial convolutional layer (a 7×7 convolution) followed by four stages of residual blocks. The number of residual units in each stage is 3, 4, 6, and 3, with corresponding feature map channels of 256, 512, 1024, and 2048, respectively. Each residual unit employs a pre-activation design, which includes batch normalization, ReLU activation, and sequential 1×1 and 3×3 convolutional layers, along with skip connections.

The decoder branch begins from the final feature map of the encoder and diverges into two parallel branches. The general decoder structure comprises upsampling layers, dense decoder units, skip connections, and a final convolutional layer. The first branch, the cell/nucleus pixel branch, performs binary segmentation (cell/nucleus vs. background), producing an 80×80 output map. After applying the Softmax function, this map represents the probability distribution across two classes. The second branch, the HoVer branch, regresses the horizontal and vertical distances to the nuclear centroid, generating two 80×80 maps. Through the synergistic operation of the NP branch and the HoVer branch, the model effectively distinguishes between touching or overlapping cells, a challenge traditionally difficult to address with conventional methods.

The training of the segmentation model is conducted in two phases. In the first phase, pre-trained weights are utilized, with partial layers of the network frozen. In the second phase, all layers are unfrozen, and training continues from the first phase. Each phase comprises 50 epochs. The batch size is set to 16 per GPU in the first phase and reduced to 4 per GPU in the second phase. The Adam optimizer is employed with an initial learning rate of 1.0e-4. A StepLR scheduler is used to adjust the learning rate every 25 epochs. The model is implemented using the PyTorch framework and trained on a computational environment equipped with two NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPUs.

LightGBM model

The prediction model is based on quantitative morphological features, extracting 11 cellular and 12 nuclear morphological features (Fig. 3). The model predicts 17 biological characteristics spanning multiple dimensions of immunomodulation and cellular phenotype (Fig. 6c, d). Each biological characteristic is modeled as an independent regression task.

Data preprocessing involved the following key steps: First, the feature matrix and target variables were extracted from experimental data in Excel format. Second, target variables in percentage format were standardized by removing the percentage symbols and converting them to floating-point numbers within the 0–1 range. Finally, the dataset was divided into training (80%) and test (20%) sets using stratified random splitting.

A systematic hyperparameter tuning strategy was employed. The baseline model configuration included: number of trees (n_estimators=200), learning rate (learning_rate=0.05), and a fixed random seed (random_state=42). To further optimize model performance, a Grid Search method was implemented. Key tuned hyperparameters included: number of trees (100, 200, 500), learning rate (0.01, 0.05, 0.1), number of leaves (31, 63, 127), maximum depth (-1, 5, 10), minimum child samples (20, 50, 100), row sampling ratio (0.8, 1.0), and feature sampling ratio (0.8, 1.0). The optimal parameter combination was selected via k-fold cross-validation.

To prevent overfitting and optimize training efficiency, an early stopping mechanism based on validation set performance was adopted. The model training process continuously monitored the Mean Squared Error (MSE) and MAE on the test set. Training was automatically terminated if no significant improvement in validation loss was observed over 20 consecutive epochs. This strategy ensured model generalizability while significantly reducing unnecessary computational overhead.

The GBDT model was implemented using the LightGBM framework with the following key configurations: the histogram algorithm was used to accelerate the search for feature split points; a leaf-wise growth strategy was set to optimize model accuracy; and Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) was applied to improve training efficiency. For the multi-target prediction task, dedicated regression models were independently trained for each biological indicator. Model evaluation primarily used MSE and MAE as the performance metric, supplemented with SHAP analysis for interpretability studies to quantify the contribution of each morphological feature to the prediction outcomes.

Cell staining

MSCs was inoculated in a 6-well plate at a density of 2500 cells per well and cultured in growth medium for 24 hours. Growth medium containing 0, 10, 25 or 50 ng/mL IFN-γ (T&L Biotechnology) was replaced and cultured for another 24 hours, then fixed with 4% (wt/vol) PFA for 15 min and permeated with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime) for 15 min. Cellular and nuclear morphology were evaluated using HCS CellMask (Thermo Fisher) and DAPI, respectively. In brief, the fixed sample was incubated with 2 μg/mL HCS CellMask at room temperature for 30 min, washed with PBS, incubated with DAPI for 5 min, and washed with PBS before imaging.

Cell images acquisition

The stained cells and live cell were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus) and automated microscope (BioTek Lionheart FX, Agilent) for training dataset. The training dataset contains a total of 37,540 images. Each set comprises four images (from the same field of view), including brightfield, cell staining, nucleus staining, and merge image. The 24 combinations of 4 image parameters, along with the number of images in each combination, are presented in Supplementary Data 4.

The live cells were imaged through Intelligent Manufacturing Platform of Fully-Enclosed Pipeline (Cellauto) for testing dataset. Just brightfield images were collected and then analyzed by the DL model.

Image preprocessing and annotation for training

First, we removed images that were severely damaged, excessively blurred to the extent that effective feature extraction was impossible.

During training data preprocessing for the cell instance segmentation task, we designed a systematic annotation generation pipeline to fully leverage the complementary information contained in brightfield and stained images acquired from the same field of view. The specific procedural steps are as follows: First, global cell masks were extracted from the stained images (including cell and nucleus staining), aiming to preliminarily distinguish cell regions from the background. Subsequently, we employed CellProfiler to perform cell instance segmentation on the stained images to accurately identify the contours of each individual cell instance. The resulting segmentation outputs were used to generate corresponding cell boundary masks. Next, an image subtraction operation was performed between the initial global cell masks and the cell boundary masks to obtain a binary image highlighting the cell boundaries. Based on this binary image, connected component analysis was further conducted to assign unique grayscale value labels to each independent cell instance in the image. Concurrently, non-cellular noise regions with excessively small areas or aberrant morphology were removed via area-based filtering and morphological operations. Finally, the preprocessed stained images served as high-quality training annotation data, where each cell instance was distinguished by a different grayscale value, thereby effectively supporting the training of the instance segmentation models. This method is uniformly applicable to both cell and nucleus stained images.

Morphological features extraction

Cell and nucleus morphological features were automatically quantified using the open access CellProfiler (V4.2.8) software to obtain a unique “morphological features “ for each cell, consisting of 11 cell morphological features and 12 nucleus morphological features. Formal definitions for each feature are acquired from Supplementary Data 1. The CellProfiler algorithms (pipeline) are acquired from Supplementary Data 2.

Measure the major axis length of cells in ImageJ (V1.54p). Use the “Analyze > Set Scale” function to establish the conversion between pixels and actual length units based on the scale bar in the stained image. Select the “Straight Line” tool from the toolbar and manually measure the major axis length of each cell.

The DL segmentation model incorporates python math module (V3.4) for automatically extracting morphological features of segmented cells and nuclei. Feature definitions and formulas align with CellProfiler standards, as documented in the available model code.

MSCs/PBMCs co-culture

The stimulated hUC-MSCs were harvested from culture flasks and add them to 12 well plates (Corning), in standard MSCs growth medium and allowed to adhere for 6 hours, with 1.2 × 105 cells per well. The PBMCs for proliferation detection were thawed and suspended in PBS and labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Beyotime). The CFSE-labeled PBMCs were stimulated with 7 ng/mL PHA (Beyotime) and cocultured with MSCs (5:1) at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a 12-well plate for 5 days. Following the incubation period, all lymphocytes were collected, and cell proliferation was determined by monitoring the gradual reduction in CFSE fluorescence by flow cytometry. PBMCs without PHA stimulation and coculturing were used as negative controls. PBMCs used for intracellular factor detection do not require labeling and are cocultured with MSCs (5:1) in a 37°C and 5% CO2 in a 12-well plate for 3 days. Proliferation inhibition rate (%) = [(A-B)/A] × 100%, where A is the proliferation rate of lymphocytes that were not co-cultured with MSCs (positive group), and B is the proliferation rate of lymphocytes that were co-cultured with MSCs (experimental group).

MSCs differentiation potential evaluation

MSCs differentiation potential was assessed using the OriCell® osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation Kit (Cyagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, for osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation, the cells were cultured in a 6-well plate at 37°C with 5% CO2 until reaching approximately 70-100% confluency. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with osteogenic or adipogenic differentiation medium and exchanged every 3 days. After 2 to 4 weeks of incubation, the cells were stained with Alizarin Red S solution or Oil Red O solution to evaluate osteogenic or adipogenic differentiation, respectively. For chondrogenic differentiation, (3–4) × 105 cells were used to differentiate into chondrogenic cells for a period of 3 to 4 weeks, which were then stained with Alcian Blue solution.

Then cells were washed twice with staining buffer (1×PBS with 2% FBS), stained with antibody CD45 and incubated in dark for 30 minutes at 4°C. Finally cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cells were washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in an appropriate volume of staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cells were then washed twice with Perm/Wash™ Buffer. Finally, cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Then cells were washed twice with staining buffer, resuspended in an appropriate volume of staining buffer with antibodies (CD4, CD25) and incubated in dark for 30 minutes at 4°C. Cells were added 1×Fixation/Permeabilization solution after twice wash, and incubated in dark for 30 minutes at 4°C. Then cells were washed twice with Perm/Wash™ Buffer and resuspended in an appropriate volume of Perm/Wash™ Buffer with antibody Foxp3, then incubated in dark for 30 minutes at 4°C. Finally cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of Perm/Wash™ Buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Evaluation metrics for segmentation

We established a test dataset independent of the training dataset images. The combinations and the number of images are presented in Supplementary Data 4.

Split and miss error rates are detected using the ratio of the area intersection between predicted cell instances and true cell instances to the area of predicted cell instances. A split error occurs when the intersection ratio of multiple predicted cells to true cell instance exceeds 0.5. A miss error occurs when no such intersection exists.

Merge and add error rates are detected using the ratio of the area intersection between predicted cell instances and true cell instances to the area of true cell instances. A merge error occurs when a predicted cell instance has an intersection ratio greater than 0.5 with multiple true cells. An add error occurs when no such intersection exists.

For evaluation, we use semantic segmentation, instance segmentation and panoptic segmentation metrics, all commonly used for quantification of the performance in the field of image segmentation. Taken ground truth as standard, segmentation model not only needs to distinguish between object and background, but also segment each cell instance.

Dice is an evaluation metric for semantic segmentation, typically used to calculate the similarity between predicted and true values. Dice is defined as: Dice=2×(X∩Y)∣X∣+∣Y∣

Aggregated Jaccard Index (AJI) is a connectivity domain-based instance segmentation evaluation metric commonly used for cell segmentation. AJI is defined as: AJI=∑i=1N∣Gi∩PMi∣∑i=1N∣Gi∪PMi∣+∑F∈U∣PF∣.

Panoptic quality (PQ) is a metric for panoramic segmentation evaluation. The panoptic segmentation task includes semantic and instance segmentation, but introduces new algorithmic challenges. PQ is defined as: PQ=∣TP∣∣TP∣+12∣FP∣+12∣FN∣×∑(x,y)∈TPIoU(x,y)∣TP∣.

Animal experiments

All procedures performed on mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shenzhen Lingfu Topu Biotechnology Co., Ltd (License No.: SYXK (Guangdong) 2022-0293) (approval number TOPGM-IACUC-2024-0184). Mouse strain MRL/lpr was obtained from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd (License No.: SCXK (Beijing) 2024-0001). 54 9-10 weeks female mice were maintained at a SPF level environment for 7 days before research. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

54 mice were randomly divided into 3 groups and injected via tail vein with 200 µL saline, 1×106 untreated MSCs, and 1×106 IFN-γ treated MSCs, respectively. Four consecutive injections were given at 1-week intervals. All cells were tested for sterility and endotoxin before every injection. Samples were taken for testing within 7 days after the end of the last injection.

50 μL mice serum was collected to test BUN by Urea Nitrogen Assay Kit (Beyotime). 50 μL mice serum was collected to test creatinine by Amplex Red Creatinine Assay Kit (Beyotime). 100 μL mice urine for 24 hours was collected to test urine total protein by Protein Content Assay Kit (Solarbio). The operations were refer to the manufacturer’s instructions. All results were analyzed by Multiskan FC (Thermo fisher).

At the end of the experimental period, all mice were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation. The euthanasia procedure was performed in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Mice were placed in a dedicated induction chamber, and CO2 was introduced at a controlled fill rate (approximately 20-30% of the chamber volume per minute) to minimize distress. The flow was maintained for at least 2 minutes after respiratory arrest to ensure certainty of death. Death was confirmed by assessing respiratory arrest and the lack of a pedal withdrawal reflex.

Following euthanasia, the specific organs (spleen and kidneys) were immediately harvested, excised of any adherent connective tissue, rinsed in PBS to remove residual blood, and blotted dry. The wet weight of each organ was recorded using an analytical balance. Subsequently, the organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours for subsequent histopathological analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

The experiments were independently repeated at least 3 times. All the experimental data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10 (V10.4.1, San Diego, CA). For normally distributed data, statistical comparisons were conducted using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (more than two groups) or two-tailed P-values by paired t-test (two groups). Differences were considered statistically significant at p value < 0.05. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. F-test is used to test the overall significance of the regression model, when the probability p value is less than 0.05 then the model is significant.
