Large language models for interpretation of health checkup results
Authors: Jiwon You, Hangsik Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge language models (LLMs) show strong generalization, yet their ability to interpret structured medical data remains insufficiently studied. This work evaluated four LLMs—Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o, and LLaMA 3.1-70B—using comprehensive health checkup data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service. Multiple prompting strategies (few-shot, role-based, constraint-based, and Chain-of-Thought) were tested. Zero-shot accuracy averaged 0.69 (SD 0.06), increasing to 0.92 (0.06) with combined strategies and to 0.95 (0.07) with Chain-of-Thought. Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracies (≥ 0.98), while LLaMA 3.1-70B showed lower but improvable performance. Item-level analysis of 10,000 cases demonstrated near-perfect accuracy (0.99–1.00) for most biochemical markers, including glucose, cholesterol, triglycerides, and liver enzymes. In contrast, blood pressure showed lower accuracy (0.61–0.91), with age-related decline, likely due to the complexity of multi-categorical thresholds requiring integration of systolic and diastolic values. Subgroup analyses revealed model-specific biases: sex-related biases were observed in body mass index (Claude Sonnet 4) and urine protein, serum creatinine, and gamma-glutamyl transferase (LLaMA 3.1-70B), while age-related biases were identified in blood pressure (Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and hemoglobin (LLaMA 3.1-70B). Overall, advanced prompting markedly improved model performance, and top-tier LLMs demonstrated robust interpretive capability, while caution is needed for variables with complex clinical semantics such as blood pressure.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Health care

Introduction

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence have focused on the use of large language models (LLMs) across various domains, including healthcare, where their potential as data interpretation tools is actively being explored1–5. LLMs are trained on large-scale corpora, such as PubMed Central, MIMIC-III, CORD-19, and Wikipedia6,7, enabling them to learn medical terminology and contextual patterns and facilitate tasks such as summarization and record analysis in addition to providing clinical decision support. Research validating the performance of LLMs across various medical domains is actively underway, with some studies demonstrating expert-level capabilities in specific applications. Notably, GPT-4 (OpenAI, CA, USA) passed the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE)8,9, whereas Med-PaLM 2 (Google, CA, USA), which was fine-tuned on medical data, achieved an accuracy of up to 86.5% on the MedQA dataset, demonstrating performances approaching those of medical experts, both specialists and generalist physicians10. GPT-4 demonstrated a performance (69%) comparable to that of expert ophthalmologists (median 76%, range 64–90%), exceeding that of ophthalmology trainees (median 59%, range 57–63%), and markedly outperforming unspecialized junior doctors (median 43%, range 41–44%)11.

In studies assessing the feasibility of LLMs as clinical decision-support tools, GPT-4 demonstrated comparable or superior performance (F1-score ≥ 85%) to that of traditional machine learning-based disease classification models12. However, its performance varied depending on the data format and complexity. When applied to the American College of Gastroenterology board exam questions, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, CA, USA) achieved a maximum accuracy of 74.0% for text-based questions, whereas the performance for image-based questions decreased by up to 11.4%13. These findings highlight the potential of LLMs in medical data interpretation while acknowledging performance variability depending on the data type and reasoning requirements.

LLMs have greater potential for clinical interpretation in relatively simple tasks with large case volumes or those requiring frequent patient interactions regarding results. Interpretation of health checkup results involving standardized measurements such as blood pressure (BP), cholesterol levels, and other key health indicators requires both accuracy and context-sensitive reasoning. This straightforward process can harness the repetitive, large-scale processing capabilities of LLMs. Health checkups on a national scale generate enormous data, necessitating efficient, repetitive processing.

According to the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of South Korea, approximately 22 million individuals are eligible for health checkups annually, with over 75% undergoing examination14. Although current health checkup reports flag abnormal values and provide basic interpretations, patients continue to face significant challenges in interpreting laboratory test results. More than 60% of patients had difficulty in immediately understanding their health checkup results and attempted to interpret them through internet searches and similar sources, whereas approximately 10–17% directly contacted healthcare providers or scheduled a medical appointment15. LLMs have the potential to assist patients in interpreting medical data by generating patient-friendly explanations and contextual guidance that rule-based systems cannot provide. This capability positions LLMs as complementary tools that may enhance patient understanding and expand the accessibility to comprehensive health information.

This study examined the utility of LLMs in medical evaluations, specifically focusing on the interpretation by class of health checkup results by using large-scale health checkup data from the NHIS. Specifically, we evaluated four LLMs (Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o, and LLaMA 3.1-70B), without prior fine-tuning on specialized medical datasets, to assess their classification and diagnostic performance on health checkup results. Input prompts were designed to simulate real-world health checkup queries. This study further examined the variability across these models, the effect of prompt design, and the reproducibility of the results, providing insights into the performance of different models in health checkup result classification. It consistently achieved near-perfect classification across most items, including a perfect accuracy of 1.00 for fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), urine protein (UP), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and very high accuracy for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC; 0.99), hemoglobin (Hb; 0.98), and body mass index (BMI; > 0.97). Even in the more challenging analysis of the BP results, Claude Sonnet 4 (0.91) outperformed the other models outperformed the other models, followed closely by Gemini 2.5 Pro at 0.89. GPT-4o attained a mean accuracy of 0.98. It attained a perfect accuracy (1.00) for FBG, TC, TG, HDLC, UP, AST, and ALT, and maintained a strong performance for LDLC (0.98) and serum creatinine (SC; 0.98). Nevertheless, it showed a relatively lower performances for BP (0.88), indicating that BP assessment was the most challenging item for all models. By contrast, LLaMA 3.1-70B exhibited the lowest overall performance, with a mean accuracy of 0.85 (SD = 0.01). Although it performed well for certain items such as SC (0.97), AST (0.98), and ALT (0.97), it consistently underperformed for BMI (0.59), BP (0.61), and LDLC (0.66). Across all models, FBG, TC, TG, HDLC, SC, AST, ALT, and UP were classified with very high accuracies (0.97–1.00), demonstrating the relative simplicity of these classification tasks. By contrast, BP emerged as the most challenging item to assess for all models, with marked performance gaps between Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 pro, GPT-4o (0.88–0.91), and LLaMA 3.1-70B (0.61). Similarly, the assessments of TC, LDLC, Hb and gamma-glutamyl transferase (γ-GTP) revealed disparities, with Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o consistently achieving accuracies of > 0.95 whereas LLaMA lagged behind (< 0.88). Figure 2C presents the mean accuracy across all tasks for the four evaluated models.

Statistical comparisons using a binomial generalized linear model (GLM) and Cohen’s h revealed practical performance differences between the models for BMI, BP, FBG, TC, HDLC, LDLC, Hb, and γ-GTP. By contrast, no significant differences were observed for TG, UP, SC, AST, or ALT (Supplementary Table 9). Post-hoc pairwise proportion tests with Holm correction demonstrated no significant differences in performance between Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, or between Claude Sonnet 4 and GPT-4o, for any variable; Cohen’s kappa values (κ) ranged from 0.9 to 1.0, indicating a nearly perfect agreement. A similar pattern was observed between Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-4o. By contrast, LLaMA 3.1-70B exhibited a significant decline in performance when compared with those of the other models. Specifically, in comparison with Claude Sonnet 4, its performance was significantly lower for all variables except SC; in addition, its performance was inferior to that of Gemini 2.5 Pro for all variables except UP, SC, and AST, and inferior to that of GPT-4o for all variables except UP and SC. The detailed post-hoc results of all models for each test variable are provided in Supplementary Table 10.

Reproducibility of Evaluation Results

In three repeated tests conducted under identical conditions, all models demonstrated highly stable performances, with the SD of accuracy being 0 or below 0.01. Cochran’s Q test for reproducibility across 10,000 samples revealed no statistically significant or practically meaningful differences between the results of the repeated tests for Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o. The values of κ for Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 pro, and GPT-4o were 0.98–1.0, 0.98–1.0, and 0.85–1.0, respectively, indicating excellent agreement across the repeated tests. By contrast, LLaMA 3.1–70B exhibited a significant practical difference across the repetitions for the LDLC variable, with a lower reproducibility (κ: 0.42–0.95) when compared with those of the other models. The detailed results of Cochran’s Q test and the across-run kappa analysis are presented in Supplementary Tables 11 and 12, respectively.

Subgroup Analysis Results

In the subgroup analysis by sex, Claude Sonnet 4 showed a higher accuracy for males in the BMI assessment, suggesting a mild male-oriented bias in this variable. LLaMA 3.1–70B demonstrated higher accuracies for UP and SC for males, whereas the accuracy for γ-GTP was higher for females, reflecting inconsistent directional biases across the variables. By contrast, Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-4o showed no evidence of a sex-related bias across any test variable, indicating a stable performance regardless of sex.

In the subgroup analysis by age group, Claude Sonnet 4 showed a significant practical decline in the BP assessment accuracy with increasing age. This trend was also observed for Gemini 2.5 Pro, suggesting reduced generalizability for older populations. LLaMA 3.1–70B exhibited significant practical differences across the age groups for LDLC and Hb, implying age-dependent variability in the classification accuracy. However, GPT-4o showed no statistically or practically significant differences by age, maintaining a consistent performance across all age groups. The detailed results of the subgroup analyses based on age group and sex are presented in Supplementary Tables 7 and 8, respectively, along with their corresponding statistical assessments.

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of LLMs in interpreting health checkup data by designing prompts and utilizing the NHIS data in South Korea. The performances of four LLMs—Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o, and LLaMA 3.1–70B—were compared. The results revealed that although the LLMs showed different performances, they demonstrated an accuracy of 0.6–0.8 solely based on pre-trained knowledge. However, when appropriate prompts were employed, the accuracy improved to 0.9–1.0. Furthermore, the models exhibited good reproducibility, with an average SD of less than 0.01 across all models. The results for health checkup interpretation demonstrated relatively superior performance when compared with those of previous studies on the analysis of medical data using LLMs (e.g., 0.86 accuracy achieved by GPT-4 on USMLE question-solving10). Although the interpretation of health checkup results is slightly complex owing to the integration of multiple test items and reference values, it generally follows standardized clinical criteria. Therefore, it is relatively more straightforward than unstructured tasks such as disease prediction or diagnostic decision support. This characteristic likely explains why the LLMs demonstrated a more consistent and stable performance in this study.

Regardless of the prompt, the classification accuracy was the highest for Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, followed closely by that of GPT-4o, whereas it was the lowest for LLaMA 3.1–70B. The superior accuracy of Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-4o over that of LLaMA 3.1–70B likely stems from multiple factors. First, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o have been trained on large-scale, high-quality datasets, incorporating extensive domain-specific knowledge, including up-to-date medical information16–19. In contrast, LLaMA 3.1–70B, as a lightweight open-source model, was developed with comparatively limited training data and computational resources20. Second, differences in reinforcement learning methodologies may have contributed to the observed performance gap. Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o leverage reinforcement learning from human feedback to refine response precision and coherence, whereas LLaMA 3.1-70B relies on a simplified approach, such as direct preference optimization, which may have been trained with less extensive or lower-quality feedback data, potentially affecting its ability to generate medically accurate interpretations21. Third, disparities in computational resources and optimization strategies may have influenced the performance. Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o have undergone prolonged optimization with extensive computational infrastructure, enhancing their interpretative capabilities. In contrast, the lightweight design of LLaMA 3.1-70B may have constrained its performance improvements, particularly in complex medical interpretation tasks. Finally, given the rapid evolution of LLMs, the differences between the model release versions may also have affected the results: Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.5 Pro used in this study were the 2025 versions, whereas GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.1–70B were the 2024 versions. Such temporal disparities in model maturity can partially account for the observed performance differences.

The prompt design significantly influenced the model performance, with the application of the CoT prompt yielding the best results among the five tested prompt strategies. Notably, although role-based prompting led to a modest performance improvement in health checkup result interpretation across all LLMs (accuracy: 0.69 → 0.74), the addition of few-shot prompting (0.74 → 0.80) and constraint prompting (0.80 → 0.92) substantially increased the accuracy. Finally, applying CoT contributed to the achievement of the highest performance (0.95 on average, SD = 0.07), consistently improving the interpretation accuracy across all four models (Claude Sonnet 4: 0.99, Gemini 2.5 Pro: 0.99, GPT-4o: 0.98, LLaMA 3.1–70B: 0.84). This suggests that the interpretation of health checkup results is a task closely aligned with structured rule-based reasoning, where providing concrete examples based on real-world data and clear decision criteria could enhance the performance of the model. Moreover, explicitly guiding the reasoning process through CoT prompting likely improved the performance by allowing the LLMs to adopt consistent multistep evaluation standards, thereby compensating for variations in the pre-trained judgment criteria across the different models.

In the performance analysis according to the examined item, a relatively lower accuracy was observed in tasks requiring multiclass classification, such as BP and BMI, or those involving synthesis of multiple test values. This finding is consistent with well-documented evidence that the classification performance generally declines as the number of target classes increases, as the models must learn additional decision boundaries, resulting in narrower inter-class margins and a higher risk of misclassification22. Similarly, recent research on generative LLMs has reported that their performance decreases when moving from binary to multiclass classification tasks, as the increased number of categories demands finer semantic discrimination and enhances the likelihood of overlapping representations among the classes23.

A comparable trend was identified in our analysis: the LLMs maintained stable accuracies for the common categories, but their performances declined for underrepresented strata such as specific BP or BMI ranges. Collectively, these findings indicate that reduced multiclass performance is not unique to LLMs but reflects a broader limitation of classification models. From a clinical perspective, this suggests that LLMs may be less reliable for fine-grained stratification, underscoring the need for cautious application and future methodological refinement. Comprehensive analyses of both the examination items and models revealed that simple biochemical markers performed reasonably across all models, regardless of the model type. However, parameters such as BMI, which can be influenced by factors like sex and age24, or BP, which requires complex analysis, exhibited relatively lower performance. These findings suggest that the architecture and pretraining data of the models significantly influence the robustness of the interpretations, especially for parameters that require nuanced classification.

In the subgroup analysis, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and GPT-4o demonstrated greater robustness in clinical reasoning when compared with the open-weight model LLaMA 3.1-70B. This was possibly associated with their broader exposure to biomedical corpora and more extensive instruction tuning. These findings reinforce the importance of domain-specific alignment for achieving consistent interpretability and reasoning stability across structured clinical tasks. The observed decline in the model performance with increasing age may be partly attributable to biases in the training data distribution. BP rises progressively with age owing to arterial stiffening and vascular remodeling25. Therefore, the training dataset should ideally maintain a balanced joint distribution across the age groups and BP levels. However, if the model was trained on data that ensured a uniform distribution of the BP values without concurrently accounting for age, it may have inadvertently learned biased associations. Such an imbalance could limit the ability of the model to generalize physiological age-related trends and lead to systematic underperformance for older populations. Most models in this study showed no statistically significant sex-related deviations across most variables. Nevertheless, the sex-specific discrepancies observed in the BMI results of Claude Sonnet 4 and γ-GTP results of LLaMA 3.1–70 B suggest that LLMs may exhibit subtle gender-associated biases. In particular, the findings for γ-GTP—a variable with well-established sex-dependent reference ranges26—highlight the importance of constructing more refined and demographically stratified training datasets for variables inherently influenced by biological sex. These observations collectively highlight the need for context-aware evaluation frameworks and demographically balanced datasets that explicitly account for the sex and age composition to ensure fairness, reliability, and clinical validity in LLM-based medical applications.

This study has several limitations. As our method did not involve retraining or parameter adjustments, potential biases embedded in the underlying base models such as GPT remain unaddressed. Such intrinsic biases may affect the generalizability of the results and should be carefully considered when interpreting the results. The analyses were based on the South Korean national health screening program, which may also limit the generalizability. Health checkup items and reference ranges differ across countries, institutions, and populations, including variability by ethnicity, sex, and age27. Although subgroup analyses by sex and age provided preliminary evidence of adaptability, a broader validation across multiethnic and multilingual cohorts is needed to ensure equity. In addition, differences between the reference values used during model training and those applied in this study could have influenced the interpretation accuracy. In this study, prompting was conducted in Korean. However, the performance may vary for other languages. Furthermore, LLMs are inherently probabilistic and may introduce variability in tasks requiring deterministic reasoning, where rule-based methods achieve perfect accuracy. Finally, this study was limited to structured health checkup values without incorporating patient history or concomitant treatments, constraining the direct clinical applicability of the findings. Ethical and practical issues, such as privacy, interpretability, and patient safety, must also be addressed before the clinical use of the findings. Moreover, because the task was restricted to numerical classification rather than narrative reporting, hallucination risks were outside the scope of the analysis. The findings should be regarded as exploratory rather than be used as a substitute for physician judgment or comprehensive diagnosis.

Despite these limitations, this study highlights the complementary strengths of LLMs in health checkup interpretation. Rule-based algorithms remain indispensable for deterministic classification, but LLMs extend beyond categorical outputs by providing patient-friendly explanations that contextualize health information and offer actionable guidance. For example, rather than simply reporting that a fasting glucose level of 127 mg/dL meets the threshold for diabetes, an LLM can explain the clinical implication, suggest lifestyle modifications, and clarify the need for follow-up care. Such capabilities are especially relevant in large-scale programs, where interpretation places a substantial burden on healthcare providers. By improving patient comprehension and reducing repetitive explanatory tasks, LLMs can enhance both workflow efficiency and patient engagement. Moreover, when combined with retrieval-augmented generation, LLMs have the potential to deliver evidence-based explanations aligned with clinical guidelines, further strengthening their trustworthiness and transparency.

Importantly, despite being general-purpose rather than healthcare-specific models, the LLMs in this study achieved high accuracies (up to 0.99). Given that fine-tuning improves the model performance in diverse domains28, these results also suggest the potential for further improvements through domain-specific fine-tuning and prospective validation. In addition, the methodology is inherently adaptable: because it relies on prompt engineering rather than model retraining, population-specific reference ranges can be directly incorporated into the prompts. This flexibility enables application across diverse demographic and clinical contexts. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first systematic, multicenter evaluations of LLMs for structured health checkup interpretation, distinguishing it from prior single-institution or qualitative studies and offering a foundation for future translational research.

LLMs have been increasingly applied to medical data interpretation in diverse contexts. For instance, some prior studies29 have examined their ability to generate patient-friendly explanations of diagnostic test results, whereas others30 have explored their role in enhancing patient–clinician communication through the interpretation of pathology reports. By contrast, the current study evaluated whether LLMs can accurately classify structured health checkup parameters using established clinical reference ranges. This design positions LLMs as complementary tools that augment quantitative interpretation and automated classification, rather than as substitutes for existing diagnostic workflows, and provides a conceptual foundation for future extensions to patient-facing explanatory functions. Although patient-oriented report generation and comprehension assessment were beyond the scope of this study, future efforts toward automated patient communication systems will require rigorous user-centered evaluations to assess comprehension, trust, and clinical acceptability in real-world settings. Furthermore, the successful integration of LLMs into clinical workflows will necessitate robust safeguards and sustained human-in-the-loop oversight to ensure safe and responsible use. After the demonstration of long-term performance stability, a staged adoption pathway—beginning with internal validation, followed by supervised clinical integration—may facilitate a controlled and reliable progression toward routine clinical reporting.

Methods

Dataset

This study utilized the national health checkup data provided by the Korean NHIS31–33. The data were collected through standardized procedures at nationally designated medical institutions accredited by the NHIS. The data included basic physical measurements (height, weight, BMI, BP, etc.), blood tests (glucose, lipid profile, liver function, etc.), urine tests, and chest radiography. Each year, the NHIS publicly shares the anonymized data of 1,000,000 randomly selected individuals with a history of health checkups. The dataset included demographic information such as age and sex, along with the results of multiple clinical examinations. Variables not directly relevant to threshold-based clinical interpretation were excluded. These included ophthalmic examinations limited to simple visual acuity measures, hearing test results, waist circumference, lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption, and dental findings including missing teeth and tooth wear. This study used publicly available, fully de-identified data from the 2023 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), which is conducted under the National Health Promotion Act. According to the KNHANES 2022–2023 Data User Guide, the survey was approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Because this analysis used de-identified public-use data, additional institutional review was not required. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

We selected 16 clinically relevant variables for the analysis: BMI, BP, FBG, TC, TG, HDLC, LDLC, Hb, UP, SC, AST, ALT, γ-GTP. Only records without any missing values were selected for data preprocessing. We removed rows with missing values from the original dataset, yielding ~320,000 records. No normalization or unit conversion was applied. To ensure a representative sample, stratified sampling was applied, resulting in the selection of 10,000 cases, which corresponds to 1% of the 1,000,000 records, while preserving the original distribution. A Python-based script was used to analyze the distributions of sex, age, height, and weight in the population. The final sample distribution is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Establishment of Reference Ranges and Labeling Criteria

The reference ranges for interpreting the health checkup results were selected based on the guidelines from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency34 or Mayo Clinic35; specific examples are provided in Supplementary Table 1. For items where the reference ranges and thresholds vary by sex, separate reference ranges were established for males and females. Health checkup results can be categorized into multiple classes according to the corresponding reference ranges. For instance, BP is categorized as normal, elevated, pre-hypertension, stage 1 hypertension, stage 2 hypertension, and isolated systolic hypertension, whereas FBG is classified as normal, impaired fasting glucose, and diabetes. For each item, labels ranging from 0 to 5 were assigned according to the defined reference classes, although the number of classes for each item may vary. Some items were classified into two classes (e.g., 0, 1), whereas others had three or more classes (e.g., 0, 1, 2, 3, etc.). Furthermore, for items such as Hb and γ-GTP, where the reference ranges differ by sex, labels were assigned based on the sex-specific reference ranges.

LLMs

We employed four LLMs to evaluate the accuracy of health checkup result interpretation. The models were selected based on their demonstrated performance in established benchmark evaluations, such as Massive Multitask Language Understanding36. The most recent versions available through their respective application programming interfaces were utilized for the analysis—Claude Sonnet 4 (Anthropic, CA, USA)37, Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google, CA, USA)38, GPT-4o (OpenAI, CA, USA)16,19, and LLaMA 3.1-70B (Meta, CA, USA)39 (Table 3).

Zero-Shot Prompt

The zero-shot prompt method is designed to assess the zero-shot learning capabilities of the model using simple textual commands as inputs40,41. This approach was utilized to evaluate how accurately the LLM interprets health checkup data based solely on its pre-existing knowledge, without requiring additional context. For instance, a straightforward command such as “Interpret the numerical values and generate a report based on the provided data” was used to evaluate the performance of the model in data interpretation. Although this method minimizes the user input by eliminating the need for complex prompt designs, the absence of defined reference values or detailed guidelines may diminish the performance and lower the output consistency.

Zero-Shot Prompt + Role-Based Prompt (Persona)

This prompting method assigns a specific role (persona) to the model, prompting it to interpret the data from a focused perspective, rather than functioning solely as an information provider40–42. We designated the model as a “Health Checkup Interpreter.” This approach is expected to improve the consistency and reliability of the outputs, especially when handling complex medical data, by ensuring that the model adheres to domain-specific analytical frameworks. However, as a zero-shot approach, the performance of the model depends entirely on its pre-trained knowledge base. Therefore, excluding certain reference values during training might compromise its interpretative accuracy.

Few-Shot Prompt + Role-Based Prompt

Few-shot learning provides the model with real-world input–output examples, enabling it to identify and learn specific patterns40,41,43,44. This approach enhances the ability of the model to interpret the data according to the predefined criteria, improving the reliability and consistency of its output. We employed a role-based prompt incorporating a few-shot example to guide the model in interpreting the health checkup data like a medical professional, thus ensuring that the generated results reflect clinical reasoning. We structured the process of selection of few-shot examples by categorizing them into six representative groups rather than presenting arbitrary cases: (i) normal, (ii) normal (boundary value), (iii) abnormal (boundary value), (iv) abnormal, (v) mixed case 1, and (vi) mixed case 2. Here, a boundary value was consistently defined according to the change in the least significant digit of the measurement unit (e.g., ± 1 for a unit of 1, ± 0.1 for a unit of 0.1).

Few-Shot Prompt + Role-Based Prompt + Constraint Prompting

Constraint prompting enforces adherence to the predefined evaluation criteria, ensuring that the model interprets the data with a structured framework40,41,43–45. We provided specific evaluation thresholds for each health checkup item as inputs, thus establishing a reference point for the decision-making process of the model. Additionally, input–output examples were incorporated, allowing the model to utilize both medical knowledge and the provided patterns. By combining few-shot learning with expert role assignment and constraint prompting, we ensured that the model could effectively extrapolate from previously unseen data while maintaining a consistent and reliable interpretation of the health checkup results.

Few-Shot Prompt + Role-Based Prompt + Constraint Prompting + Chain-of-Thought Prompting

CoT prompting guides the model through an explicit multi-step reasoning process, ensuring that the interpretation of health checkup data follows a logically structured pathway. In this approach, prompts are designed to induce stepwise reasoning (e.g., Step 1: Verify measurement → Step 2: Compare with reference range → Step 3: Output the corresponding category), which provides transparent decision criteria while reducing the reliance on implicit judgment patterns derived from pretraining. By explicitly structuring the reasoning process, CoT prompting enabled a more reliable generalization of the models for unseen cases. Supplementary Table 2 presents the details of the prompts.

Examples of Few-Shot Prompting

In few-shot prompting, real-world examples are employed to assess their potential influence on the model outputs. In the few-shot experiments, we systematically defined the selection criteria for the examples to ensure reproducibility. A total of six representative scenarios were established to construct the support set: (1) normal, (2) normal (at threshold), (3) abnormal (at threshold), (4) abnormal, (5) mixed case 1, and (6) mixed case 2. Thresholds were defined as the minimum measurable variation in the last significant digit of the measurement unit (e.g., ± 1 when the unit is 1, ± 0.1 when the unit is 0.1). For each class, the support set consisted of six examples per scenario, balanced across categories to avoid overrepresentation. The query set was composed of independent test cases that were not included in the support set, with at least 100 queries per class to ensure statistical robustness. This design allowed us to evaluate whether the LLM could generalize across diverse but systematically defined case types, particularly at boundary values where interpretive ambiguity is the greatest. Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 present the detailed descriptions of the five examples used in this study.

Output Format

To ensure the consistency of the model outputs, the interpretations generated by all LLMs were standardized into a unified comma-separated value (CSV) format. Each row in the CSV file contains the data of an individual examinee, with each medical test result recorded in its respective column. Furthermore, the results produced by the models were translated into indices corresponding to predefined classes and systematically stored (Supplementary Table 1).

Performance Evaluation

The model performance was assessed using standard classification metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correctly classified samples among all predictions, whereas sensitivity and specificity measured the ability of the model to correctly identify positive and negative cases, respectively. The F1-score, calculated as the harmonic mean of the precision and recall, was used to balance the performance across the classes. To evaluate the reproducibility, we quantified the variability across runs as the SD of the per-run accuracy and computed Cohen’s κ to assess the agreement between repeated runs beyond the chance level.

Reproducibility Test

To assess the reproducibility of the LLM outputs, we conducted three separate runs using identical input data and prompts. In each run, we computed the classification accuracy for each health checkup item and calculated the mean accuracy and SD across the runs. Additionally, we examined the variability in the model outputs across different items to determine whether certain health checkup items exhibited systematic inconsistencies.

Subgroup Analysis

To assess potential training bias, we conducted subgroup analyses based on age and sex. Age was categorized according to the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) classification of the U.S. National Library of Medicine46 into four groups: 20–24, 25–44, 45–64, and ≥65 years. Sex was classified as male or female for a comparative evaluation.

Statistics

Differences in the overall accuracy across the four LLMs for each variable were assessed using a binomial GLM, followed by multiple comparison adjustment with the Holm method. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the pairwise proportions Z-test with Holm correction for multiple testing. For subgroup analyses by sex and age group, chi-square tests were used to determine whether significant differences in accuracy existed among the groups. Reproducibility across repeated trials for each variable was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test. Given the large sample size, even minimal differences could yield statistically significant p-values; therefore, the effect sizes were computed to complement the p-values and assess the practical significance.

Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s h for inter-model performance comparisons, Phi coefficients for binary subgroup analyses (e.g., sex), Cramér’s V for categorical variables with more than two classes (e.g., age groups), and w for Cochran’s Q tests. The thresholds for practical significance were defined as |h|≥ 0.2, ϕ ≥ 0.1, V ≥ 0.1 (small effect), and w ≥ 0.1.
