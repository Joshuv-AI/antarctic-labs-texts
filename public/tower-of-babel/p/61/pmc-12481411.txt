Ratio of haemorrhagic area to retinal area as a novel indicator for AI-based screening of diabetic retinopathy in type 2 diabetes: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Ronsong Sun, Tianlin Zhang, Su Zhao, Zongli Hu, Junhua Wang, Bo Jiang, Qiying Pan, Yi Yang, Yuangdong Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe application of artificial intelligence (AI) technology in the screening of diabetic retinopathy (DR) has made significant strides. However, there remains a lack of comprehensive validation and evaluation of AI-derived quantitative indicators in DR screening.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the diagnostic performance of retinal microvascular indicators in the early detection of DR in patients with type 2 diabetes and to identify potential novel indicators for early DR screening.Research design and methodsThis cross-sectional study included 533 community-recruited patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus who underwent fundus imaging. Based on the results of the fundus examination, the eyes were categorised into non-DR, mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), moderate NPDR and severe NPDR groups. AI systems were employed to quantify various retinal microvascular indicators, including microaneurysms (MAs), haemorrhage count (HC), haemorrhagic area (HA), the ratio of HA to retinal area (HA/RA), the ratio of HA to MA (HA/MA) and HC and/or MA (H/MA). Multivariable logistic regression was used to analyse the association between fundus indicators and DR severity, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive and screening value of these indicators, determining sensitivity, specificity, ROC area under the curve (AUC) and optimal cut-off values.ResultsAmong the 533 participants (mean age 64.03±9.71 years; 51.6% female), the DR prevalence was 10.0%. After adjusting for age, gender, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes duration, glycated haemoglobin levels, smoking and alcohol consumption, multivariable logistic regression indicated that HA/RA (OR 1.873, 95% CI 1.453 to 2.416) and HA/MA (OR 1.115, 95% CI 1.063 to 1.169) were associated with mild NPDR. Similarly, HA/RA (OR 1.928, 95% CI 1.509 to 2.464) and HA/MA (OR 1.165, 95% CI 1.112 to 1.220) were associated with moderate NPDR, and HA/RA (OR 2.435, 95% CI 1.921 to 3.086) and HA/MA (OR 1.171, 95% CI 1.117 to 1.226) were linked to severe NPDR. ROC curve analysis revealed that before adjustment, HA/RA demonstrated the highest screening value for DR, with an AUC of 0.917, sensitivity of 86.14%, specificity of 93.41%, Youden’s index of 0.796 and an optimal cut-off value of 0.063. After adjusting for confounding factors, the AUC for HA/RA in diagnosing DR was 0.900, with sensitivity of 83.17%, specificity of 86.28%, Youden’s index of 0.695 and an optimal cut-off value of 0.093.ConclusionsThe HA/RA and HA/MA show robust screening performance for early DR. These indicators should be considered for inclusion in AI-based early DR screening systems in the future.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common ocular complication in individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM),1 and a leading cause of blindness in the working-age population globally.2 It is estimated that the global prevalence of DR among diabetic patients was 22.27% in 2020, with approximately 103.1 million individuals suffering from severe visual impairment due to DR, a number projected to increase to 106.5 million by 2045.3 The onset of DR is insidious, and in its early stages, the disease is often asymptomatic. By the time patients experience noticeable vision loss and seek medical attention, DR is typically in its advanced stages, reducing the effectiveness of treatment and increasing the disability and mortality rates among diabetic individuals.4 Early detection and management of DR can prevent blindness in over 90% of patients.5

The classification and staging of DR are crucial for determining the rate of disease progression, identifying risk factors and selecting the appropriate timing for interventions.6 7 Currently, two main grading systems are used for DR staging: the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) severity scale8 and the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) severity scale.9 Both of these scales primarily rely on the clinical evaluation of visible retinal microvascular changes.10 Notably, these scales do not incorporate haemorrhagic area (HA) into their grading criteria, a limitation arising from the technical constraints at the time of their development, which made it difficult to manually quantify HAs. Additionally, since both scales lack objective, quantifiable measures, interobserver variability in scoring may occur, particularly among individuals with differing levels of experience and educational backgrounds.11 12

Previous studies have explored the use of new biomarkers, such as the triglyceride-glucose index,13 and combinations of triglyceride-glucose index, retinol-binding protein and β2-microglobulin for DR screening.14 However, these approaches exhibit relatively low sensitivity and fail to meet the high-sensitivity requirements for efficient screening. In recent years, the advancement of AI has attracted widespread attention for its application in DR screening due to its significant sensitivity and convenience. Some have proposed the use of AI to automatically detect microvascular changes associated with various severities of DR as a new tool for rapid DR diagnosis.11 Previously, Sadda et al identified the HA and the distance from the optic disc as novel risk parameters for the progression of DR to proliferative DR (PDR) in a prospective study that used manual measurement of HA and count.5 Similarly, Silva et al demonstrated that the haemorrhage and/or microaneurysms (H/MAs) ratio correlates with DR progression.15 However, due to technical limitations at the time, they were unable to conduct large-scale screening applications or compute ratios such as the ratio of HA to retinal area (HA/RA).

This study recruited individuals with type 2 diabetes from the community to undergo colour fundus photography for screening. Using a validated AI system, we quantified fundus abnormalities and evaluated the diagnostic performance of various retinal indicators for early DR via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Optimal cut-off values for each indicator were determined, providing novel reference information for the early diagnosis and screening of DR.

Method

Recruitment and study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February 2022 and September 2023, recruiting individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes by endocrinologists from four community health service centres located in Guiyang (Shijicheng, Beijing West Road, Tongxin West Road) and Liupanshui (Huangtupo), Guizhou Province. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 30 and 85 years; (2) meeting the WHO diagnostic criteria for type 2 diabetes established in 199916; (3) ability to communicate effectively and cooperate with the questionnaire survey and related examinations and (4) possession of clear consciousness and cognitive abilities. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of mental illness; (2) presence of other severe diseases, such as cancer; (3) intellectual disability; (4) history of eye surgery and (5) presence of turbid refractive media that affected fundus examination.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using R V.4.3.3 software. Based on previous studies, the incidence rate of DR is 22.27%.3 Based on the sample size calculation formula for cross-sectional studies, the sample size was calculated using the epi.sssimpleestb function from the epir package in R, with the parameters set as a two-sided test, power=0.80 and confidence level=0.95, the required sample size was calculated to be 430 participants. Considering a 20% non-response rate, the final required sample size was determined to be 516 participants. The number of patients recruited in this study meets the sample size requirements.

Questionnaires and anthropometric measurements

Patients’ demographic and clinical information, such as age, gender, DM duration and other fundamental conditions, were obtained through face-to-face interviews with trained investigators who gave standardised questionnaires. Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were obtained using a Shanghe (SH-20A) height and weight measuring instrument. Blood pressure was measured using an electronic sphygmomanometer (Yuyue 680CR).

Fundus photography acquisition

Participants were escorted into a completely dark room, where trained staff, certified by ophthalmologists with a senior associate title or above, used the ML-800s fundus camera (Mole Medical Technology, Hangzhou) to capture 45° fundus images centred on the macula for both eyes. To ensure image quality, three ophthalmologists with senior associate titles independently assessed the images for adequacy. In cases of inconsistent assessments, a consensus meeting was convened to review the images until agreement was reached. Each patient’s eyes were evaluated independently.

DR grading and quantitative measurement

DR grading and quantitative measurement: The colour fundus images were uploaded to the EVision AI system (Yi Wei Technology (Beijing)) for diagnosis and quantitative analysis. This system, designed based on human visual biomimicry principles, compares the uploaded fundus images with classic DR lesion images to assess the severity of DR and perform quantitative analysis of lesions in the retinal images. The process begins with image preprocessing, including denoising and normalisation. Normalisation ensures that the colour, brightness and exposure levels of the images fall within a consistent grayscale range, enhancing feature extraction accuracy across large datasets. Subsequently, grayscale thresholding is performed using features such as colour, brightness and contrast, followed by morphological techniques to extract lesions such as MA, haemorrhages and exudates.1719 The system’s sensitivity for diagnosing no DR, mild non-PDR (NPDR), moderate NPDR, severe NPDR and PDR was 86.19%, 83.18%, 88.64%, 89.59% and 85.02%, respectively, with specificities of 63.07%, 70.96%, 64.16%, 70.38% and 74.79%.20 Final diagnostic results were confirmed by ophthalmologists with a senior associate title or above. The system follows the ICDR grading criteria,9 automatically identifying lesion locations and assisting in determining disease stages by marking lesion areas, as shown in figure 1.

AI use

We used the EVision AI system (Yiwei Technology (Beijing)) to screen patients with diabetes for retinal diseases and quantitatively measure various lesion indicators. This AI system employs deep learning semantic segmentation algorithms. We employed this AI technology because there is a clinical need for objective descriptions and quantification of retinal lesions. By using segmentation algorithms, we obtained the lesion areas, providing a foundation for feature quantification. The TransUnet network has demonstrated significant advantages in lesion segmentation tasks, primarily in the following aspects:

Integration of CNN and transformer strengths

The CNN feature extractor excels at capturing local details and texture features in images, effectively extracting edge and branching information of blood vessels in retinal images. In contrast, the Transformer encoder possesses strong global modelling capabilities, capturing long-range dependencies and generating global semantic feature sequences, thus enhancing the understanding of vascular distribution and structural relationships in the entire retinal image. The combination of these two components allows the network to accurately capture local features while maintaining a comprehensive global semantic understanding, thereby improving the completeness of feature representation.

Outstanding multiscale feature processing

The multiscale feature maps extracted by CNN cover information at different levels, from low-level detailed features to high-level abstract features, providing a rich foundation for subsequent processing. The Transformer encoder further processes these multiscale features, and the reconstructed 2D feature maps retain the advantages of multiscale information. This enables the network to effectively learn and recognise features, even in the presence of varying vessel diameters and shapes.

Accurate and efficient upsampling decoding process

The upsampling decoder uses transposed convolutions for upsampling, effectively restoring the spatial resolution of feature maps and ensuring the detail accuracy of segmentation results. By combining the global semantic features and multiscale local features obtained in the previous steps, the final output binary map of artery-vein segmentation not only has clear boundaries but also accurately distinguishes the artery and vein regions, significantly enhancing the accuracy and reliability of the segmentation task.

The AI technology we employed enables the automated extraction of retinal lesions based on a unified cognitive standard. This approach provides clinicians with a significantly larger quantity and greater dimensionality of feature metrics, thereby enhancing the evaluation and research of clinical symptoms, drug efficacy and treatment strategies.

Laboratory tests

Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) values were measured using Sinocare (PCH-100) portable glycosylated haemoglobin analyser and PCH-100 HbA1c kit (Sannuo Biosensing).

Definition of indicators

Hypertension was defined according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension (2018 Revised Edition).21 Body mass index (BMI) was categorised according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Control.22

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as mean±SD (x-±S), while categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). During data preprocessing, variables involving area were scaled by a factor of 1000. To compare continuous variables across different DR severity levels, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed, and categorical data were analysed using the χ² test, multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction to control the type I error rate. Due to the identification of only one case of PDR in this study, this case was classified as severe NPDR during data preprocessing, corresponding to the lesion stage most similar to its condition. Given that the number of NPDR severity levels exceeded two, and the parallelism test yielded a p<0.05, the relationship between different NPDR severities and retinal lesion indicators was assessed using unordered multicategory logistic regression, sensitivity analysis was subsequently performed, excluding cases of mild NPDR. Model 1 analysed the association between retinal lesion indicators and DR severity without adjusting for confounders. Model 2 adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes duration, HbA1c, smoking and alcohol consumption. The screening value of HA/RA, HA to MA count ratio (HA/MA), HA, H/MA and MAs for different NPDR severity levels was evaluated using ROC curve, and diagnostic thresholds were determined based on the maximum Youden index. All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi V.2.5.1, with a significance level set at α=0.05.

Results

Basic information

A total of 655 individuals with type 2 diabetes were recruited from the community, with 615 completing the examinations. Data from 533 participants with complete information were used for the analysis in this study. Among the 533 patients with type 2 diabetes (1012 eyes) included in the study, 258 were male (48.4%) and 275 were female (51.6%), with a mean age of 64.03±9.71 years. ROC analysis for screening different severities of NPDR using various indicators

The ROC analysis for screening different severities of NPDR using various indicators is shown in figures3 4 and table 3. Before adjusting for confounding factors, the area under the curve (AUC) for HC was the highest (AUC=0.925), followed by H/MA (AUC=0.924), HA/RA (AUC=0.917), HA (AUC=0.917), HA/MA (AUC=0.815) and MAs (AUC=0.808). The highest Youden index was observed for HA/RA and HA (0.796), with optimal cut-off values of 0.063 and 6.995, respectively. This was followed by HC (0.792), with an optimal cut-off value of 0, and H/MA (0.792), with an optimal cut-off value of 1. HA/MA had a Youden index of 0.626, with an optimal cut-off value of 0, while MAs had the lowest Youden index (0.603), with an optimal cut-off value of 0. After adjusting for age, gender, BMI, hypertension, diabetes duration, HbA1c, smoking and alcohol consumption, HC exhibited the largest AUC (0.934), followed by H/MA (AUC=0.933), HA (AUC=0.901), HA/RA (AUC=0.900), HA/MA (AUC=0.858) and MAs (AUC=0.842). The highest Youden index was observed for HC (0.803), with an optimal cut-off value of 0.020, followed by H/MA (0.789), with an optimal cut-off value of 0.072. Both HA and HA/RA had a Youden index of 0.695, with optimal cut-off values of 0.093, while MAs had a Youden index of 0.623, with an optimal cut-off value of 0.101. HA/MA had the smallest Youden index (0.616), with an optimal cut-off value of 0.107. In this study, all indicators demonstrated favourable AUC values (>0.8), indicating strong discriminatory power. While some indicators, such as HA/MA, exhibited relatively lower Youden’s index, their AUC value was 0.815. After adjusting for confounding factors, the AUC increased to 0.858, further supporting their robust discriminatory ability at the overall level.

Discussion

In this study, we employed a validated AI system to quantify retinal lesion indicators in patients with type 2 diabetes. Our analysis revealed that the HA/RA and HA/MA demonstrate superior screening capability for early DR compared with MAs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use the ratio of HA to RA as a screening indicator for early DR and the first community-based cross-sectional study identifying associations between HA/RA and HA/MA with NPDR. Previous studies predominantly recruited hospital-based patients, who were often in the advanced stages of NPDR or PDR, limiting their applicability for early detection of DR. Furthermore, manual quantification methods15 23 and angiographic techniques24 25 employed in such studies are time-consuming, labour-intensive and require costly and invasive procedures like fluorescein angiography (FFA), making them unsuitable for large-scale screening. In contrast, our community-based study sample included individuals at various stages of DR, from no retinopathy to severe NPDR, thus offering a more representative cohort for early screening research. By using a validated AI tool for screening, we significantly reduced the time and labour involved, providing an efficient and scalable approach for early DR detection in type 2 diabetes patients.

HA/RA and HA/MA can serve as novel metrics for the early screening of DR. HA/RA represents the ratio of HA to the total RA. In contrast to isolated haemorrhagic spots or total HA, HA/RA integrates the proportion of haemorrhage within the entire retinal field. A higher HA/RA value indicates a larger HA and more severe pathology, reflecting the progression of DR more accurately. HA/MA, on the other hand, is the ratio of HA to MAs. Retinal arteriolar dilation is an early physiological indicator of microvascular dysfunction,26 reflecting impaired autoregulation of small retinal arterioles. According to Starling’s and Laplace’s laws, further arteriolar dilation increases the pressure in the retinal capillaries, leading to the formation of MAs, which may further progress into haemorrhages or exudates, exacerbating retinal damage. Therefore, as the disease advances, MAs tend to develop into haemorrhages, leading to an increased HA. Consequently, HA/MA also increases with the severity of DR. Theoretically, HA/MA is a more precise indicator of DR severity. However, our findings suggest that both before and after adjustments, the sensitivity of HA/MA remains relatively low. This may be due to the fact that the study participants were recruited from a community setting, with most being in the early stages or without DR. Future validation in larger, population-based cohort studies is needed, as longitudinal approaches have shown promise in identifying incident DR using machine learning in type 1 diabetes populations.27 Sadda et al5 highlighted HA as a key driving factor for increased DR risk, but both the ETDRS and ICDR grading scales primarily focus on lesion quantity based on standard photographs, rather than lesion area.5 This is because the technology available during the development of DR grading standards did not support the measurement of lesion areas. Previous studies have shown that MAs are the first observable indicators of DR.28 Klein et al were the first to confirm this in both cross-sectional and subsequent cohort studies.29 30 Further studies, including quantitative ultra-widefield FFA by Ehlers et al, demonstrated that the severity of DR correlates with MAs.31 A 12-year follow-up study by Kohner et al32 further confirmed the significant predictive capacity of MAs for DR progression, consistent with the findings of the current study. Currently, the ICDR staging system mainly relies on MAs, HC and venous beading for early DR grading. However, our study found that even after controlling for confounding factors, the sensitivity of MA-based screening for DR was only 63.37%, with a Youden index of 0.623, significantly lower than that of HA/RA and HA/MA. Our findings suggest that HA/RA and HA/MA, both before and after adjustments, exhibit higher AUC values, sensitivity, specificity and Youden index compared with MAs. This indicates that HA/RA and HA/MA have superior diagnostic and screening capabilities for DR at different stages of severity. We propose that HA/RA and HA/MA should be incorporated into AI-based DR screening algorithms in the future.

HC, HA and H/MA demonstrate robust screening capabilities for the early detection of DR. After adjusting for confounding factors, the AUC for HA, HC and H/MA was 0.901, 0.934 and 0.933, respectively. A cohort study by Silva et al15 identified H/MA as a risk factor for the progression of DR from no DR to PDR. However, in Sadda et al’s cohort study,5 H/MA was found to be a significant risk factor only in univariate analysis, whereas in multivariate analysis, HA remained the only significant factor. The authors hypothesised that, in cases with extensive HA, the contribution of MAs to the total lesion area might be minimal, suggesting that considering MAs alone is insufficient for assessing DR severity. In contrast, our study found that H/MA was strongly correlated with the severity of NPDR, with an even stronger association after adjusting for confounding factors. We speculate that the difference in findings may be attributed to the distinct methods used for differentiating HC and HA. In this study, an AI system was used for automatic measurement of haemorrhagic spots and HA, whereas Sadda et al’s study relied on manual measurements by graders. Moreover, the outcome of our study differed from previous research; in our study, the outcome was the severity of different stages of NPDR, including classifying PDR as severe NPDR, whereas previous studies primarily focused on PDR as an outcome. Sadda et al’s analysis also indicated that lesion location is crucial for predicting the risk of PDR progression. On average, eyes with H/MA or lesions located farther from the optic nerve head were associated with a higher risk of progression. This finding is consistent with the report by Silva et al,33 who highlighted that peripheral lesions are associated with an increased risk of progression to PDR. Regrettably, due to technical limitations, this study did not differentiate between lesion locations. This aspect is planned for further exploration in future research.

Strengths and limitations

This study introduced DR indicators with enhanced diagnostic efficacy based on AI-driven quantitative analysis. With the growing adoption of the DR-AI system, more precise early diagnoses of DR may be achieved. Future research, including larger-scale cross-sectional and cohort studies, can further validate the diagnostic capabilities of HA/RA and HA/MA for DR, and explore whether there are differences in the distribution of lesion locations among DR indicators. However, there are several limitations to this study. First, due to the cross-sectional design, we were unable to assess the predictive power of these new indicators over long-term follow-up. Although preliminary results suggest high sensitivity and specificity in screening for DR, their role in predicting disease progression remains unverified. Second, although the sample was derived from a specific community, the limited sample size and the regional and demographic characteristics of the participants may impact the generalisability of the results. Therefore, future studies should aim to increase the sample size and validate these findings across diverse populations. Additionally, while the proposed indicators performed well in screening, the study exclusively included patients with type 2 diabetes, and the sensitivity and specificity of these indicators for screening DR in type 1 diabetes patients require further validation. Thus, future research could evaluate the applicability and performance of these new indicators across a range of ocular diseases.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of community-recruited patients with type 2 diabetes, we identified that HC exhibited the highest sensitivity for early DR screening. Additionally, novel markers, HA/RA and HA/MA, demonstrated significant screening potential for early DR. These findings warrant further prospective studies to validate their predictive value and explore their clinical implications.
