A Cross-Sectional Social Network Analysis of Decision-Making About Recruiting a Living Donor for Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Briana E. Lee, Heather M. Gardiner, Crystal A. Gadegbeku, Peter P. Reese, Zoran Obradovic, Edward L. Fink, Avrum Gillespie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveMost living kidney donors are members of the recipient’s social network. Our study aims to characterize patient and social network factors associated with recruiting a living kidney donor.Study DesignA cross-sectional study.Setting & ParticipantsA total of 106 patients receiving hemodialysis (mean age 60 ± 13 years, 45% female, 75% Black) identified 508 network members (106 who offered to donate and 38 who received a donation request).PredictorsDemographic and network factors (eg instrumental support, strength of relationships between the patients and members).OutcomesWhether the member offered to donate and whether the member’s offer was accepted. Patients’ qualitative reasoning regarding decision-making for recruiting a living kidney donor.Analytic ApproachWe performed a mixed-methods egocentric network analysis using multilevel logistic regression models as well as qualitative analysis of open-ended survey questions.ResultsMembers who provided instrumental support were more likely to offer to donate (odds ratio 3.54; 95% CI [1.80-7.00]; P < 0.001) and have their offer declined (odds ratio 0.12; 95% CI [0.02-0.74]; P = 0.02). Members with stronger relationships with the patient (9.4 [1] vs 8.4 [2.5], P = 0.02) were more likely to be evaluated at a transplant center. Concern and guilt for the member was the most common reason that patients declined offers (41%) or did not make a living donor request (28%), while members’ insistence on donating was a common reason for accepting an offer.LimitationsSmall sample of only patients receiving hemodialysis.ConclusionsFor many patients, the barrier to living kidney donation was not a lack of potential donors within their network but reticence to recruit a donor due to concern and guilt for the donor. Future living donor kidney transplantation interventions should engage patients and their networks to facilitate communication, address patient-specific concerns and emphasize donor retention.

A living donor kidney transplant (LDKT) is the optimal treatment for kidney failure;1,2 however, there are inherent psychosocial and ethical challenges in burdening another to accept the risks of donation.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 As a result, patients’ reluctance to initiate LDKT discussions is one of the most common modifiable barriers to donor recruitment.5,10, 11, 12, 13 Most living donors are members of the patient’s social network (ie, family, friends, etc.), yet few studies have examined the role of patients’ social networks in the living donation decision-making process.12,14,15 Further, most research has focused on the network member (member) at the time of donor evaluation and not at the critical stage before when patients and members are deliberating over potential donation. This is because transplant centers do not routinely collect social network data.16, 17, 18 Social networks are an important social determinant of health and differences in sociodemographic factors among members of the social network may contribute to disparities in living donation.12,19

Social network analysis involves the examination of both network composition and characteristics such as the relationship to the patient, strength of relationships, type of support the members provide, and structure of the network (eg, mean strength of relationships and member interconnectivity).16,20, 21, 22, 23 Our previous research has shown that transplant candidates with larger networks made more living donor requests.24 We also found that transplant candidates tended to request for donations from members they had strong relationships with and from members who provided them with instrumental support. In a subsequent study of patients receiving hemodialysis, we found that those with larger networks and more weakly connected members received more donation offers as patients received offers from weakly connected members.23,25

In this previous work, we focused on the living donor offers and overall network structure (eg, network constraint, position as a core network member).25 This study builds on this pervious work and focuses on how the members’ characteristics and their relationships with patients are associated with whether a patient receives a donation offer, accepts this donation offer, and requests for a donation from a member. We also examined qualitative responses about patients’ decision-making for recruiting or not recruiting specific members as potential donors. Finally, we evaluated if characteristics of the member were associated with subsequent transplant evaluation. The goal of our study is to mitigate extant disparities in LDKT by identifying barriers to donor recruitment via a network approach.19

Methods

Setting and Participants

This is a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data of a social network intervention clinical trial. Participants were recruited as a census at 2 hemodialysis facilities located in Southeastern Pennsylvania and Central New Jersey between October 2018 and February 2020. Patients were eligible if they had kidney failure, spoke English, and were at least 18 years old. The Temple University institutional review board approved the study protocol; written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The clinical and research activities being reported here are consistent with the Principles of the Declaration of Istanbul outlined by the Declaration of Istanbul on Organ Trafficking and Transplant Tourism as well as the Declaration of Helsinki.26,27 For clarity, we will refer to patients with kidney failure or potential transplant recipients as participants and potential donors as members for the remainder of the study.

Measurement

Data were collected via an interviewer-administered questionnaire developed from previously validated questionnaires and egocentric methods: the name generator, the name interpreter, and a who-to-whom matrix.28, 29, 30

The name generator included 6 questions and could generate up to 12 members: (1) members whom the participant seeks out for the participant’s important matters (seek discussion); (2) members who seek out the participant to discuss the participant’s important matters (unsolicited discussion); (3) members who seek out the participant to discuss the members’ problems, even if the participant’s problems are not discussed (member discussion); (4) members the participant discusses their health with (health discussion); (5) members who offered to donate; and (6) members who received a donation request. For members who offered to donate, participants were asked whether they accepted or declined that offer. Notably, members could be generated by more than one question (eg, a member could discuss important matters and receive a donation request from a participant).

The name interpreter identified members’ sex, cohabitation status, frequency of contact with the participant, types of social support (ie, emotional support, telling the participant they care, providing advice, instrumental support [eg, help with chores, childcare, and transportation], and financial support), and their role or relationship to the participant (eg, parents, siblings, etc.). An open-ended option was provided for roles not listed, and responses were individually inspected and re-categorized.25 Participants were not asked to report their members’ age or race to reduce survey burden because typically social networks have 95% racial concordance.31 The strength of the participant-member relationship was measured using a 10-point scale (ie, “with 10 being very close and one being not close at all”).

The who-to-whom matrix questions assessed relationships between each member pair. Participants were similarly asked to rate the strength of the relationship between each member on an 11-point scale (ie, “with 10 being especially close and zero being total strangers”). Network variables (eg, size, density, and constraint) were calculated using relationship strength data from the name interpreter and the who-to-whom matrix.25

The questionnaire also assessed participants’ self-reported kidney transplant waitlist status, dialysis vintage, and demographic variables such as age, sex, racial identity, annual income (categorical), education level, and marital status.

Qualitative Analysis

We asked participants open-ended questions about their reasons for accepting or declining an offer and reasons participants did or did not request a donation from a member. Participants were then asked to share the outcomes of accepted donation offers and requests (ie, “what happened?”). Responses were individually inspected and coded by 3 trained researchers into categories (ie, personal health, member’s health, concern or guilt for the member, personal disinterest, and member’s disinterest). Coding of responses was blinded by participant demographics and reviewed by the research team. Inconsistent categorization was discussed until a consensus was reached.

Network Statistics

A core network member (core member) was defined as a member that was generated by one of the following questions: seek discussion, unsolicited discussion, member discussion, and/or health discussion. A detailed glossary of network statistic terms (eg network size, mean network strength, and degree centrality) are available in Table S1.

Descriptive Statistics

The χ2 and Fisher exact tests were used for categorical dependent variables, and a t-test or analysis of variance was used for continuous dependent variables.

Modeling Outcomes

Our primary outcomes were as follows: (1) whether a member offered to donate (yes/no), (2) whether a members’ offer was accepted (yes/no), and (3) whether a member received a donation request (yes/no).

We first examined the bivariate associations among our 3 primary outcomes and a priori demographic and network variables.14,32,33 We then constructed 3 parsimonious multilevel logistic regression models to simultaneously test the associations of both participant and member variables with each of our primary outcomes. Model building was completed in 3 steps: (1) participant measures, (2) member measures, and (3) network measures. Confounders were identified a priori (complete description in Tables S2 and S3).25 Of note, we omit our model testing of the outcome of whether a member received a donation request as we calculated an intraclass correlation of 0.96, therefore significant variance was explained by participant-level attributes likely due to insufficient sample size.

Missing Data

Participants’ surveys were excluded if sections of the questionnaire were less than 90% complete. Four participants’ network densities could not be calculated because the participant had only one member. The proportion of missing data was low (<2% member’s sex and strength of relationship). Twenty-six participants (25%) chose not to report their income level, and one participant (1%) did not report their education level. Income was categorized into ≥ $40,000 or < $40,000 based on the federal poverty line for a 2-family household in the United States.34 The participants who chose not to report their income level were assigned an income of ≥ $40,000 for analysis.

Sensitivity Analysis

We retested our final models with the reassignment of income for participants who did not report their income level from ≥ $40,000 in our original models to < $40,000. In addition, the participants did not report their members’ age; however, some members may be ineligible to donate due to age. Thus, we created member and participant eligibility variables and retested our final models with both variables included. Details for the construction of both variables are in Tables S4 and S5; model results in Table S6.

Statistical Software

The SPSS version 25 was used for data management.35 R version 4.1.3 was used for data analysis.36 Igraph was used for social network visualization.37

Members Who Received Living Donor Requests

Participants requested donations from members with whom they had stronger relationships (9.53 ± 1.03 vs 8.93 ± 1.75, P = 0.002), from whom they received instrumental support and financial support (71% vs 29%, P = 0.02 and 68% vs 32%, P = 0.03, respectively), and from whom they sought discussion (53% vs 47%, P = 0.04). Participants did not request donations from members who initiated discussions about the member’s problems (member discussion; 37% vs 63%, P = 0.02).

Qualitative Responses

Most participants responded to the open-ended questions asking why they accepted or declined a donation offer (84% participant response rate, 83% of responses among participants with offers) and why they did or did not request a donation (96% participant response rate with responses for 87% of members).

The most common reasons participants declined member offers or did not request a donation from a member were concern (41%; eg “if something bad were to happen to them”) or guilt (28%; eg “I would feel guilty if something happened to them”) for the member and member ineligibility, such as poor health or histological incompatibility (19% and 32%, respectively; Fig 3 and Table 4). Participant disinterest was also a common reason for not requesting for a donation (14%). Discussion

In this mixed-methods social network analysis of LDKT decision-making of patients receiving hemodialysis, we found that 69% of participants self-reported potentially eligible donors in their network. However, only 40% of the members’ offers were accepted and only 7% of eligible members received a donation request. Although participants were more likely to receive offers from members who provided instrumental support, they were also more likely to decline these offers. Based on our qualitative analyses, this is most often because of concern and guilt for the member who supports them, demonstrating that participants’ concerns for the donor, the relationship, and the support of the relationship limits LDKT access rather than a lack of willing donors.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 These findings provide unique insights into how both quantitative and qualitative dimensions of patients’ relationships with their members may affect access to living donation. Further, these data are often not collected by transplant centers and national registries and thus identify important and underrecognized areas for interventions.16, 17, 18

In our study, participants received and accepted more donation offers than made donation requests. Requesting for a donation is more of an active behavior compared with accepting another’s offer to donate.38 To make a living donor request, the patient must first believe the benefits outweigh the risks of LDKT, and notably, the risks for the donor are often overestimated by patients.38 In addition, they must select a member with whom they feel comfortable enough to make a personal request for a living donation and have the skill to effectively communicate their need with sensitivity and empathy.8,38,39 Like our previous study of kidney transplant candidates, we found that participants requested donations from members with whom they had the strongest relationships with and who provided instrumental support.24 However, many participants also declined offers from strongly connected members who provide instrumental support and instead accepted offers from weakly connected members.25 Our qualitative results suggest this is because participants’ concern about complicating their relationship with the member and others in the network and losing the relationships and support of valuable members. In contrast, one of the major reasons participants accepted donation offers was the members’ insistence on donating to the participant (eg, they care for the participant and do not wish to see them suffer). For this reason, future LDKT interventions should engage the network to not only facilitate conversations between recipients and donors and address recipient feelings of guilt and concern for the donor but also engage donors to provide the opportunity for donors to share their motivations and reduce the onus on the recipient to identify and recruit a donor.

In addition, our previous study found weakly connected members were the most likely to have their offer accepted; however, these members were also the most likely to be lost before donor evaluation.25 Given our current findings that the most common outcome of accepted donation offers and requests before transplantation evaluation is donor disinterest (eg, loss to follow-up), interventions to facilitate donor engagement may promote recruitment of strongly connected members and retention throughout the transplantation process. It should be noted that causes of donor attrition, such as not a match and member ineligible for health reasons, may also reflect a common strategy to provide donors means of opting out of donorship.40 Therefore, engagement of potential donors should be carefully balanced with an emphasis on the voluntary nature of donation and safeguards in place for the donor.

Differences in people’s social networks have been proposed as an explanation for extant disparities in LDKT.16,18,23 Although we found no difference in the likelihood of accepting a member’s offer based on socioeconomic status (eg, income and educational attainment) and race, self-identifying Black participants received fewer donation offers than non-Black participants. To reduce survey burden, we did not collect the members’ race however previous studies have found that donors identified from recipient social networks typically have 95% racial concordance.31 We hypothesize that the observed disparities of self-identifying Black participants receiving fewer donation offers may reflect inequality at the level of health care systems, in which potential donors are likely also self-identifying Black individuals and experience similar disparities as patients. In addition, previous research has found that women are more likely to be living kidney donors; however, we found that male members completed donor evaluations at higher rates compared with female members despite there being no difference in rates of donor recruitment (accepted offers or requests) based on sex.24,41,42 Further research is needed to understand potential racial and sex disparities in LDKT and should evaluate disparities of both transplant candidates and their potential donors.43,44

Altogether, we encourage LDKT network interventions to engage the social network to assist in identifying potential donors and facilitating communication between recipients and donors, specifically addressing feelings of guilt, concern, and uncertain donor outcomes.4,45, 46, 47 Notably, a successful intervention by Rodrigue et al addresses many of the barriers identified in our study with home-based educational sessions involving both patients and their networks.43,47 This network intervention offered the opportunity to clarify misunderstandings and fears and bypass the need for patients to initiate donor discussion, creating the opportunity to recruit strongly connected and motivated donors who may be the most likely to complete transplant evaluation based on our study.47 Furthermore, involving living donor champions and using social media may be effective strategies to expand and engage patients’ social network.24,25,48, 49, 50 Such interventions may be particularly effective at overcoming racial disparities in LDKT by addressing disparities at both the participant and network level.47,48

These findings should be interpreted in the context of our study’s limitations. First, the generalizability of our sample was limited by data collection at 2 Mid-Atlantic urban and suburban facilities and focused only on in-center patients receiving hemodialysis. Therefore, we cannot generalize our findings to patients receiving peritoneal dialysis or those who have had successful living donor transplants. Furthermore, we had a response rate of just over 50%, and participants motivated to enroll in our study may not be representative of the national sample of patients with kidney failure. Our sample also consisted of predominantly self-identified Black participants (76%), which limited the generalizability to other racial groups. Second, this was an egocentric network analysis, which relies on participants’ recollections of their relationships with members and the relationships between members rather than direct reports by the members themselves. For this reason, the member relationships may be under or overestimated based on the participants’ perceptions. Furthermore, the members’ donor evaluations were participant-reported, and we did not validate these evaluations with the transplant centers. Finally, members were generated through 6 questions to map out the relevant social connections in terms of LDKT.9 We did not generate all potential members, and networks may not be truly representative of all members that existed in participant networks and influenced participant attitudes toward LDKT. There is still much to learn about the social dynamics and networks of patients with kidney failure. We recommend that further network analysis include larger samples of patients with kidney failure and with greater diversity to validate these results.

In conclusion, for many patients, the barrier to LDKT was not a lack of potential donors within their social network but reticence to recruit a donor due to concern and guilt on their behalf. Furthermore, many of the recruited donors who ultimately did not get evaluated at a transplant center had weak relationships with the patient. Future network interventions should engage both patients and potential donors to encourage potential donors to participate in donor recruitment and improve retention through the transplantation process. Such network interventions may be especially effective at reducing disparities in LDKT.
