Geospatial analysis of primary healthcare accessibility and qualitative assessment of affordability in rural communities of Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District, Ghana
Authors: Peter Ansah Boakye, Daniel Buor, Francis Tei-Nartey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegardless of the efforts of the government and the private sector to improve health facilities in Ghana, a significant number of rural communities still experience inadequate access to primary healthcare. This study used the mixed-methods approach to provide an overview of the geographic distribution of healthcare facilities and the accessibility and affordability of primary healthcare within the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District. Accessibility was assessed using Euclidean buffer analysis around primary healthcare facilities, while affordability was examined qualitatively using in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with community members, community leaders and health workers. Focus group discussions and interviews were conducted using the purposive sampling technique to select 97 respondents including community members, community leaders and healthcare personnel. Additionally, the Geographic Information System (GIS) software, specifically ArcGIS Pro, was used to analyze the spatial distribution of health facilities. The findings reveal that approximately 95% of sampled communities were located within 5 km of a primary healthcare facility based on straight-line distance; however, qualitative findings indicated that transport costs, poor road conditions, and physical barriers substantially constrained effective access. Furthermore, primary healthcare was found not to be affordable due to the high cost of transportation because of the poor nature of roads. Recommendations include government investment in road infrastructure to reduce transportation costs, thereby improving the affordability of primary healthcare and facilitating access to services. These findings bear important implications for achieving the health-related Sustainable Development Goals set forth by the United Nations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03260-2.Keywords: Primary healthcare, Geospatial analysis, Rural communities and affordability

Introduction

Access to primary healthcare is a critical determinant of population health and well-being, particularly in rural areas where socio-economic and infrastructural barriers often limit healthcare utilization [17]. In Ghana, like many developing countries, rural communities experience disproportionately higher burdens of disease, poverty, and limited access to essential health services [26]. While primary healthcare serves as the first point of contact for individuals seeking treatment, prevention, and health promotion, geographic access to these services remains a significant challenge in rural settings [4]. This disparity contributes to poor health outcomes, including higher rates of maternal and child mortality, preventable diseases, and delayed care-seeking behavior.

Primary healthcare (PHC) is defined as essential services that utilize practical, scientifically validated, and socially acceptable methods and technologies. These services are made universally accessible to individuals and families within the community, ensuring their active participation. Furthermore, PHC is designed to be accessible to both rural and urban residents, supporting sustainable development while promoting self-reliance and self-determination (World Health Organization (WHO) [33]). Thus, primary healthcare is a paramount approach to achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC).

Primary healthcare access is shaped by multiple dimensions, including availability, affordability, and most importantly, geographic accessibility (the physical proximity and ease with which individuals can reach primary healthcare facilities) [18]. Studies have shown that travel time, distance to health centers, and transport infrastructure are key factors influencing primary healthcare service utilization [20]. In rural Ghana, where transportation networks are poorly developed and facilities are sparsely distributed, residents often must travel long distances to access care, leading to delays or outright avoidance of treatment [9]. Additionally, natural features such as rivers, hills, and forested areas further complicate mobility, exacerbating inequities in primary healthcare access.

Despite Ghana’s efforts to implement community-based health interventions, including the establishment of the Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds, significant gaps persist in ensuring equitable access to primary healthcare in rural areas. The spatial distribution of health facilities remains uneven, with many remote communities underserved (Amoah-Nuamah et al. [2]. Geographic Information Systems (GIS) provide a powerful tool to analyze these spatial disparities, allowing researchers to map service areas, estimate travel times, and identify populations at risk of inadequate healthcare access . Understanding these geographic challenges is essential for developing targeted policies and interventions to improve health equity.

Enhancing the accessibility to primary healthcare facilities is a crucial element in the pursuit of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly Goal 3, which emphasizes the attainment of universal health coverage (United Nations (UN) [31]. This includes ensuring financial risk protection, facilitating access to quality essential healthcare services, and providing safe, effective, high-quality, and affordable essential medicines and vaccines for everyone. The interconnection between health and development necessitates that governments and various development partners allocate resources to the health sector. The United Nations' SDG 3, Target 8, aims to realize universal health coverage, encompassing financial risk protection and access to quality essential healthcare services and safe, effective, high-quality, and affordable essential medicines and vaccines for all individuals.

Globally, rural populations constitute nearly 46.66% of the population, with data indicating 3,422,928,507 individuals residing in rural areas (World Development Report [32]. In Ghana, the scenario is comparable, as 40.76% of its population lives in rural areas (World Development Report 2023). To meet the targets set for 2030, it is essential to concentrate efforts on rural areas that exhibit the lowest levels of healthcare accessibility. Developing nations including Ghana and rural locales have been recognized as such areas of concern [14] .

Moreover, limited access to healthcare services in disadvantaged and rural regions significantly impacts the impoverished population, leading to suffering from the financial burdens associated with severe illnesses in two main ways: the expenses incurred in obtaining medical services and the loss of productive workdays [12] . Cyr et al. [10] indicated that insufficient medical infrastructure, extended travel times, ineffective transportation systems, and a shortage of healthcare professionals are significant obstacles to accessing medical care in rural areas, contributing to the disparity in mortality rates between rural and urban populations. This situation is exacerbated by rural inhabitants who must navigate rivers to reach healthcare facilities [25].

The accessibility of primary healthcare encompasses five key dimensions: availability, physical location, affordability, adequacy, and acceptability [35]. It is essential to recognize that the ongoing discussions surrounding access to primary healthcare often concentrate on only one or two of these dimensions. For example, Agbenyo et al. [12] used GIS models to assess rural communities’ accessibility to health facilities based on major transportation means in rural Ghana while Ganle et al. [14] investigated the challenges women with disabilities encounter in accessing maternal healthcare services in Ghana. Similarly, Asaah and Ussiph [3] highlighted the need to optimize access to primary healthcare services in rural communities within the Atwima Mponua District of Ghana, and Comber et al. [8] conducted a spatial analysis of health access variations, linking geography, socio-economic status, and access perceptions in the UK. However, there is a notable lack of studies that thoroughly address the accessibility and affordability dimensions of access to primary healthcare in the context of sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in Ghana. In Ghana, physical accessibility and financial affordability remain critical determinants of primary healthcare utilization, particularly in rural settings where poverty, poor transport networks, and uneven distribution of health facilities persist. Although the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) was introduced to reduce financial barriers, out-of-pocket payments for medicines, diagnostics, and transportation continue to limit healthcare utilization. At the same time, long travel distances and deteriorated road infrastructure constrain timely access to care. Examining accessibility without affordability risks underestimating the true burden faced by rural populations, while focusing solely on affordability ignores the geographical challenges that shape healthcare-seeking behaviour. Therefore, assessing both dimensions concurrently is necessary to capture the multidimensional nature of healthcare access in rural Ghana and to generate policy-relevant evidence for strengthening primary healthcare delivery.

To help fill this knowledge gap, this study explores access to primary healthcare delivery through the accessibility and affordability dimensions in rural communities in the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District of Ghana. Such insights may have significant implications for achieving the United Nations’ health-related Sustainable Development Goals. The research specifically analyzes the spatial distribution of primary healthcare facilities in the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District and their effects on the access and affordability of primary healthcare for rural residents.

Literature review

Healthcare accessibility

Healthcare accessibility refers to the ability of individuals to obtain appropriate healthcare services when needed (Penchansky & Thomas [24]; Levesque et al. [21]). Geographical accessibility is commonly measured using distance or travel time to the nearest health facility, often operationalized using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) through buffer or network analyses (Delamater et al. [11]). The World Health Organization recommends that populations should reside within 5 km or approximately 30 min of a primary healthcare facility to improve service utilization and health outcomes (World Health Organization 2010). Studies in sub-Saharan Africa show that greater distance or travel time negatively affects maternal and child health service utilization; for example, women living within 5 km of health centers were significantly more likely to use antenatal and delivery services in Benin (Tanou, Kishida & Kamiya [27]), and longer distances were associated with reduced maternal healthcare use in Burkina Faso .

Healthcare affordability

Healthcare affordability reflects the capacity of individuals or households to pay for healthcare services without experiencing financial hardship (SDG 3.8.2 definition; WHO 2015). It is commonly assessed using indicators such as out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure as a proportion of household income or consumption and the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2; WHO/World Bank [28]). Under Sustainable Development Goal monitoring, health expenditures are considered catastrophic when out-of-pocket payments exceed a defined share of total household expenditure or income (for example, 10% of total expenditure) or a high proportion of non-food expenditure (SDG 3.8.2; WHO/World Bank, 2025). Despite the implementation of Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme, studies show that insured households still incur out-of-pocket payments for medicines and services not fully covered by the scheme, and some households continue to face financial catastrophe due to healthcare costs (Akazili et al. [1]).

Indicators for measuring accessibility and affordability

Indicators for healthcare accessibility commonly include travel time to the nearest health facility, distance to health facilities, availability of transport services, and facility density as measures of geographic access to care ( Hashtarkhani et al. [15]). Affordability indicators typically comprise average or out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure relative to household income or consumption and the incidence of catastrophic health spending, as defined under the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.8.2 financial protection framework (World Health Organization & World Bank 2021; WHO 2024). Integrating GIS-based accessibility measures with household-level affordability data enables a more comprehensive assessment of healthcare access by jointly capturing spatial and financial barriers to utilization (Delamater et al. 2012; Pérez-Fernández et al. 2025).

Materials and methods

Study area

Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District is one of the twenty administrative districts in the Central Region of Ghana. It was formerly known as the Twifo-Hemang Lower Denkyira District with its capital at Twifo Praso. The district is bounded on the north by the Upper Denkyira East Municipal, at the South by the Hemang Lower Denkyira District, at the West by the Mpohor -Wassa East District and at the East by the Assin North Municipal [16] ). The population of Twifo Ati Morkwaa district (100,851) represents 3.5 per cent of the total population of Central Region (2,859,821). It comprises 49,998 males (49.6%) and 50,853 females (50.4%). Also, the District covers a land area of 955.8 square kilometers. Again, the district has a population density of 105.5 per sq. km [9]. The district is largely rural, with approximately 90% of the population engaged in local agriculture. Geographically, the district lies approximately between latitudes 5°30′N and 6°05′N and longitudes 1°30′W and 1°55′W in the Central Region of Ghana.

Research design

An exploratory research design was utilized in this study to investigate the accessibility of primary healthcare through two key dimensions: accessibility and affordability. The case study approach, as part of the exploratory design, was implemented to analyze the spatial distribution of primary healthcare facilities and their impact on access to these services in rural communities within the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District of Ghana. This research design was selected to provide a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of primary healthcare access in predominantly rural areas. It is deemed the most suitable for this investigation as it facilitates a thorough, contextual, and detailed exploration of the factors influencing accessibility to primary healthcare [10].

Data collection procedure

The data was gathered through Google Maps, GPS devices during fieldwork, interviews with key informants, and focus group discussions with residents. GPS software facilitated the collection of coordinates for various primary healthcare facilities and communities. These GPS coordinates were subsequently recorded in an Excel spreadsheet and saved in a CSV format. The data was then transformed into X and Y coordinates using Geographic Information System (GIS) ArcGIS Pro, allowing for the integration of field-collected data to create maps of the study area. The resulting features from this process include the delineation of the area, the locations of primary healthcare facilities, and the road networks. Additionally, data regarding the characteristics of the facilities and health personnel were obtained from the district health directorate and local residents. The key informants for the interviews included the Head of the District Health Directorate, In-charges of primary healthcare facilities, and assembly members.

These individuals were purposively selected due to their comprehensive understanding of the subject matter. A focused group discussion was held with residents of the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District to gather information regarding the accessibility and affordability of primary healthcare services in the area. The district is segmented into five sub-districts: Bimpong Egya, Nyinase, Morkwaa, Wamaso, and Praso. One community was randomly selected from each of these sub-districts for the Focus Group Discussion (FGD). In total, ten FGDs were conducted across the five chosen communities, with five discussions held for male participants and five for female participants. The selection of participants for the focus group discussions was guided by the research of Manoranjitham et al. [22], who suggested that the ideal number of participants for such discussions ranges from eight to ten. Consequently, nine members were included in each group (men and women). Participants were required to be natives of the communities and to have resided there for a minimum of five years. The selection of individuals for the focus group discussions was carried out randomly. A focus group discussion guide and an interview guide from a previous study were used for both the focus group discussion and the key informants interview. Consent, both written and oral, was obtained from all participants, who were also assured of the confidentiality of the information they provided. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary.

Trustworthiness was established through the implementation of several strategies: 1) both the interviews and focus group discussions with participants were extended in duration, lasting approximately 45 min. Pilot testing was conducted for both the interviews and focus group discussions. These sessions concluded when a point of saturation was reached. A knowledgeable individual facilitated the discussions to enhance the participants' comprehension of the subject matter. The moderator's role was to guide the group discussion rather than dominate it. Interviews and focus group discussions were conducted in Twi, the local dialect of the participants, while interactions with health providers were held in English. With the informed consent of the participants, interviews were audio-recorded, and handwritten field notes were also taken.

Data analysis

The Ghana Health Service, in conjunction with the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and the World Health Organization (WHO), has established a recommended distance of 5 km to the nearest healthcare facilities (UNHCR) [30]. This distance served as the benchmark for the buffer analysis. A comprehensive buffer analysis was performed on various primary healthcare facilities within the district, with distinct evaluations for Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS), Health Centers, and District Hospitals, all adhering to the 5 km reference distance on the provided maps.

The buffer-based analysis represents straight-line (Euclidean) distance and estimates theoretical spatial proximity to healthcare facilities rather than actual travel distance or travel time. Due to limitations in detailed road network attributes and transport cost data, network-based or cost-surface modelling could not be implemented. To address this limitation, qualitative data was used to contextualize functional accessibility and affordability. This analysis aimed to identify the number of communities that are situated beyond the WHO-recommended distances for accessing healthcare services.

The qualitative data obtained from the fieldwork underwent a systematic process involving coding, pattern recognition, and thematic categorization. This approach facilitated an examination of the relationships among the variables, contributing to a coherent and systematic body of evidence. The qualitative analysis framework utilized in this study comprised two distinct stages. First, the research team members engaged in familiarization with the data by reviewing the transcripts. Second, themes were delineated within the framework, with discussions among the team aimed at achieving consensus on these themes derived from two dimensions: geographical location, and affordability.

Results

Accessibility to primary healthcare

This research investigates access to primary healthcare by evaluating various factors such as spatial distance, travel time, means of transportation utilized to reach the facility, and the characteristics of the road network, among others, to assess physical accessibility.

This includes one (1) district hospital (DH), which provides services for the entire district population, and thirteen (13) Community Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds. Spatial distribution of health facilities and its effects on access to primary healthcare

This section examines the geographical distribution of primary healthcare facilities and its impact on access to primary healthcare services.

The healthcare buffer analysis in Fig. 2 indicates that approximately 95% of the sampled settlements were located within a 5 km Euclidean buffer of a primary healthcare facility; this estimate is based on settlement counts and is not weighted by population size.

Buffer of healthcare facilities in Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District

Also, the average travel time to the nearest primary healthcare facility reported by respondents was 70 min, with a range of 60 min to 130 min. Approximately 10% of respondents accessed healthcare within 30 min, while 90% exceeded this threshold. The World Health Organization recommends that populations should reside within 30 min or 5 km of a primary healthcare facility. Compared to this benchmark, 90% of households in the study area failed to meet the recommended standard. GIS-based analysis revealed that approximately 95% of communities were located within 5 km of a healthcare facility, indicating spatial proximity: however, poor road conditions significantly increased travel time, thereby limiting effective accessibility, reinforcing distance as a significant barrier to primary healthcare within the district.

The following are excerpts from key informants and focus group discussions that elucidate the relationship between the geographical distribution of health facilities and access to primary health care within the study area.

The poor state of the roads delays our ability to reach primary healthcare facilities. It can take hours to travel short distances, and sometimes, we can't even get to certain areas during the rainy season. [Female, 46 years, no formal education, Wamaso, FGD].

Pregnant women, especially, suffer a lot. Some give birth on the way to the hospital because it takes too long to reach the nearest health center. [Male, 55 years, basic education, Morkwaa, FGD].

During the rainy season, the roads become impassable, and we are cut off from primary healthcare facilities hence we rely on traditional medicine because it is almost impossible to travel. [Female, 32 years, tertiary education, Nuamakrom, FGD].

Patients miss routine check-ups or antenatal care because of transportation challenges (bad nature of roads). Therefore, they often arrive in worse health conditions, making it more difficult to treat them. [In-Charge, Bimpong Egya, II].

Residents must walk long distances because no cars or motorbikes are willing to go to their villages due to the road conditions. [In-Charge, Nyinase, II].

Means of transport

Most of the rural communities in this study district are accessible by walking or motorbike due to poor road conditions, especially in the rainy season. Therefore, the lack of accessible and affordable vehicles is a major obstacle to accessing primary healthcare. Moreover, most rural folks are unwilling to walk for so long to access primary healthcare.

We have seen cases where pregnant women die en route because it takes too long to reach the health center. The roads are impassable, and walking long distances is not an option for many. [In-Charge, Wamaso, II].

We must walk for hours to get to the nearest primary health center. It is very difficult, especially for the elderly and pregnant women. [Female, 40 years, no formal education, Morkwaa FGD].

The actual distance from most villages to the health facilities is relatively short; however, the poor condition of the roads often necessitates walking. This situation results in a longer travel time to access primary healthcare than would typically be expected. [Assembly member, II].

I know people who avoid going to the clinic because they don't want to walk such long distances. They wait until they are very sick, and by then, it is sometimes too late. [Male, 39 years, secondary education, Nyinase, FGD].

It is heartbreaking to see patients delay seeking primary healthcare because they simply can't afford a motorbike ride or don't have access to any vehicle at all. By the time they arrive at the primary healthcare facility, their conditions have worsened. [Assembly member, II].

Affordability of primary healthcare

Affordability is a fundamental dimension of healthcare access, significantly influencing individuals' ability to utilize primary healthcare services. It refers to the economic capacity of individuals or households to pay for healthcare services without experiencing financial hardship. In many regions, especially low- and middle-income countries, the cost of healthcare remains a primary barrier to access, affecting both the frequency and quality of care that individuals seek (Xu et al. [34]). Ensuring affordable healthcare is essential for achieving equitable health outcomes and improving overall well-being.

On the issue of affordability, respondents consistently described healthcare services as financially burdensome, primarily due to transportation costs, purchase of medicines not covered by the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), and diagnostic fees. Although specific monetary values were not reported, the majority of respondents indicated that healthcare expenses competed directly with essential household needs such as food and school fees.

Using the World Health Organization and World Bank benchmark that healthcare is considered unaffordable when out-of-pocket expenditure exceeds 10% of household income, respondents’ narratives suggest exposure to financial hardship, as healthcare payments were perceived as substantial relative to their income levels. These findings indicate that financial barriers remain significant despite NHIS coverage. Respondents had these to say:

Affordable healthcare in my opinion is a healthcare service that can be easily accessed by community members without any financial difficulty. [Male, 25 years, tertiary education, Nuamakrom, FGD].

I have a national health insurance card, so I pay a small amount whenever I access primary healthcare but the additional cost (transportation cost) I incur makes my overall healthcare cost too high. So, I think the total cost one incurs in accessing primary healthcare is too much and so it is not affordable. [Female, 38 years, no formal education, Nyinase, FGD].

Transportation cost is the main factor that makes primary healthcare expensive to these rural residents because several of them have national health insurance cards and so pay very little or nothing when they visit the health facility. But the amount of money they spend on transportation is very huge and this is what increases their overall cost of accessing primary healthcare.” [In-Charge, Morkwaa, II].

The cost of primary healthcare is a big problem for us in this community. Sometimes we don’t even have the money to pay for transportation to the health facility, let alone the consultation fees for medication. I skipped check-ups because I couldn’t afford the transport fare to get to the clinic, and I didn’t want to walk too. [Community leader, 47 years, Bimpong Egya, II].

Discussion

This study examined geographical accessibility and affordability of primary healthcare in the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District using GIS and household data. The findings indicate that although buffer analysis suggests high theoretical spatial coverage of primary healthcare facilities, this does not translate into functional accessibility for many rural residents. Qualitative findings indicate that poor road infrastructure, and seasonal impassability substantially increase travel distance and cost, thereby limiting effective access despite geographical proximity. This highlights the distinction between potential spatial access and realized healthcare utilization in rural settings. And reiterate that spatial proximity alone does not guarantee effective accessibility, particularly in rural settings where infrastructure quality shapes healthcare-seeking behaviour.

Consistent with previous studies in rural Ghana and other parts of sub-Saharan Africa including Mseke et al. [23] and Buor, [7], long travel times were found to discourage timely utilization of primary healthcare services. Studies have shown that exceeding the World Health Organization’s recommended threshold of 5 km or 30 min significantly reduces healthcare utilization, especially for vulnerable populations. The findings of this study therefore reinforce the importance of considering both distance and travel time in assessing healthcare accessibility.

Similarly, affordability analysis using the WHO and World Bank threshold of 10% of household income reveals that healthcare imposes a substantial financial burden on rural households, primarily due to transportation costs. These findings support the need for integrated policies addressing both infrastructure and financial protection mechanisms.

Findings from this study indicate that most communities in the Twifo Ati-Morkwaa District are located within the recommended 5 km straight-line distance to a primary healthcare facility. Consequently, this outcome may appear unexpected, particularly in light of Blanford et al. [6], who reported considerable geographic disparities in access to hospitals, maternity, and integrated health centers in Niger. Similarly, Yin et al. [35] highlighted inadequate spatial accessibility to healthcare facilities in China. In the present study, approximately 95% of the population resides within a 5 km Euclidean buffer of all healthcare facilities, suggesting high theoretical spatial coverage. However, qualitative findings indicate that functional access is constrained by poor road infrastructure, and transport-related barriers, which may increase travel distance and cost. Nonetheless, the theoretical proximity observed aligns with Ashiagbor et al. [4], who found that 81.48% of the population in the Ashanti Region, Ghana, lives within 5 km of healthcare facilities, and with Lechowski and Jasion [20], who reported that the average distance to primary healthcare is approximately 5 km for rural populations in Poland.

The research indicated that the poor condition of roads connecting communities to healthcare facilities contributes to inadequate access to primary healthcare in the district. The effects of transportation difficulties on healthcare utilization have been thoroughly examined in existing literature [19, 29]. Deteriorated road conditions lead to increased travel time and costs for patients seeking medical services. This observation aligns with findings from Atuoye et al. [5], which noted that “walking for more than 10 km to CHPS compounds is common in rural areas due to the poor state of the roads.” In the rural communities examined in this study, residents primarily rely on walking or motorbikes to reach primary healthcare services. The use of cars is often prohibitively expensive because of the road conditions. Consequently, the absence of accessible and affordable transportation options poses a significant barrier to obtaining primary healthcare [20].

Regarding the affordability of primary healthcare, the study revealed that respondents experience challenges in accessing primary healthcare, primarily due to their inability to cover travel costs to health facilities and their difficulty in purchasing medications during visits to these facilities. Additionally, it was observed that individuals possessing health insurance cards benefit from a certain degree of affordability when seeking care, in contrast to those without such coverage, as the insurance often subsidizes some or most of the medication costs. This observation aligns with the findings of Fenny et al. [13], which indicate that a greater proportion of insured patients express satisfaction with the overall quality of care compared to their uninsured counterparts.

The combined evidence from GIS-based accessibility analysis and respondents’ experiences indicates that, although most communities are located within the recommended 5 km straight-line distance to primary healthcare facilities, functional access is substantially constrained by poor road infrastructure and transportation challenges. Similarly, financial barriers continue to limit effective utilization of services, even among individuals with health insurance coverage. These findings highlight that theoretical spatial proximity does not always translate into equitable access and underscore the need for integrated policy interventions targeting road infrastructure, transport availability, and cost-related obstacles to improve healthcare accessibility in rural communities.

Conclusion

Although spatial analysis suggests that primary healthcare facilities are geographically proximate to most communities, functional access is constrained by infrastructural and financial barriers, indicating that proximity alone does not guarantee equitable healthcare utilization. Consequently, an effective transportation system is essential for enhancing access to primary healthcare facilities, particularly in developing nations where both private and public transportation options are often scarce. Health planners must prioritize road network quality data alongside facility location data when assessing healthcare accessibility.

Based on the findings of this study, several policy-relevant recommendations are proposed. First, travel time remains a major barrier due to poor road conditions and limited transport options. There is therefore a need for improvement of rural road infrastructure and transport networks to enhance functional accessibility to primary healthcare services. Upgrading feeder roads and improving transport availability would reduce travel time and facilitate timely healthcare utilization in remote communities.

Second, strengthening community-based primary healthcare delivery is necessary to reduce travel burden for rural populations. Expanding the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) programme and ensuring adequate staffing and medical supplies in existing facilities would improve service availability within communities and reduce dependence on distant health centres.

Finally, financial barriers to healthcare persist despite widespread enrolment in the National Health Insurance Scheme. Since transportation costs and out-of-pocket payments for medicines and diagnostic services constitute major components of healthcare expenditure, policies aimed at expanding NHIS coverage for essential medicines and diagnostics would reduce financial burden on rural households. In addition, targeted financial protection mechanisms for low-income rural populations would help mitigate the risk of financial hardship associated with healthcare seeking.

A limitation of this study is the absence of precise monetary estimates of healthcare expenditure. Respondents were unwilling or unable to report specific amounts spent on healthcare. Consequently, affordability was assessed using qualitative indicators of financial burden and established international benchmarks rather than direct expenditure ratios. Future studies should incorporate detailed household expenditure modules to allow for more precise measurement of catastrophic health spending.

Moreover, the spatial analysis technique utilized in this research involved the application of Euclidean distances. This approach is pertinent to the study as it aims to determine if the residents are within the recommended threshold distance established by the World Health Organization and the Ghana Health Service. Nonetheless, a major limitation is that it (Euclidean distances) operates under the assumption that travel takes place in a direct line, overlooking the impact of transportation services on accessibility and the various travel impediments, including road infrastructure. This limitation was minimized by using a qualitative approach to explore the impacts of transportation.
