Rare variant in intracellular loop-2 alters the spatial distribution and transducer coupling selectivity of the ghrelin receptor
Authors: Elsa M Balfe, Alexandre Torbey, Tomasz M Stepniewski, Yang Zhou, Lara Kohlenbach, Jade A Sency, Asuka Inoue, Lauren M Slosky, Jana Selent, Lawrence S Barak, Joshua D Gross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractG protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the largest class of membrane proteins and the most common pharmaceutical drug target. Prior work from our group and others supports that a conserved, proline-hydrophobic residue (ProH) motif within intracellular loop-2 (ICL2) biases GPCR-transducer coupling between canonical G proteins and β-arrestin (βarr). Here, we systematically characterize a rare missense mutation within the ProH motif of the ghrelin receptor (growth hormone secretagogue receptor [GHSR])—Leu149Pro34.51 (L149P)—to dissect how naturalistic perturbation of ICL2 dynamics pleiotropically regulates the spatial distribution, trafficking, and transducer selectivity of a prototypical, rhodopsin-like GPCR. Molecular dynamics simulations indicate that the L149P mutation destabilizes the α-helical conformation of ICL2, thereby increasing loop flexibility proximal to the receptor-transducer coupling interface. Using a panel of bioluminescence resonance-energy transfer biosensors combined with pharmacological and genetic tools, we demonstrate that the GHSR-L149P mutation (1) enhances receptor surface expression by reducing constitutive GHSR trafficking; (2) biases transducer engagement toward βarr1/2 and Gαi/o over Gαq and Gα13, despite partial mini-Gq coupling efficacy; (3) expands GPCR kinase (GRK) subclass utilization from GRK2/3-dependent to an additional, partial reliance on GRK5/6; and (4) promotes βarr1/2 recruitment independent of G protein-mediated second messenger kinase activation. The evolutionary conservation of the ProH motif suggests that cognate, ICL2-destabilizing variants likely confer comparable disruptions in GPCR structure/function beyond established effects on G protein coupling. Collectively, these results establish a naturalistic model to study ICL2-dependent GPCR regulation and illuminate the ProH motif as a promising drug target for biased allosteric modulators.Significance StatementG protein-coupled receptors are the most common pharmaceutical target in medicine. This study exploited a rare variant in intracellular loop-2 of the ghrelin receptor to characterize a conserved, allosteric “hotspot” controlling G protein-coupled receptor expression, trafficking, and signaling. These findings highlight intracellular loop-2 as a promising target for biased allosteric modulator drug design.Key words: G protein-coupled receptor, G protein, Arrestin, GPCR kinase, Intracellular loop-2, Biased allosteric modulator

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the largest class of integral membrane proteins and the most common pharmaceutical drug target.1 GPCRs convert extracellular stimuli into biological responses by coupling to intracellular transducers, including G proteins, β-arrestins (βarrs), and GPCR kinases (GRKs).2 The evolutionary conservation and ubiquitous expression of GPCRs highlight how even rare, naturally-occurring GPCR mutations can manifest clinically-relevant phenotypes.3 To enable adaptive biological responses, GPCRs adopt diverse conformational landscapes that can asymmetrically engage distinct G proteins and/or βarrs, leading to pathway-dependent physiological outcomes—a phenomenon known as biased signaling or functional selectivity. To fine-tune signaling, GPCRs harbor various allosteric binding sites/pockets accessible to endogenous regulators (eg, ligands, accessory proteins, lipids, and transducers) that influence receptor conformational dynamics, subcellular localization, and signaling. Based on these principles, therapeutic interests have emerged around leveraging these mechanisms to generate biased allosteric modulators that could enhance therapeutic efficacy while simultaneously reducing side effects, toxicity, or tachyphylaxis.4

The growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHSR) is the receptor target for the gut-derived, peptide hormones ghrelin (endogenous agonist) and liver-expressed antimicrobial protein-2 (endogenous antagonist/inverse agonist). The GHSR is a prototypical, rhodopsin-like GPCR that preferentially couples to Gαq and promiscuously couples to the Gαi/o and Gα12/13 subfamilies.5 Upon activation, the GHSR is phosphorylated by G protein-dependent intracellular kinases, including Gαq-dependent protein kinase C (PKC) and Gβγ-dependent G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2/3 (GRK2/3), leading to βarr recruitment and subsequent receptor desensitization, internalization, and signaling.6 The GHSR is an integral regulator of energy balance, reward, neuronal health, and affective behavior.7 Accordingly, the GHSR is considered a promising drug target for metabolic and neurological diseases.8 However, GHSR activation elicits widespread physiological effects that can generate treatment-limiting side effects, particularly on blood glucose control.9,10 Thus, more selective drug action via biased signaling or allosteric modulation could provide superior pharmaceutical drug candidates.

The GHSR can stabilize conformations conducive to signaling bias, suggesting that the development of biased GHSR ligands or discovery of natural GHSR variants is mechanistically feasible.5,8,11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Although several GHSR X-ray crystallography and cryogenic electron microscopy structures offer insights into the molecular basis of GHSR ligand binding and G protein (Gαq and Gαi/o) coupling,16, 17, 18, 19 the identification of GHSR allosteric sites and the molecular determinants of GHSR bias remains undercharacterized, limiting GHSR-directed drug development efforts. Our prior work with the GHSR shows that a highly conserved motif within intracellular loop-2 (ICL2)—characterized by a proline followed by a hydrophobic residue (ProH) located +6 (Pro14834.50) and +7 (Leu14934.51) amino acids downstream of the E/DRY motif—regulates surface expression and signaling bias between canonical GHSR transducers (Gαq and βarr2).11,15 In particular, the bulky hydrophobic ProH residue at ICL234.51 is a well established determinant of G protein subtype-selectivity across rhodopsin-like GPCRs.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 The GHSR-Leu14934.51 site lies within the short α-helix of ICL2—a structurally conserved motif that stabilizes G protein binding27 and is present in recent GHSR structures.13, 14, 15, 16 Mutagenesis of the ICL234.51 site in the Gαq-coupled 5-HT2A receptor completely biases signaling toward βarr.28 Despite this existing knowledge, the broader cellular/functional impacts and physiological relevance of ICL234.51 perturbation on GPCR expression, trafficking, and subtype-selective transducer engagement across G proteins, βarr’s, and GRK isoforms is lacking. In our recent companion preprint by Zhou et al,29 we estimate an appreciable frequency of rare genetic variation in the ProH motif of ICL2 across nonolfactory, rhodopsin-like GPCRs. Therein, we describe how a natural mutation in ProH motif of neurotensin receptor-1 biases receptor signaling between its canonical transducers—Gαq and βarr2—highlighting the ProH motif as a potentially conserved, biased allosteric “hotspot.”

In this study, we identify a rare missense variant in the ProH motif of the GHSR, Leu149Pro34.51, then characterize its biophysical and functional profile to uncover novel ICL2-dependent GPCR functions using a representative, naturalistic, rhodopsin-like GPCR model. We hypothesized that a natural, “helix-breaking” proline substitution in Leu14934.51 would perturb the conformational and spatial organization of the GHSR proximal to its transducer coupling interface, thereby pleiotropically dysregulating receptor expression, transducer engagement, and trafficking beyond well established effects on G protein coupling. To this end, by comparing the natural Leu149Pro34.51 (L149P) mutant to the wild-type (WT) GHSR across a series of computational and live-cell studies, we identify ICL234.51 as a critical regulator of GHSR conformational dynamics, subcellular distribution, trafficking, and signaling bias across multiple transducer classes, including G proteins, βarrs, and GRKs.

Materials and methods

Molecular dynamics simulations

All simulated systems were generated using the structure of the active-state GHSR [Protein Data Bank [PDB] code: 7NA7]. The refined structural model was obtained from GPCRdb. For simulations, we removed the bound ligand as well as the G protein. Protonation of residues was assigned using the Protonate3D module in Molecular Operating Environment (MOE). Internal waters were introduced using the homol-wat tool. The receptor was aligned to the membrane using the Orientations of Proteins (OPM) database. The generated complex was then embedded in a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) bilayer and solvated using transferable intermolecular potential 3-point (TIP3P) water using the Chemistry at HARvard Macromolecular Mechanics graphical user interface (CHARMM-GUI) server. The N-terminal (F38) and C-terminal (F340) ends were capped with ACE and CT3 patches, respectively.

Simulations were carried out similarly to previously published protocols30,31 with the ACEMD simulation package.32 Protein and lipid parameters were obtained from the CHARMM36m33 and CHARMM36,34 respectively. The systems were first relaxed for 200 ns under constant pressure and temperature with a time step of 4 fs and constraints applied to the protein backbone. Temperature was maintained at 310 K using the Langevin thermostat and pressure was kept at one bar using the Berendsen barostat. The equilibration run was followed by 3 × 1 μs production runs under constant volume and temperature, with a 4 fs time step. Simulation analysis was carried out in the Visual Molecular Dynamics package.35 For conformational and root mean square fluctuation analysis, simulation frames were aligned to the initial frame, utilizing the backbone.

Cell culture and transfections

HEK293/T-17 cells (Cat no. CRL-11268) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. HEK293/A parental (ThermoFisher) and their derivative CRISPR/Cas9-engineered GRK knockout (ΔGRK2/3/5/6, ΔGRK2/3, and ΔGRK5/6) were described previously.36 All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, ThermoFisher; Cat no. 11995073) supplemented with 10% febal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, ThermoFisher; Cat no. 26140095) and antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, ThermoFisher; Cat no. 15240062), referred to here as complete DMEM. Cells were cultured in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C and grown on coated tissue culture plates. All cell lines were routinely passaged using trypsin/EDTA (Gibco, ThermoFisher; Cat no. 25200114) and never exceeded >30 passages.

For all transfections, cells were first seeded at a density of 5 to 7.5 × 105 cells/well in a 6-well plate. Then 16–24 hours later, cells were transfected using a standard calcium phosphate precipitation method. Transfection reagents—CaCl2 (2.5M; Cat no. TBS5072) and HEPES-buffered saline (2xHBS, pH 7.05; Cat no. TBS5076)—were purchased from TriBioscience. As described, L149P was transfected at a 0.5:1 ratio relative to the WT-GHSR (typically, 50:100 ng, respectively) to adjust for total receptor expression, except when indicated otherwise. The dynamin-K44A (DynK44A), mVenus-hβarr1/2, and all GRKYFP plasmids were transfected at a mass of 1 μg, whereas TRUPATH sensors were all transfected at equal masses of 100 ng. Filler cDNA (pcDNA3.1+ or pcDNA5 for TRUPATH experiments) was used to bring the total transfection reaction mixture up to 2–3 μg. Six-to-eight hours after transfection, the media was exchanged for complete DMEM, then incubated overnight before 96-well plate seeding (described below). All cell counting and transfection efficiency measurements were performed using a Countess 3 FL automated cell counter (ThermoFisher; Cat no. AMQAF2000).

Plasmids and pharmacological reagents

Plasmid cDNA synthesis, cloning, and site-directed mutagenesis of the N-terminally hemagglutinin (3xHA)-tagged human GHSR-WT, GHSRL149P, GHSR-WT-RLuc8, and GHSRL149P-RLuc8 was performed by GenScript using the stock cDNA clone, Homo sapiens GHSR transcript variant 1a (Clone ID: OHu21750), as a template. All GHSR plasmids retained the native start codon (ATG) and were cloned into a pcDNA3.1(+) vector backbone. For empty vector (EV) control conditions throughout the study, pcDNA3.1(+) was used, with the exception of TRUPATH assays in which pcDNA5/TO was used as an EV control. The rat dynamin-1 dominant negative, DynK44A, was originally generated by the Caron Laboratory (Duke University) and described previously.37 The bystander bioluminescence resonance-energy transfer (bBRET) sensors mVenus-CAAX,38 MyrPalm-mVenus,5,39 and 2xFYVE-mVenus40 have been described previously. The N-terminally mVenus-tagged human hβarr1 (hβarr1) and hβarr2 (hβarr2) plasmids were a gift from Dr Lauren Slosky (University of Minnesota). All TRUPATH sensors were gifts from Dr Bryan Roth (Addgene Kit #1000000163). The mVenus-tagged mini-Gq plasmid was a gift from Dr Nevin Lambert (Augusta University). The dominant negative mutant, bovine GRK2-K220R, was a gift from Dr Robert Lefkowitz (Addgene plasmid #35403). The GRK2WT-eYFP41 and GRK5/6-eYFP42 plasmids were previously generated in the Caron Laboratory (Duke University) and described elsewhere (see References).

Human acyl-ghrelin (hGhrelin; Cat no. 1463), MK0677 (Cat no. 5272), Go6983 (Cat no. 2285), and Compound-101 (Cmp101; Cat no. 5642) were purchased from Tocris Bioscience. GRK2i (Cat no. HY-P1396A) was purchased from MedChemExpress. Pertussis toxin (PTX; Cat no. 23221) and FR900359 (Cat no. 33666) were purchased from Cayman Chemical. All peptide compound stocks were resuspended in 50% glycerol and 50% distilled H2O at a concentration of 1 mM, whereas all nonpeptide compound stocks were resuspended in 100% DMSO to a concentration of 10 mM for storage at −20 °C. For assays, all compounds were diluted in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; Gibco/ThermoFisher, Cat no. 14025092) supplemented with 20 mM HEPES, unless otherwise indicated.

On- and in-cell ELISA assays

ELISA assays were performed as previously described.5 Briefly, transfected cells were seeded on poly-D-lysine-coated 96-well plates at a density of ∼45,000 cells/well in phenol red-free opti-MEM (Gibco, ThermoFisher; Cat no. 11058021) supplemented with 2% FBS and antibiotic-antimycotic (“BRET media”). After overnight culture, BRET media was aspirated and serum-starved in opti-MEM (containing no FBS or antibiotic-antimycotic) for 2 to 4 hours before stimulation with vehicle (opti-MEM) or ghrelin (1 μM) for 45-minutes at 37 °C. Next, media was rapidly aspirated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15-minutes at room temperature (RT). Fixed cells were washed 3 times (∼5-minutes/wash) in PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco), then blocked for 1-hour in fish gelatin (Rockland Immunochemicals; Cat no. MB-067-0100). Cells were then probed with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-HA polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, Cat no. PA1-29751) for 1-hour at RT (1:2500). Next, cells were washed 3 times with PBS, then incubated with 1x SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (ThermoFisher; Cat no. 34577) for ∼5-minutes before measuring luminescence in a TriStar3 multimodal plate reader (Berthold Technologies). For in-cell ELISA assays, blocking buffer, antibody buffer, and PBS wash buffer contained 0.2% triton-X-100 to permeabilize the cells. For on-cell ELISA assays, triton-X-100 was omitted in order to label only surface receptors.

Flow cytometry

Briefly, HEK293T cells were transfected in 6-well plates with EV (pcDNA3.1(+)), 3xHA-GHSR-WT, or 3xHA-GHSRL149P (100 ng each) along with DynK44A (1 μg) or pcDNA3.1(+), then the final transfection mass brought up to 3 μg using filler pcDNA. After 8-hours, 4 mL of complete media (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotic/antimycotic) was added to the cells for incubation at 37 °C overnight. The following day, the cells were seeded onto 10 cm cell culture dishes and grown overnight. Next, the cells were serum-starved for 2-hours in 10 mL of opti-MEM before surface staining of the N-terminal HA epitope on the receptors. Cells were collected for each condition as a suspension in Ca2+/Mg2+-free Dulbecco's Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) following dissociation with Versene (Gibco; Cat no. 15040-066) and a centrifugation wash with Ca2+/Mg2+- free DPBS at 300 g. Next, ∼1 × 106 cells were stained with 0.5 μg of Alexa-Fluor 647 anti-HA antibody (BioLegend; Cat no. 682404, Clone 16B12) on ice without light for 30-minutes. After the antibody incubation, cells were washed with 1 mL of cold DPBS (Ca2+/Mg2+-free), and the pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of cold DPBS (Ca2+/Mg2+-free) and transferred to flow cytometry tubes on ice. The cell suspension was kept on ice until analysis in Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) using full spectrum mode at the Pennsylvania State University Huck Flow Cytometry Facility. Flow cytometry data analysis was performed using Kaluza software (Beckman Coulter).

Bioluminescence resonance-energy transfer

All bBRET and BRET1/BRET2 assays were performed as previously described.5 Briefly, transfected cells were seeded on poly-D-lysine-coated 96-well plates at a density of 40–45,000 cells/well in BRET media. After overnight culture, cells were serum-starved in HBSS supplemented with 20 mM HEPES for 2–4 hours at 37 °C. Then, cells were either pretreated with inhibitors for 30-minutes at RT or proceeded immediately to agonist treatment. Five- to ten-minutes before agonist treatment, cells were incubated with coelenterazine-H (NanoLight Technology; Cat no. 301) for BRET1 (mVenus/YFP) or coelenterazine-400a (NanoLight; Cat no. 340) for BRET2 (GFP2) based on manufacturer instructions. All BRET1/2 measurements were taken on a TriStar3 plate reader at RT (22–25 °C), whereas bBRET measurements were taken at 37 °C, using the manufacturer-recommended filter settings. All “basal” activity measurements represent the maximal net BRET (acceptor/donor) of receptor-alone conditions (ie, nonnormalized/corrected); whereas, all “constitutive” activity measurements represent the maximal difference between receptor-alone net BRET minus pcDNA/EV-alone net BRET to derive Δ net BRET—both conditions contained the same mass of BRET acceptor/biosensor transfected. All agonist-stimulated response data represent the maximal difference in agonist-stimulated net BRET (at each ligand concentration) minus vehicle-treated/receptor-alone net BRET to derive Δ net BRET (termed “agonist response”). To control for time-dependent effects, all Δ net BRET measurements (constitutive and agonist-stimulated) were calculated/normalized relative to its respective control within the same column on a 96-well plate.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using the Maestro platform (Schrödinger). Structures of the hGHSR1a with PDB codes 9UY3 and 7NA7 were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. The complexes were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard. The Leu149Pro mutant was generated by the “Mutate Residues” function in Maestro. To assess individual residue contribution to receptor-Gα interactions, other receptor residues were removed while keeping Leu149 or Pro149. Receptor grids were generated through the Glide Receptor Grid Generation function and centered on Leu149 with the grid size approximating the size of Leu149. Docking simulations were then performed using Glide’s Ligand Docking module, with the receptor grid and Leu149/Pro149 ligands as inputs. The docking protocol used “None (score in place only)” ligand sampling in standard precision mode.

Statistics and data analysis

As indicated in the figure legends, all data in the manuscript are presented as the pooled mean ± SEM derived from ≥3 independent experiments (ie, ≥3 biological replicates). For each independent experiment, ≥2 technical replicates were included, as indicated in the figure legends. Within each individual experiment, technical replicates were averaged, then the individual experiment averages were pooled into the final dataset for the graphical presentation and statistical analysis shown in the main and Supplemental figures; thus, all statistical tests were performed on the pooled, experiment-level means ± SEM. All data were plotted and analyzed in GraphPad Prism version-10 with a statistical significance threshold defined as the following: ns, P > .05; ∗, P < .05; ∗∗, P < .01; ∗∗∗, P < .001; ∗∗∗∗, P < .0001. All post hoc, multiple comparisons corrections controlled the family-wise error rate. Tukey’s post hoc test was used when performing all pairwise comparisons. Holm-Sidak’s post hoc test was used when comparing independent experimental groups of unequal sample sizes to a single, predefined control condition. Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used when two-way ANOVA showed no statistically significant interaction effect or when preselected pairwise comparisons were made between conditions. Unless otherwise indicated, all data from each experimental condition represent the maximal biological response (eg, net BRET, Δ net BRET, luminescent counts, etc) measured over the complete assay duration. When indicated, data are normalized to the WT control mean (defining 100%), and in some cases, also to an EV (pcDNA) control condition (defining 0%). All concentration-response (C/R) curves were fit by a 3- or 4-parameter nonlinear regression based on the statistically preferred model determined by an Extra Sum-of-Squares F-test (ie, 4-parameter fit used when P < .05). Additionally, all C/R curves were normalized to the WT control condition Top/Emax (100%) and bottom (0%) within each individual experiment, then technical replicate averages were pooled into the final data set for graphical presentation and statistical analysis. All data (mean ± SEM) and statistical comparisons are presented in the results, main tables, figure legends, or Supplemental Material.

Results

The rare missense variant, GHSR-L149P34.51, destabilizes the α-helical conformation of ICL2

The ProH motif residues GHSR-Pro14834.50 and GHSR-Leu14934.51 within ICL2 are key determinants of GHSR surface expression and signaling bias.11,15 To determine if naturally-occurring variants exist in either of these conserved residues, we searched public genomic databases (National Center for Biotechnology Information Database for Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms; build 157, released September 3, 2024; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/rs1390872592) and identified an ultrarare nonsynonymous (missense) coding variant in GHSR-Leu149, Leu149>Pro (rs1390872592; reported allele frequency = 1/595,518; Fig. 1A; https://gpcrdb.org/protein/ghsr_human/). We did not identify natural variants in the upstream Pro14834.50 site, perhaps because of its high conservation across rhodopsin-like GPCRs.43

The GHSR-Leu14934.51 residue is located toward the N-terminus of ICL2; thus, a Leu149>Pro mutation could destabilize the backbone and disrupt the hydrogen bonding network essential for ICL2 α-helix formation44—a process required for G protein signaling.20,45,46 To test this, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of both the WT-GHSR and L149P mutant, accumulating 1 μs of simulation time for each system across 3 independent replicates. The simulations were initiated from the active-state structure of GHSR [PDB code: 7NA716], in which ICL2 adopts an α-helical conformation (Fig. 1B). Although ICL2 maintained a stable α-helix throughout the WT-GHSR simulations, the L149P mutant exhibited increased flexibility and progressively lost its helical structure (Fig. 1, C and D; Supplemental Fig. 1). Root mean square fluctuation of the Cα atoms in ICL2 was elevated in the L149P mutant relative to the WT-GHSR, indicating increased loop flexibility (Fig. 1E).

The L149P mutation increases GHSR surface expression by reducing constitutive, dynamin-dependent receptor trafficking

To begin functional profiling of the natural L149P mutant, we first evaluated GHSR surface expression and constitutive internalization. Previously characterized, nonnatural GHSR-Leu149ICL2 mutations (eg, L149G) display increased surface expression and lack Gαq signaling11,15; however, the underlying mechanism(s) are unknown. We hypothesized that the natural L149P mutation would increase GHSR surface expression via reduced constitutive internalization.

To test this, we transiently coexpressed N-terminally 3xHA-tagged L149P or WT-GHSR receptors with the dynamin dominant negative and endocytosis inhibitor DynK44A or pcDNA, then measured GHSR surface expression (Fig. 2A) using on-cell ELISA in fixed, nonpermeabilized HEK293/T cells. The pcDNA/EV control condition was included to define background (ie, 0% expression). Consistent with our hypothesis, the surface expression of L149P-pcDNA was >2-fold higher than WT-pcDNA (Fig. 2B) and DynK44A coexpression increased the surface expression of both WT-GHSR and L149P relative to their respective pcDNA controls (Fig. 2B), supporting that both receptors undergo constitutive, dynamin-dependent internalization. Notably, WT-DynK44A surface expression was comparable to L149P-pcDNA surface expression (Fig. 2B), suggesting that the difference in baseline surface expression between receptors is related to reduced constitutive trafficking of the L149P mutant. To quantify this, we normalized WT-DynK44A and L149P-DynK44A surface expression to their respective pcDNA controls, then expressed the data as percent increase in surface expression upon DynK44A coexpression. These analyses supported quantitatively that constitutive WT-GHSR internalization was ∼3.5-fold higher than the L149P mutant (Fig. 2C).

In the same on-cell ELISA experiments, we stimulated WT ± DynK44A- or L149P ± DynK44A-expressing cells with hGhrelin (ghrelin) to evaluate agonist-induced GHSR internalization. A reduction in receptor surface expression upon ghrelin treatment relative to respective vehicle-treated controls is indicative of receptor internalization. The vehicle-adjusted magnitude of ghrelin-induced internalization was comparable between the WT and L149P receptors when coexpressed with pcDNA (∼25% reduction in surface expression); whereas, DynK44A coexpression completely blocked ghrelin-induced internalization of both WT and L149P, indicating that both receptors undergo similar levels of dynamin-dependent, agonist-induced internalization (Fig. 2D).

As an orthogonal approach, we used flow cytometry to confirm both (1) increased baseline surface expression and (2) reduced dynamin-dependent constitutive trafficking of the L149P mutant. We transiently coexpressed DynK44A or pcDNA with N-terminally 3xHA-tagged WT-GHSR and L149P receptors, or EV/pcDNA to define background, then measured surface expression using nonfixed, nonpermeabilized HEK293/T cells (Supplemental Fig. 2, A and B). As predicted, L149P-pcDNA displayed a >2-fold increase in baseline surface expression relative to WT-pcDNA (Supplemental Fig. 2C). Furthermore, DynK44A coexpression markedly increased WT-GHSR surface expression but had a minimal effect on L149P when normalized to each receptor’s respective pcDNA control (Supplemental Fig. 2D), indicative of markedly reduced dynamin-dependent internalization of the L149P mutant. These flow cytometry findings align with our ELISA-based results (see Fig. 2, B and C) and further support that the L149P mutation increases GHSR surface expression by reducing constitutive GHSR trafficking.

We then determined total receptor expression levels of the WT and L149P receptors by in-cell ELISA using permeabilized HEK293/T cells transiently expressing L149P at equal (1:1) or estimated surface expression-adjusted (1:2) masses relative to the WT-GHSR. Expression of L149P:WT at a 1:2 ratio produced similar total receptor expression levels to the WT-GHSR; whereas, a 1:1 ratio of L149P:WT produced total L149P expression levels >2-fold higher than the WT-GHSR (Fig. 2E), similar to baseline surface expression differences (see Fig. 2B). Thus, to achieve comparable total expression levels in subsequent functional studies, we expressed the L149P mutant at a 1:2 ratio relative to the WT-GHSR.

Although dominant negative (DynK44A) overexpression could potentially alter receptor surface expression because of changes in protein loading, this approach was used to assess constitutive internalization under equilibrium conditions. To obviate this, we next evaluated real-time, live-cell constitutive trafficking of the WT-GHSR and L149P mutant using a bBRET approach. The WT and L149P receptors were C-terminally fused to RLuc8 and coexpressed with mVenus-tagged subcellular markers at total expression-adjusted masses (2:1), including: (1) the plasma membrane (PM) sensor CAAX derived from the prenylation CAAX box of KRAS with an upstream polybasic sequence, targeted preferentially to nonlipid raft PM microdomains47; (2) the PM sensor myristoyl-palmitoyl (MyrPalm) derived from the N-terminal segment of Lyn kinase,39 targeted preferentially to cholesterol-rich, lipid raft PM microdomains47; and (3) the early endosome (EE) sensor 2xFYVE derived from hepatic growth factor-regulated tyrosine kinase substrate40 (Fig. 2F). We monitored basal (agonist-independent) bBRET between the WT or L149P receptors with each compartment-specific sensor over 2-hours, then calculated the area under the curve during predefined early (0–60 minutes) and late (65–120 minutes) phases of the total assay window. Compared with WT, the L149P mutant displayed increased basal CAAX-PM throughout the entire assay window, and both receptors steadily increased CAAX-PM bBRET throughout the 2-hours (Fig. 2G). In contrast, although L149P bBRET with MyrPalm-PM was increased in the early phase relative to WT, their signals converged in the late phase because of an apparent, but nonstatistically significant (P = .058), rise in WT-MyrPalm bBRET that was absent for L149P (Fig. 2H). Conversely, basal bBRET with 2xFYVE-EE did not differ between WT and L149P in the early phase; whereas, in the late phase, L149P bBRET with 2xFYVE-EE was reduced because of a continuous rise of WT-2xFYVE bBRET that was absent with the L149P mutant (Fig. 2I). Taken together, these results further strengthen our on-cell ELISA (see Fig. 2, B and C) and flow cytometry (see Supplemental Fig. 2, C and D) findings, which support that the L149P mutation increases GHSR surface expression by attenuating constitutive receptor internalization and endosomal trafficking. Furthermore, our bBRET results suggest that the L149P mutant may differentially partition or equilibrate into disordered, CAAX-positive microdomains relative to cholesterol-rich, lipid raft-like MyrPalm-positive PM microdomains. For data and statistics in Fig. 2, see Supplemental Table 1.

Differences in basal and agonist-stimulated βarr1/2 recruitment between the WT-GHSR and L149P mutant are isoform-specific and dependent on constitutive receptor trafficking

We previously showed that the nonnatural GHSR-L149G mutation produces βarr-biased signaling relative to Gαq.11,15 However, whether mutations in the GHSR-Leu14934.51 site differentially affect βarr recruitment in a βarr isoform-specific (βarr1 vs βarr2) manner remains unknown. Thus, we next used a BRET1-based approach to evaluate mVenus-tagged human βarr1/2 (hβarr1/2) recruitment to C-terminally RLuc8-tagged WT or L149P receptors under basal and agonist-stimulated conditions. Ligand-independent GHSR-βarr coupling is low at baseline48,49 and thus measurement of basal βarr recruitment to the GHSR can be variable when normalized to background. For this reason, we evaluated basal GHSR-βarr recruitment using the nonnormalized net BRET between the RLuc8-tagged GHSR and mVenus-tagged βarr at baseline, which exhibits minimal variability across independent experiments.

Basal, ligand-independent βarr1 recruitment (Fig. 3A) to the L149P mutant was similar to the WT-GHSR (Fig. 3B). C/R analysis of ghrelin-induced βarr1 recruitment showed that ghrelin potency (EC50) at L149P was comparable to the WT-GHSR, whereas maximal efficacy (Emax) was reduced by ∼52% at the L149P mutant (Fig. 3, C and D). Because of the saturable effect of GHSR-βarr1 BRET over the 1-hour measurement, we assessed βarr1 recruitment kinetics by fitting a one-phase exponential model to derive Ka as a proxy for GHSR-βarr1 association rate. Ghrelin (1 μM)-stimulated βarr1 association rate (Ka) was comparable between the WT and L149P receptors (Fig. 3E). Similar to ghrelin, the nonpeptide GHSR agonist MK0677 evoked L149P-βarr1 recruitment with equal potency, reduced efficacy (∼57%) (Supplemental Fig. 3, A and B), and similar βarr1 association rate kinetics (Supplemental Fig. 3C) relative to the WT-GHSR.

In contrast to βarr1, basal βarr2 recruitment (Fig. 3F) to L149P was reduced relative to the WT-GHSR (Fig. 3G), indicating a βarr isoform-selective effect on ligand-independent βarr2 recruitment. Although we cannot exclude residual differences in receptor expression upon coexpression with βarr1/2, we used constant cDNA ratios of receptor: βarr1/2 across experiments; thus, this finding supports a relative, but not absolute, isoform-selective effect of basal βarr2 recruitment to L149P compared with the WT-GHSR. Upon ghrelin stimulation, L149P-βarr2 recruitment was equipotent and efficacy was reduced by ∼33% relative to the WT-GHSR (Fig. 3, H and I), whereas ghrelin-stimulated βarr2 association rate was similar between the L149P and WT receptors (Fig. 3J). To test if (i) reduced basal L149P-βarr2 recruitment and/or (ii) reduced agonist efficacy of L149P-βarr1/2 recruitment was dependent on the differential surface expression or constitutive endocytosis of the L149P mutant relative to the WT-GHSR, we next coexpressed DynK44A or pcDNA, then measured mVenus-βarr1/2 recruitment to RLuc8-tagged WT or L149P receptors using the same BRET1 assay. Because MK0677 displayed a similar βarr1/2 recruitment profile relative to ghrelin at both the WT and L149P receptors (see Supplemental Fig. 3, A–F), only ghrelin was tested in these experiments. DynK44A coexpression did not affect basal βarr1 recruitment to either receptor (Supplemental Fig. 4A); whereas, DynK44A reduced ghrelin-stimulated βarr1 recruitment efficacy, but not potency, at both receptors (Supplemental Fig. 4, B and C). Notably, DynK44A coexpression with the WT-GHSR decreased ghrelin-induced WT-βarr1 recruitment efficacy to a level statistically similar to L149P-pcDNA (Supplemental Fig. 4, B and C). Additionally, the reduction in basal βarr2 recruitment to the L149P mutant at baseline (ie, when coexpressed with pcDNA) was unaffected by DynK44A, whereas basal βarr2 recruitment to the WT-GHSR upon coexpression with DynK44A was reduced to a level identical to L149P-pcDNA (Supplemental Fig. 4D). Similar to βarr1, ghrelin-stimulated βarr2 recruitment to both receptors was reduced by DynK44A coexpression and βarr2 recruitment in the WT-DynK44A condition was reduced to an efficacy statistically equivalent to L149P-pcDNA (Supplemental Fig. 4, E and F). Thus, decreased basal L149P-βarr2 recruitment and attenuated L149P-βarr1/2 agonist efficacy are phenocopied by the WT-GHSR upon DynK44A coexpression, suggesting that reduced constitutive L149P internalization likely accounts, at least in part, for its distinct basal βarr2 and agonist-stimulated βarr1/2 recruitment profile relative to the WT-GHSR. The L149P mutation abolishes agonist-induced Gαq heterotrimer activation but retains partial efficacy coupling to nucleotide-free mini-Gq

We next assessed if the L149P mutant engages the canonical G protein for the GHSR, Gαq.5 To first test proximal GHSR-Gαq interactions, we coexpressed mVenus-tagged mini-Gq with RLuc8-tagged WT or L149P and measured mini-Gq recruitment by BRET1 (Fig. 4A). The GHSR exhibits remarkably high constitutive activity in the Gαq pathway (≥50% ghrelin Emax48); thus, to provide a direct measure of constitutive Gαq activity, we included an EV control for normalization (ie, to derive receptor-mediated Δ net BRET relative to EV). From this point, we distinguished between basal and constitutive recruitment or activity based on whether an EV control was included for normalization (see Materials and Methods). Constitutive mini-Gq recruitment to the L149P mutant was reduced relative to WT (Supplemental Fig. 5A), but only by ∼25% (Fig. 4B). Upon agonist stimulation by ghrelin (Fig. 4C) or MK0677 (Supplemental Fig. 5B), the potency (pEC50) for mini-Gq recruitment to L149P was similar to the WT-GHSR (ghrelin: Fig. 4D; MK0677: Supplemental Fig. 5C) and agonist efficacy was reduced by ∼75% (ghrelin: Fig. 4E; MK0677: Supplemental Fig. 5D).

Next, we tested if the L149P mutant could activate the Gαq heterotrimer using the BRET2-based TRUPATH assay, which measures RLuc8-tagged Gαq dissociation from GFP2-tagged Gβγ subunits (GFP2 fused to the Gγ subunit) upon guanine nucleotide exchange (GDP>GTP)50 (Fig. 4F). Mini-G proteins mimic the nucleotide-free active-state of the G protein and removes the Gα’s membrane anchoring, Gβγ binding, and α-helical domain51; thus, a comparison of Gαq-Gβγ dissociation by TRUPATH to mini-Gq recruitment allows for the separation of nucleotide-dependent heterotrimeric G protein activation from direct, nucleotide-independent receptor-G protein coupling. The constitutive Gαq activity of L149P was decreased by ∼80% relative to the WT-GHSR (Fig. 4G), indicating markedly attenuated ligand-independent dissociation of the Gαq heterotrimer under basal conditions (Supplemental Fig. 5E). Furthermore, ghrelin- (Fig. 4, H and I) and MK0677- (Supplemental Fig. 5, F and G) stimulated Gαq activation at L149P was abolished, supporting that the L149P mutant is completely βarr-biased (see Fig. 3) relative to canonical GHSR-Gαq signaling, presumably by stabilizing a Gαq-inactive conformation of the agonist-bound GHSR. For data and statistics in Fig. 4, see Supplemental Table 4.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the L149P mutation reduces the number of competent, agonist-stabilized GHSR-Gαq complexes. As agonist-induced mini-Gq coupling inhibition was incomplete and Gαq dissociation was completely abolished, L149P may both reduce the favorability of Gαq nucleotide exchange, thus potentially acting as a guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitor (GDI), consistent with other ICL2 perturbations.21,52

G protein signaling at the L149P mutant is Gα subtype-specific

The GHSR preferentially couples to Gαq, but it also couples with lower intrinsic efficacy to alternate G proteins in the Gαi/o and Gα12/13 subfamilies—including Gαi2, GαoA, and Gα13.5 To our knowledge, the effect of GHSR-ProH mutations on G protein selectivity has not been investigated. Thus, we next tested if the natural L149P mutant activates noncanonical, GHSR coupling G proteins constitutively or upon ghrelin stimulation. Gα13 (Fig. 5A) constitutive activity was comparable, albeit highly variable, between the WT and L149P receptors (Fig. 5B); whereas, ghrelin-stimulated Gα13 activation was abolished at L149P (Fig. 5, C and D). Gαi2 (Fig. 5E) constitutive activity was reduced (Fig. 5F); however, in contrast to Gαq, ghrelin-stimulated Gαi2 activation was retained for L149P, albeit with lower efficacy compared with WT (∼60% decrease in ghrelin Emax; Fig. 5, G and H), indicating Gαi2 partial agonism at the L149P mutant. Similarly, GαoA (Fig. 5I) constitutive activity was markedly reduced at L149P (Fig. 5J); whereas, ghrelin-stimulated GαoA activation (Fig. 5, K and L) was reduced by only ∼40% at L149P relative to the WT-GHSR, further supporting that the L149P mutation shifts G protein selectivity toward the Gαi/o subfamily. These findings collectively support that the GHSR-Leu14934.51 site governs G protein subtype-selectivity and signaling bias across both the apo- and ghrelin-bound receptor states.

To gain mechanistic insight into how the L149P mutation affects GHSR coupling to Gαq, we next used molecular modeling to examine the cryogenic electron microscopy structure of the GHSR-Gαq complex (PDB: 9UY3), focusing on the local interactions between Leu14934.51 and the Gαq subunit; wherein, we introduced the L149P34.51 mutation in silico to evaluate its structural impact. In the WT-GHSR, L14934.51 is positioned within a hydrophobic pocket formed by Gαq residues L41, F376, C379, K380, and I383. In contrast, the L149P mutation introduces steric clashes between the γ and δ hydrogens of P149 and the γ hydrogen of K380 on Gαq (Fig. 5M). Docking analysis further quantified these effects, showing that the native L149 residue contributes a favorable interaction energy (WT docking score: −3.035) by fitting snugly into the Gαq pocket, whereas the L149P mutation results in a sterically unfavorable, positive docking score (L149P docking score: >0). These findings corroborate our functional data (see Fig. 4, H and I; Supplemental Fig. 5, F and G) and suggest that the L149P mutation disrupts Gαq activation via steric hindrance at the GHSR-Gαq interface. For data and statistics in Fig. 5, see Supplemental Table 5.

To investigate how the L149P mutant retains Gαi/o partial agonism/efficacy (see Fig. 5, G, h, K and L), we analyzed the Gαi-coupled GHSR structure (PDB: 7NA7) and again modeled the L149P mutation in silico. Both the WT-GHSR (L14934.51) and mutant (P14934.51) occupy a similar pocket on Gαi formed by residues L194, F336, V339, T340, and I343 (Fig. 5N). Docking analysis showed comparable interaction energies for the WT-L149 (docking score: −1.891) and mutant P149 (docking score: −2.433), indicating that, in contrast to GHSR-Gαq, the L149P mutation does not substantively alter the GHSR-Gαi interface. Thus, these findings suggest that the local environment of Gαi can accommodate the conformational constraint imposed by proline substitution at GHSR-L14934.51; and moreover, provides a biophysical mechanism to explain Gαi/o-biased signaling at the L149P mutant.

L149P recruits GRK2 and enhances basal GRK6 proximity

In addition to G proteins and βarr, GRKs act as transducers by converting GPCR active states into intracellular signaling cascades via receptor phosphorylation. Although recruitment of the GRK2/3 subclass is primarily G protein-mediated,53 including for the GHSR,54 βarr-biased ligands and receptors preferentially utilize GRK5/6 over GRK2/3.36,55 Based on this, we hypothesized that the L149P mutant would recruit both GRK2/3 and GRK5/6, whereas the WT-GHSR would exclusively recruit GRK2. To test this, we measured GRK2-YFP, GRK5-YFP, and GRK6-YFP recruitment to C-terminally RLuc8-tagged WT and L149P receptors under basal and ghrelin-stimulated conditions using BRET1. Starting with GRK2 (Fig. 6A), we found that basal GRK2 recruitment (Fig. 6B) and ghrelin potency (Fig. 6C) did not differ between WT and L149P receptors, whereas ghrelin Emax at the L149P mutant was moderately reduced relative to WT (Fig. 6D). For GRK5 (Fig. 6E), basal recruitment did not differ between WT and L149P receptors (Fig. 6F). Although ghrelin treatment did not appreciably increase GRK5 recruitment to either receptor up to a 1 μM concentration, there was a statistically significant difference in GRK5 recruitment between the L149P and WT receptors when analyzed by two-way ANOVA (Fig. 6G); however, this small main effect did not persist across ghrelin concentrations after post hoc analysis, supporting that it is not likely biologically meaningful. In contrast, basal GRK6 recruitment (Fig. 6E) to the L149P mutant was increased relative to WT (Fig. 6H), then remained elevated after ghrelin treatment (Fig. 6I). L149P requires both endogenous GRK2/3 and GRK5/6 expression for βarr recruitment

To determine whether the endogenous expression of GRK2/3 and/or GRK5/6 are required for βarr recruitment to L149P, we used HEK293/A cell lines lacking complements of GRK isoforms, including GRK2/3/5/6 (ΔGRK2/3/5/6), GRK2/3-only (ΔGRK2/3), or GRK5/6-only (ΔGRK5/6).36 βarr1 (Fig. 7A) basal recruitment to the WT-GHSR was not affected by loss of any GRK isoforms (Fig. 7B). Upon ghrelin stimulation, WT-βarr1 recruitment was abolished in ΔGRK2/3/5/6 and ΔGRK2/3 cells, whereas ghrelin efficacy was not affected in ΔGRK5/6 cells (Fig. 7, C and D), supporting that the GRK2/3 subclass is sufficient for βarr1 recruitment to the WT-GHSR. Basal βarr1 recruitment to the L149P mutant was also not affected by loss of any GRK subclasses (Fig. 7E); whereas, ghrelin-stimulated L149P-βarr1 recruitment was abolished in ΔGRK2/3/5/6 and modestly reduced in ΔGRK5/6 cells. Reliable analysis of ghrelin efficacy in L149P-βarr1 recruitment in ΔGRK2/3 cells was precluded by a poor curve-fit because of high background and low agonist efficacy (Fig. 7, F and G). When assessing βarr2 recruitment (Fig. 7H), we found that basal recruitment to the WT-GHSR was not affected by loss of any GRK isoforms (Fig. 7I), similar to βarr1 (see Fig. 7B). Ghrelin-stimulated WT-βarr2 recruitment was effectively abolished in ΔGRK2/3/5/6 and ΔGRK2/3 cells, whereas it was increased in ΔGRK5/6 cells (Fig. 7, J and K), further supporting that the WT-GHSR requires only endogenous GRK2/3 for βarr recruitment. We speculate that the increased WT-βarr2 recruitment observed in ΔGRK5/6 cells was due to a compensatory upregulation of GRK2/3, as observed elsewhere.36 At the L149P mutant, basal βarr2 recruitment was modestly, but statistically significantly increased in ΔGRK2/3/5/6 cells and otherwise not affected by loss of GRK2/3 or GRK5/6 (Fig. 7L). Conversely, ghrelin-stimulated L149P-βarr2 recruitment was abolished in ΔGRK2/3/5/6 cells, markedly decreased in ΔGRK2/3 cells (but not abolished), and modestly reduced in ΔGRK5/6 cells (Fig. 7, M and N). These results support that, in contrast to the WT-GHSR, the L149P mutant requires both endogenous GRK2/3 and GRK5/6 for βarr recruitment. For data and statistics in Fig. 7, see Supplemental Table 7.

βarr recruitment to L149P is independent of G protein-mediated second messenger kinases and Gαi/o, but it still requires upstream Gαq engagement and GRK2-mediated phosphorylation

Agonist-induced GHSR phosphorylation occurs upstream of βarr recruitment via GαqGTP-dependent PKC activation and Gβγ-dependent GRK2/3 translocation,54 with the latter mediated by free Gβγ interaction with the C-terminal pleckstrin homology domain of GRK2/3.53 Both PKC and GRK2/3 are required for βarr recruitment to the WT-GHSR15,54; thus, we assessed how the L149P mutant stimulates βarr1/2 recruitment despite a loss of agonist-induced Gαq activation (see Fig. 4, H and I).

We first assessed βarr1/2 recruitment in the presence and absence of the pan-PKC inhibitor (Go6983) and GRK2/3-selective inhibitor (Cmp101). In pilot studies, we observed that Go6983 nonspecifically increased BRET signals, presumably because of its coloration. To control for this when measuring constitutive βarr recruitment, we included a pcDNA/EV condition that also contained Go6983 pretreatment, as performed in G protein assays (see Figs. 4 and 5). At the WT-GHSR, Go6983-alone, Cmp101-alone, or Cmp101-plus-Go6983 did not affect constitutive βarr1 recruitment (Supplemental Fig. 6A). In contrast, Go6983-alone and Cmp101-alone reduced ghrelin-stimulated WT-βarr1 recruitment, and Go6983-plus-Cmp101 additively abolished WT-βarr1 recruitment efficacy. Ghrelin potency was not affected by any pretreatments (Supplemental Fig. 6, B and C). Constitutive L149P-βarr1 recruitment was also not affected by any pretreatments (Supplemental Fig. 6D). But in contrast to the WT-GHSR, Go6983-alone did not affect ghrelin-stimulated L149P-βarr1 recruitment; whereas, Cmp101-alone and Go6983-plus-Cmp101 reduced L149P-βarr1 recruitment efficacy similarly. Ghrelin potency was not affected by any pretreatments (Supplemental Fig. 6, E and F). When testing βarr2 recruitment, we found similar effects on both constitutive and ghrelin-induced recruitment to the WT-GHSR (Supplemental Fig. 6, G–I) and L149P (Supplemental Fig. 6, J–L), and we verified a βarr isoform-selective reduction in constitutive L149P-βarr2 recruitment relative to WT (∼65% decrease; Supplemental Fig. 6, G and K). To determine if βarr1/2 recruitment to the L149P mutant requires GRK2/3 catalytic activity or Gβγ-dependent GRK2/3 activation, we next coexpressed the phosphorylation-deficient GRK2 mutant, GRK2K220R, or pretreated cells with the Gβγ-GRK2/3 interfering peptide, GRK2i, then performed the same BRET1-based βarr1/2 recruitment assays. Basal WT-βarr1 recruitment was decreased by GRK2K220R, but not GRK2i (Supplemental Fig. 7A). Similarly, ghrelin-stimulated WT-βarr1 recruitment efficacy was markedly reduced by GRK2K220R and not affected by GRK2i pretreatment. Neither GRK2K220R nor GRK2i affected ghrelin potency at the WT-GHSR (Supplemental Fig. 7, B and C). At L149P, neither GRK2K220R nor GRK2i affected basal βarr1 recruitment (Supplemental Fig. 7D). Upon ghrelin stimulation, GRK2K220R markedly reduced L149P-βarr1 recruitment efficacy, whereas GRK2i had no effect, and ghrelin potency for L149P-βarr1 recruitment was not affected by GRK2K220R or GRK2i (Supplemental Fig. 7, E and F). When testing βarr2 recruitment, we found that GRK2K220R reduced basal WT-βarr2 recruitment (by ∼21%), whereas GRK2i showed no effect (Supplemental Fig. 7G). Ghrelin-stimulated WT-βarr2 recruitment efficacy was reduced by both GRK2K220R (57% decrease) or GRK2i (28% decrease), whereas ghrelin potency was unaffected (Supplemental Fig. 7, H and I), supporting that the WT-GHSR requires both GRK2-mediated phosphorylation and Gβγ-GRK2/3 interaction for ghrelin-stimulated βarr2 recruitment. At L149P, GRK2K220R coexpression, but not GRK2i pretreatment, moderately reduced basal L149P-βarr2 recruitment (by ∼8%; Supplemental Fig. 7J). Similarly, GRK2K220R reduced ghrelin-stimulated L149P-βarr2 recruitment efficacy, but notably, GRK2i had no effect at all; ghrelin potency was not affected in either condition (Supplemental Fig. 7, K and L). Together, these results support that basal and agonist-stimulated βarr1/2 recruitment to both the WT and L149P receptors require GRK2/3 catalytic activity (phosphorylation), but the L149P mutant does not require free Gβγ interaction with GRK2/3 for βarr2 recruitment, at least upon acute pharmacological inhibition. Considered with our PKC and GRK2/3 inhibition studies (see Table 1; Supplemental Fig. 6), these results support that L149P recruits βarr2 in GRK2/3 phosphorylation-dependent, but a G protein signaling (PKC- and Gβγ)-independent manner, consistent with its βarr-bias (see Figs. 3 and 4). Furthermore, GRK2K220R disproportionately reduced basal WT-βarr2 recruitment (∼21%) relative to L149P (∼8%), suggesting that reduced basal L149P-βarr2 recruitment could be due to lower GRK2-mediated GHSR phosphorylation at baseline. Discussion

In this study, we characterize an ultrarare missense variant in the GHSR and elucidate how the ICL234.51 residue within the conserved ProH motif regulates diverse GPCR functions beyond well established effects on G protein coupling.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 We demonstrate that the natural L149P34.51 mutation (i) destabilizes the α-helical conformation of ICL2; (ii) increases GHSR surface expression by reducing constitutive GHSR trafficking; (iii) biases transducer selectivity toward βarr1/2 and Gαi/o over Gαq and Gα13, despite the retention of some mini-Gq coupling; (iv) uses both endogenous GRK2/3 and GRK5/6 for βarr recruitment; and (v) promotes βarr1/2 recruitment via mechanisms largely independent of G protein-mediated second messenger kinases (Fig. 8). In parallel, our companion preprint by Zhou et al29 demonstrates that rare, natural variants in the ProH motif of the neurotensin receptor-1—a GPCR phylogenetically related to the GHSR—engenders biased signaling similar to the GHSR-L149P variant, highlighting the conservation and broader applicability of ICL2 regulation across rhodopsin-like GPCRs. Collectively, our results support that the ICL234.51 site within the ProH motif is an evolutionarily conserved control hub that integrates GPCR spatial distribution with transducer selectivity via the regulation of ICL2 conformational dynamics across both apo- and agonist-bound receptor states.

Model summarizing the functional effects of the rare, natural L149P34.51 mutation on GHSR structure/function. The ultrarare, natural L149P missense mutation in the evolutionary conserved GHSR-ProH34.51 site (teal, right) destabilizes the active-state α-helical conformation of ICL2, thereby increasing GHSR flexibility proximal to its transducer coupling interface. The L149P mutation increases GHSR surface expression by attenuating constitutive receptor internalization and trafficking to EEs, consistent with attenuated constitutive Gαq activity and reduced GRK2-sensitive basal βarr2 recruitment. The L149P mutation completely biases the GHSR toward βarr1/2 relative to canonical GHSR-Gαq signaling potentially by reducing guanine nucleotide exchange activity on Gαq, consistent with ICL2 α-helical destabilization. The L149P mutation also drives G protein selectivity bias toward Gαi/o over Gαq and Gα13 due to (i) the Leu149>Pro substitution causing a steric clash with Gαq-Lys380 and (ii) a reduced reliance of Gαi/o on stable ICL2 α-helices, thereby permitting L149P “tolerance” within the Gαi binding pocket. Consistent with abolished Gαq activation, βarr recruitment to the L149P mutant does not rely on canonical Gαq-dependent second messenger kinase activity for βarr recruitment—such as PKC or Gβγ-dependent GRK2 activation—or Gαi/o, although L149P does require upstream/proximal Gαq, suggesting that βarr interaction with the G protein-overlapping receptor core is important for βarr recruitment to the GHSR. In contrast to the WT-GHSR, βarr recruitment to L149P requires both endogenous GRK2/3 and GRK5/6, with the latter associated with increased L149P-GRK6 proximity at baseline. Thus, we speculate that L149P-GRK6 precoupling may contribute to receptor “priming,” such that the L149P mutant is constitutively shifted toward a βarr-favoring conformational state. As ICL2 forms a short α-helix in active-state GPCR-transducer complexes,45,58 we predicted that a “helix-breaking” proline substitution at Leu14934.51 would reduce ICL2 α-helical stability and thereby, disrupt intermolecular interactions critical to G protein activation (eg, with the upstream E/DRY motif)2 but not necessarily prevent βarr coupling.59 Indeed, MD simulations revealed that the L149P mutation disrupts stable ICL2 α-helices, thereby leading to an unfolding of ICL2’s α-helical secondary structure that is unfavorable for Gαq activation. The L149P mutant retained Gαi/o partial agonism despite a complete loss of agonist-induced Gαq and Gα13 activation, consistent with both (i) ICL2 perturbation causing G protein signaling bias across Gα subfamilies20,46,60 and (ii) Gαi/o requiring less ICL2 α-helical stability than other G proteins, such as Gαq46 and Gαs.20 In addition to increased loop flexibility, our molecular docking analyses revealed that the GHSR-Leu149>Pro substitution induces a steric clash between the GHSR and Gαq-Lys380, whereas the L149P mutation is tolerated within the GHSR-Gαi coupling interface, thereby providing a molecular explanation for a shift toward Gαi/o-biased L149P signaling relative to canonical GHSR-mediated Gαq signaling. Despite a complete lack of Gαq activation, L149P retained high-potency/low-efficacy mini-Gq recruitment, in accordance with the observation that ICL2 mutations can prevent guanine nucleotide exchange (GDP>GTP) without precluding G protein coupling.46 Thus, we predict that the L149P mutation abolishes canonical GHSR-Gαq signaling principally by inhibiting the hallmark conformational transitions required for guanine nucleotide exchange and therefore, likely acts as a GDI for Gαq. In support of this, alanine substitutions at ICL234.51 of the β2 adrenergic receptor (β2AR-F13934.51)21 or M3 muscarinic receptor (M3R-L17434.51)22 retain coupling to their cognate G proteins (Gαs and Gαq, respectively) but are unable to drive GDP release and G protein activation.

In addition to effects on transducer selectivity and signaling bias, we showed using parallel fixed-cell ELISA, flow cytometry, and bBRET approaches that the L149P mutation increases baseline GHSR surface expression in a dynamin-dependent and PM microdomain-dependent manner, supporting that basal ICL2 conformational dynamics influence GHSR subcellular distribution and compartmentalization via the regulation of constitutive receptor trafficking. Consistent with this observation, the L149P mutation markedly reduced both constitutive Gαq activity and GRK2-sensitive basal βarr2 recruitment, indicating that the L149P mutation lowers the intrinsically high constitutive activity of the GHSR—a process important for the age-dependent metabolic effects of GHSR signaling in vivo.61 Therefore, reduced constitutive L149P internalization and enhanced L149P surface expression are likely due to decreased (i) constitutive GHSR activity and/or (ii) basal βarr2-dependent GHSR endocytosis. In support of this hypothesis, both enhanced L149P surface expression and attenuated basal L149P-βarr2 recruitment were phenocopied by the WT-GHSR upon inhibition of constitutive internalization by DynK44A. These experiments also showed that reduced L149P-βarr agonist efficacy was mimicked by the WT-GHSR upon coexpression with DynK44A, suggesting that βarr recruitment to the GHSR is location- and/or endocytosis-dependent, consistent with recent findings using the angiotensin II-type 1 receptor.62

We found that the L149P mutant requires both endogenous GRK2/3 and GRK5/6 for βarr recruitment, whereas the WT-GHSR requires only endogenous GRK2/3, consistent with other βarr-biased ligands (eg, TRV-027 for angiotensin II-type 1 receptor)36 and βarr-biased receptors (eg, atypical chemokine receptor, CXCR7/ACKR3).55 Additionally, the L149P mutant exhibits elevated, ligand-independent GRK6 preassociation and sustained GRK5/6 proximity after agonist stimulation relative to the WT-GHSR, which we speculate may prime the L149P mutant for βarr-bias. We note however that BRET-based measurements of GRK5/6-GHSR proximity cannot reliably report absolute recruitment events or could be biased by overexpression; thus, these findings should be interpreted strictly as a relative difference between the WT and L149P receptors. As GRK5/6 is membrane-anchored, it is plausible that the L149P mutant disproportionately recruits, or has access to, GRK5/6 because of its increased surface expression and/or potentially differential membrane microdomain partitioning relative to the WT-GHSR. But upon agonist stimulation, our results strongly support that βarr recruitment to both the WT and L149P receptors is driven primarily by GRK2/3, whereas GRK5/6 plays a modest secondary or supportive role for the L149P mutant. These results suggest that the L149P mutant may obtain a unique phosphorylation “barcode” on the GHSR C-terminus or ICL3. However, phosphorylation was not measured directly in this study; thus, we cannot rule out the possibility that distinct engagement of the βarr finger loop region—rather than distinct C-tail/ICL3 phosphorylation signatures—could account for the differences in βarr recruitment patterns between the WT-GHSR and L149P mutant.

Despite reduced G protein signaling, the L149P mutant retains βarr1/2 recruitment efficacy, supporting that G protein- and βarr-coupled GPCRs have, at least partially, distinct and dissociable molecular determinants. Consistent with this, βarr1/2 recruitment to the WT-GHSR requires G protein-dependent second messenger kinase activation, including both PKC and Gβγ-dependent GRK2/3 activation; whereas, L149P recruits βarr1/2 independently of PKC, Gβγ-dependent GRK2/3, and Gαi/o. Furthermore, L149P requires endogenous GRK2/3 expression and GRK2/3 catalytic activity (ie, phosphorylation) to recruit βarr1/2, and it also directly recruits GRK2 with nearly equivalent efficacy to the WT-GHSR, supporting that GRK2/3 translocation to the PM is likely required for L149P-βarr recruitment. Thus, the L149P mutant might utilize a noncanonical, G protein-independent GRK2/3 recruitment mechanism. However, we note that acute pharmacological inhibition of Gβγ-GRK2/3 interactions with GRK2i might not be sufficiently sensitive or prolonged to detect effects of βarr recruitment to the L149P mutant relative to the WT-GHSR. In future studies, extended Gβγ scavenging may be required to substantiate Gβγ-independent L149P-βarr recruitment. Moreover, we found that the Gαq/11/14-selective GDI—FR900359—reduced L149P-βarr recruitment, which apparently contradicts a G protein-independent mechanism of L149P-βarr recruitment. We hypothesize that this discrepancy could be due to (i) L149P-mediated activation of Gα11 or Gα14, which was not measured in this study or (ii) FR900359-mediated disruption of proximal/structural interactions between L149P and inactive Gαq required for βarr coupling. Or alternatively, FR900359 could prolong or stabilize the inactive GHSR-Gαq complex and thereby sterically occlude βarr coupling to the receptor core/TM bundle, which would suggest that both the WT-GHSR and L149P utilize a “core-engagement” mode of βarr interaction.

Although the clinical relevance of this ultrarare GHSR variant (rs1390872592) is currently unknown, our findings underscore how single amino acid mutations can confer dramatic, pleiotropic changes to GPCR structure/function. Given the conservation of the ICL234.51 residue within the ProH motif, it is conceivable that rare ProH variants in other rhodopsin-like GPCRs could produce meaningful pathophysiological effects. In a physiological context, we predict based on prior literature8 that L149P variant carriers might exhibit reduced calorie intake, body weight, blood glucose, growth hormone secretion, and/or sensitivity to GHSR agonists (eg, MK0677 and anamorelin) due to an abrogation of GHSR-Gαq signaling. Whether GHSR-mediated signaling bias toward βarr or Gαi/o would influence specific physiological effects remains to be determined, as the contribution of these pathways to GHSR physiology is not well understood.8 In conclusion, we propose that the ICL234.51 site within the conserved ProH motif is a biased allosteric “hotspot” and thus, provides a structural landmark to rationally design biased allosteric ligands, which could improve therapeutic efficacy with fewer side effects.63
