When the U Wave Tells the Story: Andersen–Tawil Syndrome Unmasked
Authors: Shasha Yu, Hang Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 26‐year‐old woman with recurrent syncope was diagnosed with Andersen‐Tawil syndrome (ATS) following abnormal electrocardiographic (ECG) findings. Her history of ventricular arrhythmias and prominent U waves on ECG led to genetic testing, which identified a KCNJ2 mutation. This case underscores the importance of recognizing ATS‐specific ECG patterns, including broad U waves, in diagnosing arrhythmias. Following medication adjustments, her symptoms improved, highlighting the significance of tailored treatment for ATS‐associated arrhythmias.Keywords: Andersen‐Tawil syndrome, arrhythmias, syncope, torsades de pointes, U waves

Case Report

A 26‐year‐old woman presented with four episodes of syncope within a day. Two weeks earlier, she was diagnosed with frequent ventricular premature beats and underwent radiofrequency ablation, followed by amiodarone therapy (200 mg/day). On admission, the patient's height was 151 cm, weight 38.8 kg, with a BMI of 17. Physical examination was notable for scoliosis, but other vital signs were normal. Electrolytes, including potassium and calcium, were within normal ranges. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal cardiac structure and function. A 12‐lead ECG (Figure 1A) and Holter monitoring (Figure 1B) were performed on admission. What are the key findings of the ECG? How does the striking ECG feature inform our diagnosis and guide treatment decisions?

12‐lead ECG and Holter monitoring on admission. (A) The 12‐lead ECG on admission shows sinus rhythm with ventricular bigeminy, and prominent U waves, particularly in leads V1 to V4. (B) Holter monitoring performed on admission revealed frequent ventricular premature beats and captured multiple short runs of non‐sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT), some of which manifested as torsades de pointes.

The 12‐lead ECG (Figure 1A) revealed sinus rhythm with frequent ventricular premature contractions occurring in bigeminy. Notably, prominent U waves were observed in leads V1 to V4 (blue arrows), with amplitudes exceeding the height of the T wave and measuring greater than 0.5 mV (normal U wave amplitude is typically < 0.2 mV). Additionally, the U wave had a broad morphology, with a width of approximately 260 ms (normal U wave duration is generally < 200 ms (Surawicz 1998)). The interval from the peak of the T wave to the peak of the U wave (TpUp) measured 150 ms. The corrected QT interval (calculated using the Fridericia formula) was normal at approximately 450 ms. However, the corrected QT(U) interval extended to nearly 670 ms, highlighting a significant abnormality in ventricular repolarization.

Continuous 24‐h Holter monitoring (Figure 1B) demonstrated the presence of large, broad U waves throughout the entire recording, regardless of heart rate. These findings were consistent with those observed on the initial 12‐lead ECG. Frequent polymorphic ventricular premature beats were recorded, totaling 24,234 episodes over 24 h. Multiple episodes of non‐sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) were detected, some of which exhibited torsades de pointes (TdP). Although these arrhythmic episodes were brief and self‐limiting, they strongly suggested that the patient's syncopal episodes were likely due to malignant ventricular arrhythmias.

Upon admission, amiodarone was immediately discontinued. Follow‐up ECGs continued to show prominent U waves and a prolonged QTU interval, but the patient did not experience any further syncope. Repeat Holter monitoring revealed that no further episodes of TdP were observed. To further investigate the underlying cause, genetic testing was performed, which identified a V302M mutation in the KCNJ2 gene, consistent with a diagnosis of Andersen–Tawil syndrome (ATS). This syndrome is known to cause both ventricular arrhythmias and distinct electrocardiographic abnormalities, including the broad and prominent U waves observed in this patient.

Discussion

The U wave, although often overlooked, plays a significant role in the diagnosis of various cardiac and metabolic disorders. Elevated U waves may be observed in conditions such as hypokalemia, hypercalcemia, myocardial ischemia, or infarction, as well as in patients taking class IC or III antiarrhythmic drugs and antipsychotic medications. In this case, despite the patient's history of amiodarone use, known to cause elevated U waves, no other contributing factors were identified. Notably, after discontinuing amiodarone, the U waves remained large and prominent on subsequent ECGs. The persistence of these prominent U waves, along with the patient's characteristic QTU prolongation, short stature, and scoliosis, led to the consideration of ATS. Ultimately, genetic testing confirmed the presence of the mutation in the KCNJ2 gene, solidifying the diagnosis of ATS.

ATS is a rare genetic channelopathy characterized by a triad of episodic flaccid muscle weakness (periodic paralysis), ventricular arrhythmia with prolonged QTU interval, and skeletal anomalies, like low‐set ears, widely spaced eyes, small mandible, fifth‐digit clinodactyly, syndactyly, short stature, and scoliosis. (Pérez‐Riera et al. 2021) In approximately 70% of cases, ATS is caused by mutations in the KCNJ2 gene, which encodes the Kir2.1 inward‐rectifying potassium channel that alters late cardiac repolarization and leads to both distinctive T‐U wave morphology and an increased propensity to ventricular arrhythmias (Delannoy et al. 2013). Previously, ATS was classified as LQTS type 7. However, we observed that it is the QTU interval, rather than the QT interval, that is typically prolonged. In ATS, the QT interval is usually normal or only slightly prolonged, with characteristic ECG findings centered on the U wave. These often include prominent (large, broad) U waves and prolonged QTU intervals, with the U wave appearing relatively independent of the T wave (Horigome et al. 2020). A prolonged Tp‐U interval results in a distinct separation between the T and U waves, creating a wide T‐U junction, as observed in our patient. Despite a high arrhythmia burden, patients with ATS rarely experience syncope or cardiac arrest (Chun et al. 2004). In this case, the patient had a long history of palpitations but had never experienced syncope. Post‐radiofrequency ablation, the patient's Holter monitor showed a significant reduction in the ventricular ectopy burden. However, 2 weeks later, the patient began experiencing recurrent syncope, raising concerns that the use of amiodarone post‐ablation may have contributed to this adverse outcome. Amiodarone blocks the delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr), which is crucial for cardiac repolarization. In patients with pre‐existing large and broad U waves, the use of amiodarone further impairs the heart's ability to repolarize efficiently after an action potential. This results in prolonged action potential duration (APD) and a reduced “repolarization reserve,” increasing the susceptibility to TdP. The management of arrhythmias in ATS is challenging, with no clearly established effective treatments. Pharmacological options, such as beta‐blockers and sodium channel blockers (e.g., mexiletine and flecainide), are often used, although supporting data for their efficacy remain limited. A key component of managing patients with ATS is the avoidance of QT‐prolonging drugs, particularly class IC agents, which are generally not recommended due to their increased risk of exacerbating malignant arrhythmias. For patients at high risk of sudden arrhythmic death, such as those with frequent or sustained ventricular arrhythmias, the implantation of a cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) is recommended to prevent life‐threatening events.

After discontinuation of amiodarone, the patient was started on oral metoprolol succinate (23.75 mg/day) and potassium–magnesium aspartate (420 mg/day). At 6‐month follow‐up, no further syncope occurred. This case offers several novel insights into Andersen–Tawil syndrome (ATS). First, the recurrence of syncope after radiofrequency ablation while on amiodarone illustrates how QT‐prolonging therapy can further reduce repolarization reserve in ATS and precipitate torsades de pointes (TdP)—an interaction rarely documented in genetically confirmed ATS. Second, quantitative electrocardiographic analysis—particularly Tp–Up and QTU—highlights that the primary abnormality resides in the U wave rather than the QT interval, underscoring the diagnostic value of systematic U‐wave assessment. Third, the contrast between a long‐standing ectopic burden without syncope and abrupt malignant events after an external trigger emphasizes the dynamic, trigger‐dependent nature of arrhythmic risk in ATS, with practical implications for management. Taken together, these observations stress the importance of recognizing the often‐overlooked U wave: accurate identification of these characteristic manifestations enables earlier diagnosis and guides therapy, and different choices may lead to markedly different outcomes.
