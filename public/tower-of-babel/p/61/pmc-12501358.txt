Susceptibility mechanisms for analyzing depressive symptoms in differently disadvantaged children from an integrative perspective: a network analysis and network comparison
Authors: Wei-Liang Wang, Qiu Li, Tang-Rong Bao, Zhao-Ling He, Tian-Hua Wu, Lu-Yao Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to investigate the network structure of depressive symptoms in disadvantaged children and adolescents and the network differences between them and non-disadvantaged individuals. We classified disadvantaged children from an integrated perspective, taking full account of sociodemographic factors and environmental risks. Latent profile analysis was used to identify environmental risk categories. Network analysis was used to examine the interrelationships between 27 symptoms of the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) and to identify the core symptoms. We evaluated network structure and global strength between different groups. If the difference in network structure was significant, the edge (connection) strength was evaluated. A total of 4574 children completed the survey. Combined with the results of the latent profile analysis, disadvantaged children were classified into four categories as follows: the socially demographic disadvantaged group, the environmental risk group, the doubly disadvantaged group and the non-disadvantaged group. There was a significant difference in the incidence of depression between the different groups, with the lowest incidence in the non-disadvantaged group (15.8%) and the highest incidence in the doubly disadvantaged group (52.72%). There was a significant difference between the network structure of depressive symptoms in the doubly disadvantaged and non-disadvantaged groups, with specific differential edges centered on the entries for self-esteem and interpersonal relationships. The global connectivity of the network was similar for the different groups. The results revealed that children with disadvantages have a greater incidence of depression. The core node of the depressive symptom network focuses on negative emotions. The susceptibility characteristics of depressive symptom networks focused on network structure, including specific edges of “self-esteem” and “relationships”, rather than the global connectivity of the network.

Introduction

Disadvantaged children refer to children who are in a relatively difficult position in terms of social status, protection of rights and interests, and financial situation, such as those in single-parent families, children left behind and children living in poverty. The lack of effective social resources and coping skills in a disadvantaged upbringing has resulted in varying degrees of developmental difficulties. Disadvantaged children generally have a significantly greater risk of depression than other children [1]. Fellmeth et al. [2] conducted a meta-analysis of the mental health outcomes of 264,967 children (106,167 left-behind children and 158,800 non-left-behind children) in 111 studies, 91 of whom were from China, and showed that parental mobility had a significant impact on children’s mental health, with left-behind children having higher depression scores and a greater risk of depression than non-left-behind children. Disadvantaged developmental difficulties not only affect mental health in childhood but also have a significant impact on emotional and behavioral problems in adulthood [3]. However, there is a lack of clarity regarding the mechanisms of susceptibility to depression in disadvantaged children.

The traditional medical view of mental illness is based on the latent variable perspective, which suggests that the symptoms of mental illness are due to underlying common causes. However, with the development of clinical evidence, the “materialized” and “dimensionalized” view of psychiatric symptoms has been challenged, and Borsboom et al. proposed a network theory of psychopathology [4, 5], which argues that the traditional view ignores the interaction between symptoms and that the development and maintenance of psychiatric disorders is based on the causal interactions between symptoms within the disease. From the network theory perspective, depression is a network of interactive connections between multiple symptoms. Symptom network nodes are activated by external factors and activate the network through inter-symptom connections. If an individual’s symptom nodes are tightly connected to each other and the network nodes reinforce each other through feedback loops, the individual has a high susceptibility to depression, i.e., individual symptoms are easily transmitted to other symptoms, thus activating, and maintaining the entire depression network. Conversely, individuals whose symptoms are not strongly connected to each other have a lower susceptibility to depression, i.e., symptoms are not easily activated and are not easily transmitted to each other [5–7]. Research suggests that interventions targeting the core symptoms of depressive networks not only affect the network itself and alleviate symptoms of mental illness but also favor the improvement of peripheral symptoms in the network [8, 9]. Therefore, clarifying the network mechanisms of depressive symptoms may improve the effectiveness of interventions.

The network analysis model corresponding to network theory overcomes the limitations of traditional latent variable analysis by presenting the characteristics and information of a certain system in the form of a network and portraying the symptoms and structural characteristics of the system through the indicators of the centrality of nodes in the network and the connectivity of the network. In recent years, the network analysis method has been widely used in various fields of psychology and psychiatry research, in order to describe the structural characteristics of symptoms and the complex interactions among variables [10, 11]. A large body of research has focused on differences in the structural characteristics of depressive symptom networks across groups with different cultural backgrounds, gender, depression levels, and stages of disease progression to achieve effective interventions for depressive symptoms [12–16]. However, no studies have explored the mechanisms of depressive networks under different situational disadvantages based on a network analysis perspective. Therefore, the hypothesis of the present study was that the high incidence of depression in disadvantaged children may stem from their susceptibility to structural features of the depressive symptom network.

As children and adolescents develop environmental factors become increasingly complex. They start from a predominantly family-centered environment in early childhood and then there is a shift to a peer-, school-, and community-centered environment in late childhood and adolescence [17]. The greater the number of different environmental risk factors, the more likely adolescents are to experience a psychological crisis. A study focusing on the effects of overlapping home, school, and community risks showed that adolescents exposed to overlapping home, neighborhood, and school risks had poorer mental health for two years later than those experiencing a single environmental risk [18]. The Needs-Resources Model [19] suggests that risk and protective factors exist at different levels, such as family, school, and community, and that psychological crises are the result of the overlap or interaction of these factors on an individual’s psychological behavior. Therefore, this study was conducted from an integrative perspective, based on individual, family, and school factors, using latent profile analysis to explore the combination of risk and resource patterns and further explored the susceptibility mechanisms of children’ depression in the context of the risk of disadvantage based on network analysis.

Measurements

Demographic questionnaire: This questionnaire was compiled based on the purpose of the study and included age, gender, grade, and other sociodemographic data to assess the specific problems of disadvantaged children and adolescents (such as parents’ marital status, annual household income, parents’ length of work outside the home, home environment (house size, quality of decoration, geographical location, etc.).

Variable used in network analysis

Depression was measured by the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) [20]. The CDI consists of 27 self-reported questions that measure cognitive, emotional, and behavioral symptoms of depression in children, including the five dimensions of anhedonia, negative emotions, low self-esteem, feelings of inefficiency, and interpersonal problems. Each item on the scale was scored on a scale of 0, 1, or 2, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A total score greater than 19 was considered to indicate depression. The reliability and validity of the Chinese version have been verified in a number of studies [21]. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.86.

Variables used in latent profile analysis

The Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) [22, 23] includes 5 factors, namely, punishment, loss, interpersonal pressure, learning pressure and adaptation problems, which are used to assess the occurrence and impact of adolescent life events and include 27 items. The participants were asked whether the events presented in the questionnaire had happened to them or their families in the past 12 months. When an event occurred, it was selected according to its degree of impact, which was divided into 5 levels: 1 = no effect, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, 4 = severe, and 5 = extremely severe. In this study, there were 6 options: no occurrence (score 0) and impact degree after occurrence (score 1 ~ 5). The higher the score is, the greater the degree of stress caused by negative life events. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.93.

A revised version of the Friendship Quality Questionnaire (FQQ) was used to measure the friendship quality between adolescents and their best friends [24, 25]. The questionnaire included six dimensions, namely, affirmation and concern, help and guidance, companionship and entertainment, intimate disclosure and communication, conflict resolution strategies, and conflict and betrayal, with a total of 18 items, and uses a 5-point score, with 1 being “completely inconsistent” and 5 being “completely consistent”. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.89.

The Chinese version of the Family Resilience Rating Scale (FARS) revised by Li Yuli et al. was adopted to measure children’s family resilience level [26]. The scale consists of 32 items and assesses family resilience from three aspects: family communication and problem solving, the use of social and economic resources, and the family belief system. The questionnaire options ranged from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” on a scale ranging from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating higher levels of family resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.96.

The Parental Bonding Instrument (PBI) was used to evaluate the parenting style of children in families [27, 28]. The Chinese version of the scale contains 23 items, including three subscales, “caring”, “control” and “encouraging autonomy”, and each item adopts a 4-level scoring method. This study combined caring and encouraging autonomy scores into positive parenting and control scores into negative parenting. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.84 for fathers and 0.85 for mothers.

The Perceived Teachers’ Emotional Support Questionnaire (PTESQ) [29] consists of 18 questions. It is used to measure students’ perceived teachers’ emotional experience in four dimensions: understanding, caring, respect and encouragement. The scale items adopt a five-level scoring method, and the higher the score is, the greater the level of students’ understanding of teachers’ emotional support. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.96.

The Social Support Rating Scale for Children and Adolescents (SRS-CA) is a tool for assessing population-specific cultural adjustment based on the Social Support Rating Scale [30]. The scale has 10 items, including three dimensions: objective support, subjective support, and utilization of social support. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.75.

The Benevolent Childhood Experiences scale (BCEs scale) [31] is based on the theory of psychological resilience and measures experiences that may have a long-term positive impact on an individual. The BCEs include 10 items, including a safe parent, good friends, strong beliefs, a sense of belonging to school, caring teachers, close neighbors, support from other adults, opportunities to play, a good sense of self, and regular family schedules. In this study, the scale was used to assess positive resources in children’s past growth experiences. Each entry represents one positive experience, with a “1” point for presence and a “0” point for absence. The total score of the 10 items represents the total score of the BCEs scale of the subjects. The higher the total score is, the more positive experiences the subjects have experienced and the richer the resources that can support their healthy development. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.77.

Methods

Sample

The study participants were students from two township middle school in a northern city of China. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (XZHMU-2023029). The test was completed after informed consent was obtained from the students and their parents. All teachers participating in the survey received standardized training. During the survey, the main experimenter read out a unified instruction guide, requiring the subjects to complete the questionnaire independently and then collected it on site after the end of the survey.

In this study, disadvantaged children were classified based on two aspects: first, the sociodemographic background of disadvantaged children: ① single-parent families; ② left-behind children (both parents or one parent working outside for more than 6 months); ③ migrant children (who had been studying outside of their registered residence for more than 6 months); ④ children with low socioeconomic status were evaluated based on the following items in the demographic questionnaire: annual income of households (lower than the local average level was recorded as 1, higher than the average level was recorded as 0); the educational level of the parents (if the educational level of fathers and/or mothers was only primary school and below it was recorded as 1, and educational level above primary school was recorded as 0); the ownership of household appliances (“computers, TVs, air conditioners, refrigerators and washing machines”, if there were 0–1 kinds, it was recorded as 1, and if there were more than 1 kind, it was recorded as 0); and the subjective feeling of the home environment (a poor subjective feeling was recorded as 1, and the general and good subjective feelings were recorded as 0). The scores of the four conditions were added together and if the total score was greater than or equal to 3, the children were considered to be socially demographic disadvantaged.

The environmental risk for children was assessed by seven variables, namely, the Benevolent Childhood Experiences and Social Support Rating Scale for Children and Adolescents (individuals), the Parental Bonding Instrument and Family Resilience Rating Scale (families), the Perceived Teachers’ Emotional Support Questionnaire and Friendship Quality Questionnaire (schools), and Social Support for Children and Adolescents (Community). Patterns of heterogeneity in environmental risk were identified based on latent profile analysis (LPA). According to the above standard, disadvantaged children can be divided into four groups: socially demographic disadvantaged group; environmental risk group; doubly disadvantaged group; and non-disadvantaged group.

Statistical analyses

At the outset, descriptive statistics for demographic characteristics of the sample were computed using Stata software, version 16.0 for macOS. Continuous variables are presented as the mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The chi-square test was used to analyze differences in the incidence of depression between groups. RStudio for macOS (version 2022.02.3) was used to conduct the statistical analyses of LPA analysis, network estimation, network stability, and network comparison. All P values were 2-tailed, and 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

LPA was used to identify the potential classes of environmental risk. A series including the Akaike information criterion (AIC), sample size-adjusted Bayesian information criterion (saBIC), entropy and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were chosen to identify the optimal solution for LPA analysis. LPA analyses were performed using the R package “tidyLPA” (version 1.1.0) [32]. Our research considers the richness of the concept of disadvantageous circumstances, using demographic information to determine if there is social demographic disadvantage, and LPA to identify if there is environmental risk exposure disadvantage. Based on these two categories, the population is divided into four groups: solely socially demographic disadvantaged (without environmental disadvantage); solely environmental risk disadvantage (without social demographic disadvantage); dual disadvantage (both social demographic and environmental risk disadvantages); and non-disadvantaged (neither socially demographic nor environmentally disadvantaged).

Network estimation

The structure of the network is constructed by the correlation between the variables; the nodes represent the variables, i.e., the 27 entries of the CDI scale represent the 27 nodes in the network, the connecting lines between the nodes indicate a weighted correlation between 2 nodes after conditioning all of the other nodes in the network, and the darker and coarser colors of the connecting lines indicate stronger correlations [5]. The partial correlation network model was chosen to increase sensitivity (include few false-positive edges) while taking into account the specificity (identifying the strongest edges), and “threshold = “sig” was set to not show the thresholded edges that were not significant, which simplified the network and improved interpretability [33, 34]. Within this procedure, symptom networks in the whole sample and different groups were estimated. The R package “bootnet” (version 1.6) was utilized to complete this analysis [35].

Strength, as the most stable centrality index, was used to select the most important node in the network [36–38]. Strength was calculated by the sum of the absolute values of the edge weights connecting a specific node [39]. The “qgraph” package was used to estimate the strength centrality to construct a “centrality plot” [40].

Nonparametric bootstrapping methods were used to examine network stability. A case-dropping subset bootstrap with 1000 replicates was used to calculate correlation stability (CS) coefficients. Strength centrality ranges between 0 and 1, and higher values indicate greater stability. CS coefficients above 0.5 are preferred, whereas values below 0.25 are unstable and error prone [34, 35]. The bootnet package was used for the above analysis.

Network differences

Using the R “NetworkComparisonTest” package [41], a permutation test was used to test the significance of network structure and global strength among different disadvantaged classifications. If the omnibus network invariance test was significant, we proceeded with exploratory post hoc testing of individual edge strength. The differences were quantified using permutation tests with 1000 iterations.

Discussion

Childhood depression involve complex mechanisms that should be explored from an integrative perspective. Numerous factors influence the mental health of children, including sociodemographic factors and family, school, peer, and social environment factors, yet existing research has not sufficiently focused on children and adolescents in challenging environments [42]. In the present study, we used LPA to explore the potential categories of different disadvantaged children in a large sample from the joint model of demographically disadvantaged situation and environmental risks. We analyzed the symptom network of depression in different disadvantaged children for the first time from an innovative perspective to explore the facilitating mechanisms of depression in disadvantaged children.

According to ecosystem theory, children’s and adolescents’ family, school, and community factors are all cumulative risks that can lead to psychological crises. However, previous studies usually regard family, school, and community risks as independent factors and examine their relationships with adolescents’ psychological crises separately, which lacks the ecological validity of the complex model of the crisis-promoting mechanism, and makes it difficult to clarify whether there is a cumulative effect of psychological crises in combination with multiple environmental risk factors [43, 44]. This study takes into full consideration the demographic disadvantage and environmental risks from an integrated perspective. The overall incidence of depression in this study was similar to that of previous studies [45, 46]. The incidence of depression among children in the doubly disadvantaged group (52.72%) was significantly greater than that in the other groups, while the lowest incidence of depression among children and adolescents in the non-disadvantaged group was 15.8%, which supports our hypothesis. The incidence of depression was also significantly greater in the environmental risk group (44.91%) than in the demographically disadvantaged group (21.75%). The results of the study illustrate the validity of the disadvantaged categorization as well as the joint influence of the demographically disadvantaged situation and environmental risk on depression.

The core nodes of the overall sample depressive symptom network were “sadness”, “self-blame”, “irritability”, “self-hatred” and “loneliness”. Sadness, as a core node of depressive symptom networks, has been consistently reported across studies [13, 47] and was also validated across different disadvantaged groups in the present study. Self-blame and self-hatred, especially self-hatred, as core nodes of the depressive symptom network have also been consistently reported across studies [48, 49], which demonstrated the significant role self-worth plays in adolescent depression. Loneliness is also a core node reported more consistently across studies. It has been shown that low-income adolescents report more loneliness and that loneliness is most strongly associated with a network of anxiety-depressive symptoms [50]. Studies have used the CDI scale for the assessment of depressive symptoms in adolescents, and network analysis found that loneliness was a central factor in depression networks [14, 51, 52]. Research suggests that the core node of the adolescent depressive symptom network focuses on negative emotions, and irritability was also verified as a core node characteristic in this study. However, there is limited infomration on network analysis based on the CDI, and the central role of this node needs to be further verified.

Although socially demographic disadvantaged groups showed lower network stability, “negative emotions” remained the core node in their symptom networks, which is consistent with previous research [53]. From a methodological perspective, while node stability in cross-sectional networks has limitations, the overall network structure characteristics and global connectivity are more meaningful [54]. Our finding shown that only the doubly disadvantaged group and the non-disadvantaged group differed significantly in terms of network structure. This result suggested that the difference between specific sides of the two depressive symptom networks may be responsible for the susceptibility to depression [34, 41]. The results of invariance of the strength of edges test showed that the differences in edges between the networks in the doubly disadvantaged and non-disadvantaged groups were concentrated in the dimensions of “self-esteem” and “relationships”, which may be a potential reason for the high depressive vulnerability network in doubly disadvantaged children. In stressful situations, low self-esteem individuals are prone to negative interactions with the environment due to the lack of sufficient coping resources and are prone to depression, while high self-esteem individuals can effectively alleviate the disadvantages of stress due to the availability of more coping resources. Meanwhile, good interpersonal relationships can enhance children’s self-esteem and buffer negative emotions in the depression network [55–57]. However, previous research has shown that disadvantaged children tend to report more low self-esteem and interpersonal problems [58–61]. Therefore, adequate attention should be given to the self-esteem and interpersonal problems of disadvantaged children, and targeted interventions should be implemented to reduce activation of depressive symptom networks.

The present study suggests that the interaction of the two types of disadvantaged environments may lead to mechanistic differences in the depressive network, which is also consistent with previous studies that have emphasized the importance of environmental factors and their interaction with susceptibility traits in the mechanisms of depression [62–64]. Studies have been conducted to validate differences in the characterization of the depressive symptom network among depressed individuals with different genetic and environmental risk factors in the general population, but the results have all shown that the depressive network does not differ in either structure or global strength [65, 66]. There may be several reasons for this. First, the inconsistency between the results of the present study and those of previous studies may be due to the actual differences in the network mechanisms of depressive symptoms between children and adults with depression. In addition, previous studies have not found differences between depressive symptom networks, possibly because environmental factors have not been adequately considered [66]. The analysis of environmental risk in the present study was more detailed and took into consideration the multidimensional factors of individuals, families, and schools, and therefore provided a more realistic picture for assessing the mechanisms of differentiation in the structure of the depressive symptom network.

Conclusion

Disadvantaged children have a greater incidence of depression. The core node of the depressive symptom network emphasized negative emotions. The susceptibility characteristics of depressive symptom networks focused on network structure, including specific edges of “self-esteem” and “relationships”, rather than the global connectivity of the network. The research findings contribute to understanding the driving mechanisms of depressive symptoms in disadvantaged children from a network perspective. In practical applications, greater attention should be devoted to special children, and early screening should be conducted based on susceptibility symptoms (self-esteem and interpersonal relationships) to identify high-risk groups and carry out early interventions. Meanwhile, during intervention follow-ups, the recurrence of susceptibility nodes for depressive symptoms should be continuously monitored to prevent the activation of symptom networks. Future research should utilize longitudinal study designs to further validate the dynamic mechanisms driving depressive symptom networks and assess the effectiveness of population-level interventions based on susceptibility mechanisms.

Limitations

First, some studies have reported differences in the prevalence of depression among children and adolescents in rural and urban areas [43]. The sample for this study was drawn entirely from rural areas, so the results of this study cannot account for differences between urban and rural samples. Our study has no hypothesis regarding specific edges, and the omnibus network invariance test indicated that there is at least one edge that differs across networks. An exploratory test was also conducted. However, whether these different edges indeed represent relevant differences between networks of doubly disadvantaged and non-disadvantaged groups might be confirmed by subsequent studies, specifically designed to test the related a priori-specified hypotheses [41]. The stability of the networks in each group in this study was relatively low, especially in the socially demographic disadvantaged group, which requires further verification in subsequent studies. The use of cross-sectional data indicates that only undirected networks and results based on between-subject data could be estimated.
