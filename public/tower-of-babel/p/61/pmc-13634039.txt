A changed landscape: five-year retrospective on the paradigm shift in genetic testing practices for ALS in Canada
Authors: Maya Binet, Gordon Jewett, Ari Breiner, Marvin Chum, Angela Genge, Kerri Schellenberg, Christen Shoesmith, Gerald Pfeffer, Kristiana Salmon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Offering genetic testing is increasingly recommended for all individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), particularly following the development of gene-targeted therapies, such as tofersen for SOD1-ALS. Historically, testing was routinely offered to those with familial ALS (fALS), but inconsistently to those with sporadic ALS (sALS). We evaluated changes in genetic testing and counseling practices among Canadian ALS physicians over a five-year period spanning pivotal clinical trial results and regulatory approval of tofersen. Members of the Canadian ALS Research Network were surveyed in 2020, 2022, and 2025 about genetic testing practices for symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, gene panel composition, access to genetic counseling, and perceived drivers of change. Clinics offering genetic testing for sALS increased from 33% of clinics in 2020 and 57% in 2022 to 100% of respondents in 2025. Genetic testing for patients with a family history (fALS) was near-universal across all timepoints. Broader use of multi-gene panel testing increased over time, coinciding with sponsored testing availability. 61% of respondents reported that Health Canada approval of tofersen directly influenced their practice. Predictive testing offerings increased from 37% in 2020 to 61% in 2025. Genetic testing practices in Canada shifted substantially during late-stage clinical development and following regulatory approval of a gene-targeted therapy (tofersen). Proactive planning during the clinical trial phase facilitated rapid, nationwide adoption. This study captures a key turning point in ALS care, illustrating how therapeutic breakthroughs can redefine national clinical standards.

Introduction

ALS is a progressive neurodegenerative condition affecting upper and lower motor neurons, with an average survival of two to five years after symptom onset. ALS is typically classified as either sporadic (sALS), in the absence of a known family history, or familial (fALS), when a family history of ALS or related neurodegenerative conditions is reported. Several limitations have been noted with these terms, such as the conflation of fALS with genetic ALS, or the assumption that sALS lacks a genetic etiology [1, 2]. Approximately 10-20% of individuals with supposed sALS have a genetic etiology, while up to 30% of fALS cases may not have an identifiable monogenic etiology [3–8]. Recent guidelines recommend offering genetic testing to all individuals diagnosed with ALS, regardless of reported family history [9].

Recent advances in the genetic characterization of ALS have led to the identification of multiple monogenic subtypes and the development of genetically targeted therapies. They have also highlighted the complex nature of genetic contributions to the disease, which includes various susceptibility genes and evidence of oligogenic inheritance [10]. A milestone in the field has been the availability and approval of the first gene-specific disease-modifying therapy for individuals with SOD1-ALS. Treatment with tofersen (QALSODY) has demonstrated a reduction in plasma and CSF neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels as a precursor to favorable clinical outcomes [11–13]. While only a small proportion of ALS patients will qualify for this treatment, it is imperative that no treatment-eligible individual is missed because genetic testing was not offered. Determining treatment eligibility further emphasizes the importance of universal genetic testing. In recent years, the field has seen a clear shift in genetically targeted therapeutic development, translating to changes in clinical practice. This study aimed to evaluate how genetic testing practices among Canadian ALS physicians have shifted in response to therapeutic developments. Previous research has demonstrated that, while genetic testing is routinely offered to patients with fALS, the offer rate for those with sALS has remained substantially lower, at approximately 30–50% globally [14–16]. In the initial study leading to this work, only 33% of clinics reported offering testing for sALS in Canada [17]. We hypothesize that the availability of a genetically-targeted therapy has increased the perceived clinical relevance of genetic testing and contributed to a nationwide shift in genetic testing practices.

Methods

The nature of this work was recognized as quality improvement for the benefit of improved clinical practice, and as such, Research Ethics Board waivers were granted to cover all instances of data collection (McGill University Health Centre Neurosciences-Psychiatry & University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Boards).

Genetic testing practices were surveyed at three timepoints over the course of a five-year period (2020, 2022, 2025) (Tables 1; 2). These timepoints were selected based on alignment with two national projects to improve access to genetic counseling and testing for Canadians living with ALS (one running from 2020 to 2022, and one ongoing project initiated in 2025). Where possible, the same question structure was used at all timepoints. Previous iterations of the survey were consulted and, where applicable, minor modifications to terminology and available responses were made to reflect evolutions in the field (e.g., terminology, gene panel composition, etc.).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize survey responses. For each survey year (2020, 2022, 2025), we calculated proportions (n/N) and two-sided 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) using the Wilson score method, which performs well for small samples. For multi-category items (e.g., yes/sometimes/no), each category was treated as a binomial proportion against all other responses. Denominators reflect the number of respondents answering each item. Missing responses were not imputed.

The 95% CIs are reported to characterize the precision of within-timepoint estimates given the small number of respondents at each wave. Formal comparisons across years were not performed, as the survey waves were not independent. Respondents could participate across multiple timepoints, and in 2025, multiple physicians per clinic were invited to respond rather than one per clinic. Proportions and 95% CIs were calculated in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

In 2025, 33/51 (65%) of invited physicians responded to the survey, including at least one respondent from 21 of the recognized 24 CALS clinics (88%) (Table 2). There were longitudinal data for 11 of the 24 CALS clinics, with the same respondent answering the survey across all 3 timepoints. Variability in practices was observed between clinics, largely related to what testing is offered (if there is a choice between local testing or a sponsored panel) and predictive genetic counseling practices.

Genetic testing of symptomatic ALS cases

In 2025, 33/33 (100%; 95% CI: 89.6–100.0%) of respondents reported routinely offering genetic testing for suspected cases of fALS, similar to 25/27 (92.6%; 95% CI: 76.6–97.9%) in 2020 and 21/23 (91.3%; 95% CI: 73.2–97.6%) in 2022 (Fig. 2A). For fALS, 13/33 (39%; 95% CI: 24.7–56.3%) of physicians offered testing with a comprehensive gene panel ( > 5 genes), while 13/33 (39%; 95% CI: 24.7–56.3%) of physicians offered limited gene testing, with SOD1 and C9orf72 at a minimum, sometimes including FUS, TARDPB and ATXN2 (Fig. 2C). For sALS, 18/33 (55%; 95% CI: 38.0–70.2%) of physicians offered testing with a comprehensive gene panel, while 14/33 (42%; 95% CI: 27.2–59.2%) of physicians offered limited gene testing. Physicians may be more likely to adopt a tiered testing approach for fALS (7/33; 21%; 95% CI: 10.7–37.8%) compared to sALS (1/33; 3%; 95% CI: 0.5–15.3%), wherein targeted gene testing is first performed, and only followed by a comprehensive gene panel if negative. 16/33 (48%; 95% CI: 32.5–64.8%) of physicians utilized an available, at the time of survey, sponsored ALS multi-gene panel, occasionally in combination with available local testing. Sponsored genetic testing programs offer genetic testing at no cost through industry partners, and were available Canada-wide to all individuals with ALS, as well as asymptomatic individuals with a family history of ALS. While we had insufficient data to support meaningful conclusions on diverging practices within the same clinic, where responses were received by multiple physicians practicing within the same clinic, different approaches within clinics were noted for genes being tested.

This marks an increase in broader testing compared to previous years. In 2022, testing was limited to only the C9orf72 and SOD1 genes at 5/20 of clinics testing fALS (25%; 95% CI: 11.2–46.9%) and 5/13 of clinics testing sALS (38%; 95% CI: 17.7–64.5%).

Changes in practice

In 2022, 18/23 (78%; 95% CI: 58.1–90.3%) of respondents reported that their genetic testing practices had changed since 2020. When looking at qualitative data provided through free-text survey responses, two main changes were described. The first was broader testing, either through the availability of a sponsored panel or changes to testing available through the provincial healthcare system. The second reported change was testing practices for sALS, even if not necessarily reflected in the closed-answer portion of the survey. These changes were largely described as an expansion of genes tested in sALS, including increased use of comprehensive gene panels as opposed to limited gene testing (e.g., just SOD1 and C9orf72). Some respondents indicated that they had begun testing in sALS for the first time.

In 2025, 20/33 (61%; 95% CI: 43.7–75.3%) of physicians reported that tofersen approval by Health Canada directly influenced their genetic testing practices (Fig. 2B).

Genetic counseling practices

In 2025, 31/33 (94%; 95% CI: 80.4–98.3%) respondents indicated that pre-test genetic counseling is performed by an ALS physician (Fig. 3A). Predictive genetic testing practices in ALS

In 2025, 20/33 (61%; 95% CI: 43.7–75.3%) of physicians selected “yes” when asked if they offer predictive genetic testing to asymptomatic family members, while 12/33 (36%; 95% CI: 22.2–53.4%) selected “sometimes” and 1/33 (3%; 95% CI: 0.5–15.3%) selected “no” (Fig. 4A). This is in contrast to 10/27 (37%; 95% CI: 21.5–55.8%) and 6/22 (27%; 95% CI: 13.2–48.2%) selecting “yes”, in 2020 and 2022, respectively. 11/32 (35%; 95% CI: 20.4–51.7%) physicians routinely offer predictive testing, compared to just 5% (95% CI: 0.9–24.6%) in 2020 and 38% (95% CI: 17.7–64.5%) in 2022 (Fig. 4B). The alternate option to routinely offering testing was offering testing only if a family member requests it.

20/32 (63%; 95% CI: 45.3–77.1%) of physicians indicated that they would refer predictive genetic testing cases to a clinical genetics professional (Fig. 4C). Alternatively, they may be handled by the ALS clinic (6/32; 19%; 95% CI: 8.9–35.3%) or collaboratively by both departments (6/32; 19%; 95% CI: 8.9–35.3%) (Fig. 4). In 2020, cases were referred to the medical genetics department 16/20 (80%; 95% CI: 58.4–91.9%) of the time.

In 2025, 14/32 (44%; 95% CI: 28.2–60.7%) of physicians reported that they routinely follow asymptomatic individuals with inherited genetic risk for ALS. Four respondents noted that they follow asymptomatic individuals with a SOD1 variant only, due to potential treatment implications, but not other at-risk individuals. These follow-up practices vary widely from one physician to another, even within clinics, and range from biannual or annual electromyography and NfL measurement, both, or follow-up as needed based on symptom development. In 2020, 8/27 (30%; 95% CI: 15.9–48.5%) of respondents reported that they routinely follow asymptomatic individuals with inherited genetic risk.

Discussion

Our data demonstrates a shift in genetic testing practices following both the anticipated availability and subsequent approval of tofersen by Health Canada in March 2025. In 2020, with the pivotal tofersen clinical trial (VALOR) actively recruiting, the majority of physicians ordered genetic testing for fALS patients, while only 33% ordered genetic testing for sALS patients. Other studies published immediately prior to, and during, that period of time showed similar numbers, ranging from 30 to 50% of physicians offering genetic testing for sALS [14–16]. This study demonstrates that, as of 2025, Canadians living with ALS are being offered genetic testing in a systematic fashion, regardless of family history. This approach has been highlighted as best practice in ALS care in Canada and globally [6, 19, 20]. While early publications were critical in pushing for a departure from the distinction between familial and sporadic ALS as a trigger for genetic testing, this is now reinforced by evidence-based guidelines and other recent studies [9, 21].

While our data demonstrate how rapidly practice can evolve following the availability of a new therapy, they also underscore the value of initiating proactive, system-level preparation for patient access during clinical development, when therapeutic benefit is plausible but not yet confirmed. Following an initial national landscape assessment in 2020, a Canada-wide initiative, supported by dedicated project management, was launched to address barriers to genetic counseling and testing in anticipation of potential shifts in ALS care [4]. As reflected in our findings, genetic testing practices began to more substantially shift following the publication of the VALOR results in 2022 [22], suggesting that groundwork laid during the clinical trial phase facilitated timely adoption once efficacy signals emerged. Importantly, the initiatives to improve access to genetic testing in Canada extend beyond SOD1-ALS and have positioned our community well for the broader integration of genetically-targeted therapies as they continue to emerge in development.

Experiences in other neuromuscular disorders, including spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), have similarly demonstrated the importance of early and sustained stakeholder engagement, particularly in the context of newborn screening implementation [23, 24]. A quick succession of disease-modifying therapy approvals in SMA created an unprecedented need for coordinated changes across screening programs. While many of these system-level adaptations were successfully implemented in the years following the first regulatory approvals, the pace of therapeutic advancement highlights the challenges of establishing new care pathways reactively, after treatments had already become available. This example highlights that, although practice changes should be implemented to keep abreast of regulatory approvals, preparation for such changes should begin once therapies enter advanced clinical evaluation, in order to ensure equitable and timely access to the best possible care.

Genetic testing for SOD1 and C9orf72, at a minimum, is available in every province, and when broader testing is available, many physicians offer a comprehensive ALS gene panel. Testing with a multi-gene panel is supported by both guidelines and multiple studies [9, 10, 25]. Given that the Canadian healthcare system is publicly funded but provincially regulated, the availability and coverage of genetic testing for ALS are province-dependent. Certain provinces cover testing limited to SOD1 and C9orf72, available through a provincial laboratory. Other provinces may cover a gene panel containing a few dozen genes through an out-of-country genetic testing laboratory. In principle, there should be no out-of-pocket costs associated with genetic testing; however, if a province only covers a limited number of genes, then a more comprehensive testing panel may be available through private insurance coverage or private-pay options. Additionally, funded coverage in many provinces requires documentation and an approval process, which may create functional barriers or delays to accessing testing. For provinces lacking direct access to a multi-gene panel, the shift in testing largely coincided with the availability of sponsored testing programs. Sponsored testing programs can decrease barriers in provinces where testing with a larger gene panel is not publicly funded, thereby contributing to better access to broader genetic testing. The two available sponsored programs running during the data collection period for this study offered genetic testing to individuals in Canada with ALS, completely free of charge. It is important to note the end of a prominent sponsored testing program, as of January 2026, after the data collection for this survey.

Given that multi-gene panels are not yet publicly funded in certain provinces, the absence of this sponsored genetic testing will further exacerbate this inequality across Canadian provinces going forward. Fundamentally, broad genetic testing is key to furthering our understanding of the genetic underpinnings of ALS, characterizing geographically and ethnically diverse populations, and the development of genetically targeted treatments [10, 19, 26]. Testing with a broad panel also allows for the identification of individuals who may be eligible for a current or future genetically targeted clinical trial or therapy outside of SOD1. While we’ve largely highlighted the utility of genetic testing in the context of genetically targeted therapies for ALS, consideration of the personal, clinical and familial utility of genetic testing remains essential [27, 28]. Broad genetic testing may provide valuable information, such as prognostic information, to an individual and to their blood relatives, such as the opportunity to pursue predictive genetic testing and various family planning options. For individuals with ALS in Canada, the scope of the genetic test that they are offered, and the opportunities that may come with that information, is contingent on their province of residence. This lack of uniformity is something that remains to be addressed in Canada.

Interestingly, an increase in predictive genetic testing was also evident. Although all blood relatives of individuals given a genetic diagnosis of ALS should be offered predictive genetic counseling, the context has changed for individuals with a family history of SOD1-ALS. The available tofersen data suggest that earlier treatment initiation, in symptomatic individuals, yields better outcomes, including the potential for improvement [13]. Thus, the earliest identification of clinical manifestations in a carrier of a relevant genetic variant is critical for effective treatment. Furthermore, eventual completion of the ATLAS trial, examining the role of tofersen in presymptomatic SOD1 carriers, may further punctuate the importance of early identification of individuals with SOD1 mutations (NCT04856982) [29]. Given this potential additional motivation to pursue predictive genetic testing, this may place further burden on medical genetic departments, already facing limited resource availability. The need for guidelines specific to predictive genetic counseling in ALS, as well as the management of asymptomatic individuals with a confirmed ALS pathogenic variant, has also been recognized [30, 31]. Our data show physician-dependent differences in monitoring practices for individuals at risk of genetic ALS. Much like access to genetic testing, individuals should have a uniform standard of care, regardless of where they live or which physician is overseeing their care. Similar to genetic testing for those living with ALS, proactive, thought-provoking discussions on how to effectively follow and monitor those at risk of genetic ALS are the next frontier for Canadian ALS clinics to confront.

In 2020, long wait times for appointments with a genetic counsellor were recognized as a significant barrier to access to genetic testing for ALS. The findings of this study suggest that, as genetic testing has increased, ALS physicians have addressed this by taking on these responsibilities directly. Nearly all physicians performed pre-test genetic counseling themselves and only referred patients to a clinical genetics specialist in approximately half of the cases with a positive result. Some physicians also handled pre-test predictive genetic counseling and testing independently, facilitated by the availability of testing through the aforementioned sponsored program. Although wait times are a considerable barrier, especially in the context of a rapidly progressive condition such as ALS, non-genetic professionals often have limited training and time to comprehensively guide patients through the process of genetic testing, which has potential risks, such as misinformation or over-testing [32, 33]. For predictive testing in particular, a study of individuals at-risk for genetic ALS highlighted genetic counseling, with a genetic counsellor, as critical [34]. Recognizing the need for timely access to genetic counseling, ALS Canada is piloting a national genetic counsellor program offering genetic counseling to any person in Canada with a diagnosis or family history of ALS. Continued collaboration and appropriate task sharing of genetic counseling, between ALS clinicians and clinical genetics professionals will help ensure broad access to education on genetics in ALS and ensure that access to genetic counseling remains a barrier of the past.

Our findings capture a shift in genetic counseling practices, from provision by clinical genetics professionals to provision by ALS clinicians. While there may be challenges associated with genetic counseling delivered by non-specialist clinicians, this approach may be considered as a strategy in other countries or rare disease communities facing similar resource constraints. Additionally, alternative service delivery models, such as group counseling or mainstreaming models, could be explored to reduce wait times for patients, while ensuring access to genetic counseling with a genetic counsellor. Such approaches have been successful in other fields, such as cancer genetics [35].

This study is not without limitations, one of which is self-selection bias. Physicians who routinely offer genetic testing and demonstrate greater comfort with the topic may be more likely to respond to the survey than those not as involved in their clinic’s genetic testing workflows. While this survey aimed to achieve nationwide representation, three CALS clinics were not represented, as well as physicians who follow ALS patients outside of CALS-affiliated centers. Their current practices, which may differ, were therefore not captured in this survey. Additionally, we intended to determine if there was variability within sites, but the sample size was not large enough to draw meaningful conclusions overall. Given that respondents could not be linked across timepoints for all centers, and that variability is observed within centers, it is possible that a small proportion of the observed differences reflects the differences between respondents, rather than longitudinal change in practice. Another limitation is that the survey did not explore, in cases of fALS with a known variant, if targeted testing for the familial variant was offered or if broader testing was offered. Additionally, while this survey captured the offer of genetic testing, the uptake of genetic testing was not ascertained. An important next step would be to capture the patient perspective to ensure alignment with reported genetic testing practices and published guidelines. Lastly, while the authors recognize the genetic spectrum underlying ALS and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), data on the latter were not captured in this study given that people diagnosed with FTD, with not motor manifestations, are followed in different clinics. This is an important area of future research.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a transformative paradigm shift in practice, marked by a surge in genetic testing for individuals with sALS. It highlights that, with the availability of a genetically targeted treatment and clear clinical utility, rapid adaptation in clinical practice is possible. A shift in the breadth of testing offered was also observed, marked by broader testing with multi-gene panels, coinciding in part with the availability of a sponsored genetic testing program. Although this shift in practice is now well supported by evidence, it was enabled by early, critical discussions with stakeholders across the country. Canada continues to strategically adapt to this rapidly evolving field by piloting new mechanisms to improve access where needed and possible. The next frontier lies in pre-symptomatic care, and the collaborative, well-integrated Canadian clinical community is well-positioned to continue driving meaningful change.
