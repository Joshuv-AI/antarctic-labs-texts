Severe Psoriasiform Blepharitis Accompanying with Phthisis Bulbi
Authors: Kivanç Güngör, Sevim A. Seyyar, Gizem G. Soysal, Mehmet Ç. Soysal, Erhan Kanat
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study is to present a case of severe chronic blepharitis secondary to psoriasis in an eye that developed phthisis bulbi during the treatment of juvenile glaucoma. A 19-year-old male patient, who had been treated for bilateral juvenile glaucoma for many years and had phthisis bulbi in his left eye for the last 4 years, presented with the complaint of chronic eyelid inflammation in the same eye for 2 years. Examination revealed hyperemia, discharge, scaling, inflammation, and diffuse edema on the lower and upper eyelids. It was determined that she had been diagnosed with psoriasis by the dermatology clinic 3 years before her history. Psoriasis-like hyperplasia was observed in the lower eyelid biopsy. He was treated with topical periocular low potency steroid ointment (1% hydrocortisone). Clinicians should be vigilant about eyelid and eye involvement in psoriasis.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease of multifactorial etiology that may be associated with abnormal T-lymphocyte function.[1] Men are more likely than women to experience ocular symptoms associated with psoriasis, which often occur during disease flares. Ocular involvement, which is seen in approximately 10% of psoriasis patients, is observed as chronic blepharitis, which can cause madarosis, entropion, or ectropion by affecting the eyelids most.[23] Other ophthalmic manifestations are keratoconjunctivitis sicca, conjunctivitis and uveitis, punctate keratitis, pinguecula, cataract, glaucoma, and corneal abscesses.[4] In this case report, we aimed to present a case of chronic blepharitis due to psoriasis in a patient who developed phthisis bulbi during the management of juvenile glaucoma. To the best of our knowledge, this case is the first documented example of the coexistence of psoriasis-related chronic blepharitis and phthisis bulbi in the literature.

Case Report

A 19-year-old male patient was admitted to the ophthalmology clinic with the complaints of mild scaling, itching, and burning sensations on the free edge of the left eyelids, as well as hyperemia, inflammation, and edema. He stated that these complaints had continued for 2 years and were recurrent, and that when these symptoms increased, he used artificial tears and drops containing topical antibiotics.

When his ophthalmological history was examined, it was understood that he had been treated with the diagnosis of bilateral juvenile glaucoma for about 10 years and that he developed endophthalmitis and panophthalmitis after trabeculectomy operation in his left eye 4 years ago. It was determined that phthisis bulbi developed in the eye after panophthalmitis and that he had not used antiglaucomatous drugs for his left eye for 4 years. When his detailed systemic history was examined, it was determined that he was diagnosed with psoriasis 3 years ago by the dermatology clinic and was under treatment. He had signs of psoriatic disease on some areas of his skin and was using topical steroids and topical calcipotriol ointments for erythematous and squamous lesions in the elbow and knee area. It was observed that no medication was prescribed by dermatology for eye symptoms. The dermatology clinic sent the patient to the ophthalmology clinic after considering that there might be eyelid inflammation as a result of phthisis bulbi problems. There was no additional feature in his systemic history.

Visual acuity, perception, and projection were negative in the left eye. Anterior segment examination of the left eye revealed phthisis bulbi findings. There was corneal total Leukoma. Biomicroscopic examination revealed thickening and edema of the lids, and dense plaques with squams and pustules on the erythematous base [Figure 1]. These plaques were cleaned and a lower eyelid biopsy was performed. Microscopic analysis of the lower eyelid revealed psoriasis-like hyperplasia of the epidermis with hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, and ulceration, and a mixed inflammatory infiltrate containing neutrophils in the parakeratotic layer and eosinophils in the dermis [Figure 1]. These findings were consistent with psoriasis-like changes. Ophthalmologic examination of the right eye did not reveal any evidence of ocular involvement secondary to psoriasis.

(b) Pathological changes specific to psoriatic lesions (h and e, original magnification). (c) Reduction of plaques of squamous cells and pustules after 15 days of topical 1% hydrocortisone twice daily

Two times per day, 1% hydrocortisone skin ointment was started. He reported a subjective improvement in the eyelids 10 days later [Figure 1]. His examination revealed decreased edema and redness in the eyelids, which supported his symptomatic recovery. The patient provided written, fully informed consent for the case to be published.

Discussion

Herein, we document a patient with phthisis bulbi and chronic blepharitis in one eye and a history of psoriasis. It should be considered that the primary cause of chronic blepharitis may be existing psoriasis in a patient whose etiology of blepharitis was thought to be an infection associated with phthisis bulbi at the beginning and who received treatment in the ophthalmology clinic several times but whose symptoms worsened and recurred. Clinicians should be careful in terms of their patients’ systemic histories.

While psoriasis of the forehead, scalp, and eyebrows is relatively common, psoriasis of the eyelids is extremely rare. Facial psoriasis begins earlier and follows a more chronic course. There are few reports of cases of psoriasis involving the eyelids. Aliagaoglu et al.[5] reported a case of periorbital psoriasis as a result of chronic eyelid itching. Zhu and Tao[6] reported a case of bilateral blepharitis due to psoriasis that started a few months after bilateral cataract surgery. Zug et al.[7] showed that environmental factors such as drugs, trauma, chronic friction, and streptococcal infection can trigger psoriasis. We think that the mechanism that triggered chronic blepharitis due to psoriasis in our case was the development of endophthalmitis and panophthalmitis and phthisis bulbi in the patient who was operated for juvenile glaucoma. The absence of ocular involvement of psoriasis in the previously unoperated eye of the patient with bilateral juvenile glaucoma seems to support our hypothesis.

In conclusion, psoriasiform blepharitis is rare entity. Dermatologists and ophthalmologists should be careful when examining psoriasis patients and should be aware that psoriasis may be the cause of chronic blepharitis. Ophthalmological examination should be performed at regular intervals in psoriasis patients.
