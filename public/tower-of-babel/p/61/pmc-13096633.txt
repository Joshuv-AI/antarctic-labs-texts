Resilience and physical–functional HRQoL in cirrhosis: a cross-sectional study of nursing-relevant direct and indirect associations linked to psychological distress and frailty
Authors: Siyu Qiu, Liping Wang, Zeze Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with cirrhosis frequently experience functional limitations and impaired health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Patient-centered care frameworks increasingly recommend routine use of patient-reported outcomes to support nursing decisions. This study examined how resilience is statistically associated with Physical–Functional HRQoL and whether these associations are consistent with indirect pathways via performance-based frailty and psychological distress, overall and by disease stage. In a single-center cross-sectional study at Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (December 2024 to August 2025), adults with cirrhosis were consecutively enrolled (n = 579; compensated n = 353, decompensated n = 226). Resilience was measured with the Brief Resilience Scale, frailty with the Liver Frailty Index, psychological distress with the DASS-21, and Physical–Functional HRQoL with a prespecified CLDQ composite. All constructs were assessed at the same visit. Multivariable linear regression estimated adjusted associations between resilience and HRQoL. Parallel indirect-effects models with bootstrap confidence intervals and HC3 corrections were used to decompose the cross-sectional association structure into direct and indirect components (associations consistent with mediation), not evidence of transmission or temporal ordering, adjusting for demographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates. Resilience was positively associated with HRQoL in the overall sample (standardized β = 0.174). The total indirect association was statistically significant and was largely accounted for by psychological distress, whereas the indirect association via frailty was small and not significant; the residual direct association was modest and imprecise. In compensated cirrhosis, both indirect and residual direct associations were observed. In decompensated cirrhosis, only the indirect association via psychological distress reached conventional significance, while total and direct associations were small and imprecise. In this single time point analysis, resilience showed statistical associations with better Physical–Functional HRQoL, with a larger distress-related indirect component, whereas frailty did not show consistent indirect associations (reflecting cross-sectional association structure only, with no inference about temporal ordering or causal transmission). These hypothesis-generating findings suggest that brief, nurse-executable bundles jointly assessing resilience, frailty, psychological distress, and HRQoL could be explored within stage-sensitive nurse-led care pathways. Longitudinal and interventional studies using time-lagged mediation models are needed to clarify temporal ordering and evaluate potential HRQoL benefits.

Introduction

Cirrhosis is a progressive chronic disease that markedly impairs patients’ physical functioning, emotional well-being, and social adaptation. Against this background, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has become a core indicator of patient-centered care and is increasingly recommended as part of routine assessment and management of chronic liver disease to support nursing decision making and individualized interventions1. Patients with cirrhosis have substantially lower HRQoL than the general population, and HRQoL typically deteriorates with disease progression2. Existing work highlights the need for multidisciplinary assessment and management, with particular emphasis on nurse-led approaches3.

Psychological resilience, conceptualized as a modifiable positive psychological resource, has attracted growing attention. Higher resilience has been associated with lower psychological distress, less severe frailty, and better HRQoL4,5, making it a promising target for intervention6. In parallel, frailty, which reflects diminished physiological reserve and functional decline, has been consistently linked to poorer HRQoL in cirrhosis7,8. Clinical guidelines and recent reviews recommend incorporating frailty into routine management and targeting its association with adverse outcomes and poorer patient-reported outcomes as a key focus of nursing interventions9,10. Mental health variables are also strong predictors of HRQoL in liver disease, and reductions in psychological distress have been accompanied by improvements in HRQoL11,12. However, most studies have examined these variables separately and have not evaluated resilience, frailty, psychological distress, and HRQoL within a unified conceptual framework.

From a nursing practice perspective, frailty and psychological distress are core targets that can be assessed by nurses and addressed through multidisciplinary care, particularly nurse-led pathways. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guideline highlights frailty, malnutrition, and sarcopenia as key elements of cirrhosis management9. Pragmatic randomized trials of nurse-led follow-up in cirrhosis further support the importance of structured nurse-led pathways for improving HRQoL13,14. Because the pathophysiologic burden and treatment priorities differ across stages of cirrhosis, stage-specific perspectives are essential. In decompensated cirrhosis, portal hypertension–related complications, systemic inflammation, and symptom clusters such as fatigue and ascites-related discomfort often dominate HRQoL impairment3,15. Stage-stratified analyses are therefore crucial for informing bedside nursing that is tailored to the clinical phase of disease.

Despite this rationale, there is a lack of empirical studies that, across different stages of cirrhosis, use standardized instruments that can be implemented by nurses to simultaneously examine the relationships among resilience, frailty, psychological distress, and HRQoL. Existing nurse-led or nurse-involved follow-up and outpatient interventions have largely focused on outcomes such as readmission, survival, or perceived quality of care, and have not systematically characterized the pathways from resilience through frailty and psychological distress to HRQoL within a single analytic framework13,16,17. This gap limits the optimization of evidence-based, stage-specific nursing protocols and follow-up pathways.

To address this gap, we propose a hypothetical pathway model that organizes resilience, frailty, psychological distress, and HRQoL into a directional structure informed by existing theoretical and empirical work in chronic illness. In this conceptual model, resilience is treated as a relatively stable psychological resource that shapes how individuals appraise and cope with disease-related stressors, whereas frailty and psychological distress are viewed as intermediate manifestations of physiological vulnerability and emotional burden that are closely linked to HRQoL. Similar multiple-mediator structures have been reported in studies of people with heart failure and in older adult populations, where frailty, depression and anxiety, and social support or connectedness mediate associations between symptom burden or social vulnerability and functional outcomes or HRQoL18–21 (Fig. 1).

Building on this conceptual model, the present study addresses two questions separately in patients with compensated and decompensated cirrhosis: (1) Is resilience directly associated with HRQoL? (2) Are these associations consistent with indirect pathways via frailty and psychological distress? By answering these questions, we aim to provide hypothesis-generating evidence to inform nurse-led clinical workflows. If robust patterns of indirect association consistent with the proposed mediation pathways are observed, this would suggest that combined screening of resilience, frailty, and psychological distress in routine nursing assessment could be useful and could support the development of stage-specific, stepped intervention strategies.

Methods

Participants

This single-center cross-sectional observational study consecutively enrolled adults with cirrhosis who attended the Hepatology outpatient clinics or inpatient wards of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital between December 2024 and August 2025. Cirrhosis was confirmed by clinical evidence, including presentation, laboratory tests, endoscopy, imaging, or histology. All participants were aged 18 years or older and provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older with the capacity to consent and complete Chinese-language questionnaires, and definite cirrhosis established by either of the following: (1) Histologic stage F4, or a prior or current decompensating event (ascites, variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice) with corroborating imaging, endoscopic, and laboratory documentation22. (2) In the absence of liver biopsy and without decompensation, compensated advanced chronic liver disease was defined non-invasively as liver stiffness (LS) by transient elastography (TE) ≥ 20 kPa, in line with the Baveno VII “rule-of-five” for LS, and combined with evidence of advanced fibrosis from a second serum-based test (Enhanced Liver Fibrosis [ELF] score > 10.5 or FibroTest > 0.72) to increase specificity23,24. To ensure measurement quality, TE required at least ten valid acquisitions and an interquartile-range–to–median ratio of 30% or less25. When noninvasive findings were discordant, two hepatologists independently adjudicated the diagnosis, with disagreements resolved by a third senior expert.

Exclusion criteria were acute-on-chronic liver failure at enrollment; impaired consciousness or cognition that precluded consent or questionnaire completion (for example, West Haven grade ≥ 2) or severe sensory impairment; active psychosis or severe cognitive impairment; hemiplegia; diabetes mellitus with end-organ damage; active malignancy, including hepatocellular carcinoma, or active autoimmune disease; prior liver transplantation; inability to complete Chinese-language questionnaires.

Stage classification at baseline was based on the first decompensating event. The presence of any one of the following defined the decompensated stage: ascites, variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice. Patients without these events were classified as compensated26. Event ascertainment relied on the electronic medical record contemporaneous with enrollment and was verified through imaging—specifically, ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging—as well as endoscopy reports. Chronic stigmata of liver disease, such as spider nevi, caput medusae, or peripheral edema, were described but not used to define decompensation22.

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. KT2024035, dated April 15, 2024).

Exposure: Psychological resilience

The primary exposure was psychological resilience, assessed with the validated Chinese version of the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS; 6 items)27–29. Participants responded on a five-point Likert scale from 1 to 5, with items 2, 4, and 6 reverse-scored. Following the original developers, we computed the total (sum) score (range 6–30) as the primary index of resilience; higher values indicate a stronger ability to “bounce back” from stress and adversity. Prior studies in Chinese-speaking samples from mainland China, Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, and overseas Chinese communities have supported a unidimensional structure, good internal consistency (typical Cronbach’s α approximately 0.80 to 0.90), construct validity, and measurement equivalence, confirming suitability for research and clinical screening27,30. The BRS has been widely used among populations with liver disease internationally, including cohorts with cirrhosis, liver transplantation, and hepatitis C31–33.

Primary outcome: HRQoL

HRQoL was measured with the Chinese version of the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ). The instrument comprises 29 items across six domains—abdominal symptoms, fatigue, systemic symptoms, activity, emotion, and worry. Each item is rated on a seven-point scale in which a higher score reflects better HRQoL34. The Mandarin version, validated in clinical samples, demonstrated good internal consistency as well as structural and criterion validity, supporting its use in Chinese-language contexts35,36. Subsequent work has developed and validated etiology-specific CLDQ variants in targeted populations, such as those with chronic hepatitis B and metabolic-associated fatty liver disease, indicating the strong transferability and psychometric performance of the CLDQ framework in Chinese settings37,38.

To reduce construct overlap with the parallel mediator psychological distress and to enhance conceptual separation between mediators and outcome when interpreting indirect associations, we prespecified the CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite as the primary endpoint. For each of the four domains—abdominal symptoms, systemic symptoms, activity, and fatigue—we calculated the domain mean on a scale of 1 to 7, then averaged the four domains with equal weights to obtain the composite score (range 1 to 7, with higher values indicating better HRQoL). This approach aligns with AGReMA guidance to avoid conflating mediator and outcome content39,40 and follows standard practice in HRQoL research that distinguishes physical and psychological components, analogous to the PCS and MCS presentation in the SF-3614,41.

Mediators

We specified two parallel mediating pathways: (1) frailty measured by the Liver Frailty Index (LFI) and (2) psychological distress. Both mediators entered the model as observed variables, and indirect effects were estimated within compensated and decompensated strata, respectively. All mediator and outcome assessments were completed at the same clinic visit or during the same hospitalization period to minimize bias from timing mismatch.

Frailty: Liver frailty index (LFI)

The LFI combines grip strength, five-times chair stands, and static balance tests according to a published formula. It has been repeatedly validated in patients with cirrhosis and among liver transplant candidates, providing an objective characterization of performance-based frailty42,43. In this study, grip strength was measured using a calibrated dynamometer, with the mean of three attempts recorded in kilograms. The chair-stand test captured completion time in seconds, and balance time was summed across three stances—feet together, semi-tandem, and full tandem—in seconds. A calculator for the LFI is available at http://liverfrailtyindex.ucsf.edu43. Compared with subjective scales, the LFI is a performance-based, formative index rather than a reflective latent construct; therefore, the index score was entered directly into the models. Conceptually, we treated the LFI as a dynamic state index of physical frailty that reflects accumulated vulnerability over months or years, rather than as a fixed trait or an acutely modifiable outcome of short-term psychological fluctuations. In our hypothesized pathway, any resilience–frailty–HRQoL sequence is assumed to unfold over longer periods through behavior and self-management, rather than within a single clinic visit.

Psychological distress

Psychological distress was assessed with the Simplified Chinese version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)44. The instrument comprises three seven-item subscales—depression, anxiety, and stress—administered with a 0 to 3 response format referencing the past week. Following the manual and common practice, we summed items within each subscale. We then combined the three subscale totals to create an overall distress index, ranging from 0 to 63, with higher values indicating greater distress. Chinese validations and multiple studies in Chinese samples support strong internal consistency, structural validity, and measurement invariance, with a robust three-factor structure that also reflects a general distress factor (typical Cronbach’s α approximately 0.81 to 0.95)45–47. The DASS-21 has been widely applied in liver disease cohorts48,49.

Covariates

To control for clinical and behavioral confounding, all models adjusted for age, sex, comorbidity burden, current smoking, alcohol use, body mass index (BMI), cirrhosis etiology, and psychotropic medication history. Age, sex, BMI, etiology, and psychotropic use were retrieved from routine electronic records and were almost complete. Comorbidity burden was calculated using a modified Charlson Comorbidity Index based on ICD-10 diagnoses in the 24 months before enrollment, excluding dementia to avoid overlap with hepatic encephalopathy50. Smoking was coded as current smoking (yes/no). Alcohol exposure was the number of drinking occasions in the past week, a simple frequency measure that aligns with screening recommendations and is associated with poorer liver-specific and HRQoL outcomes51,52. BMI was included as a crude anthropometric indicator of body size and a broad proxy for nutritional reserve, given the links between malnutrition, frailty, and HRQoL in cirrhosis. However, as it can be distorted by ascites and fluid overload in advanced disease, its findings were interpreted cautiously, particularly in decompensated cirrhosis8,9,53. Etiology was collapsed into viral versus non-viral (alcohol-related, metabolic, autoimmune, and other), capturing broad differences in disease profile and potential stigma54–56. Psychotropic medication use was coded as any prescription history for antidepressant or anxiolytic agents (yes/no), as such treatment usually reflects clinically significant distress that may influence both psychological distress and HRQoL57,58. The same covariate set was used for all models to maintain comparability.

Missing data handling

We adopted a complete-case strategy for the primary analyses and did not apply multiple imputation, because overall missingness was low. Before scoring, item-level missingness was handled using standard proportional rules. For the BRS and DASS-21, if 20% or fewer items within a scale or subscale were missing, the score was prorated from the mean of completed items; otherwise, the total was set to missing59–61. For the CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite, domain scores were calculated as the mean of available items when 20% or fewer items in a domain were missing; if any domain exceeded this threshold, the composite was not calculated. The LFI is a formative index derived from three objective tests; all components were required, and cases with missing LFI components were excluded from LFI-based analyses. For regression and mediation models, we used complete-case analysis, excluding any cases with missing values on the exposure, mediators, outcome, or covariates62,63.

Data analysis

Regarding timing and stratification, all core variables were assessed at T0, defined as the day of enrollment for outpatients or within the first 72 h of the index hospitalization. This corresponds to a cross-sectional assessment in which exposure, mediators, and outcome were measured at the same time point. All analyses were stratified, with separate modeling and reporting in the compensated and decompensated groups. Statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics 26.

For data preparation and descriptive analyses, scoring followed the procedures outlined in Sect. 2.6, and inferential analyses were conducted using complete cases. Continuous variables are summarized as means with standard deviations, or medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables are summarized as counts with percentages. Between-group comparisons used the t-test or rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or exact test for categorical variables. To facilitate comparison of effect sizes, continuous predictors—resilience (BRS), frailty (LFI), psychological distress (DASS-21 total), weekly drinking occasions, and BMI—were z-standardized within stratum. The outcome, the CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite, retained its original 1 to 7 metric; regression results are reported as standardized beta coefficients (β), computed within stratum to facilitate effect-size comparisons.

To estimate direct effects, within each stratum we fitted ordinary least squares models with the CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite as the dependent variable. The principal predictor was resilience (BRS, z-standardized within stratum). Covariates were age, sex, modified Charlson comorbidity count, current smoking status, weekly drinking occasions, BMI, etiology (viral versus non-viral), and a history of psychotropic medication use (yes/no); weekly drinking occasions and BMI were entered in z-standardized form. We report standardized beta coefficients, their 95% confidence intervals, and the incremental R-squared. To align with the mediation analysis and enhance robustness to non-normality and heteroscedasticity, coefficients were bootstrapped using 5,000 resamples. We present standardized betas with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. Statistical significance was determined by whether the confidence interval excluded zero with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. We also provide model diagnostics, including residual plots, probability plots, and variance inflation factors less than 564.

For the parallel indirect-effects analysis, within each stratum we used Hayes PROCESS v4.3, Model 4 to estimate a parallel indirect-effects model (cross-sectional) with X = resilience (BRS, z-standardized), Y = CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite, M1 = LFI (z-standardized), and M2 = DASS-21 total (z-standardized). The covariate set was identical to that used in the direct-effect models (age, sex, modified Charlson comorbidity count, current smoking, weekly drinking occasions, BMI, etiology, and psychotropic medication use). We obtained nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals from 5,000 resamples for the two indirect effects (via LFI and via distress), the direct effect, and the total effect, with significance determined by confidence intervals that excluded the value of zero. HC3 was enabled to obtain heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors65.

For the interpretation of mediation and causal language, we followed AGReMA and contemporary causal mediation guidance40,66,67 and specified resilience as the exposure, frailty and psychological distress as parallel mediators, and HRQoL as the outcome to reflect a clinically plausible hypothetical temporal ordering. However, all variables were assessed at a single time point (T0). Within this framework, resilience (BRS) was conceptualized as a relatively stable psychological resource, whereas the LFI was conceptualized as a dynamic but slowly changing state index of physical frailty. It is physiologically implausible that resilience would modify performance-based frailty acutely within a single visit; instead, any resilience–frailty–HRQoL chain is assumed to unfold over longer periods through behavior and self-management. Our models therefore estimate statistical associations that are consistent with, but do not establish, mediation or causal effects. In this cross-sectional setting, the estimated indirect effects primarily represent a decomposition of the cross-sectional association structure (shared variance) rather than evidence of transmission, temporal mediation, or causal ordering. Reverse and bidirectional relationships are also possible. For example, poorer HRQoL may lead to higher psychological distress and, over time, to lower resilience, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Throughout this study, we therefore interpret the estimated indirect effects as “associations consistent with mediation” rather than as definitive evidence of causal mechanisms.

Sample size and power were estimated a priori in G*Power 3.1 for the F test of an increase in R-squared due to one key predictor (resilience) over and above a small set of covariates. In this simplified planning scenario, we assumed a Cohen’s f-squared of 0.05 (small-to-moderate effect), a two-sided alpha of 0.05, power of 0.80, one tested predictor and five total predictors. The resulting required per-stratum sample size was approximately 160–200 patients for effect sizes in the range f-squared = 0.04–0.06. We therefore aimed for at least 180–200 participants per stratum.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Patient characteristics

We initially enrolled 596 patients. In accordance with the prespecified complete-case strategy, 17 were excluded due to missing key baseline variables, yielding 579 patients with complete data for description and subsequent analyses. Of these, 353 (61.0%) had compensated cirrhosis and 226 (39.0%) had decompensated cirrhosis.

Compared with the compensated group, patients with decompensated cirrhosis were older (56.67 ± 6.74 vs. 49.03 ± 7.19 years, p < 0.001), reported more frequent drinking in the past week (0.94 ± 1.71 vs. 0.28 ± 0.91 occasions per week, p < 0.001), and had a higher comorbidity burden (modified CCI 1.01 ± 1.03 vs. 0.41 ± 0.79, p < 0.001). Distributional profiles indicated a clear shift toward higher levels of weekly drinking frequency and higher CCI grades in the decompensated group (Table 1).

By contrast, the two stages did not differ in the proportion of males or in current smoking status (both p > 0.05). Mean BMI was also similar between compensated and decompensated patients (24.96 ± 4.48 vs. 24.92 ± 4.71 kg/m², p = 0.93). Approximately half of the sample had viral-related cirrhosis (HBV or HCV; 47.8% overall, 47.0% in compensated vs. 49.1% in decompensated, p = 0.69), and only a small minority had a documented history of psychotropic medication use (1.9% overall; about 2% in each stage, p = 1.00).

Health-related quality of life

We prespecified four domains for the primary analysis—abdominal symptoms, systemic symptoms, activity, and fatigue. The equal-weight mean of these four domains constituted the CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite. The emotion and worry domains were summarized descriptively only. As shown in Table 2, the overall composite was 4.93 ± 0.89. The compensated group scored higher than the decompensated group (5.33 ± 0.68 versus 4.31 ± 0.82, p < 0.001). The six-domain overall mean showed a parallel decline, with 5.18 ± 0.66 in the compensated group versus 4.13 ± 0.81 in the decompensated group (p < 0.001). By domain, the decompensated group exhibited larger decrements in activity, fatigue, emotion, and worry (all p < 0.001), as well as larger standard deviations, indicating greater heterogeneity. Internal consistency was good: Cronbach’s α was 0.885 overall (0.815 in compensated; 0.846 in decompensated).

Resilience

The overall BRS mean total score was 17.95 ± 3.47. By stage, the compensated group scored 18.07 ± 3.34 (n = 353) and the decompensated group scored 17.77 ± 3.65 (n = 226). The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.367). Internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.886). See Table 3.

Direct effects

Full results are presented in Table 4; Fig. 2. In the overall sample (n = 579), resilience showed a positive association with HRQoL in fully adjusted models that included sociodemographic and clinical covariates (age, sex, modified Charlson comorbidity count, current smoking, weekly drinking frequency, BMI, etiology, and psychotropic medication history): β = 0.174 (95% CI 0.103 to 0.243), R² = 0.238, ΔR² = 0.030 (12.6% of the total R²). In other words, after adjustment for these covariates, a one standard deviation higher BRS score was associated with an average CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite score that was about 0.17 standard deviations higher. Resilience alone explained approximately 3.0% of the additional variance beyond the covariates.

Compensated stage. In patients with compensated cirrhosis, the association between resilience and HRQoL was clear and statistically significant, β = 0.251 (95% CI 0.151 to 0.344), with R² = 0.131 and ΔR² = 0.062 (47.4% of the model R²), indicating a relatively strong independent contribution of resilience at this stage (about 6.2% added variance). Decompensated stage. In those with decompensated cirrhosis, the association was smaller and not statistically significant, β = 0.085 (95% CI − 0.037 to 0.202), with R² = 0.165 and ΔR² = 0.007 (4.3% of the model R²). In more advanced disease, HRQoL appears to be driven primarily by other clinical factors, with only a modest independent contribution from resilience.

Covariates showed generally plausible clinical patterns. Older age was consistently associated with lower CLDQ Physical–Functional Composite scores across all strata (all β < 0, 95% CIs excluding zero). A higher modified Charlson comorbidity count was associated with worse HRQoL in the overall sample and in the decompensated stage, whereas its association in the compensated stage was small and imprecise. More frequent weekly drinking was related to worse HRQoL in the overall and compensated groups, but not clearly in the decompensated group. BMI showed a stage-specific pattern: it had only a weak and non-significant association with HRQoL in the overall and compensated samples, but a more pronounced positive association in decompensated patients (β ≈ 0.25, 95% CI approximately 0.12 to 0.37), which is compatible with better nutritional reserve being linked to better physical–functional HRQoL in advanced cirrhosis. By contrast, sex, current smoking, viral etiology, and psychotropic medication history tended to have smaller and less consistent associations, with most 95% CIs including zero.

Although several standardized coefficients (β) reached conventional statistical significance, the corresponding differences on the original 1–7 CLDQ Physical–Functional scale were small. For example, a standardized association of β = 0.10–0.20 SD corresponds to roughly 0.07–0.16 CLDQ points (β × SD_Y, with SD_Y ≈ 0.68–0.82 by stage, Table 2), which is below commonly reported meaningful score differences (often on the order of approximately 0.5 points) in chronic liver disease populations35,68.

Multicollinearity among the predictors in the direct-effect models in Table 4 (BRS together with all covariates) was minimal: all variance inflation factors were close to 1 and well below conventional thresholds (overall approximately 1.01–1.28; compensated 1.01–1.25; decompensated 1.02–1.24).

Indirect associations

In the parallel indirect-effects models, adjusted for the same covariates as the direct-effect analyses (age, sex, modified Charlson comorbidity count, current smoking, weekly drinking frequency, BMI, viral etiology, and psychotropic medication history), the total indirect effect of BRS on HRQoL was significant in the overall sample (0.113, 95% CI 0.073 to 0.154), driven primarily by the pathway via psychological distress (DASS-21) (0.099, 95% CI 0.069 to 0.133). The pathway via frailty (LFI) was not significant (0.013, 95% CI − 0.009 to 0.037). The direct effect was small and did not reach conventional significance (c′ = 0.062, 95% CI − 0.005 to 0.123). In this cross-sectional setting, these estimates should be interpreted as a variance decomposition of the association between resilience and HRQoL into direct and indirect components, not evidence of transmission or temporal mediation. The proportion of the total standardized association attributable to the two indirect components was approximately 64.7%.

In the compensated stage, the total indirect effect was again significant (0.118, 95% CI 0.075 to 0.164), with a significant pathway via DASS-21 (0.102, 95% CI 0.062 to 0.146) and a non-significant pathway via LFI (0.016, 95% CI − 0.007 to 0.040). The direct effect also remained significant (c′ = 0.133, 95% CI 0.030 to 0.232), consistent with both indirect and residual direct associations between resilience and HRQoL in this cross-sectional analysis. In this stratum, about 47.0% of the total standardized association between resilience and HRQoL was attributable to the two indirect components.

In the decompensated stage, the pathway via DASS-21 was significant (0.079, 95% CI 0.031 to 0.135), whereas the pathway via LFI was not (− 0.018, 95% CI − 0.061 to 0.020). The total indirect effect (0.061, 95% CI − 0.005 to 0.126) and the direct effect (c′ = 0.024, 95% CI − 0.107 to 0.141) were both non-significant, indicating that in advanced cirrhosis the overall relationship between resilience and Physical–Functional HRQoL is weaker and less precisely estimated, although higher resilience still appears to be linked to better HRQoL primarily through lower psychological distress. These estimates reflect cross-sectional association structure and remain compatible with reverse or bidirectional relationships. See Table 5; Fig. 3.

Collinearity diagnostics for these mediation models, which included BRS, LFI, DASS-21 and the same covariates as the direct-effect analyses, indicated only modest correlations among predictors. Variance inflation factors ranged from approximately 1.01 to 2.71 in the overall sample (1.02–2.32 and 1.03–2.06 in the compensated and decompensated strata), all well below conventional thresholds.

Discussion

In this stage-stratified cirrhosis cohort, resilience showed a positive association with Physical–Functional HRQoL after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates. In the overall sample, the standardized total effect of resilience on HRQoL was modest to moderate, and about two thirds of this association was statistically accounted for by the specified indirect paths, mainly through psychological distress. The pathway through frailty was small and not robust. In compensated patients, both indirect and residual direct associations were evident: roughly half of the total association was attributable to the indirect component (cross-sectional decomposition), again primarily through distress, and a remaining direct component persisted after accounting for frailty and distress. In the decompensated stage, the estimated total and direct effects of resilience on HRQoL were small and imprecise, while only the indirect association via psychological distress reached conventional significance. Given the non-significant total effect and the wide confidence intervals, this stage-specific pattern is best interpreted as an exploratory signal rather than as evidence of a clearly delineated pathway, and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating for future longitudinal work.

Decompensated cirrhosis is, however, a highly heterogeneous state with complications such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, infections, sarcopenia, malnutrition, and portal hypertension–related events, all of which can have major and fluctuating impacts on HRQoL and may overshadow or dilute psychosocial associations in this subgroup9,69. This pattern is broadly consistent with theoretical models that conceptualize resilience as a modifiable psychosocial resource that may be linked to functional health in part through buffering negative affect and stress70, although our cross-sectional data cannot confirm this temporal ordering or exclude alternative directions of influence. Consistent with the conception of the BRS as an index of the capacity to “bounce back” from adversity, associations with lower depression and anxiety and with better quality of life have been reported across multiple conditions, including liver disease and transplant populations1,31,71. The present findings extend this evidence by describing stage-specific patterns of association in cirrhosis and by showing that these patterns remain observable after adjustment for comorbidity burden, alcohol use, BMI, etiology, and psychotropic medication history.

In this context, interpretation of the stage-stratified findings should also consider that established severity scores such as MELD or MELD-Na are commonly used to summarize prognosis and within-stage severity gradients in cirrhosis, particularly among decompensated patients23,72,73. In the present cohort, MELD (and MELD-Na) could not be calculated consistently because the laboratory variables required for these scores were not uniformly available within an analyzable window around the study visit. Therefore, although we stratified analyses by compensated versus decompensated status, this binary staging does not capture within-stage severity heterogeneity (for example, MELD-Na differences), and residual confounding by disease severity may remain. Accordingly, stage-specific estimates should be interpreted as cross-sectional association patterns within a coarse clinical grouping rather than severity-adjusted comparisons.

In contrast, LFI did not show a stable indirect role in the resilience to HRQoL association. This should not be taken to imply that frailty is unimportant for patients’ quality of life. Longitudinal and cross-sectional studies have shown that frailty and sarcopenia are common in cirrhosis and are associated with worse baseline HRQoL and poorer subsequent HRQoL trajectories8,74,75. This suggests that, in the cross-sectional associations observed here, there is limited support for a short-range and reversible link from resilience to performance-based frailty and onward to HRQoL. LFI is a formative and objective performance index and may be less responsive in the short term to psychological resources and coping styles43. Our indirect-effects results indicate that although higher LFI scores were cross-sectionally related to worse HRQoL, the association between resilience and LFI was weak, yielding a small and non-significant product term. In decompensated cirrhosis, high symptom burden and complications may directly drive HRQoL, creating floor or ceiling effects that attenuate transmission of variance through physical performance76. These findings are compatible with current guidance that emphasizes LFI for prognosis stratification and nursing care planning, while not necessarily positioning it as the main bridge between resilience and HRQoL9. Finally, our models suggested a modest stage-specific pattern for BMI. In the decompensated group, higher BMI values were weakly associated with better Physical–Functional HRQoL, whereas in compensated patients the BMI to HRQoL association was small and imprecise. Because BMI is a crude and easily distorted indicator in cirrhosis, especially in the presence of ascites and fluid overload9, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as an exploratory signal that may reflect body habitus or residual confounding and should not be interpreted as a nutritional assessment indicator or a direct treatment target, particularly in decompensated disease.

Among patients with decompensation, sleep disturbance, frailty, and symptoms of depression and anxiety are repeatedly linked to worse HRQoL. In the decompensated subgroup, the combination of a diminished direct association between resilience and HRQoL and a statistically detectable indirect association via distress is coherent with cross-condition models in which emotional and sleep problems amplify symptom experience and restrict activity. It also resonates with evidence that nurse-led identification of psychological and functional problems during clinic visits improves perceived care quality and patient activation13. At the systems level, recent patient-centered care initiatives in hepatology call for routine collection of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) to support nursing decisions, risk stratification, and individualized follow-up. The present findings are compatible with viewing resilience, psychological distress, and HRQoL as a cluster of linked and partly modifiable constructs that could be targeted within nurse-led care pathways1. However, alternative temporal orderings and bidirectional relationships remain plausible; for example, lower HRQoL may exacerbate distress and, over time, undermine resilience, rather than resilience operating upstream of distress and HRQoL. Longitudinal studies are needed to test whether interventions that modify resilience or distress translate into sustained HRQoL gains and whether changes in frailty indicators lag behind psychological change.

The implications for nursing practice and research are necessarily tentative: in the absence of MELD-based severity adjustment and given the single time-point assessment, the stage-stratified patterns should be viewed as hypothesis-generating cross-sectional associations rather than severity-adjusted contrasts or evidence of a confirmed causal chain. Within these limitations, the stage-stratified findings point to some tentative directions for nurse-led care. In compensated cirrhosis, where physiological reserve is relatively preserved, resilience showed a clearer overall association with Physical–Functional HRQoL and a residual direct component after accounting for distress. This pattern is consistent with current guidance that emphasizes early, proactive support for self-management, maintenance of activity, and prevention of decline in this stage9,10. In decompensated cirrhosis, by contrast, the non-significant total association between resilience and HRQoL together with a remaining indirect association via distress highlights psychological symptoms as particularly salient correlates of HRQoL amidst high symptom burden and complications, in line with guideline recommendations to address symptom clusters and mental health in advanced cirrhosis3,69.

At a practical level, nurses could test the feasibility of embedding a brief, nurse-executable ePRO bundle at baseline and at regular follow-up contacts, for example every three to six months or around major clinical transitions. Such a bundle might combine measures of resilience (BRS), psychological distress (DASS-21), performance-based frailty (LFI), and Physical–Functional HRQoL (CLDQ), consistent with recent calls to integrate PROs into routine cirrhosis care and nurse-led follow-up1,14. Screening results could then inform a stepped response. For instance, patients whose resilience scores fall in the lower tertile of the BRS distribution and whose DASS-21 scores are at least in the moderate range could be prioritized for more intensive psychological input or referral, whereas those with milder distress might receive nurse-delivered psychoeducation, sleep and activity counseling, and brief self-management support17.

Evidence from mindfulness-based stress reduction in cirrhosis and from the LIGHTMind trial of supported mindfulness-based cognitive therapy self-help in depression shows that scalable psychological interventions can improve patient-reported outcomes and may be deliverable within nurse-coordinated pathways77,78. Nevertheless, the specific monitoring intervals, cut-offs, and stepped-care algorithms outlined here should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as ready-to-implement protocols. Prospective cohorts and pragmatic trials are needed to determine whether implementing such ePRO-based, stage-sensitive pathways leads to sustained improvements in HRQoL and to refine the operational details of nurse-led interventions.

This study has several strengths and limitations. Strengths include the synchronized assessment of resilience, psychological distress, performance-based frailty, and HRQoL in the same clinical cohort; the use of stage-stratified models aligned with established prognostic staging in cirrhosis; and the application of complete-case analysis with nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals and HC3 corrections to improve robustness to non-normality and heteroscedasticity. Prespecifying the Physical–Functional Composite as the primary HRQoL outcome also reduced construct overlap with distress. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design constrains causal inference, because all variables were measured at a single time point and temporal ordering cannot be established. Reverse or bidirectional relationships are plausible; for example, poorer HRQoL may increase psychological distress and, over time, erode resilience, rather than resilience operating upstream of distress and HRQoL40. Second, unmeasured or imperfectly measured confounders such as social support, cognitive impairment, or sleep parameters may bias associations despite adjustment for a broad set of clinical covariates67. Third, because we did not consistently capture the laboratory variables required to calculate MELD-Na or Child–Pugh scores, we were unable to include these well-established indices of disease severity and prognosis as covariates. Because MELD-Na and Child–Pugh are key determinants of mortality risk, symptom burden, and HRQoL—particularly in decompensated disease—our compensated versus decompensated stratification is a coarse proxy that does not account for within-stage heterogeneity (e.g., MELD 10 vs. 25), so residual confounding by more granular differences in severity remains possible79,80. Fourth, frailty was operationalized using the formative, performance-based LFI and assessed on a single occasion. Acute influences such as transient decompensation, pain, sleep disturbance, or sedating medication could simultaneously worsen LFI scores and psychological distress, potentially attenuating or distorting estimated indirect effects via frailty. The non-significant indirect associations via frailty should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and longitudinal analyses of change in frailty may yield different estimates of its mediating role81. Fifth, the study was conducted at a single tertiary center, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings and a wider range of etiologic profiles. Finally, we estimated multiple regression and indirect-effects models, including stage-stratified analyses, without formal correction for multiple comparisons. Some statistically significant associations may therefore reflect chance, so greater weight should be placed on the overall pattern and consistency of associations than on individual p values, and replication in independent samples is needed. Accordingly, our indirect-effects models should be interpreted as estimating associations that are consistent with the hypothesized pathways rather than as proof of causal mechanisms40.

Conclusions

In this stage-stratified sample of patients with cirrhosis, resilience was positively associated with Physical–Functional HRQoL after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. The pattern of effects was consistent with indirect associations that acted mainly through psychological distress as measured by the DASS-21. Both indirect and residual direct associations were observed in compensated patients. In decompensated patients, the total and direct effects of resilience on HRQoL were small and imprecise, while the indirect association via distress reached conventional significance; this stage-specific pattern should be viewed as exploratory rather than as evidence of a firmly established pathway. LFI did not show a consistent indirect role, a pattern that provides limited cross-sectional support for a short-term resilience → frailty → HRQoL pathway, even though frailty itself remains a strong prognostic and care planning indicator. Because all constructs were assessed cross-sectionally, these findings should be regarded as describing patterns in the cross-sectional association structure and as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in longitudinal studies that implement time-lagged mediation models, as well as in interventional work.

For nursing practice, our cross-sectional findings tentatively support exploring the integration of a nurse-led ePRO bundle, comprising BRS, DASS-21, LFI, and CLDQ, into nurse-led care pathways and routine workflows, with stage-specific and stepped-care delivery. In compensated cirrhosis, resilience and distress appear to be appropriate dual targets, complemented by physical and nutritional strategies that aim to delay frailty progression. In decompensated cirrhosis, care should prioritize timely recognition and management of psychological distress, alongside safe physical maintenance and monitoring of frailty as a process indicator. Such an assess, trigger, intervene, and reassess pathway may help to improve HRQoL, a possibility that should be tested in prospective and interventional studies, and could help align daily nursing practice with patient-centered care goals. Given the single-center and cross-sectional design and the use of complete-case analysis, the observed associations and mediation chains warrant replication and extension in prospective cohorts and randomized trials, including evaluations of implementation effectiveness and equity.
