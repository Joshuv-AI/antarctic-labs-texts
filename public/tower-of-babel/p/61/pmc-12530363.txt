The association between friendly community environments and depressive symptoms in older adults: a longitudinal study in China
Authors: Zhengyu Wu, Shang Meng Huang, Fei Wang, Wen Bin Lan, De Wen Wang, Song Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine the association between friendly community environments and depressive symptoms among older adults in China and to investigate the potential mediating roles of happiness and confidence in this association.DesignA nationally representative longitudinal study employing the parallel mediation model to estimate the direct association. Bootstrapping procedures were employed to test the hypothesised mediating effects.SettingThe China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) database, a nationally representative survey.ParticipantsThe analytical sample comprised 3182 individuals aged 60 years or older from the CFPS 2016, 2018 and 2020 waves.Outcome measuresDepressive symptoms were measured using an eight-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. Higher scores indicated more depressive symptoms. Friendly community environment, happiness and confidence were assessed using corresponding survey items.ResultsThe analysis revealed a significant direct negative association was observed between friendly community environments and depressive symptoms (β=−0.062, 95% CI −0.092 to −0.035, p<0.001). This effect was significantly mediated by increased happiness (indirect effect=−0.025, 95% CI −0.036 to −0.017, p<0.001) and increased confidence (indirect effect=−0.036, 95% CI −0.048 to −0.026, p<0.001). The urban-rural heterogeneity analysis demonstrates that the research findings remain robust across both urban and rural subsamples.ConclusionFriendly community environments are negatively associated with depressive symptoms among older adults in China, primarily by enhancing happiness and confidence in life. To promote healthy ageing, it is crucial to create comfortable, clean and convenient community environments tailored to the older adult population.Keywords: Aging, China, Community Participation, Health & safety

Introduction

Recent demographic data indicate a notable trend in the ageing population globally. In 2021, individuals aged 65 and older numbered 761 million, accounting for 10% of the total world population.1 Projections suggest that this demographic will expand to 1.6 billion by 2050, representing 16% of the global population.2 Focusing on China, the findings from the Seventh National Population Census reveal a significant ageing trend within the country. By the end of 2020, China had approximately 190 million elderly individuals aged 65 and above, which constituted 14.9% of its total population.3 Forecasts indicate that, given the ongoing decline in fertility rates and a persistent decrease in the number of newborns, the elderly population in China is expected to double by 2050, reaching 380 million. This demographic transition will solidify China’s status as a moderately ageing society. In light of these demographic changes, promoting healthy ageing has become a fundamental aspect of national strategies in various countries, aimed at enhancing the physical, mental and social adaptability of the elderly while creating friendly community environments. Acknowledging this necessity, the WHO launched the Age-Friendly Communities initiative.4 This initiative seeks to improve outdoor spaces, transportation systems, housing, social engagement opportunities, inclusivity measures, civic participation, communication channels and healthcare services within communities to facilitate healthy and active ageing.5 Currently, the WHO Global Network of Age-Friendly Communities includes 1542 members across 51 countries, collectively serving over 320 million residents worldwide.6 Consequently, the significance of age-friendly communities in fostering healthy and successful ageing should be emphasised.

An expanding corpus of research has underscored the substantial positive effects of friendly community environments on the mental health of older adults.7,11 However, investigations into the mechanisms that connect community environments to depression remain relatively limited. Evidence suggests that older adults living in amicable communities are more likely to engage in active lifestyles, which in turn enhance their physical and functional abilities. These elements, physical and social activities, along with functional capacity, act as mediators between friendly community environments and diminished depressive symptoms in older adults.12 Additional studies propose that physical exercise serves as a mediating factor in the relationship between community environments and depression,13 as individuals residing in well-structured communities are more inclined to participate in physical activities, thus alleviating depressive symptoms. Furthermore, some research indicates that subjective social status may also function as a mediating variable, with community environments helping to mitigate depression associated with perceived social stratification.14 However, the majority of existing studies primarily concentrate on physiological and social mediators when examining the relationship between community environments and individual depression, often neglecting essential psychological mechanisms. Given that depression is fundamentally a psychological condition, it is imperative to explore how intrinsic psychological factors interact dynamically with environmental influences at both cognitive and affective levels, thereby influencing the risk of depression.

The diathesis-stress model represents a psychological framework frequently employed in the study of mental disorders to elucidate the aetiology of psychological illnesses. This model seeks to account for the emergence of mental disorders through the interplay between inherent psychological vulnerabilities and external stressors.15 For example, individuals possessing certain risk factors or predispositions may be more susceptible to developing depression in response to stressful life events. The model asserts that psychological diatheses, including aspects such as happiness, self-confidence, self-efficacy and emotional regulation capabilities, serve as significant intrinsic vulnerabilities or resilience factors. These elements substantially influence an individual’s interpretation of and reaction to external environmental stressors, thereby altering the environmental impact on the risk of depression. From the perspective of the diathesis-stress model, the relationship between community environments and depression among older adults can be understood as a dynamic interaction between external contexts and psychological diatheses. A thoughtfully designed and supportive community environment may enhance older adults’ sense of happiness, security and confidence. These psychological attributes contribute positively to the mental health of older adults, potentially alleviating the onset of depressive symptoms. In conclusion, in light of the existing research gaps regarding the mechanisms that connect friendly community environments to depressive symptoms in older adults, this study intends to use the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) panel data to investigate the relationship between friendly communities and depressive symptoms in this demographic, with a specific emphasis on the influence of psychological factors such as happiness and confidence. This inquiry is essential for comprehending the mechanisms that underpin mental health in ageing populations and for formulating effective strategies to mitigate depression risk among older adults.

Methods

Participants

The longitudinal data used in this research are derived from the CFPS, a comprehensive survey administered by the China Social Science Survey Centre at Peking University.16 Launched in 2010, the baseline survey included participants from 25 provinces, capturing data from all family members within the selected households. The survey is conducted biennially. This study specifically targets the demographic aged 60 and above, selecting elderly participants from three distinct waves of survey data (2016, 2018 and 2020). These particular waves were selected due to the introduction of survey questions pertaining to community environments starting in 2016, while the 2020 dataset represents the most recent publicly accessible data. In the sample selection process, only respondents who participated in all three survey waves were included. Individuals who did not complete the questions related to depression and community environment variables, or who withdrew during follow-up, were excluded from the analysis. The final analytical sample comprises 3182 individuals.

Measurement

Explained variable: depressive symptoms

The CFPS employed the shortened version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) to assess subjects’ depression levels. The CES-D was developed by Radloff.17 Chinese researchers translated and revised the simplified version to create the Chinese version and establish national urban norms for China.18 The scale items included “(1) I felt sad (2) I felt lonely (3) I felt depressed (4) My sleep was restless (5) I thought my life had been a failure (6) I was happy (7) I enjoyed life (8) I could not get going”. All items were scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 = ‘never’ to 3 = ‘most of the time’. The scores of these eight items were summed to create a depressive symptoms index, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The Cronbach’s alpha value of this scale was 0.81.

Core explanatory variables: friendly community environments

The assessment of friendly communities was based on four items evaluating community environment characteristics. The items included “(1) How are the public facilities such as medical care, transport and other facilities around the community? (2) How is the sanitary environment around the community? (3) How is the safety situation around the community? (4) How are the neighborhood relations in community?” All items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = ‘very poor’ to 5 = ‘very good’. The scores of these four items were aggregated to construct a friendly community environment index, with higher index scores indicating more favourable community environments. The Cronbach’s alpha value of this scale was 0.72.

Mediating variable: happiness and confidence

Happiness and confidence were measured using a single item, respectively. The respondents were asked to report their happiness in life and confidence in future life on scales ranging from 0 = ‘very unhappy’ to 10 = ‘very happy’ and from 1 = ‘very unconfident’ to 5 = ‘very confident’. Higher scores indicate greater levels of subjective well-being and more positive outlooks towards future life among respondents.

Control variables

We controlled for variables including gender, age, household registration, years of education, marital status, chronic disease status, self-rated health, parent–child relationship and number of family members. For gender, ‘male’ was coded as 1 and ‘female’ as 0. For household registration, ‘rural’ was coded as 1 and ‘urban’ as 0. Regarding marital status, ‘married’ was coded as 1, while ‘unmarried, divorced and widowed’ were collectively coded as 0. For chronic disease status, ‘having chronic diseases’ was coded as 1 and ‘no chronic diseases’ as 0. Self-rated health was coded on a 5-point scale: ‘very healthy’ as 1, ‘quite healthy’ as 2, ‘relatively healthy’ as 3, ‘average’ as 4 and ‘unhealthy’ as 5. The parent–child relationship was also coded on a 5-point scale: ‘very distant’ as 1, ‘somewhat distant’ as 2, ‘average’ as 3, ‘close’ as 4 and ‘very close’ as 5. Age, years of education and number of family members are continuous variables, where the number of family members refers to the total count of individuals in the household including oneself.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a parallel mediation model using Amos 26. To enhance the accuracy of causal inference, the key study variables were incorporated into models with a one-wave time lag. Model fit was assessed using the following indices:19 the incremental fit index (IFI), comparative fit index (CFI), adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) and normal fit index (NFI). Values above 0.80 indicate a good model fit, while values exceeding 0.90 suggest excellent fit. Additionally, a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) value below 0.05 indicates excellent fit, and values between 0.05 and 0.08 indicate a good or acceptable fit. We estimated 95% CIs and employed 5000 bootstrap resamples.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Table 1 delineates the baseline characteristics of the study sample. The findings indicate that the mean ages of respondents from urban (66.37±5.12) and rural (66.01±4.88) areas are comparable. The gender distribution reveals a marginally higher percentage of males in urban settings (49.40%) relative to rural settings (47.80%). Furthermore, urban participants exhibited significantly greater educational attainment, with a mean of 6.48 years (SD=4.67), compared with their rural counterparts, who had a mean of 4.16 years (SD=4.14). Analysis of marital status indicates that rural respondents have slightly higher marriage rates (87.20%) than their urban counterparts (86.10%). Significant disparities are observed in the prevalence of chronic diseases, with urban residents reporting substantially higher rates (32.00%) than rural residents (26.60%). Nevertheless, self-rated health levels were found to be similar across both rural and urban residents. In terms of family structure, rural households were larger, with a mean size of 4.04 (SD=2.23), although parent–child relationship scores were comparable between the two groups. Finally, rural respondents exhibited marginally higher community environment scores (mean=14.38, SD=2.77) compared with urban respondents (mean=14.13, SD=2.63), alongside increased depressive symptom scores (rural: 13.78±4.50; urban: 12.52±4.16).

Difference analysis

Table 2 presents the distribution of community friendliness scores across different variables. The results show no significant differences in community friendliness between age groups, gender, marital status and family size. However, years of schooling demonstrate a statistically significant association with community friendliness (F=18.132, p<0.001), with those having no formal schooling reporting the highest mean score and those with more than 6 years of schooling reporting the lowest. Chronic disease status is also associated with community friendliness (t=4.355, p<0.05), as individuals without chronic diseases report higher scores than those with chronic conditions. Self-reported health status shows a strong gradient (F=38.930, p<0.001), where respondents who consider themselves very healthy or quite healthy report the highest mean community friendliness, while those with average or poor health report the lowest. Parent–child relationship is significantly associated with community friendliness (F=21.140, p<0.001), with participants reporting closer relationships indicating higher scores compared with those with distant relationships. Finally, depressive symptoms are negatively related to community friendliness (t=6.314, p<0.001), with individuals scoring higher on depressive symptoms reporting lower levels of perceived community friendliness.

Parallel mediation models

Figure 1 illustrates the outcomes of the parallel mediation model. The fit indices for the model indicate a satisfactory overall fit, with values as follows: IFI=0.877, CFI=0.875, AGFI=0.943, NFI=0.869 and RMSEA=0.063. After controlling for variables such as age, gender, years of education and other covariates, the analysis identified significant positive correlations between friendly community environments and the happiness (β=0.112, 95% CI 0.083 to 0.144, p<0.001) and confidence (β=0.144, 95% CI 0.113 to 0.174, p<0.001) of older adults. In contrast, friendly community environments were negatively correlated with depressive symptoms (β=−0.062, 95% CI −0.092 to −0.035, p<0.001). Additionally, both happiness (β=−0.136, 95% CI −0.171 to −0.107, p<0.001) and confidence (β=−0.153, 95% CI −0.184 to −0.122, p<0.001) exhibited negative associations with depressive symptoms.

The model of friendly communities and depressive symptoms.

With respect to control variables, several factors were found to have significant correlations with depressive symptoms: gender (β=−0.104, 95% CI −0.131 to −0.075, p<0.001), marital status (β=−0.045, 95% CI −0.074 to −0.015, p=0.008), years of education (β=−0.112, 95% CI −0.140 to −0.083, p<0.001), residence (β=−0.121, 95% CI −0.149 to −0.094, p<0.001) and parent–child relationship (β=−0.053, 95% CI −0.082 to −0.023, p<0.001) all demonstrated negative correlations. Conversely, chronic disease (β=0.048, 95% CI 0.017 to 0.078, p=0.006) and self-rated health (β=0.117, 95% CI 0.088 to 0.146, p<0.001) were positively associated with depressive symptoms. It is noteworthy that neither family size nor age exhibited statistically significant relationships with depressive symptoms.

Bootstrap intermediary mechanism test

We used bootstrap testing to investigate the mediating effects of happiness and confidence. As illustrated in table 3, indirect effect 1 signifies the mediation through happiness, whereas indirect effect 2 indicates the mediation through confidence. The estimated mediation effect through happiness was −0.025 (SE=0.006, 95% CI −0.036 to −0.017, p<0.001), which constitutes 15.24% of the total effect. In a similar vein, the mediation effect via confidence was calculated at −0.036 (SE=0.006, 95% CI −0.048 to −0.026, p<0.001), accounting for 21.95% of the total effect. These findings underscore the significance of both happiness and confidence as mediators in the relationship between friendly community environments and depressive symptoms.

Urban-rural heterogeneity analysis

China’s enduring urban-rural dual structure has led to systematic inequalities between urban and rural populations across various dimensions, including income, consumption, education and healthcare.20 Combining urban and rural samples in analyses may undermine the validity of the results. Our study identified statistically significant differences in community environment scores between urban and rural settings (t=2.690, p<0.01). Consequently, performing distinct analyses for urban and rural regions may provide a more precise representation of social realities and further evaluate the robustness of the conclusions drawn in this study.

As illustrated in figure 2, within urban samples, friendly community environments were positively correlated with the happiness (β=0.112, 95% CI 0.083 to 0.144, p<0.001) and confidence (β=0.144, 95% CI 0.113 to 0.174, p<0.001) of older adults, while exhibiting a negative correlation with depressive symptoms (β=−0.059, 95% CI −0.090 to −0.031, p<0.001). Additionally, significant negative correlations were found between depressive symptoms and both happiness (β=−0.144, 95% CI −0.178 to −0.113, p<0.001) and confidence (β=−0.153, 95% CI −0.184 to −0.122, p<0.001).

Discussion

This research employs longitudinal data from the CFPS to conduct an empirical examination of the association between friendly community environments and depressive symptoms in older adults, while also investigating the mediating mechanisms involved. The results indicate that friendly communities play a significant role in mitigating depressive symptoms among older adults, a finding that is consistent across both urban and rural demographics. This conclusion aligns with prior research in the field15 and supports the ecological systems theory, which emphasises the interaction between macro-environmental factors and individual psychological development.21 First, improved community amenities provide a safe and accessible environment for seniors to participate in outdoor activities, including physical exercise, which is known to stimulate the release of mood-enhancing hormones such as dopamine.22 Existing literature suggests that moderate physical activity not only contributes to physical health but also enhances overall well-being and resilience against depression.23 Additionally, a clean and sanitary community environment is essential in reducing the risk of infectious diseases among older adults. Inadequate sanitation can facilitate the transmission of pathogens, thereby compromising the immune system and increasing vulnerability to illnesses, which are recognised triggers for depressive episodes in this population.24 Furthermore, the social dynamics within friendly communities are critical. Enhanced social interactions in cohesive neighbourhood settings act as a protective factor against loneliness, a major contributor to depression among older adults.25 By fostering a sense of belonging and companionship, such environments promote mental well-being and help to alleviate the onset of depressive symptoms.26 Moreover, a well-regulated living environment fosters a sense of security among older residents, which has a direct impact on their mental health. The perception of safety and support within their community reduces feelings of anxiety and tension,27 thereby contributing to a healthier mental state and a decrease in depressive symptoms.

Our findings emphasise the critical mediating role of happiness and confidence in the relationship between friendly communities and depressive symptoms among older adults, a result that remains robust across both urban and rural samples. This conclusion extends research on the mechanisms linking friendly communities to depressive symptoms in the elderly and aligns more closely with the assumptions of the diathesis-stress model and ecosystem theory, indicating that external environments can influence psychological protective factors in older adults. Happiness, often synonymous with satisfaction, encompasses positive emotional states and overall contentment with life circumstances.28 For older adults, experiencing positive emotions like contentment and joy fosters psychological resilience and cultivates a positive outlook on life,29 serving as a buffer against negative emotions. Similarly, a sense of confidence reflects older adults’ belief in their abilities and optimistic outlook towards the future.30 Despite facing challenges such as health issues and aging, older individuals with a strong sense of confidence exhibit resilience and adaptability,31 mitigating the impact of retirement or health concerns on their mental well-being. Research indicates a strong correlation between happiness, confidence and the mental health of older adults,32 with higher levels of these traits associated with greater resilience to depressive states. The community environment serves as a crucial setting for older adults to live in their later years. A friendly and supportive community offers social engagement opportunities, fostering a sense of belonging and enhancing overall well-being. Additionally, a secure and welcoming living environment bolsters feelings of safety and belonging among older residents, reinforcing their sense of confidence in facing the future. Ultimately, a conducive community environment plays a pivotal role in shaping the confidence and happiness of older adults, thereby reducing their vulnerability to depression.

Limitations

The present study is subject to several notable limitations. First, while the CFPS offers nationally representative data, it is constrained by the limited number of variables included, which results in the omission of several pertinent variables relevant to this research. This limitation may hinder a comprehensive exploration of the research topic. Second, the constructs of confidence and happiness were assessed using single-item measures. Although the use of one-item scales is acknowledged in the literature, future investigations would benefit from employing more comprehensive scales to ensure adequate validity. Third, both the perception of a friendly community and depressive symptoms were evaluated based on participants’ subjective reports, which may be influenced by individual perceptions that do not necessarily align with objective reality. This reliance on self-reported data may introduce measurement bias. Finally, our model analysis incorporated one-period lagged explanatory variables, dependent variables and mediating variables. While this methodology enhances the precision of causal inference, it is important to note that future research should consider additional methodologies, such as instrumental variable techniques, to further validate causal relationships.

Implications

The establishment of friendly communities designed to meet the needs of older adults is of critical importance. As population ageing emerges as a significant demographic trend globally, it is imperative for governments to prioritise the creation of such communities to effectively serve the increasing number of older individuals. This initiative involves the development of accessible environments, the enhancement of community-based recreational and healthcare facilities, the promotion of a positive social atmosphere and the fostering of well-being and self-assurance among older residents. Furthermore, it is essential to recognise the notable disparities between urban and rural social contexts, as these differences should inform the development of age-friendly communities to optimise the effectiveness of supportive initiatives. In addition, within the framework of age-friendly communities, it is crucial to address not only the physical requirements of older adults but also their psychological needs, particularly in terms of alleviating loneliness and improving their overall sense of happiness and security.
