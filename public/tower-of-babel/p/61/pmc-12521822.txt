Central Nervous System Tuberculosis: A Case of Tuberculous Myelitis
Authors: Huan Nie, Xin Liu, Hui Song, Qingxia Liu, Linyuan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculous myelitis is rare in central nervous system (CNS) tuberculosis. Misdiagnosis initially may lead to poor outcomes; empirical antituberculosis treatment can improve symptoms. Early diagnosis via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and spinal MRI, plus timely treatment, is vital—clinical awareness needs enhancement.

Summary Mycobacterium tuberculosis is a very rare cause of transverse myelitis, especially tuberculous myelitis without meningitis, which is even rarer. Doctors can identify spinal cord tuberculosis early through medical history, physical examination, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination results, and spinal cord MRI results,and treat it early so that patients can have a better prognosis.

Introduction

Tuberculosis, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis , remains a global health concern, affecting various organs and systems [1, 2]. Among its complications, central nervous system (CNS) involvement is particularly severe. CNS tuberculosis encompasses a spectrum of manifestations, with tuberculous meningitis being the most common form, followed by intracranial tuberculomas, tuberculous brain abscesses, and spinal tubercular arachnoiditis [3, 4]. The prevalence of CNS tuberculosis among all tuberculosis cases is approximately 1%, and it accounts for 5%–10% of extrapulmonary tuberculosis cases [3, 5]. When considering spinal cord involvement within CNS tuberculosis, it becomes even rarer. This condition often has an insidious onset and lacks distinct clinical features, frequently leading to missed or misdiagnosed cases. However, it is associated with high rates of disability and mortality. Most of the existing literature on tuberculous myelitis is derived from case reports [6]. The pathogenesis of tuberculous myelitis is mainly attributed to the dissemination of M. tuberculosis from other body areas, mainly the lungs, via the bloodstream, direct infiltration from spinal tuberculosis, or through the pathogenic bacteria and inflammatory exudates associated with tuberculous meningitis [7]. The bacteria spread through the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), resulting in damage to the spinal cord. Tuberculous myelitis that originates specifically in spinal cord segments is extremely rare. The clinical manifestations of this condition vary depending on the site of involvement. Most patients eventually experience paralysis, sensory deficits, and urinary and fecal dysfunction below the level of the spinal cord lesions. In this case, the patient initially presented with low back pain, followed by a gradual onset of weakness in both lower limbs and urinary retention. The condition progressed rapidly. At the time of admission, no signs of tuberculosis in other regions of the body were identified, suggesting a greater likelihood of spinal cord origin.

Case Presentation

Case History

A 66‐year‐old man was admitted to the hospital at 20:43 on March 5, 2023, due to low back pain that lasted for 1 day and lower limb weakness for half a day. The day before admission, the patient began to experience back pain and chose to perform cupping treatment at home. However, the back pain symptoms were not relieved, and he was also accompanied by low back pain and hip pain. Burns appeared on the skin where cupping was performed. Subsequently, the patient went to the emergency department of our hospital and went home after symptomatic treatment. Half a day ago, the patient began to feel weakness in both lower limbs after getting up, mainly on the right side. At that time, he was still able to walk with the help of others, but then his limb weakness worsened, and he was unable to walk, accompanied by the inability to urinate. Positive physical examination signs after admission included left lower limb muscle strength grade 3, right lower limb muscle strength grade 2, and hyporeflexia of the right lower limb. When asking about the patient's past medical history, it was learned that the patient has no chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, or coronary heart disease. The patient denied having had infectious diseases such as hepatitis, tuberculosis, syphilis, or AIDS. On a daily basis, the patient feels that his health is good.

Investigations and Treatment

The patient's lumbar spine CT examination ruled out bleeding and fractures, and initially no tumors or other lesions were found. The patient underwent spinal cord MRI, and the results showed no tumors or infections. Since the spinal cord MRI was normal (Figure 1), Guillain–Barré disease (GBS) could not be ruled out initially, but the possibility of myelitis still needs to be alerted. The patient's condition progressed significantly after admission. The muscle strength of the left lower limb dropped to level 1, and the muscle strength of the right lower limb dropped to level 0. The pain was symmetrical on both sides, and there was no obvious bandage sensation. Subsequently, puncture examination was perfected. First Lumbar Puncture (before treatment): The CSF appeared yellow, with a pressure of 150 cmH2O. Tests revealed a CSF chloride level of 119.40 mmol/L, glucose of 2.79 mmol/L, protein of 11.77 g/L, and a positive Paneth's test. The white blood cell count was 88 × 106/L (monocytes/lymphocytes + monocytes accounted for 24.5%, and polymorphonuclear cells for 75.7%). CSF culture showed no abnormalities; no pathogenic bacteria were detected in CSF metagenomic testing, and the T‐SPOT test result was negative. Second Lumbar Puncture: After the patient received hormone pulse therapy and immunoglobulin therapy, there was no significant improvement in symptoms. A follow‐up lumbar puncture showed that the CSF remained light yellow, with pressure increased to 200 cmH2O. CSF chloride was 107.60 mmol/L, glucose 1.39 mmol/L, protein 11.80 g/L, and the Paneth's test remained positive. The white blood cell count rose to 825 × 106/L. M. tuberculosis TB‐DNA detection in CSF was negative. Meanwhile, abdominal pressure and neck pressure tests were significantly positive, suggesting CSF circulation obstruction. Third Lumbar Puncture: 1 week after the initiation of empirical antituberculosis treatment, a re‐evaluation lumbar puncture was performed. The appearance of CSF returned to normal, with a pressure of 110 cmH2O, and no obstruction was found in either abdominal or neck pressure tests. Laboratory tests showed CSF chloride at 121.00 mmol/L, normal glucose levels, a protein concentration of 0.62 g/L, a weakly positive Paneth's test, and a white blood cell count of 24 × 106/L (monocytes/lymphocytes + monocytes accounted for 93.20%, and polymorphonuclear cells for 6.80%). Results of CSF tuberculosis smear, culture, and TB‐DNA were all negative (Table 1).

As the condition progressed, a contrast‐enhanced lumbar MRI was completed, showing abnormal enhancement in the posterior lateral dura mater/vertebral body attachment and subcutaneous area at the L2–L5 segments. This indicated local blood–brain barrier disruption, consistent with the imaging characteristics of infectious lesions (Figure 1). To rule out secondary tuberculosis infection, physical examination revealed no palpable enlargement of superficial lymph nodes, no skin nodules or ulcers, no spinal deformity or tenderness, and a soft, flat abdomen without tenderness. A chest CT scan showed no intrapulmonary tuberculosis foci, and abdominal ultrasound revealed no hepatosplenomegaly or peritoneal effusion. Based on symptom characteristics (only lower extremity involvement, no vision loss), dynamic laboratory tests (no protein–cell dissociation, no bacterial growth in culture), imaging findings (no space‐occupying lesions), and treatment response (ineffectiveness of hormone/immunoglobulin therapy), GBS, autoimmune myelitis, suppurative myelitis, and spinal tumors were excluded. One week after treatment, CSF appearance, pressure, chloride, glucose, protein, white blood cell count, and differential count all returned to normal, the obstruction was resolved, and clinical symptoms improved. This was consistent with the core feature that “tuberculous lesions are sensitive to specific antituberculosis treatment.” Although CSF tuberculosis smear, culture, and TB‐DNA were all negative, considering the low sensitivity of tuberculosis etiological detection, the clinical diagnosis was established. The patient had no systemic tuberculosis symptoms such as low‐grade fever or night sweats, and chest CT, abdominal ultrasound, and other examinations ruled out tuberculosis in other parts of the body, consistent with the characteristics of “isolated tuberculous myelitis.” It is supposed that tuberculosis caused arachnoiditis, which may have later evolved into myelitis without triggering systemic infection manifestations.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Following antituberculosis therapy and rehabilitation, the patient's latest follow‐up on June 1, 2024, indicated a recovery of muscle strength in both lower limbs to level 5.

Discussion

CNS tuberculosis infection is the most severe form of tuberculosis infection, with the meningitis type being the most common and the spinal type having a relatively low incidence. This disease is prone to misdiagnosis as other infections, tumors, or demyelinating lesions. Current understanding suggests that abnormal immune system activation leads to an immune attack, which constitutes the pathological mechanism of tuberculous myelitis [8, 9, 10]. The clinical manifestations of this disease vary based on the affected site. When nerve roots are involved, the primary manifestations include radicular pain and sensory impairment; when the meninges are affected, segmental asymmetrical sensory impairment is observed; and when the spinal cord is involved, early‐stage symptoms may include urinary retention and urgency, while late‐stage symptoms may progress to urinary incontinence and dissociative sensory disorders. Most patients ultimately experience paralysis, sensory impairment, and urinary and fecal dysfunction below the level of the spinal cord lesion. In this case, the condition initially presented with radicular pain, which was subsequently followed by limb paralysis and urinary impairment. Notably, this patient did not exhibit the sensory deficits commonly associated with most spinal cord lesions. It is essential to rule out GBS and myelitis upon admission. The patient was not diagnosed with tuberculous myelitis after lumbar puncture for the following main reasons: First, the patient onset was acute and progressed rapidly, and its onset characteristics were inconsistent with the subacute or chronic manifestations of spinal tuberculosis. Second, the patient became ill during the outbreak of the epidemic in China, so we are more inclined to consider the patient to have an autoimmune reaction in the CNS after viral infection. The results of the MRI scan after admission showed no abnormalities. In addition, the CSF examination showed increased cells, mainly multinucleated cells, and all tuberculosis‐related examinations were negative. Finally, the patient had no history of tuberculosis, and no pulmonary tuberculosis, spinal tuberculosis, or tuberculosis in other locations was found after admission. The patient's second lumbar puncture revealed significant spinal cord obstruction, and the laboratory results did not show any improvement. This situation raised our concerns. Given the possibility of tuberculous myelitis, we conducted an enhanced MRI examination of the spinal cord, which indicated abnormalities. In cases of tuberculous myelitis, CSF analysis typically demonstrates elevated protein levels, hypoglycemia, and cytosis [11, 12]. If a patient develops subarachnoid stenosis or obstruction in the spinal cord, the protein levels in the CSF may be abnormally elevated. In this patient, the onset of symptoms primarily manifested as nerve root irritation, which may result from the adhesion of inflammatory exudates that lead to stenosis or obstruction of the subarachnoid space within the spinal cord. Consequently, the patient's CSF protein levels were significantly elevated. Some researchers have noted that the detection of adenosine dehydrogenase in CSF has a certain specificity for diagnosing CNS tuberculosis, which is particularly important for the diagnosis of tuberculous meningomyelitis [13]. Spinal cord MRI, both plain and enhanced scans, can effectively delineate the location, size, and extent of lesions, with reported sensitivity and specificity rates of 100% and 88%, respectively. Furthermore, when combined with CSF examination, the diagnostic accuracy for tuberculous myelitis is significantly enhanced. Typically, T1‐weighted images exhibit low signal intensity, while T2‐weighted images demonstrate high signal intensity, indicating spinal cord edema and thickening, along with thickening of the meninges. Enhanced scans may reveal irregular enhancements, such as lines and plaques, in the meninges. However, it is important to note that these imaging findings lack specificity. The cervicothoracic spinal cord is the most commonly affected region, accounting for approximately 90% of cases, with hyperintensity on T2‐weighted images being the most consistent finding [14, 15]. The discrepancy between the plain lumbar spine MRI scan conducted on March 8, 2023, which revealed no abnormalities, and the enhanced lumbar spine MRI scan performed on March 21, 2023, which indicated abnormalities, remains unclear. This situation underscores the importance of recognizing that the symptoms of tuberculous myelitis cannot be entirely dismissed based solely on normal test results. A comprehensive evaluation of the patient's individual circumstances, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, and treatment responses is essential for accurate diagnosis in tuberculosis cases.

The management of tuberculous myelitis should be grounded in a consistent regimen of systemic antituberculosis therapy. It is crucial to ensure an adequate dosage and a sufficient duration of combined antituberculosis treatment, particularly in patients at early stages of the disease [10]. The prognosis for patients is often closely linked to the promptness of the treatment administered. Several studies have demonstrated the potential efficacy of combining steroids with antituberculous drugs in the treatment of tuberculous myelitis. Clinical improvement is typically observed approximately 12 weeks after the initiation of treatment [11, 12, 16]. The presence of bone marrow softening or cavitation on MRI may serve as a poor prognostic indicator. Additionally, some studies have indicated that patients with syringomyelia tend to have a poorer recovery [13]. In contrast, spinal edema or extensive spinal cord involvement does not appear to significantly impact recovery [17].

Gupta et al. [18] reviewed and summarized the medical history data of 71 patients who were newly diagnosed with tuberculous myelitis. The results indicate that patients with simultaneous nerve root involvement tend to have a poor prognosis, with significantly elevated CSF protein levels (greater than 2.5 g/L) serving as an important predictive factor. Although the lesions in this patient involved nerve roots and CSF protein levels were significantly elevated, a follow‐up visit revealed that the patient's prognosis was fair. This outcome may be attributed to early treatment or the absence of underlying conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and heart disease, as well as the patient's heightened sensitivity to antituberculosis drugs. Studies indicate that 82%–95% of patients respond well to drug treatment [19].

Conclusions

M. tuberculosis is a very rare cause of transverse myelitis, especially tuberculous myelitis without meningitis, which is even rarer. The main cause is thought to be abnormal activation of the immune system in the spinal cord. Other suspected mechanisms include direct invasion of bacilli and toxic effects of antituberculosis drugs [20]. This case was not considered to have tuberculous myelitis at the time of initial diagnosis. Although the disease is rare, clinicians should combine early clinical diagnosis and treatment to avoid misdiagnosis and delayed treatment, reduce the risk of paralysis and death, and improve prognosis.
