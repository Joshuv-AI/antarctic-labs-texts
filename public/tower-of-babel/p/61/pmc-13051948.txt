Use of Z‐Scores and Percentages to Assess Structural Brain MRI Findings in Patients With Schizophrenia
Authors: Calypso A Mitkani, Panagiotis Prassopoulos, Anthony H Aletras, Konstantinos N Fountoulakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesSchizophrenia is associated with structural brain abnormalities, although findings have been inconsistent. This study aimed to investigate global and regional volumetric measures in patients with schizophrenia using an automated volumetric analysis tool.MethodsEighty‐one patients with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (aged 18–65 years) underwent brain MRI on a 3T Siemens scanner using a high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted MPRAGE sequence. Volumetric analysis was performed with vol2Brain, providing intracranial volume‐adjusted percentages and normative z‐scores. Group mean values were compared with normative data. One sample t‐test was used as well as exploratory t‐tests to examine sex differences, while Pearson correlations assessed associations with age.ResultsPatients showed decreased brain tissue volumes relative to normative values. Mean white matter (z = −1.12) and gray matter (z = −0.55) were reduced, while cerebrospinal fluid was increased (z = +1.75). Cortical gray matter was moderately decreased (z = −0.77), whereas cerebellar gray matter was relatively preserved (z = +0.19) but displayed high variability. No significant sex differences were observed. Age correlated significantly with volumetric percentages but not with z‐scores, suggesting parallel age‐related changes in patients and controls.ConclusionsPatients with schizophrenia demonstrated widespread reductions in brain tissue volumes and enlarged CSF spaces, consistent with previous literature. Preservation and variability of cerebellar gray matter highlight the heterogeneity of structural alterations. Automated volumetry offers a reproducible approach for characterizing brain morphology in schizophrenia and may support future efforts to define patient subgroups.Keywords: brain volumetry, gray matter, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), schizophrenia, white matter

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a chronic, severe mental disorder consistently associated with in vivo structural brain abnormalities as shown in numerous MRI investigations(Wright and McGuire 2000). These structural changes reflect neurodevelopmental alterations and potentially neurodegenerative processes in a subset of patients, involving gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (van Erp et al. 2019). However, this has not been definitely shown in all research studies (Fountoulakis and Stahl 2022).

Over the past 3 decades, a large body of MRI research has consistently reported global and regional reductions in GM volume, enlargement of lateral ventricles, and subtle WM changes in patients compared to healthy controls(Shenton et al. 2001; van Erp et al. 2019). However, the reported findings are far from uniform. Variability in study populations, illness chronicity, medication status, and methodological approaches has led to a heterogeneous pattern of structural abnormalities (Deniz and Landis 2019; Sadeghi et al. 2022). Moreover, voxel‐based morphometry and automated volumetric analyses have revealed that not all patients with schizophrenia exhibit the same degree or distribution of structural deficits, suggesting the presence of subgroups with distinct neuroanatomical profiles (Szendi et al. 2017).

An important question is whether these structural alterations in schizophrenia represent a progressive decline or remain relatively static over time. Recent findings suggest that while structural deficits emerge early in the disease course, subsequent age‐related decline parallels that of healthy individuals, supporting the concept of relatively stable neuroanatomical changes rather than continuous deterioration(Merritt et al. 2021).

The present study aimed to explore the volumetric pattern of WM, GM, cortical GM, cerebellar GM, and CSF in a sample of patients with schizophrenia. We also examined potential sex differences and correlations with age to further elucidate the heterogeneity of structural brain changes in this population and their nature.

Methods

The study sample consisted of 81 patients (34 females‐41.97% and 47 males 58.03%) who are under regular follow‐up and care at the outpatient clinic of the third Department of Psychiatry at AHEPA University Hospital in Thessaloniki, Greece, between February 2022 and July 2025. Participants were aged 18–65 years, had a DSM‐5 diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, and were receiving stable antipsychotic treatment for at least one month prior to MRI scanning. Exclusion criteria included any brain MRI performed within the last 5 years, a history of vascular risk factors (e.g., arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, stroke, or peripheral vascular disease), and neurological or systemic conditions with known effects on the central nervous system (CNS). Patients with severe substance or alcohol dependence or abuse during the past 2 years were also excluded.

All participants underwent brain MRI at the Department of Radiology of AHEPA University Hospital using a 3 T S MAGNETOM Vida scanner. The protocol included a high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2200 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.4 ms, flip angle = 3°, and slice thickness = 1.0 mm. The acquisition matrix was 256 × 256 with a field of view (FOV) of 256 mm, resulting in an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm. All images were visually inspected for motion artifacts prior to volumetric analysis.

Image preprocessing and volumetric analysis were performed using the vol2Brain platform, an upgraded version of volBrain(Manjón and Coupé 2016; Manjón et al. 2022). The vol2Brain pipeline includes automated quality control procedures such as skull‐stripping verification, segmentation consistency checks, and comparison with normative templates In the absence of a control group, results were compared with normative values derived from the built‐in vol2Brain normative database, which is based on large‐scale healthy reference populations adjusted for age, sex, and intracranial volume (Ge et al. 2023; Potvin et al. 2016). Age‐, sex‐, and intracranial volume‐adjusted z‐scores were calculated for each participant. The volume percentage (% volume) represents the proportion of the structure's volume relative to the total intracranial volume (ICV), expressed as a percentage. This value is directly provided by the VolBrain system and allows for comparisons between individuals of varying brain sizes, as it normalizes structural volume to total ICV. The z‐score for each brain structure was computed by subtracting the expected mean volume (derived from a healthy reference population) from the observed volume, and then dividing the result by the standard deviation of the healthy population. Positive z‐scores indicate volumes greater than the normative average, whereas negative z‐scores reflect volume reductions.

The statistical analysis included one sample t‐test to examine the difference of z‐scores from zero. Exploratory t‐tests and Pearson correlation coefficients were used to evaluate sex differences and associations with age. Because the study did not include a control group and relied on comparisons with normative datasets, the statistical approach was primarily descriptive and exploratory rather than inferential or causal.

A Bonferroni correction was applied to the results. Since nine brain structures were studied, the p‐level chosen as the level of significance was 0.05/9 = 0.0055 that is p < 0.005.

The mean white matter (WM) volume corresponded to 35.50% of the intracranial volume, with values ranging from 29.49% to 43.70%. The corresponding mean z‐score was −1.12, indicating that patients had lower WM volumes than expected based on normative data, with a large portion of the sample falling below the population mean.

Gray matter (GM) volume averaged 47.64% ICV (z = −0.55), while cortical GM volume was 36.78% ICV (z = −0.77), both reflecting moderate reductions compared to the normative mean. Cerebellar GM was relatively preserved (mean = 7.78% ICV, z = 0.19), though with considerable individual variability.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) volume showed a mean value of 15.56% ICV and a positive mean z‐score of 1.75, suggesting increased ventricular and subarachnoid spaces relative to age‐ and sex‐matched controls.

Total brain volume (GM + WM) represented 83.14% of ICV, with a mean z‐score of −1.70, indicating substantial global brain tissue reduction. Cerebral volumes were also reduced bilaterally (right hemisphere: 36.63% ICV, z = −1.63; left hemisphere: 36.45% ICV, z = −1.75), pointing to diffuse atrophy without significant hemispheric asymmetry. Discussion

The results of the current study suggest that in our sample of patients with schizophrenia there were no differences in brain MRI between males and females. Overall, on the basis of normative data, there was a substantial total brain volume (GM + WM) tissue reduction bilaterally, suggesting a diffuse pattern of brain tissue reduction without clear hemispheric predominance. The only structure that did not manifest a volume loss was the cerebellar gray matter. Patients had lower than expected WM volumes, moderate reduction in GM volume and subsequently increased ventricular and subarachnoid spaces relative to age‐ and sex‐matched controls. On the contrary, the cerebellar GM was relatively preserved.

Several studies have demonstrated reductions in total GM in schizophrenia, supported by both individual imaging studies and meta‐analyses. In particular, Wright and McGuire (2000) and van Erp et al. (2019) reported widespread GM reductions, notably in prefrontal, temporal, and hippocampal regions(van Erp et al. 2019; Wright and McGuire 2000). Our findings are consistent with these observations: the negative mean z‐scores for GM (−0.55) and WM (−1.12) indicate deviations from age‐ and sex‐adjusted norms, while the total brain tissue volume (WM + GM) also shows a pronounced reduction (mean z = −1.70).

We observed increased CSF volume both in raw percentage (15.56% ICV) and z‐score (1.75), which aligns with previous studies emphasizing ventricular enlargement in schizophrenia (Merritt et al. 2021; Sadeghi et al. 2022). This likely reflects secondary expansion due to parenchymal loss.

Our data also lend nuance to cerebellar involvement. Although cerebellar GM appears relatively preserved in percentage terms (7.78%), the wide z‐score range (mean z = 0.19; −2.47–6.45) reveals substantial inter‐individual variability, paralleling findings from the literature(Deniz and Landis 2019). Such variability could implicate cerebellar contributions to executive and cognitive deficits beyond motor function (Constantinides 2022).

Population heterogeneity is further highlighted by the broad range of WM (−3.68 to 2.34) and GM (−3.15 to 1.70) z‐scores. This echoes voxel‐based and automated volumetry literature suggesting structural abnormalities vary substantially among patients (Keller et al. 2003; Lyu et al. 2019) supporting the notion of neuroanatomical subtypes within schizophrenia (Sadeghi et al. 2022; Shenton et al. 2001; Szendi et al. 2017).

Interestingly, while older literature has emphasized progressive brain changes in schizophrenia, more recent longitudinal data challenge this notion, indicating that the majority of structural deficits emerge early in the disease and remain relatively stable over time (Merritt et al. 2021). Our findings, which show age‐related declines that are comparable to those seen in normative aging, further, support this perspective.

As in every volumetric study, there are several limitations. In this study volumetric values were expressed as percentages relative to intracranial volume (ICV). Since ICV may be smaller in patients with schizophrenia, normalization could obscure genuine anatomical differences. In addition, comparisons with normative datasets are influenced by inter‐individual variability and methodological differences(Ge et al. 2023; Potvin et al. 2016). Finally, as emphasized in recent reviews, neuroimaging findings in schizophrenia remain inconsistent across studies, partly due to methodological heterogeneity (Sadeghi et al. 2022).

In summary, our study confirms the presence of widespread but variable brain structural abnormalities in schizophrenia, particularly involving GM, WM, and cerebellar volumes, along with an increase in CSF. These alterations are largely consistent with recent neuroimaging literature and underscore the heterogeneous nature of structural brain changes in this disorder. Future research incorporating multimodal imaging, longitudinal follow‐up, and genetic data could provide further insights into the neurobiological underpinnings and clinical relevance of these heterogeneous volumetric findings.
