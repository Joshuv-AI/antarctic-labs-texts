Relapse risk and outcome of discontinuing asparaginase in children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Xingchen Wang, Chenmeng Liu, Yunlong Chen, Junxia Wang, Yang Wan, Wenbin An, Xiaolan Li, Lipeng Liu, Fang Liu, Tianyuan Hu, Yingchi Zhang, Li Zhang, Yao Zou, Xiaojuan Chen, Yumei Chen, Ye Guo, Xiaofan Zhu, Wenyu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsparaginase is a cornerstone of pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) therapy, but treatment discontinuation due to toxicity may increase relapse risk and compromise outcomes. We retrospectively analyzed 993 pediatric ALL patients (aged 1.0–17.9 years) treated under the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol from May 2015 to October 2020. Patients were followed from the last administered asparaginase dose until relapse, death, secondary malignancy (SMN), or end of follow-up (median: 6.0 years). Asparaginase was truncated in 40 (4.0%) patients, primarily due to hypersensitivity or pancreatitis. Compared to non-truncated patients (n = 953), truncated patients had a higher 6-year cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR: 31.5% vs. 17.5%, HR: 2.01, P = .038), as well as inferior event-free survival (EFS) (HR: 2.11, P = .015) and overall survival (OS) (HR: 2.74, P = .046). CIR was also significantly higher among patients receiving < 53% of planned asparaginase doses (32.1% vs. 14.0%, HR: 2.71, P = .004). In intermediate-risk subgroup, truncation was associated with significantly reduced EFS (HR: 1.95, P = .046). Older age (≥ 10 years), higher MRD levels (day 19 ≥ 0.1%), and higher asparaginase exposure percentage were predictors of discontinuation. In conclusion, asparaginase discontinuation is associated with inferior outcomes, emphasizing the importance of replacement with alternative formulations to maintain treatment intensity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06682-7.Keywords: Pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Asparaginase, Discontinuation, Relapse, Outcome

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) is a heterogeneous haematological malignancy that occurs mainly in children [1]. The overall survival (OS) for children with ALL is now above 90% in most contemporary protocols [2]. The treatment of ALL relies on long-term multi-agent chemotherapy, with asparaginase being one of the cornerstone drugs. In recent years, immunotherapeutic approaches have also been increasingly integrated. Studies have shown that adequate dosing of asparaginase is associated with improved survival in pediatric patients [3–5].

Asparagine is a nonessential amino acid for normal cell growth but is considered essential for leukaemic cells as these cells are dependent on exogenous sources of asparagine for survival. Asparaginase selectively kills leukaemic cells by depleting asparagine levels [6]. Asparaginase formulations are primarily derived from either Escherichia coli or Erwinia chrysanthemi. Currently used products include native E. Coli L-asparaginase (L-asparaginase), PEGylated asparaginase (PEG-asparaginase), and Erwinia-derived asparaginase. Additionally, newer formulations such as recombinant E. coli asparaginase (Spectrila®), recombinant Erwinia asparaginase (JZP458), and calaspargase pegol (a PEG-conjugated formulation with improved pharmacokinetics) have been approved in certain regions. Due to its longer half-life and reduced hypersensitivity, PEG-asparaginase is the preferred first-line asparaginase formulation in most contemporary treatment protocols [7]– [8]. Although asparaginase plays a crucial role in the treatment of ALL, its toxicities may lead to treatment discontinuation. The leading causes of asparaginase discontinuation include hypersensitivity, pancreatitis, thromboembolism, and silent inactivation (SI) [3, 9–11]. Evidence from large-scale studies, particularly from the Children’s Oncology Group (COG), has demonstrated that discontinuation of asparaginase significantly increases the risk of relapse. Importantly, no increased risk of relapse has been observed when patients with hypersensitivity to PEG-asparaginase are promptly switched to Erwinia asparaginase. The COG analysis was the first large study to establish this critical association [12–14].

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relapse risk associated with asparaginase discontinuation in a Chinese pediatric cohort treated with the Chinese Children’s Cancer Group ALL-2015 (CCCG-ALL-2015) protocol, a contemporary regimen differing from the COG-based protocols. In addition, we sought to identify clinical factors associated with early discontinuation of asparaginase therapy.

Materials and methods

Patients

Between May 2015 and October 2020, children and adolescents aged 1.0–17.9 years with newly diagnosed ALL who were treated according to the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol were enrolled. Philadelphia chromosome positive ALL, mature B-ALL, mixed phenotype leukemia, second tumor, congenital immunodeficiency disease, and metabolic disease were excluded. We restricted this analysis to patients who completed reinduction treatment and excluded patients who were taken off the study before reinduction treatment and patients who proceeded to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). All patients received risk-stratified and minimal residual disease (MRD)-directed therapy without prophylactic cranial irradiation. Details regarding protocol, risk stratification, and classification of central nervous system (CNS) involvement and MRD response are provided in the supplementary material. At diagnosis, cytogenetic/molecular testing included conventional karyotyping, Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH), and targeted Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) according to institutional standards. Cytogenetic risk groups were defined a priori as follows: favorable—ETV6::RUNX1 fusion or high hyperdiploidy (≥ 50 chromosomes); unfavorable—hypodiploidy (< 44 chromosomes), KMT2A (11q23) rearrangements, intrachromosomal amplification of chromosome 21 (iAMP21), and TCF3::HLF. Intermediate cytogenetics included normal karyotype, and other abnormalities not meeting favorable or unfavorable definitions. CNS status at diagnosis was assessed via cytospin morphology of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) obtained by lumbar puncture. Patients classified as CNS3 were excluded from the low-risk group and were treated with protocol-defined triple intrathecal chemotherapy. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Asparaginase in the CCCG- ALL 2015 protocol

The CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol is based on St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital Total XV study. It comprises window, induction (weeks 1–7), consolidation (weeks 8–15), maintenance I (weeks 16–34), and maintenance II chemotherapy (weeks 35–126). All patients received the same window treatment containing dexamethasone (6 mg/m2/day, 4 days). For low-risk ALL pediatric patients, the treatment includes 3 doses of L-asparaginase: one during induction chemotherapy and two during reinduction therapy 1 and 2. For intermediate and high-risk patients, the treatment includes 8–9 doses PEG-asparaginase: two during induction chemotherapy, five during continuation therapy, and one during reinduction chemotherapy. For T-cell ALL or day 19 MRD ≥ 1%, an extra dose of PEG-asparaginase was given. There are three types of asparaginase agents commonly: E. coli L-asparaginase, PEG-asparaginase and Erwinia asparaginase. Peg-asparaginase 2000 U/m2 one dose, Erwinia asparaginase 10,000 U/m2 three times per week for 2 weeks, or L-asparaginase 6000 U/m2 3 times per week may be interchangeable each other if necessary (Supplementary file 1).

Outcomes and statistical analyses

Asparaginase truncation was defined as failure to receive all protocol-scheduled doses due to toxicity or intolerance. Patients who discontinued asparaginase before completing the planned doses were classified as truncated, whereas those who received full substitution with an alternative formulation (e.g., Erwinia) and completed the scheduled number of doses were categorized as non-truncated. Asparaginase exposure percentage was calculated as the ratio of the number of doses actually received to the total number of doses specified by the protocol. This variable was analyzed as a continuous variable in survival analyses to evaluate its association with clinical outcomes. The optimal cut-off for asparaginase exposure percentage was determined through exploratory testing. Multiple thresholds were assessed, and 53% was selected based on its ability to most effectively distinguish relapse risk between groups with statistically significant differences.

Patients were followed from ALL diagnosis until relapse, death, SMN, lost to follow-up or Sep 2024. The study did not record asparaginase activity thus, we could not account for truncated asparaginase therapy based on silent hypersensitivity. The cumulative incidence relapse (CIR) of asparaginase discontinuation was calculated in the overall cohort using the Aalen-Johansen estimator, with non-relapse death, and SMN considered as competing events. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as time from diagnosis to relapse, death, development of SMN, or last follow-up. OS and EFS data were compared using the Kaplan-Meier estimate and Mantel-Cox log-rank test to assess. CIR were estimated by the Aalen-Johansen estimator, and Cox proportional-hazards model was used to estimate unadjusted hazard ratios (HR) and adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with significance evaluated by Wald tests. Predictors of asparaginase discontinuation were identified using logistic regression analysis, with variables significant at P <0.1 in univariable analysis included in the multivariable model. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0) and R statistical software (version 3.6).

Results

Patient characteristics

After excluding patients who experienced an event (defined as relapse, death, or SMN) or underwent HSCT before completing scheduled asparaginase treatment, a total of 993 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of these patients are given in Table 1. The median age of patients was 5.3 years (range: 0.5–17.0), and 594 (60%) were male. The median WBC was 10.1 × 109/L (range: 0.7–846.5), 805 (81.1%) had WBC < 50 × 109/L at diagnosis, and 188 (18.9%) had WBC ≥ 50 × 109/L. A total of 478 patients (48.1%) were classified as low-risk, 508 (51.2%) as intermediate-risk, and 7 (0.7%) as high-risk. The majority of patients (90%) were CNS1. Cytogenetic data were available for 898 (90.4%) patients. Among these, 71 (7.1%) were categorized as favorable cytogenetics, and 827 (83.3%) as other cytogenetics. Of the 993 patients, 40 (4.0%) had their asparaginase treatment truncated. By risk stratum, permanent discontinuation occurred in 8/478 (1.7%) low-risk and 32/508 (6.3%) intermediate-risk. Median number of asparaginase doses given in the truncated group was 3 (range: 1–8) versus 8 (range: 3–9) in the non-truncated group. Among all 993 patients, 27 cases of pancreatitis (2.7%) and 4 cases of thromboembolism (0.4%) were recorded. Of the four patients with thromboembolic events, one experienced a hypersensitivity reaction to PEG-asparaginase during the second dose, and subsequently developed cerebral sinus thrombosis (left sigmoid sinus) after the fifth dose, which led to permanent discontinuation of asparaginase. In total, 487 patients (49.1%) developed grade 3–4 coagulation disorders, of whom two discontinued asparaginase due to intracranial hemorrhage. The reasons for truncation were clinical hypersensitivity (52.5%), pancreatitis (40.0%), thromboembolism (2.5%) and coagulation disorder (5.0%) (Table 2). There were significant differences between the truncated group and the non-truncated group in these subgroups with respect to age (< 10 vs. ≥ 10; P =.038) and risk stratification (P =.001).

Survival analysis

With a median follow-up time of 6.0 years (range: 0.5–9.3), 39 patients died, 3 had developed a SMN, and 139 patients relapsed. The OS and EFS rates were 96.1% and 85.3%, respectively. EFS was significantly lower in the truncated group compared with the non-truncated group (HR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.14–3.90, P =.015). OS showed a borderline decrease in the truncated group (HR = 2.74, 95% CI 0.97–7.71, P =.046) (Fig. 2A, B). There was no impact of discontinuing asparaginase on OS (P =.68) or EFS (P =.83) in patients with low-risk ALL(Fig. 2C, D). Since only 7 patients were classified as high-risk and none of them experienced asparaginase discontinuation, these patients were excluded from the intermediate/high-risk subgroup analyses. In intermediate-risk patients, truncation of asparaginase was associated with a trend toward inferior OS (HR: 2.60, 95%CI: 0.90–7.51, P =.078), and EFS was significantly reduced in the truncated group (HR: 1.95; 95% CI: 1.01–3.76; P =.046) (Fig. 2E, F). Multiple Cox model indicated that WBC at diagnosis (HR: 1.68, 95% CI: 1.13–2.59, P =.01), cytogenetics (HR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.08–2.34, P =.02) and MRD at 46 days (HR: 2.33, 95% CI: 1.56–3.48, P <.001) were significantly associated with EFS. Asparaginase exposure percentage as a continuous variable did not impact OS (P =.10) or EFS (P =.06) (supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Cumulative incidence and risk of relapse

Until the end of follow-up, the 6-year CIR for the non-truncated group was 14.0% (95% CI: 11.8–16.3) and for the truncated group 27.5% (95% CI: 14.7–41.9) with HR of 2.24 (95% CI: 1.21–4.15, P =.01) (Fig. 3A). Among low-risk patients, asparaginase dosage was not associated with relapse (p =.84). In the intermediate-risk patient group, the CIR was significantly higher for patients who truncated asparaginase compared with those non-truncated patients (31.3% vs. 17.6%, HR: 2.00, 95%CI: 1.04–3.85, P =.04) (Fig. 3B, C). Among the 139 patients with relapse, 103 experienced bone marrow relapse, 10 had testicular relapse, 8 had CNS relapse, and the remaining involved combined relapses. The 6-year cumulative incidence of bone marrow relapse for the truncated patients versus the non-truncated patients was 22.5% (95% CI: 11.0–36.5) and 11.6% (95% CI:9.6–13.8), respectively, and the HR was 2.21 (95% CI:1.1–4.4, P =.02) (Fig. 3D). The 6-year cumulative incidence of extramedullary relapse for the truncated patients versus the non-truncated patients was 5.9% (95% CI: 1.0–17.4) and 3.9% (95% CI: 2.8–5.4) (P =.0.57; Fig. 3E). Excluding 16 patients who discontinued asparaginase treatment due to pancreatitis, the 6-year CIR was 26.1% in the truncated group (95% CI: 10.3–45.2) and 14% in the non-truncated group (95% CI: 11.8–16.3), with a HR of 2.04 (95% CI: 0.90–4.63, P =.09). Among patients who received Erwinia asparaginase substitution (n = 14), the 6-year CIR was 7.1% (95% CI: 0.4–28.5), compared to 14.1% (95% CI: 11.9–16.4) in patients who completed the planned asparaginase doses without substitution (n = 939) (P =.47). WBC ≥ 50 × 109, cytogenetics (intermediate/unfavorable vs. favorable), MRD (d46 ≥ 0.01%) and higher asparaginase exposure percentage were significantly associated with the relapse risk (Table 3).

Impact of asparaginase exposure percentage on relapse risk

Of the 40 truncated patients, the median asparaginase exposure percentage was 33% (range: 11–89), with a cut off at 53%. Based on asparaginase exposure percentage, we divided the patients into two groups: 28 (asparaginase exposure percentage < 53%) and 965 (asparaginase exposure percentage > 53%). The 6-year CIR for patients receiving < 53% of the asparaginase doses was 32.1% (95% CI: 15.8–49.7), compared to 14.0% (95% CI: 11.8–16.3) for those receiving > 53% of the asparaginase doses, with a HR of 2.71 (95% CI: 1.4–5.3, P =.004) (Fig. 4A). Compared to patients with asparaginase exposure percentage > 53%, those with exposure percentage < 53% had significantly reduced EFS (P =.004), while OS showed no significant difference (P =.057) (Fig. 4B, C). In multivariable analysis, older age, higher MRD levels, and asparaginase exposure percentage were associated with a higher risk of asparaginase discontinuation (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effect of asparaginase discontinuation on relapse risk and survival outcomes in pediatric patients with ALL treated under the CCCG-ALL 2015 protocol. We found that asparaginase discontinuation was significantly associated with an increased risk of relapse, particularly in intermediate-risk patients. Additionally, truncation of asparaginase therapy was associated with a significant reduction in EFS and showed a borderline reduction in OS (HR = 2.74, 95% CI 0.97–7.71, p =.046). This finding is consistent with Valtis et al. [13], who reported a similar association in pediatric patients.

Previous studies have shown that patients receiving higher cumulative doses of asparaginase have improved EFS, which is consistent with our findings. In our study, EFS was significantly lower in patients in the truncated group compared to those in the non-truncated group(p =.015) [3]– [4, 8, 12]– [13, 15–17]. When analyzing the impact of asparaginase discontinuation within intermediate-risk subgroup, we observed a significant reduction in EFS among patients in the truncated group (p =.041). In contrast, reduced exposure to asparaginase had no effect on OS or EFS in the low-risk subgroup. However, this observation should be interpreted with caution, as only eight low-risk patients experienced treatment truncation, which is insufficient for statistical comparisons. Moreover, the CCCG-ALL 2015 protocol only scheduled three doses of asparaginase for low-risk patients, potentially limiting the clinical impact of asparaginase discontinuation in this group. A report from the COG demonstrated that discontinuation of asparaginase was associated with significantly inferior disease-free survival (DFS) in National Cancer Institute (NCI) high-risk patients (P =.002), while no significant difference was observed in standard-risk patients (P =.23) [12]. In our study, patients who discontinued asparaginase were more frequently older (≥ 10 years) and belonged to intermediate-risk group. As both age and risk stratification are recognized adverse prognostic factors, this association may partially confound the observed relationship between asparaginase discontinuation and clinical outcomes. Consistent findings were reported in a Brazilian study [18], in which high-risk patients who received fewer than 10 doses of asparaginase were associated with poorer EFS (P =.013). The differential impact of asparaginase discontinuation between low-risk and intermediate-risk patients may be explained by differences in planned treatment intensity. According to the CCCG-ALL 2015 protocol, low-risk patients were scheduled to receive only 3–4 doses of asparaginase, and discontinuation typically resulted in the omission of just 1–2 doses. In contrast, intermediate-risk patients were scheduled to receive 8–9 doses, and most discontinuations occurred in this group. In our study, the median number of asparaginase doses in the truncated group was three, which may reflect the protocol-defined interval between the second dose during induction and the third dose during continuation therapy, when discontinuation frequently occurred.

Among asparaginase-related toxicities, pancreatitis is a leading cause of permanent treatment discontinuation, and patients who resume asparaginase therapy with an alternative formulation face a high risk of recurrent pancreatitis [19]– [20]. In our cohort, 16 out of 40 patients discontinued asparaginase due to pancreatitis, of whom 5 experienced relapse. Pancreatitis may lead not only to discontinuation of asparaginase but also to broader treatment delays or omissions, which could contribute to relapse risk. We conducted a subgroup analysis excluding these patients, the 6-year CIR in the remaining truncated group remained higher than in the non-truncated group, although the difference was no longer statistically significant (P =.093). Despite this being inconsistent with prior reports [14, 21], we assumed that the elevated relapse risk observed in truncated patients was primarily attributable to interruption of asparaginase therapy.

The observed venous thromboembolism (VTE) rate in our study (0.4%) is lower than many previously reported pediatric ALL series. Consistent with current guideline-based practice, protocol-mandated pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was not used in our centers. We believe this primarily reflects our ascertainment strategy—events were captured only when they were symptomatic and imaging-confirmed, and no routine surveillance imaging was performed. Furthermore, nearly half of our cohort were low-risk patients scheduled to receive only three doses of L-asparaginase, resulting in lower cumulative asparaginase exposure, which may further attenuate VTE risk.

Asparaginase-related toxicities are an important contributor to treatment discontinuation and require careful clinical management. While many of these toxicities are manageable, prior studies have shown that the risk is influenced by patient age [3, 22]– [23]. Silverman et al. [3]reported that patients aged 9–18 years had a significantly higher incidence of asparaginase-associated toxicity compared to those under 9 years (48% vs. 24%, P <.01). In our study, we similarly found that older age (≥ 10 years), higher MRD levels (d19 ≥ 0.1%), and greater asparaginase exposure percentage were associated with an increased likelihood of treatment discontinuation. These results underscore the challenge of maintaining treatment intensity in patients at higher risk of both relapse and asparaginase-related toxicities. Strategies such as early identification of patients at risk for treatment discontinuation, appropriate management of toxicities, and prompt substitution with alternative asparaginase formulations may help prevent premature discontinuation while preserving treatment efficacy.

The optimal cumulative dose of asparaginase for pediatric ALL remains uncertain [24]. In our study, patients were stratified into two groups based on asparaginase exposure percentage, using an exploratory cut-off of 53%. Patients receiving less than 53% of the scheduled doses had a significantly higher 6-year CIR (HR: 2.71, P =.004), suggesting that asparaginase exposure below this threshold may increase relapse risk. However, the number of patients with < 53% exposure was small (n = 28), resulting in wide confidence intervals. Moreover, given the clinical heterogeneity of the cohort and the limited number of truncation events, the 53% threshold should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Further validation in larger and independent datasets is warranted.

According to our treatment protocol, patients who developed hypersensitivity to PEG-asparaginase were switched to Erwinia asparaginase. EFS was comparable between patients who received Erwinia asparaginase substitution (n = 14) and those who completed the planned asparaginase doses without substitution (n = 939) (P =.44). Studies from the COG have shown that substituting with Erwinia asparaginase does not adversely affect DFS [12], and Ishida et al. found that additional chemotherapy cannot compensate for the impact of asparaginase discontinuation [17]. Accordingly, replacing missed doses of asparaginase with alternative formulations remains a key strategy to preserve treatment intensity and optimize outcomes.

Our study has several limitations. First, the observed discontinuation rate was lower than previous studies. Nearly half of the cohort were low-risk patients with only three planned doses of L-asparaginase, lowering the opportunity for permanent discontinuation. The analytic cohort excluded patients who were taken off study before re-induction or proceeded to HSCT, potentially removing early severe-toxicity cases. In addition, we did not monitor serum asparaginase activity (SAA), precluding detection of silent inactivation and potentially underestimating functional discontinuation. Vrooman et al. demonstrated that SAA monitoring allows for early detection of SI and timely substitution, which is associated with improved outcomes compared to patients without monitoring [11]. Similarly, Gottschalk et al. reported a significantly higher relapse risk in truncated patients within the monitored subgroup (aHR = 1.69, P =.03), whereas no significant difference was observed in the full cohort [14]. Given that SAA monitoring is now considered standard of care in many contemporary protocols, including international consensus guidelines [25, 26]—future studies should incorporate SAA data to more precisely assess asparaginase exposure and its impact on outcomes. Second, although the median follow-up duration was 6.0 years, a small subset of patients had relatively short follow-up (as little as 6 months), which may have limited our ability to capture late relapses or survival events. Third, asparaginase formulation varied by risk group (L-asparaginase for low-risk, PEG-asparaginase for intermediate/high-risk patients), and some patients underwent substitution with alternative formulations during treatment. This variability may have introduced heterogeneity in drug exposure, toxicity profiles, and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that asparaginase discontinuation was associated with a significantly increased risk of relapse, particularly in intermediate-risk patients. While our exploratory analysis suggests that receiving at least 53% of the planned cumulative dose may be sufficient to maintain disease control, this threshold requires further validation in larger and independent cohorts. Our findings also support the importance of timely substitution with alternative formulations, such as Erwinia asparaginase, and emphasize the need for individualized treatment strategies—such as early identification of patients at higher risk of toxicity and timely supportive management of toxicities—to help minimize premature discontinuation and preserve treatment efficacy.
