Suboptimal vaccine coverage for preventable respiratory infections in children with cystic fibrosis in the Cystic Fibrosis Regional Reference Centre of Tuscany: Need for improving preventive strategies
Authors: Federico Forgione, Clementina Canessa, Giovanni Taccetti, Cristina Fevola, Chiara Azzari, Silvia Ricci, Vito Terlizzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdvances in standards of care and the recent introduction of Cystic Fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators have significantly improved the prognosis for individuals with cystic fibrosis (CF). Prevention of respiratory infections in children with cystic fibrosis (cwCF) remains a cornerstone of treatment. Health authorities recommend both mandatory and non-mandatory vaccinations. This retrospective study aims to analyze compliance with the free vaccination program for preventable respiratory diseases in cwCF. This retrospective study assessed compliance with mandatory (H. influenzae type B, Bordetella pertussis) and non-mandatory (S. pneumoniae, COVID-19, Influenza) vaccinations for preventable respiratory diseases in cwCF in regular follow-up at the Regional Reference Center of Tuscany. Regarding mandatory vaccination, 131 (98.5%) out of 133 cwCF were regularly vaccinated, showing good coverage for Haemophilus influenzae type b(Hib). Influenza vaccination coverage peaked during the COVID-19 pandemic, reaching 79.6% in 2020–2021, then declined in the post-pandemic period across all age groups. For pneumococcal vaccination, 97% of cwCF received a complete primary series. Regarding COVID-19 vaccination, 67 (50.3%) out of 133 cwCF received at least two doses; no patients under 7 y were vaccinated. Coverage for pneumococcal and Hib vaccines was excellent. Conversely, Influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations had coverage below the targets set by health authorities. Further studies are needed to understand parental reasons for non-compliance and to develop strategies to increase Influenza and COVID-19 vaccination coverage in this high-risk cohort.KEYWORDS: Cystic fibrosis, preventive strategies, vaccine coverage, SARS-CoV-2, S. pneumoniae, vaccine

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disease caused by variants in the Cystic Fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene. The lack or the dysfunction of the CFTR protein leads to the thickening of secretions, reduced mucociliary transport, and consequently, mucus retention and airway obstruction.1,2

In recent years, considerable progress has been made in the treatment of CF, resulting in a significant increase in life expectancy. These outcomes are largely attributed to improved standards of patient care, advances in the prevention and treatment of lung infections (still responsible for 90% of mortality), and in the introduction of modulators of the CFTR protein.1–5

The most common pathogens isolated from the airway of people with Cystic Fibrosis (pwCF) include Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Haemophilus influenzae, Burkholderia cepacia complex, other non-fermenting Gram-negatives, and nontuberculous mycobacteria, such as Mycobacterium avium and Mycobacterium abscessus.1–4 Despite significant research efforts, vaccines for specific CF pathogens, such as P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, M. avium, and M. abscessus, are not yet commercially available, although research in this area remains active.6,7

Previous publications have noted a low vaccine coverage in patients with various chronic diseases, especially CF.6–9 Historically, poor clinical conditions and frequent hospitalizations were considered among the factors contributing to incomplete immunization.8,9 While the current standards of care for CF and the recent introduction of CFTR modulators have significantly improved the CF prognosis,1–4,10–12 active and passive immunoprophylaxis against vaccine-preventable respiratory infections remain a cornerstone of treatment to prevent lung complications.3,6

Regarding vaccine-preventable infections, health authorities recommend both mandatory and non-mandatory vaccinations (e.g. against Influenza and COVID-19) in accordance with national or local programs to minimize lung complications.6,8,13–18

In Tuscany, the vaccination calendar for infants born after 2017 includes mandatory vaccinations against Poliomyelitis (Inactivated Poliovirus Vaccine IPV), Diphtheria – Bordetella pertussis – Tetanus (DTaP), Hepatitis B Virus (HBV), H. influenzae type b (Hib), Measles, Mumps, Rubella, and Varicella.14 Furthermore, the Regional Health Service offers free of charge pneumococcal conjugate vaccination (PCV), which, while not mandatory in Italy, is strongly recommended in children.14 Given that immunoprophylaxis against viral infections is crucial for preventing lung damage, pwCF in Italy are also advised to receive non-mandatory vaccinations for seasonal Influenza15–17 and COVID-19.18

The role of respiratory viruses in CF airway disease has become increasingly recognized over the last few years, particularly with the widespread use of molecular detection methods such as polymerase chain reaction.19–22 However, debate still exists on the real impact of viral infections in children with CF (cwCF), and the literature is scarce. The prevalence of viral infections during pulmonary exacerbations is probably underreported, ranging between 40% and 60%.21–23 The most common viruses detected are Respiratory Syncytial Virus, Human Rhinovirus, Influenza A and B, Parainfluenza Virus,8,9 and, more recently, Coronaviruses and SARS-CoV-2.19–23

Viral respiratory infections may interact synergistically with bacteria in the CF lung, leading to an increased number of pulmonary exacerbations. In the pre-modulator era, some studies showed a more rapid decline in lung function following a viral infection.21,22 While Influenza is not generally more common in cwCF compared to the general population, it can be associated with decreased lung function, and its overall role is probably underestimated.17 One study indicated that cwCF are 11 times more likely to be hospitalized due to Influenza compared to children without chronic lung disease.24 Moreover, Influenza A is known to cause dysfunction of the CFTR protein in healthy individuals, thereby increasing the bacterial burden on the airway surface and consequently raising the risk of co-infection with bacterial species, most notably S. pneumoniae.25

Literature focusing on the prevention of respiratory infections and vaccine uptake in cwCF is scarce, and specific indications from CF experts may be outdated8 or may be adopted primarily within specific regional contexts.26

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, single-center study to assess compliance with mandatory and non-mandatory vaccinations for preventable respiratory diseases in cwCF <18 y of age living in Tuscany and in regular follow-up. Children with CFTR-related disorder or Cystic Fibrosis Screen Positive, Inconclusive Diagnosis (CFSPID) were excluded from the analysis.

We analyzed data from two primary sources: the database of the Cystic Fibrosis Regional Reference Center, Department of Pediatric Medicine, Meyer Children’s Hospital IRCCS, in Florence, for demographic and clinical information, and the Tuscany regional vaccination registry (27) to assess the vaccination status for all mandatory and non-mandatory immunizations offered free of charge in Tuscany (Influenza, S. pneumoniae, and COVID-19). The study focused exclusively on vaccinations for preventable respiratory infections that are mandatory or freely offered by the Regional Health Service: mandatory free of cost (hexavalent: DTaP – Hib – HBV – IPV) and non-mandatory free of cost (Influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19).

Mandatory vaccinations

We evaluated Hib and B. pertussis vaccination based on a complete primary series of hexavalent vaccination, which includes Diphtheriae, Tetanus, Pertussis combined vaccine (DTaP), HBV vaccine, Inactivated Poliovirus Vaccine (IPV), and Hib and consists of three doses administered during the first year of life according to Italian health authorities.14,27

Influenza vaccination

We specifically evaluated compliance with Influenza vaccination over a 5-y period (from 2020–2021 to 2024–2025), as during this time vaccination records were systematically updated in a specific regional registry by health operators.28 CwCF age was defined as October 1st of each epidemic season, marking the start of the influenza vaccination campaign in Italy. To analyze Influenza vaccination coverage, we included cwCF aged 6 months to 18 y as of October 1st, as this is the lower age limit for the Influenza vaccination.14,15,28,29 We calculated the proportion of children (older than 6 months) who received a complete vaccination series (one dose if already vaccinated in a previous season or if over 6 y old, two doses for children under 6 y old in their first season receiving the influenza vaccination14,27). Coverage was further stratified into specific age groups (6 months-6 y, 7–12 y, 13–18 y).

We also assessed the number of seasons in which each cwCF aged 6 months to 18 y received the influenza vaccine. We consider this data as a marker of compliance with receiving immunization throughout the years.

Pneumococcal vaccination

Information on pneumococcal vaccination was collected on the primary immunization series (three doses for children under 1 y old, two doses for children between 1 and 2 y of age, a single dose after the 2 y of age, according to Italian health authorities14,27) or on the last administered vaccine (PCV-7, PCV-13, PCV-15, PCV-20, or PPV-23) in case of booster doses.

COVID-19 vaccination

We assessed the proportion of children by age group who received a complete primary series of COVID-19 vaccination. The availability and schedule of the Comirnaty (COVID-19 Vaccine, mRNA) produced by Pfizer and BioNTech18 in Italy were considered: ages 12–17 y, available since July 2021 (two 30 mcg full doses); ages 5–11 y, available since December 2021, with a two reduced doses (10 mcg) schedule; ages 6 months-5 y, available since December 9, 2022, three-doses primary series (3 mcg), administered intramuscularly.

Statistical analysis

All collected data were stored in an electronic database. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize quantitative variables, after testing for normal distribution. Differences between groups for continuous and categorical variables were assessed using descriptive statistics and ANOVA test.

Finally, findings were compared with previous literature on vaccine uptake and relevant coverage targets.

Informed consent

We conducted a service evaluation regarding vaccine coverage in cwCF; therefore, this study is a retrospective, observational analysis based solely on anonymized and aggregated data. No direct identifiers or personal information were used. According to current regulations and ethical guidelines, individual informed consent was not required, as the research involves no identifiable human subjects, poses no risk to participants, and is purely observational or uses existing, publicly available data.

Results

We investigated a cohort of 133 cwCF aged 0 to 18 y on January 1st, 2025, for Hib vaccination and PCV compliance. The analysis for Influenza vaccination included a total of 158 cwCF aged 6 months to 18 y of age at the start of each influenza season from 2020 to 2021 through 2024–2025. No cwCF had undergone a bilateral lung transplant. Regarding mandatory vaccination, compliance was high: 131 (98.5%) out of the 133 cwCF were regularly vaccinated in the first year of life using the hexavalent vaccination (DTaP + IPV + Hib + HBV). This resulted in good coverage for both Hib and Bordetella pertussis.

Discussion

Our study provides a detailed analysis of vaccination coverage for vaccine-preventable respiratory infections in a large cohort of cwCF residing in Tuscany. Despite the established clinical benefits of vaccination for cwCF,8 the international scientific literature features only a limited number of reviews or original studies on this topic.8,9,30,31 Furthermore, the guidance published by the Vaccination Group of the European CF Society in 2005 has received no substantial updates, despite the significant progress made in respiratory disease and immunoprophylaxis since then.8

The lack of a uniform immunization schedule for cwCF across Europe introduces regional and national variations, namely the collection of homogeneous data and the comparison of clinical experiences.8,9 Compounding this issue is the fact that some vaccinations, theoretically beneficial for preventing respiratory complications in CF, are recommended but not mandatory. There is also a lack of specific clinical trials investigating vaccinations in the cwCF population.

The data presented here confirm that cwCF in Tuscany demonstrate good vaccination coverage for mandatory immunizations but exhibit suboptimal uptake for non-mandatory vaccines.

Regarding mandatory and routine vaccination, the Italian health authorities mandate the B. pertussis and the Hib vaccine, which is effective against the invasive disease caused by the type b strain,32 requiring a three-dose schedule in the first year of life (commonly administered together within the hexavalent vaccination). Our cohort demonstrated a high compliance rate, with 98.5% coverage (131/133 cwCF fully vaccinated), consistent with the Tuscany general pediatric population, which showed 98.5% coverage in the 2021 cohort at 24 months of age,33 surpassing the national target of ≥95%,27 and only two cwCF were not vaccinated. In cwCF, H. influenzae is a well-known pathogen, and data regarding chronic infection or the prevalence of bacterial isolation are regularly described in many registries.34,35 However, H. influenzae isolated in CF airways is often non-typable, meaning the Hib conjugate vaccine is ineffective in preventing chronic infection and subsequent lung damage, despite its efficacy against invasive disease. Our compliance rates contrast favorably with a previous French pediatric CF study including 415 cwCF,9 which reported lower coverage for Hib (85% at 1 y and 74% at 14 y of age) and pneumococcus (around 80% of the population born after 2002 at 2 y of age). No other recent studies investigating vaccine coverage for routine immunizations over a large cwCF cohort are available. Regarding the hexavalent vaccine, booster doses are not currently recommended in pwCF,8 and no additional doses beyond the primary series were observed.

Regarding Influenza vaccination, since across all studies Influenza vaccination generated a satisfactory serological antibody response, a 2015 Cochrane review concluded that annual Influenza vaccination is advisable for people with CF, aligning with national recommendations and clinical practice in many units caring for CF.30 The suggested minimum coverage target for high-risk groups in Italy, according to the WHO, is ≥75%,27,36 with an ideal target of ≥95%.27

Consistent with general Italian epidemiological data, which show that Influenza vaccination coverage is below target thresholds, especially in pediatrics,16 our cohort also showed suboptimal uptake. The highest coverage (79.6%) was achieved during the 2020–2021 season, coinciding with the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, mirroring a temporary rise observed across the general Italian population.16 Subsequently, coverage declined below 70% and has continued to fall, reflecting the national trend;16 however, the lack of statistical significance suggests that these fluctuations should be interpreted with caution.

Our results contrast with a previous Italian experience from 2012 involving 57 cwCF (mean age ±SD 9.5 ± 4.9 y), which reported a 91.2% (n = 52) vaccine coverage for influenza.37 However, our experience, conducted a decade later in a larger and more homogeneous cohort, showed a considerably lower percentage. Our 68% coverage is similar to the rate found in a Spanish study,38 though that study reported a greater difference between the 6 months to 14 y old group (81.0%, n = 124) and the ≥15 y group (61.0%, n = 178). Unlike the Spanish cohort, the population we describe did not show statistically significant differences in vaccine coverage across different age groups throughout the study period, despite the temporary rise associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.

More recently, an Australian study described a successful strategy where immunization was administered by nurses during the patient’s review, achieving coverage rates that trended upwards from 64.5% in 2016 to over 75.0% in most subsequent years.39 This suggests that alternative models of vaccine delivery may be key to increasing compliance.

S. pneumoniae is a known carrier in up to 20% of school-age children and adolescents with CF.40 Our national and regional health authorities categorize patients with chronic lung disease as people at risk for invasive pneumococcal infection.14,41 PCV is offered free of charge and recommended for all infants in Tuscany.14 Although a Cochrane review found a lack of definitive evidence to support the use of pneumococcal vaccines in people with CF,31 the increased use of molecular diagnostic tools highlights the growing importance of S. pneumoniae in this population.42–44 Studies investigating S. pneumoniae prevalence and serotypes40 conclude that the PCV13 vaccine offers good protection against colonization and infection in CF children.

Although PCV is not mandatory, it is offered free of charge and recommended for all infants in Italy. Italian health authorities set a coverage target of ≥95%.26 Our cohort achieved a high coverage rate of 96.9% (129/133 cwCF completed a primary series), which is slightly above the target and better than the 91.9% observed in Tuscany’s general population aged 2 y.45

Health authorities also recommend an additional dose with vaccines covering a broader spectrum of S. pneumoniae serotypes in high-risk individuals like pwCF.41 In Tuscany, the PCV-15 conjugate vaccine is currently in use. The PCV-20 conjugate vaccine is sometimes used in clinical practice to extend coverage in frail subjects, but it currently lacks formal health authority recommendations or guidelines. Consequently, only 3 (2.2%) patients out of 133 received PCV-20, and 1 patient received the PPV-23 vaccine. Over the years, only 22 patients received an additional dose (using PCV-13 first, then PCV-15) following the initial three-dose primary series. Reduced awareness among both patients and health-care providers regarding the need for booster doses beyond the primary vaccination schedule may play a role, particularly as pneumococcal vaccination recommendations in patients with cystic fibrosis can be perceived as less emphasized, compared to other routine immunizations. Fragmentation of care between specialized centers and primary care services may contribute to missed opportunities for vaccination, especially when responsibility for administering boosters is not clearly defined.

Initial concern about SARS-CoV-2 during the pandemic led to the prioritization of immunization for pwCF.44 Subsequent studies indicated that while people with CF are not at an increased overall risk of contracting COVID-19, certain subsets of patients remain vulnerable to severe sequelae.46,47 More recent data even suggest that CFTR loss of function may offer a protective role,47 and cwCF generally performed similarly to their non-CF peers.48 Regardless, the vaccine has been proven safe and immunogenic in cwCF,45 and Italian health authorities recommend an mRNA COVID-19 vaccine dose for all cwCF aged 6 months and older.

Despite the pandemics and current recommendations, the uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine in our cohort was disappointing, with only half of our cohort (50.3%, 67/133) receiving a complete primary series. Coverage was especially low in the youngest ages, with no vaccines administered to children under 6 y of age, although licensed and recommended for this age group. Uptake was better in adolescents, with 78% of cwCF aged 12 to 18 y receiving a complete series. The relatively low uptake of COVID-19 vaccination observed in our cohort should be interpreted in the context of evolving national recommendations, age-specific vaccine availability, and changing public perception following the acute phase of the pandemic. In Italy, access to COVID-19 vaccination for younger children was introduced later compared to adolescents, which may partly explain the absence of vaccinated individuals in the youngest age group. The rates found in our cohort are largely consistent with the general Italian pediatric population,33 which also saw suboptimal uptake. As observed in other studies,49 the reasons for parental vaccine hesitancy regarding COVID-19 vaccines in the high-risk cohort warrant further in-depth investigation.

Conclusion

More studies are needed to fully elucidate the importance of routine and seasonal vaccinations in the modulator era, which has introduced profound changes in CF management. While vaccination was historically not a priority,8 the increasing lifespan of individuals with CF and the resultant rise in the adult population could potentially increase the number of people at risk for vaccine-preventable infections. Furthermore, for pwCF who have the option of a transplant, it is recommended that any gaps in vaccinations be remedied.8 Regarding pneumococcal immunization, the coverage for the primary series is satisfactory. However, a concerningly low number of cwCF received the recommended additional doses of the PCV, as advised by health authorities. Further data are needed to reinforce the indication of PCV additional doses in pwCF and to confirm the efficacy of the most recently available formulations, such as PCV-20.

Our work confirms the urgent need to promote influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations in cwCF, as the observed coverage rates fall well below the targets set by the health authorities. An in-depth investigation into the parental reasons for non-receipt of influenza vaccine in this high-risk pediatric cohort is warranted.50

The actual strategy of providing vaccinations in cwCF, as in the whole Italian general pediatric population, is based on the work of primary care pediatricians. We propose that a complementary strategy, incorporating on-site vaccination during routine hospital follow-up clinical examinations, might significantly increase the vaccine coverage, as suggested for other vulnerable pediatric groups.51 This strategy should be integrated with national measures aimed at increasing influenza vaccination coverage across the general pediatric age group.52

Given the multidisciplinary approach required for optimizing CF care, a structured plan to enhance vaccine awareness and increase the uptake of recommended immunizations could include regular evaluations by clinical immunologists during the follow-up of pwCF.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The monocentric nature of the study, conducted at a single regional referral center in Tuscany, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings, populations, or health-care systems with different organizational structures and vaccination practices. Future multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to confirm and extend these findings. Exploring factors such as vaccine hesitancy through structured tools, including standardized questionnaires, could provide valuable insights into patients’ attitudes, perceptions, and barriers to immunization. Due to the retrospective design and the structure of the study, individual-level variables potentially associated with vaccination behavior (e.g. health-care utilization, socioeconomic factors, parental attitudes) and clinical variables (e.g. disease severity, CFTR genotype, lung function, modulator therapy) were not available. Therefore, multivariable analyses could not be performed.
