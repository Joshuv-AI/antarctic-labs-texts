Effect of Valemetostat on the Pharmacokinetics of Midazolam and Digoxin: A Phase 1 Drug–Drug Interaction Study in Patients With Non‐Hodgkin Lymphoma
Authors: Masaya Tachibana, Steven Horwitz, Eric Jacobsen, Francine Foss, Pamela Allen, Pierluigi Porcu, Tatyana Feldman, Jia Ruan, Jonathan E. Brammer, Jie Wang, Shinichi Inaba, Yuka Iko, Keiko Nakajima, Yasuyuki Kakurai, Noriaki Kitami, Yang Chen, Yvonne Lau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTValemetostat tosylate (valemetostat) is an oral, potent, dual inhibitor of enhancer of zeste homolog (EZH)2/1, under investigation in non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs) and solid tumors. In vitro, it inhibits cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) when combined with sensitive CYP3A or P‐gp substrates. This drug–drug interaction (DDI) sub‐study is part of the phase 1 trial of valemetostat monotherapy (DS3201‐A‐J101; NCT02732275), assessing the effect of valemetostat on the pharmacokinetics (PK) of sensitive CYP3A and P‐gp substrates midazolam and digoxin, respectively, in patients with relapsed or refractory NHL, and its safety and efficacy. Patients received two simultaneous single doses of midazolam and digoxin: once alone and once concomitantly with a daily 200 mg valemetostat dose in a 28‐day cycle. Of 24 enrolled patients, 15 and 16 were evaluable for PK analyses of midazolam and digoxin, respectively. Valemetostat co‐administration caused a slight decrease in midazolam maximum plasma concentration (C max) (geometric least‐squares mean ratio [GMR], 0.966 [90% confidence interval (CI), 0.769–1.21]) and area under the plasma concentration–time curve up to the last quantifiable time (AUClast) (GMR, 0.87 [90% CI, 0.75–1.03]), increased digoxin C max (GMR, 1.30 [90% CI, 1.07–1.57]), and AUClast (GMR, 1.27 [90% CI, 1.06–1.52]). The overall safety and efficacy outcomes were similar to other studies. Valemetostat showed a manageable and tolerable safety profile, without clinically meaningful DDI with sensitive CYP3A substrates. Co‐administration of valemetostat and P‐gp substrates with a narrow therapeutic index may require careful monitoring and/or DDI risk management.

Introduction

Valemetostat tosylate (valemetostat; DS‐3201b) is an oral, potent, and selective dual inhibitor of enhancer of zeste homolog (EZH)2 and EZH1. EZH2 and EZH1 are methyltransferases that facilitate the trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 27, a marker for transcriptional suppression that silences genes via chromatin compaction, preventing transcription. Therefore, EZH2 and EZH1 are important in the development and survival of different types of cancer [1]. Overexpression of EZH2 has been shown to enhance the development and progression of non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs), including adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL), and is reported to correlate with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Conversely, depletion of EZH2 in cancer cells induces cell cycle arrest and inhibits cell proliferation [7, 8, 9]. Valemetostat is approved in Japan (EZHARMIA; Daiichi Sankyo Co. Ltd.) for the treatment of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) ATLL and R/R peripheral T‐cell lymphoma (PTCL) at a dose of 200 mg/day, administered orally, once daily (QD) [10]. Valemetostat is currently in clinical development for the treatment of other hematologic malignancies and solid tumors [1, 11]. Based on preclinical data, valemetostat is known to be primarily metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP)3A4. In vitro studies have also identified valemetostat as a substrate of P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) [12, 13].

A phase 1 drug–drug interaction (DDI) study in healthy Japanese participants showed that concomitant use of valemetostat with itraconazole (a P‐gp and strong CYP3A inhibitor) resulted in an increased maximum plasma concentration (C max) and area under the plasma concentration–time curve extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf) of valemetostat with a geometric least‐squares mean ratio (GMR) of 2.92 (90% confidence interval [CI], 2.26–3.78) and 4.19 (90% CI, 3.45–5.09), respectively. Co‐administration of valemetostat with fluconazole (a moderate CYP3A inhibitor) resulted in a GMR of 1.61 (90% CI, 1.12–2.32) and 1.58 (90% CI, 1.23–2.04) in the C max and AUCinf of valemetostat, respectively [14]. Another DDI trial demonstrated that co‐administration with rifampicin, a strong CYP3A and P‐gp inducer, decreased the C max and AUCinf of total valemetostat by approximately 60% and 70%, respectively [12]. Findings from these studies indicate that in the presence of CYP3A or P‐gp modulators, the pharmacokinetics (PK) of valemetostat may be altered due to potential DDIs.

In vitro studies using human liver microsomes demonstrated inhibition of CYP3A4 by valemetostat with an inhibition constant value of 56.4 μmol/L, with midazolam as a probe substrate (data on file). Based on calculations using the static mechanistic model, a predicted ratio of AUC (AUCR) value was 1.3 at the steady‐state C max reported for the clinically recommended dose (200 mg QD) in patients with R/R ATLL [10]. This value exceeds the cutoff value of 1.25 for determining whether in vivo studies are needed [15, 16]. Valemetostat also inhibits P‐gp transport in the Caco‐2 [17] cell monolayer with a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 18.2 μmol/L for 3H‐digoxin (data on file). At the clinically recommended dose of valemetostat 200 mg/day, the intestinal luminal concentration divided by IC50 value was 90, exceeding the guidance for conducting an in vivo study (cutoff value of 10) [15, 16]. Midazolam and digoxin are known substrates of CYP3A and P‐gp, respectively, with no interference in each other's activity [18].

The purpose of this phase 1 DDI sub‐study was to assess the effects of valemetostat on the PK of midazolam (a CYP3A index substrate) and digoxin (a P‐gp index substrate) when orally administered concomitantly in patients with R/R NHL.

Methods

Study Design

This was a DDI cohort‐specific sub‐study conducted across sites in the US to assess the effect of valemetostat on the PK of midazolam (a known substrate of CYP3A) and digoxin (a known substrate of P‐gp) after multiple doses of valemetostat in patients with NHL. It is part of the first‐in‐human, multicenter, open‐label, phase 1 trial of valemetostat monotherapy (DS3201‐A‐J101; NCT02732275), which also included a dose‐escalation part in patients with R/R NHLs followed by a dose‐expansion part in patients with R/R PTCL and R/R ATLL.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the effect of multiple valemetostat doses on the PK of single‐dose midazolam and digoxin, using a single arm, fixed‐sequence design (Figure 1). The secondary objectives were to assess the safety and antitumor effect of valemetostat.

Schematic overview of the DDI cohort study design. C, cycle; D, day; DDI, drug–drug interaction.

All eligible patients in this DDI sub‐study were ≥ 18 years of age and had a confirmed diagnosis of NHL, including PTCL, ATLL, or B‐cell lymphoma as defined by the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, and cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma (CTCL) as classified by 2018 WHO‐European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) [18, 19]. Exclusion criteria included a history of treatment with other EZH inhibitors, use of any CYP3A inhibitors/inducers and P‐gp inhibitors within 14 days prior to study drug administration, use of corticosteroids for disease control other than short‐course systemic corticosteroids, or a non‐cancer indication within 6 days before the start of study drug administration. However, stable ongoing corticosteroid use (≥ 30 days) for disease control up to an equivalent dose of prednisolone 20 mg/day was permitted. Strong CYP3A inhibitors and P‐gp inhibitors were allowed with dose reduction of valemetostat beginning with Cycle 2. Moderate and weak CYP3A inhibitors were allowed to be used concomitantly without a dose reduction of valemetostat beginning with Cycle 2. Concomitant use of moderate or strong CYP3A inducers remained prohibited throughout the study. The consumption of food products or supplements with CYP3A‐inhibiting or ‐inducing properties, such as St. John's Wort or grapefruit, was prohibited from the day of informed consent until the end of the study. Full details regarding study design, patient eligibility, and endpoints have been published [20].

The DDI sub‐study was initiated after the recommended dose for expansion of valemetostat (200 mg QD) had been determined in the dose‐escalation part, and the study enrolled patients ≥ 18 years of age with NHLs R/R to standard treatment, or for whom no standard treatment was available. Single doses of midazolam (2 mg in syrup formulation) and digoxin (0.25 mg in tablet formulation) were orally administered simultaneously on Day −4 (± 0 days) of Cycle 1 under modified fasting conditions (at least 1 h before or at least 2 h after a meal). Valemetostat was administered orally (200 mg QD) under fasting conditions in repeated 28‐day treatment cycles starting on Day 1 of Cycle 1 (C1D1). On Day 15 (± 3 days) of Cycle 1 (C1D15), valemetostat, midazolam, and digoxin were orally administered concomitantly to patients under modified fasting conditions (Figure 1). Patients could continue to receive valemetostat after completing Cycle 1 Day 28 (C1D28) if they continued to derive benefit and had not met any protocol‐defined conditions for treatment withdrawal [20].

This trial was performed in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use Consolidated Guideline E6 for Good Clinical Practice, and local regulatory requirements. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to screening.

PK Assessments

Blood samples were collected pre‐dosing of midazolam (2 mL) or digoxin (2 mL) on Day −4, and at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 24 h post‐dose of midazolam, and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post‐dose of digoxin, to establish index PK values. Simultaneously, additional samples were taken on Day 15 to measure the impact of valemetostat on midazolam and digoxin PK parameters (Figure 1). To determine the PK profile of valemetostat alone, blood samples were collected (5 mL) prior to valemetostat dosing, and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h post‐dose on C1D1 and C1D15. On Days 2, 8, 16, and 22, blood samples were collected pre‐dose.

Plasma concentrations were determined using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assay with a lower limit of quantification of 0.05 ng/mL for both midazolam and digoxin (Supporting Information). Based on plasma concentrations of midazolam and digoxin, the C max, median time to C max (T max), AUC from time 0 to the last quantifiable time (AUClast), AUCinf, and half‐life on Day −4 and on C1D15 were calculated by noncompartmental analysis. Plasma concentrations of valemetostat were determined according to a method described elsewhere [21]. PK parameters for valemetostat concentrations in plasma included C max, T max, minimum concentration achieved after dosing (Ctrough) (Day 15 only), average steady‐state drug concentration, AUClast, and AUC from time 0 to tau (24 h) (AUCtau). For the PK of midazolam and digoxin, data from patients who (1) did not have a C max, AUCinf, or AUClast value either on Day −4 or Day 15; (2) did not receive three consecutive doses of valemetostat prior to, and one (for midazolam) or three (for digoxin) consecutive doses after co‐administration on Day 15; (3) received midazolam and/or digoxin 15 min or earlier before valemetostat treatment; (4) received CYP3A inhibitors/inducers (for midazolam) and P‐gp inhibitors (for digoxin) during the DDI evaluation period; and (5) did not have sufficient plasma concentration data to permit reliable PK parameter estimation, were excluded from statistical analyses.

Safety Assessments

Safety was evaluated by reporting and monitoring the type, frequency, severity, timing, and relationship to the study drug(s) of any treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), as well as laboratory data, vital signs, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, body weight, electrocardiogram abnormalities, and histamine levels. Patients were evaluated for safety beginning on Day −4 to assess new or worsening adverse events (AEs) after receiving midazolam and digoxin only (Day −4 to Day 0); valemetostat only (Cycle 1 Days 1–14); and after midazolam, digoxin, and valemetostat (Cycle 1 Days 15–28). Overall valemetostat safety was also monitored starting on C1D1 until 30 days after the last dose of valemetostat. The severity of TEAEs was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Version 4.0.

Efficacy Assessments

The antitumor effect of valemetostat was assessed by measuring the overall response rate (ORR). Clinical responses were defined according to the 2007 revised International Working Group response criteria for malignant lymphoma [22] for NHL (except for ATLL and CTCL), modified 2009 ATLL response criteria for ATLL [23], and 2011 Olsen response criteria for mycosis fungoides and Sezary syndrome for CTCL [24].

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was determined based on practical considerations. Assuming the intra‐individual variability (intra‐individual coefficient of variation) of C max and AUC for midazolam and digoxin was commonly around 30%, approximately 14 patients were to be enrolled in this study to provide a reliable estimate of the magnitude and variability of the interaction. The PK parameters of midazolam and digoxin alone and with concomitant valemetostat dosing were compared based on an analysis of variance (ANOVA) model using a linear mixed‐effect model with natural logarithms as response variables; the treatment period (Day −4 and Day 15 of Cycle 1) was considered as a fixed effect, and patients as a random effect. The natural logarithms were inversely transformed, and the GMR of the test (C1D15) versus reference (Day −4) and its two‐sided 90% CI were calculated for the relevant parameters. The time course of plasma concentrations of drugs and primary endpoints (C max, AUClast, and AUCinf) were compared for each dosing period (Day −4 and C1D15) and presented graphically. PK parameters were calculated by noncompartmental analysis using version 8.1 of Phoenix WinNonlin (CERTARA; Radnor, PA, USA). Statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software version 9.3.

Results

Patient Disposition

A total of 24 patients with R/R NHL, including PTCL (n = 17), ATLL (n = 5), and CTCL (n = 2), were enrolled in this DDI sub‐study to ensure at least 14 PK evaluable patients. All eligible participants were included in the safety population and overall valemetostat PK analysis population. Two patients discontinued study treatment before completing Cycle 1 due to clinical progression, and 15 patients discontinued study treatment on or after Cycle 2 due to progressive disease (n = 9), clinical progression (n = 2), physician decision (n = 1), or other reasons (n = 3). The remaining seven patients were still receiving valemetostat at the time of data cutoff (December 31, 2022). The median (range) age of patients was 63 (23–85) years, and most were male (62.5%) and White (62.5%). Baseline patient demographics and disease characteristics are shown in Table 1.

PK Outcomes

The PK analysis subgroups for midazolam (n = 15) or digoxin (n = 16) included patients in the safety analysis group who met the PK evaluability criteria described in the PK assessment section. Nine patients were excluded from the midazolam subgroup due to lack of valemetostat (n = 5) and/or midazolam dosing (n = 6) at the required timepoints, protocol violation (n = 1), and/or insufficient PK data (n = 2). For the digoxin subgroup, eight patients were excluded due to lack of valemetostat (n = 5) and/or digoxin dosing (n = 6) and/or insufficient PK data (n = 2). The mean plasma concentration–time profiles of midazolam and digoxin on Day −4 (midazolam or digoxin alone) and Day 15 (midazolam or digoxin with valemetostat) are shown in Figure 2. Both midazolam and digoxin were rapidly absorbed when administered alone and in the presence of valemetostat as indicated by similar median T max values on Day −4 and Day 15 (Table 2). Overall, the exposures of midazolam were slightly lower in the presence of valemetostat than with midazolam alone. The exposures of digoxin were slightly higher in the presence of valemetostat than with digoxin alone. The GMRs (90% CI) for midazolam were 0.966 (0.769–1.213) for C max, 0.874 (0.745–1.025) for AUClast, and 0.850 (0.721–1.002) for AUCinf (Table 3). For digoxin, GMRs (90% CI) were 1.298 (1.074–1.568) for C max, 1.270 (1.061–1.520) for AUClast, and 1.077 (0.910–1.274) for AUCinf. The GMR of AUCinf for digoxin needs careful interpretation due to the small sample size used for analysis (n = 3). Comparisons of the C max and AUClast of midazolam and digoxin values for individual patients with and without concomitant valemetostat administration are presented in Figure 3. After patients received valemetostat 200 mg QD in Cycle 1, the mean C max and AUCtau of valemetostat were generally similar to those observed in previous valemetostat studies [20, 25] (Table S1).

Safety Outcomes

Safety was assessed in all 24 patients who received study drugs. All patients (N = 24) reported at least one TEAE. Overall, the most common events were dysgeusia, anemia, diarrhea, and decreased platelet count (Table S2). Grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were reported in 16 (66.7%) patients after C1D1; the most common Grade ≥ 3 events were anemia, decreased neutrophil and platelet counts, and hypertension (Table S3). Overall, drug‐related TEAEs occurred in 21 patients; drug‐related TEAEs by CTCAE Grade ≥ 3 were reported in seven (29.2%) patients. Among drug‐related TEAEs reported after C1D1, the most common events were dysgeusia, diarrhea, and decreased platelet count. Drug‐related Grade ≥ 3 TEAE events in two or more patients were decreased neutrophil and platelet counts and hypertension. Serious TEAEs (Table S4) were observed in seven (29.2%) patients after C1D1.

After C1D1 (N = 24), 15 (62.5%) patients experienced at least one TEAE associated with dose interruption. The events reported in two or more patients were COVID‐19, neutrophil count decreased, and platelet count decreased (each occurred in four patients) and anemia and decreased white blood cell count (each occurred in two patients). No patients experienced TEAEs associated with study treatment discontinuation or TEAEs associated with study treatment dose reduction. There were four on‐treatment deaths reported due to disease progression (n = 3) and AEs (n = 1, acute respiratory failure and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis occurred in the same patient).

Efficacy Outcomes

The efficacy analysis group included 24 patients who received valemetostat, of which four patients with PTCL were not included in the ORR analysis due to no measurable disease at baseline. Of the 20 patients with measurable disease at baseline, the ORR was 45% overall, with a CR of 25% and a partial response of 20% as best overall response (Table S5).

Discussion

Assessment of clinically relevant, potential DDIs between valemetostat and other drugs is important, especially for those frequently used to treat patients with cancer. In particular, the information on DDI is critical for patients receiving treatments with a narrow therapeutic index that are metabolized by CYP3A and/or transported by P‐gp. To assess the DDI liability of valemetostat as an inhibitor at the steady state, as well as by covering the plasma concentration–time curve of digoxin, administration of multiple doses was required.

Therefore, this DDI sub‐study was conducted as part of the phase 1, multicenter, open‐label trial of valemetostat monotherapy (DS3201‐A‐J101) in a patient population representing the anticipated clinical use. This study assessed the single‐dose PK of midazolam and digoxin when administered in the absence or presence of valemetostat. The application of a fixed‐sequence design allowed the PK assessments to be conducted with or without the inhibitor in the same participants, reducing inter‐individual variation. This approach provided an efficient assessment of potential DDIs.

Midazolam is an established index substrate for CYP3A‐dependent PK DDI and is frequently used in key clinical DDI studies [26]. In this study, the AUC and C max ratios of midazolam were less than 1.25 when co‐administered with valemetostat, which suggests that valemetostat is not a CYP3A inhibitor in vivo. Furthermore, a 15% decrease in midazolam AUCinf was observed, contrary to the in vitro data, which predicted a potential increase in midazolam exposure. As valemetostat does not increase CYP3A levels or enzymatic activity in primary human hepatocytes (data on file), the mechanism of this decrease in exposure remains unknown. Taken together, valemetostat is unlikely to cause clinically relevant DDIs with CYP3A substrates.

This study employed a low dose of digoxin (0.25 mg) as a clinical index substrate of P‐gp to assess the effect of valemetostat on intestinal P‐gp activity. The active disposition of digoxin seems to be primarily influenced by P‐gp‐mediated secretion across the apical cell membranes. Its expression in the gut wall (for secretion into the lumen), liver (for transport into bile), and kidneys (for renal secretion) all plays a role in the systemic PK of digoxin [26].

An approximate 30% increase in digoxin exposure was reported with multiple doses of valemetostat, suggesting a potentially weak DDI. Previous studies have indicated that an increase in digoxin exposure by > 25% is clinically pertinent, supporting the possibility of a weak DDI with valemetostat [27, 28]. Digoxin has been reported to have a very narrow therapeutic index; even small changes in the exposure may lead to significant changes in pharmacologic effects [29, 30]. When co‐administration of valemetostat and a P‐gp substrate with a narrow therapeutic index is unavoidable, careful monitoring of adherence, efficacy, and safety may be required [31].

Overall, valemetostat 200 mg QD, administered in 28‐day cycles, was well tolerated. TEAEs were similar to those reported in the overall patient population of the DS3201‐A‐J101 study [20] and were manageable by routine patient monitoring and supportive care. All patients experienced at least one TEAE, and Grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were reported in 66.7% of patients. Serious TEAEs were reported in 29.2% of patients; four on‐treatment deaths occurred that were not due to TEAEs. TEAEs did not lead to treatment discontinuation or dose reductions. Although dose interruptions due to TEAEs occurred in 62.5% of patients, most events were resolved with appropriate management. The overall safety outcomes in this sub‐study were in line with those seen in other valemetostat studies conducted in patients with R/R NHL [24].

An encouraging efficacy of valemetostat was observed in this sub‐study. To ensure evaluable PK data of at least 14 patients, a total of 24 patients with R/R NHL who had been treated with at least one prior line of therapy were enrolled in this sub‐study. With the limitations of a relatively small sample size and a non‐randomized study without a comparator group, the efficacy results from this sub‐study were generally consistent with those of the DS3201‐A‐J101 [20] study and showed favorable clinical activity in patients with R/R NHL.

This DDI study conducted in patients with R/R NHL indicated that valemetostat does not have a clinically meaningful DDI with the CYP3A substrate. Valemetostat has a weak DDI (≤ 30% increase in systemic exposure) with sensitive P‐gp substrates such as digoxin. These outcomes inform DDI risk management for sensitive CYP3A substrates and P‐gp substrates, such as midazolam and digoxin, when administered concomitantly with valemetostat. Data from this DDI sub‐study indicate that diligent monitoring of adherence, efficacy, and safety may be warranted when valemetostat is co‐administered with a P‐gp substrate with a narrow therapeutic index.
