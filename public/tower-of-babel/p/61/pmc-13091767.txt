Published: 2026

Introduction

Ultrasonic casting controls the microstructure of metal solidification by applying external fields to the molten metal. It has been extensively investigated and applied because of its advantages in large-scale casting, including grain refinement [1], improved solute segregation [2], and reduced hydrogen content in the melt [3], [4]. The ultrasonic system transmits ultrasonic waves into the melt via the radiation rod during the semi-continuous casting of aluminum alloys [5]. Because of the radiation rod is in direct contact with the high-temperature aluminum melt, the selection of materials for the radiation rod is particularly demanding. TC4 titanium alloy has become the preferred material for ultrasonic casting radiators used in aluminum alloys because of its high melting point, high strength, low acoustic impedance, and low thermal conductivity [6]. However, under high-temperature environments and cavitation impact, TRRS inevitably undergoes severe chemical corrosion and physical erosion [7], [8], [9], [10]. Notably, chemical corrosion leads to rapid mass loss of the radiation rod [11], which in turn results in resonance frequency drift of the ultrasonic vibration system, a reduction in ultrasonic amplitude, and consequently a decline in the overall ingot quality.

Recently, numerous scholars have investigated the chemical corrosion mechanisms and protection strategies of TC4 titanium alloy. Zhang et al. [12] investigated the corrosion behavior of TC4 in fluoride-containing solutions and observed a significant difference between the corrosion rates of the two-phase. Yang [13] employed laser powder bed fusion to refine the grain structure of TC4, thereby enhancing its resistance to chemical corrosion. Xiao et al. [14] modified TC4 by incorporating highly corrosion-resistant elements such as Ni and Nb, improving the stability of the passivation film and reducing the corrosion rate of TC4 in hydrochloric acid. However, the above studies have primarily focused on the chemical corrosion of TC4 in corrosive media at normal temperatures, whereas limited research has addressed the chemical corrosion mechanism and protection of titanium alloys in high-temperature molten aluminum.

Surface coating technology effectively reduces the chemical corrosion rate of materials by forming a dense protective layer on their surfaces [15], [16], [17]. However, during ultrasonic casting, the cavitation effect causes microjets to continuously impact the TRRS, leading to the detachment of the coating layer. The detached particles contaminate the melt, thereby affecting the properties of the ingot.

In previous studies, we found that the TRRS undergoes chemical corrosion during ultrasonic casting, leading to a sharp decrease in the weight of the radiation rods [11]. Nevertheless, after the SPS formed on the TRRS, no chemical reaction products were observed on the surface of the radiation rod, and the rate of mass loss of the radiation rod decreased significantly [11]. Importantly, ultrasonic transmission is more stable during this period, which is essential for achieving homogeneous aluminum alloy casting. It was shown that the microstructural characteristics of the SPS formed under the combined effects of ultrasonic vibration, cavitation impact, and high-temperature environments include reduced crystal defects, increased average grain size, and decreased β phase content [18]. The SPS is a specific combination of microstructural features in TRRS, including dislocation density, phase distribution, and grain boundary characteristics. The experiment demonstrates that the SPS can effectively inhibit chemical corrosion and reduce the rate of mass loss. Additionally, it is important to note that the quality of the aluminum alloy ingots remains consistent throughout the entire ultrasonic casting process [19]. This indicates that the formation of SPS does not affect the effectiveness of the ultrasonic treatment.

While our previous study mainly focused on the role of SPS in suppressing chemical corrosion of TRRS in molten aluminum, the formation mechanism of SPS during ultrasonic casting has not yet been clarified. Therefore, based on our previous research, this study aims to elucidate the formation mechanism of the SPS from the TRRS microstructural evolution during ultrasonic casting. By investigating the effect of ultrasonic vibration, cavitation impact, and high-temperature environment on the evolution of TRRS microstructures through multiple experimental, a mechanism for SPS formation is proposed. In addition, the contribution of each factor to the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS was confirmed through aluminum melt immersion corrosion experiments. This work provides a theoretical basis for SPS-based protection strategies for titanium alloy radiation rods and a guidance for optimizing ultrasonic casting.

High-temperature environment experiment

During the ultrasonic casting process, the TRRS is in direct contact with the aluminum melt at 700 °C. To investigate the effect of high temperature on the microstructure of TRRS, high-temperature experiment III was designed. Sample C was prepared from the TC4 rod stock of the same batch and was cut into specimens with dimensions of 80 × 15 × 10 mm. These specimens were placed in a resistance furnace at 700 °C, as shown in Fig. 1c, and held at this temperature for 0, 2, and 4 h. The surface microstructure of Sample C was characterized using EBSD.

Aluminum melt immersion corrosion experiments

To verify the effects of the three-factor combination—ultrasonic vibration, cavitation impact, and high-temperature environment—on the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS in molten aluminum, immersion corrosion experiments were designed. Samples A, B, and C obtained from Experiments I–III were machined into specimens with dimensions of 20 × 15 × 10 mm. Each specimen was immersed in molten pure aluminum at 700 °C for 4 h to complete immersion corrosion experiments IV–VI (Fig. 1d–f). After cooling and polishing, the morphology and thickness variations of the titanium–aluminum reaction zone (TARZ) on samples D, E, and F were characterized using SEM.

Discussion

Analysis effects of ultrasonic vibration on TRRS microstructure and chemical corrosion resistance

Analysis effect of ultrasonic vibration on dislocation density

Experiment I demonstrated that ultrasonic vibration reduced the dislocation density in TRRS. Dislocations are inevitably introduced into TC4 radiation rods during the casting process. When the ultrasonic system is activated, ultrasonic waves are transmitted through the radiation rod via cyclic strain, thereby inducing oscillatory motion of dislocations within the TRRS. The average number of dislocations, △N, encountered per unit time by an oscillating dislocation can be described by the following equation [20]:(1)ΔN=v0απτvμτyμ

In the equation, v0 represents a normalized constant with the dimension of frequency, α denotes a constant, τv represents the amplitude of vibration stress, τy represents the yield stress, and μ represents the shear modulus. The relationship between the yield stress τy of the material and its dislocation density ρ can be described by Taylor’s hardening law:(2)τy=αμbρ

In the equation, ρ represents the dislocation density, and B denotes the Burgers vector. The larger average number of dislocations, ΔN, encountered during oscillation, the higher the frequency of opposite-sign dislocation encounters, va. When dislocations of opposite signs encounter each other in a metal, cross-slip may occur, leading to the annihilation of dislocation dipoles and the mutual cancellation of dislocations [20]. The probability of dislocation dipole annihilation, ρann, can be expressed as follows [21]:(3)ρann=ρvaexp-Qσ/kT

In the equation, [-Q(σ)/kT] represents the Boltzmann factor, which is determined by the applied stress and temperature of the material. Based on the analysis of Eqs. (1), (2), the probability of dislocation annihilation increases with increasing ultrasonic vibration stress amplitude. Ultrasonic vibration treatment can effectively reduce the dislocation density in materials.

Fig. 9 schematically illustrates the reduction in dislocation density under ultrasonic vibration. Before ultrasonic vibration, numerous dislocations are distributed around the grain boundaries (gray) in the TRRS (Fig. 9a). During ultrasonic vibration, a large number of dislocations in the TRRS undergo oscillatory motion and annihilate, leading to a reduction in dislocation density (Fig. 9b) and the partial development of SPS features in the TRRS.Fig. 9Schematic illustration of the effect of ultrasonic vibration on the distribution of TRRS dislocations before (a) and after (b) treatment.

Schematic illustration of the effect of ultrasonic vibration on the distribution of TRRS dislocations before (a) and after (b) treatment.

It should be noted that, owing to variations in vibration amplitude at different positions along the TRRS, the effect of ultrasonic vibration exhibits regional characteristics. Li et al. [22] investigated the amplitude distribution across different regions of the TRRS during ultrasonic vibration and found that it follows a sinusoidal pattern: the amplitude is highest at the core and decreases progressively toward the edges. It is evident from Eqs. (1), (2) that an increase in amplitude leads to a higher number of dislocation encounters per unit time, thereby increasing the probability of dislocation annihilation and ultimately resulting in a lower dislocation density. These findings are consistent with the results in Fig. 2, which show that after 6 h of ultrasonic vibration, the dislocation density at the TRRS core is lower than that at the edge.

Analysis effect of ultrasonic vibration on dislocation distribution

As shown in Fig. 2a, ultrasonic vibration progressively untangles the dislocation networks in the TRRS. Fig. 9 schematically illustrates the effect of ultrasonic vibration on the dislocation distribution in the TRRS. As shown in Fig. 9a, a large number of dislocations are tangled around the grain boundaries of the TC4 titanium alloy. During ultrasonic vibration, atoms in the metal transmit acoustic energy through oscillatory motion, and part of this energy is absorbed in the process [23]. Due to the disordered atomic arrangement near grain boundaries, atoms experience stronger constraints, causing ultrasonic energy to be preferentially absorbed at the grain boundaries. As a result, the free energy near the grain boundaries continuously increases [24]. With continued energy accumulation, dislocations migrate from high-energy grain boundary regions toward the low-energy grain interiors, thereby reducing dislocation accumulation around the grain boundaries, as shown in Fig. 9b.

Analysis effect of ultrasonic vibration on the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS

As demonstrated in Experiment IV, ultrasonic vibration enhances the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS. The dissolution of titanium is a necessary condition for TRRS chemical corrosion, and inhibiting the dissolution of titanium atoms is therefore the fundamental approach to suppressing TRRS chemical corrosion [18]. Due to the severe lattice distortion around dislocations, atoms possess higher energy and therefore require less activation energy for diffusion. Consequently, dislocations act as fast pathways for the dissolution and diffusion of Ti atoms [25]. Following Experiment I, ultrasonic vibration reduced the dislocation density in the TRRS, thereby decreasing the dissolution rate of Ti atoms. In addition, ultrasonic vibration reduces dislocation tangle and blocks the fast diffusion pathways of Ti atoms. Both effects inhibit the diffusion and dissolution of Ti atoms, thus delaying the chemical corrosion process of the TRRS.

Analysis effects of cavitation impact on TRRS microstructure and chemical corrosion resistance

Analysis effect of cavitation impact on phase distribution

Experiment II shows that the β-phase content in TRRS decreases after cavitation impact. TC4 titanium alloy is a two phases alloy primarily composed of the α phase (hexagonal close-packed, HCP) with the β phase (body-centered cubic, BCC) distributed along the grain boundaries [26]. Differences in the crystal structures of the two phases lead to variations in their mechanical properties. In the HCP-structured α phase, each unit cell possesses only three independent slip systems, resulting in limited cross-slip capability and poor plasticity, which makes it prone to lamellar exfoliation. Moreover, a large number of Al atoms are solid-solved into the α-Ti lattice, causing lattice distortion that hinders dislocation motion [27]. Consequently, the α phase exhibits high strength and hardness. In the BCC-structured β phase, up to 12 slip systems are available, resulting in strong cross-slip capability and superior plasticity, which is beneficial for energy absorption during deformation [28]. The differences in the mechanical properties described above lead to an uneven stress distribution between the α and β phases during deformation. Based on the stress distribution calculations for duplex stainless steel during deformation reported by Kuang et al. [29], the stresses σα and σβ experienced by the α and β phase in TC4 under true strain εp can be estimated as follows:(4)σα=Kαε0α+εpnασβ=Kβε0β+εpnβ(5)Kα=σbαεuαnαKβ=σbβεuβnβ(6)ε0α=EαKα1nα-1ε0β=EβKβ1nβ-1

In the equation, K is the proportionality coefficient, ε0 represents the true strain at yield point, εu denotes the maximum uniform true strain, E is the elastic modulus, n is the work-hardening exponent, and σb and σ0 are the tensile strength and yield strength, respectively. The relevant calculation parameters are listed in Table 4 [30], [31]. When the true strain εp = 2.5%, calculations based on Eqs. (3), (4), (5) yield a stress ratio between the two phases of σa/σβ = 49.93. This indicates that the α phase bears substantially higher stress than the β phase during cavitation impact, a phenomenon known as the “stress shielding” effect. Fig. 10 schematically illustrates the two-phase behavior of TRRS during cavitation impact from the side view of the sample. During the cavitation impact process, under the effect of “stress shielding,” the α phase (blue) experiences higher stress and is preferentially exfoliated, thereby exposing the more intact β phase (red) to the melt. This observation is consistent with the results of experiment II shown in Fig. 4. Analysis effect of cavitation impact on dislocations and grain

As shown in Fig. 3a, cavitation impact leads to a continuous increase in dislocation density, enhanced dislocation tangle, and a reduction in grain size in the TRRS. When ultrasound transmits through the melt, microscopic bubble nuclei repeatedly grow, expand, compress, and eventually collapse under the alternating pressure field, a phenomenon known as the cavitation effect [32]. In fact, the impact of cavitation on TRRS is primarily characterized as the impact of microjets formed when cavitation bubbles collapse near the TRRS. The microjet impact pressure Pwh can be estimated using the water hammer pressure equation [33]:(7)Pwh=12ρalcΔv

In the equation, c denotes the speed of sound in molten aluminum, taken as c = 4600 m/s, ρ represents the density of molten aluminum, taken as ρ = 2450 kg/m3, and Δv is the velocity of the microjet at the TRRS. To quantitatively evaluate the velocity of the microjet at the TRRS, the bubble dynamics were analyzed using the Keller–Miksis equation [34], [35], [36], which can be expressed as:(8)1-R˙cRR¨+321-R˙3cR˙2=1ρ1+R˙cPb-P∞+RρcddtPb-P∞

In the equation, R, R˙, and R¨ represents the bubble radius, wall velocity, and acceleration, respectively, P∞ is the pressure in the far field liquid and Pb represents the pressure inside the bubble. In ultrasonic cavitation, the far-field pressure P∞ is typically expressed as(9)P∞=P0+Pasin2πftwhere P0 is the environmental pressure, taken as P0 = 105pa, Pa is the acoustic pressure amplitude, and f is the frequency of the ultrasonic vibration, taken as f = 20 kHz. The acoustic pressure amplitude Pa can be estimated based on the vibration parameters of the ultrasonic transducer.(10)Pa=2ρcAπfwhere A represents the vibration amplitude, taken as A = 20 μm. The pressure inside the cavitation bubble Pb is assumed to follow a polytropic gas process and can be written as(11)Pb=P0+2σR0-PvR0R3γ+Pv-2σR0-4μR˙Rwhere R0 represents the initial bubble radius taken as R0 = 10-6 m, σ represents the surface tension, taken as σ = 0.865 N/m, μ is the liquid viscosity taken as μ = 0.0013 Pa·s, Pv is the vapor pressure inside the bubble taken as Pv = 2000 Pa, and γ is the polytropic exponent of the gas, taken as γ = 1.33. Substituting the initial conditions given above and solving using Eqs. (7), (8), (9), (10), the results show that the bubble rapidly collapses after expansion. During this process, the wall velocity increases sharply, reaching a maximum of approximately 188 m/s.

It should be noted that the K-M equation describes the spherical collapse of a bubble and cannot directly calculate the velocity of a microjet, which is generated by asymmetric collapse near a solid boundary. However, due to the energy focusing effect, it is generally believed that the velocity of the microjet is of the same order of magnitude as or slightly higher than that of the bubble wall. In this study, the calculated velocity of the cavitation bubble wall at the moment of collapse is essentially consistent with the microjet velocities (140–180 m/s) reported in previous studies [33], [37]. Therefore, we take the velocity of the microjet as Δv = 188 m/s.

Based on the above parameters, substituting into Eq. (7) yields an estimated impact pressure Pwh = 1394 MPa. The yield strength of TC4 is approximately 800 MPa [38]. When the cavitation impact pressure exceeds this value, TRRS undergoes plastic deformation. Dislocation motion is the microscopic manifestation of macroscopic plastic deformation. During this process, dislocations multiply and progressively evolve into dislocation tangles. Under sustained cavitation impact, dislocation tangles progressively absorb surrounding dislocations, gradually evolving into dislocation bands [39]. These dislocation bands subdivide the grains into multiple smaller regions and, through their rearrangement and evolution, eventually form new grain boundaries, thereby achieving grain refinement.

Analysis effect of cavitation impact on the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS

In cavitation impact experiment II, the TRRS exhibited dislocation growth, grain refinement, and a significant reduction in β phase content. Based on the preceding analysis, it can be concluded that dislocation growth and grain refinement accelerate the chemical corrosion of the TRRS. However, no significant difference in the TARZ thickness was observed among the samples in experiment V, as shown in Fig. 7. This is attributed to the significantly lower diffusion rate of Ti atoms in the α phase than in the β phase at 700 °C [18]. The reduction in the β phase retards the dissolution diffusion of Ti atoms, decreases the Ti-Al reaction rate, and thereby enhances the chemical corrosion resistance of the TRRS.

Analysis effects of high-temperature environment on TRRS microstructure and chemical corrosion resistance

Analysis effect of high-temperature environment on TRRS microstructure

As shown in high-temperature experiment III, the average grain size of TRRS increases with extended holding time, resulting in a decrease in grain boundary density and a reduction in dislocation density. Grain growth is the process by which larger grains consume smaller grains, accompanied by the straightening of concave grain boundaries. During this process, the interfacial energy of the system decreases, making it thermodynamically spontaneous. The 700 °C environment in experiment III accelerated atomic diffusion, providing the driving force for grain boundary migration and resulting in an increase in the average grain size.

Additionally, the high-temperature environment provides kinetic energy for atomic motion, increasing the average atomic kinetic energy and thereby reducing lattice resistance within the TRRS. This promotes the merging and annihilation of dislocations with opposite signs within TRRS, thereby decreasing dislocation density.

Analysis effect of high-temperature environment on the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS

After High-Temperature Experiment III, the TRRS exhibited an increased grain size, a reduced grain boundary density, and a decreased dislocation density. An increased grain size reduces crystal defects such as grain boundaries, thereby decreasing the number of fast diffusion pathways for Ti atom dissolution [20]. This further reduces the Ti–Al reaction rate and enhances the chemical corrosion resistance of the TRRS. Accordingly, in Experiment VI, a longer high-temperature holding time results in stronger corrosion resistance and a thinner TARZ.

Synergistic mechanism of SPS formation in ultrasonic casting

In previous research, we experimentally confirmed the formation of surface SPS structures on TRRS during ultrasonic casting and demonstrated that this structure provides limited suppression against chemical corrosion [18]. It should be noted that the evolution of the TRRS microstructure during ultrasonic casting occurs under the combined effects of ultrasonic vibration, cavitation impacts, and high-temperature conditions. Therefore, the changes in TRRS observed during ultrasonic casting in Reference [18] can serve as experimental evidence of the behavior of TRRS under the coupled action of these three factors. However, the present results indicate that ultrasonic vibration, cavitation impact, and high-temperature environment individually can only partially reproduce the characteristic features of SPS. This indicates that the formation of SPS is not caused by a single factor, but rather results from the combined action of these three factors.

Ultrasonic vibration promotes dislocation migration and annihilation, making TRRS more susceptible to deformation during cavitation impact. Under cavitation impact, the α phase is first destroyed due to “stress shielding,” altering the TRRS phase distribution. This altered phase distribution influences Ti atom diffusion behavior. Concurrently, the high-temperature environment accelerates grain growth and grain boundary migration. This dual effect reduces rapid diffusion pathways for Ti atoms while promoting dislocation annihilation. The combined effects of these effects effectively promote the formation of SPS, inhibit the diffusion of titanium atoms, and enhance the chemical corrosion resistance of TRRS.

Conclusion

This study investigates the effects of ultrasonic vibration, cavitation impact, and high-temperature environments on the microstructural evolution and chemical corrosion resistance of the TRRS during ultrasonic casting. The formation mechanism of SPS is analyzed, and the following conclusions are drawn:(1)Ultrasonic vibration promotes dislocation motion in TC4, increasing the probability of encounters between dislocations of opposite signs, thereby enhancing dislocation annihilation and reducing the overall dislocation density.(2)During cavitation impact, the α phase in the TRRS is preferentially damaged, followed by the detachment of the β phase. The “stress shielding” effect arising from the mechanical property differences between the two phases is the primary reason for the decrease in β phase content under cavitation impact.(3)The high-temperature environment reduces lattice resistance within the TRRS, decreases dislocation density, and accelerates atomic diffusion and grain boundary migration, resulting in increased average grain size and a reduced number of grain boundaries.
